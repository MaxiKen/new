## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:72

> The herald ˹along with the guards˺ replied, “We have lost the King’s measuring cup. And whoever brings it will be awarded a camel-load ˹of grain˺. I guarantee it.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Object Is Named as Royal Property**

The answer identifies what is missing: the king's cup. The commentators describe it as a goblet belonging to the ruler, used both for drinking and as a measuring vessel for grain, and one account adds that it was of gold and set with jewels. The specification matters legally. This is not a traveller's possession or a merchant's utensil; it is crown property, and its loss is a matter of state rather than of private complaint. In a famine administration where the same vessel served as the standard measure for food, the disappearance of the king's cup would be noticed by the granaries as well as by the palace. The charge the herald announces is therefore serious in a way that ordinary theft is not.

**Two Speakers and One Announcement**

The commentators note a division of labour in the announcement. The identification of the missing object is attributed to Joseph's companions, while the offer of a camel-load and the guarantee are attributed to the herald. The Qur'anic verse presents the speech as a single reply, and the translation reflects the composite by naming the herald along with the guards. The detail is worth recording because it shows the scene: an official party pursuing a caravan, one of them stating the loss and another stating the reward. The chapter does not need the division and does not mark it, but the tradition preserves it, and it gives the exchange a realistic texture. The texture is that of an actual roadside procedure. A herald calls out the charge and the reward; an official adds the guarantee that makes it acceptable. Two voices, two functions, one continuous announcement, reported by the Qur'an without marking its boundaries. What reaches the reader is the exchange as the brothers experienced it — a single demand from the government.

**The Reward Is a Camel-Load**

The prize offered is a camel-load of grain, and the amount is not incidental. Verse 59 recorded that the brothers arrived with eleven camels and requested eleven loads — ten for themselves and one for a brother at home — and that Joseph refused the eleventh until the brother appeared. The reward now on offer is precisely that eleventh load. A reader who has followed the arithmetic will notice that the object the brothers could not obtain by request is being offered as a prize for returning a cup they did not take. The chapter does not point this out. It simply sets the same measure in front of them twice, once as a refusal and once as an inducement.

**"I Am Its Guarantor"**

The herald adds a personal undertaking: *wa-anā bihī zaʿīm* — I am guarantor for it. The commentators gloss the phrase as meaning that whoever produces the cup will certainly receive the camel-load. The guarantee converts a general offer into a binding commitment from a named official, which in a famine economy is a meaningful addition. Grain is scarce, promises of grain are cheap, and a party searching a caravan has no obvious reason to trust that a reward will be paid. The guarantee removes the doubt and makes the search worth conducting, which is the immediate practical purpose of the whole announcement. The undertaking is also legal theatre with a practical function. By staking himself on the outcome the official converts an arbitrary demand into a bounded one, and bounded demands are the kind a caravan can accept. The brothers submit to the search because they have been given a warrant for it, and the warrant is a man's own person.

**A Charge Turned Into an Incentive**

The verse performs a rhetorical shift. Verse 70 was an accusation: you are thieves. Verse 72 is an offer: whoever brings the cup will be rewarded. The second formulation does not withdraw the first; it works alongside it. The effect on the accused party is to give them something to do other than protest. A caravan told only that it is composed of thieves can deny and move on. A caravan told that a reward is available for producing the missing object has a reason to submit to a search, and the search is what Joseph requires. The offer is therefore not generosity but procedure, and the following verses show the procedure working. The transformation is complete within the verse. What begins as an accusation of theft ends as a contract, with a specified reward, a named guarantor and a search conducted by consent. Joseph has produced, from a planted cup and a camel-load of grain, the legal instrument that will let him detain his brother before nine witnesses who will testify the process was fair.

**The Brothers Are Included in the Offer**

Nobody in the announcement excludes the accused from the reward. The wording is general — whoever brings it — and the brothers, who have just denied nothing and asked only what is missing, are as eligible as anyone in the caravan. That inclusion is what makes verse 73 possible: they answer with an oath about their own character rather than with a suspicion about someone else. The chapter places them in the position of people who could earn a camel-load by finding a cup they know nothing about, and who respond instead by insisting that they did not come to the land to do harm. Their inclusion is what makes the denial necessary. A reward offered only to outsiders would have left them as witnesses; offered to everyone present, it makes each of them a potential claimant and therefore a potential thief. The protest that follows is not indignation at the search but at the suspicion it implies, and it is answered by their own conduct in the next verse.

**Why the Object Must Be Publicly Named**

The whole device depends on specificity. A general accusation of theft would produce a general denial and no search. Naming the king's cup, offering a defined reward and guaranteeing it creates a procedure with a definite endpoint: the bags are opened and the object is either there or not. Joseph needs the search to happen and to happen in a particular order, and verse 76 will report that order. This verse supplies the public justification for it. The reader watches a legal process being constructed out of a reward announcement, and the construction is complete enough that the brothers accept it and submit. Naming the object also fixes the penalty in advance. Once it is identified as the king's drinking measure, its theft becomes an offence against the state's supply system, and the punishment that follows is no longer at the discretion of whoever finds it. The verse assembles, out of a reward announcement, a procedure whose outcome the accused cannot negotiate.

**The Verse Sets Up the Penalty Question**

Once the object is named and the search is agreed, the next question is what happens if the object is found. That question is asked in verse 74 and answered in verse 75, and the answer will come from the brothers themselves. Verse 72 does not state a penalty; it states only a reward. The omission is the mechanism. Had the herald announced the consequence of theft along with the reward, the brothers would have had nothing to supply and Joseph would have been bound by Egyptian law — which, as verse 76 explains, would not have allowed him to keep his brother. The reward-only formulation is what leaves the penalty open, and what leaves it open is what makes it the brothers' to state.
