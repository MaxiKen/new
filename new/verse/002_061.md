## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:61

> And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses! We cannot endure the same meal ˹every day˺. So ˹just˺ call upon your Lord on our behalf, He will bring forth for us some of what the earth produces of herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions.” Moses scolded ˹them˺, “Do you exchange what is better for what is worse? ˹You can˺ go down to any village and you will find what you have asked for.” They were stricken with disgrace and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah for rejecting Allah’s signs and unjustly killing the prophets. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Complaint About a Gift**

The community's words are quoted, and what they complain about is the food they have been given. The Arabic describes a single kind of food that they cannot bear to continue with, and the list that follows names vegetables of the commonest sort. The Book records the sentence as spoken, which allows the reader to measure the complaint against the provisions described in the preceding verse.

The request itself is not framed as a plea for survival. Moses is told to call upon God on their behalf, and the food requested is named item by item with a specificity that suggests a preference rather than a need. The Book's account of the period gives no indication that the community was short of anything at the time.

**Moses' Answer: the Comparison He Puts to Them**

Moses responds with a question rather than a refusal: would they exchange what is better for what is worse? The Book's phrase makes the exchange the substance of the offence, and it treats the community's preference as a bad bargain rather than as a small request. The comparison is between what had been provided and what could be obtained, and the answer assumes the first is greater.

He then tells them to go down into any town and buy what they want. That instruction is itself a judgment. The provision that came from the sky was available to a people with no land; the food they were asking for grows in cultivated ground, and obtaining it meant leaving the arrangement that had fed them. The verse records the dismissal without adding a comment.

**The Three Charges at the End**

The sentence that closes the passage names three things at once: disgrace and misery, the displeasure of God, and the reason for both. The Book lists the reasons as the rejection of God's signs and the unjust killing of prophets. That second charge is one the sūrah raises against this community repeatedly, and it is also recorded in the Book's account of earlier messengers (5:70).

Placing the killing of prophets in the same verse as a complaint about vegetables is not a mismatch. The Book's purpose in this passage is to show a pattern of conduct that runs from small refusals to grave crimes, and it presents the whole sequence as one history rather than as separate incidents. What the community did with its prophets and what it did with its dinner belong to the same account of a people that would not accept what it was given.

**"A Fair Reward for Their Disobedience"**

The verse states that what the community suffered was earned, and it uses two words for the conduct that earned it: a departure from obedience and a crossing of limits. Both terms have appeared earlier in the sūrah, applied to the hypocrites and to the covenant-breakers, so the vocabulary ties this community's conduct to the categories the reader has already met.

The Book's language of recompense is consistent throughout: it describes punishment as the fit consequence of a chosen course, and it does not present disasters as arbitrary. The clause at the end of the verse therefore functions as an explanation rather than as an additional penalty, and it closes the account of the wilderness period by naming its cause.

**The Manna Refused, and What It Meant**

What the community rejected was food that arrived without cultivation, and what they asked for was food that required land. The Book's later account treats the request as a step away from dependence rather than as a change of diet, and it makes the point through Moses' answer about going down to a town. The desire for ordinary produce was a desire for a life in which provision arrived by ordinary means, which is precisely the arrangement the community was being trained not to rely on.

The Book keeps this theme in view in its description of the provisions elsewhere, where the same gifts are named alongside the warning not to corrupt the land (7:160). The two commands — eat what is provided, and keep within limits — describe a settlement in a territory rather than a march through a desert, and the community in this verse was asking to be moved into that mode before its time.

**How the Passage Reads Within the Address**

This verse concludes the recitation of the exodus period, and it does so with a summary of the community's standing. Their ancestors were fed, they complained, and the complaint marked the beginning of a decline that the Book traces through the rejection of signs and the killing of prophets. From this point the sūrah turns to a wider statement about belief and its rewards before returning to the covenant.

The reader reaches the end of the sentence with the full picture the sūrah has assembled: gifts without measure, failures without excuse, and mercy extended more than once. The address to the descendants of these people continues on the strength of that picture, and the next verse steps back from the narrative to state who is acceptable to God on the basis of belief and good conduct rather than ancestry.
