## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:61

> And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses! We cannot endure the same meal ˹every day˺. So ˹just˺ call upon your Lord on our behalf, He will bring forth for us some of what the earth produces of herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions.” Moses scolded ˹them˺, “Do you exchange what is better for what is worse? ˹You can˺ go down to any village and you will find what you have asked for.” They were stricken with disgrace and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah for rejecting Allah’s signs and unjustly killing the prophets. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Complaint Against Heaven's Table**

The verse opens with the longest direct speech any wilderness generation addresses to its prophet in this recital, and the speech is a complaint about the menu. *We cannot endure the same meal* — the manna and quails, provision that fell from the sky without sowing, has become unbearable through repetition. The complaint's structure is worth naming precisely, because the verse's verdict depends on it. Nothing in the speech accuses the food of being bad: the manna was ṭayyib, sent with a command to eat (verse 57). What the speakers cannot bear is *uniformity* — one food, day after day, with no market, no variety, no Egypt. The sin is not appetite, which the law of provision permits, but the refusal of a gift's *form* while demanding its continuation — keep feeding us, but on our terms, with the crops we remember. The biblical record preserves the same complaint in the same wilderness: the people weeping for the fish and cucumbers and melons and leeks and onions and garlic of Egypt, and the quails granted in answer turning into judgment on the cravers (Numbers 11). The Qur'an's filing compresses the episode to its moral geometry — heaven's food rejected for earth's — and lets the geography speak: the menu they demand is literally the menu of the land they were delivered from.

**The Requested List**

The five crops are named one by one — herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, onions — and the naming is the verse's rhetoric. These are not delicacies but garden-commons, the cheapest produce of any village plot, listed with a specificity that reads like a shopping order. One word in the list carries its own exegetical history: *fūm*, rendered garlic, which classical lexicography also reads as wheat — either way a field-crop, either way ordinary. The list's function is contrast: against manna, food that arrived daily from above the sky, the demand is for food that arrives annually out of dirt, cultivated, stored, traded, priced. The demanders are not asking for more; they are asking for *normal* — for the economic world they recognize, where food has human mediation and the eater stands in a market rather than under a cloud. That is why the complaint is theological before it is culinary. Manna made dependence visible every morning; onions make it invisible in a supply chain. A people who cannot endure the same meal are, in the verse's diagnosis, a people who cannot endure being visibly dependent — and the recital's later dealings with this community will repeatedly expose the same nerve: the demand to see Allah plainly, the demand for a visible substitute while Moses is away, the demand now for a food-system that hides its Giver.

**Moses's Rebuke: The Direction of the Exchange**

The answer comes as a question that contains the whole verdict: *Do you exchange what is better for what is worse?* The verb is *istibdāl* — substitution, the same family the recital used for the changed word at the gate (verse 59) — and the question's force is in its direction-words. Better and worse here are not flavors but stations: the heavenly provision is the better, the earth's commons the worse, and the exchange is therefore a descent dressed as a purchase. The second half of the rebuke drives it home: *go down to any village and you will find what you have asked for*. The verb is *habaṭa*, to go down — the sūrah's word for Adam's exit from the garden (*descend from the heavens*, 2:36) and for every fall from a given station to an earned one. The crops are available; they have always been available; they grow wherever people farm, and the camp can have them the moment it accepts descent into the ordinary economy. The word *miṣr* in the command — rendered *any village* — carries the pun the exegetes debate: as a common noun it is a town, any town; as the proper name it is Egypt, and al-Ṭabarī notes that the verse's orthography requires the indefinite reading (*miṣran*, not *Miṣra*), so the command is go down to a town, not return to Egypt — though early readers heard the second meaning humming under the first. Both readings converge: what the demanders want is available anywhere below heaven, and getting it means leaving the table where heaven serves.

**Stricken With Disgrace and Misery**

The verdict arrives in two coverings and a burden. *They were stricken with disgrace and misery* — *ḍuribat ʿalayhim al-dhillah wa-l-maskanah*: abjection and destitution struck over them as a tent is struck over a camper, or a tax is struck on an account. The imagery of the verb is enclosure: the two conditions wrap the community, and the recital is not describing one bad season but a standing condition — the same sentence will be refiled in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān with the addition that the covering holds wherever they are found, except under a covenant with Allah and a covenant with people (3:112). The third element is active rather than passive: *they invited the displeasure of Allah* — *bāʾū bi-ghaḍabin min Allāh*, and the verb *bāʾū* is commercial in its root sense: they returned home carrying it, the way a trader returns carrying his goods. Wrath is filed as acquired property, earned and borne back, which is the sūrah's market idiom applied to the worst possible cargo. The two coverings and the burden together answer the complaint's logic exactly: the people who could not endure heaven's monotony receive earth's monotony as a permanent estate — disgrace that does not rotate, misery that does not vary — and the displeasure they carry is the receipt.

**Rejecting Signs, Killing Prophets**

The verdict names its grounds, and the grounds exceed the meal: *for rejecting Allah's signs and unjustly killing the prophets*. A complaint about onions is being answered with a charge sheet of murder, and the connection is the recital's method rather than disproportion. The verse addresses the community across its whole history in the second person — the same inheritance logic that made descendants parties to the sea and the calf — and files the two capital crimes of that history: the signs rejected (the demand for sight, the changed word, the calf taken after the sea) and the prophets killed without right, a charge the Qur'an repeats across sūrahs (2:87, 2:91, 3:21, 3:112, 3:181, 4:155, 5:70) with the same *unjustly* — since no prophet's killing could ever be just, the adverb marks the killings as done against known truth. The sūrah will turn the charge into an argument at verse 91: a community that says *we believe in what was sent down to us* is asked why it killed Allah's prophets before, if belief is the claim (2:91). The meal-complaint sits inside that larger indictment because it shares its root: the rejecting of what Allah sends, in the form He sends it. The manna rejected was a sign handled exactly as the prophets were handled — received, resented, replaced.

**Disobedience and Violations: The Summary Clause**

The verse closes its case file with a summary in two nouns: *this is for their disobedience and their violations* — *ʿaṣaw wa-kānū yaʿtadūn*. The first is the refusal of command, the plain disobedience; the second, from *ʿudwān*, is crossing the set bound — the transgression that does not merely decline a limit but overruns it. The pair covers the verse's whole material: wanting the sky's food replaced is disobedience to the provision's terms; demanding it while demanding heaven's continuation, mocking the gift's form, is violation. The exegetical tradition also hears in the double formula an escalation — habitual disobedience ripening into aggressive excess — and the recital supports the hearing: the changed word became the mocked gate, the mocked gate became the calf, and the calf's generation became the killers of prophets. The summary clause is therefore not a redundancy but a diagnosis of trajectory: a community is being shown where its small refusals were walking. For the sūrah's permanent readership the verse keeps its double edge. Every generation has its manna — the provision arrived in a form it did not choose — and every generation hears the same market call from the village below. The question the verse files is the rebuke's question, unchanged: which direction is your exchange running, and what will you be carrying home?
