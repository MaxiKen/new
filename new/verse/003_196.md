## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:196

> Do not be deceived by the prosperity of the disbelievers throughout the land.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Delusion Named**

The verse addresses the Prophet with a warning against being misled by the ease with which those who deny the message travel the land and prosper in it. The sentence names a conclusion that a person could draw from appearances: that the comfort of the deniers is a sign of their standing before God.

The warning is stated in the negative because the impression itself is the danger, and the verse denies the conclusion before it can take hold. The counsel is needed because the conclusion it forbids is one that a reasonable observer might reach from the facts alone. The sentences that follow in the sūrah supply the reason, and the next verse states what the freedom of movement actually amounts to.

**The Occasion Reported**

The commentators record an occasion for the passage. Some believers are said to have remarked on the contrast between their own hunger and hardship and the freedom with which the idolaters moved about the land and profited from commerce. Others report that the remark came from the hypocrites, who used the same contrast to cast doubt on the message.

Al-Rāzī observed that although the sentence is addressed to the Prophet, its audience is wider, as though a listener were being addressed directly. The verse is therefore read as a warning to anyone who measures the truth of a claim by the comfort of the people who reject it.

**Freedom of Movement as Appearance**

The observation that the deniers range over the earth appears again in the sūrah, where the same impression is addressed: they are given length of days, and their going about the land looks like success. The thought occurs to the observer because the world's arrangement places no visible obstacle in the path of those who oppose the message.

The two passages belong together, and the earlier statement had already warned the believers against drawing conclusions from the length of the interval granted to their opponents. Both treat the ease of the deniers as a postponement rather than a verdict, and both turn the reader's attention to what comes after the interval. The community is taught to read the delay as a part of the story rather than as its ending.

**The Respite Described Elsewhere**

The Book states in several passages that the comfort granted to those who deny the message is a delay and not a reward. One passage tells the prosperous to eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted by false hope, adding that they will come to know. Another states that ornaments and pleasures of that kind are no more than the passing enjoyment of the present life, while what is with God belongs to those who are mindful of Him.

A third passage states that the present life has been made attractive to those who reject the truth and that they mock the believers, while those who are mindful will rank above them on the Day of Judgment. The verse before the reader depends on the same judgment about appearances.

**The World's Standing in the Book**

The tradition preserved a statement attributed to the Prophet that explains the paradox the verse addresses: the world is a prison for the believer and a garden for the one who denies. The sentence expresses what the verse assumes, since the ease of the deniers and the hardship of the believers are not evidence about the next life and may indicate its opposite.

Al-Rāzī and the other commentators made the same point in their explanations of the passage. What appears to be a favour to one group and a deprivation of the other is measured by a scale that the world's own indications cannot reveal.

**The Judgment on the Worldly Life**

The Book describes the worldly life as a brief and delusive possession in many places. One passage states that after being rescued people transgress in the land unjustly, and that their transgression is against their own souls, with the present life being only a passing enjoyment before the return to God. Another compares the present life to play, amusement, display, and rivalry in wealth and children, and calls it the delusion of enjoyment.

The sentence before the reader rests on that estimate. What looks to the observer like the freedom of the deniers is measured against a scale in which the whole of the worldly life weighs very little, and the next verse states what remains for them when it is over.


The sūrah has been contrasting the two groups throughout its closing movement. It has described the reward of those who revere God and the destination of those who rejected the message, and the sentence before the reader applies that contrast to the observers themselves.

The members of the community were the ones tempted by the comparison, and the verse answers them by forbidding the conclusion they were drawing. The reader is asked to hold two facts together: the appearance that impresses him and the account of the outcome that the Book supplies. What they were to rely on was the account the sūrah had already given of both outcomes, and the reminder was addressed to people who had been given that account.

**What the Verse Corrects**

The verse corrects a reading of the world in which success is identified with the condition of those who appear to be succeeding. The Book's teaching is that the present arrangement is not the final one, and that comparisons drawn from it do not hold.

The correction is delivered as a prohibition addressed to a person, which is how the Book often states a conclusion it wants drawn. The community is told not to let what it sees mislead it, and the sentence leaves the reader with the fact that overturns the impression. What the deniers have secured has a limit, and the verse that follows states what that limit is.

**The Verse and the One It Echoes

The sentence is the second in the sūrah to deal with the ease of those who deny the message. The earlier passage, which comes a few verses before this one, states that the delay granted to such people should not be mistaken for an advantage, and it warns that their respite is a form of drawing them on. The sentence before the reader repeats the warning from the side of the observer rather than in a statement of fact, since the temptation it answers is the one the community felt when it watched its opponents travel and profit while it went hungry. The Book addresses the same temptation in another sūrah, where it asks those who trade in falsehood to enjoy themselves and names their destination (40:4). The three passages together show a single concern treated from three angles, and the community's teachers used them together when they explained why hardship and prosperity are not the measures of a person's standing.
