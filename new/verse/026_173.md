## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:173

> pouring upon them a rain ˹of brimstone˺. How evil was the rain of those who had been warned!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rain Becomes an Instrument of Judgment**

The verse describes rain falling upon the people of Lot and calls it an evil rain. Rain ordinarily suggests mercy, growth, and relief. Here the familiar form of provision is turned into an instrument of punishment. The reversal shows that no created element is inherently outside God's command; the same world that sustains life can become the means by which judgment arrives.

The translation identifies the rain as brimstone, giving the image a destructive quality rather than a nourishing one. The point is not to invite speculation about natural mechanisms. It is to show the precision and seriousness of the punishment that overtook a people after warning.


The rain is called the rain of those who had been warned. That description keeps the judgment from appearing arbitrary. Lot had spoken plainly, the people had threatened him, and God had given the community an opportunity to turn back. The warning was mercy before it became a witness against them.

A person who hears a warning may complain that judgment is harsh, but the verse asks what was refused beforehand. The evil rain is not the first divine act in the story. It comes after the community has chosen to defend its corruption and reject the messenger who called it to fear God.

**Mercy and Punishment Use the Same Creation**

Rain is a reminder that creation is not autonomous. Water can nourish crops, refresh the land, or, by God's command, become part of a destructive event. People cannot infer divine approval from the ordinary continuation of nature. They must read blessings and warnings through revelation and moral accountability.

This also cautions against treating material conditions as a simple measure of righteousness. A fertile season may be mercy, a test, or a delay before judgment. A disaster may be a consequence, a trial, or something whose full meaning is known only to God. The revealed account of Lot's people is certain, but human beings should not apply its verdict carelessly to every event.


The community had treated its desires as normal and Lot's warning as the offense. The punishment reverses that confidence. What the people had called secure is exposed as fragile, and what they had dismissed as a prophet's objection is confirmed by the event.

The rain therefore falls not only on bodies and buildings but on a false moral order. It ends a public system that had made wrongdoing a condition of belonging and had threatened to expel the truthful. God's judgment restores the distinction between warning and corruption that the people had tried to erase.


Quran 25:40 recalls a city passed by and showered with a dreadful rain, asking whether its ruins were not seen, while noting that the people did not expect resurrection. That parallel connects physical remains with a failure of moral imagination. The ruins were visible, but the lesson could still be ignored.

The present verse calls the rain evil because it was the consequence of a warned community's refusal. Later observers should not turn the ruins into a tourist curiosity detached from accountability. The remnant is a sign only when it leads the heart to fear God and reject the assumptions that produced the judgment.


Without the prophetic warning, the rain might be described only as a catastrophe. With the warning, it becomes a moral event disclosed by God. The community had knowledge, opportunity, and a messenger. Its destruction cannot be explained as though no choice preceded it.

This does not mean that every victim of a natural disaster is personally guilty of the same offense. The verse speaks about this revealed people and this revealed judgment. Its lesson for later readers is humility before God's knowledge and urgency in responding to guidance, not a license to accuse victims of other disasters.

**The Phrase “Those Who Had Been Warned” Is Heavy**

The people are not called merely inhabitants or victims. They are described by their relation to warning. They had been told what danger they faced and were still allowed time to change. The phrase makes their responsibility visible at the moment judgment arrives.

A warning gives life a different weight. After hearing it, a person cannot treat the next choice as morally empty. He may reject the messenger, but he cannot pretend that ignorance remains. The rain falls on a community whose opportunity had been made clear.


Some may think that a merciful God would never punish. The verse shows a more serious mercy. God warns, delays, clarifies, and preserves those who respond. To leave entrenched corruption untouched would abandon both its victims and the moral order. Judgment can therefore be part of protecting mercy.

Human beings must hold this truth carefully. They can oppose harm and seek justice, but they should not enjoy another person's suffering or claim certainty about hidden guilt. The evil rain belongs to God's judgment. The servant's task is to heed the warning and avoid becoming a source of harm.

**The Reader Should Hear the Warning Before the Rain**

The wise response comes while the messenger is speaking. Waiting until the consequence is visible turns repentance into regret and evidence into a final verdict. Lot's story gives the living a chance to change before they stand beneath the judgment they once denied.

The rain therefore remains a sign for the reader. Do not mistake ordinary blessings for permanent safety, or delayed punishment for approval. Take warning seriously, return to God, and keep compassion joined to moral clarity. The Lord who can turn rain into judgment can also make a path of rescue for those who obey.
