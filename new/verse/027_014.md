## Sūrah an-Naml 27:14

> And, although their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly. See then what was the end of the corruptors!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Denial With the Belief Still Intact**

The verse makes a claim about the interior of people who died three thousand years before it was recited: they knew. Their tongues said sorcery; their hearts had already accepted the signs. The Arabic is *wa istayqanathā anfusuhum* — their own souls were certain of them — using the same root for certainty, *yaqīn*, that verse 3 applied to the believers' conviction about the Hereafter.

That shared vocabulary is deliberate and uncomfortable. The believers of verse 3 are certain about something invisible; Pharaoh's court is certain about something they saw, and it does them no good at all. Certainty, the verse establishes, is not the same as faith. A man can be entirely convinced and entirely opposed. This dismantles a common assumption about disbelief — that it is fundamentally a failure to be persuaded, curable by better arguments. Al-Ṭabarī identifies the blockage here as arrogance rather than doubt, and the text supports him by naming the conviction and the rejection in the same sentence.

**Two Motives, Named Precisely**

The verse gives the grounds of the denial in two words: *ẓulman wa ʿuluwwan*, wrongfully and out of self-exaltation. These are not synonyms, and the pair covers the two directions in which the refusal operated.

*Ẓulm* is the violation of a right — putting something where it does not belong. Applied here it names what the denial did to others: the Israelites stayed enslaved because the court would not concede. The wrong is horizontal, measured in the suffering of the people whose release was being refused. *ʿUluww* is height, elevation, the posture of a man who holds himself above. It names what the denial did for the deniers: it preserved a hierarchy in which Pharaoh stood at the top. The Qur'an uses the same root for Pharaoh elsewhere, calling him one who exalted himself in the land. Read together the two words explain the whole calculation. Admitting the signs would have cost them their labour force and their standing, and they declined to pay either price while privately knowing the bill was legitimate.

**The Heart Knows and the Tongue Refuses**

The Qur'an's psychology here has a structure worth drawing out, because it treats knowledge and acknowledgment as separable faculties. What a person's *nafs* has accepted and what his mouth will concede can diverge indefinitely, and the divergence is stable — it does not resolve itself through the passage of time or the accumulation of further evidence.

There is a second implication, which concerns how the Qur'an can make this claim at all. No human reporter had access to what Pharaoh's officials privately believed; the statement is made on the authority named nine verses earlier, the One described as All-Wise, All-Knowing (27:6). The chapter established its source before it began asserting things about the insides of people, and this verse is the first place where that framing is doing real work.

Nine signs arrived in Egypt. Each one, on the model where disbelief is an information problem, should have narrowed the gap. The Qur'an reports the opposite: the court's position hardened as the demonstrations multiplied, because every new sign raised the cost of the reversal they were already refusing to make. A man who has publicly called something magic eight times cannot call the ninth a miracle without indicting his previous eight statements. Al-Rāzī noted that persistence in a denial becomes self-reinforcing, since each repetition adds to what must be surrendered. This is why the Qur'an so often frames faith as a moral act rather than an intellectual one. The obstacle is rarely the evidence.

**A Command to Look at the Ending**

The verse closes by turning to the audience with an imperative: *fa-anẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mufsidīn* — see then what was the end of the corruptors. The address shifts from the third-person report to a direct instruction, and the instruction is to examine an outcome.

This closing formula appears throughout the Qur'an at the end of destruction narratives, with the verb of looking and the word *ʿāqibah*, the end or upshot of a matter. Its function is historiographical. The past is presented as a set of completed experiments whose results are available for inspection, and the audience is told to inspect them rather than to take the warning on trust. What makes the formula effective in Mecca is that the evidence was partly physical: the ruins of ʿĀd and Thamūd stood on trade routes the Quraysh used. Egypt's case required no ruins, since the outcome was famous. The command is not to feel something about these peoples but to look at how their story finished, and to notice that the looking is being demanded of people currently in the middle of their own.

**Corruptors: The Word That Names Them at the End**

The final label is *mufsidīn*, corruptors — those who cause *fasād*, the spoiling or rotting of something sound. It is a different word from the one applied to them two verses earlier, where they were called *fāsiqīn*, breakers of bounds, and the shift tracks a development.

Breaking a boundary is what they did to themselves; corrupting is what they did to the world around them. The progression from one to the other is the natural course of unchecked refusal: a man who first exempts himself from the limits eventually damages whatever he holds power over. Egypt under Pharaoh is the Qur'an's standing example of exactly this, a state described elsewhere as dividing its people into castes and slaughtering the sons of one of them. The verse's last word therefore delivers the charge that the destruction answers. They were not destroyed for being unpersuaded. They were destroyed for knowing, refusing, and ruining what they governed while they did it. The chapter now leaves Egypt entirely and turns to two kings who used their power differently.
