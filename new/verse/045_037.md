## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:37

> To Him belongs ˹all˺ Majesty in the heavens and the earth. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Majesty as a Property of Ownership**

The word rendered Majesty names greatness and the grandeur of a sovereign, and the verse places it in the same two realms it has been discussing since its third verse. The heavens and the earth hold no grandeur that does not belong to their owner; the phrase does not mean that his greatness is distributed across creation, but that everything in both realms acknowledges a greatness that is his alone. The Book uses the same construction at the close of other chapters, where it places the attributes of God after a survey of his rule, and the effect is always to leave the reader with a claim about God rather than a task to perform. The chapter has just pronounced praise; this verse states the ground on which praise rests.

**The Two Names Return From the Opening**

The final words of the sūrah are the two names that appeared in its second verse, where the revelation of the Book was attributed to God the Almighty, the All-Wise. The repetition is a bracket around the entire chapter. At the beginning the pair guaranteed the message and its sender; at the end the pair seals the account of the judgment and the praise that follows it. The sūrah's first movement concerned what God has said; its final movement concerns what God has done and will do. The same two attributes cover both, and the reader who reaches this verse has been told that the power and the wisdom announced at the start have been in operation the whole way through.

**How the Two Occurrences Differ**

There is a difference in the way the names function in the two verses, and the commentators draw it out. At the opening, the attributes speak to the act of revelation: a book sent down requires power to protect it and wisdom to compose it. At the close, they speak to creation and judgment: the order of the heavens and the earth, and the exactness of the reckoning described in the preceding verses, display a might that is never arbitrary and a wisdom that misses nothing. The two kinds of signs that the chapter has been discussing since its third verse — the signs in the created order and the signs in the recitation — are therefore brought together under the same pair of names, which is the sūrah's way of saying that both came from the same hand.

**The Frame of a Chapter That Began With Letters**

The opening of the sūrah was an unexplained pair of letters, and its closing is a pair of names with their meanings spelled out. Between the two, the chapter has argued from the heavens and the earth, the human body, the animals, the alternation of night and day, the rain, and the winds, and it has reported a recitation and a reckoning. A reader who takes the whole journey knows more at the end than the muqaṭṭaʿāt disclosed at the beginning, and the sūrah provides that knowledge in the form of the two names that define the sender. The letters still have their secret, but the character of the one who pronounced them is now as clear as the argument can make it.

**Why the Chapter Ends Without a Command**

Almost every sūrah closes by turning the reader toward an action: obey, glorify, repent, be patient. This one closes with a statement about God. The Book does that when the argument itself has been the instruction, and this chapter has been an argument from beginning to end: signs, then claims, then objections, then a verdict. The last thing the reader is given is not a task but a description of the one who will judge the task, which returns the reader to where the sūrah started. The chapter's final word is therefore not about human effort at all. It is a name and an attribute, stated in the present tense, holding good whether anyone acknowledges it or not.

**Where Else the Book Uses This Pair**

The pairing of Almighty and All-Wise recurs at the close of other sūrahs and always in the context of God's ownership and rule. One chapter lists it in a passage that ends with the glorification of everything in the heavens and the earth (59:24), and another uses it in a description of the knower of the seen and the unseen (64:18). The formula also appears at the head of the chapters that open with ḥāʾ and mīm, as this sūrah has shown. The sūrah's final sentence is therefore not a private ending. It places the chapter inside a wider pattern of praise that the Book establishes elsewhere, and it lets the reader hear the last words of al-Jāthiyah as a line in a chorus rather than an isolated conclusion.

**The Last Word and the First**

Set the sūrah's last verse beside its first. It began with two letters whose meaning is known to God alone, and it ends with two names whose meaning is the entire subject of the chapter. That movement from an unexplained sign to an acknowledged sovereign is the shape of the argument the reader has been following, and it is also the shape of the response the chapter was asking for. A person who began by accepting what he could not understand has been led to the point where he can say what the verse says: majesty belongs to God in the heavens and the earth, and he is the Almighty, the All-Wise. The sūrah offers nothing further, and it does not need to.
