## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:10

> ˹Other˺ messengers had already been ridiculed before you ˹O Prophet˺, but those who mocked them were overtaken by what they used to ridicule.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Consolation Older Than the Grief**

The Prophet is told that the mockery reaching him is a treatment with precedents. The Book repeats the fact with small changes: no messenger came to a people without being mocked (15:11); the same is recorded of the prophets generally (43:7); and the disbelievers, on seeing the Prophet, only make fun of him (21:36). Earlier in this sūrah God states that He knows how their talk grieves him, and that it is not his honesty being questioned but God's signs that the wrongdoers deny (6:33).

The comfort in the verse is therefore not that mockery is harmless. It is that mockery is expected. It belongs to the work rather than to the worker, and a man who knew that in advance would not treat each jeer as a fresh verdict on himself.

**The Interval Between the Joke and the Answer**

The second half of the verse has a schedule in it. Mocking is answered, but not on the spot. Other messengers were ridiculed before you, and I delayed the disbelievers for a while and then seized them, and how terrible was My punishment (13:32); the end of the wicked is deferred while God's own planning remains flawless (68:45). The delay is what makes mockery feel safe to the mocker.

That interval is also what makes the verse a warning rather than merely a balm. A lull is not an acquittal. Years of undisturbed ridicule are the length of a rope, not the absence of a creditor, and the Book measures that interval only from the far end.

**Overtaken by the Thing They Mocked**

The phrase is exact: they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule (16:34). Not something similar, not something symbolically related — the mocked thing itself becomes the instrument of the reversal. The same formula is used when the consequences of deeds unfold before their eyes, and when the nations that mocked their messengers meet their end (39:48; 45:33). The warning about a Day becomes the Day.

This is the hardest part of the verse to hear and the easiest to state. A joke about the resurrection is a joke about an appointment that has already been fixed, and the appointment does not adjust itself to the mood of the audience. The mocker's confidence borrows time from the very event he is laughing at.

**The Grief in God's Own Report**

One passage interrupts the narrative to grieve over the mockers themselves: Oh pity, such beings! No messenger ever came to them without being mocked (36:30). That is a strange tone for a book that has just been describing their ruin, and it settles who loses the exchange. The mockers win the moment and lose the case, and the loss is reported with sorrow rather than triumph.

Read against that tone, the verse in front of us is not a threat delivered with satisfaction. It is a statement of fact handed to a man who was being laughed at daily, and it treats the mockers as the ones in danger rather than the ones in charge.

**What History Keeps of the Mockers**

The Book repeatedly sends its listeners to look at what history preserved. Similar situations came to pass before you, it says, so travel throughout the land and look at how the deniers ended (3:137); earlier societies were superior in might and were annihilated (47:10). What survives of the men who laughed is the ruin and the record, and nothing of the joke.

The verse therefore makes a claim about endurance. The message that was being mocked outlived its mockers, and the men who composed the jokes are known, if at all, for having made them. The Prophet is invited to judge the present moment by that ledger rather than by the noise in the street.

**Why the Mockers Are Not Silenced**

The verse also explains what God declines to do about the mockery, and the restraint is deliberate. The Prophet is told that his duty is only to remind (88:21), that he is not there to compel belief (50:45), and that if God had willed it every person on earth would have believed, so no one is asked to force the outcome (10:99). Enemies were appointed for every prophet, devilish humans and jinn, whispering to one another elaborate deceptions (6:112).

That structure is the reason the pattern in this verse keeps repeating. If mockery were answered immediately, faith would be a calculation about consequences rather than a choice about truth. Silence is the price of a free test, and God carries the mockery for decades in order to leave the door open. The mockers are not being ignored; they are being allowed to hold a position that only a limited lifetime can defend.

**The Task That Does Not Change**

Nothing in the verse revises the mission. The messenger is not told to answer ridicule with ridicule, to withdraw, or to win the argument by force. He is told that the pattern has run before and that its ending is known, and he is left with the work he had: warn, recite, argue, and continue. Mockery, in this passage, is weather. It is not an instruction about what to do next, and it is not evidence about who is right.

Read as a whole, the sentence does two jobs with one clause. It reassures a man who is being laughed at by people he grew up with, and it warns the people doing the laughing about an appointment they have not yet kept. Nobody who heard it could complain afterwards that he had been ambushed: the pattern was announced in advance, in public, and attached to the same messengers whose stories his audience already knew from the roads and the ruins.
