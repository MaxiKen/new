## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:41

> Believe in My revelations which confirm your Scriptures. Do not be the first to deny them or trade them for a fleeting gain. And be mindful of Me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Confirmation, Stated as the Book's Own Claim**

The verse asks its audience to accept a revelation on the ground that it confirms what they already possess. The claim is made for the Qur'an in several places, and in one of them the mechanism is named: the same angel who brought earlier messages brought this one, *“confirming what came before it”* (2:97). Another passage describes the revelation as a criterion and a light: *“There certainly has come to you from Allah a light and a clear Book”* (5:15).

On the Book's own account, confirmation means two things, and the commentators kept both in view. The revelation agrees with the genuine content of the earlier scriptures, and it fulfils the announcements those scriptures carry about what was to come. Under the second reading the verse becomes a demand rather than a compliment, since a community that reads its own books would be reading a prediction of the very thing it is being invited to accept.

**"Do Not Be the First to Deny"**

The instruction is directed at people who were in a position to lead. The Book's phrasing implies that denial would spread from them to others, and the commentators read the warning as addressing those whose standing made their refusal consequential: scholars, teachers, and the men whose opinion the community followed. A farmer's rejection of a claim affects his own harvest; a teacher's affects everyone he teaches.

The Book's broader treatment of this community makes the same point about responsibility. Elsewhere it says that they were given the Scripture and knowledge, and that their failure would therefore be measured against what they knew (7:169). What the verse adds is a warning about sequence. Whoever is positioned first in a matter carries more of its weight, and the Book tells these readers that their place in the order is part of what they will answer for.

**"Or Trade Them for a Fleeting Gain"**

The second prohibition uses the market language the sūrah has already used for a spiritual loss, where the men who preferred their position to guidance were described as traders whose commerce failed them. The Book states the same charge against this community many times, and one verse names the price exactly: the covenant was taken from those who received the Scripture that they would make it known and not hide it, *“yet they cast it behind their backs and traded it for a fleeting gain. What a miserable profit!”* (3:187).

The profit in such cases is small in reality and large in appearance, which is why the phrase describes it as paltry. What could be bought with the truth about a coming prophet? Standing in a community, an appointment, an income, the approval of men whose opinion carried weight. The verse states the exchange without describing the currency, because the reader who knows the value of the thing sold does not need the price itemised.

**The Word for the Gain, and Its Implication**

The Arabic adjective rendered fleeting describes something slight in quantity and brief in duration. It is used in the Book for the whole of this world's goods when they are compared with what comes after, and it is applied to wealth, comfort and status without exception. The word's double sense — trivial and temporary — makes it a particularly precise instrument for this verse, since the things being purchased with the signs of God share both properties.

The Book's treatment of this language is not asceticism for its own sake. It does not tell its readers that standing, income and respect are worthless; it tells them that these goods have a shelf life and that truth does not. The trade being prohibited is a comparison, and the verse assumes the reader is capable of making it.

**"And Be Mindful of Me"**

The instruction returns to the word that opened the sūrah's description of the people who profit from the Book, and it closes the verse on a note of individual discipline. The other verbs in the sentence concern belief and honesty; this one names the condition that makes both possible over time. A person who guards himself is aware that the buyer in the transaction is not the community around him but the one who sees the exchange.

That closing clause also catches up with the previous verse, where the same command was given its full form: stand in awe of Me alone. The repetition is deliberate. What the sūrah is asking of this audience is that the regard they extend to their own position in society be measured against a loyalty that does not depend on that society, and the vocabulary carries the requirement straight through from one verse to the next.

**What Confirmation Requires of a Reader**

The logic of the verse is cumulative. If the revelation confirms the scriptures, then a person who values those scriptures has a reason to consider it; if he is placed where others will follow his lead, the cost of refusing grows; if the refusal is bought with advantages that will not last, the trade is a bad one; and if God is watching, the transaction is not private. The sentence assembles those four considerations and asks for one act.

The Book states the same case to the same audience again later, in different words. This verse is the compressed version, and it is placed near the beginning of the longest address in the Qur'an to this community, so that the argument is on the record before the history is recounted, the errors are listed, and the invitation is repeated.
