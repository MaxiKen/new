## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:41

> Believe in My revelations which confirm your Scriptures. Do not be the first to deny them or trade them for a fleeting gain. And be mindful of Me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demand That Follows the Favor**

Verse 40 recalled the blessing and the covenant; this verse states the covenant's present content, and it is addressed to scholars. Three imperatives structure it — believe, do not be first in denial, do not sell the signs — and each targets a documented failure mode of the Madinan rabbis, the people who knew the coming prophet's description and debated it in their circles. The commands are not generic piety but a program for a specific crisis: a new revelation has arrived, it claims continuity with theirs, and their response will determine their standing. The verse's force lies in its addressee-awareness. It does not argue these men into belief by evidence alone — evidence will come in the following verses' recital — but commands belief as a covenant obligation already undertaken. They had sworn, the Qur'an records elsewhere, that when a warner came they would be the best-guided of communities (35:42); this verse calls that oath due. The favor remembered in verse 40 is the consideration; belief is the performance.

**Muṣaddiqan: Confirmation With Two Edges**

The Qur'an's self-description here — *muṣaddiqan li-mā maʿakum*, confirming what is with you — carries a double argument the commentators draw out. On one edge, confirmation is attestation: this Book testifies to the truth of the Torah and Gospel in their revealed substance, vouching for books its hearers already hold sacred. On the other edge, confirmation is fulfillment: the new revelation arrives as the thing those books foretold, so that *what is with you* is itself a witness for it — the Messenger himself *"whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel"* (7:157). The second reading converts the verse into an imperative generated by the addressees' own scripture: if your books announce him, your books require you. The two edges cut in the same direction — the old texts are honored *and* mobilized — and the combination forecloses the easiest escape. A rabbi cannot dismiss the Qur'an as foreign without dismissing his own Torah's promise, and cannot keep the promise while refusing the fulfillment. Confirmation, in this verse, is a trapdoor built out of honor.

**“First in Denial”: Priority as Aggravation**

*Wa-lā takūnū awwala kāfirin bih* — and do not be the first to disbelieve in it. The commentators note that "first" here is not merely temporal. Arabic uses priority-language for rank and quality as well as sequence — the Qur'an has its Prophet ﷺ commanded to be *the first of those who submit* (6:14) though prophets preceded him in submission — so the prohibition reads: do not occupy the leadership of denial. The irony the verse sharpens is historical: this community had been announcing the coming prophet to the pagan Arabs for generations, threatening them with him as the tide turned; when he arrived, the announcers were at risk of heading the rejection while the pagans they had warned entered ahead of them. Priority in denial carries a multiplied liability — the denier who leads supplies cover, argument, and precedent to every follower — and the verse names the position so its addressees can recognize they are being offered the opposite: priority in belief, the rank their own scriptures had reserved for them.

**Do Not Sell the Signs for a Paltry Price**

The third imperative returns to the market idiom the sūrah has been building since verse 16: *wa-lā tashtarū bi-āyātī thamanan qalīlan* — do not purchase with My signs a paltry price. The signs are the capital; the price is *qalīl*, little — whatever finite amount the exchange yields. The exegetes specify the Madinan prices: communal position, gifts and tribute from followers, the rabbinic authority that a new prophet would dissolve. The mechanism is named with surgical precision elsewhere: *"when Allah took the covenant of those who were given the Scripture to make it known to people and not hide it, yet they cast it behind their backs and traded it for a fleeting gain. What a miserable profit!"* (3:187). The verse's economics are the inverse of the Qur'an's positive market — the good loan repaid many times over (57:11), the commerce that rescues from a painful punishment (61:10). Selling the signs is not merely sin but bad business at the only scale that counts: the asset sold is infinite in yield, the price is a life's maintenance, and the audit of verse 48 has already announced that no ransom reverses a completed sale.

**“And Be Mindful of Me”: The Exclusive Clause**

The verse closes as verse 40 closed — *wa-iyyāya fa-ttaqūn* — with the pronoun fronted: Me, and Me alone, shall you be mindful of. The exclusivity is aimed at the actual obstacle. These men's hesitation, the surrounding verses suggest, was substantially social: fear of losing standing, of the community's displeasure, of the price their authority would pay for the truth. Taqwā reordered would dissolve the obstacle — where awe of Allah occupies the fronted position, the fear of people loses its veto. The grammar repeats al-Fātiḥah's *iyyāka* construction, the exclusivity of worship rendered as exclusivity of dread, and the repetition is the verse's pastoral core: every sale of the signs begins when something other than Allah becomes more formidable than Allah. The two frames of this passage — verse 40's *stand in awe of Me alone* and verse 41's *be mindful of Me* — hold the commands between them like brackets, telling the addressee that belief, non-denial, and non-selling are not three tasks but one task of right fear, expressed three ways.

**An Invitation Argued in the Addressee's Own Terms**

The verse's method deserves notice as much as its content. It asks the People of the Book for nothing that their own tradition does not already require: their covenant (40), their scriptures' testimony (41's confirming clause), their announced expectation (the firstness prohibition), their scholarly trust (the selling prohibition), their professed awe (the closing clause). Every imperative is an appeal from their own premises to a conclusion their premises entail. This is polemic by internal consistency, and it explains the passage's peculiar courtesy — favor remembered before duty demanded, honor to their books before the demand on their persons. The sūrah's long address to Israel begins, in other words, as invitation rather than indictment, and the indictment that follows — the recital of failures from verse 49 — arrives only after the invitation has been declined on record. The order is itself an argument about how truth addresses power: first the gift, then the term, then the audit, and at every stage the door left open.
