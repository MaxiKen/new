## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:116

> Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Comes Back**

The verse restates what the sūrah said near its beginning: that the wealth and children of those who disbelieve will not benefit them against God, and that such people will live in the Fire. The earlier statement was made before the long argument about the covenant, the House of worship, and the People of the Book; this one is made after it.

A repetition in the Book is a structural signal. The reader who has carried the first sentence through the intervening passage now meets it again with everything he has learned in between, and the second occurrence tells him that the intervening material was an explanation of how the condition arises rather than a change of subject.

**What Lies Between the Two Statements**

The distance between the two sentences is filled with the sūrah's discussion of communities that received revelations, disputed about their ancestors, contracted covenants, and then divided. Some of that material concerned the People of the Book and some concerned the believers, and all of it concerned people who possessed things they trusted.

Read with that in mind, the repeated sentence gains a specific sense. The wealth and children it names are not only money and offspring; the passage has been treating standing, lineage, and scripture as possessions of the same kind. What the verse denies is that any of them interposes between a person and his Lord, and the intervening argument has supplied the evidence for the denial.

**The Delusion the Book Describes**

The Book elsewhere gives the psychology of the condition. One passage describes a person who turns away when the revelations are recited to him, and it explains that he does so because he has been given wealth and children (68:14). Another tells the Prophet not to be impressed by the wealth and children of such people, because God's purpose with those gifts is a trial that ends with their death in disbelief (9:85).

The two passages show that the verses before the reader are describing a settled pattern rather than an isolated complaint. What the sūrah denies, the rest of the Book diagnoses: a person who measures his safety by what he holds, and who mistakes the abundance he was given for an endorsement.

**Why Children Are Named With Wealth**

The two items appear together throughout the Book because they cover both of a person's horizons. Wealth is what he has, and children are what continues him, and together they represent the whole of what he might want to secure.

The Book's verdict on the pair is not a condemnation of either. The same Book describes them as the adornment of the present life (18:46) and states that they are a test in which many people are tried (64:15). What is denied is their use as a defence: a person may love his children and possess his wealth and still answer for his conduct, and the sentence treats the belief that these things can stand in his place as the error that ends badly.

**"Against Allah" and the Meaning of Benefit**

The phrase about benefiting against God sets the terms of the denial. Nothing in the verse says that wealth is useless in the ordinary affairs of life; what it says is that wealth cannot be used against the outcome that the passage has been describing.

That is why the verse is placed here rather than at the beginning of the sūrah. The reader has now seen the ending that the passage announces, and the sentence about benefit is a statement about that ending. The Book's habit is to deny the usefulness of a thing precisely at the point where its usefulness has been trusted, and the sūrah has spent a long passage showing what people trust.

**Residents of the Fire, and the Difference From the Earlier Wording**

The earlier statement described such people as the fuel of the Fire, which named what they become. This verse calls them its residents and states that they will remain there, which names where they live and for how long.

The change of image is deliberate and the two belong together. Fuel describes the person's relation to the fire; residence describes his relation to the place. The Book uses the two descriptions in the same sūrah, and it uses them in the order of a claim and its elaboration, so that the reader meets the more vivid image first and the fuller description second.

**Who Is Described, and How the Sūrah Uses the Category**

The verse speaks of those who disbelieve, and the sūrah has used the category carefully throughout. It has distinguished the People of the Book who rejected the message from those among them who believed, and it has refused to treat a whole community as one thing.

The description here is therefore of a condition, not of a birth. What the Book condemns is the state in which a person dies, and the same Book has just described a group within the very community the passage has been criticizing as upright and among the righteous. A reader who applies the verse to a people rather than to a condition has read past the qualification the sūrah placed two verses before this one.

**What the Repetition Tells the Reader**

The sūrah closes the passage's frame by returning to the sentence it opened with, and the return is a way of stating that a verdict has been established rather than merely announced.

The reader is left with a picture of the argument complete: the condition described, the history examined, the community addressed, the opponents measured, the upright recognized, and the verdict repeated. The verses that follow take up the same two possessions and describe what happens to what those people spend in this world, which is the next step in dismantling the securities the passage has named.
