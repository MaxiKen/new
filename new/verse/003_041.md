## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:41

> Zachariah said, “My Lord! Grant me a sign.” He said, “Your sign is that you will not ˹be able to˺ speak to people for three days except through gestures. Remember your Lord often and glorify ˹Him˺ morning and evening.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sign Asked For After the News, Not Instead of Believing**

Zachariah has just been told that his prayer is answered. His next request is for a sign, and the Book reports it as a request rather than as a doubt. The distinction is preserved in the fuller account, where he asks for a sign and is given one without any suggestion that he has failed a test (19:10).

This is the same request Abraham makes when he asks to be shown how the dead are raised and explains that he asks so that his heart may be reassured (2:260). The Book's treatment of such requests is consistent: a believer may ask for confirmation of what he has been told, and the asking is not treated as unbelief. What distinguishes it from a demand is the posture of the asker and the purpose of the request, which in both accounts is the strengthening of a heart, not the testing of a claim.

**The Sign Itself: Speech Withheld, Health Intact**

The answer describes a personal condition rather than a public miracle. He will not be able to speak to people for three days, and the fuller account specifies that this will happen while he is healthy, using words that rule out illness as the explanation (19:10). The sign, in other words, is a deliberate interruption of a faculty rather than a symptom.

The form of the sign answers the purpose of the request. What Zachariah wanted was assurance that the announcement would come true, and the assurance was given as an experience rather than as a further statement. For three days his own tongue would demonstrate that the ordinary order of his life was in the hands of the one making the promise, which is a more convincing argument to a worried man than a repetition of the promise itself.

**"Except Through Gestures" — the Mode of Communication**

The verse allows one channel of expression, and it is not speech. The Book's fuller narrative shows him coming out to his people and signalling to them to glorify God morning and evening (19:11). A man who had stood before a community in the sanctuary would spend his sign unable to address them, communicating with hands and face.

The detail does two things. It keeps the sign from isolating him completely, since a person who cannot speak can still be understood by those who know him, and it strips his communication of everything except the gestures he can manage. For a priest whose daily work was conducted in words, the arrangement amounts to a fast from language, imposed as evidence of a promise that will be fulfilled.

**"Remember Your Lord Often" — the Command Attached to the Sign**

The instruction that follows is the heart of the verse. During the silence he is told to remember his Lord often, and then to glorify Him morning and evening. The sign does not leave a vacuum; it redirects the faculty it has taken.

The Book treats remembrance as the occupation of a heart that is turning toward God, and it places the command in a wide range of situations: the believers are told to remember Allah often and to glorify Him morning and evening (33:41–42), a command attached in that sūrah to the honour due to the Prophet (48:9). The same pairing of remembrance with praise is given to the Prophet himself as a remedy for the stress of opposition (20:130). In the sign given to Zachariah the instruction is thus not a punishment attached to silence; it is the assignment of a new activity to the hours that speech used to occupy.

**The Fixed Hours of Praise**

The phrase *morning and evening* recurs in the Book as a pair, and its regularity is the point. The Prophet is commanded to praise before sunrise and before sunset, and at the hours of the night, and at the ends of the day (20:130; 50:39–40). The same two ends of the day are named in the instruction to praise and seek forgiveness (40:55), and in the command to remember the name of the Lord morning and evening (76:25).

A day with a fixed practice at each end is a day whose middle is protected. The Book gives this pattern to the Prophet, to the believers, and here to a priest serving a sign, which suggests that it is meant to be the ordinary discipline of a religious life rather than an exceptional measure. Two anchors, one at each end of the light.

**Silence Turned Into Remembrance**

Commentators read the sign and its instruction together as a single arrangement. Zachariah asked for assurance; he was given a condition that made the assurance visible to himself, and along with it a command that filled the days of the condition with worship. What could have been an anxious vigil became a retreat of praise conducted three days long.

The reading turns a punishment-like phenomenon into a gift. A man whose tongue is stopped from conversation has been given time; a man whose community cannot hear him has been told where to direct his voice; and a man who wanted evidence for a promise gets both the evidence and the company of the one who promised. The Book often arranges its signs this way, so that the confirmation of a message is also an opportunity for worship.

**What the Verse Teaches About the Tongue**

A reader coming from the earlier verses will notice what the Book does with speech. The sūrah has emphasised the power of a word — the word of declaration that brings the whole argument of the chapter to a point, the word of witness, the word of a believer's prayer — and now a tongue is taken away for three days as a sign and returned to the work of praise.

The lesson is about what speech is for. A community in which words are traded constantly can treat the faculty as though it belonged to the speaker, and the verse's arrangement suggests otherwise. The tongue that could not say anything to people for three days said everything to God, and the Book records that as the appropriate use of an instrument that was never the speaker's property.
