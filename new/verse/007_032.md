## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:32

> Ask, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Who has forbidden the adornments and lawful provisions Allah has brought forth for His servants?” Say, “They are for the enjoyment of the believers in this worldly life, but they will be exclusively theirs on the Day of Judgment. This is how We make Our revelations clear for people of knowledge.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Put to the Audience**

The verse gives the Prophet the question of who has forbidden the adornments and the lawful provisions that God has brought forth for His servants, and the question is a challenge to the audience's authority to legislate in matters of religion. The Book's arguments about the invented prohibitions appear in the sūrah of the Cattle, where the audience's division of the harvests and the herds is described and answered, and in the sūrah of the Table, where the practices of the earlier communities are corrected. The question in front of us returns the matter to its source, since the audience holds its prohibitions with no warrant from God.

The Book's treatment of the lawful and the forbidden as a divine prerogative runs through the revelation, and the sūrah of the Cattle's list of the forbidden things is introduced as the statement of what God has actually prohibited. The verse in front of us states the principle in the form of a question, and the answer that follows names the beneficiaries of what has been provided.

**The Adornments and the Provisions**

The two things named in the question are the adornments that the passage on clothing has discussed and the provisions that the following verses address. The Book's use of adornment covers clothing, ornament, and the good things of the world that beautify life, and the commentary tradition notes that both the covering of the body and the ornamental use of wealth are included. The provisions are the foods and the goods that God has made lawful.

The verse states that these have been brought forth for the servants of God, and the phrase assigns them to the people who worship Him. The audience's practice had restricted them to particular groups, and the sūrah's answer restores them to those for whom they were provided. The Book's teaching about the goods of the world includes the statement that they are a provision for the creatures and that God has measured them, and the verse in front of us applies the teaching to the audience's rules.

**For the Believers in This World, Exclusively Theirs in the Hereafter**

The answer states that the good things are for the believers in the present life and that they will be exclusively theirs on the day of judgment. Two matters are joined in the clause. The first is that the believers share the world's goods with the deniers in the present life, since the provision has been given to all people in their earthly existence. The second is that the goods of the Hereafter belong to the believers alone, since the reward of God will be given to those who followed His guidance.

The commentary tradition explains the arrangement as a statement about the two abodes. The world's enjoyments are common in this life and restricted in the next, and the Book's descriptions of the Hereafter include the garden that is prepared for those who believed and acted rightly. The sūrah's later passages describe the gardens and the fire in the dialogue that occupies its middle section.

**The Form of the Answer**

The answer begins with a command to say, and the Prophet is addressed as the one who delivers the reply. The Book's use of the command to say in its arguments gives the Prophet a defined response to the audience's claims, and the sūrah of the Cattle uses the same form in its answers about the offerings and the crops. The verse in front of us supplies the reply to the question of who forbade the good things, and it states the destination of what has been provided.

The reply also implies the destruction of the audience's system of prohibition. If the good things were brought forth for the servants of God, then the claim to forbid them is a claim to overturn what God has provided, and the Book's warnings about fabricating lies against God address exactly that. The verse in front of us closes the passage on clothing and food with the statement of the divine provision.

**The Book's Teaching about the Goods of the World**

The Book's treatment of the good things of the world includes warnings against their excessive love alongside affirmations of their lawfulness, and the verse in front of us belongs to the side of affirmation. The sūrah of the Cave states that wealth and children are an adornment of the present life while the lasting good deeds are better with God, and the sūrah of the Iron states that people should not forget their share of the world. The verse in front of us states the provision's destination and leaves the manner of its use to the commands that the sūrah has given.

The audience that heard the verse lived in a society of markets and caravans, and the goods of the world were part of its daily concern. The verse affirms them as a provision and warns against the practices that restricted them, and the sūrah then states the things that God has actually forbidden.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands thirty-second in the chapter, and it follows the commands about dress and food and precedes the list of the prohibited things. The three verses together form the sūrah's treatment of the audience's dietary and ritual rules, and the verse in front of us supplies the principle that the next verse states in the form of a list.

The reader who has been told to wear his adornment and to eat without excess is now told that the goods have been provided for him and that no one has the authority to forbid what God has permitted. The sūrah then names what God has forbidden, and the list is short.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse gives the Prophet the statement that his Lord has forbidden only open and secret indecencies, sin, unjust aggression, the association of partners with God, and the attribution to God of what people do not know (7:33). The question about the good things in front of us is followed by the enumeration of the actual prohibitions, and the passage then states the term appointed for every community.

The two verses together set the two lists side by side: what God has provided and what God has forbidden. The reader is given the boundaries in their true extent, and the practices that the audience had invented are left outside them.
