## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:194

> ˹There will be retaliation in˺ a sacred month for ˹an offence in˺ a sacred month, and all violations will bring about retaliation. So, if anyone attacks you, retaliate in the same manner. ˹But˺ be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is with those mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ledger of the Sacred Month**

The verse closes the war-legislation by settling a case the treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah had left unresolved. In the sixth year of the Hijrah the Muslims had set out unarmed for the ʿumrah, been turned back at Ḥudaybiyyah, and accepted terms that barred them from the Sacred Mosque in a sacred month — while the very month's inviolability had been among the reasons some Companions feared returning: if the Meccans attacked, could the believers fight back inside the sacred precinct and the sacred time? The verse answers with a ledger formula: the sacred month for the sacred month, and the sacred things carry retaliation. The commentators explain the first clause historically — the Muslims turned back in one Dhū'l-Qaʿdah returned the following year, in the seventh, to perform the ʿumrah in the same month — and the second clause theologically: the rites restored in that return were themselves a compensation from God for the rites the previous year had cost them. The sanctity of the month is thus not repealed by this verse but armed: its inviolability stands, and it stands against violators too, so that holiness cannot be used as a shield for aggression.

**The Sacred Things Named**

The plural — the sacred things, or inviolable things — was enumerated by the exegetes: the sacred month, the sacred land of Mecca, and the iḥrām, the ritual state of the pilgrim. Al-Rāzī records the sharper occasion behind the ruling: when the Meccans learned that the Muslims were forbidden to fight during the sacred months, they calculated that this restraint could be exploited — that Muslim quiescence in the holy time was a vulnerability to be attacked. The verse answers the calculation directly: if blood is shed against a Muslim in the sacred month, the shedder's own blood becomes liable in the sacred month; the inviolability of forbidden things is to be observed, but not to the point of self-destruction. The principle generalizes beyond the month: every sanctity God has established — the sanctuary, the pilgrim's state, the truce — is protected *and* protective, and anyone who weaponizes a sanctity against its own keepers forfeits the cover it gave him. The sacred month remains sacred; what changes is who may hide behind it.

**Transgression as Measured Response**

The verse's middle clause commands retaliation in the same manner as the attack, and its wording is the exegetes' careful study: the verb used is the same root as transgression — *iʿtadā* — yet the response it commands is not seen as illegitimate, as the verb ordinarily implies. Al-Ṭabarī explains that the "transgression" ordered here is a legitimate answer to aggression, and could rightly be rendered "attack him in the like manner that he attacked you." The likeness is the law: the response is measured against the injury, equal in kind and degree, never exceeding it. The Qur'an states the same standard twice more — if you retaliate, retaliate with the like of what was done to you, though patience is better (16:126); the recompense of an evil deed is its equivalent, while pardon and reconciliation carry their reward with God (42:40). Read with those texts, this clause is the war-law's proportionality rule in its plainest form: the believer may answer blow for blow, expulsion for expulsion, month for month — and the answer's legitimacy lives entirely in its likeness. Excess converts the responder into the transgressor the previous verse named unloved by God.

**God Is With the Mindful**

The verse ends where the whole passage began, with taqwā — but now with a promise attached: be mindful of God, and know that God is with the mindful. The companionship-clause is the war-legislation's deepest assurance. A community told to fight only those who fight it, to answer only in like manner, to hold the sanctuary inviolable even under attack, and to keep the sacred months sacred even when the enemy exploits them — such a community is, by every worldly calculation, at a disadvantage against an opponent unbound by these limits. The clause answers the disadvantage with presence: the withness of God — the aid-giving maʿiyyah the tradition distinguishes from His general withness of knowledge — belongs to the ones the limits bind. The knowledge commanded is strategic and spiritual at once: know that the restraint is not weakness but alliance, that the measured response fights with a help the unmeasured response forfeits. The verse's final word thus reverses the Meccans' calculation of 2:194's occasion: the sacred month's inviolability is not the Muslims' vulnerability but their reinforcement, because God stands with those who keep it under provocation.

**The Fence Around the Fence**

Structurally, the verse fences the fence the war-code opened. 2:190 permitted fighting against the fighters and forbade exceeding; 2:191 extended the permission to the field and withdrew it at the sanctuary unless the sanctuary was attacked; 2:192–193 ended the enmity at the cessation and named persecution as the only object. This verse handles the case those rules leave hardest: the enemy who attacks inside the protected times and places themselves — the month, the precinct, the pilgrim-state — where the believer's own scruple might paralyze him. The answer keeps both truths intact: the protections stand (retaliation happens *in* a sacred month only *for* an offense in a sacred month — the sanctity is the occasion, never the casualty), and the believer is not left defenseless (all violations carry retaliation; the attacked may answer in kind). The verse thereby completes a code in which nothing is unprotected and nothing is unprotected-able: sanctity without defense becomes bait, and defense without sanctity becomes the transgression the code forbids. Between the two, the measured retaliation in the sacred month is the narrow path the legislation walks.

**The Sacred Things in the Community's Memory**

The verse's ruling outlived its occasion and became the community's standing grammar of holy time. The four sacred months — Dhū'l-Qaʿdah, Dhū'l-Ḥijjah, Muḥarram, and Rajab — kept their inviolability in Islamic law, and the sūrah itself legislates the gravity of fighting within them: fighting in the sacred month is great, though barring people from God's path and expelling the Mosque's people is greater (2:217). The pilgrimage-legislation that follows immediately (2:196–203) then fills those same months with their positive content: the ʿumrah performed in Dhū'l-Qaʿdah that the verse's ledger commemorated became the pattern of the Prophet's own pilgrimages, all performed in the sacred time. Read forward, the verse is the hinge between the war-code and the hajj-code: the months in which blood may not be shed are the months in which the rites are kept, and the retaliation-for-violations clause exists precisely so that the rites' keepers are not prey. The sacred month for the sacred month is, in the end, the community's calendar defended — its holy time made inviolable not by the enemy's goodwill but by a law that answers every violation in kind, under the eyes of the God who is with the mindful.
