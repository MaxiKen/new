## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:237

> And if you divorce them before consummating the marriage but after deciding on a dowry, pay half of the dowry, unless the wife graciously waives it or the husband graciously pays in full. Graciousness is closer to righteousness. And do not forget kindness among yourselves. Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Priced but Unconsummated Marriage**

The preceding verse handled the marriage with no settled dowry; this one handles its sibling case — contracted, priced, and dissolved before consummation. The arithmetic is exact: *pay half of the dowry*. The named price stands as the measure of the parties' original expectation, and the halving splits the loss at the point where the marriage stopped. The rule has an integrity the jurists noticed: the full bridewealth is owed where the marriage was entered — the price of the shared life actually begun — and nothing beyond the scaled compensation is owed where no price was ever set; the half sits precisely between, honoring the contract's seriousness while discounting its unfulfilled half. No waiting period attaches, since nothing was consummated; the divorce is immediate, the settlement is arithmetic, and the verse then does something the arithmetic alone would not do — it opens two doors out of the arithmetic.

**The Two Waivers**

*Unless the wife graciously waives it or the husband graciously pays in full.* The half-dowry is a default, not a cage, and the two exceptions are assigned one to each party: she may release her claim to the half, or he may surrender his right to keep it. The symmetry is deliberate and rare — the code that elsewhere allocates initiative asymmetrically (the reversal-right of 2:228, the khulʿ-ransom of 2:229) here gives each spouse one act of pure generosity, identical in structure and opposite in direction. The exegetes note what the doubling accomplishes: the settlement's floor is the half, its ceiling is the whole, and the space between is governed by nothing but the parties' grace. A divorce that could end in ledger-balancing is made capable of ending in a gift — from either side — and the verse's next clause ranks the gift above the ledger.

**The Hand That Holds the Knot**

The identity of the second waiver's agent produced one of the code's most discussed phrases. The Arabic phrase — the one whose hand holds the marriage-knot — was read by the dominant interpretation as the husband himself: he who holds the knot's power, the ṭalāq-prerogative legislated at 2:228, is the one positioned to forgo the half he could legitimately reclaim. The alternative reading identified the holder as the wife's marriage guardian, the wakīl whose consent a woman's marriage requires — in which case the guardian could release, with her approval, the half she had been entitled to keep. The exegetes record the difficulty with this position directly: the guardian is necessary to the contract but has no legal standing over the bridewealth, which is the wife's personal property; a man cannot donate what is not his. The majority reading therefore prevailed — and it prevailed by the code's own logic, since the hand that holds the knot is the hand the code gave the knot to.

**Nearer to Reverence**

*Graciousness is closer to righteousness* — the verse's pivotal sentence, and its grammar has been parsed with care. The forgoing, whichever party performs it, is aqrab li'l-taqwā: nearer to God-consciousness than the exacting of the half. The commentators draw out the comparison the sentence implies: the half-dowry is justice, and justice is not blamed — but the relinquishing of a right stands closer to the taqwā that the whole code has been invoking at its thresholds. The clause also resolves a latent tension in the exceptions: if waiving is merely permitted, the default arithmetic could be pressed without moral loss; by ranking the waiver as nearer to reverence, the verse converts the permitted into the praised and installs a gradient where the law had placed a line. This is the code's characteristic final move — the obligation fixed, the excellence pointed at — stated here in six words.

**Do Not Forget Bounteousness**

The next sentence widens the aperture: *And do not forget kindness among yourselves* — al-faḍl, the surplus, the going-beyond. The exegetes connect the two clauses: the party who forgoes his half realizes precisely this bounteousness, and the forgetting the verse warns against is the forgetting of the relationship's history in the moment of its accounting. The divorce settlement is the point at which two people are most tempted to see each other as claimants, and the verse interrupts that framing with a memory-instruction — do not forget what passed between you, and let the passing show in the numbers. Read with the preceding clause, the sentence completes a small ethic of dissolution: exact your right if you must, since the right is legislated; but the nearer path, the remembered path, the path the verse keeps pointing at, is the released half. The code ends its divorce legislation the way it began its spending legislation — with the surplus as the standard and the minimum as the mercy.

**Seclusion and the Full Dowry**

The jurists pressed one further question out of the verse's architecture: is consummation the only trigger for the full dowry? An opinion recorded by Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī holds that it is not — where the husband was secluded with his wife in circumstances making consummation possible, the full bridewealth becomes due even without it. The reasoning treats privacy itself as the marriage's activation: the seclusion is what the contract purchased, and its occurrence obliges the price. The opinion matters beyond its case, because it shows the halving-rule functioning as the jurists read the whole code to function — not as letter to be gamed but as a map of the marriage's realities. A man cannot dissolve a marriage at its threshold, after the threshold-crossing has occurred in every sense but the last, and claim the discount of a contract never begun. The half belongs to the marriage that truly never started; the seclusion opinion polices the word truly.

**The All-Seeing**

The verse closes with the name the nursing-verse closed with (2:233): *Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do*. The placement is exact to the legislation. The acts this verse governs — the waiver, the full payment, the remembered bounteousness, the forgotten — are mostly invisible transactions: no court witnesses the moment a husband decides to keep the half or release it, and no register records the spirit in which a settlement was paid. The All-Seeing name stations the code's final auditor over its quietest clauses, and it does double duty: for the generous, the unseen grace is seen; for the exacting, the unseen hardening is seen. Between the two the verse leaves its addressee with the choice it has been building toward — the arithmetic half, or the gracious whole — and with the knowledge that whichever is chosen, chosen is exactly the word: the seeing covers the deed and the deliberation alike. The code's domestic legislation ends where it began, under surveillance that is also an invitation.
