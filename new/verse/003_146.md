## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:146

> ˹Imagine˺ how many devotees fought along with their prophets and never faltered despite whatever ˹losses˺ they suffered in the cause of Allah, nor did they weaken or give in! Allah loves those who persevere.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Measures the Community's Case**

The verse opens with the Book's idiom for abundance, asking how many there have been of a certain kind. The construction is not a request for a number; it is a way of saying that the instance before the reader is one of many.

The community at Madinah had just suffered a defeat, and the verse places that experience within a long record. The Book's habit of introducing earlier communities in this way serves a consistent purpose: the listeners are shown that their situation is not unprecedented and that the conduct expected of them has been expected of others before them.

**The Two Readings of the Verb**

The verb in the opening clause can be read as referring to fighting, or as referring to being killed. On the second reading the sentence says that many devoted men stood alongside a prophet who was killed, which is why al-Ṭabarī preferred it: the reading fits the plain sense of the preceding verses about the Prophet's mortality.

Both readings support the same lesson. Whether the prophet fought and his companions fought with him, or the prophet was killed and his companions held their ground, the sentence presents people who continued in their duty while the outcome was in doubt. The difference between the readings concerns which aspect of the earlier history is being held up, and the community's own recent experience answers to both.

**The Word for the Devoted Men**

The noun describing the companions of the prophets has been given several explanations. Some connected it with the word translated as sages in an earlier verse of this sūrah, so that the men are described by their knowledge and their devotion. Others took it as a reference to large groups, and one report gives ten thousand as the number that the singular form of the word denotes.

The variety of explanations reflects the word's rarity, and the sense that emerges from all of them is a body of people marked by seriousness and endurance rather than by any particular office. The verse places such people beside the prophets as the human material of a mission that has always required them.

**Three Things They Did Not Do**

The verse names three responses that the men did not have: they did not falter because of what happened to them, they did not weaken, and they did not demean themselves. The three words describe a single moral posture from different angles.

The first concerns the initial reaction to a shock, the second concerns the exhaustion that follows continued hardship, and the third concerns the submission to humiliation that comes when a person believes the cause has failed. The Book's descriptions of steadfastness usually concentrate on one of these, and this verse covers all three, which is why it is frequently quoted where a community is recovering from a loss.

**What Befell Them in the Way of God**

The phrase about what befell them in the way of God covers injury, loss of property, and death, and it describes these things as taking place within the activities of the religion rather than outside them. The Book uses the phrase to place the cost of a struggle inside the definition of the struggle itself.

The community at Uḥud had lost men who were dear to it, and the sūrah does not present those losses as a sign that the community had been abandoned. It presents them as what befell people in the way of God, which is exactly what the verse says the earlier companions of the prophets endured without faltering.

**The Statement About God's Love**

The verse ends with the declaration that God loves the patient, and the quality named is the one that the three preceding clauses have described in action. The sentence gives the behaviour of those men its meaning, and the same declaration appears in the Book in several places.

The Book's attachment of God's love to particular qualities is one of its ways of teaching its values. Patience, generosity, justice, and trust are each said to attract that love, and the verse before the reader places the endurance of a community under attack in that category. What a community experiences as loss is described here as the occasion of a love that no loss can remove.

**The Verse's Work in the Sūrah's Argument**

The verses immediately before this one asked the community what it had expected and reminded it of what it had asked for. This verse supplies the example that answers both questions: the pattern is not a community that avoids suffering but a community that withstands it.

The Book's method here is to argue from history to the present. The community is told that its position is ancient, that the men who held it are beyond counting, and that the conduct required of it has a record behind it. That combination is more useful to people in the aftermath of a defeat than reassurance alone would be.

**A Model of Steadiness Under Loss**

The verse has been recited by Muslim communities in every generation for the picture it draws of a body of people standing where they are needed while the outcome is unknown. Its subject is not the leaders of the mission but the ordinary people who carry it.

The sūrah presents those people as the reason the mission survives its crises. The prophets deliver the message; the devoted men absorb the losses, hold their positions, and continue. The community that reads this verse is being told which of the two roles its own losses prepare it to fill, and the next verse reports the prayer such people made.

**The Construction That Sets Present Losses Inside a Pattern**

The verse opens with a question about how many, and the same figure of speech carries encouragement elsewhere in the Book. When the companions of Saul looked at the army of Goliath and lost heart, the ones who were certain of meeting God answered that a small force has often overcome a great one by God's will, and the verse closes by naming God's company with the steadfast (2:249).

The construction in this sentence does the same work for a community that had just been defeated. It does not argue that the losses were small or that the enemy was weak. It places the present event inside a series of events that have happened before, in which men fought beside their prophets, suffered, and did not waver. Al-Ṭabarī preferred the reading on which the sentence says that prophets were killed while devoted men stood beside them, which fits the sequence of the passage, since the verses before it have been speaking about death and the verses after it report the prayer of the steadfast.
