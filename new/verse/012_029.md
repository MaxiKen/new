## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:29

> O Joseph! Forget about this. And you ˹O wife˺! Seek forgiveness for your sin. It certainly has been your fault.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Instructions in Two Registers**

The verse contains a pair of commands, and the difference between them is the verse's content. To Joseph: turn away from this, let it drop, do not speak of it. To the wife: seek forgiveness for your sin, for you are among those at fault. One instruction concerns information; the other concerns guilt. Joseph, who has committed nothing, is told what to do with his knowledge. His accuser, who has committed both an indecency and a false accusation, is told what to do with her conscience. Neither is told what to do about the other. The household has been rebalanced by assigning silence to the injured party and private repentance to the injurer, and the verse states the arrangement without commentary.

**"Turn Away From This" Means Do Not Repeat It**

The commentators are explicit about the sense: Joseph is to conceal the affair and not mention it to anyone, so that it does not become public and generate speculation. The instruction is not to forgive — that is not asked of him — but to stop the story travelling. It is the ordinary reflex of a household with standing, and it would have been intelligible to any first audience: a slave in a minister's house has no interest in publishing what happened there, and the master is telling him so. The instruction also assumes, correctly, that Joseph has the option. He is the only person besides the couple who knows the whole sequence, and his silence would have been sufficient. What the verse cannot foresee is that the woman's own conduct has already put the story beyond anyone's control.

**The Sin Is Named**

*Istaghfirī li-dhanbik* — seek forgiveness for your sin. The word *dhanb* is unambiguous, and its use here matters because it is spoken by the husband. The household's own head has classified the act as a sin, in her hearing, in Joseph's. That classification is never withdrawn anywhere in the sūrah, and it is never made public either. The result is a private admission and a public silence, which is the exact structure that will fail in the next verse. It is also why Joseph's imprisonment in verse 35 is so difficult to justify even inside the story: the man who decided it had already used the word *sin* about the accuser and told the accused to say nothing.

**"You Are Among Those at Fault"**

The closing phrase, *innaki kunti min al-khāṭiʾīn*, places her inside a class: you were one of the wrongdoers, of those who miss the mark. The formulation is soft in one respect and hard in another. Soft, because a class membership can be left and because the verse stops short of specifying the act. Hard, because the perfect tense fixes the fact — you *were* among them, this has already happened and cannot be un-done. The Qur'an uses the same noun elsewhere for a category of people distinguished by a settled disposition rather than a single lapse. The husband's sentence therefore records a finding of fault without imposing any consequence for it, which is a distinction the rest of the chapter will not respect.

**No Punishment, No Restitution, No Vindication**

The verse is remarkable for what it omits. Nothing is done to her. Nothing is given to him. Joseph is not freed, not compensated, not moved to another household, not cleared before the servants. He is told to forget. The woman is told to ask God's pardon, which costs her nothing visible and changes nothing in her position. Both instructions are addressed to the interior life — his memory and her conscience — while the external arrangements remain exactly as they were. That is a settlement in the strict sense: the dispute is closed without the situation being altered. The chapter's argument is that such settlements do not hold, because the facts that produced the dispute are still in place, and Joseph is still a young slave living in the house of a woman who has just been found at fault.

**Why the Settlement Collapses Immediately**

Verse 30 opens with the women of the city already talking. The instruction to conceal has failed before the next verse begins, and the sūrah does not explain how. The commentators note that the news spread despite the command, and the reason is not difficult to supply: a banquet with locked doors, a torn garment, a summoned witness and a raised voice in a minister's house are not events that stay inside a house. The collapse of verse 29 is the chapter's demonstration that reputation cannot be managed by instruction. What the husband tried to settle by telling two people what to do is settled instead by what the city decides to say, and the saying will force the woman into a public performance in verse 31 that she cannot afterwards withdraw.

**The Man Addressed First Is the One Who Did Nothing**

The order of the two commands is the verse's final observation. Joseph is spoken to first, and what he is asked for is the harder thing. Keeping silent about an injustice done to you, in the house of the person who did it, with no prospect of redress, is a discipline the Qur'an elsewhere associates with the prophets and calls beautiful patience. The woman is asked only for an inward act she can perform in private. The imbalance is not corrected in this verse; it is corrected much later, when Joseph's silence ends — not with an accusation but with a request that the king examine the case publicly (12:50). He does exactly what he was told not to do, at the moment when doing it serves the truth rather than himself, and the sūrah treats that delay as part of his character rather than a failure of it.
