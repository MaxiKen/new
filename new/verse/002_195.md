## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:195

> Spend in the cause of Allah and do not let your own hands throw you into destruction ˹by withholding˺. And do good, for Allah certainly loves the good-doers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Spend**

The verse commands the community to spend in the way of God and forbids the withholding that the sentence describes as casting oneself into ruin. The sentence follows the passage on fighting, and the community's scholars read it as an instruction about the support of the struggle they had been commanded to undertake.

The Book's pairing of the command to spend with the command to fight appears in several chapters, and the two duties belong to the same period of the community's life. What the reader receives is a statement that the conduct of the community's affairs depends on the willingness of its members to part with what they possess.

**What It Means to Cast Oneself Into Ruin**

The commentators offered several explanations of the phrase. The most direct is that a person who withholds what he is able to give brings about his own loss, since the failure to provide for the community's needs produces the collapse that the community was warned against. Others connected the phrase with the person who gives up the struggle for the sake of his family and his wealth.

The readings converge on the idea that ruin comes from an act of the person rather than from an external cause, and the sentence states the matter in those terms. What the reader learns is that the withholding which looks like prudence is described in the Book as self-destruction, and the community's teachers used the sentence when they encouraged generosity.

**The Report About Abū Ayyūb**

The commentaries preserve a report about a companion who heard the verse used incorrectly. When a man charged the Byzantine lines and was killed, some of those present cited the phrase about casting oneself into ruin, and Abū Ayyūb al-Anṣārī corrected them, explaining that the verse had been revealed about a group of companions who had wanted to settle with their property rather than continue in the struggle.

The report shows how the community's teachers guarded the verse's meaning against a reading that would forbid courage. What the reader receives is the account of an interpretation that was corrected in the community's own lifetime, and the community's scholars used it when they explained the difference between recklessness and the risk that the religion requires.

**The Interpretation About Despair**

Another reading applies the sentence to a person who has committed a sin and concluded that his case is hopeless, so that he abandons the effort to amend his life. The community's teachers described the condition as moral despair, and they read the phrase about ruin as a warning against it.

The Book's treatment of repentance elsewhere in the sūrah supplies the answer to that condition, since the verses that follow the account of the concealers name the terms on which a person is restored. The reader carries away the assurance that the door remains open, and the sentence's prohibition applies to the assumption that it has closed.

**The Command to Do Good**

The sentence closes with the command to do good and the statement that God loves those who do good, and the Book attaches the same promise to conduct in several passages. The community's scholars read the ending as the positive counterpart of the prohibition that preceded it.

The Book's vocabulary for goodness covers acts that go beyond the required, and the sentence places the voluntary alongside the obligatory in a single instruction. The community's support of its affairs is measured by a standard that exceeds the minimum, and the sentence closes with a description of those whom God loves.

**What the Verse Adds to the Passage**

The sentence adds the disposition of property to the subjects the passage has been treating, and it connects the community's resources with its responsibilities. The Book has dealt with the conduct of conflict and now addresses the means by which the community's life is sustained, and the reader is shown that the two matters belong to one body of instruction.

The passage that follows turns to the pilgrimage, where the spending of wealth appears again in the offerings and the provisions that the journey requires. The reader is carried from the general command to the particular forms in which the community's property is used in worship, and the sentence's place at the head of that sequence is instructive.

**The Style of the Command**
