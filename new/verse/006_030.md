## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:30

> But if only you could see when they will be detained before their Lord! He will ask ˹them˺, “Is this ˹Hereafter˺ not the truth?” They will cry, “Absolutely, by our Lord!” He will say, “Then taste the punishment for your disbelief.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Crowd, A Different Judge**

The verse opens with the formula used a few lines earlier when the wrongdoers were held before the Fire, and the scene is recognisably the same: people detained, no movement, and a question that will be answered. What has changed is the one asking. Earlier they were held before the punishment; here they are held before their Lord, and the question arrives from the authority they had spent their lives avoiding.

The crowd's own answer in the previous scene was a promise to behave differently if released. The answer now demanded is a straightforward acknowledgment, and the two responses are placed close together so the reader can measure one against the other. The men who promised reform in front of a fire are asked for a simple admission in front of the Lord they denied, and they give it.

**A Question the Answer Cannot Help**

Is this Hereafter not the truth? The question is not an examination, since the reality of what they see is not in dispute. It is the last opportunity to say out loud what their whole life was spent rejecting, and they take it. They say, by our Lord, that it is true. The confession is complete and correct, and it changes nothing, which is the point the Book wants its readers to feel.

The same exchange is reported in the sūrah of the Sand-Dunes with almost identical words: the disbelievers are exposed to the Fire and asked whether this is not the truth, they swear by their Lord that it is, and they are told to taste the punishment for their disbelief (46:34). Two sūrahs record the same script because the scene is fixed for everyone who arrives at it, whatever century they came from.

**The Confession That Comes Too Late**

The Book draws a firm line between believing because the evidence has been weighed and confessing because the evidence has become unavoidable. When Pharaoh's faith arrived as he was drowning, he was told that it came after a lifetime of disobedience and corruption (10:90–91). The same rule is stated for the disbelievers who cry out at the sight of the punishment that they now believe in God alone: their faith was of no benefit when they saw the torment, and that has always been the way God deals with His wicked servants (40:84–85).

Nothing about this is arbitrary. Faith at the sight of the punishment is not a decision about God; it is an adjustment to circumstances. The test in this world was designed for people who had to choose without seeing, and a confession wrung out of a person by force is not the thing the test was measuring.

**Tasting as a Form of Instruction**

The command to taste the punishment is used throughout the Book, and the verb is chosen for what it conveys about knowledge. Tasting is how a person learns what a thing is by experience, and the Book applies it to the consequences of deeds in more than two dozen places. Earlier in the same sūrah, the wrongdoers at the point of death are told they will be repaid with the torment of disgrace for telling lies about God and for being arrogant towards His signs (6:93).

The irony is deliberate. These were people who demanded signs, disputed evidence, and treated the warnings as fables, and their final lesson is delivered by experience rather than by argument. What they refused to know by report they are made to know by contact.

**The Verse Behind the Verse**

Just before this passage the sūrah states that even if the angels had been sent down, the dead had spoken, and every sign the disbelievers demanded had been assembled before their eyes, they would still not have believed unless God so willed (6:111). The verse in front of us is that sentence carried to its conclusion. Every proof they asked for is now visible, and it produces a confession with no value.

The Book does not present this as a puzzle about God's will. It presents it as a fact about what kind of thing belief is. Belief that arrives after the outcome is known is no longer belief, and a confession that is simply the accurate report of what a person sees is no longer a confession. The sūrah will go on to describe the loss of those who denied the meeting with God, and this scene explains the shape of that loss.

**What the Question Asks the Reader to Admit**

A person reading the passage in a market or a courtyard in Makkah had not yet reached the point where the truth could not be denied, which is exactly why the passage was revealed then. The question in the verse, addressed to a crowd that can no longer benefit from answering it, is aimed over their heads at everyone who still can. The Book elsewhere states the same choice plainly when it reports the exchange between the residents of Paradise and the residents of the Fire, where both sides confirm that their Lord's promise proved true and the wrongdoers are then declared cursed (7:44).

The verse ends without comment on the crowd's fate, because what needed saying has been said. The acknowledgment is on record, the punishment has been ordered, and the sūrah turns next to a description of the people who insisted this day would never come.
