## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:29

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah. For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Command to Speak**

The verse begins with the same imperative as verse 26: *say*. Once more, a statement is placed in the Prophet's mouth and directed at the community, and once more the subject is a fact about God rather than a rule about behaviour.

The placement is deliberate. The previous verse handled an external act — taking another community as protector — and this one reaches behind it. A believer might reason that an alliance formed in private, an intention nursed but not acted on, or a loyalty changing hands quietly is a matter between himself and his conscience. The verse removes the space in which that reasoning operates.

**Concealment and Disclosure Named Together**

The sentence names both possibilities and treats them equally: what is hidden in the heart and what is brought into the open are equally known. The pairing is a technique the Book uses often, and it prevents a reader from concluding that the verse is only about hypocrisy. It covers the man who hides a betrayal and the man who announces one.

More importantly, the two categories are not presented as morally identical, since one may be worse than the other. What the verse establishes is epistemic rather than ethical: the distinction between secret and public, which governs so much of human accountability, does not exist in relation to God. Both are simply facts He knows.

**"It Is Known to Allah" — the Basis of the Sentence**

The claim that follows gives the reason: God knows whatever is in the heavens and on the earth. The Book states the same knowledge about the contents of hearts in verse after verse. *“And your Lord knows what their hearts conceal and what they reveal.”* (28:69). The same knowledge is described where it matters most, in a warning about mutual resentment among believers: *“When they meet you they say, ‘We believe.’ But when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage.”* (3:119). And it is made the ground of a covenant: *“And be mindful of Allah. Surely Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart.”* (5:7).

The repetition of the theme across the Book gives this verse its weight. The knowledge being asserted is not an inference from behaviour; it is direct acquaintance with what a person is, which means that self-presentation, however well managed, addresses an audience that does not include the one who matters.

**What the Verse Does in Its Context**

Read between the instruction about allegiance and the warning about the Day that follows, this sentence performs a specific service. It removes the most attractive option available to a frightened minority: keeping its standing in two camps at once.

The temptation was rational. A believer who maintained quiet connections with a powerful community would have protection if the Muslim community failed, and standing among the believers if it succeeded. The verse closes that door without argument, on the basis that the arrangement is already visible. It leaves the person addressed with a choice rather than a strategy.

**The Mustard Seed Hidden in a Rock**

Elsewhere the Book supplies an image for the reach of this knowledge that makes concealment look almost absurd. Luqmān tells his son that even a deed the weight of a mustard seed, hidden in a rock or in the heavens or the earth, will be brought out by God, who is Most Subtle and All-Aware (31:16).

A mustard seed inside a rock is not merely well hidden; it is a figure for something that has no chance of being found by any search. The Book uses it for the smallest human act, which tells the reader that the question raised in this verse is not confined to large betrayals. The knowledge in question notices the small things too, and the Allah who knows what hearts conceal is defined by that subtlety rather than by surveillance.

**"Most Capable of Everything" — the Attribute That Keeps Recurring**

The verse closes with the attribute *qadīr*, which the sūrah has now used at 3:26 and 3:29 and will use again at 3:189. Its presence at the end of a statement about knowledge is important: knowing and being able to act on what is known are different things.

A being who knew everything but could do nothing would be a witness rather than a judge; a being who could do everything but knew nothing would be a force. The Book closes its knowledge statements with capacity because the two belong together in any adequate account of accountability, and because the humans reciting the verse need to be told that what is known about them will be dealt with by someone who can act.

**Discretion Is Not Deceit**

A reader might conclude from this verse that all concealment is wrongdoing, and the sūrah does not support that reading. Two verses earlier, a believer under persecution was permitted to hide his faith, and the Book's own narratives contain people who kept their belief private in hostile courts for years.

The distinction the Book maintains is between hiding a fact and hiding a loyalty. A person may conceal what would be used against him; what he may not do is maintain an inward allegiance that contradicts the outward one while counting on the secrecy. The verse concerns the contents of the heart, and its point is that the contents are already public in the only forum that will ultimately matter.

**Why Knowledge and Power Stand Together at the End**

The two claims in this verse — that God knows the hidden, and that He is capable of everything — are the two halves of the doctrine of accountability, and the sūrah states them as one sentence for a reason. A person can live comfortably with the thought that God knows, so long as he supposes that knowing makes no difference; and he can live with the thought that God is powerful, so long as he supposes that God is uninformed about the details of his case.

Both suppositions are removed here. What the reader is told is that nothing in him is unobserved and that the one observing is not a spectator. Every use a person makes of secrecy — to preserve a position, to protect a standing, to keep an option open — rests on those suppositions, and the verse takes them away without raising its voice.
