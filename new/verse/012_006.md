## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:6

> And so will your Lord choose you ˹O Joseph˺, and teach you the interpretation of dreams, and perfect His favour upon you and the descendants of Jacob—˹just˺ as He once perfected it upon your forefathers, Abraham and Isaac. Surely your Lord is All-Knowing, AllWise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Choosing Follows the Vision**

The verb *yajtabīka*, will choose you, describes a selection made for a task rather than a preference felt toward a person. Jacob's reasoning runs from what has already happened to what must follow: the God who selected this boy for a vision of that rank will select him for work of a corresponding rank. Several commentators take the choosing to name prophethood outright, since the sūrah's later verses show Joseph receiving revelation, interpreting dreams as a commissioned function and calling his fellow prisoners to the worship of one God. Others keep it broader and read it as appointment to great affairs, of which prophethood is the highest instance. The broader reading has an advantage here, because the sentence pairs choosing with teaching. What follows is a curriculum — selection, then instruction, then completion of favour — and the order suggests a preparation for office rather than the announcement of a status already held.

**Taʾwīl al-Aḥādīth: Reading Events, Not Only Dreams**

The gift named second is *taʾwīl al-aḥādīth*, literally the interpretation of accounts or events, which the translation renders as the interpretation of dreams. The word *aḥādīth* is the plural of *ḥadīth*, a thing spoken of, an occurrence, a report. Three layers of meaning have been drawn from it, and the chapter uses all three. The narrowest is the technical skill of decoding a sleeping vision, which Joseph exercises for two prisoners (12:36–41) and then for a king (12:43–49). The middle layer is the discernment of hidden meanings in scripture, the same faculty praised in *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ has sent down the best message—a Book of perfect consistency and repeated lessons”* (39:23). The widest is the ability to see what events are doing, which is what Joseph displays when he reads a famine as a plan and a family's arrival as a reckoning. The phrase *taʾwīl* itself means returning a thing to its origin, so the skill is not guessing but tracing an appearance back to the reality that produced it.

**Where Dreams Come From, and Why the Distinction Matters**

The tradition that grew around this gift began by sorting visions into kinds. A true dream is understood to reach the sleeper from an angelic quarter, while a false one arises from the lower self or from a devil's suggestion. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī preserves the threefold division as a saying current among the early Muslims, reported there through Ibn Sīrīn: dreams are the reflection of one's waking thoughts, what Satan suggests in order to frighten, or glad tidings from God. The prophetic reports draw the same line more sharply — the good vision is from God and the distressing dream from Satan, and the one who sees the second should spit dryly to his left three times, seek refuge and turn over (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2262). Only the first kind carries meaning, which is why interpretation is a discipline rather than a pastime. Joseph's gift presupposes the distinction: to interpret a dream one must first know whether it deserves interpreting at all.

**The Favour Perfected on a Whole House**

Jacob does not stop at his son. The blessing is promised to Joseph *and to the descendants of Jacob*, which extends the gift across a family and a century. Two readings of that extension are traditional. The first takes the blessing to be prophethood, so that the house will produce a line of prophets. The second takes it to include the goods of this world along with the goods of the next, which fits Joseph's case unusually well: he receives revelation and a treasury. The second is the better fit for the sūrah, because the plot delivers both and insists that neither is complete without the other — a prophet with no authority could not save the family, and an administrator with no revelation could not forgive it. Zakariyya's prayer in another chapter asks for an heir who will inherit from him and *“from the family of Jacob”* (19:6), showing that the house remained a byword for a certain kind of blessing long after this promise.

**Two Forefathers and the Two Trials Behind Them**

Abraham and Isaac are named as the precedent, and each name carries a trial that ended in deliverance. Abraham was taken by God as a close friend (4:125) and was rescued from the fire his own people built for him: *“We ordered, ‘O fire! Be cool and safe for Abraham!’”* (21:69; the building of the furnace is reported at 37:97). Isaac's blessing is bound to the altar, where he submitted to his father's commission and was ransomed by a substitute at God's command (37:102–107). The Qur'an does not name the son in that account, and the exegetes differ between Ismāʿīl and Isḥāq; Jacob's phrasing here does not settle the question, since he cites both men as recipients of a completed favour rather than as participants in one event. What the pairing establishes is the pattern the boy should expect: elevation is reached through a trial that looks like destruction, and the favour is called complete only when the trial has been survived.

**All-Knowing, All-Wise**

The verse closes on two names, *ʿAlīm Ḥakīm*, and their order is the point. Knowledge precedes wisdom because selection requires seeing what is not yet visible — the boy in the well, the prisoner before the king, the famine years before the harvest — while wisdom concerns the arrangement of what has been seen. A chooser who is all-knowing cannot be mistaken about the person selected; a chooser who is all-wise cannot be mistaken about the sequence. Both names are needed for what Jacob has just promised, since the promise includes delay, imprisonment and false accusation, none of which looks wise while it is happening. The two names also govern the entire chapter, which is a demonstration that God's knowledge of an outcome and God's wisdom in the route to it are not the same attribute and cannot be judged by the same clock.
