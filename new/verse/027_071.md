## Sūrah an-Naml 27:71

> They ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Demand for a Date**

The objection narrows to a single question: when. Not whether, not how, but the timing, and the demand is framed with a challenge attached — *in kuntum ṣādiqīn*, if you are truthful.

The question appears in this form throughout the Qur'an, addressed to several prophets by several peoples, always about the same promise. What it refers to is the end of the world and the judgment that follows, which the chapter's previous verses have been arguing for. The demand has a surface reasonableness. A claim about the future that never specifies a date can seem to be protecting itself, and asking for a date is the natural way to test it. That is what makes the question effective as rhetoric and useless as inquiry.

**The Condition Turned Around**

The challenge clause is the same one the Prophet was instructed to use against them seven verses earlier, when he was told to say: show me your proof, if what you say is true. Now they return the identical phrase.

The symmetry is unlikely to be accidental in a chapter this carefully constructed. Both sides are invoking the same standard of honesty, and the difference lies in what each is being asked to produce. He was asked for a *burhān*, a demonstrative proof for a claim about who deserves worship, which is a thing arguments can supply. They are asking for a date, which is a thing the Qur'an has just said nobody in the heavens or the earth possesses. The two demands look parallel and are not. One requests something the position should be able to deliver; the other requests something the position has explicitly denied having.

**Why the Question Cannot Be Answered**

Verse 65 had already closed this off. Nobody in the heavens or the earth knows the unseen except God, and the resurrection was named there as the specific case. The demand in this verse asks for exactly what was just declared unavailable.

That makes the question a trap rather than an inquiry, and the questioners presumably knew it. If the Prophet names a date he either proves himself false when it passes or contradicts what he has already said. If he declines, he can be presented as evasive. The Qur'an's reply, in the next verse, does neither. It gives a partial answer that concedes the ignorance and removes the comfort the ignorance was supposed to provide, which is the only move available that is both honest and effective.

**What the Question Is Actually For**

Nobody asking this wants a date in order to prepare. The function of the demand is to convert a warning into a claim that can be falsified by waiting, and waiting is something the questioners were going to do anyway.

This connects to what Ṣāliḥ confronted at Thamūd. His people were hastening the punishment, calling for it, and he asked why they were hurrying toward the bad outcome when the good one was available. The Meccans here are performing a more sophisticated version of the same act: demanding the arrival of the thing they claim not to believe in, and treating each day of its absence as evidence. The chapter has already recorded both peoples using time as an argument. Both are spending the interval that was given for something else.

**The Address Is to the Believers**

The question is put to a group — the Arabic verb is plural — and it is aimed at the whole community rather than at the Prophet alone. That detail is worth registering.

By this stage the Muslims in Mecca were a visible minority with a doctrine their neighbours found absurd, and the demand for a date would have been thrown at them in markets and households rather than delivered formally to their leader. What the verse records is the texture of daily pressure: the recurring taunt, phrased as a question, that a person has no good answer to. The reply supplied in the next verse is short enough to be used in exactly such an exchange, and it is instructive that the Qur'an gives them something to say rather than telling them to endure the mockery in silence.

**The Same Demand Across Several Chapters**

This question recurs in Yūnus, al-Anbiyāʾ, Sabaʾ, Yā Sīn and al-Mulk in almost identical wording. Five other chapters carry it, and in each the reply is a variation on what an-Naml gives here: a refusal to name a time, coupled with a statement that the thing is nearer than assumed.

The consistency of both the question and the answer is informative. It indicates that this was the settled shape of the dispute in Mecca over a period of years — not an argument that developed, but a challenge repeated and a reply repeated. It also means the Qur'an never broke its own rule under pressure. Across every instance of the demand, no date is ever given, no approximation offered, and no sign specified by which the timing could be calculated. A text produced under that much provocation, by an author who wanted to convince, would have been sorely tempted at some point. This one holds the line in all six places.

**A Threat and a Promise Are the Same Event**

The translation renders the disputed thing as *this threat*, and the Arabic *waʿd* is the ordinary word for a promise. Arabic marks the negative sense with a related form, *waʿīd*, and the choice of the neutral word here is worth noticing.

To the questioners it is a threat, and they are treating it as one. To the believers listening it is a promise, since the same day that brings the reckoning brings what the chapter's second verse called good news. One event, two names, and which name a person uses is determined entirely by where he expects to stand. That is why the question is asked with a sneer rather than with anxiety. A man genuinely uncertain about which side of the promise he would fall on would not demand its arrival, and the mockery is itself a report of what the questioners assume about themselves.
