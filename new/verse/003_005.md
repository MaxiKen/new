## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:5

> Surely nothing on earth or in the heavens is hidden from Allah.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence With No Corner Left Out**

The verse takes the two halves of the created world and puts them in one clause: earth and heaven, nothing in either concealed from God. Hebrew and Arabic speech both use this pairing as a way of saying "everywhere", and the Qur'an uses it constantly. The translation preserves the shape of the idiom, which is why it sounds like a survey rather than a proposition.

Other passages fill in the same sentence with consequences. Abraham's descendants pray with it in mind: *“Our Lord! You certainly know what we conceal and what we reveal. Nothing on earth or in heaven is hidden from Allah.”* (14:38), and the Book of Records is described in identical terms: *“For there is nothing hidden in the heavens or the earth without being ˹written˺ in a perfect Record.”* (27:75). The same claim is made about the small things, the ones no ledger notices: *“With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him. And He knows what is in the land and sea. Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth or anything—green or dry—but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record.”* (6:59).

**What "Hidden" Covers**

The word *khafiya* is about concealment, and the Qur'an's usage shows how wide that is meant to be. It covers deeds done when nobody is looking: *“There is no activity you may be engaged in ˹O Prophet˺ or portion of the Quran you may be reciting, nor any deed you ˹all˺ may be doing except that We are a Witness over you while doing it.”* (10:61). It covers what has not happened yet, and what is buried under other facts, and what the agent himself has not admitted.

Commentators pressed the point to its psychological edge. If God's knowledge of His own creation were incomplete, the incompleteness would have to be explained by something in the creation that escaped Him; the Qur'an refuses that possibility with a question: *“How could He not know His Own creation? For He ˹alone˺ is the Most Subtle, All-Aware.”* (67:14). The knowledge is described as belonging to the act of making, not as acquired afterwards. Whoever assembled something knows where its joints are.

**A Private Thought Is Part of the Record**

The verse sits in a sūrah that will later address the gap between what people say and what they hold: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah. For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.’”* (3:29). The opening one-sentence claim is the premise on which that later instruction rests.

The claim removes a social illusion that religious people are especially prone to. Most human accountability is built on evidence: a witness, a document, a confession. A person can therefore learn to be careful in public and careless in private, and to treat his interior as a room where the rules do not apply. This verse says the room has no walls. Sincerity stops being a private virtue and becomes the only place where a person and his acts are fully visible to the one who matters.

The same doctrine explains why the Qur'an treats hypocrisy as a harder problem than open disbelief. An open denier has made a public decision and can be answered publicly; the one who believes with his tongue and withdraws with his heart can be answered by nothing a court could produce. The Qur'an's response is to place the whole affair where the evidence is already complete: in the knowledge of God, which needs no trial to reach a verdict.

**Why It Stands Between Revelation and Creation**

The line is not dropped into the sūrah at random. Just before it, the Books of God have been listed as they came down through history; just after it, the formation of human beings in the womb will be described. Revelation and embryology are two very different kinds of statement, and the verse in the middle supplies the common ground: the one who spoke is the one who makes, and nothing made can hide anything from its maker.

The two halves of the argument need each other. A revelation that did not know its audience would be useless; a creator who did not account for his creatures would be indifferent. Placing the sentence where the sūrah places it means the reader meets the knowledge of God before he meets the scrutiny of his own conduct later in the sūrah.

**A Comfort, Not Only a Warning**

The statement cuts both ways, and the Qur'an uses it both ways. For the one who has done what he should not have done, it is exposure. For the one whose efforts go unremarked, it is the removal of an anxiety that no amount of public relations can cure: he can be sure that a deed done only for God has not been filed away as a deed never done.

The Prophet's instruction to be careful about secret sins and his promise that hidden acts of worship are seen belong to the same doctrine. A person can spend a lifetime doing quiet work that no one praises, and a religion whose God is defined by this verse gives that lifetime a permanent witness.

It also explains a repeated Qur'anic habit of joining knowledge with power, as in the closing words of 3:29: *“And Allah is Most Capable of everything.”* (3:29). Knowing and being able to act on what is known are two different things, and creatures have both in broken measure — they know little, and they can do less than they know. The verse above the reader states the undivided version, which is why it can be recited as reassurance without being consoling in a cheap way. Nothing is hidden, and the one who sees can act.
