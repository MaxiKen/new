## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:152

> Indeed, Allah fulfilled His promise to you when you ˹initially˺ swept them away by His Will, then your courage weakened and you disputed about the command and disobeyed, after Allah had brought victory within your reach. Some of you were after worldly gain while others desired a heavenly reward. He denied you victory over them as a test, yet He has pardoned you. And Allah is Gracious to the believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Promise Fulfilled at the Start**

The verse opens by affirming that God was true to the promise made to the community, and it anchors the statement in the first phase of the fighting, when the believers were overcoming their opponents with His permission. The sūrah had described the positioning of the army in the early morning, and the reference in this verse points back to the engagement itself.

The commentators connect the sentence with the early events of the day, when the enemy's standard bearers were killed and the tide seemed to be running entirely in the community's favour. The promise the verse refers to is the help the Book had given the believers, and the testimony here is that the help arrived exactly as stated before the failure set in.

**Three Failures Named in Order**

The verse then lists what happened, and the sequence is precise: the community lost heart, quarrelled among itself about the matter, and disobeyed. The order moves from an inner condition to an open act.

The arrangement describes how a collapse actually proceeds. A weakening of resolve comes first, then a division about what should be done, and then the decision to do something other than what had been ordered. The sūrah does not present the three as separate incidents; it presents them as links in a single chain, and that is how the commentators treat them when they explain the loss of the day.

**The Word for Losing Heart**

The first of the three failures is rendered here as losing heart, and the word describes a slackening of resolve, a collapse of firmness. The same root appeared in the earlier verse of this sūrah that told the believers not to falter after their losses.

The connection between the two verses is instructive. The sūrah first forbids the condition and then records the occasion on which it occurred, and the community that recites the passage hears the instruction and the history in the same sequence. What is described in this verse is not a failure of the sūrah to warn in time; it is the event that the warning had been designed to prevent.

**The Quarrel About the Matter**

The quarrel named in the verse is explained by the commentators as a dispute among the men posted on the high ground. Some of them wished to remain at their positions in accordance with their orders, while others argued for going down to secure a share of what was being gathered on the field.

Al-Ṭabarī reports that the disagreement was between those determined to hold their place and those who pressed to leave it. The episode shows the community's discipline breaking from inside, in a matter where its instructions had been explicit. The verse records the quarrel as one of the three causes of what followed, and the placement of it between the weakening of resolve and the act of disobedience shows how the three belong together.

**Two Desires in One Community**

The verse states that among the community were people who desired this world and people who desired the next, and the distinction is offered as the explanation of the quarrel. The community assembled on the field was not of one mind about what the day was for.

Al-Ṭabarī records a report that Ibn Masʿūd said he had not known, before this verse was sent down, that any of the Prophet's companions could be described as desiring this world. The report conveys the seriousness with which the earliest community received the diagnosis. The verse does not divide the community into two parties of fixed membership; it states that the two motives were present among people who were fighting side by side.

**Turned Aside in Order to Be Tested**

The sentence continues by stating that God turned the community away from its opponents in order to test it. The Book describes the same arrangement in the account of Badr, where it made each army appear small in the eyes of the other for the purpose of establishing what had been decreed.

The verse therefore refuses the reading that has the community losing by accident. The turning aside is presented as an arranged test, and the failure it exposed is precisely what the test was for. The wording keeps the community's responsibility intact while placing the outcome inside a purpose, and the two ideas are held together in a single clause.

**The Pardon Stated in the Same Verse**

The verse then states that God pardoned the community, and the pardon is announced before the passage has finished describing the fault. The commentators note that the pardon followed conduct that was grave, since disobeying an order in the middle of a battle endangers the whole force.

The Book's pattern in these passages is to name the failure and the forgiveness together, and this verse follows the pattern even in the case of a defeat. The community is not left to draw the conclusion that its position has been forfeited; it is told that the offence has been dealt with, and the correction that follows in the next verses concerns its conduct rather than its standing.

**Bounty for the Believers**

The verse closes with the statement that God is possessed of bounty toward the believers. The word covers generosity, favour, and the giving of more than is deserved, and its position at the end of a verse about failure is the point.

The Book's descriptions of God's dealings with the believers frequently end in this way, with the generosity of God named after the account of human weakness. What the community is meant to take from the verse is that its losses did not come from a shortage of divine favour, and that the favour had already produced the pardon announced in the same sentence.
