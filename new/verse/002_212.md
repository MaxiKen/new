## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:212

> The life of this world has been made appealing to the disbelievers, and they mock the believers. Those who are mindful ˹of Allah˺ will rank above them on the Day of Judgment. And Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The World Beautified**

The verse opens on the disbelievers' condition with a passive verb that does quiet theological work: the life of this world has been beautified — made fair, adorned — for those who disbelieve. The beautification is not their own achievement but something done to the world in their regard, and the exegetes' readings of the agent span the scripture's whole account of trial: the world is made fair by God as the test in which human choice is real; by Satan as the decoration he promised to spread; by the world's own glamour operating on the heart that has no second horizon. The verse needs no agent named, because its point is the effect: to the disbeliever this life arrives pre-adorned, its goods luminous, its losses intolerable, its horizon total. The beautification explains the mockery that follows — the man for whom this world is the whole beautiful thing can only treat the believer's other horizon as poverty of imagination — and it explains the sūrah's standing economics: the fleeting gain the concealers traded revelation for (2:174) is exactly this beautified life, and its beauty is the mechanism of every bad trade the sūrah has catalogued. The world is not evil in the verse; it is adorned — and the adornment is the trial, beautiful enough to be believed final.

**They Mock the Believers**

The adornment's social expression is the mockery: they ridicule those who believe. Al-Thaʿlabī records the occasions — the verse was revealed about the Meccan idolaters, or the Medinan hypocrites, or the Jewish leaders of Medina who belittled the Muslim community for its composition: the poor, the weak, the slaves and laborers who filled its first ranks while the notables stayed away. The mockery has a fixed structure the verse assumes: the disbelievers measure the believers by the beautified world's metrics — wealth, status, comfort — and find them losing, then announce the verdict publicly, as though the ledger of adornment were the only ledger. The Qur'an answers this scene repeatedly and consistently: the sūrah of the Mockers devotes its opening to the woe of every scorner who counts his wealth (104:1–2); the Garden's people will call down to the Fire's people asking what their counting availed them (74:40–47 in substance); and the reversal the mockers cannot see is the verse's next clause. The mockery is also, in the passage's own logic, the diptych's continuation: the eloquent hypocrite whose worldly speech impressed (2:204) is the mocker in his community-role, and the mocked believers are the sellers of their souls (2:207) — the verse names both groups' worldly standing and then overturns it in the Day's.

**Above Them on the Day**

The reversal is stated in a single future tense: those who are mindful — the muttaqūn — will be above them on the Day of Resurrection. The elevation is the mockery's exact answer: the ranking the world performed in reverse, the Day performs in truth. Al-Qurṭubī notes the tradition's elaborations — reports describing how the belittled and mocked believers will receive great fame and standing on the Day while their abusers are humiliated and punished — and the verse's own wording carries the whole: above them, a spatial verdict in a scene the scripture consistently describes in the spatial terms of rank, the Garden's people calling down to the Fire's, the elevated couches and the lowered abode. The taqwā named as the elevation's criterion completes the passage's thread: the reverent are the ones the hajj-legislation formed (2:196–203), the ones the flexible options were granted for (2:203), the ones God is with (2:194) — and the verse now publishes the community's true standing against its mockers. The mockery measured by adornment; the Day measures by mindfulness; and the measured are placed above not by retaliation but by the simple disclosure of what the two currencies were always worth.

**The Lowliest Better Than a World Full**

Al-Qurṭubī collects, under this verse, the report that gives the reversal its most concrete image — the exchange between Abū Dharr and the Prophet. Look around the mosque, the Prophet said, and find the most exalted man you can see: Abū Dharr looked and named a finely dressed man sitting in prominence. Now find the lowliest: he looked again and named a weak man in tattered clothes. Then the verdict, sworn by the One in whose hand his soul rested: the second man weighed, before God, more than a world filled with the first man's kind. The report is the verse's commentary in scene-form: the two men the mockery ranks, the adornment's champion and the taqwā's unknown, weighed against each other in the only scales that hold, and the lowliest not merely equal but outweighing a world filled with his exalted counterpart. The hyperbole is arithmetic rather than rhetoric — the verse's without-limit clause supplies its ground: if God's giving is beyond reckoning, then the worth He places in a single mindful soul can exceed any quantity of adorned ones, and the Day's above-them will be the public reading of a weight already assigned. The mosque scene is every community's scene: the dressed and the seated and the admired on one side, the tattered and the unnoticed and the reverent on the other, and the scales hidden.

**Provision Without Reckoning**

The verse closes with the clause that re-reads everything before it: God provides for whomever He wills without reckoning. The exegetes unfold the phrase's several senses, and each cuts against the mockers' ledger. God does not give a reckoning to anyone for what He does — the Provider answers to no auditor, and the adorned life's distribution is His alone. His infinitude needs no reckoning — He does not count His giving for fear it will dwindle, the treasury beyond arithmetic. The giving spans both worlds — provision in this life and provision in the next (al-Rāzī), so the adorned man's wealth is not the whole of what is being distributed. Some read the clause eschatologically: the elect who enter the Garden directly, without reckoning, their passage as unmeasured as the provision. And the clause alludes to the incommensurability of God's giving altogether — the tenfold and beyond of the good deed (6:160), the grain that sprouts seven ears of a hundred grains (2:261), the provision found beside Mary in the sanctuary, from God, who provides for whom He wills without limit (3:37). Every sense converges on one refutation: the world's accounting — by which the mockers measured and the believers lost — is not merely unfair but unreal, a local arithmetic inside a giving that has none.

**The Verse Between the Diptych and the One Community**

The verse's placement binds the movement's three panels into one argument. The diptych (2:204–207) showed the heart's two economies in individual form — the marketed self and the sold self. The slip-and-cloud verses (2:208–211) showed the community's footing and its horizon — the proofs, the waiting, the altered favor of the witness-people. This verse shows the two economies at social scale: the beautified world with its mockers, the taqwā with its mocked, and the Day with its reversal — and the following verse will widen the scene once more to the species, humanity one community then divided, the Book sent to judge (2:213). The through-line is the currency-question the whole passage keeps asking: what is the world's adornment worth against the portion earned (2:202), the sold soul's price (2:207), the favor not altered (2:211)? The verse's answer is the provision-clause — the giving without reckoning, in which the mockers' wealth is a measured thing inside the Unmeasured's hand, and the mocked believer's standing is a weight already placed. The reader carried from Minā's gathering through the hypocrite's portrait to this verse arrives at the community's charter against contempt: the adornment is real but reckoned by another arithmetic, the mockery is loud but brief, and the above-them of the Day is provision without limit made visible.
