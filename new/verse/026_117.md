## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:117

> Noah prayed, “My Lord! My people have truly rejected me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Noah Turns to God After the People Reject Him**

Noah responds to rejection by praying to his Lord. The movement is important. He does not make the people's hostility the final authority over his heart. Their rejection is real and painful, but he carries it to God rather than allowing it to become despair, revenge, or a reason to doubt the message.

The prayer is brief because the essential fact is clear: his people have rejected him. They have not merely misunderstood one statement. They have denied the messenger and resisted the warning. Noah names the situation before the Lord who already knows it, turning his complaint into dependence rather than into a public performance of anger.

**The Word Rejected Includes Accusing Noah of Falsehood**

The source tradition explains rejection as declaring Noah a liar. The people do not simply decline his invitation. They judge his claim to be false and treat his warning as unworthy of belief. Their refusal therefore attacks both the message and the messenger who carries it.

Noah's prayer does not respond by inventing a counter-insult. He reports what happened to God. That restraint gives the complaint dignity. A person can state that he has been wronged without allowing the wrong to dictate his character. Noah's sorrow remains directed toward the Lord rather than toward the satisfaction of humiliating his opponents.

**The Prayer Admits the Pain of Prophetic Work**

A messenger is not emotionally untouched by rejection. Noah has lived among these people and warned them over time. Their refusal is not an abstract statistic. It concerns people whose safety he wants and who answer his concern with denial and threat.

The prayer makes room for sadness without treating sadness as spiritual failure. Faith does not require pretending that rejection is pleasant. It requires carrying the pain to God and remaining within the limits of justice. Noah's example shows that a servant can grieve over a people's refusal while still refusing to become vindictive.

**My People Keeps the Relation Visible**

Noah calls them my people even after they reject him. The phrase does not approve their denial. It shows that he still understands the human bond and the responsibility he feels toward them. He is not praying about a nameless enemy. He is speaking of a community to which he was sent and for whose guidance he has labored.

That language also prevents the prayer from becoming tribal revenge. Noah does not say that strangers have harmed him and therefore deserve destruction. He says that his own people have rejected the message. The grief of a messenger remains connected to the hope that the people could have accepted truth.

**Rejection Does Not Change the Source of Noah's Authority**

The people have called Noah false, but their verdict cannot make him false. A community's judgment about a messenger is not the measure of God's commissioning. Noah returns to my Lord because the source of his authority remains above the people who reject it.

This is a necessary protection for anyone who serves truth. Criticism can make a person question whether the work has value, especially when the critics are numerous or familiar. Noah's prayer locates the final evaluation with God. The audience may refuse, but the Lord knows whether the messenger has been faithful.

Noah reports rejection without yet naming a punishment in this line. His first response is to disclose the situation to God. That order matters. A believer may ask God to address oppression, but he should not rush to make personal anger sound like divine judgment.

Prayer gives the servant a place to bring grief and seek direction. It allows God to judge the matter and prevents the injured person from becoming judge, prosecutor, and executioner at once. Noah's words are honest but controlled. He trusts the Lord to determine what should follow the people's rejection.

**A Clear Warning Can Still Be Rejected**

The people have heard Noah's call, yet they deny him. Their response confirms that clarity does not force acceptance. The messenger's responsibility is not to guarantee a positive reaction. He must convey the warning, answer honestly, and remain patient until the community's refusal reaches its moral limit.

This distinction protects the believer from measuring truth by immediate success. A teacher may explain well and still be rejected. A parent may counsel sincerely and still be ignored. The pain is real, but it does not prove that the counsel was false. Noah's prayer teaches the servant to take rejection to God rather than to rewrite the truth for approval.

**Turning to God Restores the Messenger's Center**

The people have made their rejection the center of the public scene. Noah makes his Lord the center of his private response. That turn restores proportion. The community's denial matters because lives are at stake, but it does not become larger than God's knowledge, power, or mercy.

A messenger who turns to God can continue to act without being ruled by applause or insult. He may grieve, warn, and seek a just resolution, yet his heart remains attached to the Lord. Noah's prayer begins the movement from rejected warning to divine judgment while preserving the dignity of a servant who refuses to answer denial with uncontrolled anger. His complaint is therefore an act of trust: he brings the wound to the One who can judge it without distortion. The prayer does not make Noah passive. It gives him a disciplined way to continue serving without letting rejection turn his heart toward injustice. He can wait for God's decision while remaining faithful to the warning already entrusted to him. Trust becomes his response to rejection.
