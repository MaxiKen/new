## Sūrah an-Naml 27:62

> Or ˹ask them,˺ “Who responds to the distressed when they cry to Him, relieving ˹their˺ affliction, and ˹Who˺ makes you successors in the earth? Is it another god besides Allah? Yet you are hardly mindful!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Moves Inside**

The previous questions named public facts: heavens, rain, gardens, rivers, mountains, the sea. This one names something private. Who answers a desperate person who calls out.

That shift changes the kind of evidence being offered. Cosmology can be conceded intellectually and kept at a distance; the experience of calling for help and being answered cannot. Nearly everyone in the audience had been in extremity at some point — illness, debt, a lost caravan, a child not breathing — and had called out. The question asks them what they did in that moment and to whom. The Qur'an makes the observation explicitly elsewhere, that when a wave engulfs a ship its passengers call on God alone and forget their intermediaries. This verse puts the same fact as a question rather than an accusation.

**Who the "Distressed" Are**

The word is *al-muḍṭarr*, a passive form meaning one who has been driven to necessity — pushed into a corner by circumstances rather than having walked into one. Al-Qurṭubī glosses it as the helpless and powerless; al-Rāzī specifies someone in a bad situation not of their choosing, afflicted by poverty or illness.

The grammar is the point. The person described did not choose distress and cannot choose their way out; the form of the word denies them agency. That is a specific and unflattering condition, and the verse makes it the one God is named as responding to. There is no mention of the righteous, the deserving, or those with a record. The qualification for the response described here is having nothing left, which is the opposite of how access to a patron normally works in any human system.

**The Supplication With No Veil**

Al-Qurṭubī connects this verse to a report about the prayer of the wronged, and the report is well attested. When the Prophet ﷺ sent Muʿādh ibn Jabal to Yemen he instructed him at length and ended with a warning: beware the supplication of the oppressed, for there is no veil between it and God (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1496; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 19).

The context of that instruction sharpens its application here. Muʿādh was being sent as a collector of the obligatory charity, with authority over people's property, and the warning was against taking the best of their wealth. The prayer that passes without a veil is therefore the prayer of the person your own power has placed in distress. Read alongside this verse, the two texts describe the same access from two angles: the Qur'an says God answers the one driven to necessity, and the report tells those with power that this is a fact about their own position. The chapter has already shown nine notables in a city who did not think so.

**Made Successors on the Earth**

The second half of the question shifts register entirely: *wa yajʿalukum khulafā'a al-arḍ* — and who makes you successors in the earth. The word *khalīfah* means one who comes after another and takes their place.

Pairing this with the answering of the distressed produces a deliberate contrast. Human beings are, in one sentence, utterly helpless and entrusted with the earth. Both are true and the Qur'an sets them side by side without softening either. The succession also carries a warning inside it, because a successor occupies a place someone else has vacated. The chapter has just spent fifty verses on the peoples who vacated it — Thamūd's houses standing empty, Pharaoh's army gone, Lūṭ's cities buried. Whoever is being addressed holds the earth on the same terms those nations did.

**"Hardly Mindful"**

The refrain closes with *qalīlan mā tadhakkarūn* — little do you remember, or reflect. The verb is from *dhikr*, recollection, and it implies the material was already possessed.

That is the accurate charge for what this verse describes. Nobody needs to be informed that they called out in their worst hour; they need to remember it. The experience of being answered is one of the most common in human life and one of the most quickly filed away once the crisis passes, because acknowledging it has consequences for the rest of the week. The verse's complaint is therefore not against an absence of evidence but against the handling of evidence already held — the same complaint the chapter made against Pharaoh's court, who knew, and against Sheba, whose people were barred by what they already worshipped. Note also the switch to the second person: the previous verse said *most of them do not know*, and this one says *you* hardly reflect. The audience has stopped being discussed and started being addressed.

**The Response Is Described, Not Promised**

Read the grammar closely and the verse is not making a pledge about the future. It asks who is the one that responds — *amman yujību* — using a form that describes a standing characteristic rather than issuing a guarantee about any particular prayer.

The distinction matters for how the verse is used. Taken as a promise that every cry is granted as requested, it would be falsified daily by ordinary experience, and the Qur'an is not careless enough to make such a claim. What it asserts is that the one who answers the desperate is God, and that nothing else occupies that role. The question at issue is identity, not throughput. That reading also fits the polemical setting: the audience is being asked who they turned to in their worst hour, not whether they got what they asked for. Their own behaviour under pressure is the evidence being cited.
