## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:1

> The Inevitable Hour!

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Word Opens the Sūrah**

The sūrah begins with a single word left to detonate on its own: al-ḥāqqah. No verb supports it, no clause explains it, and no introduction softens it. Hearers receive one hammer-blow of sound and must wait for the rest. Such abruptness belongs to the late Makkan manner, where short sūrahs seize attention before they argue. A comparable passage opens the same way, with "The Striking Disaster!" standing alone at the head of Sūrah 101 (101:1), and the effect matches: a name gets pronounced as though it were already an event. The definite article carries weight here. This is no hour among hours but the Hour, the single appointment every soul keeps. Grammar underlines finality, since a nominal sentence with its predicate withheld leaves nothing to debate and nowhere to hide. Its very brevity rebukes the complacency of people who treated the Resurrection as rumor. Before any proof arrives, the sūrah plants a sound in the ear that the questions of the following verses will then unfold.

**Certainty Stands Inside the Word Itself**

Arabic morphology carries the argument inside the noun. Ḥāqqah derives from ḥ-q-q, the root of truth, rightness, and whatever stands established beyond dispute. The fāʿilah pattern adds emphasis, so the word means something like the overwhelmingly real, the truth that imposes itself by force. Commentators agree that the Day of Resurrection and Judgment stands behind the term, though they divide over which facet it stresses. Some hold that it names the Day on which every denied truth becomes manifest, when rejection collapses before witnessed fact. Others say it points instead to the exposure of deeds, the hour when each person sees a life exactly as it was lived. Both readings share one nerve: reality will no longer be arguable. What disbelief calls uncertain, this word calls settled. The English "inevitable" captures that edge, since the sense is not merely true but unmissable and unpostponable. Whatever else the sūrah will describe, its first word already denies the denier any ground to stand on.

**The Hour That No One Escapes**

Resurrection dominates the verse because the name itself refuses any smaller referent. All mainstream exegetes read al-ḥāqqah as the Last Day, differing only on emphasis. One group stresses manifestation: hidden realities break surface, and claims long dismissed as fantasy appear in plain view. Another group stresses recompense: rights get settled, wrongs get weighed, and nobody leaves the plain without an account. A third stresses the shattering of false confidence, the moment when excuses lose their grammar. These are facets of one event rather than rival theories, and the strongest reading keeps all three together. The sūrah will later march through destroyed peoples, the trumpet blast, and the two companies of the judged, and each scene draws its terror from this opening name. Nothing in the verse invites speculation about dates or portents. Its business is conviction: the end toward which history moves has a name, and the name means that it cannot fail.

**A Name Meant to Shake the Listener**

Rhetoric explains why the sūrah opens with a bare title instead of a sentence. Makkan hearers had grown skilled at deflecting arguments, so the Book first breaks their rhythm with a cry. A lone noun, shouted and then left hanging, works like a trumpet note before the blast it announces. Oral delivery would have sharpened the effect: the reciter pauses, silence gathers, and then the questioning begins. Ancient audiences knew such gravity from oaths and proclamations, where a single weighty word commanded a crowd to stillness. Here the command is implicit but unmistakable — stop, listen, and feel the ground tilt. The exclamation mark in translation tries to preserve that vocal force, though no punctuation quite renders a recited monosyllable in a crowded valley. Sound precedes sense by design. Only after the dread has entered through the ear does the sūrah consent to explain what the dread concerns.

**Late Makkan Warning After a Long Dispute**

Place and date sharpen the verse. Al-Ḥāqqah belongs to the late Makkan years, revealed around the time of Sūrah 67, when opposition had hardened into mockery and the Prophet faced charges of madness and possession. The preceding sūrah had already defended him against the slur of possession (68:2), and the closing passage of this sūrah will complete that defense by vindicating the Book itself (69:38-43). Between those brackets stands judgment, the sūrah's true spine. Opening with the Hour therefore answers exhaustion with certainty: however long denial lasts, it ends at an appointed morning. Arabian hearers needed no footnote for destroyed peoples, since ruined towns dotted their trade routes, and the sūrah will soon summon those ruins as witnesses. But first it sets the destination. Every road in the chapter — history, cosmic upheaval, the ledgers of deeds — runs toward the single Day named here.

**The Overture Before the Questions**

Verse 1 functions as an overture, and its incompleteness is the point. A title without a sentence creates hunger for one, and hunger makes the following questions land harder. The sūrah could have announced its theme in calm prose; instead it strikes a gong and lets the reverberation do the teaching. That choice reveals confidence in the listener's conscience: one true word, rightly placed, can unsettle years of evasion. Nothing here argues yet, and nothing needs to, because names this heavy argue by existing. What the verse withholds — explanation, date, description — the chapter will supply in stages, moving from dread to history to cosmic ruin to individual reckoning. The architecture starts narrow and widens, like a door opening onto a plain. The next verse takes the first step through it by asking aloud what this Inevitable Hour actually is.
