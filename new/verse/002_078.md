## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:78

> And among them are the illiterate who know nothing about the Scripture except lies, and ˹so˺ they ˹wishfully˺ speculate.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Class: The People Below the Scholars**

The recital completes its social anatomy. Verse 75 indicted a group that heard Allah's word and distorted it knowingly; verse 76 recorded the closed-door counsel that managed what the confession would concede. Now the camera widens: *and among them are the illiterate* — a third class inside the same community, the people who never heard, never read, and never distorted anything, because the Book never reached them except through other mouths. The sūrah is describing the structure of a scriptural religion as it actually exists on the ground: a literate clerical class holding the text, and beneath it a vast population whose religion is what the class transmits. The verse's charge is not the heavy one — no distortion, no conspiracy — but it is a charge nonetheless: their knowledge of the Scripture runs to nothing but lies, and what they hold is wishful speculation. The illiterate are indicted for the shape of their knowing: a religion held without verification, received without the capacity to check, resting where its teachers left it. The three verses together file the whole pyramid, and the sūrah's point sharpens: the call now being made in Madina cannot pass through this structure, because the structure itself is what the verses describe.

**Ummī: What the Word Carries**

The term — *ummiyyūn* — is doing careful work. In the usage the exegetes settle on, an *ummī* is one who cannot write, or cannot write well: the condition of the majority in a world where literacy was a trade. The commentators note the qualification honestly: literacy in Arabia, Jewish and Arab alike, was partial — some read and wrote fluently, some managed a rudimentary script, and the fully literate were a small fraction of the whole. A minority opinion attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās reads the word differently — a people acknowledging no prophet and no revealed book, called *ummiyyūn* precisely for that opposition to the Books of Allah — but the tradition itself marks this reading as unconvincing against the ordinary sense of the word. The term carries a second life in the Qur'an that makes its appearance here resonate: the coming Prophet is announced as *the unlettered Prophet* whom they find written in their own Torah and Gospel (7:157), and Sūrah al-Jumuʿah describes a Messenger raised *from among the unlettered* (62:2). Illiteracy in the recital's vocabulary is neither insult nor virtue but a condition — and the verse's illiterate are defined by it tragically: cut off from the Book by the very lack that, in another people, would make the Book's arrival among them the miracle it became.

**Amānī: The Lies and the Stories One Wants**

What the illiterate have instead of the Scripture is named in the Arabic *amānī* — rendered here *lies* — a word the tradition reads from two sides, and both sides belong to the verse. On one reading it means the reports they are told: their knowledge of Scripture is exactly what their scholars choose to say it is, religion received secondhand from the same class the previous verses convicted. On the other it means the things invented and wished for — the doctrines of comfort the community prefers: that the Fire will hold them only a counted number of days, that the Garden admits no one outside their own two communities. The word can be rendered lies, hearsay, hopes, or desires, and its semantic field — stories heard and stories wanted — is precisely the material an unverified religion runs on. The double sense is the verse's diagnosis in a single term: without access to the Book, the laity cannot distinguish what was transmitted from what was consoling, and the class above them, as verses 75–76 established, has both the means and the motive to blur the line. *Amānī* is scripture-by-rumor plus scripture-by-wish, and the illiterate hold it with full confidence and zero ground.

**“They Wishfully Speculate”**

The closing verb fixes the epistemic status of the whole: they *speculate* — wishfully, as the translation marks it — the Arabic *yaẓunnūn*: they conjecture, suppose, and guess. The exegetes gloss it sharply: they lie about Allah, or they think false things about Him — conjecture that has hardened into assertion. The Qur'an's epistemology runs on a distinction this verb draws cleanly: knowledge (*ʿilm*) rests on revelation received and verified; *ẓann* is everything short of it — inference, hearsay, presumption — and when *ẓann* occupies the place of knowledge in religion, the occupation is itself the sin. The illiterate's tragedy is that their conjecture is involuntary in origin: they cannot read the Book, so guessing is all the machinery they have. Yet the verse does not excuse them, and the reason is the verse's transferable lesson: they accepted a religious certainty whose warrant they never sought, from custodians whose trade the recital has just exposed. Conjecture filed as conviction is the layperson's taḥrīf — the same slant between word and meaning, produced below stairs by credulity instead of above by design. The sūrah's running audit of reason — the repeated *do you not understand?* — has its negative case here: a community whose majority has stopped needing reasons at all.

**The Corruption Chain, Top to Bottom**

Read in sequence, verses 75 through 78 describe a system, and the system is the verse's real subject. At the top: scholars who heard Allah's word, understood it, and distorted it knowingly. Around them: a private council governing what may be admitted in public, afraid of their own scripture's contents reaching rival hands. Beneath them: the illiterate majority whose entire religion is what the council permits to circulate — hearsay shaped above, conjecture held below. The flow runs one way: the literate manufacture the distortion and the unlettered consume it, and the consumer's inability to check is what makes the manufacturer's crime profitable. Accountability in the recital attaches at both ends of the chain — the verse files the illiterate's condition as culpable ignorance, the ignorance of those who took a religion on terms that made verification impossible by construction. For the young Muslim community hearing this recitation, the anatomy was immediate equipment: they were being told why the scholars of Madina would not simply hand their congregations the truth if it were shown them — the congregations had no independent access to the truth to begin with, and the custodians' position depended on keeping it that way.

**The Unlettered Nation and the Book It Cannot Alter**

The verse's irony completes itself in the community addressed. The Prophet ﷺ described his own people in the same term: *"We are an illiterate nation; we neither write, nor know accounts"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1913) — the ḥadīth's context is the lunar calendar, a ruling that fasting follows the seen crescent rather than calculation, and its self-description is exact: an *ummī* ummah. Yet the Qur'an's preservation runs through precisely this condition as its instrument. The revelation given to the unlettered Prophet was lodged not in manuscripts held by a class but in mass-recited memory — thousands of chests carrying the same text, generation upon generation, a transmission model in which the hearsay chain of verse 78 is structurally impossible: where every layperson carries the Book itself, no council can manage what the laity know. The contrast is the recital's quiet argument for its own audience. Israel's Scripture became, in the verse's description, a text the many could not check and the few could slant; the Qur'an answers by making its whole community the manuscript. The illiterate of verse 78 are pitiable and culpable at once — and the ummah hearing the verse is being shown, in the same breath, what its own unletteredness was arranged to prevent.
