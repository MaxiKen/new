## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:78

> And among them are the illiterate who know nothing about the Scripture except lies, and ˹so˺ they ˹wishfully˺ speculate.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Group Inside the Same Community**

The preceding verses described people who knew the scripture, understood it, and turned it to another purpose. This one describes a different category within the same community: people who never had direct access to the text at all. The Book names both so that the reader does not treat an entire people as a single moral type, and the two portraits stand beside each other for comparison.

The shift matters for how the passage is used. A reader who treats the earlier verses as a description of a whole nation is making a judgment the sūrah does not make, and the sūrah has already stated that not all of them are alike. This verse supplies the concrete case of people whose shortcoming is ignorance rather than manipulation, and the sentences that follow continue to separate the groups.

**The Word Translated as Illiterate**

The Arabic term describes a person who cannot read or write, and the Book uses it for the peoples who had not been given a written scripture before the revelation to Muhammad ﷺ, when it names him the messenger sent to the unlettered and to others who had not yet joined them (62:2–3). In the Book's vocabulary the word marks the absence of a written book rather than a defect in the people.

The Prophet ﷺ used the same root of his own community when he explained that God had not sent him to a people who wrote: "We are an unlettered community; we do not write and we do not calculate" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1913). The report goes on to describe how a person unsure of the lunar date should estimate thirty days. The word in this verse belongs to that ordinary, descriptive sense.

**Lies Where Knowledge Should Be**

The translation before us says these people know nothing about the Scripture except lies, and the Arabic word it renders that way carries two attested senses. It can mean recited material received by hearing, and it can mean wishes or fond hopes, which is why the translation adds that they wishfully speculate. The classical commentators took both readings seriously, and several combined them: a people whose acquaintance with revelation consisted of recited fragments and hopeful slogans absorbed from others, with no knowledge of the text itself.

The second sense connects this sentence to the claim quoted two verses later, where the same community expects the Fire to touch it only for a limited number of days (2:80). A people supplied with hopes rather than with the scripture is precisely the group that would make such a claim, and the two verses describe one condition from two angles.

**How a Community Loses Its Text**

The Book's complaint here is not that these people were incapable of learning. It is that the only knowledge available to them had been mediated by others, and those others were described in the previous verses as unreliable. A community in which the text is held by a few and delivered to the rest as summaries and encouraging stories has no check on what the intermediaries do with it.

That structure explains why the Book repeatedly warns its own audience about the same risk. The Qur'an tells the believers to remain with those who know and to ask the people of knowledge when they do not, and it describes a community that followed what its forefathers had been told without evidence (43:22–23). The verse in al-Baqarah gives the pattern in its sharpest form, and it is placed directly before the verse that names the fabricators.

**Speculation Presented Without Evidence**

The sentence ends by stating that these people do nothing but speculate, and the word chosen refers to supposition that falls short of knowledge. The Book employs it for positions held and defended without proof. What the verse identifies is therefore not doubt, which study can remedy, but confidence resting on nothing.

The sūrah later commands the believers not to follow what they have no knowledge of, naming hearing, sight, and the heart as faculties for which each person is accountable (17:36). Applied to the group in this verse, that command reads as a direction rather than a reproach: the way out of conjecture is acquisition, and the rest of this passage is an address to a community invited to exactly that.

**A Portrait Shaped to Exonerate the Misled**

The verse does not describe a people who set out to invent a religion. It describes people who were kept from a book they had never learned to read, and it places responsibility for the distortion on the group handled by the surrounding verses. The Book's practice is to distinguish between those who author an offence and those who inherit its consequences, and it warns teachers that misleading a community carries a heavier burden than private error.

That distinction raises a question the verse leaves open. Knowledge of what a scripture actually says cannot be supplied by repeating what others say about it, and the next sentence names people who present their own material as revelation. The Book sets the two verses together so the reader can see how an illiterate community is manufactured.

**Why the Pairing Is Not an Insult to Learning**

Some readers have taken this verse as a criticism of literacy itself, and the text rules that out. The Book praises the learned repeatedly, commands the Prophet ﷺ to seek increase in knowledge, and tells its audience that those who know and those who do not are not equal (39:9). The verse's complaint is about a state of information, not about the capacity of the people in it.

Read with that distinction in place, the sentence becomes a warning to every community that inherits its religion as a set of slogans. The remedy the sūrah will keep prescribing for this people, and for the believers as well, is the same: return to what has actually been revealed, and stop attributing to God what nobody has verified.
