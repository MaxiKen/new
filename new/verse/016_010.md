## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:10

> **He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky, from which you drink and by which plants grow for your cattle to graze.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Descent That Makes Life Possible**

Huwa al-ladhī anzala min al-samā'i mā'an — He is the One who sends down water from the sky. The pronoun huwa is emphatic: He, not any partner, not any idol, not any force of nature independent of Him. Anzala indicates sending down from above, a descent that humans cannot produce. In Arabian context, rain was not a minor blessing; it was life itself. Without it, wells dry, pastures die, cattle perish, trade stops. Al-Ṭabarī notes that samā' here means the sky or the direction above, not only clouds, to stress that the source is beyond human control. The Qur'an repeats this gift throughout, as in 15:22 and 23:18, always with the same emphasis: you do not send it down. The verse then divides the water's use into two immediate benefits: lakum minhu sharābun wa-minhu shajarun fīhi tusīmūn — from it you have drink and from it comes vegetation in which you pasture your cattle. Sharāb is drink for humans directly, shajar here means plants, trees, herbage generally, not only trees, and tusīmūn means to pasture, to let cattle graze freely. The same rain serves both you and your animals, linking human and animal dependence on one source.

**Drink And Pasture From One Source**

The pairing of sharābun and shajarun is deliberate. From one water come two kinds of life: human drink and animal feed. Al-Rāzī points out the wisdom: rain falls as one substance, yet it produces different tastes, colors, and uses. You drink it fresh, your cattle eat plants grown by it, then you drink milk from those cattle, as verse 66 will later explain. The chain is: rain → plants → cattle → milk and meat → human. One descent sustains multiple levels. This interconnectedness is a sign of planning, not chance. If rain were random, it might sustain one level but not all. Instead, it sustains the whole system that verses 5-8 just described. The cattle whose warmth, food, beauty, and transport were listed depend on this rain for their pasture. Without rain, there is no jamāl at evening return because there is no full belly. The verse thus ties the earlier catalogue back to its source. The One who created cattle also sends the rain that feeds them. Gratitude must go to the source, not to the intermediate.

**Shajar As All Vegetation**

Shajar is usually translated as trees, but commentators explain that here it means all plants, herbage, and shrubs that cattle graze, including what Arabs call hashīsh and kalā'. Al-Qurṭubī cites linguistic usage where shajar can mean any plant with a stalk, not only tall trees. The phrase fīhi tusīmūn, in which you pasture, uses sāma, to graze freely, distinct from raʿā which can mean to tend. Tusīmūn suggests letting animals roam in pasture, a scene of abundance where herbage is plentiful enough that cattle can be left to eat. This implies rain that is not scant but sufficient to produce pasture. In a desert, such pasture appears suddenly after rain, transforming barren land into green within days. Those who witnessed this transformation knew it as a resurrection in miniature, which is why the Qur'an often links rain to resurrection: *And We send down from the sky blessed water and give growth thereby to gardens and grain* (50:9) and *Look at the traces of Allah's mercy, how He gives life to the earth after its death; indeed He will give life to the dead* (30:50). The pasture that appears after rain is therefore both provision and proof.

**Why The Verse Says He Is The One**

The opening huwa al-ladhī is a formula of exclusivity used throughout this sūrah. It appears in verses 10, 11, 12, 14, and others, each time to attribute a blessing solely to Allah. This repetition is rhetorical: after establishing that Allah is exalted above partners, the sūrah now shows in detail why. Each blessing is introduced with He is the One who... to prevent misattribution. The Makkans attributed rain to stars, to idols, to chance. The Qur'an insists: He is the One who sends it. This exclusivity is the practical meaning of tawḥīd. It is not only to say Allah exists, but to say He alone does these things. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that huwa al-ladhī also carries a sense of personal care: the same He who created you from a drop is the He who sends down your drink. The continuity of subject — He created heavens, He created humans, He created cattle, He sends down rain — builds a single portrait of a Lord who creates, sustains, and guides, leaving no room for partners.

**A Verse That Prepares For Crops And Fruits**

Verse 10 mentions drink and pasture, the basics. Verse 11 will expand to crops, olives, palms, grapevines, and every fruit, showing increasing specialization. The order from general to specific mirrors human experience: first you need water to drink, then pasture for your animals, then crops for food, then fruits for enjoyment. Al-Rāzī sees in this a progression from necessity to delight, similar to verses 5-6 where warmth preceded beauty. The verse also introduces the idea that will become central in verse 11: rain as a sign for those who reflect. Even here, the sign is present: the same water that you drink also grows plants that you do not eat but your cattle do, showing a provision that considers both you and your dependents. A system that provides for multiple species from one source reflects mercy that is wider than human need alone.

**Lexical And Scientific Note**

Anzala min al-samā' is used for rain throughout the Qur'an, though modern meteorology explains rain formation through evaporation and condensation. The Qur'anic phrase does not contradict this; it speaks of ultimate source, not proximate mechanism, and classical commentators like al-Rāzī discussed winds and clouds as intermediaries while still attributing the sending to Allah. Sharāb is from sh-r-b, to drink, and includes all potable water. Tusīmūn is from s-w-m, to graze, to move freely for pasture. The form tusīmūn indicates ongoing, habitual pasturing, not a single event. The verse's simplicity — one sentence linking sky, water, drink, plants, cattle — condenses a whole water cycle into human terms. It is a reminder that the water cycle, which today is diagrammed in textbooks, was for the first audience a daily dependence and a visible mercy, and its regularity is itself a sign of a consistent Creator.
