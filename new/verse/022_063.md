## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:63

> Do you not see that Allah sends down rain from the sky, then the earth becomes green? Surely Allah is Most Subtle, All-Aware.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Sign, and a Return to the Opening**

*A-lam tara anna Allāha anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-tuṣbiḥu al-arḍu mukhḍarratan* — do you not see that God sends down water from the sky, and the earth becomes green?

The sūrah has used this sign before. Verse 5 described the lifeless earth stirring and swelling under rain as the final step in its proof of the Resurrection, and the commentaries noted there that the analogy is the Qur'an's most repeated one.

Its reappearance here, near the end of the sūrah, is a return rather than a repetition. The chapter opened with the argument from the embryo and the field; it closes with the argument from the field again, and the effect is to bracket everything in between. What has happened in the interval — the pilgrimage legislation, the permission to fight, the catalogue of destroyed towns — has all been delivered inside a world whose most ordinary event is a resurrection.

**The Verb: "Becomes"**

*Fa-tuṣbiḥu* — and it *becomes*, in the morning. The verb carries the sense of entering a state, and in this form it is used for what happens at daybreak.

The temporal nuance is exact. Rain falls, and by morning the ground is green. The change is not described as a slow process but as something a person wakes up to. The same verb appears throughout the Qur'an for this: a person goes to sleep in one condition and rises in another.

That is the piece of the analogy the sūrah wants. The Resurrection is described elsewhere as a waking, and the field is the daily rehearsal of it: the earth is dead at night and alive in the morning, and nobody who has seen the second has any difficulty believing it.

Morning is the Qur'an's appointed hour for the arrival of what was promised, and the idiom runs through the narratives of destruction as well as the signs of mercy: *“Their appointed time is the morning. Is the morning not near?”* (11:81). What the night conceals the morning delivers — torment to one town, green to a dead field. The verb *tuṣbiḥu* places the earth's revival inside that larger pattern: nobody watches the change happen, and everyone wakes to find it done.

**"Green"**

*Mukhḍarratan* — green, verdant. The word is from the same root as *khaḍir*, and it denotes the specific green of new growth rather than the colour of anything else.

The detail is doing theological work. What is being described is not the wetting of soil but the production of life, and the colour is the evidence of it. The argument of verse 5 was that God brings the living out of the dead, and the green is what that looks like from a distance.

Green is also the Qur'an's colour for the Garden, and the correspondence is exact enough to read as deliberate: *“There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade”* (18:31). The field after rain and the inhabitants of Paradise share a colour, which means the sign and the thing signified resemble each other — the earth's annual greening is not merely evidence for the Resurrection but a preview of what it looks like.

**A Third "Do You Not See"**

*A-lam tara* — do you not see? The question appears three times in this sūrah — at verse 18, here, and at verse 65 — and the repetition is part of the teaching method.

The sūrah is not asking whether its audience has functioning eyesight. It has already answered that question in verse 46, where it said that the eyes are not blind but the hearts are. What these three questions ask is whether the seeing has produced anything — whether the rain, the ships, the sky held overhead and the whole created order have been registered as what they are.

The questions are also, in form, rhetorical challenges that expect agreement. Nobody denies that the rain greens the earth. The sūrah takes a fact that no one disputes and asks why the one conclusion that follows from it is not drawn.

The three questions form an ascent. Verse 18 asks about the prostration of everything in the heavens and the earth — the order acknowledged. Verse 63 asks about the rain that greens the field — the order renewed. Verse 65 will ask about the subjection of the earth and the ships, and the sky held back from falling — the order sustained and restrained at once. Each question takes something nobody denies and draws it one step closer to the reader, until the cosmic arrangement is shown to be a personal keeping.

**Laṭīf: Subtle, and Reaching Into the Fine Places**

*Inna Allāha laṭīfun khabīr* — God is Subtle, Aware.

*Laṭīf* is one of the hardest divine names to render. It covers subtlety, fineness, gentleness and the quality of reaching where nothing else reaches. Applied to God it means that His action operates at a scale smaller than anything a person can observe, and that His kindness works through channels the recipient does not perceive.

The name is placed here because of what rain does. Water reaches the root of a plant through soil, and the mechanism by which a seed turns water into tissue is not something any observer of the seventh century — or indeed of any century — could see. The green appears; the process that produced it was hidden. That is exactly what *laṭīf* describes: an action whose effects are visible and whose working is not.

The pairing of the two names is itself a formula, and Sūrah al-Mulk states it as a rhetorical question that answers itself: *“How could He not know His Own creation? For He ˹alone˺ is the Most Subtle, All-Aware”* (67:14). Subtlety and awareness belong together because they are the same perfection viewed from two sides — action that reaches everywhere, knowledge that misses nothing. The One who made the seed knows where it lies, which is why the rain finds it.

**Khabīr: Aware**

The second name is *khabīr*, from the root meaning to be acquainted with the inside of a thing, to know it thoroughly.

Paired with *laṭīf*, it completes the claim. God's action reaches the finest detail, and it reaches it knowingly rather than by mechanism. The rain that finds the root is not a system running on its own; it is an act of a Being who knows where the root is.

For the reader of this sūrah, the consequence is the one the verse is building toward. A God who is *laṭīf* and *khabīr* about a seed in the ground is not going to lose track of a person who was expelled from his home, or of an emigrant who died in a strange city.

Sūrah Luqmān attaches the same pair of names to the smallest hidden thing imaginable: *“˹Even˺ if a deed were the weight of a mustard seed — be it ˹hidden˺ in a rock or in the heavens or the earth — Allah will bring it forth. Surely Allah is Most Subtle, All-Aware”* (31:16). A mustard seed inside a rock is the limit case of the concealed, and the verse places it in the same sentence as these two names. The root in the soil is found; the deed in the rock will be found; nothing hidden is hidden from the *khabīr*.

**The Sign Is Directed at a Specific Doubt**

The rain sign answers the specific objection the sūrah recorded at verse 5: *if you are in doubt about the Resurrection*. Doubt about the Resurrection is, at bottom, doubt about whether something dead can become alive again.

The field is the standing refutation, and it is a refutation rather than a metaphor. The Qur'an does not say that the Resurrection is *like* the revival of the earth; it says that the same Agent who does the second will do the first, and it uses the identical verb for both. Where the field is concerned, nobody disputes that the dead thing became alive.

That is why the sign is repeated so often across the Qur'an. It is not the most beautiful of the divine signs and it is not the most obviously miraculous. It is the one that answers the hardest question with an event that everyone has seen.

Sūrah Fuṣṣilat states the sign and the inference in their most explicit form, with the conclusion drawn inside the verse: *“And among His signs is that you see the earth devoid of life, but as soon as We send down rain upon it, it begins to stir ˹to life˺ and swell. Indeed, the One Who revives it can easily revive the dead. He is certainly Most Capable of everything”* (41:39). The verbs for the field — stirring, swelling — are the same verbs verse 5 of this sūrah used for it, which confirms that the two passages are making one argument. The field is exhibited and the inference is supplied in the same breath, because the Qur'an treats this conclusion as following too plainly to need the reader's assistance.

**Why the Rain Sign Carries More Weight Than Any Other**

The Qur'an's preference for this sign over every other is worth accounting for, since the choice is not arbitrary.

The alternatives do not work as well. The stars are distant and their governance is abstract. The mountains are impressive but static. The sea is not available to everyone. Rain is local, repeated, visible in its effects, and tied to the difference between a living year and a famine — which means that every person in the audience has a material stake in it.

A sign that everyone depends on and nobody can produce is the ideal argument. It cannot be dismissed as irrelevant and it cannot be credited to anyone else.

Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah presses the dependence one step further, from the field to the drinker: *“Have you considered the water you drink? Is it you who bring it down from the clouds, or is it We Who do so?”* (56:68–69). Nobody in the audience could answer *we do* — the caravans did not manufacture rain. A sign that every person drinks daily, that no person can produce, and that decides between a living year and a famine leaves no room for either dismissal or self-credit, which is why the Qur'an returns to it more often than to any other.
