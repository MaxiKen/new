## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:4

> and from the evil of those ˹witches casting spells by˺ blowing onto knots,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Technique Is Described, Not a Type of Person**

The verse names an activity. *Al-naffāthāt fī l-ʿuqad* — those who blow onto knots — is a phrase built entirely out of verbs and objects: a blowing, a place to blow on, and the people who do it. Nothing is said about who they are by birth, by tribe, or by temperament, and nothing is said about what the blowing achieves. The description is of a method.

That method was a recognizable trade in the Arabia of the revelation. Knots were tied in a cord one at a time; over each knot an incantation was spoken or breathed; the finished cord was kept, hidden, or buried; and the spell was released by untying the knots in the same order. The operation had a beginning, a set of instruments, and a known way of being reversed, which is why the exegetical accounts of the Prophet's own bewitchment describe a string being located and the knots being undone by recitation.

Naming a craft rather than a person has an effect on the prayer. The reciter is not asking to be protected from a category of people he might identify in his street. He is asking to be protected from something done to him at a distance, by someone he may never meet, using a method he cannot detect. Detection is precisely what the verse gives up on: the harm is described in terms of its technique because its author is unavailable.

**Nafatha Is a Blow With Almost Nothing in It**

The verb distinguishes three kinds of blowing in Arabic, and the distinction is fine enough that the commentators drew it out. *Nafakha* is to blow air; *tafaḷa* is to spit; *nafatha* is between them — a blowing accompanied by a small amount of moisture, or by none at all. The intensive form used here, *naffātha*, denotes someone who does it repeatedly and as a practice.

The choice of the middle term is not incidental. A spell performed by breathing on knots requires no material, leaves no mark, and can be done anywhere in silence. It is the cheapest and least detectable form of the craft, which is why it became its characteristic gesture and why the verse uses it to stand for the whole practice.

The feminine plural form is the ordinary way Arabic makes a class out of an agent-noun. The intensive active participle in the feminine plural here denotes those whose function is this blowing, and the grammar says nothing about whether the practitioners in question were women. That question was argued separately, on the evidence of reports rather than of the word.

**What the Reports Say About Who Did It**

The authenticated account of the Prophet's bewitchment names a man: Labīd ibn al-Aʿṣam, of the Jews of Medina, who used a comb, the hair gathered on it, and the covering of a male date-palm's pollen, and hid the material in the well of Dharwān (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3268). Other accounts, preserved in the exegetical literature, describe women of his household performing the knot-work, and the early commentators connected the feminine plural of this verse to them.

The two are not in conflict. A spell could be commissioned by one person and worked by others, and the tradition records both the employer and the operators. What the verse settles is neither: it describes the work, and leaves the workers unnamed.

Commentators from al-Rāzī onward made the same point from the grammar. The feminine plural in Arabic is used for a class in general, not only for women, so nothing in the wording restricts the refuge sought to harms caused by female practitioners. The reading that takes the form as generic is the one that fits the sūrah's purpose, which is to cover an evil the reciter cannot identify.

**Magic Is Real in the Qur'an, and It Is Never Independent**

The Qur'an's treatment of sorcery has two fixed points, and this verse sits between them. The first is that the practice exists and does damage. Al-Baqarah describes what people learned at Babylon: *“Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will. They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter.”* (2:102). Marriages broke; the buyer of the craft lost his share in what comes next.

The second point is in the same sentence: no harm occurs except by God's will. The craft has effect and no authority. It operates inside a permission it does not control, and the one who uses it trades his Hereafter for that permission.

This chapter's contribution is the correct response. Neither contempt nor terror is offered. Contempt would leave a person unguarded against something the Qur'an says can break a marriage; terror would grant the craft an independence the same Qur'an denies. The response is a request for refuge — the recognition that the danger is real and that it belongs to God.

**ʿUqda Is the Qur'an's Word for a Bind Only Someone Else Can Loose**

The noun in the verse, *ʿuqda*, appears elsewhere in the Qur'an in a personal rather than a magical sense. Moses, sent to Pharaoh, asks: *“and remove the impediment from my tongue”* (20:27) — in Arabic, *wa-ḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī*, untie a knot from my tongue. The word is the same, and so is the logic: a knot is something tied that the one tied by it cannot untie himself.

That is what makes the image right for the harm in this verse. The person affected by knot-work is, by definition, not the one who tied the knot and cannot find it. His tongue, his marriage, his health, his mind may be bound from a place he cannot reach, and the only remedy is an agency greater than both parties.

The Qur'an uses the same idea for the social bond at 2:102 — the rift between husband and wife is a loosening of a knot that was tied in the marriage contract. Tying and untying are the book's ordinary language for what joins people and what separates them. The blowers on knots are working at that level, in the dark, with a cord.

**The Same Gesture, Used in the Opposite Direction**

The tradition preserves an inversion of this verse's image, and the inversion is exact. ʿĀʾisha reported that every night the Prophet ﷺ joined his hands, breathed into them, recited into them this chapter and the next and *Say: He is Allah—One*, and wiped over as much of his body as he could, beginning with the head and face, three times — Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī.

The gesture is the same one the verse condemns: blowing, in a repeated act, directed at the person who needs help. What differs is the content. Over the knots went an incantation addressed to powers below God; into the palms went the revelation of God, and the breath carried it across the body rather than binding it into a cord.

That is the practical theology of the two chapters at the end of the codex. They do not argue that the world contains no hidden harm. They supply a counter-practice for it, using the same human instruments — breath, repetition, hands — pointed at a different object. The reciter is told to expect the blowing and to do some breathing of his own.
