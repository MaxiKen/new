## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:78

> Then when he saw the sun shining, he said, “This must be my Lord—it is the greatest!” But again when it set, he declared, “O my people! I totally reject whatever you associate ˹with Allah in worship˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Greatest of the Three**

Then when he saw the sun shining, he said, This must be my Lord. The sūrah presents the sun as the strongest candidate in the sequence, and it says so in the text: it is the greatest. The audience venerated the sun as the most powerful visible thing in their world, and the verse allows the claim its full weight before it takes it away.

The description is worth noticing, because the argument in this passage does not proceed by belittling the objects. Abraham calls the moon his Lord and calls the sun the greatest, and each statement raises the candidate as high as the audience would have raised it. What defeats the argument is not a low opinion of the sun but the fact that the sun goes down.

**The Setting That Ends the Case**

But again when it set, he declared his rejection of everything his people associated with God. The sentence moves directly from the observation to the address, and the address is public: O my people! The earlier verses recorded Abraham's words to himself or to his father; this one records him speaking to the community that worshipped the sun, and he says that he is done with the whole set of their deities.

The Book's word for the setting describes disappearance or decline, and its use here clinches the argument the previous verses were making. Whatever is subject to the horizon cannot be the One who placed it there, and the Book states the same principle in its own voice when it commands the reader not to prostrate to the sun or the moon but to the God who created them both (41:37).

**The Public Break With the Community**

The declaration is not a philosophical conclusion but a social act, and the Book treats it that way. Abraham's announcement put him outside the religion of his city, and the Book later records the consequences: his people argued with him, threatened him, and eventually tried to burn him. The sūrah of the Prophets describes the response of the community when they heard his case and decided to act against him, and the sūrah of the Poets gives the summary of his mission in his own words.

The Makkans hearing this sūrah knew what such a declaration cost. The Prophet had been told in this very sūrah to say that he had been forbidden to worship what they invoked and that he would not follow their desires (6:56), and the passage about Abraham supplies the model of what that refusal looked like when it was made by the founder of their tradition.

**"Whatever You Associate With Allah in Worship"**

The phrase Abraham uses names the practice rather than the deities, and the Book does the same throughout the sūrah. The warning is about association as an activity, and the sūrah has described that activity in several forms: assigning to God partners in worship, invoking created things for benefit and harm, and treating the divine claim as divisible among objects the community had chosen.

The Book makes the same point where it reports the idolaters admitting that their gods were names they and their fathers had invented, with no authority from God (12:40). Abraham's declaration follows from that: what he rejected was not a set of powerful rivals but a set of names attached to creatures. The word he uses in this verse states the category, which is why the sentence applies to every generation that has made the same arrangement.

**The Pattern the Book Repeats**

The Book records this declaration of disownment in other places, and it holds Abraham up as the example of it. The sūrah of the Tested describes Abraham and those with him saying to their people that they dissociate themselves from them and from whatever they worship besides God, and that the enmity between them will last until the people believe in God alone (60:4). The Book also connects Abraham's descendants with the same posture when it tells the Prophet that those who follow him have Abraham as the closest of people to him.

The pattern has a cost that the sūrah does not hide. The Book records that Abraham's people answered him with argument and with force, and it records his patience under both. What the verse establishes is that the separation happened first, in public, and was stated in the plainest terms.

**Sun Worship in the Book's Accounts**

The Book records the worship of the sun as the practice of another ancient people, described in the report of the hoopoe that Solomon received: I found her and her people prostrating to the sun instead of God, with Satan having made their deeds appealing and hindered them from the right way (27:24). The verse treats the practice as a familiar error rather than as an exotic one, and it places the sun where the sūrah has placed it, among the created things that human beings have taken as lords.

The Book also describes the end of the sun's course, since the objects the audience venerated are among the first to be extinguished on the day of judgment. The sūrah of the Resurrection says that the moon will be dimmed and the sun and the moon will be brought together (75:8–9), and the sūrah of the Folding Up opens with the sun being put out and the stars falling (81:1–2). Whatever a community has worshipped from the sky is thus shown in the Book to be waiting for its own end.

**From Observation to Allegiance**

Read as the end of the sequence, the verse shows the transition from a reasoning exercise to a religious position. Abraham began by watching the sky and asking what deserved his worship, and he ends by announcing to his people that he has left their religion. The sūrah treats the two halves as one act, and the next verse states the positive content of what he chose.

The declaration continues into that verse without a break, and the final words of the sentence state the identity Abraham claims for himself before his people. What he has chosen is described in the next line with a word that the Book uses for him more than for any other figure.
