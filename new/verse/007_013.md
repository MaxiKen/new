## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:13

> Allah said, “Then get down from Paradise! It is not for you to be arrogant here. So get out! You are truly one of the disgraced.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Descend**

The verse reports the sentence passed on Iblīs, and the command to descend is given in the same terms in the sūrah of the Cow, where the command is addressed to him and to Adam and his wife (2:36), and in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā, where it is given to the first human pair (20:123). The descent in front of us removes Iblīs from the Garden, and the sūrah later states that he retained his presence there long enough to tempt the first pair, since the temptation is recounted in the verses that follow this exchange.

The Book's accounts of the scene agree that the refusal was answered with expulsion. In the sūrah of Ṣād, the sentence includes the curse that rests on Iblīs until the day of judgment (38:78), and in the sūrah of the Rocky Tract the same elements appear. The verse in front of us states the removal and names the character that produced it.

**Arrogance Has No Place There**

The verse states that it is not for Iblīs to be arrogant in the Garden, and the phrase gives the reason for the sentence. The Garden is a place of obedience and humility, and the one who refuses a command out of pride cannot remain in it. The commentary tradition draws from the verse the conclusion that arrogance is a barrier to the paradisal state, and it cites the Book's repeated warnings against those who are too proud to worship.

The Book's descriptions of the condemned include the proud among them, and the sūrah of the Heights later states that those who deny the signs and are too proud to accept them will not enter the Garden (7:40). The verse in front of us names the pride that produced the first refusal, and the chapter's account then shows the same pride operating in the audience it addresses.

**The Humiliation Named**

The verse's final phrase states that Iblīs is among the humbled, and the Book uses the word for those who are brought low. The sūrah of the Cow records the curse that accompanies his expulsion, and the sūrah of Ṣād states that the curse will remain on him until the day of judgment (38:78). The humiliation is the direct consequence of the claim of superiority that the previous verse reports.

The contrast between the claim and the outcome gives the account its force. The one who argued that fire is nobler than clay is described as disgraced and expelled, and the Book later reports his vow to lead humanity astray as the activity of a creature whose own position is settled. The verse in front of us states the reversal in three words.

**The Pattern of Pride in the Book**

The Book's warnings against pride run through its accounts of the communities that rejected their messengers, and the sūrah of the Heights will describe those communities in its middle section. The people of ʿĀd refused the message and relied on their strength, the people of Thamūd relied on their carved dwellings, and the people of Pharaoh relied on their kingdom. The commentary tradition treats each of these as a version of Iblīs's reasoning, since each set a claim of status above the command of God.

The sūrah of the Bee states that God does not like the arrogant, and the sūrah of the Troops states that those who are too proud to worship will enter Hell in humiliation (16:23; 40:60). This verse supplies the first and clearest instance of the pattern, and the sūrah returns to it when it describes those who treat the signs with disdain.

**Grace and Repentance Contrasted**

The account in front of us is completed by the response that Adam and his wife give later in the same passage, when they admit their wrong and ask for forgiveness (7:23). The two responses to the same kind of failure produce the sūrah's teaching about repentance, and the commentary tradition sets the pride of Iblīs against the humility of Adam.

The Book's treatment of repentance in other passages rests on the same distinction. The sūrah of the Cave states that God is forgiving to the one who repents and believes and does right, and the sūrah of the Troops tells those who have exceeded all limits against their own souls not to despair of the mercy of God (39:53). The expulsion in front of us is the answer given to the one who preferred an argument to an apology.

**What the Verse Says to the Audience**

The audience of the sūrah lived in a society in which claims of lineage, wealth, and standing settled most questions, and the sūrah addresses those claims throughout its argument. The passage in front of us states that the first refusal of a divine command was made on exactly such grounds, and it shows where the reasoning led. The reader who recognizes the reasoning is given the warning in advance of the sūrah's own account of the arrogant communities.

The Book's practice of teaching through the fate of the earlier generations appears throughout the sūrah, and this scene is the first of its narratives. The status that Iblīs claimed was real in his own estimation and worthless in the scales of the command, and the chapter's remaining passages apply the same measure to the great ones of Makkah and to the peoples that preceded them.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands thirteenth in the chapter, and it concludes the exchange that began with the report of the prostration and the refusal. The verses that follow report the request of Iblīs for delay and the grant of it, and then his vow to lie in wait for the descendants of Adam. The scene in front of us is the moment at which the antagonist of the sūrah's human story is separated from the company of the obedient.

The reader has been given the command, the refusal, the excuse, and the sentence. The sūrah then turns to what Iblīs does with the time that remains to him, and the account of his activity explains the warnings that the sūrah has given about the protectors taken in place of God.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports the request of Iblīs that his end be delayed until the day of the resurrection (7:14), and the verse after it reports the grant of that delay (7:15). The request is made by the one who has just been sentenced, and it is granted, and the consequence is that the enmity between him and the descendants of Adam has the whole span of the world in which to operate.

The two verses together state the arrangement under which the sūrah's later warnings make sense. The tempter is not destroyed at the outset; he is given time, and the human being is given the revelation and the reckoning. The sūrah's reader is placed in the middle of that arrangement, and the passage that follows states the tempter's plan in his own words.
