## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:272

> You are not responsible for people's guidance ˹O Prophet˺—it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills. Whatever you ˹believers˺ spend in charity, it is for your own good—as long as you do so seeking the pleasure of Allah. Whatever you donate will be paid back to you in full, and you will not be wronged.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Guidance Relinquished**

The verse opens with a sentence addressed to the Prophet that reads, in its position, as a correction: *You are not responsible for people's guidance — it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills*. The commentators supply the occasions, and all three concern charity at the boundary of the community. In al-Qurṭubī's first account, the Prophet had been giving charity to all who asked, and when the Muslim poor grew numerous he restricted the giving to Muslims — whereupon the verse arrived to lift the restriction. In the second, a Companion woman — the narrations name Asmāʾ bint Abī Bakr in the related reports — had idolater grandparents asking her for support, and she declined to give until she asked the Prophet; the verse was the answer. In the third, Companions with relatives among the Jewish tribe of Qurayẓah refused them charity unless they converted. The three occasions converge on one question: does the recipient's faith condition the gift? And the verse's opening sentence answers by removing the question's premise — the giver is not the guide; guidance is God's assignment of Himself; the charity's legitimacy therefore cannot depend on a determination the giver has no authority to make.

**Charity Uncoupled from Identity**

The commentators state the verse's legal achievement in one formula: it uncouples charity from religious identity. The giving legislated here is not a mission-instrument — it does not purchase conversion, pressure it, or reward it — and it is not a loyalty-payment restricted to the in-group. The Qur'an's wider legislation confirms the uncoupling: the sūrah of the Examined Woman permits and commands the ordinary justice — *Allah does not forbid you from dealing kindly and fairly with those who have neither fought nor driven you out of your homes* (60:8) — and the verse here extends the kindness-and-justice to the purse specifically. The occasions' shared logic is the verse's second sentence: since guidance belongs to God's will, the giver who conditions his charity on the recipient's guidance is attempting to administer a province he does not govern — using the gift as leverage on a door only God opens. The tradition's practical ruling follows the verse directly: the obligatory alms run to the community's own poor, while the voluntary ṣadaqah crosses every boundary of belief — the idolater grandparent, the Qurayẓah relative, the asked-stranger all stand inside the verse's permission, and the giver's discernment is directed at the need, not the creed.

**The Night Charity of the Unknowing Hand**

The ḥadīth the exegetes cite at this verse tests the uncoupling at its most extreme. A man resolved to give charity and went out with his alms at night — and placed them, unknowing, in a thief's hand; the city talked in the morning, and he gave again, and the night's recipient was an adulteress; and the third night, a rich man. Each time the talk ran and each time he was answered: your charity is accepted — and perhaps, the reports reason, the gift will deter the thief from his thieving, prompt the rich man to give of his own wealth, and call the adulteress away from her sin. The story is the verse performed: the giver's knowledge of the recipients was zero, their worthiness was, by every social measure, negative, and the acceptance was total each time — because the deed's validity ran on the giver's intention and God's knowledge, not on the recipient's resume. The commentators draw the ruling the verse states as theology: *whatever you donate will be paid back to you in full* — the payment attaches to the spending, not to the spending's target, and the guidance-questions the giver cannot answer are precisely the questions the payment does not ask.

**For Your Own Good**

The verse's middle sentence turns from the recipient to the giver's ledger: *whatever you spend in charity, it is for your own good*. The commentators ground it in the Qur'an's standing principle — the sūrah of Explained in Detail states it flatly: *whoever does good, it is to their own benefit* (41:46) — and the principle does double work here. It answers the occasions' hidden arithmetic: the giving to the idolater-relative was not a loss to the community's boundary but a gain to the giver's own soul, since the deed's return is deposited with the doer whatever the recipient does with it. And it states the verse's condition: *as long as you do so seeking the pleasure of Allah* — the self-benefit runs on the God-direction, the same riḍwān-motive the garden-parable named (2:265) and the sūrah of Man's righteous voice in its purest form: we feed you only for God's sake, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you (76:9). The Face-of-God idiom the commentators attach here — spending ابتغاء وجه الله, seeking God's Face — is the section's highest motive-language, used at 2:272, 30:38–39, and 76:9: the gift whose direction is God's Face alone, with the recipient's identity, gratitude, and guidance all released from the transaction.

**Paid in Full, Not Wronged**

The verse's closing sentences are its contract-law: *whatever you donate will be paid back to you in full, and you will not be wronged*. The commentators read the fullness-clause against the section's earlier mechanics: the payment is the reward of the Hereafter, delivered without deficiency — the same custody the un-reminding givers were promised (their reward *with their Lord*, 2:262) now stated as a completed settlement. The wa-lā tuẓlamūn — you will not be wronged — is the contract's guarantee against the two frauds the section has been legislating: the fraud of the audience, which pays the show-off in perishable praise and leaves the ledger empty (2:264), and the fraud of the recipient's ingratitude, which the giver might fear has wasted the gift. Neither touches the payment: the gift to the thief is paid, the gift to the ungrateful is paid, the gift nobody saw is paid — because the payer is the Knower of the verse before, and the payment runs on His record rather than on the transaction's earthly reception. The verse's whole structure is thus a release: the giver is released from the guidance-question, from the worthiness-question, from the audience-question, and from the ingratitude-question — released into the single condition that remains, the Face-seeking — and every release is anchored in the two closing verbs: paid, in full, with no wrong done.

**The Verse in the Section's Architecture**

Positioned between the secret-giving ranking and the portrait of the dignified poor, this verse is the section's boundary-legislation: it defines whom the giving may cross (everyone, since guidance is not the giver's province), what the giving returns to (the giver's own good, under God's ledger), and what the giving requires (the Face-direction alone). The recipient-verse that follows will describe the specific poor the community must learn to recognize — the constrained, the silent, the marked — and the guidance-verse stands before it deliberately: before the section teaches the discrimination of need, it abolishes the discrimination of creed, so that the recognition commanded next operates on poverty and dignity rather than on belonging. The occasions of revelation give the sequence its historical edge — a community at war with some of the very relatives its charity-ethics now protects — and the verse's answer is the answer the whole section has been building toward: the gift is between the giver and God; the recipient stands in it as need, not as verdict; and the guidance of every heart involved, including the giver's, remains where the verse's first sentence placed it — with the One who guides whomever He wills.
