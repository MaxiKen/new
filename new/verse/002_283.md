## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:283

> If you are on a journey and a scribe cannot be found, then a security can be taken. If you trust one another, then ˹there is no need for a security, but˺ the debtor should honour this trust ˹by repaying the debt˺—and let them fear Allah, their Lord. And do not conceal the testimony, for whoever conceals it, their hearts are indeed sinful. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Journey Exception**

The documentation-legislation receives its case of impossibility: *If you are on a journey and a scribe cannot be found*. The commentators extend the clause's reach with a recorded variant reading — the absence is not only of the scribe but of the means: no writer available, or no materials to write with (al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī). The road is the verse's imagined scene, and it is exactly the scene where the deferred transaction most needs its machinery and least can have it: the caravan deal struck at a halting-place, the loan contracted between travelers, the forward-sale concluded far from the town's literate class and its paper. The verse does not suspend the transaction because the document is impossible; it substitutes — *then a security can be taken* — and the substitution preserves the contract-legislation's whole purpose through a change of instrument: where the written record cannot anchor the term, a held object anchors the debt.

**The Pledge in Hand**

The security named is the rahin maqbūḍah — the pledge in hand, the collateral physically seized and held by the creditor: property given by the debtor, or by the forward-sale's seller, as guarantee in the absence of the written, witnessed contract. The fiqh of the pledge that grew from this clause is one of the tradition's settled chapters — the pledge held, not merely promised; its value answering the debt at default; its care incumbent on the holder — and the Prophetic practice the tradition preserves gives the institution its most cited instance: the report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī that the Prophet died with his shield pledged to a Jewish merchant of Medina against thirty ṣāʿ of barley for his household's food. The commentators draw the instance's lessons freely: the pledge's legitimacy across religious lines, its compatibility with the highest personal station, and its ordinariness — the Messenger's own household ran its grocery on the instrument this verse legislates. The pledge-in-hand thus completes the documentation-verse's architecture: the written contract for the settled community with its scribes, the seized collateral for the road without them, and the same deferred-debt protected across both geographies by whichever anchor is available.

**If You Trust One Another**

The verse's second provision rises above both instruments: *If you trust one another, then there is no need for a security, but the debtor should honour this trust by repaying the debt*. The commentators debate the provision's legal rank, and the debate is recorded in both directions: some held that this trust-clause abrogated the documentation-rules of the preceding verse — the higher ethic superseding the machinery; the many, whose position al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī all record, held that the clause applies specifically to the journey-case, and that in ordinary circumstances the written contract remains the norm. The majority reading preserves the verse's structure as a ladder rather than a repeal: documentation where possible, pledge where documentation is impossible, and trust — unsecured, undocumented, naked reliance on the other's fidelity — where the parties themselves can carry it. The trust-case's legislation falls entirely on the trusted party: *let him who is trusted deliver his trust, and let him fear Allah his Lord*. With no scribe, no document, no seized object standing behind the debt, the creditor's protection is the debtor's taqwā — and the verse accordingly names that taqwā with its fullest title, God his Lord, the fear that operates where no human instrument does. The ladder's top rung is thus not the absence of law but the law's interiorization: the contract written on the heart the documentation was invented to compensate for.

**Do Not Conceal the Testimony**

The verse's third movement turns from the debt's instruments to its witnesses, and its prohibition is the testimony's concealment: *do not conceal the testimony, for whoever conceals it, their hearts are indeed sinful*. The commentators connect the clause backward to the documentation-verse's summons — the witnesses who must not refuse when called (2:282) — and forward to its rationale: the whole machinery of writing and witnessing exists to make the truth available at the dispute, and the concealing witness defeats the machinery at its terminal point, holding in his memory the very fact the document was built to preserve. The sin-clause's organ is the verse's precision: the concealer's *heart* is named sinful — al-Ṭabarī and the tradition read the attribution as the sin's location and its totality, the heart being the seat whose corruption the concealment constitutes. The Qur'an's testimony-ethics elsewhere carry the same weight — the sūrah's own earlier verse legislated the witnesses' non-refusal and God's knowledge of what they do, and the sūrah of the Starving's picture of the record that leaves nothing out, small or great, unlisted (18:49) is the concealment-clause's eschatological guarantee: the withheld testimony is withheld from men only, and the heart that withheld it is registered.

**The Knower of What You Do**

The verse closes with the knowledge-clause the whole documentation-sequence has been building toward: *And Allah fully knows what you do*. The commentators read it as the sequence's enforcement-summary: the scribe's justice, the dictator's honesty, the witnesses' availability, the pledge-holder's care, the trusted debtor's fidelity, the testifier's disclosure — every actor in the contract-machinery has an interior the documents cannot capture, and the closing name captures it. The clause also answers the verse's ladder-structure with its final consolation: at the top rung, where trust operates without instrument, the creditor who relied and lost is not simply defrauded in an unenforceable deal — the transaction stands recorded where enforcement is perfect; and at every lower rung, where the document or the pledge may still fail (the forged term, the disputed collateral, the pressured witness), the same record stands behind the human machinery as its audit and its guarantee. The knowledge-verse thus completes the sūrah's documentary legislation the way the Day-return verse completed its ribā-legislation (2:281): the human instruments are commanded, calibrated, protected — and then handed over to the Knower, in whose knowledge the contract's final copy has been kept since the moment the scribe's pen, or the trusting hand, first moved.

**The Sequence's Completion**

Read with its two neighbors, this verse completes the sūrah's movement from the economy's morals to the economy's paperwork and back to the paperwork's morals. The ribā-block legislated the debt's ethics — the increase forbidden, the principal protected, the straitened debtor respited, the whole sealed at the Day (2:275–281). The documentation-verse built the debt's instruments — the writing, the scribe, the dictation, the witnessing, the harm-protections (2:282). This verse handles the instruments' limits and their interior: the road without a scribe, the pledge that substitutes, the trust that transcends, the testimony whose concealment is a sin of the heart. The three movements together are the Qur'an's complete treatment of the deferred obligation — its ethics, its documentation, and its conscience — and the verse's own structure enacts the completion: it descends from the ideal machinery to the emergency substitute, ascends from both to naked trust, and closes with the knowledge that holds all three levels equally. The believer who has traveled the sūrah's economy from the devourers' rising to the pledged shield and the trusted word now carries, the verse's closing clause assures him, a ledger kept at every altitude: documented where possible, secured where necessary, trusted where grace allows, and known — in every case, completely — to God.
