## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:173

> Those who were warned, “Your enemies have mobilized their forces against you, so fear them,” the warning only made them grow stronger in faith and they replied, “Allah ˹alone˺ is sufficient ˹as an aid˺ for us and ˹He˺ is the best Protector.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning That Reached Them**

The verse reports what people said to the community on the road: that a force had gathered against them, and that they should be afraid. The commentators identify the speakers in two ways, either travellers who had passed the Makkan contingent and carried the news on to Madinah, or the hypocrites who had been discouraging the community from fighting all along.

The content of the message is a report of the enemy's strength. The men receiving it were wounded, fewer in number than before, and in no condition to meet a renewed attack, and the report was designed to produce the reaction the verse's next clause denies.

**The Reply Recorded in the Verse**

The community's reply was that God is enough for them and that He is an excellent guardian. The phrase has remained one of the prayers most used by Muslims in danger, and the Book uses the same declaration in other passages: in the sūrah about Repentance the Prophet is told to say that God is sufficient for him, and in the sūrah of the Crowds the faithful are described as saying that God is sufficient for them.

The reply's structure is the exact answer to the warning. The report said that a force had gathered; the response named the one on whom the community relied. The sentence does not deny the report and does not discuss the numbers.

**The Effect of the Warning**

The verse states that the warning increased the community in faith, and the statement reverses what the report was meant to achieve. Warnings of this kind work by narrowing a person's attention to the danger; in this case the effect was to turn the community's attention to its reliance.

The Book describes similar reactions in other passages, where the believers are said to have grown firmer when they were tested. The sentence is a statement about the community's responsiveness, and the Book's praise of it is praise of the way the report was received rather than of the report itself. The sentence is one of the Book's regular teachings about the way faith works: the same event can produce fear in one person and confidence in another according to what each of them trusts.

**The Disposition Behind the Reply**

The sentence describes the community's reaction as an increase in faith rather than as a display of courage, and the distinction matters. The men who went out had been told what they would find, and their reply indicates where their judgement of the situation rested.

The Book does not present their confidence as a calculation about the enemy's willingness to fight. The Makkans had been eager to return, and the community knew it; its security rested on something other than an assessment of the balance of forces, and the verse's wording reflects that. The men who spoke this reply had been asked to be afraid, and their answer shows what they had already decided to rely on before the question was put to them.

**The Deterrent and Its Results**

The reports describe how the threat was turned back. The message carried to the Makkans was that the Madinans were pursuing them in far greater numbers than before, and the many campfires lit within sight of the enemy's camp supported the claim.

The Makkans concluded that another engagement would be unwise and abandoned their plan, and the believers returned without a battle. The enemy turned back toward its own city, and the community came home without further losses, which is the outcome the next verse describes in the language of blessing and bounty. The verse before the reader records the community's reply at the moment of the warning, and the fruits of that reply are described in the verse that follows.

**The Prayer in the Community's Practice**

The declaration that God suffices and is an excellent guardian has been used by Muslims at the outset of difficulties, and the Book contains it in more than one place. The sūrah of the Prophets and the sūrah about Repentance both use the same formula in different situations.

Its frequent repetition in the Book has made it one of the most recognisable sentences of prayer in the community's life. The verse before the reader places it on the lips of a wounded company on the road from Uḥud, and the tradition's use of it has been shaped by that setting ever since.

**A Reading of the Verse's Wider Application**

One reading of the passage draws a wider lesson from it: that a person whose concern is with God should not be discouraged when people devoted to worldly interests unite to oppose him, since their opposition is a sign of the difference between the two orientations rather than of the man's error.

The reading is applied to the interior life rather than to the battlefield, and the commentators who offered it did so without displacing the verse's plain sense. The Book's sentences about trust have been used in this way throughout the community's teaching, and the verse's wording supports the extension.

**What the Verse Teaches About Fear**

The verse closes the exchange without adding a threat of its own, and the next verse states the command that follows from it. The community had been asked to be afraid and had answered with a statement about sufficiency, and the sūrah treats that answer as the correct one.

The teaching is taken up in the verse that follows, where the source of the fear is identified and the community is directed to the fear that belongs to its faith. The passage thus moves from a report of danger to a discipline about what a person should be afraid of, and the sūrah's dealings with the community's insecurity reach their sharpest expression in the sentence about Satan and his friends.
