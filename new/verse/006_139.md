## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:139

> They ˹also˺ say, “The offspring of this cattle is reserved for our males and forbidden to our females; but if it is stillborn, they may all share it.” He will repay them for their falsehood. Surely He is All-Wise, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rule About the Offspring**

They also say, The offspring of this cattle is reserved for our males and forbidden to our females. The verse records another of the community's rules, and this one governs the young of the animals they had set apart. The males of the community were permitted to eat the offspring, and the women were not, which made the rule a matter of the household's internal order as much as of food.

The Book records the same kind of regulation elsewhere. The sūrah of the Table lists the set-apart animals of the Arabian system, naming the animals freed in the name of the gods, those dedicated to them, and the males reserved from work and from slaughter, and it states that the disbelievers invented these categories (5:103). The system described in the verse in front of us belongs to the same family of practices.

**The Exception for the Stillborn**

But if it is stillborn, they may all share it. The sentence states the qualification the community attached to its rule, and the qualification exposes the arbitrary character of the whole arrangement. The same animal was reserved for the men if it was delivered alive and shared by everyone if it was not, which means that the permission depended on a circumstance that had nothing to do with the eater.

The commentators noted the inconsistency as part of the sūrah's argument. A rule that distinguished between people by sex and then dissolved the distinction in the case of a dead animal was not a law but a set of inherited usages, and the Book's presentation lets the reader see the incoherence without adding a comment.

**The Charge Reiterated**

He will repay them for their falsehood. The sentence repeats the formula that closed the previous verse, and the repetition binds the two rules together as a single offence. The audience's system attributed to God a set of regulations that He had not given, and the Book states that the attribution will be answered by the One who knows what they said.

The Book's treatment of such attributions appears in several passages. The sūrah of the Bee tells the audience not to utter falsehoods about what God has permitted and forbidden and to attribute the rulings to Him (16:116), and the sūrah of Yūnus asks the audience whether God has given them permission for what they have made lawful or unlawful, or whether they are inventing the rulings themselves (10:59). The verses in front of us state the practice those passages address.

**The Attributes at the End of the Verse**

Surely He is All-Wise, All-Knowing. The two names answer the regulations the verse has described. The wisdom belongs to the One whose rulings are ordered to their purposes, and the knowledge belongs to the One who knows what the audience said and why they said it. The closing formula stands in deliberate contrast to the rules just recorded, which were neither wise nor founded on knowledge.

The same pair of names appears at the end of the sūrah's accounts of the reckoning, where the judgement is attributed to the One who knows what each person did. The sūrah's use of the names in this verse therefore connects the dietary system to the larger account of accountability that runs through the chapter.

**What the Rule Revealed About the Community**

The regulation described here used food as a way of ordering the community's members, and the distinction it drew fell along lines of sex and household standing rather than along lines of conduct. The Book's own regulations in this sūrah run in another direction: what is permitted is what God has permitted, what is forbidden is what God has forbidden, and the rules apply to the believers alike.

The sūrah of the Cattle's later passage about the gardens instructs the community to eat of the fruit they bear and to pay what is due at the harvest, without adding any of the distinctions the audience had built into its system (6:141). The verses in front of us show what the message was replacing, and the contrast is presented in the book's own account of provision rather than in a comment on the audience's customs.

**The Place of the Passage in the Sūrah**

The passage that begins with the shares assigned to God and the idols (6:136) and continues through the killing of children, the reservation of the herds, and the rule about the offspring ends with the declaration that those who killed their children and forbade what God provided have strayed and are not guided (6:140). The four verses form a survey of the audience's dietary and domestic system, and each of them states a practice and the judgement it receives.

The sūrah's argument has been building toward the declaration of what God has actually forbidden and commanded, which appears in the long passage beginning at 6:151. What the passage in front of us provides is the negative side of that instruction: the system that the revelation came to replace, presented in the audience's own terms.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that those who murdered their own children foolishly out of ignorance and forbade what God has provided have certainly strayed and are not guided (6:140). The sentence gathers the offences described in the preceding verses, and it passes the final judgement on the community that built its religion on them.

The sūrah then turns to a series of declarations about the created order and to the announcement of what God has actually forbidden and commanded, and it concludes with the testimony of the Book. The verse in front of us completes the survey of the inherited system, and the reader who has followed the passage now knows what the message had come to correct.
