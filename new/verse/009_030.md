## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:30

> The Jews say, “Ezra is the son of Allah,” while the Christians say, “The Messiah is the son of Allah.” Such are their baseless assertions, only parroting the words of earlier disbelievers. May Allah condemn them! How can they be deluded ˹from the truth˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Critiques Claims About Divine Sonship**

Verse 30 presents two theological claims and rejects the idea that a created servant can be God's son. The Christian claim about the Messiah is a central question in the Qur'an's dialogue with Christian theology. The statement about Ezra has generated more discussion because it is not a standard description of Jewish belief in all communities and all periods. Early exegetes transmitted reports that it referred to a particular group or to a historical claim that later disappeared. The text itself gives the theological principle without requiring every Jewish person to hold the same statement.

The verse therefore needs precision. It criticizes attribution of sonship to Allah, not every Jew or Christian as an undifferentiated person. The Qur'an elsewhere speaks respectfully of revelation given to the People of the Book and acknowledges upright members among them. Criticism of a doctrine should not become permission to falsify the beliefs of every individual.

**“Son” Cannot Be Used as a Harmless Metaphor**

Some theologians tried to explain divine sonship as a title of honor or closeness rather than physical generation. The Qur'an rejects the language because it changes the relation between Creator and creature. Allah is not one member of a family, nor is He dependent on a lineage. The Qur'an says He neither begets nor is begotten (112:3), a concise statement of divine uniqueness.

The issue is not a quarrel over a word while everyone agrees on the meaning. Religious language shapes worship. If a prophet or righteous person is treated as a divine offspring, the servant's role begins to compete with Allah's exclusive right to worship. The verse protects tawḥīd by refusing a form of speech that, however reverent its intention, assigns to Allah a category belonging to created beings.

**A Historical Claim Requires Careful Evidence**

Exegetes offered different explanations for the Ezra statement. Some named an individual associated with exaggeration. Some said that a group of Jews once held the view and that it did not survive. Others linked the elevation of Ezra to his role in preserving or restoring the Torah. These explanations differ, and the verse should not be used to make a claim about all Jews without evidence.

The Qur'an itself often describes beliefs through the speech of a representative group. A statement can be true of the people being addressed in a particular setting without being a census of every member. That method requires the reader to ask what claim is being corrected and whom the correction reaches. Here the doctrinal correction is clear: divine sonship is false. The historical scope of the Ezra statement remains a matter for careful scholarship rather than careless generalization.

**Words From the Mouth Need Proof**

The verse calls the statements baseless assertions and says they resemble the words of earlier disbelievers. This does not mean that every religious claim is worthless because it is spoken. It means that a claim about Allah cannot rest on inherited language alone. It must be tested against revelation and sound reason. A sentence repeated through generations does not become true merely because it has become familiar.

That principle applies inside the Muslim community as well. Muslims can repeat slogans about Allah, prophets, saints, or religious authorities without asking whether the words preserve the Creator's uniqueness. The verse invites a disciplined theology in which claims are checked, meanings are made clear, and honor does not replace evidence. Reverence for a holy person becomes misguidance when it grants that person what belongs only to Allah.

**The Condemnation Is About Delusion, Not Mockery**

The phrase may Allah condemn them is followed by a question about how they can be deluded from the truth. The tone is severe, but it is directed at a theological error that distorts worship. The question expresses astonishment that people who possess revelation or inherited knowledge can move from honoring a servant to assigning divine status. It is not a command for believers to mock ordinary people or to treat disagreement as permission for abuse.

The Qur'an elsewhere instructs Muslims to debate the People of the Book in the best manner, except with those who act unjustly (29:46). This cross-reference gives a method for applying verse 30. The believer should state the difference clearly, avoid false attribution, and speak with justice. A doctrine can be rejected without the opponent being dehumanized. The Qur'an's firmness about Allah's uniqueness must be joined to the justice it demands in every conversation. Truth becomes harder to reject when its defenders refuse exaggeration and speak about people with accuracy. A fair description does not weaken a theological argument. It shows that the believer is seeking Allah's judgment rather than collecting easy victories over a misrepresented opponent.

**Truth Is Not Protected by Misrepresentation**

The verse's modern significance includes the ethics of interfaith speech. A Muslim who criticizes Christian belief must describe it accurately rather than attack a caricature. A Muslim who discusses Jewish history must not turn a disputed report about Ezra into a statement about every Jew. Accuracy is part of defending truth. False claims weaken the very case they are meant to strengthen.

The verse also warns against the religious temptation to make created authorities absolute. A rabbi, monk, scholar, teacher, or spiritual guide may be honored and followed within the bounds of revelation. None may receive worship. The next verse will examine that form of misplaced obedience. Verse 30 prepares the ground by insisting that Allah's uniqueness is not negotiable, while honest speech about other communities remains a duty.
