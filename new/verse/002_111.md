## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:111

> The Jews and Christians each claim that none will enter Paradise except those of their own faith. These are their desires. Reply, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Show ˹me˺ your proof if what you say is true.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim Stated Plainly**

The verse reports what two communities asserted about the Garden, and it places the assertions side by side so that their symmetry is visible. Each group reserved Paradise for itself, and the effect of the arrangement is that two mutually exclusive claims are presented in a single sentence, with no arbiter named by either side.

**Desires Rather Than Proofs**

The sentence calls the claims desires, and the term in Arabic covers hopes and longings that a person entertains without evidence. The Book elsewhere uses the same word for the aspirations of people who expect a favourable outcome because of who they are, and the sūrah's use of it here reduces the two doctrines to the wish that produced them.

That reduction is the verse's argument. A claim about the Hereafter can be a conclusion drawn from revelation, or it can be the projection of a community's wish about itself, and the Book distinguishes the two by asking which one the claimant can support. The term chosen for the sentence indicates how the Book classifies the claims in question.

**The Parallel Passages**

The Book returns to this position at several points. In the same sūrah it reports the two communities each asserting that the other has nothing to stand on (2:113), and it records a claim that guidance means following only one's own religion (2:135). In the chapter of the table it reports Jews and Christians describing themselves as the beloved children of God, and in the chapter of the congregation it reports a claim to be God's chosen people apart from everyone else (62:6).

The pattern the Book identifies is consistent: a community converts a scripture into a title, and the title then generates claims about the Hereafter that the scripture itself does not make. The sūrah's answer in each case is the same, and it is not an argument about who is right but a demand for evidence.

**Why the Verse Is Kept in the Chapter**

The passage belongs to the sūrah's long treatment of the People of the Book, and it also serves the community receiving the revelation. A Muslim reader who treats his own affiliation as a guarantee has reproduced the position the verse criticizes, and the Book's warnings to the believers in the chapter use the same vocabulary of hope without grounds.

The sentence therefore stands as a test rather than as a verdict on others. What it asks of any community is whether the confidence it holds about its standing before God rests on something God has said or on the comfort of belonging, and the Book's answer to the second kind of confidence is a request for proof that it never supplies.
