## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:142

> when their brother Ṣâliḥ said to them, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ṣāliḥ Speaks as Their Brother**

The verse places brotherhood before the question of fear. Ṣāliḥ is one of Thamûd, sharing its language, environment, and human concerns. His warning is not an abstract announcement delivered from outside the community. It comes through someone whose life is woven into theirs and whose knowledge of their condition cannot be dismissed as ignorance.

Brotherhood gives the appeal tenderness without weakening its force. Ṣāliḥ does not flatter Thamûd or approve its direction simply because he belongs to it. He speaks as a person who wants his people rescued from a danger they cannot see clearly. A loving warning may sound severe when the threatened harm is serious.


The question asks whether they will fear Allah. Taqwa is not mere fright before an approaching disaster. It is an active awareness of God that makes a person guard his choices. Ṣāliḥ calls Thamûd to let that awareness govern worship, ambition, property, and treatment of others.

A community can acknowledge that God exists and still live as though it has no duty toward Him. Taqwa closes that gap. It turns belief into caution, gratitude, and obedience. The question is therefore larger than an emotional response. It asks whether God will have a practical place in the way Thamûd conducts its common life.

**The Messenger Is Close Enough to Be Known**

A local messenger carries a special kind of evidence: the people can compare his claim with the character they have observed. Ṣāliḥ is not asking them to trust an unknown adventurer. They know his family and his conduct. If they reject his warning, they must explain why the message is false rather than simply inventing a reason to distrust his motives.

Quran 7:73 also presents him as Thamûd's brother and describes his call to worship Allah alone. The parallel confirms that the family language is part of the mission's meaning. God sends guidance through a human being who can speak plainly to a human community, not through a voice detached from ordinary life.


Some people hear religious fear as a demand for constant anxiety. Ṣāliḥ's question has a different purpose. Fear of Allah restrains the hand before it harms, the ruler before he abuses authority, and the wealthy person before he turns provision into domination. It is a moral guard placed before the deed, not only a reaction afterward.

This is why the question is addressed to a people known for prosperity. Abundance can reduce a sense of dependence and make restraint seem unnecessary. Taqwa reminds Thamûd that every advantage remains under the Lord's judgment. The secure person is not the one who feels untouchable, but the one who remembers that God sees how security is used.

**Brotherhood Does Not Mean Tribal Loyalty**

Ṣāliḥ's belonging does not mean he will defend Thamûd's customs at the expense of truth. Genuine brotherhood seeks the good of the people, and their good cannot be separated from worshipping God. A messenger may oppose the practices of his own community while continuing to speak with concern for it.

This distinction prevents two distortions. Loyalty must not become silence about injustice, and correction must not become hatred of the people corrected. Ṣāliḥ's question holds both sides together. He remains their brother while asking them to abandon the arrogance that is endangering them.


The question has social force because Thamûd's choices are not private. The people manage land, water, homes, animals, and relations among families. If they fear Allah, they cannot treat every resource as the property of the strongest. They must ask whether their decisions preserve justice and acknowledge the rights of those with less power.

The religious call therefore does not withdraw from practical affairs. It supplies the standard by which practical affairs are judged. A settlement can be well planned and still be corrupt if its prosperity depends on cruelty. Taqwa makes technical ability answerable to moral purpose.

**The Question Leaves Room for Return**

Ṣāliḥ asks rather than announcing that Thamûd is beyond hope. A question invites recognition and leaves the hearer responsible for the answer. The community can still move from confidence in itself to reverence for God. Warning is therefore an opening before it becomes a record of refusal.

That opening should not be mistaken for weakness in the messenger. Ṣāliḥ knows that judgment is real, but he does not rush to it. He gives his people a clear chance to reconsider. His brotherhood makes the opportunity personal: the one asking them to fear Allah is one of their own.


Thamûd could answer with a word of agreement and continue its old practices. Taqwa must be visible in changed decisions. The question reaches beyond speech to what people build, consume, protect, and permit. Fear is genuine when it limits the actions that pride once treated as normal.

For the reader, the verse asks whether closeness to a reminder has become a reason to discount it. Ṣāliḥ's familiarity should have made listening easier, not less necessary. The Lord's claim arrives through a brother, and the decisive issue is whether that appeal will produce reverent conduct.

A community does not honor its own people by silencing the one who warns it. It honors them by judging the warning fairly and allowing truth to correct inherited confidence. Brotherhood is therefore a bridge to accountability, not a shelter from it. The question remains compassionate because Ṣāliḥ wants Thamûd to live, but compassion would be empty if it concealed the danger.

A warning from within a community can be harder to hear than one delivered by a stranger. The familiar voice knows where pride is strongest, and its closeness makes evasion less comfortable. Thamûd must not confuse personal discomfort with evidence against the message. The question is gentle in its aim, but firm about the Lord's right to be feared.
