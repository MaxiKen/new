## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:69

> Some of the People of the Book wish to mislead you ˹believers˺. They mislead none but themselves, yet they fail to perceive it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Wish Directed at the Believers**

The verse reports an intention rather than an act. Some among the People of the Book want to lead the believers astray — the verb describes a wish to pull a person off a road — and the sūrah states the desire without describing any particular attempt.

The wording keeps the matter at the level of motive while making the motive known. A community told that the pursuit of its members is hoped for by others has been warned about something that no incident will necessarily reveal, and the Book supplies the information so that the believers do not have to infer it from events. The sūrah's audience was a small community in a city where it negotiated with older groups, and the verse tells its members what is being thought about them behind the arguments.

**"They Mislead None but Themselves"**

The second half of the sentence reverses the direction of the harm. The wish to mislead is real, and the Book says its outcome falls on those who hold it.

The Book states this reversal repeatedly, and with a variety of images. The hypocrites are described as hoping to deceive God and the believers while deceiving only themselves (2:9); the community in the desert is told that those who broke faith wronged nobody but themselves (2:57); and elsewhere the Book says that a person's plotting encircles its author. The claim is not that the intended victims escape harm in every case, since the verse exists precisely to warn them. It is that the deeper damage of an act of corruption settles on the one who commits it, and the sūrah places that fact before it names the parties.

**The Envy Behind the Wish**

The sūrah's other account of the same situation names the motive: a group of the People of the Book wish to turn the believers back into disbelievers out of envy in their own souls, after the truth has become clear to them (2:109). The verse under discussion does not repeat the diagnosis, but the passages belong together, and the commentators read them as one report.

The identification of envy as the engine is what makes the wish intelligible. The groups concerned had argued against the message on doctrinal grounds; the Book's account says the argument was not the reason for their resistance. The mention of envy also sets a limit on any discussion held with them, since a disputant whose real concern is the standing of his community cannot be persuaded by evidence about the standing of a prophet. What the sūrah asks of its readers is that they understand the nature of the opposition they face.

**"They Fail to Perceive It"**

The verse closes by noting that these people do not notice what is happening to themselves. The Arabic describes an absence of awareness rather than of intelligence, and the Book uses it for the condition of people who cannot see the consequences of their own conduct.

That is the sūrah's answer to the appearance of competence. The groups being described were learned, established, and in possession of scriptures; they understood the arguments they were making. What they lacked was the perception to see that the damage they were arranging for others was being inflicted on their own position, and the Book's diagnosis is that the instrument they trusted — their standing and their learning — was exactly what their conduct was eroding. The warning in the verse is therefore for them as much as for the believers, and the sūrah continues to address them in the passages that follow.

**What the Verse Asks of the Believers**

The verse does not instruct the believers to withdraw from the members of the other communities, and it does not authorise any hostility. It tells them what is wished for them and what the wishing costs the person who does it.

What a reader is meant to take from that is a specific kind of caution. The sūrah has just told its audience that Abraham belongs to those who follow his way rather than to a lineage, and it now tells them that there are people who would like to detach them from what they hold. The two facts together produce the disposition the rest of the passage will appeal to: hold the religion firmly, argue fairly where argument is offered, and understand that the pressure on the community is an intention rather than an accident. The following verses address the parties responsible, and they ask them why they behave as they do.

**A Charge Addressed in Public**

The final note of this verse is that the Book addresses the group directly in the verses that follow. Having told the believers who wishes them ill, the sūrah turns to the other party and asks them three questions in succession, and the questions are not rhetorical in the sense of being unanswerable.

That combination of disclosure and address is characteristic of the Book's handling of opponents. The community is told what it faces, and the opposing party is told that its conduct is visible and that reasons are being requested. Nothing is kept back from either side, and no accusation is made that the accused is not invited to answer.

A reader can also measure the distance between the wish reported here and its outcome. The plan attributed to the group was to detach the believers from their religion, and the sūrah's own account of the community's later history records the opposite movement: the message travelled beyond the city, and the communities that had argued with it produced scholars who carried parts of its learning into their own traditions. The Book's remark that the planners mislead none but themselves is not a prediction of an easy outcome for the believers; it is a statement that a plan aimed at someone else's faith has its first effect on the faith of the person making it.
