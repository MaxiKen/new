## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47

> Similarly ˹to earlier messengers˺, We have revealed to you a Book ˹O Prophet˺. ˹The faithful of˺ those to whom We gave the Scriptures believe in it, as do some of these ˹pagan Arabs˺. And none denies Our revelations except the ˹stubborn˺ disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Method of Revelation Across the Ages**

The verse opens by placing the revelation given to the Prophet in a sequence rather than presenting it as an unprecedented event, and the Book does this wherever it discusses the earlier scriptures. The community of Madinah is told in another chapter to affirm what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the descendants, and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets, without distinguishing between any of them (2:136). What arrives with the Qur'an is therefore described as the continuation of a procedure God has been running for centuries, and the sentence reminds the reader that the People of the Book addressed in the previous verse are not outsiders to the story but earlier recipients of it.

Calling attention to the method also prevents the message from being treated as a novelty invented for one audience. Guidance reached earlier communities through messengers and books, and the phrase joins the revelation given to the Prophet to that sequence rather than presenting it as an exception to the way God has always addressed people.

**Two Communities That Believe**

The verse names two groups who accepted the Book: faithful members of the communities that had received earlier scriptures, and some of the pagan Arabs among whom the Prophet lived. The first group is attested in other passages that describe People of the Book believing in what was revealed to the Prophet and in what was revealed to them, humble before God and refusing to trade His revelations for a fleeting gain (3:199). The second reference gives the reader a measure of the audience in Makkah and Madinah, where the response was mixed and the verse says so plainly by naming only some of them. The Book does not claim a mass conversion of either community, and the honesty of the description is part of its argument.

Grouping the two communities in one sentence treats their acceptance as a single event in the history of revelation. The earlier communities had their own scriptures and their own scholars, and the fact that some among them recognized the new recitation is offered as evidence that the message belonged to a line rather than to a place.

**The Believers Who Recognized What They Already Had**

Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ reports the reaction of those who had been given knowledge before the revelation reached them: when it is recited to them they declare that they believe in it, that it is the truth from their Lord, and that they had already submitted before it arrived (28:53). Sūrah al-Māʾidah describes the same recognition in more physical terms, with the eyes of the believers overflowing with tears at what they recognize of the truth, and their prayer that they be counted among the witnesses (5:83). These passages matter for the sūrah's argument because they show that acceptance of the Book was not a decision made without antecedents. The people described had been preparing for it by their own reading of what they had been given.

What those believers accepted they had already received in part, which means their faith was an act of recognition rather than of conversion to a stranger's claim. The Book treats such recognition as costly in its own right, since a scholar who endorses a message from outside his community loses standing with the people who trained him.

**Denial as a Stubborn Act**

The final clause states that nobody denies the revelations except the stubborn disbelievers, and the Arabic term carries the sense of covering and of wilful resistance rather than simple unbelief. The sūrah will repeat the same verdict a few verses later in slightly different words, replacing the disbelievers with the wrongdoers (29:49), and the variation suggests that the Book is describing a condition rather than a demographic group. Denial in this vocabulary is an act performed by someone who has been shown something, which is why the Book consistently denies that its evidence was withheld. The verse therefore does not say that most people accept the revelation; it says that those who reject it do so while resisting what they have understood.

**Why the Report of Believers Matters**

A reader under pressure needs to know that the message he is carrying has been accepted by people outside his own circle, and this verse supplies that knowledge at a moment in the sūrah when the surrounding verses are dealing with dispute and objection. The report also has an evidential function. If the revelation were the invention of one man in one city, its acceptance by people trained in earlier scriptures would be a coincidence requiring explanation. The Book returns to this point repeatedly, and it treats the recognition of the believing scholars of earlier communities as a witness alongside its own arguments — a second set of eyes on the same object.

The record serves an argument the sūrah has been pressing throughout. An objection that the recitation is a local invention cannot stand beside the fact that people raised on earlier scriptures accepted it, and the Book keeps such witnesses on file for exactly that reason.

**A Sentence That Distinguishes Conviction From Numbers**

Nothing in the verse measures the truth of the Book by the size of its following, and the contrast with the previous verse's instruction about courteous argument is instructive. The Muslim is told what to say to the People of the Book, not how many of them will agree. The sentence does its work by identifying who accepts the revelation and who rejects it, and it leaves the proportion to the reader's own observation. The Book's confidence in this passage rests on the content of what has been sent down rather than on the standing of the audience, which is why a community that was a minority in its own city could be told to state its position plainly and continue the conversation.

**A Book That Confirms and Supervises**

The Book describes its own relation to the earlier scriptures with a phrase that is easy to read past: it says that what was revealed to the Prophet came with the truth as a confirmation of previous Scriptures and a supreme authority over them, before it orders him to judge by what God has revealed and not to follow their desires (5:48). The verse under discussion depends on that self-description. When a community that has received an earlier scripture believes in this Book, it is not abandoning what it was given; it is accepting a revelation that presents itself as the custodian of what came before. That is the sense in which the acceptance of earlier believers is evidence rather than a curiosity, and it explains why the sūrah can cite them in the middle of an argument about the authenticity of the message.

**The Same Claim in Sūrah al-Baqarah**

Sūrah al-Baqarah gives the widest version of the declaration, listing the prophets and the books by name and requiring belief in all of them without distinction (2:136). The placing of that verse is significant: it comes in a passage addressed to a community being drawn into disputes about religious genealogy, and it answers the disputes by refusing to rank the messengers. The verse under discussion makes the same move in compressed form. What it establishes is that the Book given to the Prophet belongs in a library rather than in a market stall, and that the community which holds it is joining a conversation that began long before its own appearance. That framing shapes how the community is expected to conduct itself in the disputes that follow, and it explains why the sūrah can move from a rule about courteous argument directly into a report about who accepted the message.
