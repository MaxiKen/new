## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:28

> We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a deliverer of good news and a warner to all of humanity, but most people do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Restriction and an Expansion in One Sentence**

The verse is built on a limiting particle and a universal noun, and the two pull in opposite directions. *Wa mā arsalnāka illā* — We did not send you except as. Everything after that is a boundary on the office: he is a bringer of good news and a warner, and nothing else is claimed. Then the sentence widens without warning: *kāffatan li-l-nās*, to the whole of humankind. So the same clause narrows the function and extends the audience to its outer limit. This is a characteristic Qur'anic combination and it answers two different objections at once. To those inflating the messenger's status, it says his role is delivery. To those shrinking his relevance to a quarrel among Meccan clans, it says the addressee is the human race. Neither correction could be made without the other, since a modest office with a universal scope is a different thing from either modesty or grandeur alone.

**kāffah: The Word That Can Modify Either Side**

*Kāffah* is the interesting word, and Arabic grammarians disagreed about what it attaches to. Read as modifying *al-nās*, it means the people in their entirety — sent to all of them, which is how this translation takes it. Read as modifying the manner of sending, it comes from the root *k-f-f*, to restrain or to gather in, and yields the sense of being sent comprehensively, combining the two functions of announcement and warning in one mission. The first reading has the stronger support from parallel verses. Al-Anbiyāʾ says he was sent *"as a mercy for the whole world"* (21:107); al-Nisāʾ says *"We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ as a messenger to ˹all˺ people"* (4:79); al-Aʿrāf has him announce it in his own voice. The second reading is not therefore wrong, and nothing forces a choice, since a mission that combines both functions is precisely what a universal audience requires.

**Good News Before Warning, Every Time**

The order of the two functions is fixed in the Qur'an and worth noticing. *Bashīr* comes first, then *nadhīr*. The root of the first is *b-sh-r*, which also gives *bashrah*, the skin — news so good that it shows in the face. A *bashīr* is the runner who arrives ahead of a caravan with word that it is safe. *Nadhīr* is from *n-dh-r*, to give notice of danger, the sentry's function. Both are messengers and neither is a judge; both deliver information that the recipient must then act on. The pairing appears repeatedly in this order — (5:19), (7:188), (11:2), (17:105), (25:56), (33:45), (35:24), (48:8) — and the consistency is itself a statement about emphasis. A religion whose messenger is described first as a bringer of good news is presenting itself differently from one whose messenger is first a threat.

**Why Universality Was the Harder Claim in Mecca**

The idea that a prophet belongs to a people was the normal assumption of the ancient world and of the Arabian peninsula in particular. Every community had its own cult, its own sanctuary and, where it claimed one, its own line of holy men. A message addressed to all of humanity broke that pattern in a way that carried direct social consequences: it implied that tribal boundaries did not define religious obligation, that the Arab and the non-Arab stood in the same relation to it, and that no clan held a franchise over the sanctuary's meaning. For the Quraysh, who derived considerable standing from custodianship of a local shrine, the claim was not abstract. It is one thing to be told that your gods are false; it is another to be told that the replacement is not yours either. The Qur'an raises the same point about earlier prophets by contrast rather than by argument, noting of several that they were sent to their own people specifically, while reserving for this mission a description with no such limit attached. The difference is not presented as a promotion of one messenger over others but as a difference in the assignment, and the sūrah's placement of the claim here — in the middle of a local quarrel about local idols — is what gives it force.

**"Most People Do Not Know"**

The closing clause is deflationary and the sūrah uses it more than once, repeating it at (34:36) about provision. *Wa lākinna akthara al-nāsi lā yaʿlamūn* — but most people do not know. Coming directly after the announcement of a universal mission, it acknowledges the gap between the scope of the message and the size of its reception. The verb is *yaʿlamūn*, they do not know — not "they do not believe" and not "they refuse", which are stronger words the Qur'an uses elsewhere. What is being described is ignorance rather than defiance, and the diagnosis is consistent with the sūrah's treatment of its opponents as men operating on unexamined assumptions (34:20). The clause also functions as a caution against measuring truth by headcount. A claim addressed to everyone that most people have not grasped is exactly what the verse says it is, and the low uptake is reported without embarrassment.

**The Limit on the Office Is Also a Defence of It**

There is one more thing this verse accomplishes that its neighbours make clear. The Meccan opponents had accused the Prophet of fabricating a message and of madness (34:8), and both charges assume a man claiming more than he was. A messenger who announces and warns, who neither compels belief nor controls outcomes, is a smaller figure than the one being accused. The sūrah will press this defence further, having him disclaim any payment (34:47) and even accept that if he is astray the loss falls on him (34:50). Read against that background, *We did not send you except as* is not a reduction of the Prophet's standing but a description of it, and an accurate description is the best answer available to a caricature. The next verse gives the opponents their reply.
