## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:64

> To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim of Ownership**

*Lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍ* — to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.

The sentence has no verb. It is a nominal construction with a fronted predicate, and in Arabic that form gives both exclusivity and permanence: the belonging is His, and it is not a temporary arrangement.

The comprehensiveness of the phrase is the point. *Mā*, whatever, covers everything without exception — not the good things, not the useful things, not the things people are willing to credit to a creator, but everything. The verse is placed after three verses of signs and before two more, and it is the conclusion they were building: the One who does all of this owns all of it.

**What Ownership Excludes**

If everything belongs to God, several things follow that the sūrah has been insisting on since its first verse.

Nothing belongs to the idols, which is the argument of verse 62 restated as a property claim. Nothing belongs to the worshipper in the absolute sense, which is why the sūrah keeps describing wealth as *what We provided you* (22:28, 22:35) and why the sacrificial animal may be used but not sold. And nothing that a person surrenders is lost, since it was never his to begin with — which is the argument behind the promise to the emigrants in verses 58 and 59.

The claim is therefore not a piece of abstract theology. It is the premise of every transaction this sūrah legislates: sacrifice, charity, expenditure, emigration, and the willingness to fight and die for something.

**Al-Ghanī: the Self-Sufficient**

*Wa-inna Allāha la-huwa al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd* — and God, He is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised.

*Ghanī* means rich, free of need, self-sufficient. The word is used for a person of independent means, and applied to God it means that nothing outside Him supplies anything to Him.

The consequence for worship is the one the Qur'an draws repeatedly and without softening: God does not benefit from being worshipped and is not diminished by being denied. The sūrah has already said this in the plainest available language at verse 37, where it stated that neither the meat nor the blood of the sacrifice reaches God. Sūrah al-Zumar puts the corollary in a divine saying: were every human being to become a disbeliever, nothing would be lost to Him.

That is why the verse's argument can be made at all. A God who needed worship could be negotiated with, pressured, or bribed. A God who needs nothing is not subject to those levers, and the worship He asks for is not a payment.

**Al-Ḥamīd: the Praised**

*Ḥamīd* is the passive form: the one who is praised, and, more strongly, the one to whom praise is due whether or not anyone offers it.

The pairing with *ghanī* is what makes the name substantive. A being who needed nothing and deserved nothing would be merely remote. A being who needs nothing and is worthy of praise is one toward whom praise is the fitting response — not because it is required of you, not because it adds anything to Him, but because it is the accurate description of the situation.

This is the second of the sūrah's two uses of the root. The *path of the Praised* appeared at verse 24 as the destination of the people of the Garden, and the sūrah's closing verse will command the believers to hold fast to God as their Guardian. What is named here as an attribute will be named there as a relationship.

**The Verse Answers the Sūrah's Opening Question**

The sūrah opened with a command to fear God because the Hour is a dreadful thing, and it spent its first seven verses arguing that the Hour will come. What it has not yet explicitly said is why any of that should concern the reader personally.

Verse 64 supplies the missing premise. If everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to God, then every human being is a tenant rather than an owner, and a tenant can be called to account. The Hour is not an intrusion into a life that was the person's own; it is the audit of something he was holding in trust.

That is why the ownership claim appears here, in the closing movement, rather than at the beginning. It is the last of the premises, and it is the one that turns the sūrah's argument from a demonstration into a summons.

**The Verse's Place in the Chain**

This is the fourth and last of the causal verses that ground the promise of help made in verse 60. The sequence has moved from the alternation of night and day, through the truth of God and the nullity of the idols, through the rain, to this: ownership and independence.

The order is ascending, and it ends where a theodicy has to end. The reason help is available to the wronged is not that the universe is arranged to reward the just; it is that the universe belongs to Someone who lacks nothing, who therefore cannot be corrupted, and who is therefore free to act without being moved by anything outside Himself.

**What the Verse Asks of the Reader**

A verse like this is easily assented to and rarely believed. Everyone concedes that God owns everything; the sūrah's own opponents did not deny that a high god existed.

What the verse requires is the specific application: that what you have is not yours, that what you lose was never withheld unjustly, and that what you give was never going to be lost. The sūrah has been moving toward that application since the emigrants of verse 58, and it will close by asking the reader to hold fast to the Owner rather than to what is owned.
