## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:35

> Adding, “We are far superior ˹to the believers˺ in wealth and children, and we will never be punished.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Claims Joined by an Unstated Premise**

The sentence has two halves and the join between them is missing. *Naḥnu aktharu amwālan wa awlādan* — we have more wealth and more children. *Wa mā naḥnu bi-muʿadhdhabīn* — and we will not be punished. Nothing explicit connects the inventory to the conclusion, and yet the speakers move from one to the other without pausing, which tells us the premise was too obvious to them to need stating. That premise is the assumption this whole section of the sūrah exists to destroy: that material success is a divine verdict already delivered. If prosperity indicates approval, then the prosperous have their answer, and the warning they have just rejected (34:34) is addressed to someone else. The argument is not stupid. It is the oldest religious mistake there is, and the Qur'an treats it as a serious position because a great many serious people have held it.

**Why Wealth and Children Specifically**

The pairing *amwāl* and *awlād* is a fixed formula in the Qur'an and names the two forms of capital that mattered in a tribal society. Wealth is the obvious one. Children — specifically sons — were the other, and arguably the more important: manpower for raids and defence, hands for the caravan, standing in the assembly, and the only available form of continuity after death. A man rich in both was secure in this world in every sense his society recognised. The Qur'an's response is never to deny that these are goods. It is to reclassify them. Al-Anfāl and al-Taghābun both state the reclassification in the same words: *"Your wealth and children are only a test"* (8:28; 64:15). Al-Kahf allows them their beauty and then ranks them: *"Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord"* (18:46). A test and an ornament are both real. Neither is a verdict.

**The Boast Names a Comparison Without Naming the Compared**

*Aktharu* is a comparative — more, greater — and a comparative requires a second term. The verse does not supply it in the Arabic, and the translation fills in what the context demands: more than the believers. This is the sting of the sentence. The comparison is with the poor men and slaves who had accepted the message, and the elite are treating that visible gap in fortune as the decisive evidence in a theological dispute. The Qur'an records the same reasoning from earlier communities in almost identical form. Noah's opponents dismissed his followers as *"the lowliest among us"* (11:27); the people addressed by another messenger asked, *"How can we believe in you, when you are followed ˹only˺ by the lowest of the low?"* (26:111). In every case the argument is not about the message at all. It is about the social register of the people who accepted it.

**The Second Half Is the Real Claim**

Of the two clauses, only the first is a fact. That they had more wealth and more sons than the Muslims of early Mecca was simply true, and the verse does not dispute it. The second clause is where the error sits, and it is stated with the same confidence as the first — *mā naḥnu bi-muʿadhdhabīn*, constructed with a negation and an emphatic particle, meaning we are certainly not to be punished. Placing a verifiable fact and an unverifiable prediction in one breath is how the sentence achieves its persuasive force, and the technique is familiar from any confident assertion that smuggles a conclusion behind an observation. The sūrah has already recorded the same people making the same kind of prediction about the Hour (34:3), and the pattern is consistent: they are certain about the future in exactly the way they accuse the Prophet of pretending to be.

**What the Qur'an Says Abundance Actually Predicts**

If wealth signals anything in the Qur'an's account, it more often signals danger than approval. Al-Tawbah issues a direct instruction not to read it as favour: *"So let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this worldly life"* (9:55). Āl ʿImrān is blunter about the settlement day: *"neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah"* (3:10). And al-Shuʿarāʾ names the one asset that will transfer: the Day *"when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit. Only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺"* (26:88–89). The Qur'an's position is not ascetic. Al-Qaṣaṣ tells a man to seek the next world with what he has been given *"without forgetting your share of this world"* (28:77). Wealth is to be used, not renounced — and never read as a report card.

**A Boast the Sūrah Has Already Answered With a Story**

The most efficient refutation of this sentence is not an argument but the passage sitting twenty verses earlier. The people of Sheba had more of everything than these speakers: two irrigated plains, a road with way-stations in sight of one another, safe travel by day and night (34:15, 34:18). By the standard being proposed here, they were the most approved community in Arabia. They were scattered until nothing remained of them but a story told by passing traders (34:19). The sūrah constructed that account before quoting this boast, so that the boast arrives already refuted by an example its audience knew from their own trade route. The Prophet is nonetheless instructed to answer in words as well, and the reply in the next verse takes the argument apart at its foundation rather than at its conclusion.
