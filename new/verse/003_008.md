## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:8

> ˹They say,˺ “Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us. Grant us Your mercy. You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Petition of the Well-Grounded**

The previous verse ended with the words of the well-grounded in knowledge — *we believe in it; it is all from our Lord* — and this verse continues with the prayer they make. Read together, the two verses describe a single posture: a person who has accepted the whole Book and then asks God to keep him from sliding away from it.

That order is the opposite of what a confident reader might expect. Knowledge of the Book does not lead to a sense of security here; it leads to a request. The people closest to the text are the ones most aware that their hold on it can loosen, and their answer to that awareness is not more study but supplication. The verse is a reminder that understanding is a gift with a keeper.

**Zaygh: the Word for a Heart That Slips Sideways**

The verb translated "deviate" is *zaygh*, and it is the same word used two lines earlier for *those with deviant hearts* who chase the elusive verses. The link is deliberate. The disease described in 3:7 as a settled condition is the danger this prayer asks to be spared, and the difference between the two is not a difference of intellect but of footing. Deviation here is a movement, a tilt, a leaning off a straight line, not a sudden departure.

The Qur'an uses the root for hearts that turned after they had known better: *“So when they ˹persistently˺ deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate”* (61:5). It is used almost gently of the believers at a moment of extremity: *“after the hearts of a group of them had almost faltered”* (9:117) — a state that God then repaired. Hearts that tilt are not yet hearts that have left; the prayer exists because the tilt can begin before anyone notices it.

**The Fear That Follows Guidance, Not the Fear That Precedes It**

The petition contains a small, important phrase: *after you have guided us*. The supplicant is not asking to be guided. He says that guidance has already happened, and asks that it not be undone. The thing he fears is not the absence of light but the loss of sight while standing in it.

This is the sober reading of religious life that the sūrah's own arguments require. The people addressed in this book are mostly people who had something and risked losing it: the communities that received scriptures, the believers whose commitment was tested by fear of the powerful, the reader who finds the passages he cannot explain. Privilege increases exposure rather than removing it. A verse given increases what a person is accountable for, and the greater the gift the further it can be surrendered.

**The Heart Held Between Two Fingers**

The Prophet described the situation with an image this prayer fits exactly. ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr reported that he said: *Verily, the hearts of the children of Adam, all of them, are between two fingers of the Merciful as one heart; He directs them wherever He wills* — and then he would add: *O Allah, the Director of the hearts, turn our hearts to Your obedience* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2654).

The report shows the prayer's place in prophetic practice. The one who brought the Book, and who had more support than anyone, did not treat his own heart as a settled matter. He asked for its direction as a matter of routine, and the doctrine behind the asking is that direction is not a state a person administers himself. If a heart can be directed, then the one who directs it is the one to ask.

**Mercy From His Presence: the Word Ladun**

The second request uses a word the translation renders "Your mercy" with an added implication in the Arabic: *raḥmatan min ladunka*, mercy from *ladun*, a preposition meaning "in the possession or presence of" someone. It is the word of a gift given from inside the giver, not passed along from elsewhere.

The Qur'an uses it when the need is extreme. The youths of the cave pray: *“Our Lord! Grant us mercy from Yourself and guide us rightly through our ordeal.”* (18:10), and Moses finds a servant whom God had granted *“mercy from Us and enlightened with knowledge of Our Own”* (18:65). Muslim scholars later coined the phrase *al-ʿilm al-ladunī* — knowledge given directly by God — from the second of these. The supplicant in this verse asks for exactly that kind of mercy: mercy that comes from the source and needs no intermediary to be sufficient.

**"Giver of All Bounties": Asking in the Right Name**

The prayer closes by naming God *al-Wahhāb*, the one who gives constantly and freely, on a form in Arabic that suggests abundance and repetition rather than a single bestowal. The name matches the request. Someone asking for mercy from the divine presence addresses the one whose habit is giving.

The double request also shows how the supplicant understands the danger. Deviation is prevented by God holding the heart firm, and the mercy asked for is what covers the shortfall that remains. A servant cannot guarantee the first and does not deserve the second; the verse has him ask for both in one breath, and the name he uses answers both. This is what confidence in God sounds like when it has stopped trusting in itself.

Two petitions, no complaint, and no reference to circumstances: the prayer contains nothing about wealth, health, or victory over opponents. The people of knowledge ask about the one thing they cannot outsource — the firmness of their own hearts — and they ask it of the one whose name is giving. Everything the rest of the sūrah will say about loss and gain is downstream of this request.
