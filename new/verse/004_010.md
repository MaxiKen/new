## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:10

> Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Eating What Will Become Fire**

The image is stark and physical. *Inna alladhīna yaʾkulūna amwāl al-yatāmā ẓulman innamā yaʾkulūna fī buṭūnihim nāran* — those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth consume nothing but fire into their bellies. The Qur’an uses the verb *akala* — to eat — for illicit wealth elsewhere, as in *“do not devour one another’s wealth illegally”* (4:29), but here the metaphor completes itself: what is eaten as wealth in this world will be present as fire in the stomach in the next. The restriction *ẓulman* — unjustly, wrongfully — is essential, because guardians do handle orphans’ property legitimately when they preserve it, invest it, and spend from it for the child’s need (4:5–6). What the verse condemns is consumption that is *ẓulm*, the putting of a thing where it does not belong, the use of a trust for the trustee’s own appetite. The sūrah has spent eight verses defining that trust — return without substitution (4:2), marriage without exploitation (4:3–4), provision without premature handover (4:5), testing and witnessing under divine reckoning (4:6–9) — and now it states the inner reality of violating that trust in a single picture. What felt like food will be felt as fire.

**Why Fire in the Belly**

The choice of belly as the place where fire settles was noted by commentators as precise justice. The sin passed through the belly as enjoyment; the punishment revisits the same place as burning. A ḥadīth vision cited in exegesis describes those who consumed orphans’ wealth with flames coming from their bodies, a narration that matches the Qur’anic image without adding to it. The sūrah’s wording does more than threaten; it re-describes the act while it is happening. A guardian who tells himself he has merely borrowed, or taken a small fee, or mixed accounts as a matter of convenience, is told that in the unseen record his stomach is already being filled with fire. That present tense — *innamā yaʾkulūna* — they are only eating fire — collapses the gap between now and then. The fire is not only a future sentence to be served; it is the true name of what is being eaten now. The verse that follows confirms the outer consequence — *wa sa yaṣlawna saʿīrā* — they will be burned in a blazing Hell — but the first clause already moves the punishment into the body, making the sin self-consuming before the Judgment even begins.

**A Great Sin Among Great Sins**

The sūrah classifies this consumption among major wrongs. The earlier phrase *ḥūban kabīrā* — a great sin — for mixing and substituting orphan wealth (4:2) anticipated this, and the later principle *“If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your lesser misdeeds”* (4:31) shows that the Qur’an grades sins and links forgiveness of smaller faults to avoidance of larger ones. Consuming the property of a child who has lost his protector sits in that larger category, and Prophetic tradition counts *akl māl al-yatīm* — eating the orphan’s wealth — among the seven destructive sins, alongside associating partners with God, sorcery, and killing a soul. That pairing is instructive. A person might rank financial wrongs as less serious than doctrinal or violent wrongs, but the sūrah places this financial wrong beside them because it strikes at the community’s most defenceless member. The warning is also proportionate to the trust involved. The guardian was chosen precisely because no one else could check him easily; violating that invisibility is a betrayal of *amānah*, a trust that the Qur’an elsewhere says the heavens and mountains refused to bear.

**What Counts as Unjust Consumption**

The qualifier *ẓulman* excludes legitimate management. Feeding and clothing the child from his property while keeping the principal as a standing support (4:5), investing with care, paying a fair provision to a poor guardian who works on the estate (4:6), and spending within what is recognized as fair — all of that is not eating unjustly. What remains forbidden is waste, haste before the child grows to claim his right (4:6), substitution of bad for good (4:2), and any use that serves the guardian’s household at the orphan’s expense. Jurists detailed many forms: taking a loan from the orphan’s funds without intent to return, selling his property cheaply to oneself, or charging an inflated fee for custody. The common element is benefit to the strong that corresponds to loss for the weak without the weak’s considered consent. The sūrah’s remedy has been procedural throughout — test, witness, account, speak kindly — precisely to make that consent meaningful when it comes. Where those procedures are bypassed and the orphan’s wealth is simply lived on, the consumption is *ẓulm* even if the guardian tells himself he meant to repay later.

**The Second Half — Burning in a Blazing Hell**

The closing *wa sa yaṣlawna saʿīrā* adds the external fire to the internal one. *Saʿīr* — blazing, fierce — describes a fire that is already kindled and intense. The sūrah pairs this with *sa yaṣlawna* — they will be burned, they will roast — a verb that suggests direct contact, not distant punishment. The two fires — in the belly and in the blaze — mirror the two stages of the sin. First, the guardian made the orphan’s wealth his own food; then, that food becomes his own punishment. The Qur’an elsewhere warns *“do not kill yourselves”* and *“whoever does this sinfully and unjustly, We will burn them in the Fire, and that is easy for Allah”* (4:29–30), and this verse specifies one path to that Fire. The sūrah’s next movement will turn to the constructive counterpart — precise shares for children, parents, spouses, and siblings (4:11–12) — but it earns that turn by first clearing the ground of exploitation. No fair division can thrive where one party has already been eaten. By naming the meal as fire, the verse makes return and restraint feel not only lawful but self-preserving.

**How the Sequence Trains the Heart**

Read with what came before, verse 10 closes the opening guardianship cycle that began with *“be mindful of your Lord”* (4:1) and *“Allah is ever Watchful”* (4:1). Verses 2–9 built the practices — give, do not mix, do not exploit marriage, sustain with kindness, test and witness, share, remember bystanders, imagine your own children — and verse 10 states the inner truth of failing them. That order matters for how law shapes character. The sūrah does not begin with fire; it begins with a common origin and a call to awareness, then details, then motive, and only then the stark image. By the time the reader hears *“they consume fire into their bellies,”* he has already been given every tool to avoid it. The verse therefore functions both as warning and as clarification: what feels like quiet use of another’s property is, in the language the sūrah has been teaching, already a consumption that ends in burning. The following verses will show the opposite use of wealth — measured, witnessed, and distributed by decree — as the path that keeps both the orphan’s right and the guardian’s soul from being consumed.
