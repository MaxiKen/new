## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:1

> I do swear by the Day of Judgment!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Falls on the Day Itself**

The sūrah opens with an oath sworn by the resurrection, and it does so before a single line of evidence for the resurrection has been given. God swears by the Day of Judgment; the forty verses that follow unpack what that oath has already declared. Elsewhere the Qur'an swears by created things that a hearer can look at — *“By the sun and its brightness”* (91:1), *“By the night when it covers”* (92:1), *“By the fig and the olive ˹of Jerusalem˺”* (95:1). Those oaths ask the listener to reason from something present to something absent. Here the thing sworn by is absent as well, and the boldness of the verse lies exactly there: a future event is treated as though it were already an object, as settled as the sun overhead. The Makkan audience denied that any such Day was coming. The reply is not an argument built out of premises they would grant; it is an oath resting on a fact they have not conceded, which leaves them either to accept it or to call the Speaker a liar.

**Why the Oath Is Cast in a Negative Mould**

The Arabic reads *lā uqsimu bi-yawmi l-qiyāmah*, literally “I do not swear by the Day of Resurrection,” and no serious reading of this verse takes it as a denial. The grammarians divided over the particle *lā*, and both positions deserve their grounds. One party treats it as extra, a particle of emphasis that Arabic regularly attaches to an oath, so that the sense is simply “I swear.” The other gives *lā* its normal work of negation and supplies a suppressed clause before it: the matter is not as you claim, and then the oath. The first reading has economy on its side, since it assumes nothing missing. The second has the stronger claim on principle, because a particle that negates everywhere else in the language should be allowed to negate here. The dispute is genuine, but it changes nothing that the verse asserts: both roads end in an affirmation, which is why the sūrah can proceed on the sense without settling the grammar.

**What the Word Qiyāmah Carries**

*Qiyāmah* comes from the root *q-w-m*, to stand. The Day is named for standing: the dead stand up out of the earth, and then every creature stands before its Lord to answer. Both motions are held in the single noun, rising and accounting, and the sūrah will use each of them. Naming it “the standing” rather than “the day” or “the event” builds a claim into the name itself — this is an assembly, a summons, a reckoning conducted face to face, not merely a stretch of time. The Qur'an elsewhere names the same reality by what it does to those who meet it: *“The Inevitable Hour! What is the Inevitable Hour?”* (69:1–2). The one name describes the posture of those who are raised; the other describes the impossibility of escape. Together they rule out the reading the deniers preferred, that death is a door which opens onto nothing.

**An Oath as Argument, Not Ornament**

Short Makkan sūrahs lean on oaths because their hearers understood the form and what it cost. An oath stakes the speaker's credibility on the statement that follows, and in the Qur'an the thing sworn by is never neutral: it is chosen because its evidential weight is already conceded. What is unusual here is the inversion. No visible sign is offered as the ladder; the disputed Day is itself the rung. The effect is to relocate the burden of proof. The denier is no longer asked whether resurrection is conceivable in the abstract. He is told that it is a fact known to the One who swears, and the conversation moves from possibility to conduct. Against a position held on the strength of custom alone — *“There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time”* (45:24) — an oath speaks as the testimony of a witness. God swears as one who was present at the first creation and will be present at the second.

**The Answer the Oath Is Left Waiting For**

Every oath expects an answer clause: I swear by X that Y. Verse 1 supplies no such clause, and the commentators differed about where to find it. Some hold that the answer is the question in v. 3, since an interrogative placed under oath does the work of an assertion: *“Do people think We cannot reassemble their bones?”* (75:3) means, under the oath, that they are wrong to think it. Others hold that the answer is suppressed entirely and should be supplied from the sūrah's own conclusion, which makes the oath govern everything between. The first reading is stronger, because the abrupt turn from oath to interrogation in v. 3 is precisely the shape an oath's answer takes, and because it binds the opening triad into one movement rather than leaving the first verse to float over the whole chapter. Either way the sūrah announces its subject in a form that cannot be read as speculation.

**A Day Sworn To, and a Day Mocked**

The certainty asserted here is the certainty the Makkan audience ridiculed, and the Qur'an records both sides of that exchange without softening either. Against the oath stands the mockers' question, repeated across the Makkan and early Madinan sūrahs in nearly identical words. Against their laughter stands the flat declaration: *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves.”* (22:7), and the oath-form is used the same way elsewhere, *“Yes—by my Lord, the Knower of the unseen—it will certainly come to you!”* (34:3). The sūrah therefore begins where a preacher would normally end. Nothing is left to be proved at the level of whether; everything that follows concerns what the Day will do when it arrives, and who will be found standing.
