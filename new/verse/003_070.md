## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:70

> O People of the Book! Why do you reject the signs of Allah while you bear witness ˹to their truth˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Puts the Accused on the Spot**

The sūrah now addresses the People of the Book directly, and it begins with a question about conduct rather than belief: why do you reject the signs of God while you bear witness? The form of the question assumes that the addressees have an answer to defend and asks them to produce it.

The Book uses this interrogative form repeatedly in its engagement with the scriptured communities, and the effect is to shift the discussion from a contest of doctrines to a matter of the parties' own consistency. The question does not ask what the other party believes; it asks why, holding what they hold, they do what they do. That is a harder question to deflect, because the premise on which it rests is supplied by the addressee rather than by the speaker.

**"While You Bear Witness" — the Heart of the Charge**

The clause about bearing witness is what makes the question incriminating. The commentators gave two readings, and both are damaging to the position being criticised. On one reading, the addressees themselves bear witness — that is, they have known the truth of the message and are acting against their knowledge. On the other, they are witnesses to the earlier prophets in their own tradition, and the message in front of them manifests the same truths those prophets brought.

Either way the charge is the same in structure: rejection here is not an absence of information but a decision made in the presence of it. The Book makes the identical point about the community that disputed the account of Jesus, describing a disagreement that continued after knowledge had come to them, and about the people whose hearts have swerved, who follow what is ambiguous while the clear verses stand in front of them. The charge in this verse belongs to that family, and its severity comes from the fact that the witnesses are the defendants.

**The Signs in View**

The word for signs covers both the verses of revelation and the events that accompany a prophet's mission, and the sūrah has just narrated a series of them: a birth without a father, a child who speaks, healings, a raising of the dead, and the removal of the messenger from the hands of those who plotted against him.

The question therefore points at a body of material, not a single proposition. A community that had argued about Jesus was being asked why it rejected the signs whose account it had heard, and the word choice keeps the argument on the evidence. The Book's habit of calling revelation itself a sign means that the recitation the community has listened to is part of the case against it, and the verse's question covers that recitation as well as the wonders it describes. What is being rejected, in other words, is not a foreign claim but what the hearers have been given.

**A Question to a Community That Argued About Abraham**

The verse's position in the sūrah matters. It follows the passage in which the Book denied that Abraham was a Jew or a Christian, described him as upright and free of partnership, and named his followers as the parties with the best claim on him.

The question about rejecting signs arrives immediately after an argument that turned on the other party's own history. A community that prized Abraham and accepted that he preceded both books is asked why its descendants are rejecting what has come, and the implication is left for the hearer: the conduct being questioned is a departure from the pattern of the ancestor whose name they invoke. The sūrah does not spell out the comparison, and the question is framed so that the addressee supplies it himself. That is the strength of the interrogative mode in this kind of passage; the accusation is carried by the listener's own premises rather than by the speaker's assertion.

**The Community Being Defended**

The versse also has a function for the Muslim community reading it, since it explains the nature of the resistance they were meeting without instructing them to retaliate. The sūrah has just told them that people wish to mislead them; it now shows them the shape of the opposition, which is a refusal made by people who have grounds to know better.

What the believers are given is therefore a way of understanding their situation that does not require them to treat their neighbours as ignorant. The charge is not that the other communities lack information. It is that they have it and are acting against it, and the Book's decision to address them directly means the disclosure is not a piece of gossip about a rival but a statement made openly to the party concerned. The following verses ask two further questions of the same people.

**A Pattern Repeated Across the Sūrah**

The sūrah contains, near its end, another question of the same kind: why do you turn those who believe away from the way of God while you are witnesses? The two verses form a pair separated by some thirty verses, and their agreement is a sign of how the Book's argument with the scriptured communities was conducted throughout the chapter.

The recurring formula is a question about consistency, addressed to people whose own tradition supplies the standard. The Book does not accuse them of following a different religion; it accuses them of failing to follow their own with integrity. That is a more serious charge and a more useful one, and it leaves the door open in the way the sūrah's later verses continue to do.
