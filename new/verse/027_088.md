## Sūrah an-Naml 27:88

> Now you see the mountains, thinking they are firmly fixed, but they are travelling ˹just˺ like clouds. ˹That is˺ the design of Allah, Who has perfected everything. Surely He is All-Aware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter's Most Contested Verse**

Few verses in an-Naml have produced more disagreement, and the disagreement is not about the words but about when the scene takes place. Is the mountains' movement a present fact we fail to perceive, or an event at the end of the world?

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both report the first reading: the mountains move now, and we do not see it because large objects viewed from a distance appear stationary even when travelling fast. Al-Rāzī also records the second, connecting the verse to the many passages describing mountains scattered, levelled or turned to carded wool at the Hour. The surrounding verses support the eschatological reading, since the trumpet has just sounded. The verb tense and the address to a present "you" support the other. Both camps are working from the same four words.

**The Argument From Perception**

The first reading deserves to be taken seriously on its own terms rather than treated as a curiosity. Its logic is about the reliability of the senses, and the commentators state it plainly: a very large thing seen from very far away looks still even when it is moving quickly.

That is a genuine observation, available to anyone who has watched a ship on the horizon or a cloud bank that seems fixed until one looks away and back. The verse builds the illusion into its own wording — *taḥsabuhā jāmidah*, you *suppose* they are fixed. The supposition is named as a supposition. What the reading then proposes is a general lesson about inference: the most solid-seeming thing in a person's world may be in motion, and his confidence that it is not is a product of his vantage point rather than of the thing itself.

**The Argument From Context**

The competing reading has the stronger contextual case. The previous verse describes the trumpet and the terror; the following verses describe the weighing of deeds. A verse about geology sitting between them would be an interruption.

On this reading the mountains are seen to move at the Hour, as many other chapters describe, and the present-tense verb functions as a vivid future — a common enough device in Arabic and in the Qur'an particularly, where scenes of the judgment are frequently narrated as though unfolding now. The word *tamurru*, passing, and the comparison to clouds match the imagery of those other passages closely. Nothing forces a choice between the two readings, and the classical commentators generally reported both.

**A Third Reading: The World Itself**

Al-Qurṭubī records a further possibility that reframes the image. The passing away may refer to the earth as a whole — the world, believed to be solid, is in fact as ephemeral as a cloud.

This reading treats the mountains as the Qur'an's chosen instance of apparent permanence rather than as its subject. They are the obvious candidate: an-Naml itself, at verse 61, described God as placing firm mountains on the earth, and the word used there, *rawāsī*, means anchors. Naming them now as clouds inverts the chapter's own earlier image. What was set down to stabilise is shown to be in motion, and if the anchors are moving, nothing in the visible world is what it appeared.

**Three Allegorical Readings, and Whose They Are**

Al-Qurṭubī closes his treatment by reporting, on the authority of al-Māwardī, that the verse has been taken as a similitude, and he gives three versions of what it is a similitude for. The attributions can be stated precisely.

The first is Sahl ibn ʿAbdullāh's: the world itself is the mountain, and the man looking at it supposes it fixed while it is taking its share of passing away, like a cloud. The second applies the image to faith — you suppose it settled in the heart while its work is ascending. The third takes it of the soul at death, departing the body and rising toward the Throne. All three use the perceptual argument in the same way, by insisting that the appearance of stillness is a statement about the observer rather than the observed, and the first is the most exact use of it. A popular anecdote attaches this verse to Junayd al-Baghdādī, who is said to have recited it when asked why he showed none of the visible ecstasies of his contemporaries. The reading is apt — a mountain in motion looks still, and composure is no evidence of stillness — but the story is not in al-Qurṭubī's discussion and does not belong to this chain. It circulates in the later Sufi literature and should not be attributed to the commentary.

**"Who Has Perfected Everything"**

The verb is *atqana*. Al-Ṭabarī glosses it as tying down or making firm; al-Rāzī takes it in this context as God tying things together according to His wisdom.

Both senses turn on fastening, and the word's placement is deliberate. The sentence has just said that the firmest things are moving, and it immediately describes God as the one who makes things firm. The apparent contradiction resolves into the chapter's point: firmness is not a property things have in themselves but a relation they stand in to their maker. *Itqān* in later Arabic came to mean craftsmanship, doing a thing thoroughly and well, and the word retains that sense here. What the verse claims is not that the world is stable but that it is made properly.

**The Closing Clause Returns to Conduct**

After the cosmology comes a sentence about behaviour: He is aware of what you do. The transition is abrupt and it is the chapter's signature move.

An-Naml never leaves a description of the universe standing alone. The six questions about rain and rivers ended by asking about other gods. The statement that everything hidden is recorded led into a scene of judgment. Here the mountains dissolve into clouds and the verse closes by noting that your actions are observed. The scale collapses from planetary to personal in a single clause, and the reader is left with the implication the chapter has been pressing since verse 3: the vast thing being described is the setting in which small things are being counted.
