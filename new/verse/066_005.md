## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:5

> Perhaps, if he were to divorce you ˹all˺, his Lord would replace you with better wives who are submissive ˹to Allah˺, faithful ˹to Him˺, devout, repentant, dedicated to worship and fasting—previously married or virgins.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Warning Framed as a Possibility**

The verse opens with *ʿasā*, perhaps — a word that keeps the statement short of a prediction. No divorce is announced, and no replacement is promised; a scenario is set out and left standing. This is a particular kind of rebuke, one that works by describing what could happen rather than what will. The women addressed are asked to consider their position from the outside.

The context is preserved in the reports. The Prophet ﷺ had withdrawn from his wives for a month, and Madina was unsettled by rumours that he had divorced them all. ʿUmar recounted that the wives had pressed the Prophet ﷺ and that he told them it might be that if he divorced them, God would give him better wives in their place — and then this verse came down with the same content (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4916 records ʿUmar saying exactly this). The verse therefore confirmed a warning already spoken by a human being, which is a striking sequence: a father's blunt words to his daughter and her co-wives were endorsed from above.

**"Better Than You" and What the Comparison Means**

The claim that God could supply better wives is not an assessment of the women's worth as persons. It is a statement about a specific relationship and specific conduct, and the qualities listed immediately afterwards define the standard of comparison. Every one of them concerns the woman's relation to God rather than her beauty, lineage, wealth or usefulness to a household.

That list is the verse's real content. Submissive here renders *muslimāt*, meaning given over to God; *muʾmināt*, believing; *qānitāt*, devoutly obedient; *tāʾibāt*, repeatedly returning in repentance; *ʿābidāt*, given to worship; and then the disputed term rendered *fasting*. Set beside the situation that produced the sūrah — a confidence broken, an alliance formed, a husband pressured into forbidding what God allowed — the list reads as a direct answer. What was lacking was not affection or intelligence but orientation. The women being addressed were faithful and devout; the warning is that these qualities can coexist with a heart that has tilted, and that God is not short of people in whom they do not.

**Devotion Owed to God and Not to a Husband**

The third quality, *qānitāt*, deserves separating out, because it has been misused. Words from the root *q-n-t* in the Qur'an refer consistently to obedience and constancy toward God — in standing at night, in worship, in steadiness of religious commitment. They are not used for a wife's obedience to a husband. The same term is applied to men in the great list of believers who are promised forgiveness and a mighty reward: *“devout men and women”* (33:35), and it is applied to Mary at the close of this very chapter, where she is described as among the devoutly obedient (66:12). Mary had no husband.

Reading the word correctly matters for how the whole verse is understood. If *qānitāt* meant compliant wives, this verse would be a threat that disobedient women are replaceable with obedient ones, and the chapter would be about domestic authority. Read accurately, the standard held up is God-directed in every item, and the women who close the sūrah as models — Pharaoh's wife and Mary — are commended precisely for a devotion that ran against their households rather than serving them. The hierarchy the chapter teaches places a woman's relationship with her Lord above her relationship with anyone in her house, including a prophet.

**The Word That Divides the Commentators**

The sixth quality, *sāʾiḥāt*, is the interpretive crux of the verse. Its root means to travel or roam. The majority read it here as given to fasting, on the basis of reports that the term was used this way, and some extend the sense to a general restraint of the limbs from disobedience — a fasting of the whole body, not just the stomach. A second group reads it literally as those who travel for the sake of their religion, which in a Madinan context means the women who emigrated. A third reading takes the travelling as spiritual: those who journey on the path toward God.

There is reason to hold the emigration reading in view rather than defaulting to the majority. The word's plain sense is movement, and the women of the early community had in fact made the journey from Mecca at real cost, a sacrifice the Qur'an praises elsewhere in strong terms. Later exegesis, working in settings where women's public movement had narrowed, had an obvious incentive to convert a term about travel into a term about fasting performed at home. Nothing forces a choice between the readings, since both describe real practices of real believing women. What should not happen is the quiet disappearance of the more demanding sense.

**Two Categories, One Standard**

The list ends with a pairing that is not a quality at all: *“previously married or virgins”* (66:5). After six spiritual descriptions, the verse adds a purely factual distinction and declines to rank it. Both categories appear on the same side of the comparison, both among the better wives God could supply.

The refusal to rank is deliberate and worth stating plainly, because communities have rarely managed it. A previously married woman — widowed or divorced — carries in most societies a diminished standing in the marriage market, whatever her character. This verse puts her beside the never-married woman without comment, having just spent a line and a half establishing that the qualities which actually distinguish women are submission, faith, devotion, repentance and worship. The Prophet's own household demonstrated the principle before the verse stated it: his first marriage was to a widow, and most of the women he married had been married before. The sūrah that opens by correcting him for an oath about his wives closes this passage by defining what a good wife is, and marital history is not on the list.
