## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:154

> Then after distress, He sent down serenity in the form of drowsiness overcoming some of you, while others were disturbed by evil thoughts about Allah—the thoughts of ˹pre-Islamic˺ ignorance. They ask, “Do we have a say in the matter?” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “All matters are destined by Allah.” They conceal in their hearts what they do not reveal to you. They say ˹to themselves˺, “If we had any say in the matter, none of us would have come to die here.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Even if you were to remain in your homes, those among you who were destined to be killed would have met the same fate.” Through this, Allah tests what is within you and purifies what is in your hearts. And Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Security Sent Down After Sorrow**

The verse reports that after the sorrow, God sent down security in the form of a sleepiness that settled over one company of the believers. The Book describes similar gifts in its accounts of the community's earlier battles, and the parallel is exact in one of them.

The passage about Badr describes drowsiness overcoming the believers as a gift of serenity, together with rain sent down to purify them and steady their feet. The Book treats the ability to rest in the middle of a crisis as a sign of trust, and the account of Uḥud records the same gift arriving after the worst of the day had passed.

**What the Sleep Signified**

Al-Rāzī discusses the phenomenon at length and reads the sleep as evidence that God was with the community. He reports a statement attributed to Ibn Masʿūd that sleeping during battle indicates trust in God and detachment from the world, while sleeping during the prayer indicates the opposite, because the community's situation in each case is different.

The reports about the field describe believers falling asleep where they stood, with weapons slipping from their hands, and the state was so remarkable that the tradition preserved the detail. What the verse presents is a physical mercy with a moral edge: the people whose nerves had been broken by the day were given rest, and the rest was sent by the same God whose help they had doubted.

**The Company That Remained Anxious**

Beside the sleeping group, the verse sets another company that was anxious about itself and could not rest. The two groups lay on the same field under the same conditions, and the difference between them is the subject of the sentence.

The verse does not name the second group directly, but the description that follows identifies it. These were people who were worrying about their own safety and about the outcome of a decision they had opposed, and their anxiety expressed itself in the way they spoke about God. The pairing of the two companies is the sūrah's way of distinguishing the community's members by what the crisis brought out of them.

**Thinking About God What Is Not True**

The verse describes the anxious company as thinking about God thoughts that were not true, and it calls this the thinking of the Age of Ignorance. The Book uses that name for the period before the message arrived, and the comparison is severe.

The commentators explain the thought as the belief that if the community had really been on the side of God, it would not have met with such a setback. Al-Rāzī develops the point at length, warning against judging the will of God by a person's own assessment of what is pleasant or painful. The verse identifies the error as a way of thinking about God rather than as a simple mistake about tactics.

**The Question About the Decision**

The anxious company is reported asking whether they had any decision in the matter, and the sūrah's answer is that the decision belongs entirely to God. The commentators differ about who spoke the question, with one view assigning it to the hypocrites and another holding that sincere believers might have asked it in their bewilderment.

However the question is assigned, the answer stands on its own. The community is told that the outcome of the expedition was never within its own control, and the sentence removes the basis for the complaint that had begun to circulate. The answer's brevity matches the sūrah's method of dealing with the community's arguments: it states the truth that resolves the difficulty and leaves the reader to draw the conclusion.

**Al-Rāzī on Providence**

Al-Rāzī's comment on this verse includes a discussion of providence in which he argues that people should not claim to deduce the will of God from their own evaluations. His reasoning is that human beings do not possess all the measures needed to judge whether a particular event is good or harmful, and success or failure on a battlefield is one of the clearest examples.

The argument supports the verse's own structure, which contrasts the community's anxious speculation with the statement that the decision belongs to God. The Book's teaching here is that a person's limited view of the consequences of an event disqualifies him from reading the event as a verdict, and the lesson applies beyond the case of the battle.

**The Claim Made in Secret and the Answer Given**

The verse reports that the anxious group kept to themselves a claim they did not disclose openly: that if the decision had been theirs, they would not have been killed where they stood. The answer comes in a sentence that removes the premise: had the community stayed in its houses, those who were destined to be slain would have gone out to the places where they were to die.

The sentence is one of the sūrah's clearest statements of the fixed term, and it matches the verse that declares that no soul dies except by God's permission at an appointed time. The Book denies the calculation of safety on the grounds that the location of a person's death is not the variable that determines it.

**The Purpose of the Test**

The verse closes by stating that the events were arranged so that God might test what was in the community's hearts and bring it to the surface. The test is not for the purpose of gaining information but of making the truth visible.

The Book knows what a person conceals, and the Book states that God knows what lies within the breasts. The events at Uḥud made the contents of those breasts visible to the community itself, and the sūrah treats that exposure as one of the day's purposes. What follows in the passage is the community's opportunity to act on what it has been shown about itself.
