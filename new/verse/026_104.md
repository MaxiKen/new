## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:104

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain Names Power and Mercy Together**

The account closes with two divine names: the Almighty and the Most Merciful. The first establishes that God cannot be defeated or resisted by idols, crowds, or inherited authority. The second establishes that His power is not arbitrary. He guides, warns, accepts repentance, and gives servants a path back to Him. The pairing reads the whole Abraham narrative through God's character.

Power explains why false worship will fail. Mercy explains why Abraham is given guidance, why warnings reach the idolaters, and why the door of return remains open during life. The names cannot be separated. Divine mercy is not weakness, and divine might is not cruelty. Each name clarifies the other.

**The Almighty Exposes False Authority**

Abraham's people treated idols and ancestors as authorities. The title Almighty places every created claim beneath God. An idol cannot hear or help; an ancestor cannot make error true; a leader cannot command against the Lord and remain ultimate. All created powers are limited, while God's authority does not depend on anyone's permission.

This name also corrects fear. People may fear losing family approval, wealth, status, or political protection if they abandon false worship. The Almighty is greater than the power that threatens those losses. Trust in Him does not make worldly danger imaginary, but it prevents danger from becoming a reason to call falsehood true.

**Mercy Explains the Long Invitation to Truth**

The idolaters were not condemned merely for failing to understand one question. Abraham questioned them, clarified the weakness of their objects, and called them to worship the Lord. The time given for reflection is itself a form of mercy. God allows a person to hear the truth, examine custom, and return before the final scene.

Mercy also appears in the way revelation tells the story. The audience is allowed to see the error from a distance and recognize its consequences without having to experience the regret of the condemned. A warning can be severe in language and merciful in purpose. It seeks rescue, not humiliation for its own sake.

If mercy is imagined as approval of every belief, the idolaters' error becomes harmless and judgment becomes unintelligible. The name Almighty restores moral seriousness. God can forgive, but He is not powerless before injustice or false worship. Mercy includes rescuing people from the powers that corrupt them, and that rescue may require judgment of persistent oppression.

The pairing also prevents believers from copying harshness. A human being does not possess God's complete knowledge or final authority. He may reject idolatry and oppose injustice, but he must not claim to know the final state of every heart. The Almighty judges; the servant warns, advises, and seeks mercy.

**Abraham's Prayer Shows Mercy Received Personally**

The preceding prayer names creation, guidance, provision, healing, forgiveness, wisdom, righteous company, honourable remembrance, and the Garden. Those requests are not evidence that Abraham doubts God's power. They are the language of a servant receiving mercy at every stage. The Almighty can give, and the Merciful chooses to give in ways that form the servant.

The refrain therefore turns the prayer into praise. Abraham asks, but God is the One who can answer. The idols receive requests but cannot respond. The Lord is powerful enough to govern the request and merciful enough to hear it. Prayer becomes a sign of dependence on both names.

The account of Noah in 7:59–64 displays the same balance. Noah calls his people to worship God, warns them of a tremendous day, and the story ends with God saving him and those with him while the rejecters are drowned. The parallel confirms that divine might and mercy accompany the prophetic call across different communities.

Mercy appears in the warning before judgment, while might appears in the rescue and the defeat of rejection. The names in this verse are therefore not decorative closure. They summarize how God deals with messengers and peoples: He gives clear guidance, protects truth, and remains merciful even when many refuse.

A messenger cannot force most people to believe. Abraham's people refused despite the sign. The final names remind the messenger that the outcome belongs to the Lord. God's might guarantees that rejection cannot defeat the truth, and God's mercy guarantees that the call is not a pointless demand for perfection from the hearer.

Success is therefore measured by faithful delivery and sound allegiance, not by immediate numbers. The believer can grieve over refusal without imagining that God's plan has failed. The Almighty is not weakened by a majority's denial, and the Merciful does not stop inviting people because some turn away.

**Power and Mercy Shape the Believer's Character**

A servant who remembers God's might resists false lords and refuses to be ruled by fear of people. A servant who remembers God's mercy avoids pride, forgives where forgiveness is right, and keeps hope alive for those who may return. The two names produce courage with tenderness rather than aggression or passivity.

This balance is visible in Abraham. He speaks firmly against idols, yet he asks forgiveness for his father. He rejects inherited worship, yet he seeks a truthful legacy among later generations. His courage comes from the Almighty, and his concern comes from the Merciful. The names provide the moral shape of his response.

The refrain after Abraham's story connects his debate to the chapter's earlier prophetic accounts. Different peoples invent different excuses, but the Lord remains the same. His signs vary with the messenger's circumstance, while His might and mercy remain the final explanation of rescue, warning, and judgment.

The verse leaves the hearer with a complete answer to idolatry. No created thing possesses the Almighty's power or the Merciful's care. Worship belongs to the Lord who can judge falsehood and forgive the servant who turns back. That is the truth the refrain seals into memory.
