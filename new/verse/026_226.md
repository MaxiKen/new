## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:226

> only saying what they never do?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Test Is the Distance Between Word and Deed**

The verse identifies a decisive defect: the poets say what they do not do. Speech becomes a performance detached from character. A person can praise bravery while avoiding every sacrifice, celebrate justice while exploiting the weak, or condemn falsehood while using lies whenever they serve his interest.

This is not a demand for human perfection. Everyone can fall short of his words and then repent. The condemnation concerns a pattern in which the gap is tolerated, repeated, and used to win influence. The speaker does not merely fail; he makes claims that create an image while refusing the conduct that would make the image true. His repeated contradiction trains listeners to admire appearance and to excuse betrayal, so the private gap becomes a public moral injury.

**Words Can Become a Substitute for Obedience**

A powerful description of good can make a person feel close to goodness without doing it. The audience applauds the speech, the speaker enjoys the applause, and the moral work is postponed. In that way language becomes a substitute for action rather than a guide toward action.

Quran 61:2–3 directly condemns saying what one does not do, and Quran 2:44 rebukes those who preach righteousness while forgetting themselves. These parallels show that the problem is not limited to poets. Poetry provides a vivid case because its emotional power can make a false life appear noble, but the same contradiction can affect scholars, leaders, parents, and ordinary believers. Sacred vocabulary does not cancel the obligation to embody the values being proclaimed.

**A Claim Becomes Trustworthy Through Practice**

The audience should test speech against conduct without pretending that only flawless people may speak. A person can advise patience while still struggling to become patient, provided he admits the struggle and works toward the advice. The hypocritical pattern appears when he demands a standard from others that he deliberately refuses for himself.

This distinction protects useful counsel. If every imperfect speaker were silenced, no one could remind anyone else. But if every eloquent speaker were trusted without examining his life, manipulation would flourish. Truthful speech welcomes correction and tries to embody what it recommends. Empty speech protects its reputation more than it pursues righteousness. Even small acts of consistency matter here: a promise kept, a wrong repaired, and an apology offered without publicity can make counsel credible.

**The Contradiction Damages the Audience**

Saying what one never does harms listeners because it makes virtue look theatrical. People begin to suspect every command, including a true command, because they have seen it used by a person who did not honor it. The speaker's inconsistency then becomes a barrier between the audience and the good he claimed to praise.

That harm explains why the Quran treats the gap seriously. A leader's words can authorize cruelty, a poet's praise can normalize pride, and a moral slogan can conceal a private bargain. The listener should not follow a beautiful statement into conduct that contradicts its content. He should ask whether the speaker's life gives the words weight. He should also ask whether the message itself points to Allah or merely builds dependence on the speaker.

**Practice Gives Language Its Moral Weight**

The Quran does not separate speech from action. A warning should lead to repentance, praise should lead to gratitude, and a call to justice should lead to fair dealing. The person who says what he does not do has broken that connection and made language serve vanity.

The verse therefore calls the listener to a disciplined sincerity. Speak less if necessary, but let what is said be supported by effort. When failure occurs, acknowledge it and return to Allah rather than defending the image. Words become guidance when they are joined to deeds, and deeds become clearer when truthful words direct them.

The gap between word and deed is especially dangerous in a religious setting. A speaker may use sacred language to gain trust, then exploit the trust for money, status, or control. The audience may assume that religious vocabulary proves religious character. The verse teaches the opposite: claims must be tested by conduct, and conduct must be measured by Allah's commands.

This does not require a person to wait until he is flawless before he advises anyone. A servant can remind others while admitting that he is also struggling. The difference lies in honesty and direction. He does not present himself as the finished embodiment of a standard while secretly defending the opposite. He welcomes correction and keeps working to make his life agree with his speech.

Quran 61:2–3 condemns the contradiction directly, and 2:44 asks why people command righteousness while forgetting themselves. These passages do not silence every imperfect reminder. They expose the arrogance of teaching a duty as a weapon against others while treating oneself as exempt. The cure is self-application: every command spoken to others must first be heard as a command to the speaker.

The audience also bears responsibility. It should not follow a famous speaker into wrong merely because he has said beautiful things. Nor should it reject a true principle simply because the person presenting it has failed. Separate the truth of the command from the credibility of the example, then demand that leaders repair the gap between them.

Action gives speech a history. A promise kept over time, a wrong admitted, and a sacrifice made when no one applauds can give simple words great weight. Conversely, repeated contradiction can make even a true phrase sound empty in the speaker's mouth. Allah knows the difference between a servant who fails and returns and a performer who has no intention of returning.

The verse therefore calls for integrity rather than theatrical perfection. Say what is true, do what is possible, admit what has not been done, and keep moving toward obedience. The distance between words and deeds should become a place of repentance and effort, not a permanent stage on which the speaker deceives others and himself.
