## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:17

> ˹It is they˺ who are patient, sincere, obedient, and charitable, and who pray for forgiveness before dawn.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Five Marks, and the Order They Come In**

The verse draws a portrait in a line. The people promised the gardens are *patient*, meaning steadfast under pressure; *sincere*, meaning truthful in word and intention; *obedient* in the specific sense of devotion that stands and waits before God; *charitable*, spending from what they have; and finally those who ask forgiveness in the last hours of the night.

The sequence rewards a slow reading. Steadfastness comes first because nothing else can be sustained without it; truthfulness follows because a task performed under pressure invites compromise in speech; devotion follows truthfulness, since a person who has settled the question of honesty can then settle into worship; generosity comes after, because giving is what a settled soul does with its goods. And behind all of it, unannounced, stands the night prayer, which is what the verse considers the last mark of the group.

**Qānitūn: the Posture Behind the Word**

The translation renders *qānit* as "obedient", which is accurate but thin. The word carries standing, constancy, silence, and a settled conformity to what is asked — the posture of someone who has taken his place and stayed there. The Book uses it of Mary, told to be *devout to her Lord* along with those who bow (3:43), and of the women and men who obey with devotion in 33:31.

In older Arabic a *qānit* could be the one who stands silent in prayer, and the meanings connect: devotion here is not an emotional warmth that comes and goes, but a stance. A person who has understood what he is before God does not need to be persuaded into position again each time his feelings change. That is why the word sits between truthfulness and charity in a list of durable qualities; it is the quality that makes the others durable.

**Patient Without Qualification**

The first word of the portrait is *ṣābirīn*, patient or steadfast, and the Book leaves it unqualified. No object is attached, no period is specified, and no kind of hardship is singled out, which is how the Qur'an usually uses the term. The patience in view is not one virtue among several but the capacity to hold a position under weight of any kind.

The commentators grouped the applications under three headings and left them open: patience in obeying what has been commanded, patience in avoiding what has been forbidden, and patience with what cannot be changed — loss, illness, and the wrongs of other people. All three require a person to keep doing something he does not feel like doing, and all three are invisible when they succeed. A patient man at the funeral of his child looks like a man who is not weeping rather than like a man performing a virtue, which is one reason the Book tends to name patience first and reward it last.

**Charity in the Qur'an's Sense Is Not Only Money**

The participle rendered "charitable" belongs to the verb *anfaqa*, to spend. The commentaries were careful to say that the spending in view is not limited to currency: effort, knowledge, time, and influence all fall under it. The Book's praise for those who spend attaches a condition to the act — that it be in the way of God — which is what distinguishes the generosity being praised from generosity performed for reputation.

The Book also insists that the giving of the poorest is not disqualified by its size. The widow's two coins in the prophetic tradition, and the repeated Qur'anic emphasis that God does not look at the quantity of a gift but at what the giver had and intended, belong to the same teaching. The verse's list therefore has room for the person who owns almost nothing, provided he is willing to let go of what he has.

**"Before Dawn": an Hour Chosen for Its Darkness**

The final phrase translates *asḥār*, the last part of the night before the sky begins to lighten, and the choice is deliberate. The Book describes the night prayer as a habit of the righteous in more than one place: they *“used to sleep only little in the night, and pray for forgiveness before dawn”* (51:17–18), and the Prophet is told to *“rise at ˹the last˺ part of the night, offering additional prayers, so your Lord may raise you to a station of praise.”* (17:79).

The hour has nothing to recommend it to a person seeking an audience. It is the time when sleep is heaviest and no one is watching, when the body argues against getting up and there is no reputation to be gained by doing so. The Book mentions those who stand in it as a class of their own: *“˹They are˺ those who spend ˹a good portion of˺ the night, prostrating themselves and standing before their Lord.”* (25:64), and the command to the Prophet runs to the same effect: *“Stand all night ˹in prayer˺ except a little”* (73:2).

**Why the List Ends in the Dark**

Placing the night prayer last makes the verse's argument complete. The earlier items can be seen; a patient man, a truthful man, and a generous man are all visible to neighbours, and even the most sincere have ways of knowing and receiving the respect their conduct draws. The pre-dawn request for forgiveness cannot be seen by anyone but the one addressed.

That is the note this verse chooses to end on, and it is consistent with the sūrah's insistence that the accounting happens where God sees. The same people who confessed their sins at the beginning of the previous verse are here shown confessing again while the world sleeps. The habit of asking forgiveness in the dark is the sign that the earlier virtues were not being performed for anyone.
