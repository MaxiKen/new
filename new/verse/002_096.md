## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:96

> You will surely find them clinging to life more eagerly than any other people, even more than polytheists. Each one of them wishes to live a thousand years. But even if they were to live that long, it would not save them from the punishment. And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Clinging of People**

The verdict of verse 95 becomes a portrait: *you will surely find them clinging to life more eagerly than any other people*. The address — *you will find them* — is directed at the Prophet ﷺ and through him at every observer, and its verb carries the sense of discovery upon examination: look at this community's actual behavior and you will find this, the way one finds a fact in evidence. The superlative is the verse's first shock. Among all peoples, in all their variety of attachment to living, these are the *most* clinging — and the comparison clause sharpens the shock into a paradox: *even more than polytheists*. The commentators press the paradox's logic. The polytheist addressed here is one without belief in a hereafter of reward: death, on his account, is annihilation or descent to a shadow-land, and attachment to life is therefore the whole of what his cosmology leaves him. If the people of an exclusive paradise cling harder than that, their behavior testifies against their doctrine more loudly than any refusal could: the man who expects annihilation clings because clinging is all he has; the man who clings harder while claiming paradise shows that, in the register of conduct, he expects annihilation too. The verse completes the challenge's work. Verse 94 asked for a wish; verse 95 predicted its absence; verse 96 documents the wish that is present instead — the wish for more life — and files it as the community's own refutation, observed, comparative, and public.

**A Thousand Years**

The second sentence gives the clinging its number: *each one of them wishes to live a thousand years*. The commentators divide on the number's reference and its sense. Grammatically, the wish can attach either to the Jews described at the verse's opening or to the polytheists of the comparison — the second reading would make the thousand years the idolater's wish, with the Jews exceeding even that; al-Rāzī notes the alternative construction but finds the primary reading the natural one. Historically, some exegetes connected the figure to Persian usage, citing the long-life greetings exchanged in that culture — blessings of ten thousand years spoken on ordinary occasions — and reading the verse as describing Zoroastrian-influenced expectation. Al-Rāzī prefers the simpler linguistic account: the thousand is idiom for the indefinite many, the way a language says *forever* when it means *as long as possible*. The weighing matters less than the wish's shape, which is the same under every reading: quantity invoked where quality is unavailable. A thousand years is not a plan but a panic's arithmetic — the longest number the imagination can carry, deployed against the boundary it cannot cross. And *each one of them* individualizes the panic: this is not a policy or a doctrine but a wish held person by person, the private prayer of every member, which makes it the truest available statement of what the community actually believes about death.

**What a Thousand Years Would Not Do**

The verse then runs the arithmetic to its end: *even if they were to live that long, it would not save them from the punishment*. The conditional is concessive — grant the impossible premise, the wish fulfilled, the millennium lived — and the conclusion is unchanged: the punishment arrives exactly where the extra centuries leave it. The sentence dismantles the wish from inside. Longevity is being treated by the wisher as a refuge, and the verse shows the refuge has no walls: death deferred is death still owed, and a thousand years of deferral changes the date without touching the destination. This is the recital's answer to the numbered-days doctrine of verse 80 arriving from a new direction — there, the Fire's term was imagined short; here, the life before it is imagined long; and both imaginations fail against the same fact, that the two terms are set by Allah and not by preference. The commentators add the psychological layer through a saying of al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī: the hypocrite is more covetous of the world than the idolater — and the explanation given is the gnawing awareness of consequences. The greed for life grows precisely where assurance is weakest; the man who is certain of paradise does not need a millennium, and the man who clamors for one is confessing, in the currency of desire, what verse 95 stated in the currency of prediction. The hypocrisy's delusion of safety has a truth beneath it — that his actions preclude the safety — and the truth is not fully hidden from him, which is why the clinging is *eager* rather than merely strong.

**All-Seeing of What They Do**

The closing clause repeats the audit formula with a shift of divine name: *Allah is All-Seeing of what they do*. Verse 95 closed with knowledge of the wrongdoers; verse 96 closes with sight of the deeds — the same surveillance described from its two sides, the ledger and the eye. The name *Baṣīr* carries the seeing that penetrates: not observation of surfaces but vision into the doing, its motives and its clinging included. The shift suits the verse's content. What verse 96 documents is visible behavior — eagerness for life, the wish for years, the observable contradiction between conduct and creed — and the clause files all of it under a sight that misses nothing. The verse's whole argument has been evidentiary: you will *find* them, look and see the comparison, hear the number of the wish; the closing clause adds that the finding is not only the observer's. Allah sees what they do — including the doing's inner shape, the fear beneath the eagerness, the disbelief beneath the profession — and the seeing is the warrant for the sentence to come. For the reader the clause installs the verse's permanent use: attachment to life is not hidden sin but visible testimony, and it testifies continuously. What a person's clinging says about his actual expectation of death is on display to every observer and recorded by the All-Seeing — which makes this verse the recital's instrument for reading any community, including one's own, by the single most reliable indicator available: how its members behave at the boundary of the life they claim not to fear losing.

**The Believer's Measure of Attachment**

Islam does not indict love of life as such, and the verse must be read with that distinction sharp. The prayer taught for affliction asks for life *while life is better* — life held as a good, received conditionally, surrendered when its better term arrives (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6351). The believer of the sūrah's own portrait sends his good ahead and finds it with Allah; his relation to years is stewardship rather than clutching. What verse 96 condemns is clinging as eschatological strategy — life accumulated against death, years pursued as a refuge from the audit, the millennium-wish as a substitute for the repaired record. The economics of the passage names the trade exactly: a thousand years purchased at the price of the Hereafter is verse 86's transaction conducted in the currency of time, and the verse's concessive clause publishes the exchange rate — the purchase fails even when it succeeds. The recital leaves its audience with the diagnostic in working order. Eagerness for life is a gauge, and its reading is honest: the stronger the profession of paradise and the stronger the clinging in the same person, the wider the gap between the two — and the gap is the verse's whole subject. The All-Seeing watches the gauge along with the deed, and the sūrah files the reading here so that no later claimant of guaranteed bliss can plead that the instrument was hidden from him.
