## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:96

> You will surely find them clinging to life more eagerly than any other people, even more than polytheists. Each one of them wishes to live a thousand years. But even if they were to live that long, it would not save them from the punishment. And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Comparison That Distinguishes**

The verse compares the community's attachment to life with that of the polytheists, and it says that the community's grip is tighter. The comparison is striking because the polytheists of Arabia had no belief in an afterlife at all, and a person who expects nothing after death has every reason to cling to the life he has.

The Book draws the comparison in order to expose the contradiction between the community's stated belief and its felt priorities. A people who taught that the righteous pass to a permanent reward should have been less anxious about their years than people who expected nothing, and the verse states that the reverse is the case. The comparison also prevents the reader from treating the community's attachment as a defect peculiar to it. The Book is describing what belief in an accounting looks like when it is not accompanied by preparation, and the portrait is offered to anyone who holds the doctrine without the discipline.

**A Thousand Years**

The figure of a thousand years appears in the verse as the length of life the community wished for, and the commentators discussed how it should be read. Some reports connect the expression to the Zoroastrians and other groups in the region, and even preserve a phrase used when a person sneezed. Al-Rāzī considered the detail unhelpful and preferred to read the number as an idiom for a very long span rather than a claim about a specific population.

Under either reading, the sentence's point does not depend on the number. Whether the wish is for a thousand years or simply for more time, the wish reveals an attachment that the community's own teaching should have loosened, and the verse treats the desire itself as the subject.

**The Oldest Idol**

The Book's description of this attachment is one of its most durable psychological observations. A community can be entirely correct about the Hereafter in its formal teaching and still organise its conduct around postponement, and the verse shows what that looks like from the outside. The wish for more years is not a plan; it is a reluctance to face an accounting.

The Qur'an treats this reluctance as a condition it addresses directly, telling its hearers that no soul knows what it will earn tomorrow and that the time of every person is fixed. Its purpose is not to make people indifferent to life but to remove the illusion that more time by itself would change anything. The community in this verse had more time than most and had not changed.

**What the Extra Years Would Not Do**

The middle of the verse states plainly that longevity would not remove the punishment. The Book does not present death as the punishment here; it presents the punishment as something that a longer life cannot postpone indefinitely or reduce. A person who lives a thousand years and dies on the last day still arrives at the same accounting.

The verse's reasoning matches its earlier treatment of the claim about numbered days in the Fire. The community wanted to negotiate duration, and the sūrah answered that duration was not the issue. What is at stake in the Book's accounts is the direction of a life, and no length of time repairs a direction that has been chosen. The Book makes the same point when it tells its hearers that no soul knows what it will earn tomorrow and that every term is fixed. The purpose is not to make people indifferent to life but to remove the illusion that more time by itself would change anything.

**Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī on the Hypocrite**

The commentators preserved a saying of al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī that a hypocrite is greedier for this world than an idolater, and the explanation offered is that the hypocrite has reasons to fear what comes after death while the idolater has none. The observation fits the verse exactly: the community described here combined a formal belief in the Hereafter with a horror of arriving there.

That combination has a spiritual cost the Book names elsewhere. A person who believes in an accounting and does not prepare for it lives under a pressure that the outright denier escapes, and the wish for more time becomes a way of managing the pressure without addressing what produces it. The saying illustrates why the Book portrays this condition as a contradiction rather than as ordinary fear. A person who denies the Hereafter loses nothing he expected, while a person who affirms it and clings to this life is holding two positions that cannot both be acted upon.

**Seeing What They Do**

The sentence closes with the statement that God is All-Seeing of what they do, and the attribute suits the material of the verse rather than serving as a general reminder. The community's attachment to life was visible in its conduct long before the verse described it, and the closing clause states that the observation is not the narrator's inference.

The sūrah uses the same ending at the close of the instruction to the believers about prayer and alms a few verses later (2:110), which places the two closing attributes side by side and leaves the reader with a comparison. The same seeing that exposes the community's grip on life also witnesses whatever the believers send ahead of themselves.

**Reading the Portrait as a Warning**

The verse is one of a series of portraits in this passage, and each portrait is followed by an instruction to the community receiving the revelation. What the sentence describes is a human tendency rather than a peculiarity of one group: the wish for time without the wish for accounting. The sūrah will soon command its own hearers to prepare for a day whose arrival they cannot delay, and the command lands harder after this description of people who had been told the same thing. The command that follows in the sūrah directs the believers to prepare for a day whose arrival cannot be postponed, and the instruction lands harder after a portrait of people who had been told the same thing and had wished for time instead.

**Life as a Trust Rather Than a Prize**

The Book nowhere treats long life as a misfortune, and the Prophet ﷺ described the best of people as those whose lives are long and whose deeds are good. What the verse criticises is not the desire to live but the desire to live instead of preparing, and the two are separated by what a person does with the years he is given. The community in this sentence had centuries of history and a scripture in its hands, and the verse says that its members wanted more time while treating what they had as though it were an inconvenience. Read that way, the sentence becomes a question about what a person is doing with the life already in his possession. The Book's answer is the instruction to send good deeds ahead, because they are the only part of a life that travels with the one who sent them.
