## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:35

> If you find their denial unbearable, then build—if you can—a tunnel through the earth or stairs to the sky to bring them a ˹more compelling˺ sign. Had Allah so willed, He could have guided them all. So do not be one of those ignorant ˹of this fact˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Challenge Built to Be Impossible**

The verse offers the Prophet a way out of his distress and then closes it in the same sentence. If the denial is unbearable, he may go underground and dig his way out, or climb a stairway into the sky, in order to bring them a sign they cannot dismiss. The conditional "if you can" leaves no doubt that he cannot, and the rhetorical shape of the instruction is the argument: no effort available to a human being can produce the outcome he wants.

The Book uses the same device elsewhere with the same intention. Ask them whether, if God made the night perpetual for them until the Day of Judgment, any god besides Allah could bring them sunlight (28:71), and the same question is repeated about perpetual day and the gift of night to rest in (28:72). The impossibility is not stated as a limitation on God. It is stated as a limitation on the audience's demand.

**The Sign That Was Already in Their Hands**

The demand for a more compelling sign had already been answered in a way the Makkans could examine. The Book challenges them directly: if they are in doubt about what was revealed to the servant of God, let them produce a sūrah like it and call their helpers besides Allah, if what they say is true (2:23); let them produce ten fabricated sūrahs like it (11:13); and if all humans and jinn were to combine to produce the equivalent of this Qur'an, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported one another (17:88).

So the tunnel and the stairway are not the only routes to a sign. The sign was reciting in the streets and marketplaces of Makkah in their own language, and the challenge to match it stood open for anyone who thought the message was human work.

**The Grief That Is Being Addressed**

The verse does not rebuke the Prophet for being sad. It reasons with him about what his sadness is asking for. Elsewhere the Book treats the same distress with tenderness, telling him that he will perhaps grieve himself to death over their disbelief (26:3), and asking whether he will destroy himself with grief if they do not believe in this message (18:6). The emotion is understood, and its limit is explained.

The limit is that the outcome belongs to God. A messenger is responsible for delivering the message clearly, and he is not responsible for whether a particular listener accepts it. Without that boundary, a preacher's love for his people turns into a demand he cannot meet, and the Book sets the boundary before the demand can do damage.

**"Had Allah Willed, He Could Have Guided Them All"**

The middle of the verse states the fact that settles the question: had God willed, He could have guided them all. The same claim appears in several places with variations. Had Allah willed, He could have easily made all humanity into a single community of believers, but He admits into His mercy whoever He wills (42:8); had He willed, He could have made the whole earth believe, so no one is asked to force people into belief (10:99); and had He willed, they would not have associated partners with Him, and the Prophet is told that he has not been appointed as their keeper or their maintainer (6:107).

Commentators drew opposite conclusions from such verses. Some read them as evidence that God creates human acts and determines the moral destination of each person, while others read them as a statement that God could have compelled everyone into one community but chose instead to let them choose, so that their religious destiny would be genuinely theirs. The second reading accounts better for the sūrah's steady assumption that its audience is being addressed as responsible agents and not as prisoners of a decree.

**The Instruction Not to Be Ignorant**

The verse ends with a warning to the Prophet: do not be one of those ignorant of this fact. The ignorance in view is not a lack of education. It is the failure to understand how guidance works, which would lead a messenger to measure his own worth by the number of people who accept his message, or to believe that the outcome is within his power to force.

The same warning is given to him elsewhere in the sūrah where he is told not to drive away those who call on their Lord, and where he is told that God knows best who is rightly guided. A messenger who understood the mechanism would work without either despair or coercion, because both responses come from the same misunderstanding of where the decision lies.

**What the Verse Leaves the Messenger With**

Once the tunnel and the stairway are taken away, one thing remains: the message itself, delivered with patience for as long as God grants. That is the position the verse pushes its reader toward, and the sūrah immediately turns to the different responses of different hearers. Some will respond, and the dead will be raised to be returned to their Lord, which is the subject the next verse takes up.

The passage leaves no room for a prophet who resigns, and no room for one who forces. What the sentences before and after this one supply is the working conditions of a mission whose results are not in the missionary's hands.
