## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:197

> It is only a brief enjoyment. Then Hell will be their home—what an evil place to rest!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Brief Enjoyment**

The verse follows the warning of the sentence before it and states what the freedom of the deniers amounts to: a small enjoyment, followed by the Fire as their refuge. The sentence is the answer to the impression that the previous verse had told the reader to resist.

The Book uses the vocabulary of a little or a brief span for the present life in several passages, and the word in this verse carries the same sense. The community was expected to adopt that measure as its own rather than the estimate that appearances suggest. What the deniers have secured is measured in the sentence as small, and the word chosen for it settles the comparison that the community had been tempted to make.

**The Word for Little**

The Arabic term rendered as a little names something slight in extent or duration, and the commentators applied it to the span of worldly comfort. The sentence does not deny that the deniers enjoy their position; it denies that the enjoyment is large.

The Book's descriptions of the worldly life elsewhere use the same kind of measurement, and the consistency of the judgment is what the community's teachers pointed to when they warned against envy of the prosperous. A passage says that the present life compared with the Hereafter is nothing but a fleeting enjoyment, and another states that its ornaments are a passing enjoyment while the Hereafter with God belongs to the mindful. The verse before the reader states the same estimate in a single word.

**The Refuge Named**

The second half of the verse names the Fire as the refuge of the people described, and the choice of the word is deliberate. What the deniers thought of as the world's open land ends in a place described as a destination they cannot leave.

The Book names the Fire as the destination of the arrogant and the deniers in many passages, and it does so with the same vocabulary of arrival. The word for refuge carries the sense of a place to which a person is brought, and the Book applies it to those who spent their lives opposing the message. One passage tells those who treat others as rivals to God to enjoy themselves, since their destination is the Fire, and another states that the wrongdoers will ultimately be returned to it.

**The Resting Place Inverted**

The verse closes with the exclamation that the resting place named is evil, and the term carries the sense of a place where a traveller stops or a person lies down. The image of the present life as a journey appears in the sentence through the word for movement in the previous verse.

The Book applies the same term to the Fire in other passages, where it is described as an evil place of rest for those who arrive there. The contrast is between the welcome that the next verse promises to the mindful and the destination named here, and the two are set side by side in the sūrah's closing lines.

**The Book's Other Descriptions of the Destination**

The Book describes the Fire in similar terms in several passages. One states that the people of the Fire will burn there, and asks what an evil place of rest it is. Another describes the wrongdoers being sent back to their place in it, and a third describes it as a home for those who transgressed.

The repetition of the same images across the Book gives the sentence its weight. The verse before the reader is one of a series of statements in which the destination of those who rejected the message is named in identical language, and the community's teachers used those passages together when they explained the verse.

**The Comparison With the Welcome That Follows**

The construction of the passage places this verse directly before the one that describes the gardens promised to those who revere God. The word for refuge in this sentence and the word for welcome in the next are set against each other, and the two destinations are the alternatives the sūrah has been presenting.

The last verses of the sūrah therefore close with a comparison rather than with an argument. The community that recites them is shown the two endings and left to draw the conclusion, and the sentence before the reader is the darker half of the pair.


The verse consists of two brief statements and an exclamation, and its brevity is part of its effect. What the previous verse described at length is dismissed in a clause, and the dismissal carries the judgment.

The Book uses short sentences in this way when it states the outcome of a long dispute. The reader is given the fact without embellishment, and the terseness of the sentence leaves the impression that the matter requires no further elaboration.

**What the Verse Teaches**

The verse teaches the community that the comfort of those who deny the message has a limit that is fixed in advance. It also teaches it to describe that comfort with the word the Book uses for it rather than with the estimate that appearances suggest.

The instruction belongs to the preparation the sūrah has been giving its readers for their life in a world where the two groups live side by side. The sūrah's closing verses repeat the same distinction, and the community is given the two outcomes as the frame within which it should read its own circumstances. The next verse turns to the portion of the other group, and the transition makes the two destinations the sūrah's final subject.
