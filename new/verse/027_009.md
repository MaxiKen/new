## Sūrah an-Naml 27:9

> O Moses! It is truly I. I am Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name Comes Before the Assignment**

The voice now names itself, and it names Mūsā first. Address, then identification, then attributes — and only afterwards, in the verses that follow, any instruction. Nothing is asked of him until he knows who is asking. The sequence is the opposite of how human authority usually announces itself, where the demand arrives and credentials are produced only if challenged.

Being called by name matters here as well. Mūsā has come to a stranger's fire hoping to ask directions, and the first word he hears is his own name. Whoever is speaking already knows him. The Qur'an uses direct vocative address at the openings of several prophetic calls, and the effect each time is to establish that the relationship precedes the mission. He is not recruited as a useful man who happened to be passing. He is summoned as someone already known, at a fire he was led to see. Al-Rāzī reads the naming as the first act of consolation in an encounter that is about to become frightening, and the reading is supported by what happens two verses later, when Mūsā runs.

**Three Ways to Read the Arabic, and Why It Matters Little**

The clause rendered *It is truly I. I am Allah* is *innahu anā Allāh*, and the reciters and grammarians read it in more than one way. Al-Ṭabarī reports the sense as *the matter is that I am God*, taking the pronoun *hu* as an impersonal reference to the situation. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī allow a reading in which the clause simply starts fresh, giving *O Moses, I am God*, with the particle marking a new statement rather than linking to what came before.

The variation is grammatical and does not alter the content. Every reading delivers the same two facts: the speaker is God, and He is telling Mūsā so in the first person. What the variation does show is how compressed the Arabic is. Three words carry a self-disclosure that other traditions render in long sentences, and the compression is itself a feature — there is no argument, no proof offered, no appeal to a prior expectation. The statement stands on its own authority. A being who must argue for his identity is not the being described here.

**How This Differs From "I Am That I Am"**

The obvious comparison is with the Hebrew scriptures, where Mūsā at the same mountain asks for a name and receives *ehyeh asher ehyeh*, variously rendered *I am that I am*, *I am who I am*, or *I will be what I will be* (Exodus 3:14). The Qur'anic version is not a variant of that exchange, and the difference is instructive rather than polemical.

In the biblical passage, Mūsā asks the question and the answer is a phrase whose grammar has been debated for two millennia — whether it names, refuses to name, or promises presence. In the Qur'anic passage, Mūsā asks nothing and the answer is a name plainly given: *Allāh*. The ambiguity that exists in the Arabic is about sentence structure, not about whether an identity was disclosed. Where the two traditions converge is in the reading, held by many Jewish and Christian interpreters, that the Hebrew phrase is itself a name or contains one. On that reading both accounts report the same event in the same terms: at the fire, God told Mūsā His name. The Qur'an is simply more direct about it.

**Mighty and Wise: Attributes Fitted to a Mission Against Pharaoh**

Two names follow the self-identification, and their selection is precise. *Al-ʿAzīz* means the invincible, the one who cannot be overcome — the root carries strength, scarcity and inaccessibility together. *Al-Ḥakīm* means the wise, the one whose acts are placed correctly.

Consider what Mūsā is about to be sent to do. He must return to the country he fled as a wanted man and confront the most powerful ruler of his world with a demand. The two obvious failure modes are that the sender lacks the power to back him, or has the power but no plan. The names answer both, in order. Al-ʿAzīz addresses the question of whether Pharaoh can win; al-Ḥakīm addresses the question of whether this mission makes sense. The pairing is not generic. Just three verses earlier the Qur'an was named as coming from the All-Wise, All-Knowing (27:6), where knowledge and wisdom were the relevant attributes for a book. Here, at the start of a confrontation, might replaces knowledge. The Qur'an selects divine names to fit the work in front of them.

**A Voice Without a Body, and What the Silence Covers**

The verse reports speech and nothing else. No form is described, no direction is given for where the voice came from, no account of what the fire looked like at this moment. This restraint is consistent across the Qur'an's treatment of the incident in all three chapters, and it is theologically load-bearing rather than merely stylistic.

The immediately preceding verse declared *Glory be to Allah, the Lord of all worlds.* (27:8), which is a statement that God is beyond likeness to created things. Having said that, the narrative cannot then describe a shape in the flames without contradicting itself. What remains is speech — and speech, in Islamic theology, became the point on which this episode turns. Mūsā's traditional title, *kalīm Allāh*, the one God spoke to, derives from this night and from the further statement in an-Nisāʾ that God spoke to Mūsā directly. The encounter is remembered for what was heard, not for what was seen, and the verse's silence about everything visual is the reason. The first command follows in the next verse, and it concerns something Mūsā was already holding.
