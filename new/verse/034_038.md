## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:38

> As for those who strive to discredit Our revelations, it is they who will be confined in punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Description, Returned With a Different Ending**

This verse repeats, word for word in the Arabic, the description the sūrah gave in its fifth verse — *alladhīna yasʿawna fī āyātinā muʿājizīn*, those who exert themselves against Our signs seeking to frustrate them. What changes is the second half. There the sentence ended with a torment of agonising pain (34:5); here it ends with confinement. A chapter that repeats a clause exactly and then alters its conclusion is not padding. It is completing a frame. The first occurrence opened the sūrah's case against the organised opposition; this one closes it, and everything between the two — the mockery of resurrection, the ruin of Sheba, the quarrel of leaders and followers, the boast about wealth — sits inside the brackets. Readers of the Arabic would hear the repetition immediately, the way a listener hears a musical phrase return at the end of a movement.

**Work Against the Signs, Once More**

The vocabulary was examined when the phrase first appeared, and the key terms deserve restating because the verse's force depends on them. *Saʿā* is a verb of physical exertion — hurrying, striving, labouring at something — not of holding an opinion. *Muʿājizīn* names the objective: to render the signs *ʿājiz*, incapable, unable to deliver. The people described are therefore not those who failed to be convinced but those who worked to ensure the message could not do its work. The sūrah has shown exactly what that work looked like in the verses between the two occurrences: the staged mockery inviting a crowd to look at a man (34:7), the coordinated plotting by night and day that the followers eventually named (34:33), the boast deployed to discredit the believers by comparison (34:35). The definition given in verse 5 has been filled in by twenty-three verses of illustration before being restated here.

**muḥḍarūn: Brought In and Held**

The new element is the closing word, *muḥḍarūn*, from *aḥḍara*, to bring someone into presence, to produce them before an authority. It is a legal and administrative term rather than a poetic one: the person *muḥḍar* is the one fetched and made to stand where he is required. Al-Rūm uses the same participle for those who denied the signs and the meeting with their Lord, saying they *"will be confined in punishment"* (30:16). The image is of custody rather than of burning. What this adds to the earlier verse is a change of category: verse 5 described what they will feel, and this one describes their legal status. They will be produced, held, and kept, which is a condition that begins before any sensation of pain and does not depend on it.

**Being Produced Is the General Rule; Where You Are Produced Is Not**

The Qur'an says everyone will be brought forward — Yā-Sīn states that all will be *"brought before Us"* — so the verb by itself does not distinguish these people from anyone else. What distinguishes them is the destination attached: they are brought **into the punishment** rather than merely into the presence. This distinction is worth drawing out because it clarifies what the verse actually threatens. Nobody escapes the summons; the universal appearance is not itself the penalty. The penalty is where the summons deposits you. Al-Ḥajj makes the point by pairing the same description with a location: those who strive against the revelations *"will be the residents of the Hellfire"* (22:51). Custody, in these verses, is not a stage on the way to a verdict. It is the verdict's execution.

**The Irony in the Word**

There is a reversal contained in *muḥḍarūn* that the Arabic makes audible. These are people characterised by exertion — running, striving, working at their opposition. The word that describes their end is a passive participle: they are brought. Every verb attached to them in verse 5 and in this verse's first half is active and effortful; the verb attached to their outcome removes their agency entirely. A man who spent his life making things happen is finally a man who is fetched. The sūrah has used this kind of grammatical reversal before, describing those who made themselves great and those who were made weak in the quarrel scene (34:31), where the forms of the verbs delivered a verdict before either party spoke.

**Why the Verse Is Placed Immediately After the Promise**

The two preceding verses described the multiplied reward and the secure upper rooms of those who believe and do good (34:37). This one supplies the alternative in a single line, and the brevity is itself an argument about proportion. There is also a precise contrast in the imagery of the two verses. The believers were placed in elevated chambers and called *secure*; these are brought in and held. One group is housed, the other is detained. The Arabic sets the two states against each other using the ordinary vocabulary of residence and custody, and the difference between an upper room and a cell is not primarily a difference in comfort but in whether the door is yours to open. The Qur'an frequently pairs the two destinies, and in this sūrah the pairing has appeared twice: at verses 4 and 5, and now at 37 and 38. In both pairs the promise comes first and receives more words than the threat. That ordering is consistent enough across the Book to count as a deliberate emphasis. A reader who takes the chapter's temperature from its severity would misread it; the severe sentences are short, and they follow longer sentences about forgiveness, honourable provision, multiplied reward and security. The next verse repeats the sūrah's statement about provision and adds something that has not yet been said.
