## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:98

> Whoever is an enemy of Allah, His angels, His messengers, Gabriel, and Michael, then ˹let them know that˺ Allah is certainly the enemy of the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The List and Its Order**

The verse widens the objection from a single angel to the whole order of divine agency. God is named first, then His angels, then His messengers, and then the two angels at the end of the list. The arrangement moves from the One who sends to the means by which the sending reaches human beings, and it closes with the two figures the community had singled out.

The sentence's structure implies a point the commentators drew out. A person who takes up enmity with one link in that chain has placed himself against the chain, because each item in the list acts by the command of the first. The verse does not treat the preference for one angel over another as a harmless opinion, and the closing clause states the consequence.

**Why Gabriel and Michael Are Named Separately**

The mention of two angels after the general term might look redundant, and the commentators explained that the Book often names a particular after a general category for emphasis. The example they gave is a passage elsewhere that mentions fruit and then names dates and pomegranates specifically, making clear that the general term was not intended to be exhaustive without illustration.

The pattern also appears in passages where angels are described as messengers, since the Arabic terminology overlaps and the Book uses the same word for the heavenly and the human carriers of divine commands. **The Two Angels and Their Standing**

The community's own traditions gave Michael a central place as the advocate and protector of Israel, and assigned Gabriel functions involving severity and judgement. The commentaries note that in Jewish literature as a whole Gabriel is not regarded negatively, though a strand of tradition connected him with destruction, which helps explain how the objection recorded in the previous verse could be sustained.

**Enmity With God Answered in Kind**

The verse states that God is the enemy of the disbelievers, and the sentence is phrased as a declaration rather than as a threat. The Book uses this reciprocal language for those who take up hostility toward God and His messengers, and it places the consequence in the same sentence as the offence so that the two cannot be separated.

The Prophet ﷺ described the relation from the other direction in a well-known report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, in which God declares war on whoever takes a friend of His as an enemy. The report concerns the people who draw near to God through the obligations and the voluntary acts He loves, and it states that such a person becomes one whom God protects and answers. Read beside this verse, the two texts show that the relation of friendship or hostility is reciprocal and that it is determined by conduct rather than by doctrine about angels.

**A Warning That Applies to the Listener**

The verse is addressed to a community that had made an objection, and its closing clause carries a general principle. Enmity toward the messengers of God is not a disagreement about personnel, and the Book treats it as a position adopted toward God Himself. The sūrah's later verses will repeat the theme when it describes the community's treatment of the prophets it had received.

For the community now receiving the revelation, the warning has a practical edge. The Qur'an's own account of belief includes faith in the angels and in the messengers without exception, and the sūrah states this among the defining marks of the believers. **The Verdict Left Standing**

The sentence ends with a statement rather than an instruction, and the sūrah moves on. The community's objection has been answered by describing the process of revelation in the previous verse, and the present sentence states what is at stake for anyone who maintains the objection. **The Divine Court Is Not Divided**

The objection the verse answers rests on an assumption the Book rejects: that the servants of God can be sorted into those who favour one community and those who oppose it. The sūrah's list removes the assumption by placing the two named angels inside the same category as God's messengers and the rest of the angels. The order of the sentence is itself an argument, since it moves from the One who sends to the means by which the sending arrives, and it treats the whole chain as a single act of divine administration. A community that takes up hostility toward one link has placed itself against the chain rather than against a colleague within it. The closing clause states the consequence in the language of reciprocity, and the Book's account of friendship with God elsewhere in the sūrah makes the same point from the other direction.
