## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:158

> Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺. And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing.

**The Two Hills Among the Marks of God**

Ṣafā and Marwah are two low hills at the edge of the Sacred Precinct, and the sentence enrols them among the *shaʿāʾir* of God — the appointed marks by which His worship is recognised. Al-Ṭabarī explains the word as the stations God set apart for the rites He taught Ibrāhīm, so that the hill is not honoured for its stone but for the worship attached to it by divine appointment. The walking between them reaches back before the Arabs: the long report of Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Bukhārī describes Hājar running across the valley in search of water for Ismāʿīl, climbing Ṣafā to scan the horizon, then Marwah to look again, seven times, until the water broke open at the child's heel and the well of Zamzam was given. The Prophet ﷺ told the Companions that her running is the reason the people walk between the two hills, and that God had thereby made the place a station of pilgrimage rather than a piece of terrain.

**Why the Sentence Was Sent Down**

A community in Medina hesitated over the rite. Reports gathered by al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr explain the hesitation in two ways that reinforce each other. The first is the memory of the *jāhiliyya*: people used to consider the walk an invention of the age of idols, and after Islam they held back from a practice they associated with the old religion. The second is more concrete, and al-Shaʿbī states it plainly: Isāf stood on Ṣafā and Nāʾilah on Marwah, two images the Arabs used to touch as they passed, and the believers recoiled from a route lined with stone gods. A third report adds the Ansār who had entered *iḥrām* for Manāt at a place called Mushallal before Islam; they had been taught to avoid the walk, and they carried the inhibition into Islam. The verse answers all three: the hills belong to God's rites, not to the idolaters who abused them.

**ʿĀʾisha and ʿUrwa on the Meaning of "No Blame"**

ʿUrwa ibn al-Zubayr read the phrase *lā junāḥa* as permission to leave the walk undone, and asked ʿĀʾisha about it. Her answer, recorded in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, is the decisive reading: if the matter were as he supposed, the sentence would have denied blame for *not* walking between them, whereas the wording denies blame for walking. The Prophet ﷺ performed the walk, and she added that he thereby established it as the practice of the pilgrimage, so that no one should abandon it. Al-Zuhrī transmits a further exchange in which ʿUrwa put her answer to Abū Bakr ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn al-Ḥārith, who had not heard the report; he replied with the observation of the learned men of his day that most people, apart from the group ʿĀʾisha named, had regarded the walk as a survival of the age of ignorance, while some of the Ansār said they had been commanded to circumambulate the House but not to walk between the two hills. He concluded that the verse had been sent down about both groups at once, which is why its wording is a removal of blame rather than a fresh command.

**The Ruling the Jurists Built on the Verse**

Al-Qurṭubī argues that the Prophet's own practice, together with his instruction that the community take its rites from him, makes the walk an obligation rather than an option, and that a plain reading of the phrase as a licence to omit contradicts a practice he never omitted. Mālik and al-Shāfiʿī counted the *saʿy* among the pillars of the pilgrimage, so that its omission cannot be repaired by expiation; the Ḥanafīs classed it as obligatory without raising it to a pillar. The report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās that the walk is not required was met with the answer that the phrase negates blame for the act, not for its absence — a difference the grammarians of the community noted before the jurists ruled on it. On every reading the rite stands, and the verse's contribution is to strip away the misgiving that the idolatrous past had attached to a place God Himself had appointed.

**Whoever Volunteers Good**

The final clause turns from the obligation to the surplus. *Taṭawwuʿ* names the deed done beyond what is required, and al-Ṭabarī reads the sentence as the promise that such a deed is not lost. Al-Saʿdī draws attention to the two names at the end: God is *shākir*, appreciative, acknowledging the small act as though it were large, and *ʿalīm*, knowing the intention that carried it even when the outward deed is slight. Al-Rāzī observes the ordering of the sentence, in which the human act is mentioned briefly and the divine response at length, so that the reader understands whose generosity is the greater. The two hills, the running between them, the extra step taken willingly — the verse weighs none of it by human measure, and names the One who weighs it instead.

**The Rite Declared, the Sign Concealed**

The verse stands in the sūrah immediately before a warning against those who hide the signs God sent down, and al-Rāzī notes the force of the sequence: God declares the marks of His worship in one sentence and condemns their concealment in the next, so that the community learns what it owes to a sign it has received. The Book elsewhere states that whoever honours the marks of God does so from the piety of the heart (22:32), and calls the ancient House, the sacred month, the offerings, and the garlands the marks by which people know what God has appointed (5:97). Ṣafā and Marwah are the smallest members of that list, two hills rather than a sanctuary, and the sūrah treats them with the same seriousness it gives the House itself.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The two hills belong to God, and the hesitation over them is answered by an appointment older than the idols that were placed on them. The negation of blame is a verdict on the walker, not a licence for the one who omits it, and the Prophet's practice settled the question with the community's first generation. The voluntary deed done on that ground is acknowledged by the One who needs nothing from the walker and names Himself appreciative of him. And the sūrah's order teaches that a sign declared is a trust delivered, while a sign concealed is a debt that the following passage calls to account.
