## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:158

> Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺. And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Hills Named**

The verse names two hills near the sanctuary and states that they are among the symbols of God, and it then addresses the practice of walking between them during the greater and the lesser pilgrimage. The sentence follows the passage on trials and opens the section on the rituals of worship that runs through the following verses.

The Book's phrasing is a permission rather than a command: whoever performs the pilgrimage or the lesser pilgrimage is not to be blamed for walking between the two hills. The wording indicates that the community had been hesitant about the practice, and the verse removes the hesitation by naming the hills as among God's symbols.

**The Story Behind the Practice**

The traditional account connects the practice with Hagar and her son Ishmael, whom Abraham brought to the place and left there in obedience to God's command. Hagar ran between the two hills in search of water for her child, and the movement she made has been preserved as the practice the pilgrims perform.

The community treats the practice as part of the rituals because it belongs to the history of the house that Abraham and Ishmael built after the boy had grown. The Book's naming of the hills in this verse therefore places a detail of the pilgrimage within the account of Abraham that the sūrah has already narrated at length, and the reader is shown how the rituals and the history belong together.

**The Reluctance of Some Companions**

The commentaries report that some companions were reluctant to perform the walk between the two hills, suspecting that it was a survival from the age of ignorance and therefore a practice connected with idolatry. The verse's permission addresses that concern directly, and the report shows the community asking whether a practice whose origins they did not know could be retained.

Other reports describe companions who thought that the motions of running and walking in circles should be performed only around the sanctuary itself. The verse answers both concerns by listing the hills among the symbols of God, and the community's scholars noted that a single sentence sufficed to settle the matter once the Book had spoken.

**The Symbols of God**

The term rendered as symbols refers to the marks by which a religion and its worship are known, and the Book uses it for the rites of the pilgrimage and for the sacrifices offered at the sanctuary. The sentence places the two hills within that category, which is what makes the walking between them a part of the pilgrimage rather than a remnant of an earlier custom.

The community's teachers drew a wider lesson from the term, since what the Book calls a symbol is something God has appointed rather than something the community has chosen. The practices of the pilgrimage therefore rest on the same authority as the direction of prayer, and the reader who has followed the previous passage about the qiblah will recognise the principle.

The Book uses the term in the plural for the rites that a pilgrim performs and for the offerings that are brought to the sanctuary, and it warns the community against violating them. What the wording establishes is that a symbol is recognised by the appointment of God rather than by the impression it makes on the worshipper, and the community’s scholars applied the principle to the practices of the pilgrimage as a whole. The reader who has followed the sūrah’s treatment of the direction of prayer will recognise the same reasoning, since the qiblah also became a matter of obedience rather than of preference.

**The Command to Walk Between Them**

The sentence's permission was understood by the community's jurists in different ways, with some schools treating the walk as one of the essential rites of the pilgrimage and others treating it as a strongly recommended act. All the schools agree that the practice is performed, and the reports include the Prophet's instruction to perform it, saying that God has prescribed the running between the two hills.

The difference concerns the consequence of omitting the rite rather than its validity, and the community's practice has preserved the walk without interruption since the earliest period. What the verse supplies is the authority for the practice, and the reports supply the manner in which it is performed.

**The Willing Performers of Good**

The sentence closes by stating that whoever performs good willingly will find that God is appreciative and knowing, and the Book uses the pair of names at the end of commands about worship. The wording places the pilgrimage among the acts that a person performs by choice, and the reader is reminded that what God accepts is the voluntary offering.

The Book's term for willing performance appears in other passages about the alms and about the sacrifices, and the community's scholars read the names at the end of the sentence as a promise to everyone who adds to the required rites. The verse therefore encourages the community to do more than the minimum in the practices of the pilgrimage, and it attaches the promise to the intention with which the extra is offered.

**The Verse's Place in the Passage**

The sentence follows the statement about the blessings, the mercy, and the guidance granted to the patient, and it opens a section on the marks of God's religion. The Book moves from the endurance required in difficulty to the practices that belong to worship, and the two subjects are connected by the term symbols, which the sūrah uses for the signs that the community is commanded to honour. The reader is shown that the community's life consists of both the patience it shows in loss and the rites it performs in obedience.

The sentence stands between the passage about trials and the verses on the signs of God in creation, and it supplies the reader with a change of subject that is not a change of theme. What the sūrah has been describing is a community that holds to its duties under pressure, and the practices of the pilgrimage are among those duties. The Book therefore moves from the endurance that is required in loss to the rites that are performed in worship, and the term symbols connects the two by naming what God has appointed.
