## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:158

> Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺. And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Hills and a Mother's Run**

Ṣafā and Marwah are the two low hills flanking the hollow beside the Kaʿbah between which pilgrims pass back and forth seven times — a movement called *saʿy*, halfway between walking and running. The traditional account ties the rite to Hagar and Ishmael: when Abraham had brought mother and infant to the waterless valley at God's command and departed (compare the parting of Abraham and Hagar in Genesis 21), Hagar crossed the hollow between the two heights seven times, searching the horizon for water for her son — the desperate search that ended with the well of Zamzam. The rite is thus the oldest gesture in the pilgrimage: not invented by the Qur'an but reauthorized by it, a mother's panic over a dying child preserved as an act of worship. That the movement originated with Hagar yet belongs to Islam's rites is explained by its real origin — Abraham's household, the family that built the Kaʿbah when Ishmael was grown — and by the Prophet's own performance of it, which settled its place in the pilgrimage for good.

**Why the Verse Was Revealed**

A rite this old needed a verse this explicit because some Companions hesitated to perform it. Two reasons are preserved. The first: fear that the running was a residue of pagan practice from the Age of Ignorance, tainted with idolatry — a concern fed by a local legend that two people who fornicated inside the Kaʿbah were turned to stone and set near it as a warning, the stones growing over time into the two hills; in the pre-Islamic rite, moreover, idols had stood on both heights. The second: a scruple in the opposite direction — some thought the Kaʿbah alone was the object around which such movements of running and circumambulation should be made, and that extending them to two other points diminished the House. Al-Qurṭubī observed that the verse answers both questions at once: the hills are declared among God's symbols, which refutes the idolatry fear, and the going between them is legislated for every pilgrim, which refutes the House-only scruple. Revelation here does not create a ritual; it clears the conscience of those performing one.

**Shaʿāʾir: The Things by Which God Is Known**

The declaration that does this work names the hills *shaʿāʾir Allāh* — the symbols, the rites, the sacred markers of God. The word's root *sh-ʿ-r* carries the sense of that by which God is known: a shaʿīrah is at once a sacred sign, a sacred place, and a sacred act, because all three make God perceptible to a community. The Qur'an uses the term for the whole apparatus of the pilgrimage — do not violate Allah's rituals, the sacred months, the sacrificial animals, the garlanded offerings (5:2) — and states the theology of the term in the sūrah of the Pilgrimage: *"whoever honours the symbols of Allah, it is certainly out of the piety of the heart"* (22:32), and again of the sacrificial camels placed *"among the symbols of Allah, in which there is ˹much˺ good for you"* (22:36). Honoring a symbol is not stone-worship; it is the heart's piety using the visible to reach the invisible. Ṣafā and Marwah are enrolled in this class by divine sentence — two hills made holy not by their geology but by what happened on them and by God's decision to make the memory an act.

**Pillar or Recommendation: The Legal Afterlife**

The verse's phrasing — *there is no blame on him* in going between them, rendered here as a positive command — generated a genuine juristic question. The Shāfiʿī school read the saʿy as a *rukn* — an indispensable component — of the pilgrimage, without which the ḥajj is incomplete; the Ḥanafī school read it as recommended rather than obligatory, the removal-of-blame phrasing suggesting permission more than requirement. The dispute is textual and honest: a verse that says "no blame" can legislate or can merely license. Those who made it a pillar cited a prophetic report directing the pilgrims to perform the crossing, on the ground that God has prescribed this running — and in practice the question never divided the community's performance: adherents of all schools perform the saʿy, so the disagreement lives in the books while the hills carry the same traffic. The case is a good specimen of how Quranic legislation works through the tradition: the verse sets the rite inside the symbols of God, and the Sunnah supplies the operating detail.

**The Lesser Pilgrimage and the Volunteer**

The verse legislates for two journeys at once: the *ḥajj*, the pilgrimage of the appointed days, and the *ʿumrah*, the lesser visit performable at any other time of year. The ʿumrah contains a shortened set of the ḥajj's elements — entering the state of consecration (*iḥrām*) in the two cloths, circling the House, and crossing between the two hills — and most scholars, especially those who hold the saʿy obligatory, read the verse's closing clause, *whoever does good willingly*, as a reference to it: the ʿumrah is the volunteered good, since it is not an obligatory rite like the ḥajj. The reading gives the sentence its point — beyond the required, there is the willing, and the willing is not left unrewarded. The hills thus stand over two economies at once: the obligation every pilgrim owes and the supererogation every lover adds, one rite serving both the debtor and the devotee.

**Appreciative and Knowing**

The verse closes with two names aimed at the volunteer: *Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing*. *Shakūr* — Appreciative — is God described in the language of gratitude: the One who accepts the small and returns the large, who treats the willing extra as a debt He has chosen to owe. Paired with *ʿAlīm*, All-Knowing, the name covers the unseen portion of voluntary good — the ʿumrah performed when no one knew, the crossing of the hills done in exhaustion, the intention hidden inside the act. The Appreciative does not need the act publicized, and the Knowing cannot miss it. The two names together answer the verse's occasion one final time: the Companions who hesitated over the hills were worried how their rite would be *regarded* — by custom, by scruple, by the memory of idols. The verse ends by relocating the regard: the audience of the saʿy is the Appreciative, the All-Knowing, and in that audience a willingly done good is never lost.
