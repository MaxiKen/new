## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:72

> A group among the People of the Book said ˹to one another˺, “Believe in what has been revealed to the believers in the morning and reject it in the evening, so they may abandon their faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Stratagem Reported in Full**

The verse reports what a group among the People of the Book said to one another, and it reports it as a plan rather than as an accusation. The members of the group are not named; the sūrah records the scheme in their own words, which is why the reader meets it as a conspiracy rather than as a charge laid by an adversary.

The Book's decision to disclose the plan has a purpose beyond warning the believers. A community that has been told what is being said about it in private can no longer be surprised by the manoeuvre, and the sūrah has been preparing its readers for exactly this information in the verses that precede it. The disclosure is part of the defence.

**The Manoeuvre: In by Morning, Out by Evening**

The plan is described with precision: the group proposes that its members appear to accept the revelation in the morning and publicly retract it in the evening. The point is not the acceptance, which is false, and not the retraction, which is intended. The point is the sequence, and the sequence is the weapon.

The reasoning behind it is easy to reconstruct and the commentators supplied it. When people who are respected observe a position being adopted and then abandoned by those who adopted it, they draw conclusions about the position rather than about the people. A message that its own apparent converts have left within a day looks unstable, and doubt about a message is easier to plant that way than by direct argument. The plan's target is not the Prophet's claim but the confidence of the ordinary believer who watched the performance.

**What the Plan Assumes About the Believers**

The stratagem is a piece of applied psychology, and its assumptions are worth spelling out, because the sūrah's answer to it addresses those assumptions.

The planners assumed that the Muslims' conviction depended on appearances — on who was seen to be joining and staying — rather than on the content of what they had accepted. They also assumed that a community of new believers, many of whom had left their families and their standing to join, could be destabilised by the sight of important people coming and going. Both assumptions would be reasonable in a community that had never been told what its own foundation was, and the sūrah's response in the surrounding verses is to keep telling them. The plan's weakness is that a person whose faith rests on an argument he has understood cannot be moved by a performance, and the Book's remedy for the manoeuvre is the account of that argument.

**The Verse's Reporting Style**

The sūrah introduces the quotation without commentary and lets it stand. The Book uses this technique elsewhere with the statements of opponents, and the effect is to let the reader judge the plan by hearing it.

There is a certain kind of dignity in the Book's restraint. The scheme is presented as it was formulated, in the plotters' own voice, and no adjectives are added. A reader who encountered the plan second-hand through polemic might suspect that it had been exaggerated; the Book's method removes that suspicion and leaves the reader with the words themselves. What the verse does not do is gloat, and it does not follow the quotation with a denunciation. The answer to the trick is delivered elsewhere, generally and without reference to the incident.

**How the Book Answers a Stratagem**

The sūrah does not tell the Prophet to expose the group publicly, and it does not instruct the believers to treat the retractions as a test of their loyalty to the people involved. What the Book does, in the passage as a whole, is to continue stating the case for the message with the same arguments it used before.

That is the Book's characteristic way of dealing with this kind of attack. A claim about the truth of the revelation is answered by the truth of the revelation, and the community is equipped by being given reasons it can hold onto rather than suspicions it must guard. The next verse records a second element of the same plan and the command given to the Prophet in response to it, and the response is neither a counter-conspiracy nor a public rebuke but a statement about where guidance comes from.

**The Vulnerability the Plan Exposes**

Read from the outside, the manoeuvre identifies something true about religious communities: their confidence can be eroded by the visible conduct of prominent people, and this happens whether or not the conduct is sincere.

The sūrah's answer to that vulnerability is not to deny it but to locate the community's certainty elsewhere, which is what the verses around this one have been doing from the beginning. The believers are being told about a group that intends to undermine them, and the same passage tells them that guidance is God's, that bounty is in His hands, and that He chooses whom He wills. What is being defended is not a reputation to be managed but a position the community holds by evidence and by gift, and neither of those is available to the tactics in this verse.

**A Record Kept for the Community's Use**

The verse preserves for later readers a picture of the environment in which the earliest Muslim community formed, and it shows that the opposition was not confined to argument. Some of it was designed to work on the perceptions of onlookers.

A reader in a later century can take the verse as a model of what to expect rather than as a story about others. Any community whose members are admired by their neighbours will face the same kind of pressure, and the condition for withstanding it is the one the sūrah keeps supplying: knowledge of what one has accepted and of the ground on which it was accepted.
