## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:9

> and the mountains like ˹tufts of˺ wool.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mountains Soften to Wool**

Verse 8 melted the sky; verse 9 teases the mountains into fibers. Al-jibāl ka-l-ʿihn — mountains like carded wool — inverts the most fixed simile in human speech. Mountains mean permanence in every tongue; wool means lightness, drift, the handful that scatters at a breath. The Book stages this reversal identically in Sūrah 101 (101:5), where the same noun ʿihn describes the same unmaking. Doubled witness, single doom. ʿIhn denotes wool fluffed and teased for spinning — fibers separated, airy, adrift. What tectonics raised over eons, the Day unmakes in a moment. The conjunction "and" binds sky and peak into one collapse: above melts, beside dissolves, and between them no refuge stands. Geology itself will testify that only one permanence ever existed. When mountains go woolly, every lesser solidity has already failed.

**The Immovable Learns to Drift**

Proverbial firmness makes the mountains heaven's chosen exhibit. Humans swear by their stability — "firm as mountains" — and the Book elsewhere shows them passing like clouds on that Day (27:88), reduced to shifting sand-heaps (73:14), scattered to dust (77:10). Each image attacks a different facet of trust: motion against fixity, sand against rock, dust against mass. Wool adds texture to the sequence — not merely moved but transformed, solidity unmade into softness. The progression teaches by accumulation: no single metaphor exhausts the unmaking, so metaphors multiply. What resists one image falls to the next. Readers who trust geography must watch every landmark dissolve in turn. The Day does not relocate the mountains; it redefines them. Immovable becomes ungraspable, and proverbs collapse with their referents.

**Carded, Dyed, Scattered**

ʿIhn repays lexical lingering. The noun evokes wool carded — teased apart by combs into separate strands — and some lexicographers add dyed in various colors, suggesting peaks loosened into drifting hues. Either way lightness dominates: fibers that winds toss, fingers part, and children scatter. Mountains will hang in the Day's air the way thistledown hangs in summer — visible, touchable, utterly without weight. The image also inverts creation's order, where mountains were set as pegs to steady the earth; pegs pulled, the tent collapses. Such reversal runs through the whole passage: skies melt, peaks drift, friendships freeze, children become currency. Every created trust gets un-created in sequence. Wool-mountains preach what molten skies began — permanence was always loan, never possession. The Day repossesses everything, starting with the metaphors.

**Sky Melts Above, Peaks Dissolve Beside**

Verses 8-9 compose a diptych of vertical collapse. Above, the ceiling flows; beside, the pillars soften. Between melting roof and drifting wall, the human figure stands exposed — the passage's true subject, though unnamed till verse 10. The sūrah paces its unmaking one fixture per verse, letting each dissolve fully before dissolving the next. Readers descend solidity's ladder rung by rung, discovering each rung was mist. Order matters: heaven first, then earth, then society, then family — the Day strips shelter from outside in, like an auctioneer selling a house room by room. What remains when roof and wall go? Only the soul and its ledger, the subjects of coming verses. Cosmic demolition clears the stage for moral reckoning. Scenery removed, actors stand judged.

**What Permanence Was Always Worth**

Theology hides inside topography. Mountains symbolize whatever each age deems unshakable — empires, markets, dynasties, dogmas of progress. Their wooling announces that every such trust was misplaced from the start. Only the Maker outlasts His mountains; all else holds a lease the Day terminates. This relativizes without despising: mountains served nobly as pegs, watersheds, and landmarks, but service is not sovereignty. Mistaking servants for masters is idolatry's oldest form, whether the servant is stone or stock. The verse smashes the confusion with a simile. Wool cannot be worshipped, leaned on, or fled toward — and that is precisely the point. When the Day reveals mountains as fibers, it reveals every earthly refuge as threadbare. One shelter stands un-wooled, and the sūrah's later verses will name its address: prayer, charity, chastity, trust.

**Geology Undone in a Line**

Brevity is the verse's hammer. Seven words unmake the planet's skeleton — no process described, no mechanism offered, only the accomplished simile. Such compression assumes omnipotence the way everyday speech assumes sunrise: the Day does not struggle with mountains but redefines them effortlessly. The Book's eschatology consistently refuses mechanics, declining to explain how skies melt or peaks drift. Means belong to the Commander; results belong to the warned. This reticence also quickens dread — unexplained unmaking frightens more than explained, since explanation domesticates. Readers get the finished horror without the comforting physics. What remains is pure spectacle: look up at brass, look across at wool, and understand that nature kept no promises. The verse's shortness mimics the suddenness it announces. Mountains fall faster than sentences.

**Color Completes the Collapse**

Some lexicographers hear dyed hues inside ʿihn — wool teased and tinted, fibers of many colors drifting together. If mountains keep their varied strata while losing all solidity, the Day's sky will hang with slow ribbons of red, white, and dust, geology become aurora. Spectacle compounds dread: beauty itself enlisted as terror's palette. The Book elsewhere drapes the Day in striking colors — skies like molten brass, faces brightened or blackened — and tinted peaks would join that gallery. Whether or not hues are intended, lightness is certain, and lightness suffices to unmake every trust. Readers should picture the familiar ridgelines — gray granite, green slope, white cap — loosened into floating threads. What eyes verified daily as solid dissolves between glances. Color makes the collapse memorable; memorability makes it persuasive. Even the mountains' beauty will testify against reliance on them.

**Nature Fails, Then Society**

Cosmos dissolved, the sūrah turns to company. Verses 8-9 unmade shelter; verses 10-11 will unmake friendship — the Day strips refuges from outside in. This sequencing follows dread's logic: first the world fails, then the people in it. Readers who survive the mountains' wooling must next survive abandonment, discovering that fellow creatures offer no firmer hold than melting skies. The passage thus moves from geology to sociology, each collapse deeper than the last. What good are firm friends under a flowing sky? The next verse answers: none will even ask. With peaks turned to drifting fibers, the sūrah severs the bonds between the exposed souls below.
