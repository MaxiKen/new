## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:85

> Make me one of those awarded the Garden of Bliss.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham Asks for a Place Among the Blessed**

Abraham asks to be made one of those awarded the Garden of Bliss. The request is personal, but it is not selfish. He does not ask for an isolated reward detached from the righteous. He asks to belong among those who receive the Garden as the outcome of God's mercy and acceptance. Salvation is pictured as a place and a community, not merely as escape from punishment.

The translation's Garden of Bliss renders a word that carries ease, blessing, and settled well-being. The request reaches beyond temporary comfort. Abraham has already recognized that worldly food, drink, health, and reputation are gifts that pass. He now asks for the lasting form of blessed life that belongs to the Hereafter.

**The Garden Is an Award and a Gift**

To be awarded the Garden does not mean that Abraham can purchase it through his own deeds. An award is received, and the language keeps God's generosity in view. Deeds matter because they express faith and obedience, but the servant remains dependent on the Lord's acceptance. The request is therefore a confession of need rather than a claim of entitlement.

This balance corrects two mistakes. A person may imagine that good works force God to reward him, or he may imagine that deeds do not matter because mercy is everything. Abraham's prayer joins effort and dependence. He has struggled for truth and asks God to place him among the blessed. The final gift remains God's decision.

**Bliss Is More Than Pleasure**

The word bliss suggests a condition in which the heart is safe, content, and free from the disturbances that define worldly life. It is not merely a larger collection of pleasures. Worldly pleasure can coexist with fear, rivalry, loss, and the knowledge that it will end. The Garden's bliss is settled because it is received within God's nearness and approval.

A person who asks for this Garden is asking for the completion of every sound desire. He wants peace without anxiety, joy without guilt, and companionship without betrayal. The request also purifies desire. Abraham does not ask for continued possession of idols, status, or domination. He asks for a reward whose goodness comes from the Lord.

**The Request Follows a Life of Examination**

The prayer comes from a servant who has examined worship, rejected powerless objects, and placed his trust in the Lord of all worlds. The Garden is therefore not a decorative ending added to a life of unrelated ideas. It is the destination of the allegiance declared in the surrounding passage. Abraham's hope for bliss grows from monotheism and obedience.

This connection matters for anyone who wants the reward without the path. A person cannot make the Garden a substitute for turning away from false lords. The request is powerful because Abraham has already chosen the Lord over inherited worship. The reward does not replace surrender; it completes the life shaped by surrender.

**Being Among the Awarded Preserves Humility**

Abraham asks to be one of those awarded the Garden, not to be praised as a solitary exception. The plural category keeps the prayer humble and communal. He wants to stand with servants whose faith has been accepted. Righteousness is not a private performance in which one person must outshine everyone else. It is shared belonging under God's mercy.

This also protects the believer from imagining that spiritual rank makes him independent. Even a prophet asks to be joined to a class of the blessed. The request acknowledges that honour in the Hereafter is not self-appointed. God gathers His servants, and the servant hopes to be included among them.

**The Garden Is Described as Near, Not Merely Promised**

The following scene in the chapter will describe the Garden as brought near to the reverent, but this verse should be understood on its own as the prayer for admission to that blessed destination. Nearness in the Qur'anic account conveys welcome and accessibility for those whom God honours. Abraham's request is not for an abstract reward without presence. It is for entry into a real abode of peace.

The parallel description in 37:43 places the blessed in Gardens of Bliss. That cross-reference confirms that bliss is not a vague metaphor for moral satisfaction. It names the lasting home associated with the faithful. The believer may not see it now, but the prayer gives the future a concrete direction.

**A Garden of Bliss Reorders Worldly Ambition**

People naturally seek security, comfort, and honour. Abraham does not condemn those desires; he directs them to their lasting source. The Garden offers what worldly power cannot secure. It cannot be seized by wealth, inherited through family, or guaranteed by public reputation. It is awarded by God to those whose hearts and lives have turned toward Him.

This redirection frees a person from making the world carry the weight of eternity. Work and family remain important, but they are no longer treated as final possessions. The highest ambition becomes acceptance by the Lord. A person can enjoy lawful blessings without worshipping them because he knows that the lasting Garden is better than every temporary form of ease.

**The Prayer Holds Hope and Responsibility Together**

Abraham's request is hopeful, but it does not encourage delay in obedience. A person who asks for the Garden must let that hope shape conduct now. He guards worship, seeks wisdom, joins the righteous, and turns away from the idols that compete with God. Hope becomes practical when it gives present choices an eternal direction.

The Garden of Bliss is therefore the fitting destination of the prayer's earlier movement. The servant began by recognizing God as Creator, Guide, Provider, and Healer. He now asks for the final mercy: to be received among those whose life with God becomes everlasting bliss.
