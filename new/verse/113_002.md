## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:2

> from the evil of whatever He has created,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Widest Category in the Qur'an's List of Fears**

The word that does the work is *mā* — what, whatever. It is the general relative, and it makes the object of the refuge unlimited: from the evil of whatever He has created. No class is specified, no exception is admitted, and no boundary is drawn around the request.

The four protections in this chapter are arranged so that the widest comes first. After it come three named cases: the night when it darkens, the blowers on knots, the envier when he envies. Each of the three is a subset of the first. If the general clause stood at the end it would look like a summary; standing at the beginning it looks like a covering blanket under which the specific petitions are placed.

That arrangement makes the verse the most comprehensive prayer for safety in the Qur'an. Nothing created is excluded, which means nothing that can harm a person is excluded. The reciter is not asking to be protected from a list but from a category, and the list that follows is offered as illustration rather than as limit.

**The Evil Belongs to the Created Thing, Not to the Creating**

The construction is exact: *sharr mā khalaqa* — the evil of what He created. It is not *sharr khalaqa*, the evil He created. The genitive attaches evil to the creature, and the act of creation stays with God.

That distinction is not decorative. It reflects a consistent convention in the Qur'an, which attributes good to God without qualification and handles evil through other grammars. The jinn in their own chapter say of themselves: *“We do not know whether evil is intended for those on earth, or whether their Lord intends for them what is right.”* (72:10) — even the possibility of evil is spoken of passively, without naming its author.

The verse in this chapter does the same thing in two words. God is the creator of everything, including the things from whose evil refuge is sought; the evil is theirs, not His. A reader who wanted a doctrine of evil from the Qur'an would find the grammar settled before the argument begins: creation is divine, harm is a property of creatures, and the response to harm is not to renegotiate the first claim but to seek shelter under it.

**What the Category Is Understood to Contain**

Four readings are recorded, and they differ mainly in scope. Some take the phrase to mean Satan and his kind, on the ground that the greatest evil among created things is the one that works on the heart. Others take it to mean the Fire, the created evil that is final. A third group reads it generally — all evil, whatever its source. A fourth restricts it to the evil committed or manifested by creatures: the harm human beings and jinn do.

The general reading is the strongest, for a structural reason. If the verse meant Satan alone, the following three verses would be additions to a list; since the verse says "whatever He has created," the following three are instances of it. The chapter then reads as one large petition followed by three examples, which is how the Qur'an usually handles such lists.

The reading that takes the phrase as the Fire is not incompatible with that. It narrows the reference to the worst created thing, and the Qur'an does elsewhere describe the Fire as the evil that is prepared. But it costs the verse its function in this chapter, which is to cover everything the reciter cannot name.

**Creation as a Veil That Also Reveals**

A further interpretation treats the phrase as referring to creation itself, on the ground that the created world is a veil: it hides the Creator by occupying attention, and reveals Him by displaying His qualities. On this reading the refuge sought is from the veil as veil — from being stopped by the world at the world.

The reading belongs to the contemplative strand of commentary rather than to the plain sense, and it is preserved in the tradition alongside the others. Its support in the Qur'an is the language of light and sign: *“Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth.”* (24:35), and the constant claim that created things are *āyāt*, signs, whose function is to be seen through.

The plain reading remains the one that fits the sūrah. What follows this verse are harms, not distractions: darkness, sorcery, envy. A chapter that lists those three is a chapter about danger, and the general clause at its head is best understood as the totality of danger rather than as the totality of existence.

**Asking Protection From What God Made**

An objection has been pressed against this verse since the earliest centuries: if God created the thing, how can one ask God for protection from it? The answer is inside the Qur'an's own account of harm, which makes created evil real and never independent.

The clearest statement is in the verse about magic at Babylon: *“Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will.”* (2:102). The harm is genuine — it broke marriages — and it operates only within a permission it does not control.

Refuge in the Lord is therefore refuge in the owner of the thing that threatens you. The prayer does not set God against His creation; it appeals from a creature to its Creator, on the ground that the Creator's authority over it is total. That is why the title used in verse one is *Lord* — *rabb*, the one who governs and disposes — rather than a name of power or of mercy. Lordship over the daybreak includes lordship over everything the daybreak reveals.

**A Clause That Covers What Has Not Been Invented Yet**

Because the petition is general, it does not age. The three specific evils named in this chapter belong to a world of darkness, cords, and neighbours' envy; the general clause covers harms that had no name when it was revealed and have names now.

That is a deliberate feature of a text meant to be recited for as long as the religion lasts. A list would have been exhausted by its own contents. A category is not.

The verse therefore does two things at once, and the reciter may use either. He may ask for protection from everything created, in which case the rest of the chapter adds emphasis. Or he may find that the rest of the chapter has named three of his actual fears — the hour he cannot see through, the person working against him in secret, the friend who resents his good fortune — and that the general clause at the head covers whatever he has not thought to name.
