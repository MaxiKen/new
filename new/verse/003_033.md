## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:33

> Indeed, Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ’Imrân above all people ˹of their time˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Names Under One Verb**

The sentence rests on a single verb, *iṣṭafā*, to choose or select something as pure, to take one thing out of a group. All four entries in the list fall under that act, and the Book uses the same verb for the election of individuals, families, and communities throughout: Moses is chosen above the people of his time, Mary is chosen above the women of the world, Israel is preferred above the nations of its era.

Putting the verb at the head of the sentence makes election the subject of the verse rather than the achievements of the chosen. None of the four named parties is described as having earned the position. The Book's consistent grammar of selection is that God chooses whom He wills, and the names here are illustrations of a principle rather than a ranking table.

**Adam and Noah: the Two Beginnings**

The list starts with the first human being and the second beginning of humanity. Adam was chosen without a lineage, made from dust and given the knowledge of names; Noah was chosen out of a generation that had abandoned its Lord, and his survival carried the human story forward.

Placing these two at the top establishes the range of what election means. It can be prior to any family line, as with Adam, and it can be a rescue from the general destruction, as with Noah. Neither case could be explained by inherited standing, which prepares the reader for what the rest of the list does with families.

**The Family of Abraham and the Family of ʿImrān**

The two houses named after Abraham and ʿImrān are connected, since the sūrah's narrative makes ʿImrān the father of Mary and thus a figure within the broad family of the Children of Israel, which traces itself to Abraham through Isaac and Jacob.

The Book uses "family" in a way that combines blood and mission. A house is chosen when a line of it carries the religion forward, and the sūrah's own account will show a woman's vow, a birth, a sanctuary, and a prophet emerging from a single household. The chapter takes its name from this house because the story it tells is the story of what was produced inside it.

**ʿImrān: the Name and the Root Behind It**

The name comes from the root *ʿ-m-r*, which covers building, dwelling, and long life; *ʿumrān* means a structure or settlement, and the ending *-ān* is a common Arabic form found in names such as ʿUthmān and words such as *furqān*.

Some commentators identified this ʿImrān with Amram, the father of Moses, and treated the two as one figure, though the sūrah's narrative places him centuries later as Mary's father. The identification is widespread in early tafsīr but strained by chronology, and the name ʿImrān appears separately in 66:12 for Mary's father as well. What the reader needs from the name is modest: a father, a house, and a lineage standing at the head of a story about chosen people.

**"Above All People of Their Time"**

The phrase has been read in two ways that converge on the same meaning. The older understanding takes it as preference over the nations of each figure's own era, which is why the translation supplies *of their time*. The alternative reading takes the word for the world in the sense of all peoples, with the context of the same phrase in verses addressed to Israel determining the scope.

Either way, the verse is not making a claim about every individual in those families in every generation. The Book says of Israel that it was preferred above the worlds and then goes on to criticise that people's conduct in detail, which shows that corporate election is about carrying something, not about being excused from an accounting. A house can be chosen and still be held to account, which is the pattern the sūrah will follow in its own narration.

**What Election Is For**

The four names are all connected to a task. Adam received the knowledge of names and began human life; Noah warned a people and built the ark; Abraham broke the idols, argued with a king, and left a house in the desert; the family of ʿImrān produced a woman raised in the sanctuary and a prophet born without a father.

The list therefore describes a series of commissions rather than a series of honours. Each was chosen for something to be done, and each was held responsible for doing it. The Book never presents election as a cushion beneath a person; the more a person is chosen, the heavier the expectation, and the sūrah is about to demonstrate that pattern in one family's history.

**The Narrative That Begins Here**

With this verse the sūrah turns from argument to story. The previous lines addressed the community's loyalties, prayers, and claims about God; the narrative that opens here will run through the next forty verses and will include a woman's vow, a birth, a sanctuary, a barren couple, and a child born of a virgin.

The placement is not decorative. The sūrah has just told its readers who to obey and what God knows; it now shows them a house in which obedience and knowledge produced three generations of worship. The argument about choosing is followed by the record of what the chosen did.

It is also worth measuring the distance the reader has travelled. Five verses ago the sūrah was warning a community about political alliances and the contents of its hearts; now it is naming the first human being and the founder of a household in Israel. The pivot is not a change of subject. The Book is showing the same principle in a longer frame: God chooses, the chosen serve, and the accounting applies to everyone, including the ones who were selected.
