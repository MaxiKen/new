## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:198

> Had We revealed it to a non-Arab,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Hypothetical Changes the Messenger, Not the Truth**

The verse introduces a hypothetical revelation delivered to a non-Arab. The point is not to praise one people and condemn another. It exposes the shifting standards of those who reject the Quran. If the message had arrived through a foreign speaker, the deniers would have had a new complaint ready, even though the source and truth of revelation would remain unchanged.

The imagined change is useful because it removes the excuse that the existing form alone caused disbelief. The people already heard the Quran in a language familiar to them, yet familiarity did not produce submission. A different messenger or linguistic setting would not have repaired a heart determined to reject the claim.

**Non-Arab Can Describe Language as Well as Origin**

The Arabic term behind non-Arab can carry more than one shade. It may refer to a foreigner, to someone outside the Arab linguistic world, or to a speaker whose Arabic is not fluent. The verse therefore touches both the identity of the messenger and the hearer's ability to understand the recitation.

That range strengthens the argument. If the messenger were foreign, they could object to his origin. If the messenger recited Arabic fluently despite being foreign, they could still object to the unusual combination. The problem would not be solved because the objection is being moved rather than answered. Allah exposes the instability of a standard that changes whenever truth approaches.

**Language Becomes a Moving Excuse**

A person who wants guidance asks for a clear route to meaning. A person who wants refusal searches for a feature that can be blamed. The hypothetical non-Arab lets the deniers imagine that they would have believed under different conditions, while the verse reveals that the imagined conditions would generate another complaint.

This pattern appears outside questions of language. People say they would obey if the command came from a different person, a different family, or a different social setting. When the condition changes, they add another condition. The verse teaches that endless conditions can hide a settled decision not to submit.

**The Message Is Not the Property of an Ethnic Group**

Because the Quran is in Arabic, it first addresses an Arab audience in a language it can understand. Quran 14:4 gives the broader rule that messengers speak in the language of their people for clarity. That rule serves communication, not ethnic ownership. A non-Arab community can receive the message through translation and teaching without the revelation becoming a tribal possession.

The hypothetical therefore protects two truths at once. Allah is merciful in making the first warning clear to its audience, and the truth of the warning does not depend on the audience's ethnicity. The deniers would not have gained a valid excuse from a non-Arab messenger. Their refusal would still be a refusal of the Lord's message, not a necessary response to its form.

The hypothetical also clarifies why the Quran mentions the messenger's language and the audience's language together. A messenger must communicate, and a people must be given a fair chance to understand. Once that condition is met, the listener cannot make his own prejudice into a defect in revelation. Allah's mercy does not remove the work of learning, but it removes the excuse of an impossible communication.

A foreign messenger might have unsettled the pride of the Arab audience because people often confuse familiarity with authority. They may trust a speaker from their own circle more readily, yet a local messenger can be rejected precisely because he is too familiar. The verse shows that neither preference is a measure of truth. Authority belongs to Allah, who can send guidance through the person and language He chooses.

The word non-Arab also reminds the reader that language and ethnicity are not identical. A person can learn another language, speak it well, or remain marked by an accent. None of these features decides whether a message is true. What matters is whether the message comes from Allah and whether it can be understood well enough for responsible response.

This thought prevents a community from treating translation or explanation as a threat to its identity. Guidance crosses boundaries because the Lord of all worlds is not the Lord of one dialect only. People need faithful teachers and clear renderings, but the truth does not become foreign merely because the hearer must learn new words.

The deniers' imagined alternative therefore collapses. A non-Arab messenger would not have removed their resistance; it would only have supplied a new surface for it. The verse exposes a heart that wants conditions under which it can never be held accountable. Honest faith does the opposite: it seeks understanding, then accepts the claim of the Lord even when the form challenges pride.

This hypothetical is not a dismissal of sincere questions about translation, pronunciation, or the status of a messenger. Those questions can be asked for the sake of understanding. The defect appears when every answer becomes another reason not to obey. The same outward concern can be honest in one heart and a shield for denial in another.

A language chosen for the first audience also shows that revelation respects human responsibility. People cannot be held to a command they were never given a fair chance to understand. Allah sends messengers with clarity, while the audience remains responsible for hearing and considering. The non-Arab scenario demonstrates that the problem would not lie in a lack of divine fairness.

The verse makes ethnic pride look as unstable as linguistic excuse. A person may prefer a messenger from his own people, but that preference does not decide whom Allah appoints. He may prefer revelation in his own language, but preference does not define what the Lord sends. Submission begins when the creature allows Allah's choice to outrank personal expectations.

The imagined non-Arab reciter would still be a servant carrying a message from the Lord of all worlds. His foreign origin would not lower the truth, just as Shuʿayb's belonging to his people did not make the command about justice optional. Guidance can enter a community from outside its preferred identity, and the honest response is to examine it rather than reject it for being unfamiliar.
