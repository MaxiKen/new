## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:7

> Woe to every sinful liar.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Words, Both Built for Intensity**

The verse is four words long in Arabic and it convicts with two nouns. The first, athīm, comes from a root meaning to commit sin, and its form is intensive: not a man who has sinned once, but one whose life has settled into the practice. The second, affāk, is built on afaka, to turn a thing from its proper face, and it names the person who does that to the truth habitually — the fabricator, the one who reverses what he has heard and hands it back in a new shape. The pairing is deliberate. A man may be a liar by trade without being wicked in private, and a man may be a sinner without inventing anything about God. This verse fuses the two, describing a character in which the fabrication has become the sin. The Qur'an uses the same second word for the sorcerer and for those who made the messengers out to be deceivers; it always carries the sense of turning something right-side up into its opposite.

**"Woe" Is a Verdict, Not an Emotion**

Wayl, rendered here as woe, is not a sigh. In the Book's usage it is a noun of ruin, a sentence passed on a class of people, and it stands at the head of some of the shortest and heaviest verses in the Qur'an. Other chapters attach it to those who write the Book with their own hands, to those who hoard and count their wealth, and to those who use false measures. In each case the word announces a verdict rather than describing a feeling, which is why the sūrah can move from it directly into an account of how the condemned man behaves. The brevity of the verse is part of its force: the reader is given no narrative, no name, no circumstance, only the sentence and the two qualities that earned it. A longer verse would invite discussion of the case. This one closes the file. Anyone who recognises his own habits in the two nouns has been told what the verdict is before he has had a chance to argue.

**The Man Who Has Persuaded Himself**

The root behind "liar" describes a turning, and the turning can begin at home. A person who tells a lie often enough may end by believing it, not because the lie becomes true but because his judgment has been bent into the shape of the interest it serves. That is how the Qur'an usually presents the hardened denier: not as a man tormented by evidence he cannot answer, but as one who has arranged his ideas so that the evidence never has to be faced. The verse's condemnation therefore falls on a condition rather than an isolated act. Habitual lying about the things that matter most, revelation and its messenger, is not a small vice among others; it is the mechanism by which a person keeps his life the way he prefers it. The two words describe a man at peace, which is what makes the verse frightening rather than merely harsh.

**Who the Verse Reached, and Who It Keeps Reaching**

Reports connect the verse to Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, a well-born Qurayshite who had travelled in the lands of the Persians and brought back their stories. He is said to have set his tales against the Qur'an, offering crowds an alternative entertainment when they gathered to hear the Prophet recite, and to have argued that the old stories were the better of the two. The report is not the verse's scope. Commentators treat the address as a standing description that applies to everyone who fabricates in the same way, at any time, and this is why the verse names no one. The Qur'an's habit in such cases is to leave the historical occasion where it stood and to widen the terms until they cover the reader. A specific man may have prompted the words; the class they describe will outlast him, and the judgment they announce cannot be avoided by the accident of being born after he died.

**Fabrication Is a Heavier Charge Than Doubt**

The Book treats doubting differently from manufacturing. A person who has not yet worked out what he believes can be answered, and the Qur'an answers that person at length, in arguments that fill pages and address a reader who is still deciding. The fabricator is a different case, because he has already supplied his own replacement for the message being offered to him, and the replacement needs an audience to survive. Doubt asks for evidence; fabrication builds a market. This is the evil the verse names, and it explains why the punishment described in the following verses falls so heavily on those who mock rather than on those who merely hesitate. Mockery is what fabrication does once it has an audience, and the two verses that follow describe that audience being gathered. A trade in false stories is not a neutral alternative to faith, since it claims for itself the authority it denies the revelation.

**A Class the Reader Is Asked to Examine**

"Every" is the widest word available, and the verse uses it. The Qur'an often names its opponents, records their arguments, and replies to each one in place, but here it does something blunter: it defines a type and warns it. That definition stands as a mirror set at the door of the sūrah. Every person who has repeated a claim about God without checking it, or shaped the truth to fit an audience, finds the two adjectives waiting for him, and there is no name attached by which he can deflect them onto someone else. The rest of the chapter will describe what becomes of those who hear the recitation and walk away. This verse marks the point before that walk, in a man whose mouth has already replaced the words he heard.
