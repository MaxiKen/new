## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:16

> They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Purchase Described After Two Parables Were Announced**

The verse does not introduce a new group; it closes the portrait of the hypocrites before their two parables are given. *“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance”* (2:16) gathers the behaviours detailed from (2:8) to (2:15) — the false profession, the attempted deception, the diseased heart, the renaming of corruption as reform and of believers as fools, the double tongue, the mockery that is returned — and names them as a single transaction: a trade. The demonstrative *ulāʾika* — “those ones” — points at a distance, as *dhālika* did for the Book (2:2) and as *ulāʾika* did for the mindful who prosper (2:5). The trade is not described as a slip but as a purchase (*ishtaraw*), an active preference that, like any purchase, assumes a prior valuation. What was given up is guidance (*hudā*); what was taken is error (*ḍalālah*). The verse then judges the trade with the market’s own language: *their commerce has not profited, and they are not guided*.

**Purchased as Preferred, and Why the Mercantile Image Persists**

The source notes that *ishtaraw* can be heard as “they preferred,” as in *“they preferred blindness to guidance”* (41:17), where the same verb is glossed by Ṭabarī, Rāzī, and Qurṭubī (Ṭ, R, Q) as choice rather than shopkeeping. That gloss is possible because *shirāʾ* in Arabic can denote any taking of one thing in place of another, not only a bazaar exchange. Yet the verse itself immediately invokes commerce (*tijārah*), which makes the mercantile sense the more pointed: *their commerce has not profited*. The image therefore does double work. It says the hypocrites chose error over guidance, and it says the choosing was conducted as a calculation of advantage — to be counted among the believers when belief seemed to pay, and to be counted among the satans when satanic company seemed to pay (2:14). The calculation is precisely what makes the trade intelligible as a trade, and precisely what makes its profitlessness intelligible as a business failure.

**Why the Language of Commerce Recurs in This Sūrah**

Al-Baqarah returns to the commerce image for other spiritual losses, and the recurrence shows it is not ornament. *“They have purchased disbelief with faith”* is echoed in *“they purchased the life of this world at the price of the Hereafter”* (2:86), in *“they purchased error at the price of guidance”* here, and in *“they purchased a small price for the verses of Allah”* (2:175; 3:177). Each instance treats the unseen as a good that can be exchanged for a visible good, and each judges the exchange as a bad deal on its own terms. The hypocrite’s trade is therefore not an exotic perversity but the same economy writ small: to prefer the immediate hedge — to say “We believe” to one audience and “We are with you” to another — over the single, continuing standing upon guidance (*ʿalā hudā*, 2:5). The sūrah’s later legislation on actual commerce — *“when you contract a debt for a term appointed, write it down”* (2:282) and the prohibition of *ribā* — will show that material commerce is not condemned; mispriced spiritual commerce is.

**Their Commerce Has Not Profited — Profit as Exposure**

*Fa-mā rabiḥat tijāratuhum* — “their commerce has not profited” — denies profit not in a future audit but as a present fact. The perfect *mā rabiḥat* treats the transaction as already assessed and found wanting. Classical commentators gloss the unprofitability as exposure: the hypocrite’s two-faced performance was meant to secure advantage in both camps, but it secures standing in neither. Among the believers he is not trusted, because his prayer is not established and his spending is not released; among the satans he is not needed, because his mockery adds nothing to their open rejection. The trade that was meant to keep both doors open (cf. the curtained doors of the straight-path parable, Tirmidhī 2859) leaves him in the corridor. That is why the same unprofitability is predicated of those who *“purchase error”* elsewhere: the purchase does not merely underperform; it leaves the purchaser without the good he claimed to buy and without the good he sold to buy it.

**Not Guided — A Finality That Mirrors the Opening**

*Wa-mā kānū muhtadīn* — “and they are not guided” — uses the perfect of *kāna* to deny that guidance has ever become their state. The mindful were *upon guidance* (2:5); these traders are *not guided* at all. The denial is categorical, not comparative. It does not say their guidance is defective; it says the standing that (2:5) named has not been achieved, so that the “purchase” cannot be redescribed as a different kind of guidance. The verse thus closes the first large movement of al-Baqarah — the sorting of humanity into mindful, persistently rejecting, and hypocritical — before opening the second, which will show the hypocrite’s state from inside through two extended parables: a fire kindled and then withdrawn (2:17-18) and a rainstorm that blinds (2:19-20). The closing judgment — not profitable, not guided — is the ledger entry for the trade; the parables that follow are the phenomenology of what it feels like to live inside that ledger.

**A Trade Whose Price Is the Self**

To trade guidance for error is not to exchange one opinion for another; it is to exchange the organ by which opinions are weighed. The sealing of hearts and hearing (2:7) and the sickness increased (2:10) have already shown that acts shape capacities, and capacities shape further acts. The hypocrite’s commerce trades the very capacity — the heart that believes in the unseen (2:3), the certainty about the Hereafter (2:4) — for the territory that capacity was meant to navigate. That is why the commerce is profitless in a way a merely overpriced purchase is not. An overpriced purchase still leaves the purchaser with a good, albeit at too high a cost; this purchase leaves the purchaser without the instrument that would have allowed him to recognize cost at all. He has not simply lost wealth; he has traded the scale that weighs wealth.

**Guidance and Misguidance as Direction and Error**

*Hudā* and *ḍalālah* are not neutral alternatives. *Hudā* evokes a path that is there to be followed; *ḍalālah* evokes a wandering from a path that was there. To purchase *ḍalālah* with *hudā* is therefore to pay with the path to buy the wandering. The sūrah’s earlier image of guidance as an elevation one stands upon (*ʿalā hudā*) makes the trade vivid: the hypocrite steps down from the elevation to purchase the lowland that surrounds it, finding that the lowland’s only product is the blindness described next — *deaf, dumb, and blind, they return not* (2:18). The commerce’s profitlessness will then be shown, not argued, in the fire that is lit and then taken away.

**The Same Verb Will Return for the Opposite Exchange**

The commerce image is retained for the opposite exchange to prevent the trader from concluding that all commerce is condemned. Elsewhere the Qur'an invites the believer to a different purchase: *“Shall I guide you to a commerce that will save you from a painful punishment? Believe in Allah and His Messenger and struggle in His way”* (61:10-11). The verb *ishtarā* thus frames both the hypocrite’s loss and the believer’s gain, so that the moral world is not divided into traders and non-traders but into those who trade well and those who trade badly. The verse’s finality — not profitable, not guided — is therefore also an invitation to the reader who has not yet traded: the market is open, the goods have been named, and the ledger for this particular trade has been closed as a warning.
