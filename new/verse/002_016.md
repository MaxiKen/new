## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:16

> They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Transaction Described in Commercial Terms**

The verse converts a spiritual collapse into a business deal, and it uses the vocabulary of the market deliberately. Arabic *ishtarā* means to buy, and the sentence says they acquired misguidance by parting with guidance, with the price named first in the reader's mind because it is the thing that was given away. The Qur'an returns to this metaphor repeatedly in this sūrah and outside it, always with the same accounting: *“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance and forgiveness for punishment. How persistent are they in pursuit of the Fire!”* (2:175), and *“Those who trade belief for disbelief will never harm Allah in the least, and they will suffer a painful punishment.”* (3:177).

The metaphor has a fixed shape across those verses. One side of the exchange is always a reality that guides; the other side is a state that ruins. Nobody is described as accidentally ending up on the wrong side of the bargain, and the transaction is always the same transaction.

**Which Direction the Bargain Runs**

Some commentators read the verb as preferring rather than purchasing, since Arabic uses the root that way in a verse about a people who chose their own blindness: *“they preferred blindness to guidance”* (41:17). Read that way, the verse describes a selection rather than an exchange, and the commercial language that follows is a way of showing what the selection cost. The two readings arrive at the same place, and the mention of trade in the very next clause supports the second: whether the man bought ruin or chose it, the ledger records a loss.

What the difference in reading affects is the question of what a person holds to begin with. Guidance in the Qur'an is given before it is traded: the sūrah has already called itself a guide and described those who accept it, and the audience for this verse is made up of people who had access to what they abandoned. The bargain is described as a sale of something the seller possessed, not of something he never had.

**Profit and Loss as the Book's Accountancy**

The verse closes the deal with a verdict drawn from trade: the transaction brought no profit. The Book keeps a running account of this kind, and it never leaves the arithmetic ambiguous. Those who break the covenant after it has been affirmed *“are the ˹true˺ losers”* (2:27), and the same sūrah warns that anyone diverted from the remembrance of God by wealth or children *“it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers”* (63:9).

The word for profit here is the ordinary word for commercial gain, and its absence is stated as a fact about the outcome rather than as a moral comment. A merchant who sells an asset for nothing and calls it a strategy has not made a mistake about morality; he has made a mistake about arithmetic. The verse treats the spiritual case as the same kind of error, and it states the loss in the plainest available language.

**Guidance Held in Trust, Not Owned**

The metaphor also decides how guidance should be understood. If guidance were a possession, selling it would be a straightforward exercise of ownership; the verse's tone suggests something closer to betrayal, and the rest of the Book supports that tone. Guidance is granted, and what is granted can be refused, neglected or covered over, but the refusal does not make the grant into private property.

The sūrah's own description of the guided supports this. They are described as guided *by their Lord*, and the response of the human being is described as acceptance and practice. Nobody in these verses produces his own guidance and then trades it away. The men in this verse were members of a community that had received a message, and what they sold was their position inside it.

**A Verdict That Leaves Nothing to Negotiate**

The second half of the sentence is emphatic in the way the Book reserves for final statements: *it is they* who failed, and *it is they* who are not guided. The construction repeats the pattern used earlier for the guided and later for the corruptors, and it places this verse in a series. The sūrah is assigning identities, and it does so with the same grammatical instrument for every group it describes.

The verdict does not say they are being punished, and it does not say their state is permanent. It says the trade produced nothing and that its author did not arrive. The Book elsewhere describes people who returned from such a position, which means the sentence is a description of the condition rather than a lock on it. What the verse establishes is where the road they chose actually goes, and it uses the plainest available measure — the balance sheet — to say so.

**Why the Hypocrite Is Described as a Trader**

The image fits the men described in the preceding verses precisely. A trader wants the value of a good while keeping the capital that could be put at risk, and the hypocrite wants the standing that comes with faith while keeping himself free of its obligations. He performs the profession, keeps the protection and the social position it brings, and preserves his connections to the other side against the day fortunes turn.

The Qur'an calls that position a losing trade, and the loss it names is measured in the thing that was surrendered. What the buyer receives in exchange is the absence of the guidance he wanted to keep available as an option. The verse states that the option has been exercised and the asset is gone, and the last two words of the sentence record the result without a single word of encouragement or reproach.
