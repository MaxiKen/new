## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:16

> They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Soul Stated as a Ledger**

The verse re-describes everything the passage has shown in the vocabulary of the market. The hypocrites are *alladhīna ishtarawū* — the ones who purchased, who struck a bargain — and the goods exchanged are named: misguidance bought, guidance paid. Commerce is the Qur'an's favored idiom for the soul's decisions, and al-Baqarah will return to it: of another group it says *"These are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world"* (2:86), and the sūrah al-Ṣaff opens its counter-offer in the same register — *"O believers! Shall I guide you to an exchange that will save you from a painful punishment?"* (61:10). The metaphor is chosen with care. A market presumes free buyers, real alternatives, known prices, and a moment of decision; sin read as commerce keeps all four. Nothing here is fate or weakness — it is a transaction, entered by parties who preferred one commodity over another, and the verse's judgment is an audit of that preference.

**Buying Can Mean Preferring**

The verb *ishtarā* carries a documented ambiguity, and the exegetes worked both sides of it. In standard usage it means to purchase; in an established extension it means to prefer, to choose one thing over another regardless of payment — as when Thamūd is described in Fuṣṣilat: *"We showed them guidance, but they preferred blindness over guidance"* (41:17). Al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī note the preference-reading here, and also note what weakens it: the verse itself goes on to mention *tijārah*, commerce, which pulls the metaphor back toward the literal market. Both readings survive, and they are not rivals so much as two exposures of one photograph. Preference describes the interior act — the tilt of the will toward misguidance. Purchase describes its structure — something held was surrendered to obtain it. Guidance was genuinely in the hypocrites' hands: they had the creed, the community, the prayers. You cannot prefer away what you never possessed, and the verse's economics depends on that prior ownership.

**An Audit With Three Findings**

The commercial frame lets the verse issue its verdict as findings of fact. First: the goods. What they acquired, misguidance, they already had — it was the state of their private rooms before the purchase; what they surrendered, guidance, was the one asset they could not regenerate. The trade is therefore not an exchange of values but a surrender of value for possession of what was never lost. Second: the result — *their commerce has not brought them profit*. Not "brought little profit" or "brought tainted profit": no profit at all, the ledger closed at a loss beyond recovery of the purchase price. Third: the standing of the traders — *they are not rightly guided*, which reads in commercial terms as the revocation of their license. A merchant whose entire inventory is counterfeit is not merely poorer; he is outside the market. The three findings move from the deal to the balance sheet to the trader's status, and each is final in a way that leaves no appellate language in the verse.

**The Double Verdict at the End**

Why does the verse need both closing clauses — profitless, *and* not guided? The first is economic, the second existential, and together they answer a question the market metaphor invites. A failed trader can remain a trader; a loss can be absorbed by reserves. The second clause forecloses that reading: the loss consumed the reserves. Guidance in the Qur'an is not an asset held alongside the self — it is the condition of the self's orientation, and its sale leaves the seller disoriented toward every subsequent deal. There is also a structural echo in the doubling. Verse 5 of this sūrah ended its portrait with two locked clauses — *it is they* who are guided, *it is they* who are successful — and this verse inverts the architecture for the opposite type: *it is they* whose trade failed, *it is they* who stand unguided. The sūrah is keeping parallel ledgers, and the restriction-pronouns mark each entry as complete.

**Everyone Trades at This Market**

The hypocrites are the verse's named clients, but the transaction type generalizes, and the Qur'an applies the same verb elsewhere to whole communities. Thamūd preferred blindness to guidance (41:17). Another group in this very sūrah trades the covenant and its oaths for a paltry price (2:86; 3:77). The common structure is visible once named: some possession of truth surrendered for some advantage of position, appetite, or safety. The hypocrite version is the paradigm case only because it trades at the highest price difference known to the market — the whole of guidance for the whole of its opposite, undertaken by men who could recite both sides fluently. Every reader who has ever softened a conviction to suit a room, or traded honesty for standing, has transacted here in smaller denominations. The verse is terrifying not because the trade is exotic but because the market is universal and its currency is what each person is holding.

**The Counter-Offer Left Standing**

A market verse implies a rival offer, and the Qur'an makes the counter-trade explicit: *"O believers! Shall I guide you to an exchange that will save you from a painful punishment?"* (61:10) — belief and striving in Allah's cause presented as the commerce that rescues. Set beside verse 16, the two transactions share everything but direction: both require surrendering something held, both promise an acquisition, both are entered knowingly. The difference is that the profitable exchange buys the lasting with the fleeting, while this one does the reverse and calls the reversal profit. Verse 16 of al-Baqarah is the negative plate of that offer — the photograph of the trade that fails — and the sūrah keeps it in circulation for a pastoral reason: no one enters a bad deal intending to; the hypocrites believed they were managing risk. The audit published here exists so that later traders can price their goods before the sale rather than after.
