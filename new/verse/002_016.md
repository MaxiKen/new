## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:16

> They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Trade, Not a Fall**

The sūrah could have said that these people lost their way. It says instead that they *bought* something. The commercial metaphor assigns agency: nobody drifts into this condition by accident of temperament or upbringing. A transaction took place, a price was paid, and goods were received.

The metaphor also assigns blame in a particular place. A person who is lost is pitiable; a person who has made a bad purchase is responsible. The sūrah is not describing victims of confusion. It is describing people who looked at two options, priced them, and chose — repeatedly, and over a period of time long enough to be described as a career.

The choice of a commercial image over a military or a medical one is worth pausing on. Warfare would make these people enemies to be defeated; illness would make them patients to be treated; commerce makes them agents who got what they asked for. Each metaphor carries a different remedy, and the sūrah has chosen the one in which the remedy is a change of mind rather than a conquest or a cure. Nobody can be argued out of an illness or defeated out of a disease. A person can, in principle, be argued out of a bad bargain — which is what the rest of the sūrah proceeds to try.

**"Purchased" or "Preferred"?**

There is a lexical argument here worth reporting, because the outcome changes the emphasis. One reading takes the verb in its ordinary sense of buying. Another takes it in the sense of *preferring*, on the basis of a verse that uses the same root in that way: *As for Thamûd, We showed them guidance, but they preferred blindness over guidance* (41:17).

Most commentators reject the second reading for a reason that is visible in the sentence itself: the verse goes on to use the word *tijārah*, commerce. A writer who had meant "preferred" would not immediately speak of a trade. The commercial reading is therefore the stronger one, and it is also the more demanding, since preferring can be passive while purchasing is not.

**Why the Trade Fails**

The verdict is given in two words: the commerce did not profit. The failure is not that they were cheated by a cleverer party, nor that circumstances turned against them after a sound investment. The trade carried no profit in it from the beginning, because what they exchanged was worth infinitely more than what they received.

There is a second failure underneath the first, and it is the more interesting one. To trade guidance away, you must first have it. These people were not shopping in a market where guidance was unavailable; the sūrah's whole address assumes it was offered to them, and that they held it long enough to hand it over. The loss is not deprivation but disposal, which is why the verse can call them both the buyers and the losers without contradiction.

A profitless trade is also, on this account, a strange one. Nobody knowingly pays everything for nothing; the purchase only makes sense if the buyer believes he is getting something. What the verse implies about these people is that they misjudged the goods at the time of sale — that the world they were buying looked, from inside the transaction, like the better bargain. The profit is assessed at settlement, and settlement has not yet arrived.

**The Quran's Commercial Vocabulary**

This is one of the Quran's most consistent registers, and the parallel verses are nearly formulaic: *These are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world* (2:86); *They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance and forgiveness for punishment* (2:175); *Those who trade belief for disbelief will never harm Allah in the least* (3:177). Commerce is the metaphor of choice because it is the one arena in which a sane person knowingly accepts a bad bargain.

What makes the metaphor exact rather than decorative is the structure of the transaction. In both trade and faith, a person gives up something present for something claimed; in both, the value can only be assessed at settlement; and in both, the settlement comes later than the payment. A community of merchants in Makkah needed no explanation of the image.

**"They Are Not Rightly Guided"**

The closing clause looks like a repetition of what has already been said, and the redundancy is deliberate. The first clause described what they *did*. This one describes what they now *are*. Action has become condition, and the transition is the point: a person who keeps making the same trade does not simply hold a series of bad positions, he becomes the kind of person who holds them.

The sūrah states the result without assigning a penalty. Nothing is inflicted. What is described is the shape of a life after a particular kind of exchange — the possession of what was bought and the absence of what was sold, with no mechanism left for getting the second one back.

**The Transaction Everyone Is Running**

The verse is written about the hypocrites and addressed to everyone, because the structure it describes is not peculiar to them. Every person is exchanging something for something else, and the only variables are the goods and the settlement date. The sūrah's question, put as plainly as the metaphor allows, is what a person is currently handing over and what he expects to receive for it.

The advantage of the commercial image is that it can be audited. A person who claims to value the Hereafter can be asked what last week's calendar says. Guidance, on this verse's account, is not a feeling to be reported but a holding to be checked.
