## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:15

> removing rage from their hearts. And Allah pardons whoever He wills. For Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Justice Reaches the Interior**

Verse 15 completes the healing promised in the previous verse by naming the emotion to be removed: rage from the hearts. The Qur'an does not treat the injured believers as if they should feel nothing after betrayal and violence. It acknowledges that anger has entered them. The promise is not simply that the external enemy will be defeated, but that the believers will eventually be freed from the inner heat that the wrong produced.

The word for rage suggests a fierce, pressured anger rather than a passing irritation. Such anger can preserve memory of injustice, but it can also take over the person who carries it. By saying that Allah removes it, the verse places emotional healing within divine care. The believers are not told to justify the wrong, and they are not told to nurse the anger forever. Justice addresses the wrong; mercy can then release the heart from being governed by it.

**Anger Can Point to Justice Without Becoming Its Master**

A community that feels no anger at betrayal may have lost its sense of moral reality. The verse's promise to remove rage implies that the emotion arose in response to something that mattered. The wronged people need not call the attack harmless in order to become peaceful. At the same time, anger is a poor ruler. It exaggerates, seeks new targets, and makes the original injury a reason for fresh injury. Divine healing is not the suppression of moral concern. It is the restoration of proportion.

This distinction matters for the believer's personal life. A person can be right about a wrong and still be damaged by the way he continues to carry it. The Qur'an's prayer, Our Lord, forgive us and our brothers who preceded us in faith, and do not place in our hearts resentment toward those who believe (59:10), shows that the heart's purification is a separate need from the judgment of an act. One can name injustice accurately and still ask Allah to remove its poison.

**The Door of Pardon Opens After the Sentence**

The verse then turns from the rage of the believers to the possibility that Allah may pardon whoever He wills. The transition is important. Punishment of aggression and pardon of a repentant person are not opposites. A person may have been part of a hostile group and later abandon that position. Allah's pardon is not a denial of the harm committed. It is a new judgment made possible by repentance, changed conduct, and divine mercy.

The root of the word for pardon is related to the sūrah's central language of turning. Human beings turn back, and Allah turns toward them. This relationship is visible in the chapter's opening title, al-Tawbah, and in the repeated invitations to repent. Even after the verse has promised punishment and victory, it does not declare that every former enemy is beyond return. The door remains open because Allah's mercy is not limited by the believer's assumption about who can change.

**Knowledge and Wisdom Govern the Pardon**

Allah is named All-Knowing and All-Wise at the end. His knowledge means that no hidden motive is missed. A false conversion cannot deceive Him, and a sincere return cannot be overlooked. His wisdom means that pardon is not random indulgence. It belongs within an order in which repentance, justice, and the protection of the community are all considered.

These names also guide human judgment. Believers do not know every inward state, so they should not claim to possess Allah's final knowledge of people. They may evaluate conduct and protect the community, but they should leave ultimate classification to the One who knows what lies behind the act. Nor should mercy be granted in a way that places victims in renewed danger. Wisdom joins forgiveness to discernment. Knowledge prevents naivety; mercy prevents permanent hatred.

**A Community Must Let Healing Finish**

The promise to remove rage suggests that the community should not preserve anger as a political resource after the danger has passed. Leaders can keep people mobilized by repeating an old injury, and individuals can build identity around grievance. Verse 15 points toward another end. The harm must be addressed, but the heart should not be required to remain in an emergency forever.

This is not a command to force survivors into instant reconciliation. Healing cannot be ordered by a slogan. It is a gift Allah grants through truth, justice, safety, and time. The community can support that gift by honoring the injured, refusing denial, and preventing new harm. It can also support it by allowing those who genuinely repent to become different from what they were. A society that refuses both accountability and pardon traps everyone in the original wound.

**The Verse for a Person Carrying Old Anger**

For the individual believer, verse 15 offers a precise prayer. Do not ask only for the strength to hide anger. Ask Allah to remove its hold after the truth has been faced. A person may need to state what happened, seek restitution, create a boundary, or involve a just authority. None of these steps contradicts forgiveness. They can be the means through which the heart stops confusing surrender to rage with loyalty to the truth.

The verse also offers hope to the one who fears that his anger has become part of his identity. Allah can remove what the person cannot remove by willpower alone. He knows the wound and the wisdom of the cure. The final names are therefore not a distant conclusion. They are the assurance that justice is not blind and mercy is not careless. The God who sees the injury can bring the heart beyond the injury without asking it to call the injury good.
