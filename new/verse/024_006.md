## Sūrah an-Nūr 24:6

> And those who accuse their wives ˹of adultery˺ but have no witness except themselves, the accuser must testify, swearing four times by Allah that he is telling the truth,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Intimate Crisis of Marital Allegation**

The revelation addresses a unique domestic predicament that ruptures the ordinary rules of evidence: *wa-alladhīna yarmūna azwājahum wa-lam yakun lahum shuhadāʾu illā anfusuhum*. The law of slander in 24:4 established an unyielding barrier against defamation, requiring four eyewitnesses and sentencing unproven accusers to eighty lashes. In typical public interactions, that standard shields innocent citizens from malice. Marriage, however, introduces an intimate sphere of life shielded from the public gaze. An act of marital infidelity occurs in private spaces where four independent observers are virtually never present.

A husband who discovers his wife in betrayal faces an agonizing dilemma. If he remains silent, he endures psychological devastation, risks raising a child not his own, and tolerates a desecrated marriage bed. If he speaks publicly without four external witnesses, the general statute of 24:4 would command that he be flogged with eighty lashes as a slanderer. This verse resolves the crisis by introducing the procedure of *liʿān* (mutual imprecation), providing a distinct judicial pathway tailored strictly to the spousal relationship. The law balances personal grief with institutional equity, acknowledging domestic vulnerability while preventing unchecked slander.

**The Substitution of Four Oaths for Four Eyewitnesses**

The legal mechanism substitutes four solemn oaths sworn by God in place of four external witnesses: *fa-shahādatu aḥadihim arbaʿu shahādātin bi-Allāh*. Each individual oath stands in the place of one eyewitness. By commanding the husband to swear four distinct times by the divine name, the law elevates personal testimony into a sacred legal deposition. Swearing by God four times establishes procedural equivalence with the four-witness standard required in ordinary allegations.

Classical jurists emphasized the ceremonial precision of this act. The husband must pronounce each oath distinctly in the presence of the presiding judge. He cannot compress the four declarations into a single consolidated statement, because each repetition represents an independent testimonial step. Divine law harnesses the profound spiritual reverence the believer holds for the Almighty, using solemn oaths to substitute for the sensory corroboration of external spectators. The four oaths constitute a complete judicial structure of proof, binding the speaker's mortal conscience to divine oversight.

**The Assertion of Truthfulness: innahū lamina al-ṣādiqīn**

The husband must conclude each of the four oaths with a definitive formula: *innahū lamina al-ṣādiqīn*, affirming that he is truly among the truthful in his accusation. Truthfulness (*ṣidq*) in the Qur'anic worldview is an objective reality linked to divine order. The husband does not merely state a subjective opinion or convey suspicion; he binds his identity directly to truthfulness under the gaze of the All-Hearing, All-Seeing Creator.

Perjury committed under such conditions is classified in classical jurisprudence as *al-yamīn al-ghamūs*—the catastrophic oath that submerges the swearer into divine condemnation. A believer who fears God recognizes that lying while repeatedly invoking the divine name invites immediate spiritual ruin. The formula leaves no room for ambiguity or hesitation; the accuser must profess absolute certainty regarding the veracity of the charge, acknowledging that God knows every hidden motive. False swearing under the divine name destroys the soul from within.

**The Jurisprudential Distinction Between Spouses and Strangers**

Islamic jurisprudence strictly confines the procedure of *liʿān* to legally married couples. Scholars across the major schools—including Mālik, al-Shāfiʿī, and Abū Ḥanīfah—emphasized that a stranger who accuses a married woman without four eyewitnesses receives no exemption and is immediately sentenced to eighty lashes for *qadhf*. The exception is granted exclusively to a husband because of the existential interests at stake.

A husband bears legal and financial responsibility for the household, the maintenance of the wife, and the legitimacy of the offspring. If a child is born of an adulterous encounter, attributing that child to the husband impacts inheritance rights, familial prohibitions, and lineage integrity (*nasab*). A stranger shares none of these existential burdens. The law therefore grants the husband a unique procedural mechanism to protect his lineage without suffering the penalty of defamation, shielding family integrity from irreparable disruption while denying malicious third parties any opportunity to defame families.

**The Historical Context: The Dilemma of Hilāl ibn Umayyah**

The historical occasion for this legislation is preserved in authoritative traditions, including the report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4747 concerning Hilāl ibn Umayyah. Hilāl came before the Prophet ﷺ after witnessing his wife in compromise with another man. Upon hearing the claim, the Prophet ﷺ held firmly to the established law of slander, declaring: Either bring your proof or face the whip upon your back.

Hilāl cried out in distress, pleading that when a man sees such an outrage in his home, searching for four witnesses is impossible, and he expressed conviction that God would send down revelation to relieve his back from the whip. The descent of verses 6 through 9 provided immediate divine relief for the community, demonstrating how revelation intervened to address concrete human dilemmas while maintaining the transcendent majesty of divine justice. Divine revelation arrived precisely to establish equity where human law was trapped, offering a merciful resolution to an intolerable domestic crisis.

**The Moral Gravity of the Public Assembly**

Classical jurists stipulated that the administration of these oaths must occur in a public and venerated setting. Imām al-Shāfiʿī and Imām Mālik recommended that the ceremony be conducted in the central congregational mosque immediately following the afternoon prayer, surrounded by a gathering of upright believers.

Exposing the marital grievance to the solemn scrutiny of the community and the sanctity of the mosque impresses upon the accuser the terrifying weight of his words. The public setting strips away flippant bravado. Standing before fellow worshippers in the house of God, the husband is compelled to confront the eternal consequence of uttering a false oath before the Almighty, ensuring that litigation is approached with awe rather than reckless passion. The presence of the congregation guarantees that justice is solemn, transparent, and revered.
