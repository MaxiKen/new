## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:56

> O My believing servants! My earth is truly spacious, so worship Me ˹alone˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Call That Treats Believers as Servants**

The verse changes address completely, and its opening is one of the most affectionate constructions in the Book: the One who speaks calls the believers His servants. The word chosen is the same one used for every creature, but attached here to faith it becomes a title of honour, and the plural possessive fixes a relationship rather than a status. The command that follows is deceptively short — worship Me — and the whole force of the sentence lies in the clause between the address and the command, which supplies a fact the audience needed: the earth is spacious. In a chapter that has documented the pressure on believers from every angle, the instruction is not to endure the pressure where it stands but to recognize that the ground underneath them is not confined to one city.

The address also places the community inside a relationship rather than a nationality. What the listeners are called is servants of God, a description that follows them across borders and outlives the town they leave behind, and the instruction gains its authority from that relationship rather than from any territory.

**The Earth's Width as a Practical Answer**

The verse was reportedly revealed in connection with Muslims who could not practise their religion openly in Makkah and who were advised to emigrate to Abyssinia, where they would be free to worship. The report fits the situation the sūrah has described from its opening, where the pressure on the first community included confinement, intimidation, and the prevention of migration itself. The Book's answer to that condition is not a promise that the pressure will lift but an assertion about geography. A person whose worship is impossible in one place is told that the earth does not consist of that place, and the sentence treats the discovery of this fact as a matter of obedience rather than a concession to weakness.

The reply is also unusually practical for a chapter so concerned with the Hereafter. Instead of asking the believers to endure a specific harm, the verse points them to a place where the harm does not exist, which is the Book's standard advice to a community whose worship has become impossible where it lives.

**Worship as the Purpose of the Journey**

The command at the end of the verse keeps the migration from becoming an end in itself. What the believer is told to seek is not safety, prosperity, or a better market; it is the ability to worship God. Commentators read the verse as an instruction to anyone who cannot be faithful to his religion in the land where he lives, and the instruction is stated in the simplest possible terms. The Book elsewhere makes the same point by the effect it attributes to the move rather than by the move itself: what the emigrants gain in this world is a good home, and what they gain in the next is better. The sūrah's placement of the sentence immediately after a description of the fire is instructive, since it presents the journey as a person's answer to a warning rather than as a remedy for homesickness.

The wording also keeps the two motives of the instruction together. The command is to worship, and the reason given for moving is the freedom to do so, so the journey is described as an act of devotion rather than as a search for better circumstances.

**The Question the Angels Will Ask**

The seriousness of the instruction is stated in another chapter where the angels seize the souls of those who have wronged themselves and ask them what state they were in. When they answer that they were oppressed in the land, the angels reply by asking whether God's earth was not spacious enough for them to emigrate in, and the verse reports their destination with the phrase that it is an evil journey's end (4:97). The passage continues with an exemption for those who genuinely cannot find a way out, describing helpless men, women, and children, for whom the Book allows the hope of pardon (4:98–99). Read together with this verse, the two passages establish both halves of the rule: the earth's width is a fact to be acted on, and anyone truly trapped inside a narrower space is not held responsible for what he cannot do.

**What the Instruction Says About This World**

The sentence also revises a believer's attachment to a particular location. A person who has invested his identity in a city, a clan, or a trade route may find the suggestion that he move to be a demand to abandon himself, and the verse anticipates the objection by addressing the reader as a servant of God rather than as a resident of anywhere. The Book's treatment of Abraham, who announced his emigration in obedience to his Lord, and of Lot, who declared that he was fleeing to his Lord, sets the pattern that this verse applies to the community. What is portable in a life is the relationship with God; everything else can be left at the border.

The instruction also settles what a believer's attachment to a place is worth. A town, a family plot, or a trading connection can be left when it stands between a person and his worship, and the sūrah treats that willingness as a normal feature of faith rather than an emergency measure reserved for extreme cases.

**A Command That Cannot Be Framed as Retreat**

The Book never presents migration in these passages as a defeat. It is described as a transfer of operations to ground where the work can proceed, and the reward attached to it in the Book is stated in both worlds. Sūrah al-Naḥl promises those who emigrated after being persecuted a good home in this life and a better reward in the hereafter, and describes them as people who endured patiently and placed their trust in their Lord (16:41–42). The two clauses are the answer to the objections the reader might raise. The first makes the move materially possible, and the second names the dispositions that make it a form of worship rather than an escape.

**The Earth as a Place Given to the Righteous**

The Book treats the habitable earth as something granted to a particular kind of inhabitant, and it states the arrangement twice: Moses reassures his people that the earth belongs to God alone, that He grants it to whoever He chooses of His servants, and that the outcome belongs to the righteous (7:128), and another passage reports that God decreed in the Scriptures that His righteous servants shall inherit the land (21:105). The command to emigrate in this verse sits inside that promise. A believer who leaves a hostile place is not abandoning a territory to its owners; he is moving within an earth whose title belongs elsewhere, and the sūrah's instruction to spread out in it is the practical form of the assurance. The spaciousness of the land is mentioned because the entitlement is not what the residents assume it is.

**Why the Sūrah Says This Now**

By this point in the chapter the reader has been told that trials are certain, that the community will be sorted by its response to them, and that the fire is the destination of those who mock the warning. What the sūrah supplies next is the practical instruction that makes the rest survivable, and it puts that instruction in the mouth of God speaking to believers directly and calling them His servants. The verse that follows begins with the same concern in a different key by naming the one event that no migration can avoid. Together the two sentences cover the whole of the believer's situation: he is told that the ground beneath him is wider than his difficulties, and then that the time available to him is shorter than his plans.
