## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:127

> I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messenger Removes a Suspicion**

Hūd has asked a powerful people to change its worship and conduct. Such a demand can provoke the accusation that the messenger is pursuing a private benefit. This verse answers that suspicion directly. He does not seek payment from ʿĀd for conveying the warning. His service is not a commercial arrangement in which acceptance purchases his approval.

The statement does not make the message less valuable. It makes its value independent of the audience's ability or willingness to pay. A poor person can hear the same warning as a chief, and a chief cannot buy a softer version. The message comes with equal force to every member of the people.

**Reward From the Lord Frees the Messenger**

Hūd locates his reward with the Lord of all worlds. That phrase expands his horizon beyond ʿĀd's wealth, praise, and threats. If his reward depended on the people, he might flatter them, conceal their wrongdoing, or abandon the call when they resisted. By expecting reward from God, he remains free to speak truthfully.

This independence is not emotional indifference. Hūd wants his people to return to God and fears their punishment. Yet his concern cannot be reduced to the success of a campaign or the growth of a personal following. He serves because God entrusted him with a duty, and God knows the worth of that service even if the people refuse it.

**A Verified Parallel Establishes the Same Principle**

Quran 11:51 records Hūd telling ʿĀd that he asks no reward and that his reward is with the One who created him. The parallel confirms that the absence of payment is part of his public claim, not a detail invented by later readers. His mission is grounded in dependence on the Creator rather than on the community.

The Qur'anic pattern also appears in the call of Noah and in the other prophetic accounts of this sūrah. The repeated refusal of payment distinguishes revelation from a scheme for social control. A messenger may be opposed, but he does not place salvation behind a fee or sell access to God.

**No Reward Does Not Mean No Cost**

Hūd's lack of a financial demand should not be confused with an easy mission. He risks rejection by people stronger than he is, and he must confront customs connected to their pride. A servant can receive no worldly payment and still bear a heavy cost in patience, loneliness, and danger.

The verse therefore teaches the difference between compensation and sacrifice. Hūd does not bargain for gain, but he gives his time, courage, and reputation to the call. His refusal of payment shows the purity of his motive; it does not show that obedience is effortless. Those who follow a truthful mission should expect service, not profit, to define their work.

**The Lord of All Worlds Is the Proper Recipient of Hope**

By naming God as the Lord of all worlds, Hūd places his reward with the One who owns every audience and every outcome. The people of ʿĀd may control one region, but they do not control the creation. Their approval is local and temporary. God's knowledge and recompense encompass the messenger, the people, and the consequences of their choices.

This title also prevents tribal bargaining. Hūd is not saying, “I ask no reward from you because another tribe will pay me.” His hope is in the Lord of every people. The call is therefore universal in its moral scope, even while he speaks to one particular society.

**A Pure Message Cannot Be Bought**

If religious guidance becomes a product, the wealthy can expect special treatment and the poor can be made to feel excluded. Hūd's declaration breaks that arrangement. He cannot be bribed into silence, and he cannot turn God's mercy into a private possession. The message remains a trust that must be conveyed to all.

This does not condemn every lawful payment received by teachers or workers. Human labor can be compensated, and communities may support those who serve them. The point here is the messenger's motive and authority: acceptance of his warning is not a purchase, and rejection does not deprive him of his true reward.

**The Statement Exposes the Chiefs' Motives**

The chiefs may have enjoyed wealth and influence, but Hūd asks nothing from their stores. If they still reject him, their refusal cannot honestly be explained by the cost of his demand. The issue is not a fee. It is whether they will surrender the illusion that their power belongs to them without accountability.

A person who refuses truth because it threatens his interests may accuse the speaker of self-interest. Hūd's independence turns that accusation back into an invitation to examine the heart. What benefit is the hearer protecting? What authority would he lose if he obeyed God?

**The Reward Beyond the Audience**

Hūd's words comfort every servant whose good work is overlooked. The audience may not thank him, and its response may even be hostile, but God is not unaware. The servant's task is to be trustworthy, clear, and sincere; the Lord decides how the message will be received and records the sacrifice made in conveying it.

For ʿĀd, the statement is another mercy before judgment. They are told that Hūd seeks no worldly advantage, so they can listen without fearing a hidden transaction. If they reject him after that clarification, they reject guidance for the sake of pride. The messenger has released them from suspicion and directed every hope back to the Lord of all worlds.

His independence makes the call a matter of conscience, not negotiation. They must decide whether to submit to God rather than calculate what Hūd might gain.
