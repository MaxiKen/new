## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:77

> ˹To distance themselves,˺ Joseph’s brothers argued, “If he has stolen, so did his ˹full˺ brother before.” But Joseph suppressed his outrage—revealing nothing to them—and said ˹to himself˺, “You are in such an evil position, and Allah knows best ˹the truth of˺ what you claim.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Defence That Damages Someone Else**

The brothers' first reaction to the discovery is not a protest of Benjamin's innocence. It is a comparison: if he has stolen, a brother of his stole before. The move is defensive and it works by association — the offence is presented as a family trait rather than an individual act, which spreads it across a household and thereby dilutes it. The chapter records the strategy in its own parenthetical: they spoke in order to distance themselves. Ten men who have just watched a penalty they proposed become applicable to one of their number respond by attaching the offence to a person who is not present and, as far as they know, not alive. The mechanism is familiar and the chapter identifies it without naming it. A group under suspicion protects itself by locating the fault in someone who cannot answer, and in this family that person has been unavailable for twenty years. What the brothers do here is what they did in verse 18, and the second performance is offered to the reader as evidence about the first.

**The Brother They Mean Is Joseph**

The commentators are unanimous that the reference is to Joseph, and they record the incident behind it. He is said to have taken an idol belonging to his maternal grandfather and destroyed it so that it would not be worshipped. The story is not in the Qur'an and cannot be verified from it, but its function in the brothers' argument is clear: they are claiming a precedent of theft inside the same maternal line, which is Benjamin's line as well as Joseph's. The accusation therefore does two things at once. It defends Benjamin by comparison and it disparages Joseph, and the brothers do not know that the man they are disparaging is the man they are addressing.

**The Claim Reaches a Dead Brother**

There is a further consequence the brothers cannot see. Joseph is, in their account, long dead — devoured by a wolf, according to the report they gave their father (12:17–18). They are now naming him as a thief in a public setting, in a foreign country, before an official of the crown. The slander falls on a person who is standing in front of them. The chapter has produced ironies before, but this is the sharpest: a false statement about a dead man, made to save a living one, addressed to the dead man himself. The verse does not remark on it. It reports the statement and Joseph's response. The report is flat and the flatness is deliberate. Twenty years of grief, a bloodied shirt, a father whose eyes went white and an invented wolf are compressed into a clause offered as legal precedent, and the verse records the clause without marking what it costs to say. What the brothers have turned into an argument was the worst thing that ever happened to their father.

**"He Concealed It in Himself"**

The narrator's comment is precise. Joseph keeps the matter inside himself and does not disclose it to them. The commentators specify what was concealed: the sentence that follows was spoken to himself rather than aloud. The chapter therefore shows a man receiving an insult about his own character, in public, from the people who sold him, and not answering. The restraint is not indifference. The verse names what is being restrained — the response he would have made — and reports that it stayed inside him. Nothing in the narrative suggests he lacked the power to speak; he had the power to detain a man under a law his brothers supplied. What he conceals is knowledge rather than weakness, and the concealment has a purpose. To reveal himself now would end the process he has built, confronting the brothers with their crime before they had reached any understanding of it. Joseph withholds the disclosure because a confession extracted at the moment of maximum shock is worth less than one arrived at by a long and difficult road.

**"You Are in a Worse Position"**

The words Joseph does not say are the verse's judgement. The commentators gloss them as meaning that the brothers stand in a worse position than Benjamin, because they stole Joseph from his father and treated him unjustly, while Benjamin took no part in that act. The comparison is exact and it reverses the brothers' own move. They argued from a lesser theft to a greater one, claiming that Benjamin's offence has a precedent in his brother's. Joseph's unspoken answer accepts the logic of comparison and applies it correctly: the real theft in this family was of a person, and it was committed by the men now protesting about a cup. The comparison is exact in its terms. They have just accused a brother of stealing a cup; the speaker knows that the same men stole a brother and sold him for twenty pieces of silver. The verse lets the true statement be made in a form its audience cannot recognise, so they hear a rebuke they will understand only years later, when they are told who is speaking.

**"God Knows Best What You Describe"**

The second half of the unspoken sentence refers the matter to divine knowledge. The brothers have described a situation — a family prone to theft, a precedent, a character trait — and the description is false. Joseph does not correct it in front of them. He states that God knows best what they are describing, which is both a rejection of their account and a refusal to argue with it. The formula has appeared earlier in the chapter, in Jacob's mouth, when the brothers brought the bloodied shirt: *God alone must be sought for help against what you describe* (12:18). Father and son use the same recourse about the same men and the same habit of describing falsely.

**Restraint as the Chapter's Portrait of Joseph**

Verse 77 belongs to a consistent pattern. Joseph does not reveal himself in verse 58, does not accuse the brothers in verse 59, does not retaliate in verse 70, does not gloat in verse 76 and does not answer here. The disclosure comes in verse 90, after the brothers have been tested and after they have admitted their fault. The chapter is describing a person who withholds a true statement because saying it at the wrong moment would destroy the thing he is trying to accomplish. What he is accomplishing is not revenge but a reunion, and a reunion requires that the brothers arrive at their own admission rather than be confronted with his. That requirement shapes every scene from here to the end. Joseph could have spoken at any moment and the story would have closed; instead he waits through a search, a detention, a private conference and a second journey, until the men who sold him describe their own crime to his face without knowing it. The reunion he is preparing is an admission before it is an embrace.

**The Last Unknowing Insult**

After this verse the brothers stop defending themselves and start pleading. Verse 78 asks for a substitution; verse 80 records the eldest's refusal to leave Egypt; verse 81 has them return home with a report their father does not believe. The insult in verse 77 is therefore the last thing they say about Joseph while believing him dead, and the chapter places it in the mouth of men who will spend the next twenty verses discovering that everything they said about him was wrong. The verse is also the only place in the sūrah where Joseph's feelings about his brothers are recorded at all, and they are recorded as feelings he did not express.
