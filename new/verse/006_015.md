## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:15

> Say, “I truly fear—if I were to disobey my Lord—the torment of a tremendous Day.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messenger Is Told to Announce His Own Fear**

The instruction here is not to warn the Makkans about the Day but to tell them something about himself. The man who has been threatened, mocked, and offered a compromise is commanded to state that he too stands under the threat: if he were to disobey his Lord, he would fear the torment of a tremendous Day. It is a startling thing for the founder of a movement to say in public, because every other kind of authority in Arabia was built on the assumption that the man at the top was the least exposed.

The confession also removes a suspicion his hearers might have carried. A preacher who announced judgment on others while standing visibly outside the judgment would be understandable, and would be no different from the tribal soothsayers who sold verdicts. By placing himself inside the warning, the Prophet makes his message expensive for himself as well as for them. The sentence is also a public refusal of the role his opponents kept offering him, which was to be a poet or a soothsayer whose words cost him nothing.

**The Same Line Repeated in Another Sūrah**

A nearly identical sentence appears in the sūrah of the Crowds: I truly fear, if I were to disobey my Lord, the torment of a tremendous Day (39:13). The repetition matters for how the verse should be read. This is not a momentary remark the Prophet improvised under pressure; it is part of the confession he was commanded to deliver, and the Book records it twice in the same form.

Repetition in the Qur'an usually signals that a statement carries structural weight. What is being established is the shape of prophethood: a man who carries a warning he believes, addressed first to himself, and then to everyone else. The Book does not permit its messenger to be the one figure exempt from what he announces.

**A Leader Standing Inside His Own Warning**

Elsewhere the same Prophet is told exactly what he does not possess: he does not say he holds God's treasuries, or knows the unseen, or is an angel; he only follows what is revealed to him (6:50). The verse in front of us continues that self-description. He is not a shareholder in the divine power and not a priest with privileged immunity; he is a servant with an instruction and a fear.

That combination is what makes his claim credible to an audience that knew him. Anyone can warn a city. A man who warns a city while describing his own exposure to the same danger has told his listeners that he is not selling them a product. The message costs him the thing he is telling them to protect: his own safety.

**The Day and Its Adjective**

The Book has many names and descriptions for the final reckoning, and one of its standing adjectives is size. The Day is called tremendous, formidable, and great, and the adjective is attached to its torment as often as to the Day itself. The language is deliberately disproportionate to anything in the audience's experience, because the argument requires them to weigh an unseen event against visible possessions.

An earlier passage uses the same register when it warns that if the addressed turn away, they are feared for the torment of a formidable Day (11:3). The vocabulary is not there to frighten for its own sake. It is there because the audience was being asked to trade a comfortable present for a claim about the future, and the Book refuses to make the trade look small.

Size language also keeps the comparison honest. The audience measured its security in things it could count: camels, wells, sons, and alliances. Against that inventory the verse sets a single adjective describing an event no ledger can absorb. The mismatch is the argument, and the adjective is doing the work of a proof.

**Fear That Lives Beside Hope**

The verse does not describe the Prophet as despairing, and the Book never puts fear in opposition to hope. The sincere are described as those who abandon their beds, calling on their Lord with hope and fear (32:16). The two travel together because each corrects the other: hope alone drifts into presumption, and fear alone curdles into a portrait of God that no one could love.

What the Prophet's confession supplies is a standard. In Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 20, when the Companions told him that God had already forgiven his past and future sins and that they were not like him, he became angry and said that he was the most God-fearing and the most knowledgeable of God among them. The point is not that he feared more than others as an achievement. It is that knowledge of God and fear of God increase together, and the man who knows most has the least reason to feel safe.

**Why the Verse Ends on the Warning Rather Than the Mercy**

Only one clause follows: a tremendous Day of torment. The sūrah has already said that God prescribed mercy for Himself (6:12), and the verse after this one describes being spared as the triumph. Between those statements the Prophet is told to hold up the frightening side of the matter without softening it, and to include himself in it.

That ordering serves a purpose in a city that had decided the Day was a joke. A warning delivered by someone visibly unafraid would have confirmed their suspicion that the whole thing was a performance. The fear of the messenger is the first piece of evidence that the Day is real, and the Book makes him supply it in his own name before he asks anyone else for anything.
