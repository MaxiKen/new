## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:22

> ˹Consider˺ the Day We will gather them all together then ask those who associated others ˹with Allah in worship˺, “Where are those gods you used to claim?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Interrogation Rather Than an Announcement**

The scene opens with a question addressed to the polytheists, and the question is not about what they did. It asks where the gods are. In a trial, that kind of question is not an attempt to obtain information, since the one asking knows the answer. It is the device a judge uses to make the accused confront the object of his own claim, and it works by requiring him to look.

The Book returns to this question with a frequency that suggests it was the most effective thing that could be said to a people who had built their lives around shrines. On the Day of Judgment He will humiliate them and say, "Where are My associate-gods for whose sake you used to oppose the believers?" (16:27). The same challenge is reported in the sūrah of Stories twice over (28:62; 28:74), and again in the sūrah of the Believer (40:73), and once in Fussilat (41:47).

**The Gathering That Comes Before the Question**

All of humanity is assembled first: We will gather them all together. The Book insists on the totality of that assembly because the Makkan argument had rested on a small society of important people. In this world a shrine belongs to the strong, who can prevent questions from being asked and shut down the ones who ask them. At the gathering, the audience is every human being who has ever lived, and the tribe can no longer determine who speaks or when.

That is why the verse says "all together" rather than naming the guilty group. The polytheists are addressed by name afterwards — those who associated others with Allah in worship — but they are addressed inside a crowd that removes every advantage they had enjoyed on earth.

**The Word "Claim" Puts the Charge in Their Own Mouths**

The verse does not say "those gods you used to worship"; it says the gods you used to claim. The substitution is pointed. A claim is a public assertion a person advances, and the Book treats the theology of the Makkans as exactly that: a proposition maintained by a community, defended in councils, and financed by the offerings made at its shrines. There is no suggestion anywhere in the verse that the gods had an independent existence which the audience had discovered.

Everything in the sentence reduces the deities to an assertion. They were claimed into being as objects of worship, kept alive by repetition, and defended by social pressure. That is why the question at the end can be so short. If the gods owe their existence to a claim, the collapse of the claimants ends them.

**Where the Absent Are**

The answer is silence. In the parallel passages the accused cry out that their gods have failed them and confess that they had invoked nothing real (40:74); elsewhere the associates are said to fail their worshippers and the worshippers realise there is no escape (41:48). The question therefore works as a demonstration rather than a rebuke. Everyone present sees, in the same instant, that the whole apparatus was empty.

Nothing is reported about their gods arriving to defend them, and nothing is reported about their intercession, which is exactly the point the verse wants to press. The Book allows the idols every kind of dignity except presence at the moment when presence would matter.

**Why the Scene Is Rehearsed So Often**

A Makkan listener could dismiss a claim about the Hereafter as a story from elsewhere, since no one had returned to confirm it. He could not dismiss what the claim implied about his own confidence. Each time the Book stages this question, it invites its hearer to imagine a situation in which the things he relies on can no longer help, and to notice what he would then have left.

The frequency of the scene also tells the reader something about how the Book argues with idolatry. It could have listed the idols' deficiencies one by one, and it does that elsewhere. Here it does something sharper: it takes the audience to a moment when no deficiency has to be explained, because the objects of worship simply are not present. Nobody in the crowd needs a lesson in theology when the defendants fail to appear.

That exercise is available to any reader in any century. The gods may be stones, or markets, or the opinion of a family, or a standing among neighbours, and the question keeps its shape: the things relied on are the things that will be asked about, by name, in front of everyone, and the answer will be given by the person who staked his life on them.

**The Question Left Standing for the Hearer**

The verse places the reader in the position of the judge's assistant: he watches the interrogation and hears the question before the accused does. That arrangement is deliberate, and it is the reason the sūrah says earlier that God knows what is concealed and what is revealed (6:3).

Nothing in this verse is settled by argument, and nothing is settled by force. A question is put, a claim is quoted back to its owner, and the answer is left to arrive in due course. The verse ends on the question mark, which is where the audience's own thinking has to begin.

It is also worth noticing what the verse does not ask. There is no request for an explanation, no demand for repentance, and no negotiation about terms. The question concerns the location of a set of objects that the accused had treated as divine, and the only honest answer available to them is the one the Book records in the parallel passages, where the gods are said to have failed them and the worshippers admit they had invoked nothing real (40:74).
