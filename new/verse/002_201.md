## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:201

> Yet there are others who say, “Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whole Prayer**

The verse quotes the second of the two supplications, and against the truncated prayer before it — give to us in this world, and nothing further — this petition is built on three clauses spanning both worlds and their peril: *Our Lord, give us good in this world, and good in the Hereafter, and guard us from the punishment of the Fire*. The architecture is exact. The first clause holds the world without shame: the pilgrim who has just spent weeks performing rites for the Hereafter is taught to ask for this world's good too, and to ask for it first, in the order of lived need. The second clause holds the Hereafter without presumption: the world's good is requested as passage, not destination, and the destination is requested in the same breath. The third clause names the one evil that makes both goods irrelevant — the Fire — and asks protection from it, so that the prayer's final word is not acquisition but refuge. Between the two giving-clauses and the guarding-clause the whole of a Muslim's wanting is gathered: provision for the road, arrival at the end, and safety from the ruin that swallows both. The verse quotes it without commentary because the quotation is the commentary: against the single-horizon soul, the double-horizon sentence stands complete.

**Ḥasanah: The Beautiful Good**

The word the prayer uses for its two gifts — *ḥasanah* — carries more than the translation's "good." The root ḥ-s-n bears the sense of beauty: the ḥasanah is the beautiful thing, the good that is also fitting, radiant, whole. The righteous predecessors elaborated its content, and the tradition's glosses gather the concrete and the comprehensive: good in this world has been read as a righteous spouse — the prayer's most intimate petition — as sufficient and lawful provision, as security, health, and the ability to worship; good in the Hereafter as the Garden itself, with everything the name contains. The word's generosity is its point: the prayer does not itemize because the ḥasanah includes every beautiful thing the two worlds hold, and the asker commits the itemization to the Giver. The Qur'an's own usage confirms the breadth — Moses prays ordain for us good in this life and the next (7:156); the virtuous are told that for those who do good in this world there is goodness, and the Home of the Hereafter is better (16:30); Abraham is granted good in this world and stands among the righteous in the next (16:122). Each instance carries the same double grant in the same beautiful word, and the pilgrim's petition inherits them all.

**The Prophet's Most Frequent Supplication**

The verse's duʿāʾ is not merely a Quranic sentence but the Prophet's own habitual prayer, and the report is among the best-attested in the tradition. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim preserves it (2690): Qatādah asked Anas ibn Mālik which supplication the Messenger of God made most frequently, and Anas answered that his most constant prayer was this — O Allah, grant us the good in this world and the good in the Hereafter, and save us from the torment of the Fire. Anas added his own practice to the report: whenever he made any supplication he included this one within it, so that every prayer he ever prayed carried the Quranic petition folded inside it. The identification is explicit in the sources — this is the verse's prayer, the duʿāʾ of 2:201, spoken by the Prophet more than any other words of asking. The report settles the verse's status in Muslim practice: not a text to be admired but a sentence to be repeated, the petition its Revealer chose to make the most-repeated petition of His Messenger. The pilgrim at Minā who prays it is praying the prayer the Prophet prayed most, at the stations the Prophet taught him, with Anas's inclusion as the model of how to carry it into every other asking.

**The Prayer's Life in the Community**

From the verse and the report grew a practice that runs through Muslim worship to this day. Ibn Kathīr notes that this supplication is frequently repeated by Muslims in their individual prayers and appears constantly in the books of collected duʿāʾ — and its most iconic placement is inside the hajj itself: the pilgrims recite it during the circuits of the Kaʿbah, traditionally in the stretch between the Yemeni Corner and the Black Stone, so that the Quran's prayer is spoken at the Quran's house in the rhythm of the tawāf. The verse thereby completes a circle with its context: revealed as the pilgrims' petition at the rites' end, it became the pilgrims' petition at the rites' center, and through them the daily petition of the whole community — at weddings and funerals, after the obligatory prayers, in the qunūt of calamity, in the private asking of the night. The comprehensiveness explains the adoption: a prayer that holds both worlds and the Fire's guard is a prayer that never needs replacing, suitable to every condition because it asks for everything that matters in the order that matters. The tradition's verdict on it is implicit in Anas's practice — the sufficient prayer, the one into which every other prayer can be folded.

**Against the Truncated Asking**

Set beside the previous verse's single-world prayer, this petition defines the Islam of the tongue. The first group asked for this world and received its limits — no share in the Hereafter; this group asks for both and, the next verse will state, receives a portion from what it earned. The contrast is not between material and spiritual temperaments: the second prayer is *more* material in one sense, since it asks for this world's good explicitly, naming what the ascetic might disdain to request. The difference is the horizon and the order. The truncated prayer treats the world as the whole; the whole prayer treats it as the first half — requested, valued, and outranked in the same sentence. And the third clause does further work against a third error: the prayer that asks for both goods without the Fire's guard would be a prayer of presumption, as though the goods were owed; the guarding-clause keeps the asker in the posture the whole passage has trained — the pilgrim who asked forgiveness at his rite's summit (2:199), who remembers God more ardently than his fathers' boasts (2:200), and who now requests everything while taking refuge from the one loss that annuls everything. Wanting whole, asking humbly: the verse's sentence is both.

**The Supplication as the Rites' Fruit**

The verse's position at the hajj-legislation's turning point makes the duʿāʾ the pilgrimage's stated product. The manāsik are completed (2:200); the remembrance replaces the boast; and the petition replaces the appetite. What the weeks of iḥrām, the plain, the surge, and the nights of Minā were for is now visible in a single sentence: a heart re-sorted in its wanting, praying with both horizons open and the Fire in view. The verses that follow will confirm the sorting — the portion earned by such askers, the reckoning swift upon it (2:202) — but the sort itself happens here, in the words. The tradition's reading of the prayer's clauses as a curriculum completes the picture: the righteous predecessors who read good in this world as a righteous spouse and lawful provision, and good in the Hereafter as the Garden, treated the duʿāʾ as the syllabus of a whole life — marriage, work, worship, death, judgment, all named or implied in three clauses. The pilgrim who learns the sentence at Minā carries home not a souvenir but a structure: every future asking already shaped by the double horizon, the beautiful good, and the guard against the Fire.
