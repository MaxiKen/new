## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:148

> So Allah gave them the reward of this world and the excellent reward of the Hereafter. For Allah loves the good-doers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Both Rewards Given**

The verse reports the result of the conduct and the prayer described in the two verses before it: God gave those people the reward of this world and the best reward of the Hereafter. The Book's promises of this kind appear in several places, and the phrasing here covers the whole of what a person can receive.

The Book does not usually promise both to those who seek only one. It tells those who want the harvest of the next life that it will be increased for them, and it tells those who want only the present one that they will receive some of it while having no share in the next. The sentence in this verse belongs to a different group, and the community at Madinah was being shown what their position could yield.

**The Reward of This World**

Al-Ṭabarī explained the reward of this world as victory, the spoils of war, and stability in the community's affairs. The phrase therefore covers the concrete goods that a community needs in order to continue: success in its engagements, the means to sustain itself, and a settled condition in which its work can go forward.

The Book states elsewhere that with God are the rewards of this world and the next, which places both in His hands rather than in the hands of circumstances. The prayer in the preceding verse asked for steadiness and help, and the answer given here describes what arrives when such a request is granted.

**The Best Reward of the Hereafter**

The second half of the sentence uses the form that marks excellence, and the Hereafter is described as the better of the two, or the most beautiful. The Book uses this comparative language in several places when it places the two lives side by side.

The arrangement does not diminish the first reward; it orders the two. The community is told that what it receives in this world is genuine and worth having, and that what is stored in the next one is better. The Book's consistent teaching about the value of the two lives appears here in a sentence that grants both, and that sentence must have carried particular weight for people who had just lost property and family.

**The Description of the Virtuous**

The verse closes by naming those whom God loves, and the term used covers people who do good and do it well. The same term appears in the Book's praise of people who spend in his way, of those who restrain their anger and pardon, and of those who keep their covenants.

The word's frequency in these contexts suggests that the Book uses it as a broad category for the conduct it commends, and the sūrah has already used it in the passage about the people for whom the garden is prepared. The verse therefore joins the steadfastness of the preceding verses with the generosity and restraint described earlier in the sūrah, and it places the whole range of such conduct under the declaration of God's love.

**The Book's Statements About Whom God Loves**

The Book attaches its love to particular qualities in a set of passages that the commentators gathered. It names the reverent, the patient, the just, and those who act equitably, and it names the virtuous as the group mentioned most often.

The variety is significant. The Book is not describing an attitude that appears in one kind of person but a set of qualities that its people are expected to hold together in their dealings. Each of the passages names a quality that appears in a different setting: worship, endurance, trade, judgment, and the settling of disputes. The sentence in this verse names the broadest of the categories, and the sūrah's readers are meant to see their own conduct inside it.

**The Link With the Verses Before It**

Read as a sequence, the three verses describe a movement: people who stand firm under loss, a prayer that asks for forgiveness and firmness, and a God who gives both the goods of this life and the better things of the next.

The arrangement gives the community a way of reading its own experience. The losses at Uḥud were real, and the sūrah does not promise that the community will recover every loss in the same currency. What it promises is that the conduct it has described is not wasted, and that the reward reaches into the situation the community is in as well as into the one that follows it.

**What the Community Heard in This Sentence**

The first listeners were people who had recently seen their dead carried back from a field they had expected to leave in better order. The verse told them that their endurance had a result and that the result would be given to them in full.

The Book's practice in these passages is to state the outcome before the history has arrived at it. The community would go on to see victories and setbacks in turn, and the sentence here teaches it to read both within a promise that the two rewards belong to the same God, and that the conduct which earns them is the conduct described in the verses immediately before.

**A Pattern for Later Generations**

Later communities have read these verses in their own circumstances, and the pattern they describe has been understood as general. The qualities required are steadiness, an honest reckoning with one's own faults, and the firm hope that God will steady what is unsteady.

The verses present that pattern without tying it to the specific events that occasioned it, which is why they have travelled so well. The battle at Uḥud is the setting for the instruction, and the instruction itself concerns the ordinary life of a community that has to keep going after something has gone badly wrong.
