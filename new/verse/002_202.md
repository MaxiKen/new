## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:202

> It is they who will receive a ˹heavenly˺ reward for the good they have done. Surely Allah is swift in reckoning.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Portion of What Was Earned**

The verse settles the account of the two prayers. To the askers of the previous verses it assigns a portion — *nasīb* — from what they earned: the reward is not a gift unconnected to the asking but a share answerable to the work, and the word of earning — *kasb* — is the Qur'an's standard term for the deed that carries its consequence. Al-Rāzī records the exegetes' division over whose portion the verse names. Some read it as belonging to the second group alone — the ones who asked for good in both worlds receive their earned share in both. Others extend it to the first group as well — the single-world askers receive a portion too, in the world they asked for and nowhere else — so that the verse describes both settlements at once: the world-seeker's portion paid in the world, the both-worlds-seeker's portion paid in both. The comprehensive reading gives the verse its sharpest edge: the portion principle is universal, and every asker is paid from what he earned, with the asking itself counted among the earnings. The prayer is the work; the portion is its wage; and the wage's geography follows the prayer's horizon.

**The Duʿāʾ as Earning**

The verse's economics make supplication a labor with a yield, and the tradition took the claim seriously. The portion is *from what they earned* — the asker's own petition is his first earning, since the duʿāʾ that spans both worlds is itself an act of faith: it confesses that the world is not the whole, that the Hereafter is real, that the Fire is possible, and that the asking reaches a Hearer. The single-world prayer earns its narrowness — the horizon of the sentence becomes the horizon of the payment. The whole prayer earns its width: the good requested for this world is granted as provision counted in the portion, the good requested for the Hereafter is granted as the Garden counted in the portion, and the guard requested against the Fire is granted as the protection that makes both portions safe to hold. Nothing in the verse suggests the portion is proportioned to the prayer's eloquence or length — the earned share answers the prayer's content, the heart's actual wanting behind the words. This is why the previous verse could legislate a formula: a sentence that asks in the right order for the right things is an earning whose portion the verse now guarantees.

**Swift in Reckoning**

The second clause names the speed of the settlement: God is swift in reckoning — *sarīʿ al-ḥisāb*. The attribute answers an objection latent in the portion-principle: the wage is promised, but the payer's audit of the whole creation would seem to require an age — how can the account of every asker be settled at all? The swiftness is the answer, and it is an attribute the Qur'an repeats at exactly such junctures: the People of the Scripture did not dispute until knowledge came to them, and whoever denies God's signs should know that God is swift in reckoning (3:19). The reckoning's speed follows from the reckoner's nature: an audit is slow in proportion to the auditor's distance from his accounts, and God's distance is zero — He knows the portion before it is asked, the earning before it is made, the prayer before the tongue forms it. Nothing must be gathered, retrieved, or reconstructed; the account stands already complete in His knowledge, and the reckoning is therefore instantaneous. The exegetes drew the practical corollary in both directions: swift for the reward, so the asker is not kept waiting on his portion; swift for the audit, so the deferrer of repentance is not granted the delay he assumes. The Day's arithmetic is not slow justice but immediate disclosure.

**The Reckoning and the Supplicant**

The swiftness has a personal edge for the praying pilgrim, and the passage's own structure supplies it. The verse before the duʿāʾ-diptych commanded the pilgrim at his rite's summit to ask forgiveness (2:199); the verse before the diptych stationed remembrance at the rites' end (2:200); the nearness-verse of the fasting legislation promised I respond to the call of the caller (2:186). The whole context is a theology of answered prayer, and swift in reckoning is its final guarantee: the portion of the earned prayer is not filed for a distant processing but settled by a reckoning that outruns the asking. The believer who prays 2:201's sentence at Minā, between the Yemeni Corner and the Black Stone, in the night or the crowd, prays into an accounting already moving — the kasb registered, the nasīb assigned, the reckoning swift. The clause thus converts the portion-principle from a warning into a comfort: what you have earned is held for you by One whose audit needs no time, and the speed that makes the sinner's case fearful makes the asker's case sure. The two movements are one attribute — the reckoning is swift whether it convicts or pays, and the prayer decides which.

**The Verse as the Diptych's Hinge**

Structurally, this shortest of the three verses carries the whole weight of the settlement. Verse 200 quoted the truncated prayer and its verdict — no share in the Hereafter; verse 201 quoted the whole prayer without verdict; verse 202 supplies the verdict for the whole — and, on the comprehensive reading, for the truncated one too. The diptych's moral anatomy is thereby completed in a single sentence: the asking reveals the earning, the earning fixes the portion, and the reckoning delivers it swiftly. The verse's brevity is proportioned to its function — it adds no imagery, no narrative, no elaboration, only the accounting — because the accounting is all the two prayers lacked. The single-world asker was told what he would not have; the both-worlds asker is told what he will: a portion, earned, paid by a swift reckoning. And the portion-language quietly answers the hajj's own economics: the pilgrim has spent wealth on the journey (2:195's spending, 2:197's provisions), earned hardship on the road, and invested weeks in rites whose yield is invisible; the verse credits all of it to the account — the kasb includes the journey — and settles it with the speed that makes the invisible yield as certain as the visible cost.

**Under the Swiftness, the Whole Passage**

The attribute's shadow falls across the legislation that follows as well. The numbered days at Minā are about to be legislated with their easy options — depart after two days or stay to the third, no sin on either for the mindful (2:203) — and beneath the ease stands the same swiftness: every choice in the rite, like every clause of every prayer, is registered instantly and settled exactly. The passage that began with the sacred month's ledger (2:194) and the spending in God's cause (2:195) has been building one continuous accounting — the retaliation measured, the offerings priced, the prayers portioned — and swift in reckoning is the seal on all of it: the ledger is kept in real time, by a knowledge that never pauses to compile. For the pilgrim the seal is mercy; for the Ḥums-type heart that prays with one horizon it is warning; and for the reader of the sūrah it is the standing condition of every verse's legislation — that the laws are not ideals posted against time but accounts entered the moment they are kept or broken. The portion from what they earned is, in the end, the hajj's final rite: the prayer completed, the earning closed, and the reckoning already swift upon it.
