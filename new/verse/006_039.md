## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:39

> Those who deny Our signs are ˹wilfully˺ deaf and dumb—lost in darkness. Allah leaves whoever He wills to stray and guides whoever He wills to the Straight Way.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Conditions in One Sentence**

The verse describes the deniers with three images that cover the whole of a person: hearing, speech, and sight. They are deaf, so the message does not enter; dumb, so no truthful word comes out; and in darkness, so nothing around them can be seen. The Book uses the same combination elsewhere, where those who are wilfully deaf, dumb, and blind will never return to the right path (2:18), and where the example of the disbelievers is a flock that comprehends nothing of the shepherd's calls and cries, deaf, dumb, and blind with no understanding (2:171).

The three conditions are linked rather than listed. A person who hears nothing speaks about what he does not know and walks where he cannot see, so the images describe one condition from three angles, and the Book uses them together because none of them explains the others away.

**Darkness in the Plural**

The sūrah opened by saying that God made darkness and light (6:1), and the Book consistently renders darkness in the plural while light stays singular. The disbelievers are led out of light into darkness, and their false gods are the ones doing the leading (2:257); the Book was revealed to bring people out of darkness into light (14:1); God showers His blessings so that He may bring the believers out of darkness and into light (33:43).

The plural fits the sense of the verse. A person in the dark does not merely lack a direction; he has as many directions as there are paths that go nowhere, and each of them looks plausible at the moment of choosing it. What the verse says about the deniers is not that they lost the map but that they are standing in a place where maps cannot be read.

**The Wilful Element in the Translation**

The rendering supplies the word wilfully, and it is not an embellishment. The Book elsewhere explains the condition as the result of what the person did: those whose hearts are sealed with their hearing, and whose sight is covered, are the ones who suffer a tremendous punishment (2:7); when they are reminded of the revelations and turn away, forgetting what their own hands did, veils are cast over the hearing and the hearts (18:57); and those who take their own desires as a god are left to stray knowingly, with their hearing and hearts sealed and a cover placed on their sight (45:23).

The same sequence appears in the story of the punishment that did not produce humility: instead of humbling themselves when suffering came, their hearts hardened and Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them (6:43). The covering in these passages is described as following on a decision, which is why the translation can fairly say that the deafness is chosen.

**The Deeper Question the Verse Raises**

Since God is said to leave some to stray, the sentence opens a question the scholars of the Book took seriously. The Muʿtazilites argued that God's leading astray cannot be a direct act of misguidance, since that would make the punishment of the misguided unjust, and they read it as the withholding of a grace that would have made the person receptive. The Ashʿarites held that God creates all acts, including human choices, and that guidance and misguidance are determined by His will.

The verses that settle the question most cleanly are the ones that connect the straying to what the people were already doing: the ones who break the covenant and spread corruption are left to stray (2:26); the wrongdoers are left to stray after they took the wrong course (14:27); and Joseph's people were left to stray after they continued doubting what he had brought (40:34). The Book attaches the leaving to a prior condition in every case, and it never describes a person as misled before he chose.

**Guidance as a Straight Way**

The second half of the verse names the alternative as the Straight Way, the phrase that opens the prayer of the Fātiḥah and that the sūrah uses for the path the Prophet was guided to (6:161). The image of the road makes the sentence complete: the deniers are lost in darkness without a path, and the guided are placed on a way with a direction.

Commentators who emphasise human responsibility read the pair as a description of consequences rather than of decrees issued at random. On their reading, God guides the person who turns to Him and leaves the person who turns away, which is exactly what the Book says when it describes the leaving as following on wrongdoing. The sūrah's own audience is being told which side of the sentence they are standing on.

**What the Verse Tells the Listener to Check**

The sentence is not addressed to a hypothetical group from the distant past. It describes a condition that the preceding verses have located precisely: people who asked for signs while signs were present, who called the recitation old stories, who turned others away from the Prophet and kept their distance. Anyone hearing the verse in Makkah could measure the description against a specific list of behaviours he had witnessed or participated in.

That is the practical function of the image. A man can argue about whether he is convinced, and he cannot argue with deafness, muteness, and darkness once he recognises the symptoms in himself. The verse names the symptoms and leaves the diagnosis to the patient, and the sūrah moves next to a question put to the same people about a moment when their sight would be perfectly clear.
