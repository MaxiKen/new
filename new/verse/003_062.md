## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:62

> Certainly, this is the true narrative, and there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah. And indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ is the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict on the Account**

The verse names what the reader has just received: this is the true narrative. The word translated true describes a report that conforms to what happened, and the phrase is applied to the whole passage about Jesus rather than to a single sentence of it.

Placing the verdict here answers the challenge in the previous verse. What the disputants were invited to test by invocation is declared by revelation to be settled already, and the sūrah states the conclusion before it states the theological claim that follows from it. The narrative about Jesus is not one version among several that a community may hold; the Book's account is presented as the report of the matter, and the reader is told to treat it as such.

**The Creed That Follows**

The declaration that comes next is a statement of God's unity, and its function in this position is to complete the argument about Jesus. The question at issue was whether a created being could be divine; the answer given is the sentence on which the whole sūrah has been built, repeated here in the frame of a verdict.

The Book joins the two themes throughout: wherever it discusses what people say about a prophet, angel, or saint, it returns to the statement that there is no deity but God. The joining is not a change of subject. It is the sūrah's method for correcting an overestimate of a creature by restating the position of the creator, and the reader is meant to hear the creed as the conclusion of the discussion rather than as a general maxim inserted into it.

**An Unusual Form of the Testimony**

The Arabic of the sentence is not the usual formula of the Islamic testimony of faith, which uses the negation followed by the exception in the familiar form. What appears here is a construction that the commentators noticed and discussed, sometimes describing it as an intensification: there is no deity at all except God, with the negation strengthened by the added particle.

The variation in wording is one of those details that a reader might pass over and that the commentators spent pages on. The gist of their discussion is that the verse's phrasing removes the possibility of any exception being smuggled in, and it does so in the immediate aftermath of a discussion about Jesus in which the exception was precisely what some parties wanted. The Book's choice of the stronger form is thus fitted to the argument it is conducting.

**The Two Names at the End**

The verse closes with the descriptions Almighty and All-Wise, the pair the sūrah used at its opening after the declaration of God's oneness and which recurs throughout.

The choice answers the two ways the argument about Jesus could go wrong. Almighty addresses the question of power: the miraculous birth is evidence of a capability no creature possesses, and the name keeps that capability with God. All-Wise addresses the question of purpose: the manner of the birth was arranged, not accidental, and it served the ends that revelation describes. A reader who accepts both names has accepted the frame in which the sūrah wants the whole discussion to be understood.

**The Creed as the Conclusion of a Story**

Read as a unit, the passage from verse 33 to this point has narrated a household, a birth, a mission, a conspiracy, and a removal, and it now closes with a statement about God that contains no narrative at all.

That is a deliberate ending. The story of Jesus, as the Book tells it, does not terminate in a claim about Jesus; it terminates in a claim about the one who sent him. The sūrah has moved from a woman's vow to the limits of the created order and back to the sentences with which it began, and the reader who has followed it has been given the meaning of the whole sequence in the same words the sūrah used before the story started.

**Where the Argument Leaves the Reader**

The verse ends the dispute about Jesus with a declaration rather than a rebuttal, and it does not address the disputants again. What follows turns to the community of the Book and to Abraham, and it begins a new phase of the sūrah's engagement with the claims of the scriptured peoples.

The two names with which the verse closes also tell the reader how to weigh the whole narrative he has just read. Almightiness explains why a birth without a father is possible; wisdom explains why such a birth was arranged for this prophet and not another. Both names are stated at the point where the account is declared true, as though the sūrah wanted the last thing said about the story to be a description of the one who decided it would happen. The accounts of the missionaries and merchants who carried these verses beyond Arabia belong to the history of the community; what the verse itself does is to hand the reader a fact about God and leave him to it.

Readers of the intervening centuries have found in these verses the summary of the Book's position on the nature of Christ, and the summary is complete on its own terms. The narrative is true, the deity is one, and the power and wisdom that arranged the birth belong to the one who arranged it. What the sūrah does next is to test the claims of the communities that disputed on the basis of an ancestor they shared with the Muslim community.
