## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:22

> They are the ones whose deeds are wasted in this world and the Hereafter. And they will have no helpers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Deeds That Come to Nothing**

The Arabic verb is *ḥabiṭa*, used of something that loses its value and effect, and the Book applies it to the works of people whose religious effort was considerable. The word appears in several places with the same meaning: associating others with God voids a person's deeds (39:65), and so does despising what God has revealed (47:9).

The verse therefore concerns a category of person who would be surprised to hear the sentence. The previous verse described people who denied the signs, killed the messengers, and killed the advocates of justice — not people who did nothing. What is wasted is not idleness but activity: prayer, charity, pilgrimage, recitation of the scriptures they inherited, all of it undone. The Book's warning here is not about effort; it is about the ground in which effort is planted.

**"In This World and the Hereafter"**

The loss is stated twice, and the two halves describe different kinds of failure. In this world, the work loses its fruit: the Book compares the deeds of those who disbelieve in their Lord to *“ashes fiercely blown away by wind on a stormy day. They will gain nothing from what they have earned.”* (14:18). The same image returns at the Day of Judgment, when God turns to whatever good they did and reduces it to scattered dust (25:23).

In the Hereafter the verdict is simpler: what weight there was has gone. *“It is they who reject the signs of their Lord and their meeting with Him, rendering their deeds void, so We will not give their deeds any weight on Judgment Day.”* (18:105). A scale with nothing on it settles the question, and the verse's double statement makes sure the reader understands that both currencies — the reputation of the world and the reward of the next — are lost by the same act.

**Why the Intention Decides the Deed**

The commentators explained the waste by pointing at intention. A good act performed by someone who has withheld the recognition owed to God is not a partial good that God overlooks; it is an act whose author has refused the relationship that would give it meaning. The Book says this in its own voice about a different group: *“That is because they detest what Allah has revealed, so He has rendered their deeds void.”* (47:9).

The principle is uncomfortable because it applies to worship. The Book never treats religious performance as a deposit that earns automatic return; it treats it as a gift offered to someone, and a gift offered to the wrong party or with contempt for the recipient is not a gift. The men of Madinah and Makkah who recited scripture and prayed were not being told their practice was too small. They were being told that what they had built was standing on the wrong foundation.

**"No Helpers" — the Removal of the Social Net**

The verse ends with a phrase that carries a great deal of the ancient world's reality. In that culture a person's security was his clan: helpers who would avenge him, ransom him, or plead for him. The Book repeatedly uses the impossibility of helpers in the Hereafter as the closing note of a warning, and it names each of the supports a person might hope for.

*“Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given.”* (2:48). The wrongdoers will have *“neither a close friend nor intercessor to be heard”* (40:18), and where they had counted on the mediation of the righteous, the answer is flat: *“So the pleas of intercessors will be of no benefit to them.”* (74:48). The verse above states the same in two words. No alliance survives into a court where the judge needs no allies.

**The Individual Standing Alone**

Taken with the previous verse, this verse produces an image that the sūrah returns to from different angles. A person arrives with an entourage of supporters, or he arrives alone. The text describes the second possibility without softening it, and the fact that the Book has to state it at all tells the reader how strong the illusion of collective protection can be.

The verses that follow will show people trying to settle their affairs in this world by turning away from an authority they dislike. The sentence here is the reminder that the settling they avoid in this life will be conducted without a single advocate once the accounts are opened.

**Why the Warning Is Worth Reading Carefully**

A reader may be tempted to assign this verse to a distant group and move on. The Book makes that reading difficult by the way it phrases the charge, and the sūrah itself is careful about quantity: the previous verse described people who deny, kill, and kill again, and the sentence here follows that description.

What the reader is given is a principle rather than a demographic. Effort invested against God is not a lesser investment; it is a loss, and the loss is reported in advance so that it can be avoided while a person still has time to change the ground of his work.

The order of the two verses is also a lesson in how the Qur'an argues. It states the crime first — denial, killing, killing — and the consequence second, so that the penalty is seen as an answer to something rather than as an arbitrary decree. Readers who find the Book's warnings severe are being invited to trace the severity back to its cause. In this case the cause is a pattern of conduct that persisted generation after generation in the presence of messengers, and the sentence is proportionate to it.
