## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:52

> ˹O Prophet!˺ Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. You are not accountable for them whatsoever, nor are they accountable for you. So do not dismiss them, or you will be one of the wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Instruction About Seating Arrangements**

The verse addresses a practical pressure rather than a doctrine. A group of prominent Makkans had indicated that they were willing to sit with the Prophet, and they did not want to be seen in the company of his poorer followers, some of them freed slaves. The reports describe two versions of the request: that the Prophet should send those followers away entirely, or should move them to the back rows so that the notables would not be visible standing behind people of lower social standing.

The wording of the verse fits either version. Do not dismiss them is an instruction about who may be kept at a distance from the message, and it settled a question the society had been making on the basis of birth and wealth. The Prophet is told that the arrangement of his gathering is not a matter of social convenience.

**What the Poor Believers Were Doing**

The verse describes the people in question by their practice: they invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. The Book uses the same description in the sūrah of the Cave, where the Prophet is told to keep himself patient with those who call on their Lord morning and evening and not to let his eyes go past them toward the trappings of worldly life (18:28). Commentators note that the phrase covers the five daily prayers, the recitation of the Qur'an, and voluntary remembrance, or all of them together.

The description does the arguing. The people being asked to leave the room were the ones occupied with the worship that the message had come to establish, and the people requesting their removal had offered no such practice. The Book's reversal of the social order is complete in a single clause.

**"Seeking His Pleasure"**

The phrase translated as seeking His pleasure refers to a motive rather than an outward act, and the Book uses it repeatedly to describe sincerity. Those who give their wealth are described as doing so seeking God's face (2:272; 30:38–39), and the same phrase appears where the Prophet is told to stay with the poor rather than to court the powerful. Commentators read it as sincerity of intention, and some note that the literal sense concerns the hope of seeing God, which is what an honest worshipper's desire ultimately reaches toward.

The criterion that emerges is worth stating plainly. What qualified these people to be near the Prophet was not their lineage, their wealth, or their standing in the market. It was the direction of their intention, which is precisely the thing the notables could not see and were trying to assess anyway.

**The Line About Accountability**

The verse then removes a practical worry from the Prophet's shoulders: you are not accountable for them, nor are they accountable for you. The Book states the general principle elsewhere, that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another (6:164; 17:15), and it applies it here to a specific social anxiety. If the poor believers turned out to have mixed motives, that was God's assessment to make, and if their poverty offended the Makkans, their own accounts would be examined without reference to it.

Commentators read the sentence in two ways, with some taking the reckoning to concern the poor believers' inner sincerity, which the Prophet is not required to audit, and others taking it to concern the notables, whose acceptance of the message the Prophet should not pursue at the cost of the community he already had. Both readings converge on the same instruction: the door of the gathering is not his to narrow.

**The Warning Attached to the Instruction**

The verse closes with a warning that is remarkable for being addressed to the Prophet himself: if he sends them away, he will be one of the wrongdoers. The Book issues such warnings to its messenger in several places, and the sūrah of ʿAbasa records the incident in which he showed displeasure at being interrupted by a blind man while he was engaged with the notables.

The warning is not evidence of a fault in the Prophet's character. It is evidence of the seriousness of the principle, and the Book states it in the second person so that no reader can take the protection of the weak to be optional. A community that arranges itself around the status of its wealthiest members has made a decision about what it values, and the verse names that decision as a species of wrongdoing.

**What the Passage Does for the Rest of the Sūrah**

The instruction is placed in the middle of a long argument about idolatry, and its placement is not incidental. The sūrah has been exposing the fabricated hierarchies by which the Makkans divided their world, including the shares they assigned to their gods and to their own clans. The verse applies the same critique to the community the Prophet was building, where a distinction between first-class and second-class believers had been proposed to him.

The next verses continue the subject directly, describing how God tests people by means of one another, and stating what the wealthy questioners would say about the poor. The passage holds together as a single treatment of a single problem: who decides the worth of a person, and on what evidence.

The same scene is recorded from the other side in the story of Noah. His people argued that they could not believe in a man followed only by the lowest among them (26:111), and Noah answered that their judgment rested with his Lord and that he was not going to expel the believers (26:113–114). The disbelieving chiefs made the same complaint to him that the Makkan notables were making in this sūrah, and Noah asked his people who would protect him from God if he were to dismiss them (11:27; 11:30). The repetition of this argument across the sūrahs shows that the pressure being applied to the Prophet was not a local peculiarity; it was the standard demand made against a messenger by the powerful of every community.
