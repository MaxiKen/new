## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:129

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ownership Stated Before the Decision**

The verse begins by stating that everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to God, and then states that He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills, closing with the two names of forgiveness and mercy. The order places the right to decide inside the fact of ownership.

The same opening formula appeared earlier in this sūrah, in the passage that ended the discussion of two outcomes, and its return here is deliberate. A reader who has followed the argument has met the sentence before and knows what it does: it removes the case from the hands of every party and places it with the owner of everything. The repetition frames the material between the two occurrences, so that the promises of aid, the battle, and the verdict on its participants are all enclosed within statements about who owns what exists and who decides what happens to it.

**Why the Right to Decide Belongs to the Owner**

A sentence about forgiveness and punishment would raise the question of authority if it stood alone. The verse answers the question before it arises by naming God as the owner of all that exists.

The reasoning runs through the Book's arguments about judgment. If a person owns nothing, then no person has a claim against another that could bind the one who owns both. The Book uses the point in its disputes with communities that claimed special standing, and it uses it here to settle a matter about which the messenger himself had been told that he had no say.

**The Two Verbs in the Sentence**

The verse names forgiveness and punishment as the two things God does as He wills, and the phrase about His will precedes the mention of either. The formulation has occupied the commentators because it appears to place the decision beyond conditions.

The Book's other statements about forgiveness supply the conditions in other places. It states that God does not forgive the association of others with Him while forgiving anything less for whoever He wills, and it commands those who have sinned not to despair of His mercy. The sentences are complementary: one establishes that the decision belongs to God alone, and the others describe how it is made. The verse before the reader states the principle in its most general form, which is why it can be read alongside passages that describe the outcome for particular kinds of people without contradiction.

**The Name That Closes the Verse**

The sentence ends with the two names that describe God as forgiving and merciful, and their presence after the mention of punishment is the verse's quiet summary of its own balance.

The Book habitually closes its statements about judgment with these names, and the effect is not to soften the warning but to state the disposition from which the whole arrangement proceeds. A reader who has been told that the case is not in his hands still needs to know toward what the one who holds it is inclined, and the two names supply the answer.

**Not Despairing and Not Presuming**

The verse occupies the middle ground between two errors, and the Book addresses both elsewhere. One passage addresses people who have wronged themselves and forbids them to despair of God's mercy, telling them that He forgives all sins (39:53). Other passages warn those who presume on forgiveness while persisting in their conduct.

The verse before the reader states the fact on which both warnings rest: the decision is God's. A person who understands the sentence will not conclude that his outcome is fixed by anything in his own control, and he will not conclude that it is arbitrary. What he is given is the knowledge that the matter rests with a forgiver who is also a punisher, and that both descriptions are true.


The passage that precedes this verse dealt with a battle and with the fate of its opposing parties. This sentence takes that material out of the hands of the community and places it with God.

That is the necessary conclusion to the passage. A community that had suffered losses, and that had been told the outcome of its enemies would disappoint them, needed to be reminded that the final settlement of the matter belonged elsewhere. The verse states the reminder in terms of ownership and disposition, and it leaves the reader with the two names at the end.

**What the Sentence Means for an Individual Reader**

For a person considering his own case, the verse states where the account will be settled and who holds it. The Book's teaching about repentance and forgiveness is not contradicted by the sentence; it is grounded in it.

The practical result of reading the verse is the removal of two things: the burden of judging others, which is not a person's to carry, and the illusion of controlling one's own outcome by anything other than the conduct the Book has described. What remains is the conduct, and the verse that follows turns to a specific item of it.


The verses that follow address the believers about their dealings with one another and about the use of their wealth, and the transition is one the sūrah makes at several points. It moves from a statement about God to an instruction about conduct.

The arrangement keeps the instructions from becoming self-contained rules. A community that has been told who owns everything and who decides the outcome of every case is placed in a position to receive commands about money, patience, and forgiveness as something other than arbitrary requirements, and the passage that follows is read in that light.
