## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:3

> who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faith Begins Where Sight Ends**

The first quality of the God-conscious is belief in *al-ghayb* — the unseen, literally "the absent": realities beyond the reach of the ordinary senses. Allah, the angels, Paradise, Hell, and the Day of Judgment all belong to it. The definition matches the one the Prophet ﷺ gave when Jibrīl came in human form and asked what faith is: to believe in Allah, His angels, the meeting with Him, His messengers, and the Resurrection (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 50). Every item on that list is unseen in the verse's sense, and the ordering of al-Baqarah agrees with the ḥadīth — belief in the unseen heads the list of acts, because nothing else on the list can be done sincerely without it. The verse is not demanding credulity. Belief in the unseen is belief on a warrant: the report of the One whose truthfulness has been established. It transfers trust from the eye to the Witness, and that transfer is the first act of every religious life.

**Two Kinds of Unseen**

Commentators split the category along a useful line. Some realities are invisible in principle — Allah, whose nature no sense could grasp even if the senses were willing. Others are visible in principle but withheld in fact — the Hour, whose timing belongs to Allah alone; when Jibrīl asked about it, the Prophet ﷺ answered that the one asked knows no more than the one asking, and recited: *"Verily, with Allah (Alone) is the knowledge of the Hour"* (31:34, cited in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 50). The distinction protects the doctrine from two errors. Against the rationalist who denies what cannot be seen, the first class says: the most real thing in existence is precisely the thing no eye contains. Against the enthusiast who claims private access to hidden things, the second class says: some of the unseen stays sealed until Allah opens it, and speculation about it is not faith. Believing the unseen therefore includes believing its edges — accepting both what revelation discloses and what it withholds.

**Iqāmah: Prayer Erected, Not Merely Performed**

"Establish" translates *yuqīmūn*, from a root meaning to set upright, to make stand — the same verb used of raising a building or straightening a leaning thing. Prayer that is *established* differs from prayer that is merely performed: it has structure, continuity, and support, and it holds other things upright with it. The word *ṣalāh* itself carries a range the technical usage narrows. In the Qur'an it names the canonical prayer of recitation and bowing, but it also names invocation — Allah and His angels make *ṣalāh* upon the Prophet, and believers are commanded to join them: *"Indeed, Allah showers His blessings upon the Prophet, and His angels pray for him. O believers! Invoke Allah's blessings upon him"* (33:56). The prophets before Muḥammad ﷺ were charged with the same institution — ʿĪsā recounts that Allah enjoined prayer upon him (19:31), and Mūsā receives the command at Ṭuway (20:14). To establish prayer is to join a structure older than the qiblah.

**Spending From, Not Spending All**

The third quality is *infāq* — expending, giving out — and two small words do the theological work. "From" renders a partitive: what is donated is a portion, not the whole, and the portion is defined by need and capacity rather than by exhaustion. "What We have provided for them" fixes prior ownership: the wealth in a believer's hand arrived as provision (*rizq*) from Allah, so spending gives away what was never absolutely his. The pairing with prayer is deliberate and runs through the whole Qur'an — ṣalāh and zakāh are joined so often that classical exegesis treats them as a single institution with two limbs, one vertical and one horizontal. The Prophet's ﷺ definition of Islam to Jibrīl bundles the same acts: worship Allah alone, establish the prayers, render the obligatory charity, fast Ramaḍān (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 50). Donation here covers the obligatory due and the voluntary gift alike; the verse praises the disposition before the ledger.

**A Ladder From the Inner to the Outer**

The three qualities descend a graded staircase, and the commentators map it precisely. Belief in the unseen is an inward act with an invisible object — nothing of it appears on the body. Prayer stands in the middle: its motions are visible, its recitation audible, yet its object remains unseen. Donation completes the descent — an outward act with a visible object, wealth passing from hand to hand. Read in order, the verse constitutes the whole human being: the heart, the body in time, and the possessions in the world, each given its assignment and each anchored to the one before. The gradation also guards against two amputations. Faith that never becomes prayer is sentiment; prayer that never becomes spending is ritual. By the verse's architecture, the inner conviction is incomplete until it has walked all the way down into the street.

**Character Named Before Any Law Is Given**

No command has been issued yet in this passage. The sūrah — the Qur'an's longest and most legislative — opens its human portrait with three qualities of the heart and hand, described rather than demanded. The God-conscious are identified by what they already do before they are addressed by statute, and the identification sets the terms for everything that follows: when the rulings arrive, they arrive to a people already shaped by unseen-faith, standing prayer, and open-handedness. There is a lesson in the order for every reader of the long sūrah. Law lands differently on character than on raw will — the same commandment produces evasion in one person and worship in another, and the difference was made before the commandment was heard. Al-Baqarah begins by showing the kind of person its two hundred and eighty-six verses are written for.
