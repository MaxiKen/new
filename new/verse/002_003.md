## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:3

> who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faith Directed at What Cannot Be Checked**

*Ghayb* means the absent, what is out of sight. In the Qur'an it covers the realities no instrument reaches: God, the angels, the revealed Books, the messengers, the Last Day, and the decree that governs events. A well-known report of the Prophet's teaching, the ḥadīth of Jibrīl recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 50, defines faith itself as belief in those six articles, and lists them as a single act of assent rather than six separate commitments.

The order in this verse is therefore not arbitrary. The first mark of the mindful is belief in what cannot be seen, because what can be seen does not require faith. The community described in this sūrah has not watched the hereafter or inspected God; it has been told, and it has accepted the report. The rest of the verse turns on that acceptance: what a person believes about the invisible decides what he does with the visible.

**Prayer as Something Kept Standing**

The verb here is not "they pray" but *yuqīmūna al-ṣalāh*, they keep the prayer standing. Its root means to rise, to set upright, to maintain. The form of the verb describes a practice held in place rather than an act completed, and the choice is precise for a ritual performed daily under conditions that encourage neglect.

The Qur'an's own instruction on the subject ties the practice to remembrance: *“So worship Me ˹alone˺, and establish prayer for My remembrance”* (20:14). Prayer is the one mark in this verse whose object is the same unseen God the believer has just affirmed, and it converts an invisible conviction into a routine that can be seen. Nothing in the verse suggests that belief and prayer are alternatives offered to different temperaments. The Book presents them as two stages of one movement, and the third stage will leave the interior of a person entirely.

**"From What We Have Provided": Wealth as a Trust**

The verse does not say that the believers give charity from their own property. It says they give from what *We* have provided, with God as the speaker and the provider, and that pronoun changes the nature of ownership. What a person holds is received, and what is received can be spent as the giver intended. The Qur'an makes the same point in the imperative mood: *“Believe in Allah and His Messenger, and donate from what He has entrusted you with”* (57:7).

The amount is left unspecified here, which is why the verse speaks about giving generally before the sūrah lays down the obligation of alms-tax in detail. The Qur'an treats this giving as a return rather than a loss: *“If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never be able to number them”* (16:18), and a gift received among countless gifts cannot be treated as untouched private property. The believer gives from what is not, strictly speaking, his.

**Why Belief, Prayer and Giving Appear in This Order**

The three marks move from what nobody can see to what only God sees to what everybody sees. Belief has no witness except God. Prayer has a witness when it is performed in company and no witness when it is performed alone. Giving has a witness whenever it happens, because something changes hands. The sequence is a description of a whole life rather than a checklist of duties, and it escalates from the most private to the most public.

The pairing of prayer and alms-tax runs through the Qur'an roughly thirty times, which makes it one of the Book's most insistent combinations. One purifies the body and the day, the other purifies wealth and the relation to those who need it. Reading them together, the sūrah is describing a community whose worship costs it both time and money, and it names generosity in the same breath as belief.

**The Community That Had Not Yet Been Named**

These verses in Madinah describe a community that the rest of the sūrah will address in the second person. The Book establishes what such people are like before it issues instructions to them, and the description is deliberately ordinary: assent to what is unseen, a maintained prayer, and spending. No miracles, no prestige, no claim on lineage; the identity of the believers is drawn entirely from practice.

That is why the same three traits recur throughout the Book as a shorthand. When the Qur'an wants to describe the believers it reaches for the same words: *“those who humble themselves in prayer”* (23:2), and the giving that follows in the same list. Al-Baqarah opens by defining the human being whose company it will seek for the remaining two hundred and eighty verses, and it does so before presenting either the rejecters or the hypocrites.

**Belief Without a Deadline**

The verse asks for belief in what cannot be verified, and the Book acknowledges what that costs. The Qur'an's answer to the difficulty is not to remove it but to make the request explicit and then reward it: *“This is the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve”* (18:29). Faith in the unseen is a decision made without a receipt, and the sūrah's next verses will describe those who refuse that decision.

What the Book never does is pretend the unseen has been shown. The entire arrangement of the sūrah depends on the distinction: those who accept what they cannot inspect are called mindful, and the passage that follows turns to people who will not accept anything they cannot inspect. The three marks listed here all follow from the first one, and the first one is a choice made in the dark.
