## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:3

> who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Believing What Nobody Has Seen**

The sūrah has called itself a guide for those who are mindful of God, and now it says what such people do. The first thing it names is not an act but a conviction: they believe in *al-ghayb*, the unseen — literally, what is absent. The category has two kinds of occupant. Some things are invisible in principle because no sense can reach them: God, the angels, the Garden and the Fire. Others are part of the unseen because their knowledge belongs to God alone, such as the time of the Hour: *Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ has the knowledge of the Hour* (31:34). The first kind cannot be observed; the second cannot be calculated.

Belief of that kind is not credulity in the Quran's account of it, because the unseen is not the unknown. It is what has been reported by a source the Quran treats as sufficient: *With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him* (6:59). The tradition later itemised what the category contains. When Jibrīl asked the Prophet to define faith, the answer was a list of unseen objects: *It is to believe in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and to believe in divine decree, its good and its bad* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 8). This sūrah gives the same inventory near its close: *They ˹all˺ believe in Allah, His angels, His Books, and His messengers* (2:285).

**Why the Unseen Stands Before the Deeds**

The order deserves attention, because the verse could have begun with what believers do. It begins instead with what they accept. The reason is not difficult: an action counts as worship only if the one it is done for and the one who commanded it are known. Prayer and charity without that knowledge are exercise and philanthropy.

This is also why the demand for a visible sign is refused as a general rule. When the opponents asked, *“Why has no ˹other˺ sign been sent down to him from his Lord?”* (6:37), the Quran answers that God has power to send one, and then declines to make revelation depend on it. A faith that required a miracle on request would not be faith in the unseen; it would be sight, and the category would collapse. Placing belief first therefore protects the whole structure: the acts that follow are responses to something already accepted, not experiments conducted to see whether it is there.

**"Establish," Not "Perform"**

The verb used for prayer is *yuqīmūn*, from a root meaning to set something upright and keep it standing. To establish prayer is to put it up properly and to keep it up — on time, in its form, and continuously. The English "perform" misses both halves: a person can perform the motions of something he has not established, and the Quran's complaint against the negligent is never that they prayed the wrong words but that they let the thing fall down.

Two further features of the word *ṣalāh* matter here. The same word is used for invoking blessing, not only for the prescribed prayer, so that the believers' attention to the Prophet and God's attention to him are described in the same vocabulary. And prayer is given its purpose rather than only its shape: *establish prayer for My remembrance* (20:14); *˹genuine˺ prayer should deter ˹one˺ from indecency and wickedness* (29:45). A prayer that leaves a person unchanged has not been established, whatever its movements looked like.

**Donating From What "We Provided"**

The third item is spending, and the wording carries the theology inside it. Believers donate *from what We have provided for them* (2:3) — not from what they own, and not from what they earned. The giver is handing back a portion of what was handed to him, and the ownership claim is stated in the same breath as the obligation to part with some of it.

The word used, *infāq*, means expenditure generally, and the Quran applies it to every kind of giving in God's way rather than to a fixed due alone. But the pairing of prayer with giving is one of the most constant features of the text: twenty-seven verses join the two, phrased almost identically wherever they appear — *those who establish prayer and donate from what We have provided for them* (8:3); *establish prayer, donate from what We have provided for them—secretly and openly* (13:22); *establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them—secretly and openly* (35:29). The recurrence is the argument. Worship in the Quran is never complete with the vertical act; the horizontal one is named alongside it so often that the two read as a single requirement.

**A Ladder From the Invisible to the Visible**

Several commentators read the three clauses as a graded movement, and the text supports them. Faith in the unseen is an inward act whose object is invisible. Prayer is an outward act whose object is invisible. Giving is an outward act whose object is visible — wealth, food, coin, a person fed. The sequence therefore runs from the hidden to the manifest and covers the whole of a life: what a person holds, what a person does before God, and what a person does before other people.

The reading is elegant enough to be treated with caution, and the caution is worth stating. The Quran does not say that this is why the clauses are ordered, and a list of three virtues could be arranged for other reasons. What the text does establish independently is the principle the ladder illustrates: there is no interior religion without an exterior one, and no exterior one worth anything without the interior. The verses that follow continue the description of this same people.

**The Standing Description of a Believer**

This is not an ad hoc list. The Quran uses the same three marks whenever it wants to identify its people, and in one place it uses almost the identical sentence: *The ˹true˺ believers are only those whose hearts tremble at the remembrance of Allah, whose faith increases when His revelations are recited to them, and who put their trust in their Lord. ˹They are˺ those who establish prayer and donate from what We have provided for them* (8:2–3). The same pairing appears in the description of those who rise at night: *They abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear, and donate from what We have provided for them* (32:16).

What the repetition establishes is that these are not heroic attainments but the ordinary marks of the community the Quran is addressing. Belief in what cannot be seen, prayer kept standing, and wealth that does not stop at its owner: the sūrah opens its account of the reverent with the baseline, not the summit.
