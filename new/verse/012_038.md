## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:38

> I follow the faith of my fathers: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. It is not ˹right˺ for us to associate anything with Allah ˹in worship˺. This is part of Allah’s grace upon us and humanity, but most people are not grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Genealogy Offered to Two Egyptian Servants**

Joseph names his line: Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. His hearers have no reason to know these names and no means of verifying the claim. In Egypt he is a purchased slave from an unspecified country, and in prison he is a man convicted by rumour and released from suspicion without being cleared. The genealogy is therefore not an appeal to authority his audience would recognise. It is a statement of where he comes from, made to the only two people available to hear it. The sūrah's first listeners, however, would have recognised all three names immediately, and the effect on them is different: the man in the cell is the third generation of the family the Qur'an has been describing since verse 4, and the creed he is about to state is the creed of that family rather than a personal opinion.

**"The Creed of My Fathers" Claims Continuity, Not Innovation**

The word *millah* denotes a religious way held in common across a community and transmitted through it. Joseph does not present his belief as something he arrived at, discovered or was granted uniquely. He presents it as inherited, and he names the chain of inheritance. That has an argumentative function in a prison and a larger one in the sūrah. In a prison it answers the obvious objection to a slave preaching to royal servants: he is not introducing a novelty but reporting a tradition older than the Egyptian state. In the sūrah it links the Egyptian section to the Canaanite one, so that the man who refused the ʿAzīz's wife and the boy who dreamed of eleven stars are shown to be operating from a single continuous conviction. The Qur'an names Joseph among the descendants given to Abraham at 6:84, which is the same lineage stated here from the other direction.

**"It Is Not for Us to Associate Anything With God"**

The negative formulation is precise. He does not say we worship one God; he says it is not for us to make anything a partner with God. The construction *mā kāna lanā* — it was not for us, it does not belong to us — expresses an impossibility of standing rather than a rule of conduct. Association is excluded by who they are, not merely prohibited by what they were told. The plural *us* extends the statement beyond Joseph to the whole prophetic house named in the previous clause, which turns a personal profession into a description of a family. The same formula appears in the Qur'an's account of other prophets, always with the same effect: the speaker refuses to claim virtue and instead reports the conditions of his office.

**Grace Upon Us and Upon Humanity**

*That is from God's bounty upon us and upon mankind.* The commentators read the referent of *that* in two ways, and both are worth keeping. The first takes it as the affirmation of God's oneness itself: monotheism is the bounty. The second takes it as the fact just stated — that a family has been kept from associating partners with God — so the bounty is the preservation rather than the doctrine. Either way the sentence does something unusual: it places a blessing on the prophetic house and then immediately extends it to everyone. The two are not ranked. What was given to Abraham's descendants is described as a gift to humanity in the same clause, which is the sūrah's answer to any reader inclined to treat Joseph's story as the private fortune of one family.

**"But Most People Are Not Grateful" Closes the Sermon**

The refrain is one the Qur'an uses repeatedly and in nearly identical form: most of mankind are not grateful, most of mankind do not know. Within this sūrah the same observation closes verse 21 (*most people do not know*) and will close verse 40 in the same terms. The repetition is not filler. Each instance marks a point where a benefit has been conferred and the conferral has gone unrecognised — Joseph established in Egypt, the creed of the prophets delivered to a prison, the upright religion stated plainly. Gratitude here means recognising a favour as a favour, and its absence is what the chapter diagnoses in every party that mishandles Joseph: the brothers who could not see a father's love as anything but a distribution, and the household that could not see an innocent man as anything but an inconvenience.

**The First Public Exercise of His Office**

Verse 38 is the first time Joseph speaks as a prophet to people outside his family, and the setting is the least advantageous available. He has no pulpit, no congregation, no freedom and no future; his audience consists of two men awaiting execution or dismissal, one of whom will forget him entirely (12:42). Nothing he says in this sermon can improve his position, and the sūrah takes care to establish that. The call to God's oneness is delivered where delivery has no price and no reward, which is the standard the Qur'an applies to prophetic speech throughout: the messenger asks nothing for it. What Joseph gains from these four verses is nothing in Egypt. What the chapter gains is a record that his conviction did not depend on his circumstances.

There is a further resonance for the sūrah's first listeners. The same three names appear in the Qur'an's account of Jacob's deathbed, where his children answer their father's question about what they will worship after him: *“We will ˹continue to˺ worship your God, the God of your forefathers— Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac—the One God. And to Him we ˹all˺ submit”* (2:133). That answer is given by the whole family together, including the sons who threw Joseph into a well. Here the identical genealogy is recited by the son they threw, alone, in a foreign prison, to two strangers. The creed survives the family's failure and travels further than the family did. Joseph states in Egypt what his brothers stated in Canaan, and the statement is unchanged by everything that happened in between.
