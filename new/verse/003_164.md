## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:164

> Indeed, Allah has done the believers a ˹great˺ favour by raising a messenger from among them—reciting to them His revelations, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom. For indeed they had previously been clearly astray.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Favour Named at the Outset**

The verse states that God conferred a favour on the believers by sending them a messenger from among themselves, and the sentence places the coming of the Prophet at the head of everything the community had received. The word for favour covers a gift and an act of kindness, and the Book uses it for the blessings that carry the greatest weight.

The choice of the term matters after the passage that has just ended. The community had been shown its failures in detail, and the verse now directs its attention to the gift that made its position what it was. What follows in the sentence describes the work the messenger did among them.

**A Messenger From Among Themselves**

The phrase describing the messenger as one of the community's own was the point that the community's opponents found hardest to accept. The Book reports the objections of those who asked whether they should follow a human being like themselves, and the reasoning behind their refusal is recorded in several passages.

The verse presents the very fact they objected to as the favour. A messenger drawn from the community could speak its language, know its conditions, and share its life, and the Book elsewhere describes the Prophet as concerned for the suffering of the believers and merciful toward them. The gift consisted partly in the closeness of the messenger to the people he was sent to.

**Reciting the Signs**

The first of the messenger's works named in the verse is the recitation of God's signs to the community. The phrase describes the delivery of the revelation in the form in which it was received, and the community's first relationship with the message was through hearing it.

The Book's descriptions of the Prophet's mission consistently begin with recitation, and the sūrah of the Congregation uses the same sequence in describing the messenger raised among the unlettered people. The recitation comes first because everything else rests on it: the purification and the teaching described in the same sentence both depend on the community's hearing of what was sent down.

**Purification and Teaching**

The verse then names purification and instruction in the Book and in wisdom. The word for purification describes the cleansing of a person from what defiles him in conduct and in character, and the word for wisdom describes the understanding that accompanies knowledge.

The order of the two is instructive. The Book's arrival changes the people it addresses before it informs them, and the tradition of interpretation reads the sequence as a description of the moral and intellectual formation that the message produced in a community that had not possessed either. The sūrah's own account of the community's transformation presumes exactly this work.

**The Book and Wisdom Together**

The pairing of the Book and wisdom in the verse appears in several passages about the messenger's task. One describes the prayer of Abraham for a messenger who would recite the signs, teach the Book and wisdom, and purify the people. Another, in the sūrah of the Cow, describes the sending of the messenger in nearly the same words.

The recurrence of the wording links the mission of the Prophet with the supplication of the patriarch from whom the community traced its descent. The Book presents the coming of Muhammad as an answer to a prayer recorded in its own earlier passages, and the verse before the reader states the fulfilment of that request as a favour to the believers.


The verse closes by describing the community's condition before the messenger came: it had been in clear error. The Book uses the word for straying and for a state of confusion from which a person cannot find his way without help.

The description is not a reproach but a measure of the change. The sūrah had already referred to the community's history before the coming of the message, and the sentence gives the contrast its full extent. The community's standing, whatever its lapses, was the result of a transformation that it had not produced for itself, and the verse names the source.

**The Objections the Verse Answers**

The Book records the objections that the message was brought by a human being rather than by an angel or accompanied by treasure, and it records the answers given in several passages. Some of those answers point to the ordinary working of God's arrangements for human guidance.

The verse before the reader supplies another kind of answer by treating the human messenger as the content of the favour rather than an obstacle to it. The community is told that the form of the gift suited its need, and the sentence makes the cause of the opponents' objection into the reason for the believers' gratitude.

**What the Verse Asks of the Community**

The verse's opening words about favour set the tone for the community's response, which the sūrah develops in the verses that follow. Gratitude for the message and obedience to the messenger are the same thing in the Book's presentation, and the sentence establishes the ground on which the community's obligations rest.

The passage around this verse has dealt with the community's failures, its losses, and the conduct required of it. The sentence about the messenger's coming places the whole of the account within a benefit that the community had received, and the effect is to make the obligations that follow read as the natural response to a gift rather than as burdens imposed on a failing people. The favour is stated in the past tense, as something already given, and the community's task is to live in a way that answers it.
