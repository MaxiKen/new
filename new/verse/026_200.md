## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:200

> This is how We allow denial ˹to steep˺ into the hearts of the wicked.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Denial Becomes a Condition of the Heart**

The verse describes denial as something that can steep into hearts. The image suggests gradual absorption. Rejection begins as a response to one command or one messenger, then becomes a settled way of seeing. After enough refusals, the person no longer meets each proof freshly. He receives it through the habits created by earlier denial.

The wording follows the hypothetical about language and messenger. The deniers have shown that changing the outward form would not change their response. Their problem is therefore not only on the tongue. Refusal has entered the inner place from which perception, judgment, and intention are directed.

**The Pronoun Carries a Real Interpretive Question**

Exegetes have differed over what is being allowed to steep into the hearts. Some understand it as denial itself, meaning that Allah leaves the guilty to absorb disbelief through their repeated refusal. Others understand the reference to be the Quran, which enters their hearts as a proof that they then reject. A further contextual reading sees their reception of Arabic without belief as like hearing a language without accepting its meaning.

The translation's bracketed denial makes the first reading explicit, and the immediate context supports it: the people reject even a hypothetical arrangement designed to remove their complaint. The other readings still clarify the tragedy. The Quran reaches them as evidence, but their settled resistance turns reception into another occasion for refusal.

**Divine Allowance Is Not an Excuse for First Rejection**

The verse should not be read as saying that people are condemned without a prior response. Quran 6:110 describes hearts and eyes being turned away after people refused to believe at first. That parallel places hardening in a moral sequence. Repeated choices matter, and divine allowance of a condition does not make the original arrogance innocent.

This preserves both divine sovereignty and human responsibility. Allah knows how hearts become fixed and allows the consequences of refusal to unfold, while people remain accountable for the refusals by which they chose that path. No denier can use the verse to claim that resistance was forced upon him before he had heard, understood, and answered.

**The Wicked Description Names Conduct, Not Blood**

The hearts are described as belonging to the wicked because of what their owners do with truth. Wickedness here is not an inherited ethnicity or an unavoidable temperament. It is the moral posture formed by arrogance, fraud, mockery, and persistent rejection. A person who turns back can leave that condition through repentance and obedience.

The warning is therefore severe but not pointless. Hearts can be trained toward resistance, and they can also be trained toward humility. Every refusal makes the next refusal easier, just as every honest return makes further honesty more possible. The verse urges the hearer to stop the process early, before denial feels like identity and a clear message is heard only as another reason to say no.

Steeping also explains why repeated wrongdoing changes a community's language. At first, a person may know that a short measure is wrong and invent an excuse for one transaction. Later, the excuse becomes a custom, and finally the custom becomes a principle defended against correction. The heart has absorbed the refusal so deeply that evidence appears to threaten the self rather than to guide it.

The verse does not describe a sudden loss of all moral capacity. People who have hardened themselves can still hear a warning, but they receive it through resistance. Their intelligence may remain active while their judgment serves denial. They can debate words, demand new signs, and discover objections, yet avoid the simple act of admitting that Allah has a right to command them.

This is why repentance must begin with truthfulness. A person cannot ask for healing while calling his refusal wisdom. He must name the harm, admit the choice, and stop defending the habit that made the heart hard. The Quran's severe description is itself a mercy because it reveals the danger before the condition becomes invisible to its owner.

The moral sequence also protects against fatalism. Allah allows the consequence of refusal and knows the heart completely, but the verse addresses people as responsible actors. The warning would be meaningless if they had no choice before the denial entered them. Divine knowledge does not turn wrongdoing into innocence, and divine allowance does not make repentance pointless.

A community should therefore watch the small refusals that precede a settled denial. Mocking a correction, protecting a profitable fraud, and demanding impossible proof may seem separate acts. Together they train the heart to resist truth. The cure is a quick return to humility: accept clear guidance, repair the harm already done, and ask Allah to keep the heart receptive rather than steeped in rejection.

The image of steeping also gives a warning about delay. A single refusal can be challenged while the conscience still feels its disturbance. If the person postpones correction, the refusal settles and begins to explain away the next warning. Delay does not remain neutral. It can become a method by which the heart protects a chosen desire from the truth.

That process can take place in public institutions as well as in private souls. A community first tolerates a dishonest practice, then praises the people who profit from it, and finally treats anyone who objects as a threat to stability. The practice has entered the community's moral imagination. The verse helps diagnose this condition before the corruption is defended as identity.

Yet the description of hardening never gives permission to stop calling people toward repentance. The people are called wicked because of their conduct, not because they are beyond Allah's knowledge or mercy. A warning can break through a settled habit if the person chooses honesty. The first act of healing is to stop inventing reasons why the warning cannot apply.

The heart is shaped by repeated attention. What a person praises, excuses, and repeats becomes easier to love; what he admits, resists, and corrects becomes easier to leave. The verse therefore asks for vigilance over small responses to truth. Accepting one clear correction may protect the heart from a long descent, while defending one profitable falsehood may begin the process of steeping denial.
