## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:73

> Joseph’s brothers replied, “By Allah! You know well that we did not come to cause trouble in the land, nor are we thieves.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath Opens the Denial**

The brothers' first word is *tallāh*, by God — an oath formula that the chapter uses at moments of maximum seriousness. Jacob's sons swear by it when reporting their first loss of a brother to their father (12:16), and they will use it again when telling their father that his grief will kill him (12:85). Its use here is not decorative. Eleven men accused of theft by an official of the Egyptian crown have no documentary defence, no witnesses of their own and no standing in the country. What they have is an oath, and the verse shows them reaching for it immediately, before any argument. The sequence is telling. An innocent man confronted with a false charge would normally begin with the facts; these men begin with God. The oath does the work that evidence cannot yet do, because the bags have not been searched and because the one fact that would settle the matter is a fact they do not possess.

**An Appeal to What the Officials Have Seen**

The second clause is the more interesting: *you know that we did not come to work corruption in the land*. The brothers do not ask to be believed; they point to knowledge their accusers already possess. The party has now entered Egypt twice, has been served at full measure on both occasions, has had its money returned without asking, and has conducted its business without incident. The officials who loaded their provisions and who accompanied the herald are the same people who observed all of it. The argument is therefore empirical rather than rhetorical — you have watched us, and what you watched does not look like the behaviour of people who came to do harm.

**Two Denials of Different Kinds**

The verse contains a denial of an act and a denial of a character. We did not come to work corruption in the land concerns purpose and conduct. We are not thieves concerns identity. The second is the more sweeping and the more vulnerable, since a person who is not a thief has never stolen, whereas a person who did not come to cause trouble may simply have kept his intentions to himself. The brothers make both claims together, and the chapter records both without comment. Verse 77 will show how the second claim is received by the one person in the scene qualified to judge it. The first denial concerns a cup and is true; the second concerns a family and is false. The brothers cannot tell the two apart and the chapter does not help them. Both are placed in the same mouth in the same verse, so the reader hears an honest statement about Egypt delivered by men whose account of Canaan is a lie they have kept for twenty years.

**The Irony the Chapter Does Not Spell Out**

These men stole a brother. They took a child from his father, put him in a well, sold him to a passing caravan for a handful of counted coins, and produced a bloodied shirt to explain his absence (12:16–20). The denial *we are not thieves* is true of the cup and false of the larger matter, and the chapter knows it. Nothing in the verse signals the irony; the reader supplies it from sixty verses of prior narrative. Joseph supplies it aloud in verse 77, in a sentence he keeps to himself, and the sūrah's decision to place the judgement inside his mind rather than in the narrator's voice is what gives the moment its force.

**Their Record in Egypt Supports the Denial**

Whatever the brothers' history in Canaan, their conduct in Egypt has been impeccable, and the verse is careful to keep the claim within those limits. They say they did not come *to the land* to work corruption — the reference is to Egypt, to this jurisdiction, to the officials' own observation. They do not claim a spotless life. The restriction is either instinctive or deliberate, and it makes the denial stronger than a general protestation of virtue would have been: it is falsifiable by the people being addressed, and they have not falsified it. The chapter is describing an argument that works because of its narrowness. Its narrowness is also its strength. They claim no general innocence, no good character and no divine favour; they claim that officials have watched them load grain, receive full measure and travel home without incident. Every element is verifiable by the men they are addressing, which makes it the one argument in the scene that cannot be dismissed as self-serving.

**Benjamin's Silence**

One member of the party knows what is in his bag and says nothing. The verse reports the brothers' answer collectively, and Benjamin is included in the collective. Whether he speaks with them, stays silent, or is answered for by the others, the text does not say. The silence is consistent with the instruction recorded in the tradition that Joseph asked him not to disclose their meeting (12:69). A reader aware of that instruction reads this verse differently from the way the officials in it do: the denial is honest for ten of the eleven men present, and the eleventh is bound by a confidence he cannot break in a public accusation. His position is the hardest in the scene and the chapter does not relieve it. He knows the cup is in his bag, he knows who put it there, and he has been told not to grieve over what his brothers used to do. To speak would betray the one man in Egypt who has protected him; to stay silent is to stand accused before nine brothers who believe him guilty.

**The Denial Requires a Penalty to Be Stated**

An accusation answered with a denial has reached an impasse unless someone proposes a test. The brothers have said they are innocent; the officials have said a royal object is missing; the bags are present and unopened. Verse 74 resolves the impasse by asking what should happen if the denial is false, and the question can only be asked because the denial was so emphatic. A hesitant answer would have justified a search on suspicion. An oath produces a demand for a standard: if you are lying, what should the consequence be? The brothers' confidence in this verse is what obliges them to answer that question in the next two. That question arrives within two verses and they answer it themselves, unpressed. The confidence expressed here is what makes the later answer possible: men who have just sworn by God that they came to do no harm are the men who will specify the penalty for a thief out of their own law. The oath commits them to the rule that will bind them.

**The Verse Shows Men Who Believe Themselves Safe**

Everything about the answer — the oath, the appeal to observed conduct, the flat denial of the character charge — indicates that the brothers do not expect a search to produce anything. They have loaded their own bags, or watched them being loaded, and they have taken nothing. On the evidence available to them the accusation is absurd, and their reply is the reply of people who expect the matter to end within the hour. The chapter lets that confidence stand unqualified. Verse 75 will have them state a penalty in the belief that it cannot apply to any of them, and verse 76 will report the search that ends the belief.
