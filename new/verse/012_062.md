## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:62

> Joseph ordered his servants to put his brothers’ money back into their saddlebags so that they would find it when they returned to their family and perhaps they would come back.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Order Given After the Travellers Have Left**

The verse reports a private instruction issued to servants, and its timing matters. The brothers have already been supplied and have already departed; the condition about their brother has been stated and accepted. Nothing in the transaction requires what follows. Joseph orders his own goods to be loaded into the bags of men who are no longer present to refuse them, which means the act cannot be part of a negotiation. It is a provision made for a journey home that is already under way, and for an effect that will occur in Canaan rather than in Egypt. Separating the act from its audience is what makes it effective. Money returned openly would have been refused or questioned on the spot; placed secretly in the bags it reaches the brothers as a discovery, at home, in front of their father, where it operates as evidence rather than as a gift. The arrangement converts a refund into testimony.

**"Their Merchandise" Has Two Readings**

The commentators differ about what exactly was returned. One reading holds that it was the goods the brothers had brought to barter — the trade items they presented in exchange for food. The other holds that it was the price they had paid, the money itself, restored to them after the grain had been given. The distinction affects the economics of the story but not its sense. On either reading the brothers come away with food and with the full value they exchanged for it, which makes the transaction a gift rather than a sale. The chapter does not settle the question, and both readings support what the following verses make of the discovery: an act of unexpected generosity by an official they had no reason to expect generosity from.

**Into the Bags, Not the Hands**

The placement is concealed. Joseph does not hand anything over, does not announce a refund and does not accept thanks. The money goes into the saddlebags, where it will be found later, by accident, at home. The concealment is what allows the discovery to function as evidence. A gift given openly is a courtesy; a gift found unannounced among one's own possessions after a long journey is a fact about the giver's character, and it arrives without the giver present to be flattered or thanked. The brothers will argue from it in verse 65 as though it were testimony, and the verse's arrangement is what makes that possible. Placement turns a refund into a document. Money handed across a counter is a transaction; money discovered inside a sealed bag on the road home is an event with a date, a place and witnesses. The verse arranges the second, and verse 65 shows the brothers using it exactly as an event rather than as a payment they received.

**"So That They May Know It When They Return to Their Family"**

The first purpose clause is about recognition. The verb carries the sense of knowing or identifying something, and the commentators gloss it as recognising the returned goods. The clause specifies the moment: when they have gone back to their people. The design is therefore temporal as well as material. Joseph is arranging for something to happen in Canaan, in front of Jacob, at a moment when the brothers need an argument. He has heard their promise in the previous verse and knows the difficulty of the persuasion they have undertaken. The returned money is supplied to them as the means of carrying out their own commitment. Joseph is funding the argument that will be turned against Jacob's refusal. He knows the brothers cannot travel again without provisions in a famine, and he removes the cost of the second journey before they think to ask him. What they will lay before their father as proof of the minister's goodwill has been paid for by the minister himself.

**"And Perhaps They Will Return"**

The second purpose clause states the objective plainly. Everything Joseph does in these verses is aimed at a second journey, and the second journey is aimed at a brother. The chapter has already explained why a second journey is unavoidable — the famine lasts seven years and ten camel-loads will not see a household through it (12:48, 12:65). The refund nevertheless makes the return more likely and more welcome. It converts a journey the brothers must make into one they will want to make, and it does so at Joseph's expense. The verse is a small study in how a person with power uses it: he spends his own goods to produce an outcome he cannot compel. The word perhaps is the centre of that study. Joseph controls the grain, the roads and the price, and he cannot compel ten men to return with a brother their father will not release. So he purchases the possibility instead, spending his own goods to leave open a door that no authority in Egypt is able to force.

**Joseph Pays for His Own Demand**

The cost of this arrangement falls entirely on him. He has imposed a condition (12:60), and he now subsidises compliance with it. The grain was given at full measure, the price was restored, and the servants performed the loading on his instruction. Nothing in the verse suggests reimbursement from the treasury, and the commentators take the act as Joseph's own. That is consistent with the pattern established since verse 58: he recognises his brothers, feeds them, sets a condition, and then quietly removes the cost of meeting it. The pressure he applies in verse 60 is real, and so is the generosity that immediately follows it, and the chapter holds both in adjacent verses without reconciling them. Held together they describe a method rather than a mood. Pressure is applied through the threat of exclusion and inducement through a refund nobody requested; neither instrument is concealed from the reader and neither is explained by him. The chapter sets the two out in sequence and allows their combination to stand as the complete account of his policy.

**The Discovery Becomes an Argument in Canaan**

Verse 65 shows the return of the money doing exactly the work this verse intended. The brothers open their bags at home, find what was put there, and use it to answer their father's reluctance: what more could we want, here is our money returned, we can provide for our family and gain an extra camel-load. Jacob's refusal in verse 64 was based on a memory; the brothers' counter in verse 65 is based on an object. The chapter is contrasting two kinds of evidence — a father's experience of his sons and a stranger's unexpected conduct — and it lets the object prevail, at least for the length of an oath. Joseph's instruction in verse 62 is what supplies the object.

**Servants, Bags, and the Absence of Any Official Record**

The verse is careful about how the act is carried out. Joseph speaks to his servants — *fityān*, young men in his service — and the loading is done by them, into the brothers' own bags, without the brothers present. No document is issued, no receipt is cancelled, and no public announcement is made. The refund is invisible to the Egyptian administration and will remain invisible until a family in Canaan opens its luggage. That informality is consistent with the chapter's treatment of Joseph's power: he controls a country's grain and uses a small part of it, quietly, for a private purpose connected to his family. The sūrah records the act without praising or criticising it, and the following verses show what it produces.
