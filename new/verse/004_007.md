## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:7

> For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave, and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave—whether it is little or much. ˹These are˺ obligatory shares.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Fixed Share for Both Sexes**

This verse establishes a principle that prior custom had denied: both men and women have a fixed portion in what parents and close relatives leave. The Qur’an states it twice in one sentence, once for men and once for women, with the same wording *naṣīb mafrūḍ* — an obligatory share — closing the verse. Repetition here is legislation. In the pre-Islamic order only adult men who could bear arms and produce revenue were counted as heirs; women and young children were excluded on the ground that they did not fight and did not earn. The Qur’an set that reasoning aside and assigned entitlement by kinship, not by military utility. Commentators link the revelation to the case of a widow with daughters who were left without support because male relatives took the estate. By saying *whether it is little or much*, the verse prevents the dismissal of small estates as not worth sharing; even a modest amount carries the same obligation. The Qur’an elsewhere confirms the same ethic with precise fractions for daughters and parents (4:11) and for spouses and siblings (4:12), but this verse is the foundation: no heir of either sex is erased from the ledger.

**Why the Verse Repeats the Same Formula**

The parallel phrasing — *lil-rijāl naṣīb ... wa lil-nisāʾ naṣīb* — does more than grant; it equalises the language of entitlement. Both have a *naṣīb*, a share that is known and assigned, not a gift left to generosity. The closing *naṣīban mafrūḍan* was read by grammarians as emphasizing obligation from above; *farḍ* in the Qur’an denotes what God fixes as duty, as in the limits that will be called *ḥudūd Allāh* two verses later (4:13–14). By naming women’s portion with the same noun and the same adjective as men’s, the verse teaches that the share does not depend on the goodwill of male relatives after death; it arrives as law. That point mattered because the initial reception of these laws met resistance. Reports recall companions who were stunned that women and children who did not ride out for booty should inherit a significant fraction from fathers and husbands. The verse answers that surprise by rooting the share not in contribution to war but in closeness of womb and in divine decree. What God has made obligatory is not open to being bargained away by custom.

**Little or Much — No Estate Too Small to Divide Justly**

The clause *mimmā qalla minhu aw kathur* — whether it is little or much — closes a loophole that frugality might have opened. In a large estate, sharing is expected; in a small one, heirs might argue that division would scatter what needs to stay together, or that the widow and daughters can be provided for informally while the core property stays with the men. The Qur’an rejects that economy of excuse. If the amount is small, the share remains, and the division must still be just. That rigor protects the most vulnerable heirs precisely when the temptation to consolidate is strongest. The sūrah will reinforce this by ordering that even bystanders at distribution be offered something and spoken to kindly (4:8), and by warning guardians to imagine their own helpless children left behind (4:9). The principle across those verses is consistent: presence creates a claim, kinship creates a share, and the size of the pot does not nullify either. A community that honours small shares trains itself to honour large ones without fraud.

**An Obligatory Share, Not a Favour**

The words *naṣīban mafrūḍan* carry legal weight that the sūrah will specify in fractions two verses later. Jurisprudence built on this verse a whole science of inheritance — *ʿilm al-farāʾiḍ* — where fixed heirs receive fixed portions after debts and bequests are settled. That science later contributed to the development of algebra, because its calculations require precise proportional reasoning, a historical detail that shows how a verse about family rights generated tools for mathematics. The deeper point is theological. Upon death, property returns to God, *“and to Allah belongs the heritage of the heavens and the earth”* (3:180), and He redistributes it by His own ordinance. The heirs do not inherit because the deceased gifted it to them; they inherit because God assigned it. That is why the verse says the shares are obligatory — imposed, not suggested — and why verses 13 and 14 will frame obedience to these limits as the difference between Gardens and Fire. What seems like a domestic settlement is, in the Qur’an’s account, an act of worship.

**The Case That Brought the Principle Down**

Early reports connect this verse to a specific family: a man died leaving a wife and daughters, and his brothers took the estate, saying women and children who did not fight should get nothing. The wife complained to the Prophet ﷺ, and this verse came down, followed by the detailed shares of 4:11–12. Whether that exact story covers every instance or one of several similar cases, the pattern is clear: a new rule arrived to correct a living wrong, not to pose a theoretical ideal. That setting explains the verse’s brevity and force. No argument is offered about women’s capacities; the verse simply assigns a share and calls it obligatory, leaving debate aside. The brevity also explains why the verse does not yet give fractions; it first breaks the old rule that exclusion is permissible, then the next passage quantifies. That two-step pedagogy — first the principle, then the portions — helps listeners absorb a social revolution without treating it as optional advice. Once the principle was accepted that women as such have a share, the fractions could be heard as detail rather than shock.

**From Principle to Practice Across the Sūrah**

This verse opens a corridor that runs through the rest of the opening third of al-Nisāʾ. Verse 8 will ask those dividing to remember the non-heirs present; verse 9 will ask them to judge as they would want others to judge their own children; verse 10 will warn that consuming orphans’ wealth is eating fire; verses 11–12 will set the arithmetic for children, parents, spouses, and siblings; verses 13–14 will promise reward and warn of disgrace for crossing the limits; and verses 19–21 will close the circle by forbidding inheritance of women themselves and forbidding seizure of dowries. The common thread is that property and persons are not to be taken by the strong from the weak under cover of custom. By naming men and women side by side in the same sentence with the same noun, verse 7 makes that thread visible from the start. It does not argue for equity in the abstract; it assigns shares and calls them obligatory, and it leaves the community to learn the rest — how to calculate, how to speak kindly while calculating, and how to live with a Watchful Reckoner over every division (4:6).
