## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:16

> Go to Pharaoh and say, ‘We are messengers from the Lord of all worlds,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mission Requires a Direct Audience**

Moses and Aaron are told to go to Pharaoh and speak to him. The command does not allow them to keep the message at the level of private advice or indirect criticism. Pharaoh's power governs the order that has harmed the Children of Israel, so the ruler must hear the claim that stands above his rule. This directness is not a license for rudeness. It is clarity about where responsibility is concentrated.

The plural pronoun in we are messengers also matters. Moses is not presenting himself as a solitary reformer with a personal program. Aaron stands with him, and both are servants of a Sender. Their authority is derived, not self-created. They do not ask Pharaoh to admire their personalities or accept a new political faction. They carry a command from the Lord who owns authority over Pharaoh and over those Pharaoh has oppressed.

**Lord of All Worlds Leaves No Space for Rival Lordship**

The title Lord of all worlds, Rabb al-ʿālamīn, is a universal claim. It reaches beyond one nation, dynasty, or sacred geography. A lord gives existence, sustains it, and has the right to command it. If God is Lord of all worlds, Pharaoh cannot be ultimate lord of Egypt, and no ruler can turn a portion of creation into a private domain beyond divine judgment.

The claim therefore challenges Pharaoh before the details of the demand are stated. Moses does not begin by bargaining over labor, taxes, or royal custom. He names the source of the order that will judge those arrangements. Political injustice is not merely a failure of administration. It is rebellion against the Lord whose care extends to every people, including those whom the court treats as property.

**Messengers Speak Under One Authority**

Calling themselves messengers establishes a relationship among three parties: the Sender, the messengers, and the ruler who must hear. The messengers are not equal to God, and they are not petitioners begging Pharaoh to grant them credibility. Their role is to deliver what they have been given. That role requires accuracy and humility at once. They cannot change the command to make it easier, and they cannot claim independent power when challenged.

The shared title also protects the audience from treating the brothers as rivals. The two voices have one source and one purpose. Their agreement is not the product of political coordination alone; it is part of the sign that the mission is ordered by God. When the ruler addresses them, he is not free to divide them by flattering one or accusing the other. Their authority rests outside his court.

**A Universal Lord Can Address a Particular Tyrant**

The phrase Lord of all worlds may sound broad compared with the immediate audience, but its breadth explains why Pharaoh must be addressed. Universal sovereignty does not remain an idea above history. It enters a particular court, names a particular wrong, and commands a particular ruler to stop. God's care for every world includes attention to the people whose names Pharaoh's system tries to erase.

This movement from universal to particular is a mark of sound religious reasoning. It is easy to praise a universal God while leaving local injustice untouched. Moses' message prevents that escape. The One who sustains the heavens also sees forced labor, abuse of power, and the fear of a vulnerable people. Worship that never reaches the treatment of others has not yet understood the Lord of all worlds.

**The Wording Refuses a Bargain With Power**

Moses is not told to ask Pharaoh whether he will permit a harmless religious gathering. The message begins with the authority of the Sender, which means Pharaoh must reconsider his own position. A tyrant can negotiate with another human institution, but he cannot place God on the list of parties whose permission he may grant or withhold.

That does not remove the need for wisdom. The messengers will speak in a court where a careless word can be used against them. Wisdom, however, serves truth rather than disguising it. The direct address keeps the mission from becoming a compromise in which the oppressed receive a small concession while the structure of domination remains untouched.

**The Command Is Public Before It Is Victorious**

No promise of Pharaoh's immediate acceptance is attached to the instruction. The messengers are simply told what to say. This makes the moment a test of obedience before it becomes a story of outcome. The truth must be placed before power even when power appears able to reject it.

The verse thus prepares the confrontation in the right order. First comes the Sender's authority, then the messengers' delivery, and only afterward the ruler's response. Pharaoh may answer with ridicule or accusation, but his response cannot change the source of the claim. Moses and Aaron enter his court carrying a universal lordship into a local tyranny.

**The Opening Sentence Sets a Rule for Confrontation**

A messenger may address power firmly without pretending to possess power. Moses and Aaron identify the Lord, identify themselves as sent, and leave the ruler no excuse for mistaking the source of the claim. This is disciplined confrontation. It avoids both flattery, which would sell the message for access, and vanity, which would make the messenger's personality the center.

The rule is useful whenever truth must enter a hostile institution. State the authority that governs the matter, speak to the person who can act, and keep the demand tied to the wrong being done. The messengers do not threaten Pharaoh with a rival throne. They announce that every throne already stands beneath the Lord of all worlds.
