## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:90

> Miserable is the price they have sold their souls for—denying Allah’s revelation and resenting Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills of His servants! They have earned wrath upon wrath. And such disbelievers will suffer a humiliating punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Price and the Commodity**

The verse returns to commercial language, and this time it names both items in the transaction. What was sold is the soul; what was received is described as a miserable price. The Book has already said that this community traded the Hereafter for this present life, and the present sentence tells the reader what the trade cost them personally rather than collectively.

The word for the price is a term of contempt, and its placement at the start of the sentence gives the verse its tone. Before naming the offence, the Book states the verdict, so that the reader hears the accusation of a bad bargain before hearing what was sold.

**Denying What Was Recognized**

The first half of the charge is the denial of God's revelation, and the previous verse had already established that this community recognized the revelation it denied. The Book does not handle this as a lesser form of unbelief. It treats knowledge that is refused as the substance of the charge, and the sūrah's opening description of people who broke their covenant after it had been confirmed rests on the same principle.

That framing explains the weight of the sentence. A person who never encountered a message has an excuse the Book acknowledges; a community that had been waiting for the message and then rejected it when it arrived has none. The verse states the denial and leaves the reader to supply the awareness that makes it serious.

**The Motive Named as Envy**

The second half of the charge identifies what drove the rejection: resentment that God granted His grace to whom He wills of His servants. The Book does not say the community doubted the credentials of the messenger or found the content of the revelation inadequate. It says the community objected to the choice.

That diagnosis recurs throughout the Book's account of opposition to the messengers, and it names the precise sin involved, which is not theological error but a claim on God's freedom to give. The sūrah will restate it in the years ahead of its revelation as the community's central grievance, and later chapters describe the same resentment in other people who could not accept that revelation had passed to a nation they considered beneath them.

**Grace Given to Whom He Wills**

The clause about God's grace is stated as a fact about God's sovereignty rather than as a rebuke. The Book uses the formula "to whom He wills" whenever it wants to remove the assumption that divine gifts follow a fixed order of merit, and it applies the formula to prophecy, to mercy, and to the ability to receive guidance.

The community's objection presupposed that the covenant created a permanent entitlement to revelation. The verse answers with a statement about who decides, and the answer is not a negotiation. That is why the sentence moves directly to consequences without an intervening argument; the dispute had reached the point where argument ends and the verdict begins.

**Wrath Upon Wrath**

The expression for the punishment is unusual, and the commentators read it as the accumulation of successive periods of divine anger. One reading takes it as wrath earned by this rejection piled on wrath earned by earlier offences, and another takes it as wrath in this world followed by wrath in the next. Under both readings, the phrase describes a sentence that compounds rather than a single penalty.

The Book uses anger language sparingly, and when it does, it marks the point at which a community's record has passed a threshold. The sūrah's narrative has now traced that record from the covenant at Sinai to the rejection of the Prophet ﷺ, and the phrase at the end of this sentence functions as the summary of the whole sequence.

**A Humiliating Punishment**

The final clause specifies the character of the punishment as humiliation. The Book distinguishes between suffering and humiliation in its accounts of the Hereafter, and it reserves the second for people whose offence involved pride or the pursuit of status. A community that rejected the truth in order to protect its standing is matched with a penalty that removes standing altogether.

The word choice also matches the community's own conduct in the passage. These were people preoccupied with their rank among the tribes of Madinah and their standing as the possessors of a scripture, and the verse that ends their account in this section describes them enduring the loss of the very thing they had defended.

**A Warning the Believers Receive as Well**

The passage is written so that its warnings apply to the readers who come after. The Qur'an tells the believers in other chapters that some of them will be tested by their own preference for worldly gain over the commands of God, and it warns against envy, which it names as a sickness of the heart. The situation described in this verse is not confined to the community that first provoked it.

That is the function of the sūrah's long treatment of this people. By the time the chapter turns to the Muslims, the record of a community that knew its scripture and refused it has been established in detail, and the exhortations that follow are addressed to people who have been shown exactly what such a refusal looks like from the inside.

**The Terms of the Charge in One Sentence**

The sentence packs together the sale, the commodity, the motive, and two stages of punishment, and each element has been established by the verses before it. What this verse adds is the connection between the motive and the outcome. A community that objected to God's freedom to grant His grace to whomever He chose received the opposite of what it sought, and the Book describes the result as humiliation because the aim had been standing. The closing attribute of the previous passage and the curse that precedes this one make the same point from different directions: nothing in the account escapes notice, and no position defended against revelation is preserved by the defence. Later generations have read the verse as a summary of what happens when a people measures revelation by what it does for them.
