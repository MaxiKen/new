## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:90

> Miserable is the price they have sold their souls for—denying Allah’s revelation and resenting Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills of His servants! They have earned wrath upon wrath. And such disbelievers will suffer a humiliating punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Price Named Miserable**

The verse opens with the sūrah's economics at its most severe: *miserable is the price they have sold their souls for*. The formula is one the recital has used before and will use again — the same condemnation closes the account of the magic-buyers at verse 102, where the sūrah pronounces: *Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls, if only they knew!* (2:102). The shared wording marks a single crime-class: the sale of the soul, in which the eternal self is the merchandise and some worldly position is the payment. Verse 86 named the trade's general form — the Hereafter sold for the world's life; verse 90 now specifies this instance's payment, and the specification is what makes the price miserable in a degree beyond ordinary sin. What was purchased: tribal precedence, the consolation of being the people from whom prophets come. What was paid: the souls' acceptance of a recognized revelation. The arithmetic of verse 79 — the sacred liquidated for a fleeting gain — runs here at its highest denomination, because the gain is not money but identity, and the payment is not a text's distortion but the soul's own denial. The exclamation the verse ends its first clause with is the recital's astonishment made audible: not merely a crime but a bad trade, a transaction whose terms the buyer himself would condemn if he saw both sides of the counter.

**Resenting the Giver**

The payment's motive is named in the verse's second clause, and it is the verse's theological center: *denying Allah's revelation and resenting Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills of His servants*. The denial is not first intellectual but emotional — its engine is resentment directed at the distribution of divine grace. The word is *faḍl*: grace, bounty, favor, superiority — the unearned excess Allah gives beyond what is owed, and the clause *to whoever He wills of His servants* states the sovereignty of the giving. This is the offense the rejecters could not absorb: that Allah's favor is not a national inheritance but a free bestowal, placed where He wills among His servants. The sūrah will state the principle against them again in its verse of the Throne and its verses on provision, and the whole Qur'an runs on it — grace distributed by divine choice, prophecy placed by divine knowledge, favor owing no lineage a debt. The resentment is therefore aimed not at the recipient but at the Giver's freedom: the objection to *whoever He wills* is an objection to the will itself. And the verse's term *servants* sharpens the indictment — in the Qur'an's usage, servant is ordinarily a term of praise, the honored designation of those who belong to Allah (2:186 and throughout): the grace they resented was descending on servants like themselves, and their resentment was a refusal to accept that Allah's household is wider than their tribe.

**The Tribal Theology Behind the Envy**

The exegetes supply the doctrine the resentment ran on: the reported belief that a prophet should come only from the Children of Israel — that the office was, in effect, a hereditary dignity of their house, and its conferral on an Arab lineage a misappropriation of their inheritance. Because that dignity was passing to another people, they purchased the consolations of tribal pride with their faith itself (the reading shared by Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī). The verse thereby documents envy's theology: not raw jealousy but a whole account of how divine gifts ought to be distributed, with the envier's own group at the center of the account. The doctrine explains what verse 89 left as fact — the recognition followed by rejection — because under this theology, recognition made rejection worse: the more clearly the prophet matched the descriptions, the more clearly the dignity was leaving the house, and every confirmation of his truth was a confirmation of their displacement. Envy of this kind cannot be answered by evidence, since the evidence itself is the injury; it can only be answered by repentance, which is why the recital's rebuttals in this passage are questions rather than proofs. The verse's pastoral edge for later readers is exact: the most dangerous form of the sin is not wanting what another has, but holding a theory of who should have what — and then meeting a reality, divine or human, that distributes otherwise.

**Wrath Upon Wrath**

The sentence's second movement states the accumulation: *they have earned wrath upon wrath*. The verb — *bāʾū* — means they became deserving, they carried it away as their earned burden; the same verb-family the verse's first clause used for selling, keeping the whole statement inside its commercial grammar: what they bought, they bought with wrath, and the wrath comes in layers. The exegetes read the doubling concretely, identifying the two (or more) descents variously: the wrath of the calf, the wrath for neglecting portions of the Torah, the wrath for rejecting Jesus, the wrath for rejecting Muhammad ﷺ (the identifications run across Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī). The readings converge on a principle: wrath upon wrath is the record of repeated rejection — each recognized truth refused adding its layer to the account, so that the final rejection arrives not as a first offense but as the summit of an accumulation. This is the covenant-history of verses 40 through 89 compressed into three words: the community did not fail once and fall; it failed at station after station, each failure witnessed, pardoned, or warned against, each adding weight to the sentence. The verse's arithmetic answers the numbered-days doctrine of verse 80 from a new side: a people imagining its Fire-term in days had not counted the layers its own history had stacked — wrath upon wrath is the tally the recital has been keeping all along, and the tally does not round down.

**Kufr as Covering**

The verse's closing reference to *such disbelievers* returns to the word whose range this passage has been unfolding, and here the lexical depth does theological work. *Kufr* in this context carries the sense the exegetes mark: covering over — specifically, covering over blessings received. The rejecters of verse 90 are not people without divine favor but people in possession of it: the Scripture in their hands, the prophecies they had prayed by, the recognition granted them at the arrival — all blessings, all covered. The same root-family the passage used for the excuse of verse 88 ties the two together: hearts *ghulf*, sheathed and wrapped, and faith *kufr*, a covering laid over what the sheath contains. The linguistic connection is the verse's diagnosis in miniature: disbelief at this stage is not absence but suppression — the blessing present and active, the denial laid across it like a hand across a lamp. This is why the resentment-clause and the covering-clause belong to one psychology: the person who resents grace given to another must cover grace given to himself, because his own blessings — the very Scripture that describes the prophet — testify against his refusal. The covering is not able to extinguish what it covers, only to hide it from the coverer, which is the precise mechanism by which a community that recognized the Book came to reject it: the recognition covered, the resentment fed, the layers of wrath added one denial at a time.

**The Humiliating Punishment**

The sentence's last clause matches the punishment to the crime in kind: *such disbelievers will suffer a humiliating punishment*. The humiliation — *hīn* in the Qur'an's standing pairing of punishment with disgrace — is the exact inversion of what the sale purchased. The payment was pride: the tribal dignity, the hereditary claim on prophecy, the consolation of being the people of the house. The sentence returns the same currency devalued to its opposite: not merely pain but exposure — the pride's purchase revealed as a loss, the precedence claimed turned into the humiliation earned, in the court whose proceedings verse 76's conspirators were already trying to manage. The pairing runs through the recital's economics: those who bought the world's standing with the Hereafter receive the Hereafter's verdict on the world's standing, and the verdict is that it was counterfeit. Verse 87 had named the crime's interior posture — arrogance at every disliked message — and the humiliation is that posture's eschatological correction: the self enlarged against the messengers is measured, at last, in public, against the truth it covered. The verse leaves its audience with the whole transaction visible: the price miserable, the motive resentment at grace's freedom, the accumulation wrath layered on wrath, the covering laid over blessings still shining beneath it, and the ending humiliation for pride. Every term is a warning to every later holder of a revelation who feels that the Giver's freedom has slighted him — the feeling is the crime's first installment, and the recital files the whole purchase so that no reader completes it.
