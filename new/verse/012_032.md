## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:32

> She said, “This is the one for whose love you criticized me! I did try to seduce him but he ˹firmly˺ refused. And if he does not do what I order him to, he will certainly be imprisoned and ˹fully˺ disgraced.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Confession Delivered as a Triumph**

The verse begins with the woman identifying Joseph to the room: this is the one on whose account you blamed me. The sentence is an admission and a vindication in a single breath, and the admission is total — she does not soften it, does not blame him, and does not retract what she said in verse 25. The commentators observe the reasoning the demonstration was built to supply: women who could not withstand one sight of him without cutting their hands can hardly fault a woman who had him in her house day and night. The confession is therefore offered as an excuse rather than as a repentance. She is not saying she was wrong; she is saying the provocation was beyond the ordinary, and the proof of that is sitting in front of the women with their hands bleeding.

**Three Statements, and Each One Is True**

Look at what she asserts. She sought to seduce him. He refused, and the refusal is described with a word indicating firmness and completion — he held back, he was chaste. And a consequence will follow if he does not comply. Nothing in the speech is false, which is unusual for this chapter and worth marking. The woman who lied in verse 25, prosecuted an innocent man in verse 25 and was found at fault in verse 29 now tells the whole truth in front of witnesses. The sūrah does not present this as a change of heart; the motive is still self-justification. What it presents is the odd fact that a person can be entirely accurate about a matter she remains entirely unrepentant about, and that accuracy of this kind still advances the case of the person she wronged.

**"He Refused" Is the Chapter's Verdict, Spoken by the Accuser**

The word for his refusal carries the sense of holding oneself back, of abstention maintained under pressure. Coming from her mouth it is the strongest available testimony, because she is the only person in the room who knows what happened behind the locked doors. The husband's finding in verse 28 rested on a torn shirt. Her statement rests on experience. The chapter thus accumulates its proof of Joseph's innocence from every interested party in turn: the family witness, the master of the house, the women of the city, and finally the woman herself. By the end of verse 32 nobody in Egypt who has spoken about the matter has said anything against him, and the only accusation ever made has been formally withdrawn by its author in public.

**The Threat Names Two Punishments**

*He will certainly be imprisoned and disgraced.* The two are joined, and the second is the more serious in a household of this kind. Imprisonment is a location; disgrace is a status, and for a slave whose standing rests entirely on the confidence of his master, the loss of it is the loss of everything. The threat also reveals what she now wants. Verse 25 sought his punishment as revenge; verse 32 seeks his compliance through the prospect of punishment. The demand is unchanged — do what I order — and the instrument has been switched from prosecution to intimidation. The commentators note that the command refers back to the original summons of verse 23, so the campaign described by the persistent verb in that verse is still running, now with the whole city's female establishment as its audience.

**The Women's Silence Is Part of the Pressure**

Nothing in this verse records a reply from the guests. The reports state that after hearing the threat they joined the woman in urging Joseph to comply, telling him he had no option but to obey his mistress or face the prison. Whether or not that exchange occurred in those words, the text supports its substance by what follows: Joseph's prayer in verse 33 speaks of what *they* invite him to, in the plural. He is not answering one woman. He is answering a room. The shift from a private advance to a collective demand is the escalation that makes his next verse necessary, and it explains why his prayer describes the danger as *their* cunning rather than hers.

**A Public Setting, A Private Demand**

The scene has moved from a locked room to a banquet hall, and the demand has not changed with it. That is the verse's structural irony. Everything that made the earlier encounter wrongful — seclusion, concealment, the absence of witnesses — has been removed, and what remains is the same instruction, now issued openly and backed by an open threat. The chapter is showing that the wrongness of the proposal was never a matter of setting. Publicity does not legitimise it; consensus does not legitimise it; the fact that everyone in the room agrees does not legitimise it. Joseph will answer accordingly, addressing not the room but God, and the sūrah will treat his refusal as the only sane response available to anyone in that hall.

The second half of her threat deserves attention on its own. *Disgraced* renders a word meaning small, humbled, brought low — the state of a person stripped of standing and made aware of it. The Qur'an reserves the same term for the final condition of the arrogant: *“Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled”* (40:60). A woman threatening a slave with humiliation is using the vocabulary the Book applies to the ultimate humiliation of those who refused God. The inversion is complete and the chapter will perform it. The man threatened with disgrace leaves the prison to be told he is esteemed and trusted (12:54), asks for the storehouses of the country (12:55), and ends up holding the position of the man who once owned him (12:78). Nothing she threatened materialises, and everything she commanded is refused.
