## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:235

> There is no blame on you for subtly showing interest in ˹divorced or widowed˺ women or for hiding ˹the intention˺ in your hearts. Allah knows that you are considering them ˹for marriage˺. But do not make a secret commitment with them—you can only show interest in them appropriately. Do not commit to the bond of marriage until the waiting period expires. Know that Allah is aware of what is in your hearts, so beware of Him. And know that Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Speech During the Waiting**

The widow's term, just fixed, immediately raises a practical question the verse answers before anyone has to ask it: what may be said to her while she waits? The answer threads between two errors — total silence, which would freeze a woman's prospects for the term's whole length, and open contracting, which would hollow the waiting out. The permission is for taʿrīḍ, intimation: speech that discloses interest without stating a proposal. The exegetes' examples define the register precisely. A man may say, *I hope to marry a righteous woman*, or *I do not wish to marry any other woman*, or simply *I desire to marry* — sentences whose referent is marriage in general and whose direction she can read without his naming her. What he may not say is the explicit proposal: *promise me you will marry me and not another*. The line between intimation and commitment is the line the whole verse is drawn around, and it is a line of words.

**The Inner Permission**

The verse's second allowance is quieter: *or for hiding ˹the intention˺ in your hearts*. Thinking about marrying a woman in her waiting period is itself unblamed — and the permission is psychologically precise, since a rule that criminalized the thought would be unenforceable and corrosive. The clause then grounds itself in divine knowledge: *Allah knows that you are considering them ˹for marriage˺*. The formulation does something subtler than prohibition or permission: it acknowledges that the consideration is happening, names God as its witness, and declines to legislate the heart — legislating instead the two thresholds where the heart goes public, the word spoken and the contract signed. The structure mirrors the orphan verse's surveillance clause (2:220): discretion is granted where human audit cannot reach, and God's knowledge is stationed over the grant, converting the private space from a loophole into a trust.

**The Secret Commitment Forbidden**

Against the permitted intimation the verse sets the forbidden thing: *do not make a secret commitment with them*. The commentators read the secret commitment in two directions, and both readings legislate the same boundary. On the first, it means a private agreement to marry — the explicit proposal exchanged in confidence during the term, binding her to him while the law still counts her days. On the second, it means consummation itself: the marriage entered in secret before the term expires. The first reading prohibits the contract's anticipation; the second prohibits its execution; and the verse's continuation — *you can only show interest in them appropriately* — limits every interaction to the intimation's register, *do not commit to the bond of marriage until the waiting period expires* naming the contract as the thing withheld. What the verse constructs is a waiting period with real integrity: the term runs, and nothing about her marital future is fixed while it runs.

**If the Line Is Crossed**

The jurists drew consequences for crossing it, and the severity of the consequences measures the value of the rule. Some held that if a couple consummates a marriage before the waiting period expires, they are forbidden to each other permanently — the premature union poisons the eventual one — and the woman must complete the waiting owed to her deceased husband and then a second waiting owed to the improper marriage. If the couple contracted without consummating, the contract is dissolved, the term is completed, and the man must propose anew when it ends. The rulings differ in severity but agree in structure: the waiting period cannot be shortened by private arrangement, and an arrangement made against it either fails or forfeits its object. The law's patience is enforced by making impatience expensive — the very outcome the eager suitor sought is the thing his eagerness destroys.

**Beware of Him**

The verse's closing movement returns to the heart it opened with. *Know that Allah is aware of what is in your hearts, so beware of Him* — the same khubr-expertise named at the end of the widow's verse (2:234), now turned explicitly inward. The sequence is deliberate: knowledge, then wariness. A person who is certain that his unspoken calculation is known relates to his own heart differently; the intimation he permits himself, the direction he angles his allowable sentences, the private agreement he does not voice — all of it stands in a lit room. The commentators connect this interior surveillance to the verse's whole legislation: the rules about words are enforceable by courts only crudely, since the difference between honorable intimation and secret commitment lives in tone and intention, and so the verse installs its own enforcement in the heart's awareness of being known. The external law is thin; the internal audit is total.

**Forgiving, Forbearing**

The final name-pair is the one the code has used at moments of interior legislation before — the oaths verse closed with the Forgiving, the Forbearing (2:225), and the pairing does its distinctive work here. Ghafūr addresses the heart's trespasses that no court will ever see: the consideration that shaded toward scheming, the intimation that leaned too hard, the intention hidden a little less innocently than the verse assumes. Ḥalīm — the Forbearing, the One who does not strike at once — addresses the interval the whole verse legislates: the waiting period is itself a divine ḥilm, a suspension of resolution in which nothing is concluded and everything is given time. The suitor is told that God's knowledge of his heart is paired not with immediate reckoning but with patience and pardon — and that this pairing, rather than licensing the hidden commitment, obliges him. A law this gentle about what it cannot see is a law that trusts the heart it has made visible to God.
