## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:22

> And when he reached maturity, We gave him wisdom and knowledge. This is how We reward the good-doers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Whole Life Compressed Into One Clause**

Between verse 21 and verse 22 the chapter crosses a decade without narrating it. A purchased boy becomes a grown man in the house of an Egyptian minister, and not one event of that period is recorded: no schooling, no service, no friendship, no incident. The next verse opens with the seduction, so the entire formation of Joseph happens inside a single subordinate clause. That compression is a decision about what the story is. The sūrah is not a biography; it is a demonstration. The years that would matter to a biographer — how a Hebrew slave learned Egyptian, how he earned a place in a minister's household — are irrelevant to the argument, which concerns what happens when such a man is tested. The verse supplies the equipment and moves on.

**"When He Reached Maturity" Uses the Language of Full Strength**

The phrase *balagha ashuddahu* denotes the arrival at full physical and mental strength, the point where a man is complete. The same formula describes Moses in another chapter: *“And when he reached full strength and maturity, We gave him wisdom and knowledge”* (28:14) — a parallel so close that the two verses have long been read together. Elsewhere the Qur'an fixes a different marker for the peak of a life, the age of forty: *“In time, when the child reaches their prime at the age of forty”* (46:15). The chapter does not specify Joseph's age, and the transmitted reports do not settle it either. What the phrase guarantees is that what follows is the act of an adult, not of a boy. The trial in verse 23 is presented as a trial of a formed man with formed desires, and the verse exists to establish that fact before the trial begins.

**Wisdom and Knowledge Are Given as Two Distinct Things**

Two nouns are named and they are not synonyms. *Ḥukm* is judgement — the capacity to decide correctly between competing claims, which the commentators gloss either as prophethood or as the ability to judge between people with justice. *ʿIlm* is knowledge, glossed as the interpretation of dreams and of coming events, or more inwardly as acquaintance with the realities of things and the etiquette of servanthood toward God. The pairing covers both directions of a prophet's work: the outward office of ruling and settling disputes, and the inward knowledge that qualifies him for it. The chapter will exercise both. Joseph judges between two prisoners and between a king and his court, and he knows things nobody present knows. The verse is a programme statement, and the rest of the sūrah is its delivery.

**The Formula "Thus Do We Reward the Good-Doers" Is a Law**

The closing sentence appears word for word in two other places: at the end of the list of prophets descended from Abraham (6:84) and at the end of the account of Moses reaching his strength (28:14). A phrase repeated verbatim across chapters functions as a stated rule rather than a comment, and the rule is that the gift follows the character. Both recipients in the other two instances are described in the same terms as Joseph is here — men of proven goodness given judgement and knowledge. The sūrah reinforces the identification by applying the adjective *muḥsin*, one who does good, to Joseph four separate times, in the mouths of his fellow prisoners (12:36), in the narrator's summary of his establishment (12:56), in the brothers' report of him (12:78) and in his own final prayer (12:90). The reward named in verse 22 is therefore not a gift to a stranger but a payment on an account the chapter keeps openly.

**Why This Verse Must Come Before the Next**

The placement is the point. A reader who moved straight from the household arrangements of verse 21 to the locked doors of verse 23 would judge Joseph's refusal as luck or coldness. The intervening verse establishes that he has been equipped: judgement, knowledge, maturity, and a standing with God described in the language of reward. When the seduction comes, his resistance is the exercise of what he has been given rather than an accident of temperament. The chapter is careful about the order because the moral weight of the scene depends on it. Virtue under temptation counts only if the person tempted was capable of the alternative, and this verse is where that capability is certified.

**Everything Here Is Passive**

The grammar of the verse deserves notice. Joseph does nothing in it. He reaches maturity — a process that happens to him — and then *We gave him* wisdom and knowledge. No striving is mentioned, no study, no ascent. The two things that will define his career are described as received. That is consistent with the whole chapter's theology, in which a well, a market and a prison are all instruments of a plan Joseph did not write. It also protects the later claim he makes in verse 53, that he does not acquit himself because the soul inclines to evil *except those shown mercy by my Lord* (12:53). The man who says that has already been shown, in this verse, where his resources came from. His innocence in the coming scene is not self-sufficiency. It is the use of a gift.

That has a consequence for how the chapter treats the years of slavery. Nothing in verse 22 suggests Joseph earned his standing by diligence in the household, though the reports assume he served well and the ʿAzīz's earlier expectation of usefulness (12:21) implies it. What the verse records is a bestowal that arrives with age and is unrelated to labour. The sūrah thereby separates two things a reader is inclined to fuse: the boy's conduct, which was good, and the equipment he received, which was given. Conduct is rewarded, as the closing formula insists; the reward is still a gift. Joseph will spend the rest of the chapter acting on that equipment in circumstances he did not choose, in a prison he did not deserve, and the verse is the reason those actions can be called prophetic rather than merely capable.
