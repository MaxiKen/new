## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:66

> Jacob insisted, “I will not send him with you until you give me a solemn oath by Allah that you will certainly bring him back to me, unless you are totally overpowered.” Then after they had given him their oaths, he concluded, “Allah is a Witness to what we have said.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Refusal Turned Into Terms**

Verse 64 opened with a rhetorical question that functioned as a refusal. Verse 66 opens with *I will not send him with you until*, which is not a refusal but a price. Something has changed between the two verses, and the change is the money found in the bags (12:65). Jacob has not been convinced that his sons are reliable guardians; he has been shown that Egypt's minister is generous, and that alters the calculation enough to make the journey acceptable on conditions. The chapter tracks the movement precisely: distrust of the travellers survives, distrust of the destination lessens, and the result is a covenant rather than a prohibition. That conversion is the verse's achievement. A prohibition would have been negotiated away or simply disobeyed; a covenant creates an obligation the sons have sworn themselves and can be held to. Jacob stops trying to prevent the journey and begins trying to bind the travellers, which is the only form of control left to a man who has decided to let his son go.

**The Form of the Oath**

The word Jacob requires denotes a binding covenant, the term the Qur'an uses for the most serious kind of undertaking between people and between people and God. It is not a promise and not a word of honour. It is sworn *bi-Llāh*, by God, and it is given collectively: *fa-lammā ātawhu mawthiqahum*, when they had given him their covenant. The religious form is the point. Jacob has already stated that God is the best of keepers (12:64); he now makes God a party to the arrangement, so that the obligation runs to God as well as to him. A son sent to Egypt under a family promise can be lost to an accident of memory. A son sent under an oath sworn in God's name is held by something the oath-takers cannot quietly set aside.

**The Exception Clause Is Jacob's Own**

*Unless you are totally overpowered* — the commentators gloss this as a general condition meaning unless you reach a situation in which you cannot uphold the oath. The clause is Jacob's addition, and it does two things. It makes the covenant realistic: ten men cannot guarantee an outcome against overwhelming force, and a father who demanded an unconditional guarantee would be demanding something impossible. And it narrows the oath to what the sons can actually control — their own willingness to return with Benjamin. Everything else is exempted in advance. The clause also becomes the hinge of verse 80, where the eldest invokes it to explain why he will not go home without his brother. Its function is to make the oath survivable. A bond with no exit would have been broken the moment the brothers were overwhelmed, and a broken oath is worse than no oath; the exception gives them a lawful way to fail without perjuring themselves. Jacob legislates for the possibility of defeat, and verse 80 shows the legislation used exactly as designed.

**"God Is a Witness to What We Say"**

The formula is one the Qur'an uses when a covenant is concluded between two parties who cannot otherwise enforce it. Moses speaks the same words to his future father-in-law when agreeing the term of service for marriage: *“˹Then˺ it is ˹settled˺ between you and I. Whichever term I fulfill, there will be no ˹further˺ obligation on me. And Allah is a Witness to what we say”* (28:28). In both cases the sentence closes a negotiation in which one party is leaving home and placing himself under another's authority. Jacob's use of it here is the chapter's way of recording that the oath is not a private family arrangement but a transaction with a divine counterparty who will remember it even if the sons do not.

**No Oath Was Taken the First Time**

The contrast with verses 11 to 14 is exact and deliberate. When the brothers asked for Joseph, they gave assurances in ordinary language: send him with us, we will guard him, we are his sincere well-wishers. No covenant was required, no oath was sworn, and no exception clause was attached. Jacob's grief in this chapter is not that he was deceived once; it is that the absence of formality then and its presence now measure how much has been lost. The verse therefore records a father's second act of trust conducted entirely differently from the first, and the difference is the cost of the first. The difference is procedural, and procedure is what a frightened father has left. In the first case he received a verbal assurance and no security; in the second he specifies the terms, invokes God as witness and excludes one circumstance that would release them. What he has learned between verse 12 and this one is not to trust less but to document more.

**Precaution and Trust Held Together**

Verse 64 ended with God as the best of keepers and the most merciful of the merciful. Verse 66 begins with an oath and ends with a witness. A reader might expect the trust to replace the precaution, since a man who has surrendered the matter to God need not bind his sons. The chapter refuses that simplification. Jacob does both. He places Benjamin in divine custody and he extracts a human covenant, and neither act is presented as undermining the other. The sūrah's treatment of means and reliance is consistent throughout: taking every available precaution is not a failure of trust, and trusting God is not a substitute for precautions. The chapter presents the combination without defending it. Jacob has just named God the best of keepers, and he now requires an oath from his sons as the condition of the journey; both statements sit in adjacent verses and neither is treated as inconsistent. What the sūrah offers is reliance that takes practical steps, not a theory of reliance that forbids them.

**The Oath Is the Measure Applied in Verse 80**

The covenant sworn here is what the brothers are judged against later. When Benjamin is detained and the eldest refuses to return to Canaan, his reason is stated in terms of the oath: *do you not know that your father took a solemn pledge from you in God's name, and how you failed before regarding Joseph* (12:80). The verse invokes both halves of Jacob's arrangement — the covenant about Benjamin and the earlier failure it was designed to prevent — and treats the oath as binding even when keeping it means staying in Egypt indefinitely. Verse 66 is therefore not a piece of family negotiation; it is the instrument the rest of the chapter uses to measure these men. Everything the brothers do afterwards is measured against it. Their return with provisions, their departure from Egypt without Benjamin, their eldest brother's decision to remain — each is weighed in the following verses against a covenant sworn before God in this one. The chapter supplies the standard first and the conduct second, so the reader can judge without being instructed to.

**Eleven Sons and One Father Left at Home**

The verse also records the household's composition at the moment Benjamin departs. Jacob keeps no son behind as a guarantee and no son stays voluntarily; the whole remaining family travels to Egypt except the father himself. The oath is therefore the only thing binding the travellers to a return, and the witness invoked is the only party who can hold them to it in a foreign country. That isolation is what gives the covenant its weight and what makes verse 80 so consequential. When the eldest declares he will not leave the land, the effect is not merely a delay; it removes one of the sureties from the arrangement and leaves Jacob with nine sons, one detained, and a father's oath that two parties now consider unfulfilled.
