## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:68

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain Reads the Sea Through Two Divine Names**

The account of Moses ends with two names: the Almighty and the Most Merciful. Al-ʿAzīz names power that cannot be overcome; al-Raḥīm names mercy that reaches servants with care. The pairing gives the correct interpretation of the sea. Israel was not rescued by chance, and Pharaoh was not drowned by uncontrolled force. The Lord acted with power and mercy together.

The names also answer the two groups differently. God's might overcame the army that treated itself as invincible. His mercy opened a path and delivered the vulnerable people who followed Moses. One act displays both names without contradiction. The power that judges oppression is the same power that protects those seeking freedom from it.

**Might Explains the Fall of the Tyrant**

Pharaoh's host possessed weapons, organization, and the confidence of a kingdom. The name Almighty places all of that within a greater scale. God's power does not need to compete with Pharaoh's army as an equal force. It governs the sea, the journey, and the end of the pursuit. The army's strength is real but derivative, while divine might is independent and decisive.

This name protects the account from a political reading in which one earthly power simply defeats another. The Israelites did not replace Pharaoh through superior military skill. The Lord exposed the limits of the ruler's claim. Al-ʿAzīz means that no tyrant can make his authority permanent by gathering enough force.

**Mercy Explains Why Warning Came Before Judgment**

The name Most Merciful recalls the long interval before the drowning. Moses was sent, signs were shown, Pharaoh was addressed, and the magicians were given an opportunity to recognize the truth. Judgment did not arrive without warning. Mercy had already appeared in the form of repeated invitations to stop the oppression and release Israel.

This prevents a distorted view of the final act. Mercy is not only the opening of the sea. It is also the time granted to Pharaoh and his people before the sea closed. A warning that exposes danger is a mercy because it makes repentance possible. When the tyrant uses the interval to deepen pursuit, the final judgment remains just rather than sudden cruelty.

**The Two Names Comfort the Messenger**

The refrain addresses the Prophet through your Lord. He is being reminded that the Lord who supported Moses remains the Lord who supports every messenger. The Prophet may grieve over people who reject guidance, but the outcome does not rest on his ability to control them. God's might is complete, and His mercy remains active in the sending of warnings.

This is consolation with a historical proof. Moses' mission looked vulnerable before Pharaoh and his army, yet the Lord brought it to a complete reversal. The Prophet's audience may be hostile, but hostility does not measure God's power. Nor does the refusal of many mean that divine mercy has ended. The refrain puts the messenger's burden inside God's character.

**Mercy Does Not Mean Approval of the Oppressor**

Some readers separate mercy from judgment as though a merciful Lord could not drown a tyrannical host. The pairing rejects that division. Mercy to the oppressed can require stopping the oppressor. Forgiveness offered to a repentant person does not mean that continuing injustice is tolerated. The Almighty can judge without ceasing to be Most Merciful.

The verse also keeps believers from imitating Pharaoh's harshness. They do not possess divine knowledge of every heart or the power to deliver final judgment. They may resist oppression and seek protection, but they must leave ultimate reckoning to God. The names teach both firmness toward injustice and humility about one's own role.

**The Refrain Makes History a Pattern of Divine Care**

The formula returns after each prophetic account because the events are not random. In every story, a community meets a messenger, a claim is tested, and God's power and mercy remain the final horizon. The details differ, but the Lord does not change. The refrain lets the reader see continuity across time without flattening the distinct moral lesson of each narrative.

After the sea, might is seen in the drowning of the pursuers and mercy in the rescue of Moses' companions. After another story, the balance may appear differently. The same names remain true because God is not divided between separate roles. His acts vary with circumstance, but His character is one.

**The Names Reorder the Meaning of Victory**

The story might be remembered as Israel's escape from Egyptian force. The refrain asks for a deeper account: the victory belongs to the Almighty, and it is experienced as mercy by those He saves. This prevents national pride from replacing gratitude. The rescued people are not invited to worship their own survival or the strength of their leader.

A believer can celebrate deliverance while remembering its source. The sea did not obey a tribe, and history did not turn because Pharaoh was simply unlucky. God acted as the Lord who is powerful enough to judge and merciful enough to save. That is the truth the repeated line preserves after the dramatic event has passed.

The names also teach the rescued people how to remember their enemy. They need not glorify Pharaoh's strength or deny its former danger. They can describe it accurately and still know that the Almighty had the final word.

Mercy lets them remember without being ruled by fear; might lets them resist without imagining that their own strength saved them. Both names turn memory into gratitude rather than boasting. The rescued community remembers power without worshipping power. It remembers mercy without treating mercy as weakness. The names teach survivors to resist both despair before danger and arrogance after danger has passed.
