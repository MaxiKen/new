## Sūrah an-Naml 27:30

> It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sender Is Named Before the Content**

She identifies the author first, then quotes. That order tells her council how to weigh what follows: this is not an anonymous threat or a rumour but a communication from a known king, and he has signed it.

Naming the sender was also a formal requirement of ancient Near Eastern correspondence, where letters conventionally opened with the identification of the writer rather than the recipient. What is notable is how little else she supplies. No characterisation of Sulaymān, no assessment of his strength, no reminder to the court of who he is. She gives them the name and the words and lets them draw conclusions. For a ruler about to ask her council's opinion, withholding her own framing is the correct procedure, and it matches what she does in the verses following, where she asks for advice before stating any position of her own.

**The Only Basmalah Inside a Sūrah**

The formula quoted here — *bismi Allāh al-raḥmān al-raḥīm* — opens every chapter of the Qur'an but one. This verse is the single place in the entire Book where it appears in full in the middle of a chapter, as part of the running text.

The distinction is exact and worth stating carefully. The formula's components appear elsewhere, and other verses mention the name of God, but the complete phrase occurs within a sūrah only here. That uniqueness has a consequence for recitation: a reader reciting an-Naml pronounces the basmalah twice, once at the chapter's head and once inside it, and the second time it is quoted speech rather than liturgical opening. The verse thus does something no other verse does — it shows the formula being used, by a prophet, in a real document, for a practical purpose. What the Qur'an's chapter-headings do ritually, Sulaymān's letter does diplomatically.

**A Letter to an Idolater Begins With the Name of God**

The recipient of this letter prostrates to the sun. Sulaymān knows it; the hoopoe told him. He opens by invoking the God she does not acknowledge, and he does it before saying anything else.

The move is not tactless, and it is not a failure to consider his audience. It establishes the frame of the entire communication in five words. Whatever the letter goes on to demand, the demand is made in the name of a specific reality, and the writer is not concealing his premise to make his request more palatable. The alternative — an opening in Sulaymān's own name and authority — would have turned the letter into an assertion of one king's will over another's, which is exactly what he is not claiming. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the letter's contents were among the possible reasons she called it noble, and this opening is the first of them. It contains no boast about the sender, no threat, and no reference to his forces.

**Two Names of Mercy, Chosen Over Every Alternative**

The formula names God twice by mercy: *al-raḥmān* and *al-raḥīm*, both from the root *r-ḥ-m*. Arabic grammarians distinguish them by form. *Raḥmān* is an intensive pattern denoting mercy in its fullest extent, understood as the mercy that covers all creation without distinction; *raḥīm* is a pattern denoting a settled, continuing quality, understood as the mercy specifically directed toward those who turn toward it.

That the letter opens with these two and not with, say, the Mighty or the Overpowering is a substantive choice. Sulaymān could have invoked attributes suited to a demand from a superior power. He invokes the two that describe God's disposition toward creatures who have not yet submitted — which is the queen's exact position as she reads it. The sequence also matters: the universal mercy is named first. Before she is told anything about what she must do, she is told what the one making the claim on her is like.

**A Letter That Becomes Scripture**

There is a curious layering in this verse. A queen is reading aloud, to her council, a document written by a prophet, and the Qur'an quotes her quoting him. Three voices are stacked, and the innermost of them is a formula that Muslims would later recite at the start of meals, journeys and every chapter of their scripture.

The layering explains why the verse carries the weight it does in Islamic practice. Sulaymān's letter became the model for prophetic correspondence: the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have sent letters to foreign rulers opening with the same formula, and the practice of beginning any written communication with the basmalah is traced to this precedent. A form used for a diplomatic dispatch three thousand years ago, preserved in a quotation inside a quotation, became the standard opening of Muslim writing. The letter's actual demand follows in the next verse, and it is two sentences long.

**Short Enough to Be Read Aloud Once**

The letter, quoted in full across this verse and the next, runs to roughly a dozen words of Arabic. It contains an invocation, a prohibition and a command. There is no preamble, no recitation of the sender's titles, no account of his forces, and no argument.

Ancient royal correspondence was rarely so spare. Letters between courts typically opened with elaborate greetings and lists of the writer's dominions, precisely because the form was part of the message. Sulaymān's brevity is therefore itself a statement: a man who needed to impress would have written more. The compression also makes the letter usable in the way the narrative requires. She reads it once to her assembled council, and every word is retained; a longer document would have needed summarising, and a summary is an interpretation. What her chiefs hear is exactly what Sulaymān wrote, which is why the deliberation that follows is a genuine one. The demand itself, two clauses long, comes in the following verse.
