## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:28

> And it is Allah’s Will to lighten your burdens, for humankind was created weak.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Will to Lighten**

After saying that Allah wills to make things clear and guide to the noble ways and turn to you in mercy (4:26), and that those who follow desires wish you to swerve greatly (4:27), the sūrah adds a third divine will: *yurīdu Allāhu an yukhaffifa ʿankum* — and it is Allah’s Will to lighten your burdens. The verb *yukhaffif* — to lighten, to make less heavy — was understood as easing the legal and moral load to what people can bear, without removing the limits that protect them. The context shows where that easing appears. The prohibition on marrying former wives of fathers was softened with *except what was done previously* (4:22); combining two sisters carries the same exception (4:23); the field lawful beyond the prohibited degrees is opened widely when sought with wealth in chastity (4:24); the bar of inability to afford a free believing wife is eased by permission to marry a believing bondwoman with permission and fair dowry (4:25); and the set dowry itself is made adjustable by mutual graciousness (4:24). Each of those details is a lightening inside a limit. The verse gathers them and says the pattern is intentional: the limits are fixed, but the way of keeping them is made lighter.

**For Humankind Was Created Weak**

The reason given — *wa khuliqa al-insānu ḍaʿīfan* — for humankind was created weak — was read as comprehensive. Weakness covers several domains that the sūrah has touched. Physically, people need shelter and maintenance, which marriage provides when it is *muḥṣin* — protective. Sexually, desire is strong and persistent, which is why unlawful intercourse is called *fāḥishah* and why chastity is protected by requiring four witnesses and by disciplining the pair and then relieving them when they mend (4:15–16). Intellectually, people know the right but act ignorantly under impulse, which is why repentance is accepted for those who act in ignorance and turn soon (4:17) and not for those who persist until death is present (4:18). Socially, kin groups press individuals to favour custom over revelation, as with inheriting women against their will (4:19) and reclaiming dowries (4:20–21). To be created weak in all these directions is not an insult; it is an explanation that makes easing a mercy rather than an indulgence. The sūrah elsewhere says *“Allah does not charge a soul beyond what it can bear”* (2:286), and this verse is the social law counterpart: the soul that is weak is not charged with what would crush it.

**How Lightening Works in This Sūrah**

Lightening does not mean erasing. The orphan’s wealth must still be returned with witnesses and reckoning (4:6), but the guardian who is well-off is asked to abstain while the poor guardian may take what is fair, and the test for *rushd* — sound judgment — allows return when readiness appears rather than at a single arbitrary age (4:6). The inheritance shares remain fixed — half, quarter, eighth, sixth, third, two-thirds — after debts and bequests (4:11–12), but bequests themselves remain permitted up to one-third and mutual graciousness about dowries is allowed (4:24). Prohibited degrees are fixed, but the past is pardoned and the lawful field beyond is broad. Fornication is not permitted, but marriage is made reachable even where means fail, with the concession for the believing bondwoman where fear of sin is real and patience still named as better (4:25). In each case the law keeps its bar and widens its door, which is exactly what lightening inside limits looks like. The sūrah therefore answers the deviators’ claim that limits are harsh (4:27) not by removing limits but by showing how many lawful paths exist to live well inside them.

**Weakness and the Need for Turn and Clarity**

The three divine wills of verses 26 through 28 belong together: to make clear, to guide to the ways before and turn toward you, and to lighten because you are weak. Without clarity, weakness would be left to guess; without turning, weakness would be left to despair; without lightening, weakness would be left to break. The sūrah has already supplied all three: clarity in the detailed lists of who may and may not be married, guidance in the precedent of noble ways, and turning in the acceptance of soon repentance (4:17) and in the command to relieve those who amend (4:16). Lightening completes the set by shaping the law so that the weak can actually walk it. Al-Rāzī notes that the verse’s placement after the concession for bondwomen is deliberate, because that concession is the clearest instance of lessening a burden without surrendering the value — chastity — the burden protects. The same logic extends to the soon-closing warnings about devouring wealth illegally (4:29) and about major sins (4:31): the sūrah will threaten Fire for deliberate overstep, but it will also promise absolution of lesser faults for those who avoid the great ones, which is another lightening for the weak who will inevitably stumble in small ways.

**A Word That Invites Reliance**

The statement that humankind was created weak was heard by early commentators as both realism and invitation. Realism, because it tells the believer not to rely on his own strength to keep limits he cannot keep alone; invitation, because it points him to the One who wills to lighten and to turn toward him. The verse therefore draws the same response as the sūrah’s opening — *“be mindful of your Lord who created you from a single soul”* (4:1) — where creation itself grounds duty. Here creation grounds dependence: the same Creator who spread countless men and women from one soul knows the weakness of each and eases the path He asks them to walk. That easing does not remove accountability; it makes accountability bearable. A guardian can keep an orphan’s wealth faithfully when the rule allows fair provision and requires witnesses (4:6); an heir can divide a large estate justly when the fractions are fixed and the bequest is limited; a man of small means can marry lawfully when a believing bondwoman is permitted with conditions, or can choose patience if he can bear it (4:25). Each is a burden lightened, not lifted, so that weakness is neither ignored nor indulged, but supported. In that support the sūrah’s purpose becomes visible: a law that is clear, a way that is noble, a turn that is gracious, and a load that the weak can carry.
