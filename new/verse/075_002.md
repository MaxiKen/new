## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:2

> And I do swear by the selfreproaching soul!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Oath, and a Stranger Object**

The first verse swore by a Day; this one swears by a soul. The pairing is the sūrah's first piece of reasoning, and it is compressed into a single conjunction. God swears by the Day of Resurrection and by the self-reproaching soul because the two perform the same work on different scales: each one summons a person, exposes what he has done, and refuses to let him plead ignorance. The Day does that once, publicly, at the end. The soul does it continuously, privately, throughout a life. An oath by such a soul is an oath by the faculty in a human being that already knows the difference between what he did and what he should have done, and that will not stop saying so. The object of the oath is therefore not a rarefied spiritual state but an ordinary one, familiar to anyone who has ever regretted something before anyone else found out.

**Three Souls, in Ascending Order**

The Qur'an names three conditions of the soul, and this verse supplies the middle one. The lowest is the soul that presses a person toward wrongdoing, named in the confession of the Egyptian minister's wife: *“And I do not seek to free myself from blame, for indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil, except those shown mercy by my Lord.”* (12:53). The highest is the soul addressed at the end of the Fajr sūrah: *“O tranquil soul! Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺.”* (89:27–28). Between them sits *al-nafs al-lawwāmah*, the blaming soul, which has not arrived at peace but has stopped being at ease with itself. The ordering matters: the middle term is not a stage one passes through and leaves behind so much as a condition of the struggle, the state of a person still in the fight. To swear by it is to swear by the unfinished, arguing, self-accusing human being rather than by the saint.

**What Makes the Blaming Soul Worth Swearing By**

The word *lawwāmah* is an intensive form: one who blames repeatedly, who returns to the charge. That persistence is the property being invoked. A single pang of regret could be dismissed as mood; a faculty that will not stop prosecuting is evidence of a moral constitution, of a standard inside the person that he did not install and cannot silence. The Qur'an locates this inward witness very precisely: *“Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them, and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein.”* (50:16). The soul's self-accusation is thus not a private psychological event only. It is the internal counterpart of the accounting that is coming, which is why one report has every soul, pious or profligate, reproaching itself on that Day — the good wondering why it did not do more, the wicked wishing it had stopped. On the Day the sūrah's first verse swears by, the prosecution that began inside a person simply continues in the open.

**The Minority Reading: an Oath Refused**

Because the verse opens with the same particle *lā* as v. 1, a minority of commentators read it as real negation: “I do *not* swear by the blaming soul.” Their ground is that an oath is normally sworn by something great — God, or a sign of His — and a human soul, however scrupulous, is not in that class. On this reading the sense is carried by what is suppressed, as though the text said: the matter is not as you suppose; I swear by the Day of Resurrection, and I do not need to swear by the blaming soul. The majority answer is that v. 1 and v. 2 stand or fall together, since the same particle introduces both, and a reading that turns the second into a denial while the first remains an oath has to explain the inconsistency. The majority reading is stronger: it keeps the parallel intact and gives the sūrah two witnesses instead of one.

**A Second Etymology: the Restless Soul**

An alternative derivation reads *lawwāmah* not from blame but from *talawwum*, constant turning, and takes the soul as fickle — happy then sad, obedient then heedless, never settled in one state. Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyyah argued for this sense in his book on the soul. The strength of the reading is that it fits what ordinary observation shows, a self that cannot hold a mood or a resolution for long. Its weakness is that the Qur'an's own vocabulary for blame is built on the same root, and the intensive form points to an agent doing the blaming rather than to instability. Both readings converge on a single picture of the human interior as a place of argument. The difference is whether the quarrel is with the self's conduct or with its own inconsistency, and the first is the more natural meaning of a word whose root appears throughout the Qur'an in the sense of censure.

**The Soul Sworn By in Another Sūrah**

The same oath-object appears in a sūrah that builds its whole argument on it: *“And by the soul and ˹the One˹ Who fashioned it, then with ˹the knowledge of˺ right and wrong inspired it!”* (91:7–8). There the soul is sworn by precisely as a thing made and then informed, which is what gives its self-reproach its authority: the standard it appeals to was put there by the One who fashioned it. A person who blames himself is not inventing the yardstick he is measured against. That is why the blaming soul can be joined, in a single oath, to the Day of Resurrection, and why the sūrah will later say that on that Day a man becomes a witness against himself despite the excuses he offers.
