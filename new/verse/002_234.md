## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:234

> As for those of you who die and leave widows behind, let them observe a waiting period of four months and ten days. When they have reached the end of this period, then you are not accountable for what they decide for themselves in a reasonable manner. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Widow's Term Fixed**

The family code turns from the divorced woman to the widow, and its first act is arithmetic: four months and ten days. The term, like the divorced woman's qurūʾ-count, serves the womb's evidentiary function — the community must know whether a new life was begun before death — but it runs longer, and the commentators note the reason pregnancy is handled separately: a widow who is pregnant waits until delivery, the two reckonings compared and the longer one observed, a rule the sūrah of Divorce states plainly — *As for those who are pregnant, their waiting period ends with delivery* (65:4). The ten extra days beyond the four lunar months have been read as completing the count with certainty, since lunar months vary between twenty-nine and thirty days and the added ten absorb the shortfall of four short months. The precision matters: a fixed term replaces guesswork, and a fixed term also ends — which, for a woman whom custom had buried alongside her husband socially, is the verse's real gift.

**The Pre-Islamic Year of Ashes**

The reform is sharpest against its background. In pre-Islamic practice, as the narrations collected by Ibn Kathīr describe, the widow's mourning ran a full year: she wore her worst clothes, avoided grooming and bathing, and remained in seclusion. At the year's end she emerged and marked the closure with rituals whose significance was already obscure to later generations — casting away a piece of dung and marking her body against an animal's hide. Four months and ten days, against twelve months of squalor, is a reduction by nearly two-thirds, and it converts mourning from a state of ritual pollution into a bounded observance with a defined end. The dung-throwing is usually explained as a gesture of contempt for the year itself — *this year is of no more worth to me than this dung* — which means the closing rite already carried the sense the Qur'anic term institutionalizes: mourning is real, but it is finite, and life resumes.

**Mourning and Waiting: Two Rules, One Term**

The prophetic tradition ties the two obligations together with a boundary that clarifies both. Zaynab bint Abī Salamah narrates that when news of Abū Sufyān's death reached Medina, Umm Ḥabībah asked for perfume on the third day and scented her cheeks and forearms, saying she had no need of it except that she heard the Prophet say: it is not lawful for a woman who believes in God and the Last Day to mourn any deceased person more than three days — except a husband, for four months and ten days (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). The same wording is reported from Zaynab bint Jaḥsh at her brother's death, and from Umm ʿAṭiyyah, who added that mourners were barred from kohl, perfume, and dyed clothing during the term. The three-day ceiling for every other death throws the widow's four-months-and-ten into relief: it is not grief regulation — grief is permitted its three days for anyone — but a legal status attached to the marriage bond itself, combining the womb's clarification with the husband's honor.

**Seclusion or Celibacy: The Jurists' Split**

What the widow must actually do during the term divided the early jurists. One reading, supported by narrations restricting the widow's use of perfume, ornaments, and dyed clothes and keeping her in her home, treats *let them wait by themselves* as prescribing cloistering: she stays in the marital home, unadorned, for the duration. The exegetes record this as the position grounded in those aḥādīth. The other reading, associated with prominent early authorities including Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, takes the waiting to mean simply refraining from marriage: the term binds her eligibility, not her movements, and she goes about her life without adorning herself for suitors. The practical difference is considerable — confinement versus celibacy — and the majority of the legal schools settled between the poles, requiring the widow's nights in her late husband's home while permitting her daytime business. The disagreement itself testifies to what the verse's wording leaves open: it fixes the term with precision and the conduct with a single phrase.

**What They Decide for Themselves**

The verse's second half hands the widow her future: *then you are not accountable for what they decide for themselves in a reasonable manner*. The addressees who are not accountable are, on the common reading, the community and its authorities — and behind the disclaimer stands the pre-Islamic reality in which a widow's remarriage was controlled by her husband's heirs, who could marry her off, block her, or inherit her along with the estate. The Qur'anic rule empties their ledger: when the term ends, whatever honorable course she chooses — remarriage or continued singleness, adornment or plainness — is hers, and no one answers for it. The qualifier *in a reasonable manner* — bi'l-maʿrūf, the code's recurring standard — bounds the freedom not by male guardianship but by the honorable itself: her decisions are free and they are measured by the community's recognized good.

**The Aware**

The closing name fits the legislation's private dimension. The widow's term is observed largely out of public sight — the counting of days, the restraint from adornment, the decisions made at the term's end about her own future — and the verse stations God's awareness over all of it: *Allah is All-Aware of what you do*. Khabīr, the name used here, carries the sense of expertise and intimate acquaintance with things as they are performed, not merely with their outcomes; the exegetes connect it elsewhere to God's acquaintance with the inward of acts. For the widow it is a double word: her unwatched fidelity is watched, and the community's temptation to overreach her freedom — to extend the seclusion past its term, to treat her honorable decisions as accountable after all — is watched too. The term is fixed; the awareness is total; and between them the widow's four months and ten days become the code's clearest statement that waiting is not erasure.
