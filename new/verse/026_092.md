## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:92

> And it will be said to them, “Where are those you used to worship

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Judgment Begins With a Question**

The condemned are asked where those they used to worship are. The question is not for information. God does not need to locate the idols, and the speakers already know that the objects have no power. The interrogation exposes the absence of the supposed gods at the moment their worshippers most need them.

A question can reveal a false claim more sharply than a direct statement. During life, the idolaters said that their objects deserved devotion. On the Day of judgment, they must face the objects' disappearance as sources of help. The question returns their own practice to them and makes them confront what they placed at the center of hope.

**Where Is a Worshipped Object When Judgment Arrives?**

The verse asks where the objects are, not where the people kept them or how they decorated them. Their physical location is not the real issue. The issue is their absence from authority. They are not present as protectors, advocates, or lords. The community's ceremonies could make them appear important, but ceremony cannot create a power that stands before judgment.

That absence reveals the difference between a symbol and a god. A symbol can remain as an object of memory, while a god must possess actual authority over the worshipper's fate. The idols fail the second test. They were addressed in life, but they cannot answer the question placed before them now.

**The Past Tense Makes Habit Accountable**

The wording refers to those they used to worship. The past tense gathers their former life into one phrase. Their worship was not an isolated mistake that can be separated from their identity. It was a repeated practice, sustained long enough to become the way they understood dependence and security.

Yet the past tense also shows that the practice has ended. The idols are no longer treated as objects of worship in any meaningful sense. Their former worshippers have been brought to a scene where the illusion cannot continue. A habit that seemed permanent in the world has become an accusation in the Hereafter.

**The Question Addresses the Whole Group**

The verse speaks to them collectively. False worship may be practiced by individuals, but it also gains strength from crowds, families, institutions, and public agreement. The question therefore reaches the social world that protected the idols. What did the community gather around, and what remains when the gathering stands before God?

Collective responsibility does not erase individual responsibility. Each person chose how to respond, but the group also made false worship normal and dissent costly. The public question strips away the comfort of saying that everyone did the same. A crowd can make an error feel safe without making it true.

**Where Is the Promise of Protection?**

Worshippers often remain attached to false objects because they expect protection, blessing, or mediation. The verse does not repeat every promise they associated with the idols. It asks one short question that includes them all. If the object was a protector, where is its protection now? If it was an advocate, where is its advocacy?

The absence is not a surprise to God. It is a demonstration for the worshippers. They are made to see that their expectations rested on an object incapable of fulfilling them. The question is painful because the answer is visible in the silence. No inherited title can turn a created thing into a source of salvation.

**A Parallel Exposes the Same Absence**

The Qur'an describes those invoked besides God as unable to create and as themselves created in 16:20–21. That parallel gives the present question a clear theological background. An object that did not create life cannot be expected to control the final destiny of life. Its worshippers may grant it a name, but they cannot grant it creative or saving power.

The parallel also shows why the question is directed at worshippers rather than at the idols. The idols have no capacity to answer. The moral conversation belongs to the people who chose them. The question calls them to account for a dependence that had no real object behind it.

**The Interrogation Removes the Comfort of Distance**

People can speak about idolatry as a theory and avoid seeing its consequences in their own lives. The question removes that distance. It does not ask what some other society worshipped. It asks those people about what they themselves used to worship. Their own history becomes the evidence.

This has a present lesson without reducing the verse to a slogan. Modern people may reject statues while giving ultimate loyalty to wealth, leaders, nations, or desire. The question can still be heard: where is the thing you trust as a final lord when you stand before God? A created support cannot answer for the allegiance given to it.

**The Broken Sentence Holds the Hearer in Suspense**

The translation ends with an open quotation because the question continues. The pause is rhetorically powerful. The audience is made to wait before hearing the test that will be applied to the absent objects. That waiting mirrors the exposure of the condemned, who cannot escape the subject even though the answer is already apparent.

The verse stands complete as an indictment of misplaced worship. It turns a former boast into a question of absence. The worshippers once gathered around their objects; now the objects cannot stand beside them as powers. The Lord's interrogation has already shown that false worship leaves its adherents alone at the moment of final need. The question does not create their helplessness; it reveals the helplessness that had always been there.
