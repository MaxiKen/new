## Sūrah an-Naml 27:73

> Surely your Lord is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most of them are ungrateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bounty Stated Immediately After a Threat**

The previous verse told the mockers that part of what they were hurrying toward might be right behind them. This one says God is bountiful to mankind. The juxtaposition is abrupt and it is the point.

What connects the two is the interval. If some portion of the reckoning is close and has not yet arrived, then the time still available is itself the bounty being described. The Qur'an makes this link explicit elsewhere, saying that if God were to take people to task for what they have earned He would not leave a creature on the earth, and that He defers them to a stated term. Read against verse 72, this verse identifies the delay as generosity rather than as delay. The threat and the favour are the same fact described from two sides.

**"Toward Mankind" — Not Toward the Believers**

The recipient named is *al-nās*, humanity at large. Not the faithful, not the grateful, not those who acknowledge Him. The bounty is described as reaching everyone, including the people who have just been mocking the promise.

That universality is a settled feature of how the Qur'an describes divine provision. Rain falls on the fields of those who deny; the man who sets up partners eats from gardens that God's water grew; the interval before the reckoning is given to the obstinate and the sincere alike. The chapter's fourth verse had God Himself named as the one who made evil deeds attractive to those who deny the Hereafter, which is a statement of how the moral order works rather than an exception to it — even that is a law operating within a world where the deniers are fed. Two divine names in the chapter's first half, Self-Sufficient and Most Generous, were paired for exactly this reason: He does not need the response and gives anyway.

**Ingratitude as the Standard Condition**

The second half is a demographic claim: *wa lākinna aktharahum lā yashkurūn* — but most of them are not grateful. The Qur'an repeats this observation in a dozen places, always with the same word for the majority.

The consistency indicates it is meant as a description rather than a complaint. What makes ingratitude the default is not wickedness but familiarity: a benefit that arrives every day stops registering as a benefit, and nothing in ordinary experience corrects the drift. The chapter has already shown the alternative in a man far better positioned to be ungrateful than most. Sulaymān, given a share of all things, asked to be marshalled into thankfulness, as though gratitude were a formation that breaks without discipline. He identified in himself exactly the tendency this verse names in the majority, and he treated it as something requiring active management rather than assuming his own good character would supply it.

**Why Ingratitude Is Grouped With Disbelief**

The root of *yashkurūn* is *sh-k-r*, thanks, and its opposite in the Qur'an is *kufr* — the same word the Book uses for disbelief. The overlap is not loose usage.

*Kufr* at root means to cover over, and the image behind it is concealment of something that is there. A farmer covering a seed with soil uses the same root in classical Arabic. Applied to gifts, it names the act of covering over a benefit received so that it no longer shows; applied to God, it names covering over Him. Sulaymān's speech at the arrival of the throne used both terms in a single sentence, asking whether he would be grateful or *akfur*. The chapter therefore has a precise vocabulary at work: the deniers of verse 4 and the ungrateful of this verse are described with one root, because concealing a giver and concealing a gift are the same operation performed on different objects.

**A Verse Placed Between Warning and Surveillance**

Look at the neighbours. Verse 72 warns that the reckoning may be near. Verse 74 says God knows what their hearts conceal and what they disclose. Between the threat and the surveillance sits a statement about generosity.

The placement prevents the passage from reading as intimidation. A God who watches and warns and gives nothing would produce fear; the sequence here inserts the bounty in the middle, so that the knowing and the warning are attributes of someone already described as generous to the very people being warned. This is the chapter's method in miniature, and it matches what it did at the start: verse 2 called the Book guidance and good news, and only in verse 4 turned to those who refuse. Threat is never the first thing said in this chapter, and it is never the only thing said.

**Bounty Described as Continuous**

The construction is *la-dhū faḍlin ʿalā al-nās* — possessed of bounty toward mankind, with the emphatic particle attached. It is a description of a standing characteristic rather than a report of particular gifts.

The Qur'an uses the same phrase in a handful of other places, always in the same form, and always without listing what the bounty consists of. That vagueness is functional. A list would invite comparison and complaint, since people's portions differ enormously and always have. What the phrase asserts instead is the disposition behind whatever any given person has received, which is a claim that survives the unevenness of the distribution. The chapter has already built the specifics in its questions section: rain, gardens, rivers, mountains, guidance through darkness, an answer to the desperate. This verse names the source of all of it in four words and moves on to the response it does not receive. Stated this way, the claim can be tested against a person's own experience rather than only against the histories the chapter has recounted.
