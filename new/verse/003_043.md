## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:43

> O Mary! Be devout to your Lord, prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command That Follows the Honour**

Having told her that she has been chosen, the verse immediately tells her what to do. The order is deliberate and it recurs throughout the Book: a station conferred by God is accompanied by an obligation, and the greater the selection the more definite the instruction.

There is no interval in which Mary is invited to enjoy her position. The verbs arrive one after another — be devout, prostrate, bow — and they are all imperatives addressed to the same person who was just told that she stands above the women of her era. The verse therefore corrects two opposite mistakes at once: the mistake of thinking that honour removes obligation, and the mistake of thinking that worship earns honour. The honour is given; the worship is the occupation of one who has received it.

**Qānitah: Devotion as a Posture**

The command "be devout to your Lord" uses a word whose feminine form is *qānitah*, from the root that describes standing, constancy, silence, and settled conformity. The Book uses the same word of devout women and men in a verse that lists their qualities together (33:35), and of the obedient wife whose devotion is part of her household's good order (4:34).

The word's physical core is worth holding on to. A *qānit* is not a person who feels devotion on a particular afternoon; he or she is a person who has taken up a position and remains in it. The command given to Mary is therefore a description of a life's shape rather than an instruction for a moment, and it is the same instruction the Book gives to the community in another passage about worship.

**Prostration and Bowing Named in Order**

The verse then names two specific postures, prostration first and bowing second. The order is interesting because the prostration is the lower of the two, and elsewhere the Book lists bowing before prostration when it describes the physical vocabulary of worship: bow down, prostrate yourselves, worship your Lord (22:77).

The variation suggests the order of the two words is not a fixed ladder but a description of the whole range of worship, from the standing bow to the full prostration in which the face is on the ground. What Mary is commanded is participation in the full practice, and the sūrah is careful to give the practice to a woman without any indication that her part in it is smaller.

**"Along With Those Who Bow Down"**

The last clause puts Mary inside a congregation, and this is the verse's most socially significant phrase. She is not told to worship alone in her chamber, although her life until now has been conducted in a private sanctuary, and she is not given a personal devotional method of her own.

The command is to join a body of worshippers. The Book uses a similar formulation when it commands the Israelites to bow with those who bow down (2:43), and the repetition of the formula for a woman in a sanctuary shows how the Book conceives of devotion: an individual practice embedded in a community's practice. A person's worship is not measured by its originality, and the reference point for it is the community that has been worshipping the same way.

**Worship Commanded of a Woman Without Qualification**

The Book's address to Mary in this verse uses the same imperatives that it uses for men, and it does not surround them with qualifications about her position, her household, or her sex. The one difference the Arabic marks is grammatical: the verbs are given in their feminine forms.

That grammatical distinction is a precision rather than a limitation. The Book is speaking to a woman and says so in its grammar, and the commands themselves are identical to those given to the community at large. A reader who takes this verse as the Book's instruction to a woman specially chosen will find in it the same practice required of every believer, which is how the sūrah's narration of this household has operated from the beginning.

**Why the Instruction Follows the Announcement**

The sequence — election, then command — explains the shape of the chapter's ethics. God chooses whom He wills, and the chosen are then given work to do. The verses before this one described a family's vows and prayers; the verse after them describes a woman bowing in a congregation.

Nothing in that arrangement permits the reader to decide that he has been chosen and may therefore relax. The only evidence of election available to a person is the practice commanded in the verse: devotion, prostration, and joining the community at prayer. That is what the sūrah now asks of the woman it has just praised, and it asks the same of whoever recites it.

**The Discipline of a Life in the Sanctuary**

Read in the setting the narrative has established, the command describes years of routine. Mary was placed in the sanctuary as a child, supplied with provision, taught, and cared for; the practice commanded shaped her days, with the bow and the prostration repeated at fixed times and the community's worship as the pattern she followed.

A life of that kind is not dramatic, and the Book presents it without embellishment. What the sūrah gives her is a routine, and the extraordinary events of her story — an announcement from angels, a child without a father — occur inside a life that had been prepared by ordinary worship. A reader looking for the conditions in which revelation visits a person is being pointed at the routine rather than at the exception.

**The Congregation the Command Assumes**

The instruction to bow with those who bow carries a setting inside it. Mary is told to join a body of worshippers rather than to perform an act on her own, and the Book uses the same phrase when it commands the community to bow down with those who bow down (2:43). The sanctuary in which she had been placed was served by a company of men, and the command places her among them in the act of worship.

The Book describes the House that Abraham and Ishmael were told to purify as a place for those who circle it, meditate in it, and bow and prostrate themselves in prayer (2:125). The two postures named in this verse therefore belong to a form of worship older than the community that reads the passage, and the command given to Mary is phrased in its vocabulary. Nothing in the sentence restricts the instruction by gender or by age. What it asks for is participation in the worship of the house where she had been dedicated, and the sūrah gives the command as the direct answer to the announcement of her election.
