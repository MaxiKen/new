## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:56

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I have been forbidden to worship those you invoke besides Allah.” Say, “I will not follow your desires, for I then would certainly be astray and not one of those ˹rightly˺ guided.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prohibition Stated in the First Person**

The verse begins with the Prophet announcing that he has been forbidden, and the passive construction carries the authority without requiring him to name himself as the source of the rule. The prohibition concerns the objects the Makkans invoke, which the sūrah has already examined from several angles: they can neither harm nor benefit (5:76), they cannot respond or protect (20:89), and they are created beings like the people who worship them (7:194).

Making the announcement in the first person is a public act of separation. The Prophet is not reporting a difference of opinion between himself and his people; he is declaring a boundary that has been drawn for him by someone else, which leaves no room for negotiation and no room for the political compromise the Makkans had been seeking.

**What "I Have Been Forbidden" Does Not Say**

The formulation is worth noticing for what it declines to claim. The Prophet does not say that he finds their gods unworthy through his own reasoning, though the sūrah has supplied the reasoning, and he does not say that the matter is open to discussion. He states the instruction and leaves the audience with the source of the rule, which is the pattern the Book follows in every dispute between a messenger and his people.

The Book uses the same construction elsewhere for the community, where the believers are forbidden certain things by the same authority. The effect is to keep the question where it belongs, since a prohibition from God cannot be renegotiated by the people it inconveniences and cannot be weakened by the fact that a majority disagrees with it.

**The Second Command and the Word "Desires"**

The verse then adds a separate instruction: I will not follow your desires. The Arabic word describes inclination and appetite rather than reasoned preference, and the Book uses it for the impulses that pull a person away from a considered decision. The same warning is given to the Prophet repeatedly, including where the Book tells him not to follow the desires of the People of the Book (5:48–49) and where it warns him against their wish that he compromise (17:73).

The word also explains what the Makkan position actually amounted to. They were not offering an alternative account of God that could be examined in the light of argument. They were asking the Prophet to accommodate the practices they were attached to, and the verse names the attachment for what it is.

**The Two Results of Following Desire**

The verse then states the consequence of the decision the Prophet refuses to make: for then I would certainly be astray and not one of those rightly guided. He is not describing a possible lapse in his own function as a messenger; he is describing what happens when a person with knowledge allows appetite to set the course. The Book states the same principle for all people when it describes those who take their own desire as a god and are then left to stray with their hearing sealed and a cover over their sight (45:23).

The language of straying and guidance returns here because the sūrah has just used it for the two roads (6:55). A person who follows desire has left the road, and the Book does not soften the description by suggesting that departure can be partial.

**The Political Shape of the Refusal**

The Makkans had asked for a reciprocal recognition: their gods acknowledged by the Prophet, and his God acknowledged by them. The sūrah records the suggestion in other places, where they sought a compromise and were told that the Prophet's position could not be traded in that market. This verse's refusal is the same answer in two sentences, addressed to the whole city.

The refusal also had consequences the audience would have understood. A public declaration of this kind closed the path of tribal reconciliation and made the Prophet's position an identifiable cause, which is precisely why the sūrah puts the declaration in his mouth rather than leaving it as a private conviction.

**What the Two Sayings Establish**

The pair of commands establishes two facts about the Prophet that the rest of the sūrah will build on. He does not worship what his people worship, and he does not accept their criteria for judging religious questions. Both statements are given as instructions rather than as preferences, and both remove the possibility that the message could be adjusted to fit the audience.

The next verses continue with the Prophet's declarations about the proof he stands on and the punishment his people were demanding he hasten. The sūrah is putting the whole of his position on the record in his own voice, so that no one could claim afterward that the terms had been left vague.

The Book shows what the compromise would have looked like in other passages, and none of them make pleasant reading. The Prophet is told that his opponents wished him to accommodate them so that they would accommodate him (68:9), and the sūrah of al-Isrāʾ reports that they nearly lured him from what had been revealed to him, in the hope that he would attribute something to God that had not come from Him (17:73), a scheme the Book says would have brought him a double punishment in this life and the next (17:75). The same refusal is stated in the shortest terms in the sūrah of the Disbelievers, which ends the matter with the declaration that each party has its own way (109:6).
