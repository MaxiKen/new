# Sūrah an-Naṣr (Chapter 110) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah an-Naṣr 110:1

> When Allah’s ˹ultimate˺ help comes and the victory ˹over Mecca is achieved˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Sentence Stretched Across Three Verses**

Nothing in this chapter stands by itself. The Arabic opens with *idhā*, the particle that sets a condition for what will come, and the whole sūrah runs as a single period: when help arrives and the crowds are seen, then praise and ask forgiveness. This verse supplies only the opening condition. It carries no command and no conclusion; it is the first half of a sentence whose meaning is completed two verses later.

That shape decides how it should be read. *Idhā* is not "if." Where the other conditional particle, *in*, leaves the outcome genuinely open, *idhā* introduces what is expected to arrive — the particle of the assured future. The help is not offered as a contingency but announced as an event on its way, and the only open question is what the one who receives it will do with it. Taken in isolation the verse looks like a promise of victory. Set inside the sentence it belongs to, it is the trigger of an obligation: the triumph is the premise, the worship is the conclusion. A chapter of three verses is short enough that every one of them has to carry structure, and this one carries the structure of the whole.

**Help and Opening Are Two Different Gifts**

Two nouns hold the verse up, and they are not synonyms. *Naṣr* is help given inside a struggle — support arriving when one side cannot win alone, which is why the Qur'an makes it God's to give rather than a property of armies: *“If Allah helps you, none can defeat you. But if He denies you help, then who else can help you?”* (3:160). *Fatḥ* is an opening. Its root *f-t-ḥ* means to open what was shut, and from that base it names both the opening of a city's gates and the opening of a case — a verdict delivered between parties who have argued too long. A city that resisted is opened; a dispute is settled.

The verse puts help first and opening second, which is the order in which such things happen. Aid arrives while the outcome is still unclear; the opening follows it. Attaching both to God removes the achievement from human accounting. What comes is not a military success that a leader afterwards dedicates to heaven. It is divine help, delivered through a leader who receives it, and the grammar of the phrase keeps that direction of travel visible.

**Mecca Fell in Ramadan of the Eighth Year**

The bracketed words in the translation name the event most commentators mean: the conquest of Mecca. It took place in Ramadan 8 AH, which falls in January 630 CE. The Prophet ﷺ marched with roughly ten thousand men, the largest force ever to leave Medina, and the city came over with almost no fighting; the general amnesty granted afterwards is what made the second verse of this sūrah possible. Quraysh had held out for eight years after the Hijrah, and their resistance had been the last public argument against the message.

Two readings compete with this one. Some take the *fatḥ* to be the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah of 6 AH (628 CE), because the Qur'an itself calls that treaty *“a clear triumph ˹O Prophet˺”* (48:1) and because the truce that followed produced conversions on a scale Mecca had never seen. Others read the verse as pointing to the conquests after the Prophet's death, when Islam crossed the borders of Arabia. The first has the stronger claim. This sūrah joins the *fatḥ* to crowds entering the religion, and the historical record puts those crowds in the ninth year, when tribal embassies came to Medina in numbers after the fall of the city they had been waiting on.

**Past-Tense Verbs for Events Still to Come**

Both verbs of the verse stand in the perfect: the help *came*, the victory *was achieved*. At the moment of revelation neither had happened. Arabic regularly puts a future event in the perfect when its realization is so certain that the language treats it as finished, and the Qur'an uses that device wherever certainty is the point: *“The command of Allah is at hand, so do not hasten it.”* (16:1). The Hour has not arrived; the sentence speaks of it as though it has.

The grammar here does the same work. A promise can fail; a report cannot. By placing the coming victory in the tense of accomplished fact, the verse tells its first audience — the Companions in Medina, and the Quraysh who heard it quoted back to them — that whether Islam would survive in Arabia was already a settled matter. A few early readers took the perfect literally and understood the verse as describing what had in fact occurred by the time the report was passed down, which turned the tense into a small chronological puzzle. That reading costs the verse its force. Its certainty is a rhetorical achievement rather than a note about dates, and the puzzle disappears once the idiom is recognized.

**The Oldest Reading of This Verse Was That It Announced a Death**

The earliest interpretation on record does not treat the verse as news of a conquest at all. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb used to seat the young Ibn ʿAbbās with the veterans of Badr, and some of them resented it. One day ʿUmar asked the gathering what they made of *“When comes the Help of Allah and the Conquest.”* Some answered that believers are commanded to praise God and seek His forgiveness when help and conquest reach them; others said nothing at all. Turning to Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿUmar was told that this is the appointed term of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, made known to him: the help and the conquest are the sign of it, and the praise and the seeking of forgiveness are what he is to do with the time remaining. ʿUmar's answer is recorded in the same report — he knew nothing about the verse beyond what this young man had said. It stands in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4970, from Ibn ʿAbbās through Saʿīd ibn Jubayr.

So the first generation read the condition as a notice of departure. The logic is plain: when a message has been delivered and accepted in crowds, the one who carried it has finished. The Qur'an states the principle elsewhere without softening it: *“We have not granted immortality to any human before you ˹O Prophet˺: so if you die, will they live forever?”* (21:34).

**The Last Complete Chapter, With a Few Verses After It**

Most exegetes put this sūrah at the end of the revelation, as the last chapter given whole. Individual verses came later — among them 2:281, 4:176 and 9:128, and, on the strongest account, 5:3 at the Farewell Pilgrimage. The disagreement concerns the setting rather than the rank. Some place the revelation at Minā on the tenth of Dhū l-Ḥijjah during that pilgrimage, a day after 5:3; others place it immediately before 5:3; others hold that it came in Medina after the Prophet ﷺ had returned home. A minority dates it to right after Ḥudaybiyyah in 6 AH, and al-Wāḥidī sets it at the departure for Ḥunayn in 8 AH, roughly two years before the end.

Weighed against each other, the late Madinan placement fits the internal evidence best. Nothing in the vocabulary belongs to the Meccan style of oath and image, and the whole chapter reads as an address to a man whose work is complete. Ibn Masʿūd called it the Sūrah of Farewell, *sūrat al-tawdīʿ*, and the name stuck.

**Nothing Human Is Named in It**

No enemy appears, no battle, no date. The verse reporting the greatest political event of the Prophet's career does not mention Quraysh, does not mention Mecca, and does not mention a single person; the city enters the English text only through a translator's bracket. The absence is the argument. An event recorded as divine action is described with the two words that belong to God — help and opening — while the human beings who fought for it appear later only as a crowd, without names or genealogies.

Contrast that with the chapter two places on in the codex, where the Qur'an names a living opponent in its very first line (111:1). Where a person is the subject of the message, the naming happens; where God is the subject, the persons drop out. That economy is why this sūrah can be recited in any century and read as describing the present. The next verse supplies the human half of the scene.

## Sūrah an-Naṣr 110:2

> and you ˹O Prophet˺ see the people embracing Allah’s Way in crowds,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sign That Has to Be Seen With the Eyes**

The second condition is perceptual. *Raʾayta* — you saw — is a verb of sight, and the verse puts the Prophet ﷺ in the position of a witness rather than of someone receiving information. God could have said simply that the people have entered. Instead the sentence waits for the sight of it: when you *see* them entering.

That verb has a wider use in Arabic. *Raʾā* can mean to know or to judge, as when a speaker says "I see that this is so," and some commentators read it that way here, taking the verse to describe realization rather than observation. The dispute matters less than it looks, because both readings keep the weight on evidence. What the sūrah waits for is a state of affairs that can be checked in the world: crowds, visible, arriving. Faith at this scale leaves a social trace — delegations on the road, oaths taken in public, whole districts changing allegiance — and the verse treats the trace as the sign.

Sight as the criterion also fixes the promise inside a lifetime. A revelation that waits for something to be seen has to arrive while the one who is to see it is still alive.

**Afwājā Is the Qur'an's Word for a Procession**

The last word of the verse is *afwājā*, plural of *fawj*: a company, a troop, a group travelling together rather than singly. "In crowds" keeps the number but loses the movement — a *fawj* is a body of people on the road as one.

The Qur'an reserves this word for processions of consequence. The damned are driven to Hell *“in ˹successive˺ groups”* (39:71), and the righteous are led to Paradise under the same term (39:73); when the Trumpet is blown, humanity *“will ˹all˺ come forth in crowds”* (78:18). Each time *afwāj* describes a multitude moving toward a destination it cannot leave, company after company. Used here, the word gives the spread of Islam the shape of an eschatological procession: not scattered conversions but an ordered arrival.

The choice has a second effect. A crowd is anonymous. The verse lists none of the tribes that came, names no convert, and does not sort those who believed from those who merely submitted. What is recorded is the movement itself, which is why the sentence stays true long after the particular companies have been buried.

**What Changed When Mecca Fell**

For eight years after the Hijrah, Islam grew in Arabia one person or one household at a time. The obstacle was political as much as theological: while Quraysh held Mecca and the Kaʿbah, the religion looked like a rebel faction, and the tribes of the peninsula waited to see which side would win. The Prophet's own city standing outside his dominion was the last argument his opponents possessed.

The conquest ended it. In Ramadan 8 AH — January 630 CE — Mecca opened with almost no bloodshed, the idols around the Kaʿbah were broken, and a general amnesty covered the population that had driven the Muslims out eight years earlier. The next year is known in the sources as the Year of Delegations, *sanat al-wufūd*. Ibn Saʿd's Ṭabaqāt lists seventy-one tribal embassies reaching Medina in the ninth and tenth years, most of them to announce that a whole tribe had accepted Islam and to ask for teachers. That is the scene the verse describes: not the old trickle but companies arriving.

**"The People" Is Left Unqualified**

The subject is *al-nās* — the people — with no adjective, no tribe, no region attached. Two attempts to narrow it are recorded. Some exegetes take it to mean the Arabs in general, since they were the ones entering in companies; others identify a large body from Yemen that had recently accepted Islam, on the strength of reports about Yemeni delegations in Medina.

Neither narrowing is required by the text, and both weaken it. The general noun is what lets the verse be read as a standing description rather than a single census. Every later mass movement into the religion — in Persia, in Anatolia, along the Swahili coast, across the Indonesian archipelago — falls under the same phrase, because the phrase names nobody. Those who specify Yemen or the Arabs are answering a historical question about which crowds the Prophet ﷺ actually saw with his eyes; the reading that keeps the noun open answers the question the sūrah is asking. The unqualified word is the reason a chapter this short has been recited for fourteen centuries as a description of something still going on.

**Entering God's Way, Not Joining a Man's Party**

What the people enter is *dīn Allāh* — Allah's Way. They are not said to enter the Prophet's following, his party, or his state; the thing entered belongs to God. The possessive does real work. A movement that grows because its leader has won can always be read as allegiance to the leader, and the grammar forecloses that reading.

The same phrase carries the Qur'an's own definition: *“Certainly, Allah's only Way is Islam.”* (3:19). And this claim has to be held together with another Madinan verse, which states that faith cannot be forced: *“Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood.”* (2:256). The two are reconciled by the amnesty at Mecca. The city was taken, the population was pardoned, and the entry that followed was chosen by people who had been given the option of remaining exactly as they were. That is why the Qur'an can describe mass conversion without embarrassment: the crowd arrived after the sword had been sheathed, not because of it.

**Why Triumph Is Made the Trigger for Worship**

The verse reporting the largest influx of the Prophet's mission sets up an order to praise God and ask His forgiveness. Success is the moment at which a movement stops examining itself. Victory produces credit, and credit is claimed — by generals, by tribes, by the winning side's own account of why it won. This sūrah answers that risk before it forms, by making the sight of the crowds a signal for worship rather than celebration.

The pattern holds wherever triumph appears in the Qur'an. Joseph, at the height of his power in Egypt, asks to die a believer rather than to go on reigning. Solomon, given the throne of a distant queen in the blink of an eye, asks whether he will prove grateful or ungrateful. The arrival of a good thing is treated as a test of a particular kind — the test of attribution. Will the recipient trace the gift to its giver or to himself? This verse puts the question in the plainest terms available: everything you asked for has arrived; now say whose it was.

**The Promise Fulfilled in Truth**

Two earlier revelations described this scene before it happened, and the verse reads as their discharge. During the truce year the Prophet ﷺ had been shown in a dream that he would enter the Sacred Mosque; the Qur'an confirmed the dream and explained the delay: *“Indeed, Allah will fulfil His Messenger's vision in all truth: Allah willing, you will surely enter the Sacred Mosque, in security... He knew what you did not know, so He first granted you the triumph at hand.”* (48:27). What the sūrah of Victory announced as coming, this sūrah reports as arrived.

The other promise was broader, and it carried a condition: *“Allah has promised those of you who believe and do good that He will certainly make them successors in the land, as He did with those before them; and will surely establish for them their faith which He has chosen for them; and will indeed change their fear into security—˹provided that˺ they worship Me, associating nothing with Me.”* (24:55). The condition and the crowds meet in the verse that follows.

## Sūrah an-Naṣr 110:3

> then glorify the praises of your Lord and seek His forgiveness, for certainly He is ever Accepting of Repentance.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to a Victory Is an Act of Worship**

The final verse is the conclusion of the sentence the sūrah began, and it is introduced by *fa-*, the particle that marks the result of a condition: *then* glorify, *then* seek forgiveness. Everything before it was setup. The help came, the crowds were seen, and the response required of the recipient is not administration, not settlement of accounts, not the consolidation of a new order in Arabia. It is worship.

That allocation is the whole argument of the chapter. A victorious leader has a hundred legitimate things to do — distribute spoils, garrison a city, receive delegations, appoint judges — and none of them is named here. What is named are two acts directed at God alone: glorification with praise, and the request for forgiveness. The verse therefore does something unusual with a political event. It converts the fall of Mecca and the mass entry of the Arabs into an occasion for private devotion, and it does so at the exact moment when the temptation to read the event as a personal achievement would be strongest.

**Glorify With Praise: Two Acts in a Single Phrase**

*Sabbiḥ bi-ḥamd rabbika* joins two things that are often separated. *Tasbīḥ* is to declare God beyond what is said of Him — the removal of every deficiency and every likeness; *ḥamd* is praise for what He is and what He has done. Joined by the preposition *bi-*, they become one act: glorify Him *with* praise, so that the removal of imperfection does not slide into a God who is merely distant, and the praise does not slide into a God who is merely generous.

The same formula recurs at turning points in the revelation: *“So glorify the praises of your Lord and be one of those who ˹always˺ pray”* (15:98); *“So be patient ˹O Prophet˺, ˹for˺ Allah's promise is certainly true. Seek forgiveness for your shortcomings. And glorify the praises of your Lord morning and evening.”* (40:55); and again at 20:130 and 50:39. In each case glorification is paired with patience or with prayer, and in 40:55 with the request for forgiveness exactly as here.

The Prophet ﷺ put the pairing into his own prayer. ʿĀʾishah reported that he often said while bowing and prostrating: "Glory be to You, O Allah, our Lord, and praise be to You; O Allah, forgive me" — and she added that he was putting the Qur'an into practice. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 484. The formula she quotes contains all three elements of this verse in a single breath.

**Asking Forgiveness at the Top of Success**

*Wa-staghfirhu* — and seek His forgiveness. The instruction is unexpected in its place. Forgiveness belongs naturally to failure, to sin, to the aftermath of a wrong; here it is attached to triumph. Three reasons are given for the attachment, and they are not in competition.

The first is that a victory of this size carries real liabilities. Cities are entered, property changes hands, old enemies are pardoned, spoils are divided, and a thousand small decisions get made under pressure. The sūrah of Victory had already legislated for those decisions; this verse asks for the spiritual posture behind them. The second is that success itself is the danger: it is easier to be grateful in want than in plenty. The third is simply the Prophet's own habit, which the Qur'an confirms rather than introduces. He said: "By Allah, I ask Allah's forgiveness and turn to Him more than seventy times a day" — Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6307, from Abū Hurayrah. A man already forgiven asked constantly for forgiveness; the command here does not create a new practice but places an existing one at the summit of his career.

Elsewhere the same pairing is addressed to him directly with the community included: *“And seek forgiveness for your shortcomings and for ˹the sins of˺ the believing men and women.”* (47:19).

**Tawwāb: The Name That Turns First**

The verse closes on a divine name, *tawwāb*, rendered here as ever Accepting of Repentance. The form is intensive: not merely one who accepts a turning but one who accepts it repeatedly, from many, without the account closing. Arabic uses the same root for both sides of the transaction. *Tāba* describes the human being's repentance and God's relenting, so that the word carries a reciprocity built into its letters.

The Qur'an exploits that reciprocity. Adam receives words of prayer and is forgiven: *“Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.”* (2:37). The three who stayed behind from Tabūk are described in the longest such sentence in the Book: *“They knew there was no refuge from Allah except in Him. Then He turned to them in mercy so that they might repent.”* (9:118) — His turning comes first, and their turning is its result. That is the theological content of the name in this verse. A man at the height of success is told to ask forgiveness, and the reason he can ask is that the asking has already been met. *Tawwāb* is not a promise that God will be persuaded; it is a statement that He begins.

**Why a Sinless Man Is Ordered to Seek Forgiveness**

The command is addressed to someone the tradition holds to be protected from sin, and the commentators took that seriously rather than waving it away. Three explanations are offered. Some hold that the request here is for the community, not for himself, on the model of 47:19; the Prophet ﷺ stands in the sentence as its spokesman, and the singular address is a grammatical convenience. Others hold that *istighfār* in this place is not expiation at all but an act of worship and gratitude — a way of saying that whatever has been achieved, the achiever falls short of what the achievement owed. A third group reads it as the recognition of a specific shortfall: victory brings with it the treatment of the defeated, and even a just settlement leaves something to be covered.

The second explanation is the strongest, because it does not require the verse to mean something other than what it says. *Istighfār* is a posture before God, and its posture is dependence. A prophet who has just been given a city asks for nothing from the city; he asks for forgiveness. The order of the two commands — glorify, then seek forgiveness — puts praise before petition, which is the shape of every prayer in the tradition.

**The Sūrah of Farewell**

Ibn Masʿūd's name for this chapter was *sūrat al-tawdīʿ*, the Sūrah of Farewell, and the name records how the first generation understood it. The help had come, the crowds had come, and the message was complete; what remained was the departure of the messenger. The Prophet ﷺ died in Rabīʿ al-Awwal of the eleventh year after the Hijrah, in June 632 CE, about two years after the conquest this sūrah announces.

The last two years of the revelation are marked by completion language. At the Farewell Pilgrimage came the verse: *“Today the disbelievers have given up all hope of ˹undermining˺ your faith. So do not fear them; fear Me! Today I have perfected your faith for you, completed My favour upon you, and chosen Islam as your way.”* (5:3). Perfection of the religion and the recall of the prophet are the same event seen from two sides. A book that was still growing stopped; the man through whom it grew had nothing further to carry. This verse is the personal version of that announcement, addressed not to the community but to the one whose work had ended.

**Endings Are Described the Same Way Throughout the Book**

Two other verses belong with this one, and reading them together shows the Qur'an's consistent way of handling an ending. The first prepares the community for a death it did not want: *“Muḥammad is no more than a messenger; other messengers have gone before him. If he were to die or to be killed, would you regress into disbelief?”* (3:144) — revealed at Uḥud, years before the conquest, and proved necessary on the day he died, when some refused to believe it.

The second is the verse most often reported as the last revealed of all: *“Be mindful of the Day when you will ˹all˺ be returned to Allah, then every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged.”* (2:281). Both are addresses about return — the messenger's return to his Lord, and every soul's return to be paid. The instruction to glorify and seek forgiveness sits between them. It is what a person does with the interval, and this sūrah measures the interval in months.
