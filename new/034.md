# Sūrah Sabaʾ (Chapter 34) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:1

> All praise is for Allah, to Whom belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And praise be to Him in the Hereafter. He is the All-Wise, All-Aware.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Five Sūrahs Open With Praise, and No Two Praise the Same Thing**

Five chapters of the Qur'an begin with *al-ḥamdu lillāh*, and each one attaches that praise to a different ground. The Opening praises the Lord of all worlds: *"All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds"* (1:2). Al-Anʿām praises the Maker of light and dark: *"All praise is for Allah Who created the heavens and the earth and made darkness and light"* (6:1). Al-Kahf praises the Giver of a straight Book: *"All praise is for Allah Who has revealed the Book to His servant, allowing no crookedness in it"* (18:1). Fāṭir praises the Originator who staffs the heavens with winged messengers (35:1). Sabaʾ takes the fifth position, and its ground is ownership — everything in both realms already belongs to the One being praised. The pattern rules out the idea that these openings are decoration repeated by habit. A formula that changes its reason every time it appears is an argument, not a refrain. Here the reason chosen is the one the rest of the sūrah will need, because a chapter about to dismantle the claims of rival deities must first establish who owns the property under dispute.

**Ownership Is Stated Before Praise Is Explained**

The clause is *lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Arabic marks possession here with a single prefixed letter, the *lām* of ownership, placed before the pronoun so that the owner is named first and the property second. The relative pronoun is *mā*, the word used for things rather than persons, and its breadth is the point: not some of what is there, not the valuable part of it, but the undifferentiated whole. The same sentence recurs across the Book in slightly varied settings — at the heart of the Throne Verse (2:255), attached to divine exaltation in *"To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And He is the Most High, the Greatest"* (42:4), attached to self-sufficiency in (22:64) and (31:26), and attached to a warning of punishment in (14:2). Placing it here, immediately after the praise, supplies the logic that praise usually leaves unstated. Praise offered to someone who owns nothing is flattery. Praise offered to the owner of everything is simply an accurate description of the situation.

**Praise That Does Not End When the World Does**

The third clause moves the praise past the horizon of this life: *"And praise be to Him in the Hereafter"* (34:1). Most human gratitude is tied to a delivery — a rescue, a harvest, a recovery — and it fades as the memory of the gift fades. This clause detaches praise from that pattern by placing it in the one location where no further gifts are being negotiated and nothing more can be extracted. The Qur'an follows through on the claim in its scenes of the next world. Those admitted to the Garden say, *"Praise be to Allah for guiding us to this"* (7:43); they say, *"Praise be to Allah, Who has kept away from us all ˹causes of˺ sorrow"* (35:34); their final word in the Garden is *"All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds!"* (10:10). Even the judgment itself closes on it: after every case has been decided with fairness, the angels around the Throne are heard glorifying, and the verdict is sealed with *"Praise be to Allah—Lord of all worlds!"* (39:75). Praise, on this account, is not the currency of petition. It is what remains when petition is over.

**Two Names That Answer the Objection Coming Next**

The verse closes with *al-Ḥakīm al-Khabīr*. The first name comes from the root *ḥ-k-m*, which in Arabic carries the sense of restraint as much as of judgment — from the same root comes *ḥakamah*, the bridle-bit that holds a horse to its course. Wisdom in this sense is not cleverness but the ordering of things toward their proper end. The second name, *al-Khabīr*, derives from *kh-b-r*, and *khibrah* in ordinary Arabic is the knowledge a person acquires from handling a thing directly rather than hearing about it: insider knowledge, knowledge of the interior. Searching this translation of the whole Qur'an, the pair "All-Wise, All-Aware" stands at only four places — (6:18), (6:73), (11:1) and here. The choice is exact for what Sabaʾ is about to face. Its opponents will argue that the promised Hour is late, and lateness suggests either bad planning or ignorance. One name closes the first exit, the other closes the second. A wise arrangement can include delay on purpose; an aware one cannot be surprised by what it delays.

**Place and Time Are Both Covered, in Two Other Verses**

Two related statements elsewhere show what this opening is doing by comparison. Al-Rūm spreads praise across locations and hours together: *"all praise is for Him in the heavens and the earth—as well as in the afternoon, and at noon"* (30:18). Al-Qaṣaṣ spreads it across the two ages: *"All praise belongs to Him in this life and the next"* (28:70). Sabaʾ takes the second axis, the temporal one, and states only the far end of it — the Hereafter — because the near end has already been covered by the ownership clause that came before. Reading the three together, no region of space and no stretch of time is left where praise would be misplaced or would lapse. This is also why the verse does not say that people will praise Him then. It says the praise is His, whether or not anyone offers it. The grammar makes praise a property of the One praised rather than an achievement of the one praising.

**A Chapter That Begins From What Its Opponents Cannot Deny**

Sabaʾ opens an argument, and the opening move is deliberately the least contested one available. The Meccan opponents addressed through this sūrah did not deny that God exists, nor that He created; their quarrel was over exclusivity, prophethood and resurrection. Beginning with praise and ownership therefore starts on ground they had already conceded, and everything contested later in the chapter is built on that footing. There is also a link backwards. The previous sūrah ends by describing the trust that the heavens, the earth and the mountains declined to carry while humanity took it up (33:72). Opening the next sūrah with unqualified praise answers the natural worry that follows: if the trust is so heavy, does God gain when it is carried and lose when it is dropped? The answer is that praise is already complete, in this world and the next, owing nothing to the performance of the one who carries it. *"It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy"* (35:15). The verse that follows will press the same independence into a claim about knowledge.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:2

> He knows whatever goes into the earth and whatever comes out of it, and whatever descends from the sky and whatever ascends into it. And He is the Most Merciful, All-Forgiving.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Directions That Between Them Leave Nowhere to Hide**

The verse sets out two pairs of opposed movements: down into the earth and up out of it, down from the sky and up into it. Taken together they describe a closed circuit. Anything that exists is either going in, coming out, coming down or going up, and the four verbs exhaust the possibilities. This is a characteristic Qur'anic way of asserting totality — not by saying "everything," which the mind hears as a vague word, but by naming the axes along which motion can occur and then covering all of them. The same four clauses appear once more in the Book, at the centre of a verse in al-Ḥadīd that adds a further clause: *"He knows whatever goes into the earth and whatever comes out of it, and whatever descends from the sky and whatever ascends into it. And He is with you wherever you are"* (57:4). Sabaʾ uses the identical inventory but closes it with mercy rather than with presence. The wording is shared; the conclusion drawn from it is not, and the difference is the commentary each sūrah is making on its own theme.

**What Actually Goes Into the Ground**

The first clause is the broadest, because the earth receives more kinds of thing than any other region. Rain soaks into it and disappears. Seed is buried and rots before it does anything else. Roots push down out of sight. Minerals and metals are already there, formed in darkness. And the dead are lowered into it, which is the meaning that matters most in a sūrah whose opponents will shortly laugh at the idea of being raised from it. Each of these things enters the ground and becomes invisible, and invisibility is exactly what the deniers of resurrection rely on: once a body has scattered into soil, they assume, the record of it is gone because no one can see it any more. The verse answers that assumption before the objection is even voiced. What enters the earth does not enter a filing gap. Al-Anʿām makes the same point at the scale of a single seed: *"not a grain in the darkness of the earth or anything—green or dry—but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record"* (6:59).

**What Comes Back Out, and Why It Is Listed Second**

The return movement is the ordinary miracle of the world — shoots breaking the crust, springs surfacing, ore lifted out of a shaft, and the eventual raising of what was buried. Placing emergence immediately after entry turns the pair into a small argument rather than a list. Something goes down and is lost to sight; something comes up that was not there before; and the person watching the field has no difficulty believing the second even though he cannot explain it. The Qur'an appeals to that familiarity elsewhere in exactly this context, telling those who doubt the resurrection to consider the ground they already trust: *"Then when He calls you out of the earth just once, you will instantly come forth"* (30:25). Naming both directions also blocks a deist reading in which God knows the world as a finished inventory. The knowledge described here is of things in transit, mid-process, half-buried, not yet arrived — the hardest kind of knowledge to have, and the only kind that could support a reckoning of lives still being lived.

**Descent and Ascent: Two Traffics, One Watcher**

The vertical pair widens the scene beyond agriculture. What comes down from the sky includes rain and light, and it includes what the Qur'an describes as sent down by decree — angels, revelation, allotted portions. What goes up includes vapour and the flight of birds, and it includes what the Book explicitly says ascends: *"To Him ˹alone˺ good words ascend, and righteous deeds are raised up by Him"* (35:10). Al-Sajdah describes the return leg of the circuit as a matter of divine administration: *"He conducts every affair from the heavens to the earth, then it all ascends to Him"* (32:5). So the four clauses read as a single system with two floors and constant traffic in both directions, physical and moral at once. Nothing in the vocabulary forces a choice between the natural and the spiritual reading, and the sūrah gains from holding both. Rain falling and a prayer rising are described by the same verb pair, which is itself a claim: the moral order is not a separate world layered on top of the physical one but the same world, watched by the same knowledge.

**Mercy Named Before Forgiveness, and Only Here**

The verse ends with *al-Raḥīm al-Ghafūr* — the Most Merciful, All-Forgiving. Reading this translation across the whole Qur'an, the two names occur in this order at this verse alone; everywhere else the familiar sequence runs the other way, forgiving first and merciful second. The reversal is worth pausing over, because it changes which name governs. Forgiveness answers an offence, so it presupposes a record of wrongdoing. Mercy is prior to any offence and is the reason forgiveness is offered at all. Placing mercy first makes forgiveness its consequence rather than its equal. The placement also does something to the four clauses that precede it. A verse listing exhaustive surveillance could easily close on a name of power — the Overwhelming, the Severe in Punishment — and the sentence would run smoothly. Choosing mercy instead tells the reader what the surveillance is for. The One who misses nothing is not compiling a case; He is the One from whom pardon comes.

**Knowledge Without Mercy Is a Threat; Here It Is an Invitation**

What holds the two halves of this verse together is a single implied claim: complete knowledge in the hands of a merciful owner is good news rather than bad. Human beings fear being fully known because the people who know most about us are rarely the people most inclined to pardon us. The verse breaks that association. Because nothing that goes into the ground is lost, no quiet good deed is wasted and no injustice done in private has escaped; because the One who knows is named merciful and forgiving, the same completeness that would terrify a wrongdoer is the ground of hope for anyone who turns back. Al-Mulk states the underlying logic as a question: *"How could He not know His Own creation? For He ˹alone˺ is the Most Subtle, All-Aware"* (67:14). Sabaʾ has just called Him All-Aware in its opening line (34:1) and now spells out the content of that awareness in four directions. Having established who knows and how completely, the sūrah is ready to report what its opponents say about the Hour.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:3

> The disbelievers say, “The Hour will never come to us.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Yes—by my Lord, the Knower of the unseen—it will certainly come to you!” Not ˹even˺ an atom’s weight is hidden from Him in the heavens or the earth; nor anything smaller or larger than that, but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Denial Phrased as a Personal Exemption**

The objection is not that an hour of reckoning is impossible in principle. It is narrower and more revealing than that: *lā taʾtīnā al-sāʿah* — it will not come **to us**. The pronoun carries the weight. These speakers are not cosmologists denying an end to the world; they are men claiming an exemption from one. That is why the reply, when it comes, fastens on the same pronoun and turns it back: it will certainly come **to you**. Elsewhere the Qur'an records the same posture in fuller form, where those addressed dismiss the promise as guesswork: *"We do not know what the Hour is! We think it is no more than speculation, and we are not convinced ˹that it will ever come˺"* (45:32). The tense in the Arabic here is the imperfect, which covers the habitual as well as the future, so the sentence describes a thing they kept saying rather than a single remark. An opinion repeated often enough stops feeling like an opinion, and the sūrah is describing men who have talked themselves into safety.

**An Oath the Prophet Is Ordered to Swear**

The response begins with *qul balā* — say, yes. *Balā* is a specialized Arabic particle with no single English equivalent: it is used only to contradict a negative statement, so it means "no, the opposite is true" while sounding like agreement. Then comes the oath: *wa-rabbī*, by my Lord. This is unusual. The Prophet is rarely instructed to swear, and searching the corpus of these translations shows only three places where he is told to answer a denial about the next world with a personal oath by his Lord — this verse, *"Yes, by my Lord! Most certainly it is true! And you will have no escape"* (10:53), and *"Yes, by my Lord, you will surely be resurrected"* (64:7). All three concern the same disputed fact. The oath is not there to add information; the argument that follows does that. It is there because a claim about the unseen cannot be settled by producing evidence in the ordinary way, and the messenger is made to stake his own honesty on it in public. The attached title — Knower of the unseen — names precisely the qualification the deniers said nobody had.

**The Smallest Unit the Language Could Supply**

*Mithqāl dharrah* is usually rendered "an atom's weight," and the phrase deserves accuracy rather than anachronism. *Mithqāl* is a weight-unit from the vocabulary of the market scales; *dharrah* in classical usage means the tiniest visible speck, the mote that shows in a shaft of sunlight, and was also used of the smallest species of ant. The expression is therefore drawn from ordinary commerce and ordinary eyesight: the least quantity a merchant's balance could register. Nothing in the word requires modern physics, and nothing in it excludes a smaller referent either, which is why the verse immediately adds *nor anything smaller*. The same measure appears where deeds are being weighed — *"So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it"* (99:7) — and where fairness is being guaranteed — *"Indeed, Allah never wrongs ˹anyone˺—even by an atom’s weight"* (4:40). Choosing a market unit to describe divine knowledge is itself an argument aimed at merchants. The men of Mecca understood exactly how small a quantity their scales could catch, and the verse tells them the reckoning is finer than their instruments.

**Both Directions Are Closed: Smaller and Larger**

Having named the smallest unit, the verse blocks the two escapes available to anyone who wants to slip beneath or above the audit: *nor anything smaller or larger than that*. The first half answers the man who assumes his small sins are beneath notice; the second answers the man who assumes his large crimes are too big to be filed, too tangled in politics and circumstance to be assigned to him personally. Both assumptions collapse in the same clause. Luqmān presses the smaller half into a father's advice to his son, complete with the hiding places a child might imagine: *"˹Even˺ if a deed were the weight of a mustard seed—be it ˹hidden˺ in a rock or in the heavens or the earth—Allah will bring it forth"* (31:16). The rhetorical construction is worth noticing. The verse does not assert exhaustive knowledge as an abstract attribute and leave the reader to apply it. It names the borderline case, then extends the rule past the border in both directions, so that the mind cannot finish the sentence with an exception still standing.

**A Perfect Record Turns Knowledge Into Evidence**

The final clause shifts from knowing to writing: everything is *fī kitābin mubīn*, in a clear and perfect Record. Knowledge that is never externalized can be doubted by the person it concerns; a record can be produced. The Qur'an keeps this vocabulary consistent. The same phrase closes the verse on the falling leaf and the buried grain (6:59), and the same phrase closes a verse addressed to the Prophet about every act performed while the revelation is being recited (10:61). Elsewhere the Book speaks of scales set up on the Day of Judgment so that *"no soul will be wronged in the least"* (21:47), and of a *"Preserved Tablet"* (85:22) in which the whole is inscribed. Bringing writing into an argument about the Hour also meets the deniers on their own ground. Commercial Mecca lived by written contracts and witnessed debts precisely because memory is disputable and ink is not. A reckoning backed by a document is a reckoning that can be contested in the open, which is what the verse is promising.

**Why This Answer Is Placed Before Any Description of the Hour**

The sūrah could have replied to the denial by describing the terrors of the Day, and later passages do exactly that. Answering first with an argument about knowledge is a structural choice. If the objection is that the Hour is a fabrication, then descriptions of it merely restate the thing under dispute; what the objection actually requires is a demonstration that the universe is the kind of place in which an exact reckoning is possible at all. The two verses just passed built that case — ownership of both realms (34:1), and knowledge of everything moving in either direction (34:2) — so this verse can now draw the conclusion the deniers were trying to avoid. A world fully owned, fully known and fully recorded is a world that can be judged. What remains unstated here, and comes next, is what such a reckoning is finally for.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:4

> So He may reward those who believe and do good. It is they who will have forgiveness and an honourable provision.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Clause That Explains the Whole Reckoning**

The verse opens with *li-yajziya* — the Arabic prefix *li-* here is the lām of purpose, "so that He may." Grammatically this clause hangs on what came before it, which means the exhaustive record described in the previous verse is not presented as an end in itself. Surveillance is not the point; payment is. This matters for how the whole passage is read. A universe in which nothing escapes notice could be a machine of unrelieved threat, and plenty of religious imaginations have understood it that way. The Qur'an instead states the purpose in a single clause and makes the first beneficiaries of total knowledge the people who did good quietly. Al-Rūm attaches the same purpose to the same theme in almost the same shape, ending an argument about the Hour with *"so that He may ˹generously˺ reward those who believe and do good, out of His grace"* (30:45), and Yūnus ties it to the resurrection itself: *"Indeed, He originates the creation then resurrects it so that He may justly reward those who believe and do good"* (10:4). Judgment exists because merit would otherwise go unpaid.

**Faith and Action Named as One Qualification, Not Two**

The phrase *alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* — those who believe and do good — is one of the most frequent formulas in the Qur'an, and its grammar keeps the two elements welded. Belief is a verb here, not a noun: not "the people of faith" but "those who believed," an act performed. The good works are then joined by a simple conjunction with no conditional between them, so the formula never allows the reading that either half might stand alone. The word *ṣāliḥāt* is worth unfolding, because it is not primarily an ethical word in Arabic. Its root, *ṣ-l-ḥ*, means to be sound, intact, fit for use, and it is used of repairing a wall or mending a relationship; its opposite, *fasād*, means rot. So the deeds in question are not decorative acts of piety but works that leave something in better repair than they found it. Naming this pairing in a sūrah about deniers of the Hour also draws a line. The dispute over resurrection was never merely theoretical, since a man's answer to it decides whether unwitnessed repair work is worth doing.

**Forgiveness Is Listed First, Before Any Reward**

The order of the two gifts repays attention: *maghfirah*, then *rizq karīm* — forgiveness, then an honourable provision. If reward were strictly a matter of accounting, forgiveness would be out of place in the list, since it is not something earned by good works but something granted despite bad ones. Its position at the head of the pair concedes something the passage never states outright: the believers being rewarded here also have a record, and the record has debts in it. The reckoning that catches an atom's weight (34:3) catches theirs too. So the first thing they receive is the cancellation of what the record holds against them, and only then the payment for what stands to their credit. This sequence is not accidental to this verse alone. Al-Ḥajj uses the identical pairing in the identical order when describing the same people: *"So those who believe and do good will have forgiveness and an honourable provision"* (22:50). Al-Anfāl adds a third element and keeps the order: *"They will have elevated ranks, forgiveness, and an honourable provision from their Lord"* (8:4).

**"Honourable Provision" Describes the Receiving, Not the Goods**

*Rizq karīm* is usually translated "an honourable provision" or "a generous provision," and the adjective is doing something specific. *Karīm* in Arabic is applied to a person of noble character, to a gift given without being asked for, and to a thing of high quality; its root sense combines generosity with dignity. Attached to *rizq*, sustenance, it says as much about the manner of the giving as about the substance given. Provision in this world often arrives with humiliation attached — earned under a harsh master, begged for, or handed down with visible reluctance. The Qur'an's picture of the next world removes that residue. The word choice also carries a quiet rebuke to the men whose voices fill this sūrah, who measured divine favour by the size of a man's estate and will shortly boast about it in their own words (34:35). Provision of the kind promised here is not indexed to wealth at all. It is indexed to belief and repair, and it arrives from a Giver whose generosity is part of the gift.

**Reward That Is Owed, and Reward That Is Grace**

A tension runs under this verse which the Qur'an handles openly rather than smoothing away. On one side, the language is that of payment: *jazāʾ*, recompense, the settling of accounts, and the Book insists elsewhere that the exchange is exactly proportionate for wrongdoing — *"Whoever does an evil deed will only be paid back with its equivalent"* (40:40). On the other side, the reward for good is described as multiplied far beyond proportion: *"Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold"* (6:160), and in the parable of the grain, seven hundredfold and more (2:261). Justice sets the floor for punishment; grace sets no ceiling for reward. Al-Rūm makes the asymmetry explicit by adding *out of His grace* to the very same purpose clause (30:45). The two need not be reconciled by weakening either. What a servant is owed, he is paid in full and not a mote short; what he is given beyond that is not owed at all, which is why forgiveness can head the list without making the accounting incoherent.

**Why the Sūrah Answers a Denial With a Promise**

Structurally this is the gentler half of a two-part reply to men who said the Hour would never reach them. Having established that the reckoning is possible because everything is known and written, the sūrah first states what the reckoning is for, and the answer is the vindication of people whose faith and repair work went unrewarded in a world that measured worth in caravans and sons. Only afterwards does the passage turn to the other party. The order is deliberate and it recurs across the Book: the promise is stated before the threat, and the threat is stated as the alternative to a promise already on the table. For an audience listening in Mecca, many of them poor and some of them enslaved, the sequence carried practical weight. The Day they were told to fear was, for them, the Day their unpaid ledger would finally be honoured. The following verse turns to those on the opposite side of that ledger.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:5

> As for those who strive to discredit Our revelations, it is they who will suffer the ˹worst˺ torment of agonizing pain.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Effort, Not Mere Opinion, Is What Is Being Punished**

The Arabic is *alladhīna saʿaw fī āyātinā muʿājizīn*, and the first verb decides the meaning of the sentence. *Saʿā* is a verb of physical exertion — to run, to walk briskly, to put effort into a task; it is the same root used for the ritual hurrying between Ṣafā and Marwah, and for earning a living. The people described here are not doubters who kept their doubts to themselves. They worked at their opposition. *Muʿājizīn* then names the aim of that work: to render something *ʿājiz*, incapable, frustrated, unable to deliver on what it promised. So the picture is of men labouring to make the revelation look impotent — arranging for the recitation to be drowned out, buying stories to rival it, spreading the rumour of madness that this sūrah will later quote in their own words (34:8). The distinction between honest disbelief and organised sabotage runs through the whole Qur'anic treatment of the matter, and this verse punishes the second. A man may fail to be convinced; that is one thing. Setting out to ensure that nobody else is convinced is a different act with a different consequence.

**A Sentence Built as the Exact Mirror of the One Before It**

Verse 4 and verse 5 are constructed as a matched pair, and reading them as a unit shows how tightly the Qur'an builds its contrasts. Both begin by naming a group by what it did rather than what it called itself. Both use the emphatic construction *ulāʾika lahum* — it is **they** who will have — which in Arabic isolates the group and assigns the outcome to them specifically, with the implication that it belongs to nobody else. The one promises forgiveness and an honourable provision; the other promises torment of agonizing pain. Even the rhythm of the two verses matches. This is not ornament. Placing the two destinations in identical grammatical frames removes the suggestion that reward is the system's normal output and punishment an exception forced on a reluctant judge, or the reverse. Both are outputs of the same reckoning described in verse 3, produced by the same record, and distinguished only by what the parties did with the revelation that reached them.

**Doubling the Words for Pain, and the Old Reading That Explains It**

The closing phrase piles one word of suffering on another: *ʿadhābun min rijzin alīm*, a punishment of *rijz* that is painful. *Rijz* is an unusual choice. It appears in the Qur'an for the plague-like scourges sent on Pharaoh's people (7:134), and in al-Anfāl for the defilement or agitation that Satan casts into a heart, which the rain washes away (8:11). Its root carries the sense of something unstable, unclean and distressing at once. Early readers of the Arabic text noticed that the vowel on *alīm* could be read two ways, attaching the adjective either to the punishment or to the *rijz* itself, which yields "a painful punishment of defilement" alongside "the torment of a painful punishment." Both readings survive in the tradition and neither changes the doctrine. What the doubling achieves is a refusal of the comfortable idea that punishment in the next world is simply the absence of reward. The vocabulary here is drawn from plague and pollution — conditions that spread, cling, and cannot be washed off by the sufferer.

**The Same People, Tracked Across the Book**

This exact description recurs, which lets the phrase be read as a standing category rather than a one-off insult. Al-Ḥajj places the identical clause in a verse that names their address: *"But those who strive to discredit Our revelations, they will be the residents of the Hellfire"* (22:51), immediately after describing the believers in the same words Sabaʾ used a verse earlier. Al-Jāthiyah attaches the same closing phrase to a different subject: *"And those who deny their Lord’s revelations will suffer the ˹worst˺ torment of agonizing pain"* (45:11). Al-Rūm supplies the courtroom image: those who denied the signs and the meeting with their Lord *"will be confined in punishment"* (30:16). The sūrah itself will return to these people once more before it finishes (34:38), which makes them one of the few groups Sabaʾ names twice. The repetition across chapters is how the Qur'an builds a definition: not by supplying a dictionary entry, but by showing the same description in enough settings that its boundaries become clear.

**What "Our Revelations" Covers, and Why the Breadth Matters**

*Āyāt* is among the widest words in the Book. It means a sign, a mark, a token, and it is used for the verses of the Qur'an, for the natural order read as evidence, for the miracles given to earlier prophets, and for the events that precede the end of time. Nothing in this verse narrows it, so all of these are in range. That breadth changes who is implicated. A man who never argued a line of scripture in his life but worked hard to make sure nobody around him could read the evidence of the created world as evidence — that man is inside the description as written. Sabaʾ makes the connection itself a few verses later, when it points at the sky and the ground overhead and underfoot and calls the arrangement *"a sign for every servant who turns ˹to Allah˺"* (34:9). Signs of the second kind are harder to discredit than recited verses, which is perhaps why the sūrah keeps returning to them when the argument over the Qur'an's authorship reaches deadlock.

**A Warning Aimed at Organisers Rather Than Ordinary Deniers**

The social reality behind this verse is worth stating plainly, because it explains the severity. In Mecca the opposition to the new message was not a diffuse mood; it was coordinated by men with standing, money and reach, and the sūrah later records their side of the argument in detail — the mockery of resurrection (34:7), the charge of fabrication and madness (34:8), the boast of wealth and children (34:35), the accusation of sorcery (34:43). Those verses describe speech. This one describes work. The people it condemns are the ones who converted private rejection into a programme, and the Qur'an consistently treats the leadership of misguidance as a heavier matter than being misled — a distinction the sūrah itself will dramatise later when followers and leaders argue before their Lord. Having set the two outcomes side by side, the passage now turns to a third group, whose response to the revelation was neither sabotage nor indifference.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:6

> Those gifted with knowledge ˹clearly˺ see that what has been revealed to you from your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ is the truth, and that it guides to the Path of the Almighty, the Praiseworthy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Knowledge Described as Something Received**

The phrase is *alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilm* — those who have been **given** knowledge. The verb is passive, and the passive is the argument. Arabic could easily have said "the learned" or "the people of knowledge," and the Qur'an does use such expressions; choosing the passive participle instead makes knowledge a thing handed over rather than a thing accumulated. This reverses the ordinary picture in which the scholar is an achiever and his learning a possession he has won. On the Qur'anic account he is a recipient, which is why the same construction appears where knowledge produces humility rather than pride: *"Allah will elevate those of you who are faithful, and ˹raise˺ those gifted with knowledge in rank"* (58:11), and *"Of all of Allah’s servants, only the knowledgeable ˹of His might˺ are ˹truly˺ in awe of Him"* (35:28). The sūrah places these people immediately after the saboteurs of the previous verse, and the contrast is precise. One group works to make the signs fail; the other has been given the capacity to see what the signs are.

**Who These Knowers Actually Were**

The identity of the group has been read three ways, and the grounds for each are worth separating. Some early authorities took it to mean the Companions of the Prophet, since they are the ones present in the sūrah's own setting and the ones whose recognition of the revelation is at issue. Others read it as the scholars among the People of the Book, because that reading has direct support in a nearby verse: al-Isrāʾ describes how the recitation affects *"those who were gifted with knowledge before it ˹was revealed˺"* (17:107), and al-Ḥajj uses the same phrase for those who recognise the revelation as truth and submit to it inwardly (22:54). A third reading declines to restrict the phrase at all: anyone with real knowledge, of whatever community, since genuine knowledge on this account leads to truth by its nature. The third reading is the strongest, and not only because it is the most generous. The verse gives no qualifier of community, and the argument it is making would be weakened by one — the point is that knowledge as such recognises the revelation, which is worth saying only if the claim is general.

**Recognition Supplied as Testimony in an Argument**

This verse functions inside a dispute, and its role is evidential. The deniers have asserted that the Hour will not come (34:3) and will shortly assert that the messenger is a liar or a madman (34:8). Against assertion, the sūrah sets a witness: there exists a class of people who, on examining the revelation, see it as true. Testimony of this kind is weak if the witnesses are partisan, which is why the identification with the People of the Book carried force in the original setting — Jewish and Christian scholars had no stake in the new preacher's success, and some of them recognised the message anyway. The Qur'an uses the same evidentiary move elsewhere, ranking knowers alongside angels as witnesses to the divine unity: *"Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and so are the angels and people of knowledge"* (3:18). Knowledge here is not being praised for its own sake. It is being called to the stand.

**What They See: Truth First, Then Direction**

The verse gives the content of their recognition in two clauses, and the order of the two is a small piece of epistemology. First, that the revelation is *al-ḥaqq*, the truth — a claim about correspondence, about the thing being so. Second, that it *guides to the Path of the Almighty, the Praiseworthy* — a claim about function, about where following it leads. These are separable judgments. A statement can be true and lead nowhere; a road can be useful and start from a lie. What the knowers see is both together, and seeing both is what distinguishes recognition from mere assent. The Arabic word for the path here is *ṣirāṭ*, borrowed into Arabic from Latin *strata* by way of Syriac, and it means a paved public road rather than a private track — the same word used in the prayer every Muslim repeats in every unit of prayer. Truth that opens onto a public road is the Qur'an's recurring image for revelation, and it is set here against a rival picture in which the message is a private invention.

**Two Names That Answer the Two Complaints**

The path leads to *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥamīd* — the Almighty, the Praiseworthy. The pairing is chosen, not automatic. *ʿAzīz* comes from a root meaning to be rare, strong, inaccessible; an *ʿizz* is a fortified height. The name carries power that cannot be overcome. *Ḥamīd* is from the same root as the *ḥamd* with which this sūrah opened (34:1), and means the One who deserves praise, not merely receives it. Naming both at the end of this verse answers the two things the opponents held against the message. They thought its followers powerless, a handful of the poor around a rejected preacher; the first name says where the real strength is. They thought the message ignoble, a break with the honour of their fathers; the second says where praise properly belongs. Al-Ibrāhīm uses the identical pair at the head of a sūrah, describing the Book as sent down to bring people out of darkness to the path *of the Almighty, the Praiseworthy*, which shows the phrase functioning as a standing description of the destination rather than a decorative close.

**A Madinan Verse Inside a Meccan Sūrah?**

Some scholars have held that this single verse was revealed at Medina while the rest of Sabaʾ is Meccan, and the suggestion is usually tied to the reading that identifies the knowers with the People of the Book, whose scholars became a live question only after the move north. The claim is reported in the commentary tradition rather than established by a report from the Prophet, and the sūrah reads perfectly well without it: a Meccan audience also contained men of learning, and the argument from recognition works in either city. What the suggestion usefully highlights is how differently this verse behaves from its neighbours. Around it the sūrah is quoting hostile speech and answering it. Here, for one verse, the voice steps back and describes a reaction rather than a rejection — a pause that makes the return to mockery in the next verse land harder.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:7

> The disbelievers say ˹mockingly to one another˺, “Shall we show you a man who claims that when you have been utterly disintegrated you will be raised as a new creation?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mockery Staged as an Invitation**

The sentence is built as an offer: *hal nadullukum* — shall we point you to, shall we direct you to. In ordinary Arabic this is the phrasing of a guide showing the way to something valuable, and the Qur'an uses it that way elsewhere. Here the speakers borrow the form of helpful direction and fill it with ridicule. That inversion is the whole rhetorical strategy of the verse. They are not arguing against resurrection; they are producing the man who teaches it as a curiosity to be looked at, the way one might point out an eccentric in the market. Notice also that they are speaking to each other, not to him. The Prophet is referred to in the third person — *a man* — while the audience being recruited is the crowd. Contempt that addresses its target directly at least concedes that the target is worth addressing. Contempt that talks about him in front of him is denying him standing in the conversation altogether, and the Qur'an preserves the exact grammar of that denial.

**"A Man" — The Deliberate Withholding of a Name**

They do not say Muḥammad, and they do not say the one who recites. They say *rajulun*, a man, with the indefinite ending that in Arabic can carry dismissal: some man, a certain man. Two things are being accomplished at once. The indefinite reduces a known figure from their own tribe to an anonymous specimen, and it quietly denies the category he claims. He is not a messenger, not a warner, just a man — which is precisely the objection their predecessors raised against earlier prophets in almost the same words: *"We see you only as a human being like ourselves"* (11:27). The Qur'an's answer to that objection runs throughout the Book and is not given here. What this verse records instead is the social mechanics of dismissal: strip the name, supply an indefinite noun, and the audience has been told how to hear whatever comes next before a word of content has been spoken.

**The Word They Chose for Decay**

The strongest word in their sentence is *muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq* — torn apart with a complete tearing, rendered here as *utterly disintegrated*. The root *m-z-q* means to rip fabric, and the form used is intensive, then reinforced by its own cognate noun, a construction Arabic reserves for maximum emphasis. They are not describing death politely. They are describing a corpse pulled to shreds and scattered until no piece is identifiable. Their choice of vocabulary is the argument. Other Qur'anic passages report the same objection in milder terms — bones and dust (17:49), decayed bones (36:78), dust alone (50:3) — but these speakers go further, selecting the most violent image available so that the claim of reassembly sounds maximally absurd. The verse also contains a small irony they did not intend. The same verb returns later in this very sūrah, when the people of Sheba are *scattered utterly* (34:19) as a punishment. The word they use to mock the power to reassemble is the word describing what that power does to a civilisation.

**The Objection Is About Identity, Not Just Physics**

What exactly did they find impossible? Not the existence of divine power in general — they conceded a creator. The difficulty is the one their wording exposes: if a person has been torn into unidentifiable pieces, what would it even mean to bring *him* back rather than something new? Their phrase *khalqin jadīd*, a new creation, contains the problem. A new creation is new; the old one is gone. The Qur'an meets this objection at several points by refusing the premise that scattering equals erasure, which is the work the earlier verses of this sūrah have already done — the Record that holds everything down to an atom's weight (34:3), and the knowledge of everything that goes into the earth (34:2). Yā-Sīn answers the same complaint by pointing at the first making: *"They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being"* (36:79). The reply turns on knowledge as much as on power, which is why this sūrah spent its opening verses establishing knowledge.

**"New Creation" Is Their Phrase, and the Qur'an Keeps It**

The expression *khalq jadīd* recurs across the Book, and almost always in the mouths of deniers. Al-Isrāʾ: *"When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?"* (17:49). Al-Raʿd reports the question and comments on the questioners: *"When we are reduced to dust, will we really be raised as a new creation?"* (13:5). Al-Muʾminūn puts it as a chief's sarcasm to his people (23:35). What is striking is that the Qur'an does not replace the term with one of its own. It adopts the deniers' phrase and affirms it — yes, a new creation, exactly as you say, and the newness is not the difficulty you take it to be. Sabaʾ has already shown the mechanism in its second verse: things go into the earth and things come out of it, continuously, and nobody watching a field regards the emergence as a violation of anything. The objection depends on treating one instance of a familiar pattern as uniquely impossible.

**Why the Mockery Is Quoted at Length Instead of Summarised**

The Qur'an could have said that they denied the resurrection, and moved on. Instead it hands them the microphone and lets the sentence run its full length, complete with the stagey invitation, the anonymous *a man*, and the shredding verb. There is a reason for the method. Ridicule works by tone rather than content, and tone cannot be refuted by paraphrase — it has to be reproduced and then allowed to sit in the open where its emptiness shows. Read cold, the sentence contains no argument at all: it names a claim, describes it vividly, and expects laughter to do the rest. By preserving it verbatim, the sūrah lets its audience notice that. The technique recurs through this chapter, which quotes its opponents more often than most — their denial of the Hour (34:3), their boast about wealth (34:35), their charge of sorcery (34:43). Their sentence is not yet finished; it runs on into the next verse, where the mockery finally produces two accusations.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:8

> Has he fabricated a lie against Allah or is he insane?” In fact, those who do not believe in the Hereafter are bound for torment and have strayed farthest ˹from the truth˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Charges Offered as a Closed Choice**

The question is framed as a disjunction with exactly two branches: either he invented this deliberately, or he is out of his mind. Arabic marks the alternatives with *am*, the particle used for a genuine either-or, so the speakers are presenting their audience with what looks like a complete set of options. It is not complete, and the missing third branch is the one the whole sūrah exists to argue for: that he is telling the truth. This is the oldest debating trick there is — narrow the field before anyone notices, then let the audience choose freely between two answers that both convict. The charges themselves are shrewdly picked because they are opposites. Fabrication requires full possession of one's faculties; madness rules it out. A man cannot be both a calculating forger and a helpless lunatic, which means the accusers do not actually hold a position. They hold a demand: any explanation but the obvious one.

**Fabrication: The Charge That Concedes the Most**

*Iftarā* means to cut out and stitch together, hence to invent a story — the same verb behind *iftirāʾ*, a manufactured accusation. Calling the message a fabrication concedes a great deal. It admits the Qur'an is coherent enough to have been composed, forceful enough to be worth composing, and effective enough to be worth opposing. The Book records this accusation often: *"You ˹Muḥammad˺ are just a fabricator"* (16:101), and *"This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but a fabrication which he made up with the help of others"* (25:4). Sabaʾ itself will hear it again later from the same quarter (34:43). What makes the charge implausible on its own terms is motive. A forger forges for gain, and the sūrah eventually puts that test to the accusers directly by having the Prophet renounce any payment: *"If I had ever asked you for a reward, you could keep it"* (34:47). Note too what the charge is against: a lie *against Allah*. Even the accusers assume the seriousness of speaking falsely in God's name, which means they share more theology with their opponent than they admit.

**Madness: The Cheaper Charge, and the Older One**

*Jinnah* is the second accusation, and the word comes from the root *j-n-n*, to cover or conceal — the same root that gives *jinn*, the hidden beings, and *janīn*, the covered thing in the womb. To be *majnūn* in Arabic usage is to have one's reason veiled, classically by contact with the jinn. This is not an accusation of illness in the modern clinical sense but of possession: the man is speaking, but something else is doing the speaking. The charge follows the prophets everywhere in the Qur'an. Al-Ḥijr: *"O you to whom the Reminder is revealed! You must be insane!"* (15:6). Al-Ṣāffāt reports the Meccans asking whether they should abandon their gods *"for a mad poet"* (37:36). Al-Dukhān records it of Pharaoh's court toward Moses: *"A madman, taught by others!"* (44:14). The denial is issued plainly in al-Qalam: *"By the grace of your Lord, you ˹O Prophet˺ are not insane"* (68:2). And this sūrah will not leave it standing either; it answers the charge with a procedure rather than an assertion, telling the accusers to stand apart, singly and in pairs, and think (34:46).

**The Verse Turns and Answers in Its Own Voice**

Halfway through, the quotation stops and the sūrah speaks: *bal* — in fact, on the contrary. This particle is the hinge. It does not engage the disjunction on its own terms, does not defend the Prophet's honesty or his sanity point by point. It redirects the whole question to the accusers and diagnoses them instead. The move is not evasion; it is a claim about where the real disorder lies. Men who describe careful speech as madness and a consistent message as a forgery have a problem of judgment, and judgment is what the rest of the verse addresses. The rhetorical effect of the abrupt switch is considerable. A reader following the taunt is carried along by its confidence, and then the ground moves: the people doing the diagnosing are the ones being diagnosed.

**Disbelief in the Hereafter Named as the Root Cause**

The verse does not identify the accusers as those who rejected the Prophet, or those who defended the idols. It identifies them as *those who do not believe in the Hereafter*. That is a diagnosis, and it explains the behaviour recorded in the previous verse. A man certain there is no reckoning has no reason to examine a warning carefully, because nothing turns on getting it right; ridicule costs him nothing and buys him social standing. The whole architecture of the accusation depends on that certainty. Sabaʾ has been building toward this identification since its opening exchange, where the same people announced that the Hour would never reach them (34:3). Here the sūrah connects that denial to its consequences in character. Disbelief in the Hereafter is not treated as one opinion among several but as the hinge on which a person's seriousness turns.

**"Strayed Farthest" — The Sentence Passed on the Judges**

The closing phrase is *al-ḍalāl al-baʿīd*, error at a far distance, translated here as having *strayed farthest*. Arabic error-words are spatial, and this one keeps its geography: the further from the road, the harder the return. But the verse says two things, not one: they are bound for torment **and** they have strayed far. Are these separate penalties or the same thing twice? The stronger reading takes them as sequential and linked — the punishment to come is the consequence, the straying is the present condition. Yet there is a way of hearing the second clause as already a punishment in itself. To be so far off course that a truthful man looks insane to you is a state worth pitying, whatever waits afterwards. The verse leaves both readings open by simply setting the two clauses side by side with a conjunction. What it does not leave open is the direction of the judgment. The accusers opened with two options about the Prophet; the verse closes with one verdict about them.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:9

> Have they not then seen all that surrounds them of the heavens and the earth? If We willed, We could cause the earth to swallow them up, or cause ˹deadly˺ pieces of the sky to fall upon them. Surely in this is a sign for every servant who turns ˹to Allah˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Argument That Requires Nothing but Looking Up**

After two verses of quoted mockery, the reply does not cite scripture or invoke authority. It asks a question about the visible world: have they not looked at what is in front of them and behind them, above and below? The Arabic is *mā bayna aydīhim wa mā khalfahum* — literally what is between their hands and what is behind them, an idiom for the whole surrounding field of vision. The choice of evidence is exact for the dispute. These men have just declared bodily reassembly impossible; the answer points at the largest assembled objects in existence and asks how they got there and what holds them. The Qur'an makes this move often, and al-Rūm makes the mechanism explicit: *"And one of His signs is that the heavens and the earth persist by His command"* (30:25). Yā-Sīn draws the inference the deniers refuse: *"Can the One Who created the heavens and the earth not ˹easily˺ resurrect these ˹deniers˺?"* (36:81). What is offered is not a proof from a book they reject but a proof from a sky they cannot.

**Stability Read as Mercy Rather Than as Furniture**

The unstated premise of the verse is that the persistence of the world is not automatic. Ground stays solid and the sky stays up, and both facts are so constant that they drop below notice — which is exactly why they make a good sign and a poor argument. The verse repairs the blindness by naming the two ways the arrangement could fail: the earth could open, the sky could come down in pieces. Suddenly the ordinary condition is visible as a condition, something being maintained rather than something simply given. This is the deeper claim behind the threat. A world that could be undone at any moment and is not being undone is a world under continuous restraint, and restraint by a power that could do otherwise is mercy. The sūrah has already named the One in question *the Most Merciful, All-Forgiving* (34:2). Here that mercy is shown operating as the absence of a catastrophe, which is the hardest form of kindness for human beings to register.

**Two Punishments Drawn From Actual History**

Neither threat is invented for the occasion; both are drawn from things the Qur'an reports as having happened. Being swallowed by the earth is what befell Qārūn: *"Then We caused the earth to swallow him up, along with his home. There was no one to help him against Allah"* (28:81). Stones from the sky are among the catalogued ends of earlier peoples: *"against some of them We sent a storm of stones, some were overtaken by a ˹mighty˺ blast, some We caused the earth to swallow, and some We drowned"* (29:40). And the Book twice asks the precise question this verse implies, once of plotters — *"Do those who devise evil plots feel secure that Allah will not cause the earth to swallow them?"* (16:45) — and once of everyone: *"Do you feel secure that the One Who is in heaven will not cause the earth to swallow you up as it quakes violently?"* (67:16). The two directions also complete the geography set up at the start of the verse. Danger is not at the edges of the visible field; it is the field itself, above and below.

**A Dare Their Predecessors Made, Quoted Back at Them**

There is a sharp historical echo in the second threat. The people of Shuʿayb, refusing his warning, taunted him with exactly this: *"So cause ˹deadly˺ pieces of the sky to fall upon us, if what you say is true"* (26:187). The Meccan opponents of this sūrah stood in the same posture — safe, prosperous, certain that nothing would arrive — and the phrasing here puts the old dare in front of them as a live possibility rather than a rhetorical flourish. The difference between the two passages is instructive. Shuʿayb's people issued the challenge themselves and were answered. Here the possibility is raised by the revelation before anyone has dared it, and raised in the conditional: *if We willed*. That conditional is the whole distance between a threat and a sentence. The power is stated, the exercise of it is withheld, and the interval between the two is where the addressees are still standing.

**Why the Sign Is Given Only to the One Who Turns**

The verse ends by narrowing its audience: *a sign for every servant who turns ˹to Allah˺*. The Arabic is *ʿabdin munīb*, from the root *n-w-b*, which means to return repeatedly to a place — the same root gives *nawbah*, a turn or a recurring shift. So the *munīb* is not the man who repented once but the one who keeps coming back. The restriction is not arbitrary favouritism. The same sky stands over everyone, and the evidence is equally available; what differs is the disposition that makes evidence legible. A man determined that there is nothing to see will look at the same heavens and find only weather. Qāf uses the identical phrase after a passage on rain, seed and palm trees, calling the whole arrangement *"an insight and a reminder to every servant who turns ˹to Allah˺"* (50:8), and these two verses are the only places in the Book where this exact description appears. Both attach it to the created order rather than to recited scripture, which suggests the returning servant is precisely the one who reads the world as writing.

**Reflection Named as the Escape From the Threat**

Placing the sign-clause after the two catastrophes rather than before them changes what the verse is doing. Had the order been reversed, this would be a warning with a pious ending attached. As written, the catastrophes are themselves part of what is to be reflected on, and reflection is offered as the way out of them. The logic is simple enough to state plainly: a man who genuinely considers that the ground beneath him is held and the sky above him is held will not be able to keep treating the warning as a joke, and the seriousness he arrives at is the thing that averts the outcome. This is why the sūrah keeps returning to thinking as a religious act, and why its later answer to the charge of madness is not a defence but an instruction to stand up and reflect (34:46). The chapter now leaves the argument with the deniers and turns to two kings who read the world correctly.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:10

> Indeed, We granted David a ˹great˺ privilege from Us, ˹commanding:˺ “O mountains! Echo his hymns! And the birds as well.” We made iron mouldable for him,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Gift Named Before It Is Described**

The verse opens with *ātaynā Dāwūda minnā faḍlā* — We gave David a bounty from Us. The word *faḍl* means surplus, what exceeds the necessary; it is favour beyond entitlement rather than payment for service. Two details in the phrasing matter. The gift is qualified by *minnā*, from Us, which locates its source before naming its content and rules out the reading that David's powers were talents he developed. And the content is withheld for a clause, so that the reader meets the fact of privilege before learning what the privilege was. Commentators have read *faḍl* here as prophethood and revealed scripture, pointing to *"And to David We gave the Psalms"* (4:163), or as the judgment and knowledge granted to him and his son together: *"Indeed, We granted knowledge to David and Solomon"* (27:15). Both readings are compatible with the verse, which names no single item. What the sūrah needs at this point in its argument is the category itself. It has just been dealing with men who measured worth by wealth, and it now introduces a figure whose distinction came from elsewhere.

**Mountains Told to Answer a Human Voice**

The command is addressed to the landscape: *yā jibālu awwibī maʿahu* — O mountains, return the praise with him. The verb *awwaba* is from the root *ʾ-w-b*, to return, the same root as *maʾāb*, a place of return. It carries the sense of repeating, coming back again and again, which is why the translation reaches for *echo*. The image is precise and physical. A voice raised in a mountain valley comes back, and the Qur'an describes that returning sound as the mountains joining in. The birds are added without a new verb, folded into the same command. Parallel passages fill out the scene: al-Anbiyāʾ says the mountains and birds were subjected *"to hymn ˹Our praises˺ along with David"* (21:79), and Ṣād supplies the timing — *"in the evening and after sunrise"* (38:18) — with the birds *"flocking together. All turned to him ˹echoing his hymns˺"* (38:19). Morning and evening are the hours when a herdsman or a king would be out on the hills. The miracle is placed inside the working day rather than in a sanctuary.

**Why the Non-Human World Would Join a Man at All**

The obvious question is what makes David's voice worth answering. The Qur'an's frame for this is stated in the same sūrah where David appears as king: *"O David! We have surely made you an authority in the land, so judge between people with truth"* (38:26). The word there is *khalīfah*, a deputy or successor, the term used at the creation of the first human. A creature appointed to represent the whole order on earth is, on this understanding, a being in whom the rest of the order is summed up — so when he praises, what he sums up praises with him. There is a second reading worth keeping alongside it. The Qur'an elsewhere asserts that everything already glorifies its Maker: *"Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth ˹constantly˺ glorifies Allah"* (62:1). On that reading David's gift was not that the mountains began praising but that his praise fell into audible step with a chorus already running. Nothing in the verse decides between these, and the two are not rivals.

**Iron Made Soft: A Miracle Placed in a Workshop**

The last clause changes register completely: *wa alannā lahu al-ḥadīd*, We made iron soft for him. The verb *alāna* is from *layyin*, soft or pliant, the word used of a supple branch or a gentle manner. Iron is the Qur'an's standing example of hardness and force — al-Ḥadīd names a whole sūrah and describes the metal as sent down *"with its great might, benefits for humanity"* (57:25). Making the hardest available material behave like clay in one man's hands is a striking gift, but the striking thing is what it was for. Not a weapon of legend, not an invincible sword; the next verse will show him making body armour. The tradition has read this softening in two ways: as a suspension of the metal's nature in his hands, or as the teaching of smithcraft — the knowledge of heat and hammer by which iron does in fact become workable. The second reading has the advantage of matching how the Qur'an describes the outcome elsewhere: *"We taught him the art of making body armour to protect you in battle"* (21:80). Taught, not simply enabled.

**Prophet, King, Singer, Smith**

What the verse assembles is a portrait unusual among the prophets. David holds political authority, receives revelation, sings, and works metal with his hands, and the Qur'an presents no tension between these. The combination is not incidental to the sūrah's argument. Sabaʾ is speaking to men who believed that God's favour showed itself in wealth and status and who will shortly say so out loud (34:35). David had wealth and status in abundance, and the verse mentions neither. It mentions a voice the hills answered and a craft learned at a forge. The Prophet Muḥammad is reported to have singled out that manual labour for praise, saying that nobody has eaten better food than what he earns with his own hands, and that the prophet of God David used to eat from the earnings of his hand (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2072, reported from al-Miqdām; the point is repeated at Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2073 from Abū Hurayrah). A king who could have lived off the treasury ate from the anvil.

**The Word That Ties This Story to the Rest of the Sūrah**

*Faḍl* is a keyword here, and its reappearance is worth tracking. Solomon's inheritance is described with the same idea when he says he and his father were *"privileged"* over many believing servants (27:15), and the whole David-and-Solomon section of this sūrah is framed as gift rather than achievement. That framing is why the section sits where it does. Between the mockery of the resurrection deniers and the ruin of Sheba, the sūrah places two men who received extraordinary favour and handled it correctly — and Sheba, which received a smaller favour and did not. The comparison is structural rather than stated. Read alone, this verse is about echoing mountains and soft iron; read in position, it is the opening term of an argument about what favour is for. The instruction attached to the iron follows in the next verse.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:11

> instructing: “Make full-length armour, ˹perfectly˺ balancing the links. And work righteousness ˹O family of David!˺. Indeed, I am All-Seeing of what you do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Miracle Is Issued as a Work Order**

Softened iron arrives with instructions attached, and the instructions are technical: *an iʿmal sābighāt* — make ample, full-length coats. *Sābighāt* comes from the root *s-b-gh*, to be complete or to reach fully; the same root produces *isbāgh al-wuḍūʾ*, performing the ablution thoroughly, and *niʿmah sābighah*, a favour that covers everything. Applied to mail, it means armour long enough to protect the whole body rather than a fashionable half-piece. Nothing in the commission is ceremonial. A prophet-king has been handed control of the hardest substance available and is told to produce defensive equipment to a specified standard. This is a distinctive feature of how the Qur'an handles the miraculous. The extraordinary capacity is not the story's climax; it is an input to ordinary work, and the story's interest lies in what the work is for. Al-Naḥl lists armour among the favours of God to humanity generally, alongside shade and clothing: *"and armour shielding you in battle"* (16:81). David's gift lets him supply, at scale, a protection the Book treats as a mercy.

**Measure the Links: Craftsmanship Given as a Command**

The second half of the instruction is the striking one: *wa qaddir fī al-sard*. *Sard* is the technical term for the interlinking of rings in mail, the row-upon-row structure of chain armour, and *qaddir* means to measure, proportion, get the quantity right. The command is about gauge. Rings too heavy and the wearer cannot move; too light and a spear point passes through. Rivets too thick tear the plate, too thin and they shear. What is being given the force of a divine instruction is accurate workmanship in a specific trade. This sits oddly beside the assumption that revelation concerns itself only with belief and ritual, which is exactly why it repays notice. A badly made coat of mail is not a technical failure with a moral remainder; it is a man killed in a battle line because his armourer was careless. Precision in the workshop is being treated as a religious obligation and placed in the same sentence as righteousness, joined by nothing more than *and*.

**"Work Righteousness" — The Same Verb, Extended**

The instruction continues *waʿmalū ṣāliḥan* — and do good work. English hides what the Arabic makes obvious: the verb is the same one used a clause earlier for making the armour, *iʿmal*. Make coats; make good. The repetition welds craft and conduct into one activity rather than two. The root of *ṣāliḥ* reinforces this, since *ṣ-l-ḥ* means to be sound, fit, in working order — a word as natural for a well-made object as for a well-lived life, with *fasād*, rot, as its opposite in both senses. So the passage moves from a soundly built coat to a soundly built life using vocabulary that does not change gear. Notice also the shift from singular to plural. The armour command is addressed to David alone; *work righteousness* is plural, and the translation supplies the addressee the Arabic implies from the following words: the family of David. The gift was personal, the responsibility is collective, and the household that benefits from the privilege is folded into its obligations.

**A Household Named, and Held to Account**

*Āl Dāwūd*, the family of David, becomes a standing phrase in the Qur'an's treatment of this dynasty, and it recurs two verses later when Solomon's workforce is told to *"Work gratefully, O family of David!"* (34:13). Naming a house rather than an individual does something particular in a tribal setting, where a family's standing was its most valuable asset and its faults were absorbed by its status. Here the household is addressed directly and given duties, which cuts against the assumption that a great lineage is itself a form of merit. The sūrah will return to that assumption with hostility when it quotes men boasting of wealth and children as proof of divine approval (34:35). Against that, the family of David is presented as a house whose distinction consists in the work it is told to do. The line of the prophet-king does not inherit protection from the command; it inherits the command.

**The Closing Clause Is a Supervisor's Sentence**

*Innī bimā taʿmalūna baṣīr* — I am All-Seeing of what you do. The verb again is *ʿamal*, work, for the third time in two lines, so the sentence reads as an inspection notice on the very activity just commissioned. The name invoked is *Baṣīr*, from *baṣar*, eyesight: not knowledge in the abstract but watching. This is a workshop clause. An armourer working for a distant king can cut corners in the certainty that the king will never count his rivets, and the man who discovers the shortfall is a soldier too far away to complain. The clause removes the distance. The same formula closes verses on charity — *"Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do"* (2:110) — and stands as a warning to those who abuse the revelations (41:40), which shows it is not a decorative name but a standing statement about unobserved conduct. Placed here, it supplies the reason why measuring the links is a religious matter rather than a professional preference.

**Power Handed Over With Its Conditions Attached**

Reading the two verses together, the sequence is: a favour given, a capacity conferred, a task specified, a standard of quality imposed, a moral instruction attached, and a reminder of supervision. At no point is the power left free-standing. This is the Qur'an's consistent handling of what it grants to prophets — the capacity arrives inside a set of obligations, and the obligations are as much a part of the gift as the capacity. The pattern also sets up the contrast that governs this whole section of Sabaʾ. David is given control over the hardest material on earth and told to make protection and to work well. The people of Sheba will be given two orchards and told to eat and be grateful (34:15), and the difference in their outcomes will turn on nothing more complicated than whether the instruction attached to the gift was obeyed. Solomon's inheritance follows next, and with it a larger command over the natural order.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:12

> And to Solomon ˹We subjected˺ the wind: its morning stride was a month’s journey and so was its evening stride. And We caused a stream of molten copper to flow for him, and ˹We subjected˺ some of the jinn to work under him by his Lord’s Will. And whoever of them deviated from Our command, We made them taste the torment of the blaze.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb Left Out, and What Fills the Gap**

The Arabic opens with *wa li-Sulaymāna al-rīḥ* — and to Solomon, the wind — with no verb between them. Classical Arabic permits this ellipsis, and the reader supplies the missing word from context. Two candidates fit. One carries over *We gave* from the account of David two verses earlier, which keeps the whole passage under the heading of gift. The other imports *We subjected* from the parallel passages, as in Ṣād: *"So We subjected to him the wind, blowing gently at his command to wherever he pleased"* (38:36), and al-Anbiyāʾ: *"And to Solomon We subjected the raging winds, blowing by his command"* (21:81). The translation here takes the second, and the choice is sound because subjection is the technical relationship the Qur'an uses for Solomon throughout. The omission itself is worth a moment. By leaving the verb out, the sentence puts Solomon and the wind in direct grammatical contact, with no action word standing between them — a compression that matches the content.

**A Month in the Morning, a Month in the Evening**

The measurement is given in units a caravan trader would understand: *ghudūwuhā shahrun wa rawāḥuhā shahr*. *Ghudūw* is the outward journey at first light; *rawāḥ* is the return leg in the late afternoon. Both are commercial terms from the vocabulary of travel, not poetic words for time of day. So the claim is quantitative: a stage of travel that would take a caravan a month was covered before midday and again before nightfall. Stating a miracle in the units of a trade route rather than in superlatives is characteristic of this sūrah, which spoke of an atom's weight on the merchant's scale a few verses earlier (34:3). The audience being addressed included men who ran caravans north to Syria in summer and south to Yemen in winter and knew precisely what a month of travel cost in animals, water and risk. A measurement they could price is a more effective statement of the gift than any adjective.

**Molten Copper and the Industrial Base of a Kingdom**

*ʿAyn al-qiṭr* is literally the spring of molten metal — *qiṭr* is copper or brass in a liquid state, and *ʿayn* is the ordinary word for a water spring. The image is a wellhead running with molten metal instead of water. The parallel with David is deliberate and the progression is clear: the father was given iron made pliant in his hands, the son is given a source of liquid copper flowing continuously. One is a craftsman's gift, the other a foundry's. The Qur'an elsewhere shows molten copper as a serious engineering material in the hands of another powerful figure, Dhū al-Qarnayn, who has iron blocks heated and then calls for *"molten copper to pour over it"* (18:96) to seal a barrier. Read alongside the workforce named in the same verse and the constructions listed in the next, the copper is not a marvel on display. It is the material supply for a building programme.

**Jinn Conscripted, and the Two Limits on Their Service**

The third grant is labour: *wa min al-jinni man yaʿmalu bayna yadayhi* — some of the jinn who worked before him. The partitive *min* is doing real work in the sentence: **some** of them, not the species. Ṣād specifies the trades — *"every builder and diver of the jinn"* (38:37) — and al-Anbiyāʾ mentions those who dived for him and performed other tasks, adding that it was God who kept them in check (21:82). Two limits are written into this verse. The service runs *bi-idhni rabbih*, by his Lord's permission, so the authority is delegated and revocable rather than owned; and it is enforced, since deviation brings punishment. The Qur'an is careful about this boundary because a claim was made in the other direction. Al-Baqarah explicitly denies that Solomon practised sorcery, laying the charge on the devils instead: *"Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved"* (2:102). A king who commands jinn only by permission, and whose command is backed by a punishment he does not administer, is not a magician.

**The Punishment Clause and Where It Lands**

*Wa man yazigh minhum ʿan amrinā nudhiqhu min ʿadhābi al-saʿīr* — whoever of them deviates from **Our** command tastes the torment of the blaze. The possessive is the key word. A jinn assigned to Solomon who downs tools is not disobeying Solomon; he is disobeying the One who assigned him, which is why the penalty is described as divine rather than royal. *Zāgha* means to swerve off a straight line, and *saʿīr* is a blazing fire. Commentators have divided over when the burning occurs: some take it as punishment in the Fire of the next world, others as an immediate scorching in this one, described in the reports as a strike from an angel. The verse does not say, and the ambiguity is not important to its argument. What matters is that the chain of authority is explicit from top to bottom. The king commands, but the sanction belongs to God, and even the most spectacular sovereignty in the Qur'an's telling operates on a licence.

**Sovereignty That Runs on Permission**

Taken whole, the verse grants Solomon control over an element, a material and a species — air, metal, and an order of hidden beings. It is the largest concentration of power the Qur'an attributes to any human figure, and every clause of it is qualified. The wind is subjected by another; the copper is caused to flow by another; the jinn work by his Lord's will and are punished by his Lord's decree. Ṣād records Solomon himself asking for a kingdom that would not suit anyone after him and framing the request as a petition: *"You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺"* (38:35). This is the frame the sūrah needs. It is arguing against men who read prosperity as entitlement and who will shortly claim that their wealth proves they will not be punished (34:35). Against them it sets a king whose powers dwarfed theirs and who held every one of them on loan. The next verse turns to what his conscripted workforce actually built.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:13

> They made for him whatever he desired of sanctuaries, statues, basins as large as reservoirs, and cooking pots fixed ˹into the ground˺. ˹We ordered:˺ “Work gratefully, O family of David!” ˹Only˺ a few of My servants are ˹truly˺ grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Inventory Rather Than a Marvel**

The conscripted workforce of the previous verse is now shown at its output, and the output is listed like a building schedule: sanctuaries, statues, basins, cooking pots. No single item is described in detail, none is called wondrous, and the effect of the list is deliberately prosaic. A king with jinn at his disposal might have produced anything, and what he produced was infrastructure. Two of the four items are for feeding people at scale and one is for washing or storing water at scale. The Qur'an is not usually interested in inventories, so a list this concrete is itself a statement. It brings a story that could have drifted into legend back onto the ground where Solomon's authority actually operated — a working state with public buildings, a water system and kitchens large enough to serve a crowd. The phrase *mā yashāʾ*, whatever he desired, sits at the head of the list and gives it its scope. Unlimited capacity, spent on this.

**maḥārīb: A Word That Travelled**

The first item is *maḥārīb*, plural of *miḥrāb*, translated here as sanctuaries. The word has a history worth tracing because it explains the range of meanings commentators have found in it. In pre-Islamic usage a *miḥrāb* was the private chamber of a house or the innermost, most honoured part of a large tent — a place of retreat, and therefore a place of standing. The Qur'an uses it that way for the chamber where Mary was found supplied with provision (3:37) and for the place from which Zachariah emerged to signal to his people (19:11), and it uses it of the enclosure David's two plaintiffs climbed into (38:21). From that sense of an honoured inner space the word later settled onto the prayer niche in a mosque. Early authorities offered two further derivations: that the place is named for the war (*ḥarb*) waged there against Satan and one's own appetite, or because the worshipper in it is cut off — *ḥarīb* — from the concerns of the world. Applied to Solomon's builders, the word has been read as palaces and halls of state, and as places set aside for worship with the direction of prayer marked. Both readings keep the underlying sense: a space set apart and given honour.

**Statues in the Hands of a Prophet**

*Tamāthīl*, images or statues, is the item that has provoked most discussion, since sculpture of living forms is prohibited in Islamic law and the prophets are not exempt from law. Several answers have been offered, and they divide on what the statues represented. One line holds that they depicted inanimate things — trees, buildings, objects — which falls outside the prohibition as generally formulated. Another holds that images of angels and earlier prophets were permitted under Solomon's law and abrogated later, which is a coherent position since the Qur'an itself records rulings that changed between communities. The verse gives no detail, and the honest reading declines to invent one. What the verse does establish is that the objects were commissioned, not smuggled in: they belong to a list the Qur'an reports without censure, in a passage whose whole argument is that Solomon used his powers correctly. A commentary that turns the item into a fault has to work against the grain of the passage it sits in.

**Basins Like Reservoirs, Pots That Do Not Move**

The last two items are domestic objects scaled up until they are civic. *Jifān ka-l-jawāb* — great dishes like *jawābī*, the word used for the cisterns and watering troughs dug for camels. *Qudūr rāsiyāt* — cooking pots that are *rāsiyah*, the same adjective the Qur'an applies to mountains as anchored and immovable, so these are pots too large to be lifted, built into their setting and filled where they stand. The picture is of continuous public catering: water held in tanks, food cooked in fixed cauldrons, quantities that assume a permanent crowd. This is the least glamorous portion of the list and probably the most revealing. A ruler's greatness is being illustrated by the size of his kitchens. For the Meccan audience of this sūrah, whose own aristocracy measured itself by feeding pilgrims and guests, the detail would not have needed explanation.

**The Command That Interrupts the List**

The inventory breaks off and a voice cuts in: *iʿmalū āla Dāwūda shukrā* — work, family of David, in gratitude. The construction can be read two ways in Arabic, and both are instructive. *Shukrā* may be an accusative of purpose, giving *work in order to give thanks*, or it may be the object of the verb, giving *perform thanks* — with gratitude itself as the work to be done. The second reading makes the sharper point. Gratitude in the Qur'an is rarely an emotion and frequently a labour; it is something performed with the hands and the estate, not felt in private. The addressee is again the household rather than the king, the same plural used when the armour was commissioned (34:11), which ties the two halves of this family's story together. David was told to work righteousness while he worked iron; Solomon's house is told to work gratitude while it builds. Neither instruction separates the craft from the duty attached to it.

**"Few of My Servants Are Grateful" — A Verdict Without Softening**

The verse ends on a sentence with no comfort in it: *wa qalīlun min ʿibādiya al-shakūr*. The form *shakūr* is intensive — not merely one who says thank you but one whose whole disposition is thankfulness — and the claim is that such people are rare. The observation is not new in the Qur'an; it is exactly the prediction Iblīs made when he was given his reprieve: *"then You will find most of them ungrateful"* (7:17), a prediction this sūrah will report as having come true a few verses later (34:20). Placing the verdict here, immediately after a list of spectacular favours, gives it its force. Gratitude is hardest precisely where gifts are most abundant, because abundance normalises itself and what is normal stops being noticed. Ibrāhīm states the stakes of the choice: *"If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful, surely My punishment is severe"* (14:7). The sūrah is about to demonstrate both halves of that sentence on a single people.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:14

> When We decreed Solomon’s death, nothing indicated to the ˹subjected˺ jinn that he was dead except the termites eating away his staff. So when he collapsed, the jinn realized that if they had ˹really˺ known the unseen, they would not have remained in ˹such˺ humiliating servitude.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Greatest Kingdom Ends in a Single Clause**

Everything the previous verses granted — the wind covering a month before noon, the spring of molten copper, the conscripted builders, the cisterns and the fixed cauldrons — is closed off by four words: *fa-lammā qaḍaynā ʿalayhi al-mawt*, when We decreed death for him. No illness, no succession crisis, no last words. The Qur'an gives the death of its most powerful human figure less space than it gave his cooking pots, and the disproportion is the point being made. A reign is described at length because the reign is the subject; the death needs no elaboration because death is the one element of the story that requires no explanation to anyone. The verb *qaḍā* is the word for issuing a binding decree, the same root as *qāḍī*, a judge. Solomon's death is not an event that happened to his kingdom from outside. It was ruled on, like everything else in the passage, by the authority that had lent him the kingdom in the first place.

**A Staff, a Termite, and the Smallest Agent in the Sūrah**

The detail the verse does supply is a small wood-eating creature: *dābbat al-arḍ taʾkulu minsaʾatah* — the crawling thing of the earth eating his staff. *Minsaʾah* is a specific word, a long staff of the kind used to drive animals or to lean on while standing, from a root meaning to push away. *Dābbah* means any creature that moves along the ground, and the tradition identifies it as the termite. Set this against the inventory of the preceding verses. Solomon commanded the wind and a species of hidden beings; what finally disclosed his condition was an insect chewing wood. The sūrah has already used a unit of the very small to make an argument about divine knowledge — not an atom's weight escapes Him (34:3) — and it now uses the very small again, this time as an instrument. Nothing in the account suggests the termite was sent as a marvel. It did what termites do. The failure of the jinn was not that they missed a miracle but that they missed an ordinary process running in plain sight.

**Standing Dead: Why the Kingdom Kept Working**

The reported circumstances fill out what the verse compresses. Solomon died upright, supported on the staff, and the jinn continued at their assigned labour because nothing in their situation had changed — the king was in his usual place, in his usual posture, issuing no new orders, and beings who are not addressed by a silent man assume the silence is deliberate. The work went on for as long as the wood held. When the termite had eaten through, he fell, and the collapse ended the illusion in an instant. It is reported that Solomon himself had asked for this, precisely so that the jinn would learn what they did not know. That motive is not stated in the verse and does not need to be for the lesson to stand. What the account demonstrates either way is how thin the evidence was that the whole arrangement rested on. An entire workforce organised itself around a posture.

**What the Jinn Learned About Themselves**

The realisation is stated in the jinn's own logic: *law kānū yaʿlamūna al-ghayba mā labithū fī al-ʿadhābi al-muhīn* — had they known the unseen, they would not have remained in humiliating labour. Read that carefully, because it is a proof by consequence rather than by assertion. Nobody tells the jinn they are ignorant of the unseen; they infer it from their own behaviour. They had continued working for a dead man, which no one with knowledge of hidden things would do, and the evidence of their limitation was produced by their own diligence. The Qur'an elsewhere records jinn making the same discovery in their own voice, admitting that they had tried to reach the heavens for news and found the way barred (72:8–9 in context), and stating the general rule plainly: *"None in the heavens and the earth has knowledge of the unseen except Allah"* (27:65). Al-Jinn adds the one exception, that God discloses the unseen *"except messengers of His choice"* (72:27). The claim being dismantled here is therefore very specific. It is not that jinn are powerless; the verses just past show them building. It is that their powers do not include seeing what is hidden.

**Why This Correction Mattered in Arabia**

The target is not mythological but social. Pre-Islamic Arabia ran an active trade in hidden knowledge — soothsayers who claimed a jinn informant, diviners consulted before journeys and marriages, fortune-tellers paid for news of the unseen. The business model depended on the premise the verse destroys: that jinn know things people do not and can be induced to share. Sabaʾ takes the most impressive jinn in its own repertoire, the conscripted builders of Solomon's kingdom, and shows them failing to notice a death in the room. If those jinn could not see the unseen, no soothsayer's informant can. The correction also connects to the sūrah's central argument. Its opponents deny a coming Hour, which is the supreme item of the unseen, and they deny it with great confidence. This verse shows what confident claims about the unseen look like from the outside when the facts arrive.

**Servitude Called Humiliating After the Fact**

The labour is named *al-ʿadhāb al-muhīn*, humiliating punishment — strong words for the construction of sanctuaries and reservoirs. The evaluation belongs to the jinn, not to the narration, and it registers how forced service feels to the one performing it however useful the product. But the humiliation the verse actually exposes is not the work itself. It is the discovery of having worked on in ignorance, obedient to a corpse, while believing oneself to possess knowledge others lacked. That specific shame is what the story is built to produce. There is also a closing symmetry with the section it ends. David was given iron and told to make protection and work righteously; Solomon was given wind, copper and workers and his house was told to work gratefully. The gifts end here, in a fall and a broken staff, having been exactly as temporary as the verse's opening clause says. The sūrah now turns to a people whose gift was smaller and whose response was worse.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:15

> Indeed, there was a sign for ˹the tribe of˺ Sheba in their homeland: two orchards—one to the right and the other to the left. ˹They were told:˺ “Eat from the provision of your Lord, and be grateful to Him. ˹Yours is˺ a good land and a forgiving Lord.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A People, Not a Queen**

Sabaʾ names a tribe and a kingdom in the south-west of the Arabian peninsula, in what is now Yemen, and the sūrah takes its title from this passage. The name is familiar from a different Qur'anic episode — the hoopoe's report to Solomon of a woman ruling there with a magnificent throne, whose people prostrated to the sun (27:22–24). Some commentators have joined the two accounts. The better reading separates them, on chronological grounds: the queen's people were contemporaries of Solomon, whose death has just been narrated, while the community destroyed here belongs to a much later phase of the same region's history. The Prophet was asked directly about the word, whether Sabaʾ was a land or a woman, and he answered that it was neither — it was a man who had ten sons among the Arabs, six of whom went south and four north (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3222, narrated by Farwah ibn Musayk, graded *ḥasan gharīb* by al-Tirmidhī; the same report appears in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 3988). So Sabaʾ is first a genealogy, then a people, then a kingdom.

**The Sign Was Where They Lived**

The verse says the sign was *fī maskanihim*, in their dwelling place. This is unusual. Signs in the Qur'an are more often events — a flood, a staff, a she-camel, a night journey — things that interrupt the ordinary. Here the sign is the address itself, the place they woke up in every morning. That relocation is the verse's first argument. A community can be given a standing miracle and, precisely because it stands, stop seeing it; the difficulty with permanent blessings is that permanence reads as ordinariness. The Qur'an makes the same move with Mecca, reminding its inhabitants that their valley was made *"a safe haven"* while people around them were seized (29:67), and that fruits of every kind were brought there as provision (28:57). Both peoples lived inside the evidence. The sūrah has just insisted that the created order is legible to *"every servant who turns ˹to Allah˺"* (34:9), and it now shows what happens when the legible thing is the farm outside the door.

**Two Orchards, Right and Left**

The description is spare and exact: *jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa shimāl*, two gardens, one on the right and one on the left. The dual form in Arabic means precisely two, not several, and the two flank the viewer — the picture is of someone standing between them with cultivation running away on either side. This corresponds closely to what is known of the region's agriculture. Maʾrib, the Sabaean capital, sat where a seasonal watercourse leaves the highlands, and its people built a dam and sluice system that spread the floodwater across two irrigated plains, one on each bank of the wadi. Sabaean inscriptions themselves refer to the site as Maʾrib and its two plains. Estimates of the irrigated area run to several thousand hectares, sustained entirely by catching water that would otherwise have run into the sand. So the two orchards are not a literary figure. They are the two halves of a hydraulic system, and the sign they constituted was engineering that worked.

**A Command in Two Words, Covering Both Hands**

The instruction given to them is as short as a sentence can be: *kulū min rizqi rabbikum wa-shkurū lah* — eat from the provision of your Lord and be grateful to Him. Two verbs, and between them the whole of what was required. *Eat* legitimises the enjoyment outright; nothing here asks them to renounce the harvest, to farm less, or to feel guilty about abundance. *Be grateful* attaches the single condition. Note that the provision is identified by its source before the command arrives — *your Lord's* provision — so gratitude is not being requested as a tax on pleasure but as an accurate acknowledgement of where the water came from. The Qur'an states the mechanism of gratitude as a standing rule: *"If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more"* (14:7). And it defines the opposite not as blasphemy but as forgetting, since *kufr* in Arabic first means to cover something over. A farmer who thinks the orchard is his own achievement has not argued against God. He has covered a fact.

**Good Land and a Forgiving Lord**

The verse closes with a pairing that repays attention: *baldatun ṭayyibatun wa rabbun ghafūr* — a good land and a forgiving Lord. These are two different kinds of thing, and putting them in one clause is the point. The first is the visible gift, the soil and the water; the second is the invisible provision that makes life with the first sustainable. *Ṭayyib* means wholesome, clean, pleasant — used of good food, good speech and good earth. *Ghafūr* is from the root *gh-f-r*, to cover or shield, so forgiveness in Arabic is a covering of faults rather than a pretence they did not occur. The combination anticipates a question their prosperity would eventually raise. A people that has everything will still fail, and the clause tells them in advance that failure was provided for. The land was good and the Lord forgiving, which means the destruction the next verse reports was not caused by the absence of a remedy. It was caused by the refusal to use one.

**What the Sūrah Is Setting Up**

This passage is placed immediately after the collapse of Solomon's kingdom and immediately before the ruin of Sheba's, and the arrangement does the argument's work without stating it. Solomon received extraordinary favour and his house was told to work gratefully (34:13); Sheba received ordinary favour — good soil, reliable water, a fed population — and was told the same thing in the same terms. The scale of the gift differs by an order of magnitude; the instruction attached to it is identical. That identity is what makes the story usable by the sūrah's Meccan audience, who commanded no winds and no jinn but did control a trading city that fed itself comfortably. The verse leaves them exactly where it leaves Sheba: inside a working arrangement, holding a two-word instruction, with nothing prohibited and one thing required.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:16

> But they turned away. So We sent against them a devastating flood, and replaced their orchards with two others producing bitter fruit, fruitless bushes, and a few ˹sparse˺ thorny trees.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words for a Civilisation's Decision**

*Fa-aʿraḍū* — but they turned away. The whole failure of Sheba is recorded in a single verb, and it is not a verb of attack. *Aʿraḍa* means to turn one's side to something, to present a shoulder rather than a face; it describes averting attention, not opposing. They did not argue against the instruction they had been given, did not denounce it, did not drive out anyone who repeated it. They simply stopped attending. This is the most common form of religious failure in the Qur'an's account and the hardest to notice from the inside, because nothing dramatic marks its onset. A people busy with harvests and trade routes drifts away from the two-word command attached to their prosperity, and no day arrives on which the drift is announced. The brevity of the clause matches the nature of what it describes. There was no confrontation to narrate.

**The Flood of the ʿArim**

*Sayl al-ʿarim* is the phrase, and the second word has been read three ways. It can be a proper name for the valley or the structure; it can be an adjective meaning violent or overwhelming, so that the sense is simply a devastating torrent; or it can mean the dam itself, giving "the flood of the dam" — the deluge released when a barrier fails. The third reading has the strongest external support. The root *ʿ-r-m* appears in South Arabian inscriptions in exactly this technical sense, used of dam works in the region. Inscriptions from the mid-sixth century record major repair campaigns at the Maʾrib dam, one of them commissioned under the ruler Abraha, and they use the word for the barrier. So the Qur'an's phrase is not a poetic name for a disaster. It is the local technical vocabulary for what happened when a specific piece of infrastructure gave way, and the reader of this translation can hear the specificity in the rendering *a devastating flood*.

**Water as Blessing and Water as Ruin**

The mechanism of the punishment is worth stating plainly because of its irony. The same water that made the two orchards possible destroyed them. For centuries the dam caught the seasonal flood and spread it through sluices across the plains on either bank; when the structure failed, the identical flood arrived unregulated and took the fields, the channels and the soil with it. Nothing new was introduced into the valley. A blessing was released from its restraint. This is a recurring shape in the Qur'an's descriptions of collective punishment — the thing a people relies on is the thing that turns — and it fits the sūrah's larger argument about provision. Al-Naḥl describes a town secure and provisioned from every side whose people met God's favours with ingratitude, *"so Allah made them taste the clutches of hunger and fear"* (16:112). The sequence is not that a gift is confiscated and a punishment substituted. The gift stops being held in place.

**Two Gardens Replaced by Two Gardens**

The verse's sharpest stroke is that the exchange keeps the form and empties it. They had two orchards; they are given two orchards. The Arabic uses the same word, *jannatayn*, for both the before and the after, so the sentence reports a swap rather than a removal, and the bitterness lies in the matching count. What grows in the replacement is then itemised in a descending list. *Ukul khamṭ* is bitter or brackish fruit — produce that exists and cannot be eaten with pleasure. *Athl* is the tamarisk, a hardy desert tree that yields nothing edible at all, rendered here as fruitless bushes. And *shayʾun min sidrin qalīl* is a few lote trees, the thorny jujube that survives on almost no water and bears a small fruit. The list is an accurate botanical description of what colonises an abandoned irrigation zone once the water regime collapses: salt-tolerant scrub, then thorn. The land did not become a desert in the sense of bare sand. It became a place where things grow that no one planted and nobody wants.

**The Contrast Between Left Standing and Taken Away**

Reading this verse against the one before it shows how carefully the pair is built. There the produce was called *rizq*, provision, and the instruction was *eat*; here the produce is bitter and nothing is said about eating. There the land was *ṭayyibah*, wholesome; here it grows tamarisk. There a forgiving Lord was named in the same breath as the good soil; here the sentence contains no divine name at all, only the verb *baddalnāhum*, We exchanged for them. The removal of the name from the closing position is itself part of the description of what was lost. What the two verses together establish is that the punishment was proportionate in a specific way: not a plague, not an invading army, not fire from the sky, but the withdrawal of the exact favour that had been named and the exact favour that had been unacknowledged. A people that stopped noticing where the water came from were shown where it came from.

**A Real City, and Why That Matters Here**

Archaeology and inscriptions place the Sabaean state at Maʾrib from roughly the early first millennium BC, sustained by dam-fed agriculture and by control of the incense route north. The dam suffered breaches in the fifth and sixth centuries AD and was repaired at least twice at great expense before the final failure, after which the oasis contracted and the population dispersed. The Qur'an's audience in Mecca sat on the northern end of that same caravan road and knew the ruins by reputation; the Yemen leg of their winter journey ran through territory whose greatness was visibly over. This is why the story functions as an argument rather than a legend. The sūrah is speaking to traders who could see, at the far end of their own trade route, what became of the last people in Arabia who had everything and stopped saying thank you. The following verse names what the exchange was a payment for.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:17

> This is how We rewarded them for their ingratitude. Would We ever punish ˹anyone in such a way˺ except the ungrateful?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict Attached to the Event**

The flood and the bitter orchards have been described; this verse supplies the accounting. *Dhālika jazaynāhum bimā kafarū* — that is how We repaid them for their *kufr*. The verb *jazā* is the word for settling a debt or paying a wage, the same root behind *jazāʾ*, recompense, which the sūrah used earlier for the reward of the believers (34:4). Applying a commercial verb to a natural disaster is the verse's first claim: what happened at Maʾrib was not weather. It was a settlement. This matters because the alternative reading was available and attractive. A dam fails, engineers examine the masonry, and the event closes as a technical failure with no meaning attached. The Qur'an does not deny the masonry. It insists that a sequence which begins with a two-word instruction, continues with turning away, and ends with the loss of exactly the thing that had been given is a sequence with an author.

**The Word Translated "Ingratitude"**

Everything in this verse turns on *kafarū* and *kafūr*, and the translation's choice of *ingratitude* rather than *disbelief* is exact for the context. The root *k-f-r* first means to cover something over — a farmer who buries seed is described with it, and night is said to *kafara* the daylight. From that comes the double sense the word carries throughout the Qur'an: covering a truth, and covering a favour. Sheba's offence is reported in the second sense. They are not shown denying a creator, arguing against a prophet, or setting up rivals; they are shown turning away from an instruction to be thankful. The verse then uses the intensive form *kafūr* in the closing question, which names not a single act of ingratitude but a settled disposition of it. The two verses immediately before this one make the reading unavoidable. The command was *be grateful* (34:15), the failure was turning away (34:16), and the charge here is the exact negative of the command.

**Ingratitude Presented as a Form of Unbelief**

Having said that, the verse does not let ingratitude remain a minor social fault. By using the word that elsewhere names outright rejection of God, it puts the two on a single scale. The logic is available in the Qur'an's own vocabulary: to cover a favour is already to cover the Giver, since a gift unacknowledged is a gift attributed to nobody, or to oneself. A people who eat from an irrigation system they did not devise the rains for, and who stop connecting the harvest to any source outside themselves, have made a theological claim with their behaviour rather than their speech. Al-Zumar puts the alternatives in exactly this pairing: *"Allah is truly not in need of you, nor does He approve of disbelief from His servants. But if you become grateful ˹through faith˺, He will appreciate that from you"* (39:7). Gratitude and *kufr* are set against each other there as the two available responses, with nothing in between.

**A Question That Is Really a Limit**

The second sentence is phrased as a question: *wa hal nujāzī illā al-kafūr* — do We requite any but the ungrateful? Arabic rhetorical questions of this shape function as emphatic negatives, so the sense is that nobody else is repaid in this manner. Early readers noted that the interrogative particle *hal* can itself carry negation in some constructions, which yields the same meaning by a different route, and that the verb admits a passive reading — *is any requited but the ungrateful* — with no change to the doctrine. What the question accomplishes is a restriction on the scope of the punishment just narrated. It reads as a threat, and it functions as a boundary: this treatment is reserved, not general. The distinction it draws is the one the Qur'an draws everywhere between the sins of those under mercy and the settled refusal of those who have covered the source. Believers sin and are forgiven; the *kafūr* is repaid.

**Why the Punishment Fitted the Offence So Precisely**

Look at what was actually done to Sheba against what other peoples in the Qur'an received. Some were drowned, some struck by a blast, some had the earth close over them, some had stones rain down (29:40). Sheba lost its irrigation. The punishment is drawn entirely from the material of the blessing — same valley, same water, same two plots, merely released from restraint and left to grow what it grows. A people who failed to notice the source of their provision were not destroyed by an unrelated catastrophe; they were shown what their land amounts to without the arrangement they had stopped crediting. This precision is what makes the event legible as a *jazāʾ* rather than a misfortune. The verse's opening word, *dhālika* — that, this is how — points back at the specific form of the loss, not merely at the fact of it.

**The Escape Route Left Open in the Wording**

Something in the closing question cuts the other way, and it is easy to miss under the severity. If this treatment falls only on the settled ingrate, then it is avoidable by the simplest possible means — not by heroism, not by wealth, not by lineage, but by acknowledging where things came from. The sūrah has already said the Lord in question is forgiving, in the very sentence that described the good land (34:15), and it placed that name there before any failure had occurred. Read together, the two verses frame Sheba's ruin as the outcome of an unused remedy. This also explains why the story is being told to Mecca at all. A city living off a trade route and a sanctuary, secure while others were raided, was in the identical structural position, and the point of the account is not that Sheba was uniquely wicked. The next verse returns to what they had been given, and shows that the orchards were only half of it.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:18

> We had also placed between them and the cities We showered with blessings ˹many small˺ towns within sight of one another. And We set moderate travel distances in between, ˹saying,˺ “Travel between them by day and night safely.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Blessing: A Road**

The account of Sheba's favours returns, and the new item is not agricultural. Between their homeland and the blessed territories to the north lay a chain of settlements, evenly spaced, each within sight of the next. This describes the incense road, the caravan route running up the western side of Arabia from Yemen to Syria, which carried frankincense, myrrh and transit goods and which made the southern kingdoms rich. The Qur'an's phrase for the northern end is *al-qurā allatī bāraknā fīhā*, the towns We had blessed — the same description the Book applies to the Syrian lands elsewhere, the well-watered country of trees and rivers that was the destination of the trade. So the sign in the previous verses was what they grew; the sign here is what they carried. Taken together the two favours cover a civilisation's whole economic base, production and distribution, and the sūrah is careful to attribute both to the same source before reporting what became of them.

**Towns Within Sight of One Another**

*Qurān ẓāhirah* is the phrase, and *ẓāhirah* means visible, apparent, out in the open. The reading that fits the context best takes it as visibility from one stage to the next: a traveller leaving one settlement could already see the next on the horizon. Anyone who has crossed open country understands what that does to a journey. A road whose next waypoint is always in view cannot be lost, cannot be missed in bad light, and removes the single greatest danger of desert travel, which is not distance but navigational error. The detail also has an economic dimension. Way-stations spaced within sight of each other can be small, because no one needs to carry supplies for a long leg, which in turn means more of them and a denser population along the route. The Qur'an is describing infrastructure again, as it did with the two irrigated plains, and again it presents the infrastructure as a gift rather than an achievement.

**Distances Measured Out**

*Wa qaddarnā fīhā al-sayr* — and We apportioned the travel in them. The verb is *qaddara*, to measure, proportion, determine a quantity, and it is the same verb used when David was told to get the gauge of his armour links right (34:11). Here the measurement applies to journey stages: the intervals were set so that a traveller finished each leg at a place where water and rest were waiting, without either exhausting himself or wasting a day. This is the unglamorous heart of what made the route profitable. Caravan economics turn on the ratio between what an animal must carry for itself and what it can carry for sale, and short, reliable stages shift that ratio decisively. A merchant on such a road travels light because resupply is near. The gift being described, in plain terms, is a low cost of trade — and it is described as something arranged rather than something the market produced.

**Safety Named as the Point of the Whole Arrangement**

The instruction attached is a single sentence: *sīrū fīhā layāliya wa ayyāman āminīn* — travel in them, nights and days, in security. Three things are worth noticing. The order puts nights first, and night travel is the test case: a road safe after dark is safe in the only sense that matters to a caravan. The phrasing covers both halves of the cycle, so the security is continuous rather than seasonal or daylight-only. And the operative word is *āminīn*, secure — the same root as *amn*, safety, and *amānah*, a trust. This is the favour the Qur'an singles out when it addresses the Prophet's own tribe, reminding Quraysh of their habitual caravans and of the Lord *"Who has fed them against hunger and made them secure against fear"* (106:4). The parallel is exact and cannot have been lost on the audience. Mecca's two annual caravans, north in summer and south in winter, ran on the same road Sheba had been given, and the sūrah is describing the southern end of Quraysh's own commercial life.

**Two Favours, One Instruction, Restated**

Set the two gifts side by side and the pattern of this passage becomes clear. The orchards came with *eat and be grateful* (34:15); the road comes with *travel in safety*. Both instructions are permissive. Neither demands a sacrifice, a levy, a pilgrimage or an abstention; each simply authorises the enjoyment of what has been provided and, in the first case, names the one condition. This is a deliberate portrait of an easy covenant. The Qur'an is not describing a people crushed under obligations who finally broke; it is describing a people asked for almost nothing, which makes the refusal recorded next harder to explain and more damning. There is also a structural echo of David and Solomon here. Each of those figures received a capacity and an instruction in the same breath — make armour and work righteously, build and be grateful. Sheba's version of that pairing is the gentlest in the sūrah.

**Why the Road Is Mentioned After the Ruin of the Orchards**

The chronology in these verses is not strictly sequential, and the arrangement is doing work. The flood and the bitter fruit were reported in verse 16, the verdict in verse 17, and only now does the sūrah return to describe another thing they had been given. The effect is to reopen the account after it appeared closed. A reader who thought the case complete — a people lost their farms for ingratitude — is shown that the farms were not the whole of what they had, and that the destruction described was therefore not the whole of what they lost. It also sets up the specific form their complaint will take. Having been given short stages and constant safety, they will ask for something else, and the request will come out of their own mouths in the next verse.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:19

> But they said, “Our Lord! Make ˹the distances of˺ our journeys longer,” wronging themselves. So We reduced them to ˹cautionary˺ tales, and scattered them utterly. Surely in this are lessons for whoever is steadfast, grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prayer Asking for a Harder Life**

The strangest thing in this verse is that it records a prayer. The people of Sheba address God correctly — *rabbanā*, our Lord — and then ask Him to lengthen the stages between their way-stations. They are not petitioning an idol and not cursing. They are using the vocative of a servant, and the content of the request is that the road be made worse. Nothing in the Qur'an's other accounts of ruined peoples looks quite like this. Others deny, mock, or demand a miracle; these ask for an inconvenience, politely, from the right address. The form of the sentence is what makes it so damaging a piece of evidence. A community that can still frame a petition to its Lord and use it to ask for the removal of a favour has not lost its religion through ignorance. It has kept the vocabulary and emptied it.

**Why Anyone Would Want the Distances Longer**

The motive is not stated in the verse, and the reports fill it in convincingly: they wanted long stages so that travel would become an achievement again — so that a journey would require provisions, endurance and money, and would be worth boasting about on return. Short hops between visible towns are available to anyone with a donkey. A crossing of empty country separates the serious merchant from the peasant, and produces a class of men whose wealth is visible in the scale of what they can undertake. Read this way, the request is for the restoration of difficulty as a means of restoring distinction. There is a second reading in the same direction: with way-stations close together, the poor could travel too, and the route's benefits spread widely. Lengthening the stages would have closed the road to all but those who could outfit a proper caravan. Either way the request is about status rather than logistics, which is why the verse comments on it in moral rather than practical terms.

**"Wronging Themselves" — The Verse's Own Verdict**

*Wa ẓalamū anfusahum* — and they wronged themselves. The phrase is the Qur'an's standard description of sin, and it is worth taking literally rather than as a pious formula. *Ẓulm* in Arabic means putting a thing somewhere other than its proper place; the wrongdoer is not primarily a rule-breaker but a misplacer. Applied here, the misplacement is precise. They put hardship where ease had been given, and put their own reputation where gratitude belonged. And the injury lands on them: no one else's road was lengthened. This clause also settles the question of whether the request was granted. The verse does not say God refused it, nor that He made the journeys longer. It moves straight from the prayer to the dispersal, which suggests the answer came in a form they had not asked for and could not decline — the road was not lengthened, it was emptied.

**Turned Into Stories**

*Fa-jaʿalnāhum aḥādīth* — We made them into tales. The word *aḥādīth* is the plural of *ḥadīth*, meaning a report, a piece of news, something people say to each other. The punishment named here is not death or defeat but conversion into subject matter. A people who wanted their journeys to be worth talking about were made into something talked about, in a sense they did not intend. This is one of the Qur'an's sharpest ironies and it is entirely contained in a single noun. The phrase also names the mechanism by which the story reached the sūrah's Meccan audience. Sheba was not known to them through scripture; it was known through exactly this — reports carried up the caravan road, ruins pointed out, a name attached to a cautionary tale. A civilisation reduced to conversation is not merely destroyed. It survives in the only form that can still do damage to it.

**Scattered Utterly, With a Verb Heard Before**

*Wa mazzaqnāhum kulla mumazzaq* — We tore them apart with a complete tearing. The reader of this sūrah has met this construction already, and the echo is not accidental. The deniers of resurrection used the identical intensive form to describe a corpse pulled to shreds: *"when you have been utterly disintegrated you will be raised as a new creation"* (34:7). They chose the most violent available image to argue that reassembly was impossible. Twelve verses later the same word describes what happened to a nation that turned away, and the sense is historical rather than hypothetical — the Sabaean population dispersed after the collapse of the dam, its tribes moving north and east across Arabia, some of them ancestral to communities the Prophet's own contemporaries could name. The scattering of a people is presented in the vocabulary that scoffers used for the scattering of a body, by a power they said could not reverse it.

**A Lesson Reserved for Two Kinds of Person**

The verse ends by naming who can learn from this: *li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūr*, for everyone who is steadfast and grateful. Both words are intensive forms — not one who endures occasionally but one whose character is endurance, not one who thanks but one made of thanks. The pairing is exact for the story just told. Sheba failed on the second count by asking for their ease to be taken away, and would have failed on the first had it been. The two dispositions map onto the two halves of any life: patience is what gratitude looks like in hardship, and gratitude is what patience looks like in comfort, and a person who has only one of them will collapse when conditions change. This phrase appears four times in the Qur'an, always after a sign — with the days of God recalled to Moses' people (14:5), with ships running by His grace (31:31), with the wind that could be stilled and leave them motionless (42:33), and here with a ruined kingdom. Three of the four attach it to something still working. Only this one attaches it to wreckage.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:20

> Indeed, Iblîs’ assumption about them has come true, so they ˹all˺ follow him, except a group of ˹true˺ believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Guess, Not a Plan**

The word governing this verse is *ẓann*, translated here as *assumption*. In Arabic it names the space between certainty and ignorance — an opinion held without proof, a supposition, a surmise. It is emphatically not knowledge, and the Qur'an is careful with the distinction elsewhere, dismissing those who *"follow nothing but assumptions"*. So the verse is not reporting that Iblīs knew what human beings would do. He guessed, and the guess turned out right. This matters because the alternative reading would grant him an insight into hidden things, and the sūrah has just spent a verse demonstrating that even the jinn who worked for Solomon could not detect a death in the room (34:14). The being described here is a strategist with a hypothesis, not a seer with foreknowledge. His accuracy is the accuracy of someone who has watched a great many people and formed an opinion about the type.

**What He Actually Said**

The Qur'an records the supposition in his own words at two places, and both are worth setting beside this verse. In al-Isrāʾ he asks for time and states his expectation: *"If you delay my end until the Day of Judgment, I will certainly take hold of his descendants, except for a few"* (17:62). In al-Aʿrāf he describes his method and predicts the result: *"I will approach them from their front, their back, their right, their left, and then You will find most of them ungrateful"* (7:17). Two elements recur in both statements — that most will be taken, and that a remainder will not. Al-Ḥijr has him make the exception explicit: he will mislead them all *"except Your chosen servants among them"* (15:40), and Ṣād repeats the same reservation (38:83). So his own prediction always contained a limit. This verse confirms the prediction and preserves the limit, which means even the enemy's estimate of his success was never total.

**Why the Verdict Is Delivered Here**

Placed immediately after the ruin of Sheba, the verse functions as a general statement drawn from a particular case. A nation with orchards and a safe road turned away and was scattered, and the question that naturally follows is whether something unusual was wrong with those people. The answer is that nothing was. What happened at Maʾrib is an instance of a pattern old enough to have been predicted before human history began. This widens the frame considerably. The preceding verses could be read as a local disaster explained by local ingratitude; this one says the local ingratitude is the standard outcome of a standing campaign. Notice too the word chosen for the confirmation: *ṣaddaqa*, he found it true, from the root of *ṣidq*, truthfulness. The verse grants Iblīs' assessment of humanity the status of an accurate statement, which is an uncomfortable concession and is made without hedging.

**Following Without Being Recruited**

*Fa-ttabaʿūhu* — so they followed him. The verb describes going after someone, walking behind, and the Qur'an is precise about what it does not say. It does not say he compelled them, seized them, or deceived them into a contract. The next verse will make the absence of compulsion explicit. What the sūrah is describing is a following that feels, from the inside, like independent movement. Nobody in Sheba experienced their prosperity as satanic influence; they experienced it as success, and their request for longer journeys felt like ambition. This is the most practically useful observation in the verse. A follower who knows he is following can reconsider. The condition being described is one in which the direction of travel is set by someone whose name never comes up. Arabic reinforces the point with the tense: the verb is in the past, reporting a completed record of behaviour rather than an ongoing compulsion, which is how a historian describes a choice and not how a physician describes a seizure.

**The Exception That Refuses to Be Rounded Down**

*Illā farīqan min al-muʾminīn* — except a group of the believers. The exception is stated without a number and without an apology, and its presence undoes any fatalism the verse might otherwise support. If the pattern were universal it would be a law of nature, and nobody is blamed for obeying gravity. A pattern with exceptions is a pattern that can be refused, and the existence of the group proves the refusal is available. The Qur'an's word here is *farīq*, a party or section — a portion of a larger body rather than a separate species. Iblīs himself predicted this remainder, which is the most striking thing about it: the enemy's own estimate included people he expected to fail with. The Book elsewhere calls them the chosen servants (15:40), which raises the question of whether their resistance is their achievement or a gift, and the Qur'an characteristically leaves both halves standing rather than resolving them.

**The Sūrah's Real Target: An Assumption About Assumptions**

There is a connection between this verse and the sūrah's opening dispute that is easy to miss. The men who said the Hour would never reach them (34:3) were also operating on *ẓann* — an opinion about the unseen held without evidence. Al-Jāthiyah quotes the type in their own words: *"We think it is no more than speculation, and we are not convinced"* (45:32). So the chapter now sets two suppositions against each other. Iblīs supposed that most people would follow him, and he was right. They supposed that no reckoning was coming, and the sūrah has been arguing at length that they are wrong. The difference between a guess that lands and a guess that fails is not the confidence of the guesser; it is whether the world happens to be built the way the guesser assumed. One of the two parties in this chapter has guessed correctly about human nature, and it is not the party that considers itself worldly-wise. Having named the follower and the followed, the passage turns immediately to the question this raises about responsibility.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:21

> He does not have any authority over them, but ˹Our Will is˺ only to distinguish those who believe in the Hereafter from those who are in doubt about it. And your Lord is a ˹vigilant˺ Keeper over all things.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Excuse Removed Before It Is Offered**

The previous verse said that most people follow Iblīs. The obvious next move for anyone listening is to plead force: if a being of that kind is working on us, what chance did we have? This verse closes that exit in its first clause. *Wa mā kāna lahu ʿalayhim min sulṭān* — he had no *sulṭān* over them at all, with the negation reinforced by *min*, which in Arabic makes the denial absolute rather than partial. Not limited authority, not weak authority; none. *Sulṭān* is a word of real weight, used for governing power and for the force of a decisive proof, and both senses fail here. He cannot compel and he cannot demonstrate. What he has is suggestion, and suggestion is refusable by definition, which is precisely why following him remains a choice attributable to the follower.

**What Was Granted, and What Was Not**

The Qur'an sets out the terms of the arrangement in al-Ḥijr, and reading them alongside this verse settles the question of scope. Iblīs declares he will tempt them all except the chosen (15:39–40), and the reply is a binding statement: *"you will certainly have no authority over My servants, except the deviant who follow you"* (15:42). The exception in that sentence is the whole doctrine in miniature. His power extends to those who have already inclined his way — he does not create the inclination, he exploits it. A man is not turned by an external force into someone who wants what he did not want; he is met, at the point where he already wants it, by an encouragement. This is why the Qur'an can hold both propositions at once without contradiction: the tempter is real and active, and the tempted is fully responsible. He has an audience, not a command.

**The Purpose Clause: Why the Arrangement Exists**

*Illā li-naʿlama man yuʾminu bi-l-ākhirati mimman huwa minhā fī shakk* — except that We may know who believes in the Hereafter from who is in doubt about it. The construction is a purpose clause, so the licence given to the tempter is presented as serving a function rather than as a concession extracted from a reluctant authority. That function is separation. The verb *naʿlam*, that We may know, has troubled readers since it appears to make divine knowledge dependent on an outcome, and the standard answer is the right one: the knowing in question is the making-manifest of what was already known, the difference between a fact and a demonstrated fact. Al-ʿAnkabūt states the principle in the same shape, saying that the testing occurs so that God will *know* the truthful from the liars while affirming that He already knows them. A quality that has never been tested is real but unevidenced. Temptation is what converts an untested disposition into a visible record.

**Belief Against Doubt, Not Belief Against Denial**

The line of separation is drawn in an unexpected place. The verse does not contrast believers with deniers; it contrasts those who believe in the Hereafter with those who are *fī shakk*, in doubt about it. Doubt is the milder condition, and choosing it as the opposite pole is a deliberate raising of the standard. A man who denies the Hereafter outright has at least taken a position. A man in doubt has taken none, and the verse places him on the far side of the line anyway. The reason emerges from the sūrah's own material. Sheba did not deny anything; they turned away (34:16). Doubt is sufficient to produce that turning, because a person unsure whether the accounting is real will behave, in every practical decision, exactly like a person certain that it is not. This is also why the Hereafter is the chosen test item rather than, say, belief in God. Nearly everyone in the sūrah's audience believed in a creator; what divided them was whether anything would be settled later.

**A Keeper Who Misses Nothing**

The closing clause names the guarantor: *wa rabbuka ʿalā kulli shayʾin ḥafīẓ* — your Lord is a Keeper over all things. *Ḥafīẓ* comes from the root *ḥ-f-ẓ*, to preserve, guard and retain; it is the root behind *ḥāfiẓ*, one who has memorised the Qur'an, and behind the preserved Tablet mentioned in (85:22). The name therefore carries two functions at once — watching over, and keeping a record. Both are needed at this point in the argument. If the world is a place of testing, the test is worthless unless someone is invigilating, and worthless again unless the results are retained. The sūrah established the record early, in the verse about the atom's weight and the perfect Record (34:3), and this name reaches back to it. There is also a note of protection in the word that softens the surveillance: the same root gives the sense of guarding something valuable, which is what a Keeper does for those under His care.

**Freedom Described as a Deliberate Design**

Putting the three clauses together yields a compact account of why the world is arranged as it is. A tempter exists but cannot compel; his presence functions as the instrument by which real dispositions become visible; and the results are kept by One who watches everything. Each element is necessary. Remove the tempter and there is no test; give him authority and there is no responsibility; remove the Keeper and the test produces nothing. What the verse refuses, throughout, is the picture of a struggle between two powers with the human being as territory. Iblīs in this sūrah is not an opposing force but a permitted instrument, and his accurate guess about human weakness (34:20) is reported without alarm because the arrangement was never a contest. The sūrah now turns from the hidden influences on worship to its visible objects.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:22

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Call upon those you claim ˹to be divine˺ besides Allah. They do not possess ˹even˺ an atom’s weight either in the heavens or the earth, nor do they have any share in ˹governing˺ them. Nor is any of them a helper to Him.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Challenge Issued Rather Than an Argument Made**

The verse begins the sūrah's long series of commands to the Prophet — *qul*, say — and the first of them is not a proposition but a dare. Call them. Go ahead and petition the beings you appeal to, and see what arrives. This is a characteristic Qur'anic method in the dispute over other gods: instead of arguing that they lack power, it invites a demonstration. The same challenge appears at al-Aʿrāf, Yūnus, Hūd, al-Isrāʾ and al-Aḥqāf, always with the same confidence that the test will produce nothing. Its strength is that it cannot be answered with a counter-argument. A believer in the old deities who accepts the challenge must produce a result, and a believer who declines has conceded the point in front of witnesses. Note also the verb *tazʿumūn*, translated *you claim* — a word used in Arabic for assertions made without evidence, closer to "allege" than to "believe". The divinity of these beings is described as a claim before a single objection has been raised.

**The Smallest Unit, Used Again**

*Lā yamlikūna mithqāla dharratin* — they do not own an atom's weight. The sūrah has used this measure before, when it said that nothing of that size escapes God's knowledge (34:3), and the repetition ties the two verses into a single argument. There the smallest unit marked the lower limit of divine awareness; here it marks the upper limit of the idols' property. One party knows down to the mote; the other party owns not so much as a mote. Placing the same phrase on both sides of the comparison is more effective than any adjective could be, because it makes the two claims commensurable. The verb *malaka* is also worth noting: it means to own, to have at one's disposal, and it is the root of *mālik*, owner, and *mulk*, dominion. The question being settled is not whether these beings exist, and not whether they are impressive. It is whether they hold title to anything.

**Three Doors Closed in Sequence**

What follows is a systematic elimination, and the order is not arbitrary. First, ownership: they possess nothing in either realm. Second, partnership: *wa mā lahum fīhimā min shirk* — they have no share in the two. Third, assistance: *wa mā lahu minhum min ẓahīr* — He has no supporter among them. These are the three ways a lesser being could plausibly hold power in a universe with one supreme God. It could own a province outright; it could hold a stake in the whole as a junior partner; or it could own nothing and hold no stake but still be indispensable as a helper, the way a minister is indispensable to a king who could in principle rule alone. The verse shuts all three in a single sentence. *Ẓahīr*, the word for the third, comes from *ẓahr*, the back — the one who backs you up, who stands behind and supports. Arabic has no more modest word for assistance, and even that is denied.

**Why the Intercession Argument Was the Real Target**

The third denial is the one that mattered most in Mecca, and understanding why explains the whole verse. The Quraysh did not generally claim that their deities created the world or governed it. Their theology was subtler and is quoted exactly in al-Zumar: *"We only worship them so they may bring us closer to Allah"* (39:3). The gods were intermediaries — figures with standing at court who could put in a word. That position survives the first two denials intact, since a courtier owns no province and holds no share of the kingdom. It is destroyed only by the third. A being whom the Sovereign does not need as a backer has no leverage to exercise on anyone's behalf, and an intercessor without leverage is an intercessor in name only. The sūrah has arranged its denials to arrive at precisely this point, which is why the following verse takes up intercession directly.

**What the Book Says the Rival Gods Own**

The Qur'an is consistent in measuring the idols' holdings by comparison with something negligible. Fāṭir puts it at less than the skin of a date stone: *"As for those upon whom you call besides Him, they do not possess even the tiniest bit"* (35:13). Al-Ḥajj asks the hearers to consider that everything they invoke could not create a fly. Al-Qaṣaṣ projects the challenge into the Day of Judgment, where the summons is issued again and the result is public: *"It will be said ˹to them˺, 'Call upon your associate-gods.' So they will call them, but will receive no response"* (28:64). Luqmān states the underlying reason in a sentence: *"That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth, and whatever they invoke besides Him is false"* (31:30). The consistency across chapters is itself part of the argument. A scripture that varied its estimate of these beings — powerful here, weak there — would suggest negotiation. This one gives the same figure every time, and the figure is zero.

**Ownership as the Sūrah's Organising Claim**

This verse returns the chapter to the ground it staked out in its first line, where everything in the heavens and on the earth was said to belong to God (34:1). That opening was a statement of title; this is the corresponding statement of exclusion, and between them they define the sūrah's central dispute. Everything in the intervening verses has been evidence for the title — the four directions of knowledge (34:2), the record that misses nothing (34:3), the kings whose powers were loans (34:10–14), the nation whose orchards were withdrawn (34:16). Having established who owns what, the sūrah can now put the case to the opponents as a challenge rather than a lecture, since the only thing left for them to do is produce a counter-example. The next verse takes away the last avenue they had.

---

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:23

> No intercession will be of any benefit with Him, except by those granted permission by Him. ˹At last,˺ when the dread ˹of Judgment Day˺ is relieved from their hearts ˹because they are permitted to intercede˺, they will ˹excitedly˺ ask ˹the angels˺, “What has your Lord ˹just˺ said?” The angels will reply, “The truth! And He is the Most High, AllGreat.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Intercession Is Not Denied — It Is Relicensed**

The previous verse dismantled the idea that the old gods had any standing at the divine court. This verse handles the doctrine that stood behind that idea, and it does something more careful than a flat refusal. *Wa lā tanfaʿu al-shafāʿatu ʿindahu illā li-man adhina lah* — intercession brings no benefit with Him except for the one He permits. The institution survives; the qualification changes hands. In Meccan practice a deity's usefulness lay in its independent leverage: you approached the intermediary because the intermediary had standing of its own. Here standing exists only where it has been granted in advance by the very authority being petitioned, which converts intercession from a channel of influence into an act of obedience. The Qur'an makes the same point in the Throne Verse with a question nobody can answer: *"Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?"* (2:255). An intercessor who must be authorised cannot be a lever against the One authorising him.

**The Word for Terror, and Its Grammar**

The middle of the verse turns to a scene, and the scene begins with fear leaving: *ḥattā idhā fuzziʿa ʿan qulūbihim* — when terror is removed from their hearts. The verb is from *fazaʿ*, sudden fright, and the form used here is the intensive passive with the preposition *ʿan*, which in Arabic gives the sense of stripping something off, clearing it away. Something had settled on the hearts and is lifted. The reading of the Arabic here was discussed by the early authorities, since a different vowelling gives "when they are relieved of terror" as against other possibilities, and the reports preserve the discussion. What no reading changes is the shape of the moment: a silence full of dread, then release, then speech. The verse is describing the interval between a decree being issued and its content becoming known, and the interval is filled with fear.

**Whose Hearts Were Afraid**

The pronoun is not identified in the Arabic, and the translation supplies the likeliest referent: the angels. This is the reading the tradition has generally taken, and it rests on solid ground elsewhere in the Book, which describes the angels as *"wary, for fear of Him"* (21:28) despite their obedience. Their fear is not the fear of punishment, since nothing in the Qur'an charges them with disobedience. It is the awe of beings who know exactly what they are in the presence of — and awe, on this account, increases with knowledge rather than decreasing with familiarity. Fāṭir states the rule directly: *"Of all of Allah's servants, only the knowledgeable ˹of His might˺ are ˹truly˺ in awe of Him"* (35:28). If the highest servants freeze when the divine command is issued, the confidence of the Meccan petitioner who assumed his patron deity could work the room is exposed as ignorance rather than piety.

**The Question the Angels Ask Each Other**

*Mādhā qāla rabbukum* — what did your Lord say? Two features of the question carry the argument. First, they ask each other, which means the command was issued and not all of them had it; even at that altitude, information is distributed rather than universally possessed. Second, they ask what He *said*, not what He wills or intends. The object of angelic knowledge is the utterance, the decree as delivered — which is to say the angels are recipients of instruction, not consultants in its formation. This is the exact opposite of the picture the idolaters worked with, in which beings close to God participate in decisions and can be induced to shift them. Nothing in the scene shows negotiation. It shows a chain of command in which the highest ranks wait, in fear, to learn what has been decided, and then relay it.

**The Answer, and Why It Is So Short**

*Qālū al-ḥaqq* — they said: the truth. The reply is one word in Arabic, and its brevity is the substance of it. Whatever was decreed, the content is not summarised, quoted or explained; it is characterised. This is how a servant reports a master's word when the master's word is by definition beyond appeal — not "He said such and such," but "He said what is right." The two names that close the verse complete the thought: *al-ʿAliyy al-Kabīr*, the Most High and the Great. One is a name of elevation, distance above; the other of magnitude. Together they explain why the answer could be so brief. A decree from a source that high and that great needs no further ground given for it. The Prophet described this moment in terms that match the verse closely, saying that when God decrees a matter in heaven the angels beat their wings in submission to His word, like a chain dragged over rock, until, when the terror is lifted from their hearts, they ask: what did your Lord say? And they answer: the truth, and He is the Most High, the Great (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4701, from Abū Hurayrah).

**What This Does to the Petitioner Who Prays to a Patron**

The practical force of the verse lands on a particular kind of religious person — not the atheist, who is not in view here, but the devotee who has organised his security around access to a powerful friend in the unseen. That system is coherent in a royal court and incoherent in the world this verse describes. The beings with the highest access do not lobby; they wait, afraid, for a decree they did not shape and then transmit it verbatim. If they cannot influence the outcome, a carved figure in a sanctuary certainly cannot. What remains of intercession after this verse is real but transformed: a permission granted from above rather than a service purchased from the side, extended to prophets, angels and righteous servants only as and when God allows. Having removed the intermediaries, the sūrah turns next to a question the opponents must answer themselves.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:24

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who provides for you from the heavens and the earth?” Say, “Allah! Now, certainly one of our two groups is ˹rightly˺ guided; the other is clearly astray.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question, Then the Answer Supplied by the Questioner**

The structure of this verse is unusual and deliberate. The Prophet is told to ask, and then told to answer his own question before the audience can reply. *Qul man yarzuqukum min al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ. Qul Allāh.* The second *say* is the striking one. A debater who answers his own question is either weak or supremely confident, and here the confidence has a specific basis: there was no other answer available. The Quraysh did not credit the rain to al-Lāt or the harvest to Manāt; they held that God was the provider, as the Qur'an reports of them repeatedly. So the question is not a trap designed to extract a confession. It is a way of putting on record something the opponents already grant, in public, in their own hearing, before the conclusion is drawn from it. Supplying the answer immediately also denies them the chance to invent a new position in the pause.

**Provision as the Test Case for Divinity**

Of all the divine acts the verse could have asked about, it asks about *rizq* — provision, sustenance, the steady supply of what a living thing needs. This is a shrewder choice than creation. A man can concede that the world was made long ago by a power he now has no dealings with; the concession costs him nothing in daily life. Provision is continuous and present-tense. It concerns this year's rain, this caravan's profit, this child's food, and it is exactly the domain in which people actually turn to gods for help. The verse names both sources — *from the heavens and the earth* — which covers the whole supply chain a farmer or a trader would recognise: rain and light from above, crops and water and ore from below. Fāṭir puts the same question in almost the same words and adds the conclusion the hearers must draw: *"Is there any creator other than Allah who provides for you from the heavens and the earth?"* (35:3).

**Two Groups, One Right, One Wrong**

Having secured the shared premise, the verse states the disagreement as a clean logical alternative: *wa innā aw iyyākum la-ʿalā hudan aw fī ḍalālin mubīn* — surely we or you are upon guidance or in clear error. The wording is peculiar and every reader of the Arabic has noticed it. It does not say *we are guided and you are astray*, though that is plainly what the speaker holds. It sets the two parties and the two conditions side by side and declines to assign them. Commentators have read the particle *aw* here as functioning like *and*, giving the direct sense; others read the sentence as leaving the pairing genuinely open — one of us is right, one of us is wrong, and I have not said which. On the second reading, the verse is doing something rhetorically sophisticated. It establishes that the two positions cannot both be correct, which is the only thing the opponent must accept before he can be argued with at all.

**Why the Insult Is Withheld**

The restraint in this phrasing has been read as a courtesy extended to an opponent, a door left open. There is good sense in that reading. A man told flatly that he is astray defends his position; a man told that one of the two of them must be astray is invited to work out which, and the working out is done in his own head where no audience is watching him lose. The Qur'an uses this technique more than once, and it is the same instinct that produces the instruction later in this sūrah to stand apart, singly and in pairs, and think (34:46). Argument that leaves the conclusion to the hearer has a better chance with a proud man than argument that hands it to him. But the courtesy has a hard edge. What the sentence definitely forecloses is the comfortable position that both parties might be somewhat right — that the new preaching and the old worship could be two roads to the same place.

**Clear Error, Not Honest Difference**

The adjective attached to the wrong option is *mubīn*, clear, manifest, evident — a word from the root *b-y-n*, which also gives *bayān*, plain speech. Whichever party is wrong is not slightly off; it is visibly and demonstrably off. This closes the second escape route. The first was pluralism, ruled out by the either-or; the second is the plea that the matter is too obscure for anyone to be blamed for getting it wrong. If the error is manifest, then the person in it is in a position to see it, and the failure is one of attention rather than of information. The sūrah has made this charge before against those who would not look at the sky and the ground around them (34:9). Here it makes it against people who agree about who provides for them and then act as though someone else does.

**The Distance Between What They Said and What They Did**

The deepest point of the verse lies in the gap it exposes. The opponents' answer to the question is correct: God provides. Their practice contradicts their answer, since the beings they petition and the sanctuaries they maintain belong to a different theology altogether. The verse does not accuse them of believing something false. It accuses them of believing something true and living as though it were not — which is a harder charge to answer, because it cannot be met by adjusting a doctrine. Al-Zukhruf records the same inconsistency among the same people, who would say that God created them and then turn away. Having established that both sides agree on the source of provision and disagree about everything that follows from it, the sūrah moves on to what each party will and will not be answerable for.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:25

> Say, “You will not be accountable for our misdeeds, nor will we be accountable for your deeds.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shortest Verse in the Sūrah, and the Most Carefully Balanced**

This is a single sentence with two clauses, and everything in it depends on how the two are matched. *Lā tusʾalūna ʿammā ajramnā wa lā nusʾalu ʿammā taʿmalūn* — you will not be questioned about what we committed, nor will we be questioned about what you do. The verb in both halves is *suʾila*, to be asked, in the passive, which in Qur'anic usage means interrogation at the reckoning. What is being drawn is a line of accountability, and it runs in both directions. The believers will not answer for the opponents, and the opponents will not answer for the believers. Coming immediately after a verse that said one of the two parties must be in clear error (34:24), this sentence does something surprising with that conclusion: rather than pressing the advantage, it separates the accounts. The dispute is real and one side is wrong, and each side will nevertheless carry only its own file.

**Two Different Words, Deliberately Chosen**

The symmetry of the sentence breaks in one place, and the break is the most interesting thing in the verse. Of our conduct it says *ajramnā*, from *jurm*, crime or sin — a word that concedes wrongdoing outright. Of theirs it says *taʿmalūn*, from *ʿamal*, work or deed — a neutral word that names activity without judging it. The speaker applies the harsh term to his own side and the mild one to his opponents. This is not diplomacy for its own sake; it removes the one thing that would have made the sentence unusable in an argument. Had the wording been "you will not answer for our righteousness, nor we for your crimes," the disclaimer would have been an insult wearing the costume of fairness. As written, it is the opposite: the speaker takes the worse description and hands the better one across. A man who can concede that much about himself in the middle of a dispute is not conceding the dispute.

**A Standing Principle, Not a Local Arrangement**

The claim behind the verse runs throughout the Qur'an and is stated there in a fixed formula: *"No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another"* (6:164), repeated at (17:15), (35:18), (39:7) and (53:38). What that principle rules out is worth spelling out, because pre-Islamic Arabia ran on its opposite. Blood-guilt attached to a tribe, not to a killer; honour and shame were collective; a man's standing was his clan's standing and his offences were his clan's liability. Against that background, a sentence assigning each party its own reckoning is not a truism. It is the dismantling of a social system's deepest assumption, and the Qur'an applies it in both directions — no one inherits another's guilt, and no one is saved by another's merit. The verse's setting in a dispute over religion makes the point sharper still. Neither side gets to be punished as a bloc.

**Separation of Accounts Is Not Agreement**

There is a reading of this verse that would make it a statement of religious indifference — your path, my path, let us not trouble each other. That reading collapses as soon as the verse is put back among its neighbours. One verse earlier the sūrah insisted that one of the two parties is in clear error (34:24); one verse later it says God will gather both and judge between them (34:26). A sentence framed by those two cannot be saying the difference does not matter. What it says is narrower and more durable: the difference will be settled by a competent authority at a fixed time, and until then neither party is the other's judge or the other's hostage. Yūnus gives the Prophet the same instruction in almost the same shape, telling him to say, if they deny him, *"My deeds are mine and your deeds are yours. You are free of what I do, and I am free of what you do"* (10:41).

**What the Prophet Is Being Relieved Of**

Hearing the verse as an instruction to a messenger under pressure gives it a different weight. The Prophet in Mecca was watching people he knew refuse a message he was certain of, and the natural response to that is to take their refusal as a personal failure. This sentence sets a limit on his responsibility. He is answerable for delivery, not for outcome; their deeds are their file, and his obligation ends where their choice begins. The Qur'an says this to him directly elsewhere, reminding him that he is not set over people as a controller. There is real relief in the arrangement, and it is the same relief available to anyone who has argued for something true and been refused. Responsibility for another person's conclusion is a burden no one can discharge, and the verse declines to place it.

**A Sentence Meant to Be Said Out Loud**

Like the verses around it, this one begins with *qul* — say. That framing matters to how the content works. It is not a doctrine stated for the reader's information; it is a line of speech handed to the Prophet for use in a live confrontation, and its effect in that setting is to lower the temperature without conceding anything. Opponents expecting either a threat or a capitulation are given a third thing: a clear statement that the argument has a judge, and that the judge is not either of the parties present. This is the first of a run of four consecutive *say* verses in the sūrah, and reading them in sequence shows a deliberate progression — first the separation of accounts here, then the naming of the judge, then the demand for evidence. The next verse supplies the court that makes this separation more than an agreement to disagree.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:26

> Say, “Our Lord will gather us together, then He will judge between us with the truth. For He is the All-Knowing Judge.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Dispute Referred Upward**

The previous verse separated the two parties' accounts; this one names the court where the separation will be enforced. The sentence has three movements in strict order: gathering, judgment, and the qualification of the judge. *Yajmaʿu baynanā rabbunā* — our Lord will bring us together. The verb is *jamaʿa*, to collect, assemble, bring into one place, and the Qur'an makes the gathering a proper name for the Day: al-Shūrā and al-Taghābun both call it *yawm al-jamʿ*, the Day of Assembly (42:7; 64:9). What is striking about the phrasing is the pronoun. It does not say He will gather them, or gather you; it says He will gather **us** — both parties, the speaker included. The Prophet is told to place himself on the same list as his opponents, subject to the same summons. An argument referred to a judge is an argument in which the referring party accepts jurisdiction over himself.

**Judgment "With the Truth" Rather Than by Power**

*Thumma yaftaḥu baynanā bi-l-ḥaqq* — then He will decide between us with the truth. The instrumental phrase is not decorative. A judgment can be rendered by authority alone, and a sufficiently powerful judge needs no further warrant; the verse specifies that this one will be rendered according to what is actually the case. That specification is what makes the referral meaningful to both sides. A party that expects to lose on the merits gains nothing from a fair court, but a party confident of the facts wants nothing else. The Qur'an holds to this standard across its descriptions of the reckoning, promising scales set up *"so no soul will be wronged in the least"* (21:47) and stating that whoever does an evil deed *"will only be paid back with its equivalent"* (40:40). The Prophet is not being told to threaten his opponents with a verdict. He is being told to submit the case.

**The Verb the Qur'an Uses for Judging Here**

The word translated *judge* is not the usual *ḥakama* but *fataḥa*, whose primary meaning is to open. Arabic uses it for opening a door, opening a city to conquest, and opening a matter that has been shut — hence its use for adjudication, since a judge opens a case that the parties have sealed by their disagreement. The image is exact for a dispute of this kind. Two sides have argued to a standstill, each holding a position the other cannot enter, and the deadlock is a closure. What the Day supplies is not merely a verdict but an opening: the concealed facts become visible, and the question that could not be settled by argument is settled by disclosure. This is also why the Qur'an says the differences will be made clear rather than merely decided. A judgment that only assigned outcomes would leave the losing party still convinced.

**al-Fattāḥ: A Divine Name Built From That Verb**

The verse ends by naming the judge *al-Fattāḥ al-ʿAlīm*, and the first of these is an uncommon name formed from the same root as the verb just used. *Al-Fattāḥ* can be rendered the Judge or, keeping the root sense, the Opener — the One who opens what is locked, whether that is a case, a door, a difficulty or a source of provision. Both senses are live, and the Arabic does not force a choice. The pairing with *al-ʿAlīm*, the All-Knowing, supplies what the first name alone would leave open, because opening a case is only useful if the one opening it knows what is inside. This is the specific qualification a human court lacks. Judges are limited by evidence, by testimony that can be bought, by motives that cannot be inspected. The verse names a judge for whom the evidentiary problem does not arise, which reaches back to the sūrah's opening claim that nothing of an atom's weight escapes Him and everything is written down (34:3).

**Why the Argument Is Left Unfinished on Purpose**

Read in sequence with what precedes it, this verse completes a deliberate refusal. The sūrah has been arguing hard — challenging the opponents to call their deities (34:22), removing the intercession they relied on (34:23), extracting their own admission about provision (34:24) — and at the point where a debater would press for surrender, it stops and refers the matter upward. This is not weakness in the argument. It is a claim about what arguments can accomplish. Positions of this kind are not abandoned because a debating point lands; they are abandoned when the facts become undeniable, and the verse says when that will be. There is also a discipline being imposed on the believing side. A community told that its dispute will be settled by God at the gathering is a community told not to settle it by other means in the meantime.

**Confidence Without Coercion**

The tone of this verse repays attention, because it is not the tone of a threat. Nothing here describes the fate of the losing party, and the sentence could be spoken without hostility to a man one hopes to persuade. Its confidence lies entirely in the referral: a speaker who invites a knowing judge to open the case is a speaker who believes the file favours him. That is a stronger move in a dispute than asserting the conclusion, and it puts the opponent in an awkward position, since to reject the referral he must deny either the gathering or the judge's competence — which is exactly the denial the sūrah opened by quoting (34:3). The chain of *say* verses continues, and the next one stops referring and starts demanding.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:27

> Say, “Show me those ˹idols˺ you have joined with Him as partners. No! In fact, He ˹alone˺ is Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Demand to See the Evidence**

*Arūniya alladhīna alḥaqtum bihi shurakāʾ* — show me the ones you have attached to Him as partners. The verb is *arā*, to show, to cause to see, and the demand is for visual production rather than explanation. This is the third challenge in the sūrah's series and the most confrontational. Earlier the opponents were told to call on their deities and see what answered (34:22); here they are told to bring them forward and point. The shift from calling to showing is not a repetition. A summons can fail for many reasons a believer might explain away — the god is busy, the petitioner unworthy, the request improper. Production has no such excuses available. The challenge asks for the simplest thing any claim can be asked for, which is the referent of its own noun.

**"Attached" — The Verb That Gives the Argument Away**

The most revealing word in the sentence is *alḥaqtum*, from *laḥiqa*, to join, to catch up with, to append. Something *mulḥaq* in Arabic is an annex or an appendix — a thing added to a structure that was complete without it. The verse does not say "the partners He has" or even "those you worship"; it says the ones **you attached**. Agency is placed squarely on the worshippers, and the grammar makes their gods a human addition to a finished arrangement rather than a discovered feature of reality. This is a considerable amount of argument compressed into one verb. It concedes nothing about the status of these beings while explaining exactly how they came to have any status at all: somebody appended them. The sūrah made the same move earlier with *tazʿumūn*, "those you claim" (34:22), where the divinity was called an allegation before it was refuted.

**Why Production Was Impossible on Either Account**

The challenge has two possible targets, and it defeats both. If what is meant is the carved figures in the sanctuary, they can certainly be produced — and producing them is the refutation, since a stone brought into the room and set down is visibly not a partner in the governance of the heavens and the earth. If what is meant is the invisible powers the figures were held to represent, then nothing can be brought at all, and the failure to produce is itself the answer. Either way the demand cannot be met in a way that helps the respondent. This is why the verse does not wait for a reply. It moves straight to *kallā*, no, and states the true position. An objection that cannot be answered either by compliance or by refusal is not really a question; it is a demonstration conducted in the form of a question.

**kallā: A Particle of Flat Refusal**

*Kallā* is one of the strongest negations in Arabic. It does not merely deny a proposition but rebukes the one who advanced it — closer to "certainly not" or "enough" than to "no". The Qur'an reserves it for moments where a claim is to be shut down rather than debated, and its placement here, between the challenge and the affirmation, gives the verse its shape: a demand, a refusal, a statement of fact. Commentators have read the refusal in two ways, and both are sound. It may mean there are no such partners to be shown, so the request cannot be fulfilled because its object does not exist. Or it may function as a rejection of the whole framework — not "you have no partners to produce" but "the matter is not as you have it at all." The second reading leads naturally into what follows, because the verse does not stop at denial.

**What Replaces the Denial**

*Bal huwa Allāhu al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — in fact, He is Allah, the Almighty, the All-Wise. The particle *bal* is corrective: not this, but that. Having cleared the ground, the verse puts something in its place, and the two names it chooses are the ones that answer what partners were wanted for in the first place. *ʿAzīz* means strong, unassailable, impossible to overcome — the root sense involves rarity and inaccessibility, the strength of a fortified height. A sovereign who is *ʿazīz* needs no allies, which removes the practical motive for imagining junior powers. *Ḥakīm* means wise, and from the same root comes the bridle that holds an animal to its course; wisdom here is the ordering of things to their proper ends. A ruler who is wise needs no advisers, which removes the second motive. Between them the two names explain why the partners could never be produced: there was no vacancy for them to fill.

**The Same Names, Deployed Against Different Objections**

The pairing *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* recurs throughout the Qur'an, and its use here is precise rather than formulaic. Al-Anbiyāʾ argues the unity of God from the consequence of its denial: *"Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted"* (21:22) — two authorities cannot govern one order without collision, which is an argument from wisdom. Al-Muʾminūn attacks the evidentiary side: whoever invokes another god *"for which they can have no proof"* will find his penalty with his Lord (23:117), which is the argument this verse makes. Read together, the Qur'an's case against partners runs on two tracks, one about coherence and one about evidence, and this verse takes the second and puts it in the imperative. Having refused the partners and named the sole authority, the sūrah turns from the object of worship to the scope of the messenger's mission.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:28

> We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a deliverer of good news and a warner to all of humanity, but most people do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Restriction and an Expansion in One Sentence**

The verse is built on a limiting particle and a universal noun, and the two pull in opposite directions. *Wa mā arsalnāka illā* — We did not send you except as. Everything after that is a boundary on the office: he is a bringer of good news and a warner, and nothing else is claimed. Then the sentence widens without warning: *kāffatan li-l-nās*, to the whole of humankind. So the same clause narrows the function and extends the audience to its outer limit. This is a characteristic Qur'anic combination and it answers two different objections at once. To those inflating the messenger's status, it says his role is delivery. To those shrinking his relevance to a quarrel among Meccan clans, it says the addressee is the human race. Neither correction could be made without the other, since a modest office with a universal scope is a different thing from either modesty or grandeur alone.

**kāffah: The Word That Can Modify Either Side**

*Kāffah* is the interesting word, and Arabic grammarians disagreed about what it attaches to. Read as modifying *al-nās*, it means the people in their entirety — sent to all of them, which is how this translation takes it. Read as modifying the manner of sending, it comes from the root *k-f-f*, to restrain or to gather in, and yields the sense of being sent comprehensively, combining the two functions of announcement and warning in one mission. The first reading has the stronger support from parallel verses. Al-Anbiyāʾ says he was sent *"as a mercy for the whole world"* (21:107); al-Nisāʾ says *"We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ as a messenger to ˹all˺ people"* (4:79); al-Aʿrāf has him announce it in his own voice. The second reading is not therefore wrong, and nothing forces a choice, since a mission that combines both functions is precisely what a universal audience requires.

**Good News Before Warning, Every Time**

The order of the two functions is fixed in the Qur'an and worth noticing. *Bashīr* comes first, then *nadhīr*. The root of the first is *b-sh-r*, which also gives *bashrah*, the skin — news so good that it shows in the face. A *bashīr* is the runner who arrives ahead of a caravan with word that it is safe. *Nadhīr* is from *n-dh-r*, to give notice of danger, the sentry's function. Both are messengers and neither is a judge; both deliver information that the recipient must then act on. The pairing appears repeatedly in this order — (5:19), (7:188), (11:2), (17:105), (25:56), (33:45), (35:24), (48:8) — and the consistency is itself a statement about emphasis. A religion whose messenger is described first as a bringer of good news is presenting itself differently from one whose messenger is first a threat.

**Why Universality Was the Harder Claim in Mecca**

The idea that a prophet belongs to a people was the normal assumption of the ancient world and of the Arabian peninsula in particular. Every community had its own cult, its own sanctuary and, where it claimed one, its own line of holy men. A message addressed to all of humanity broke that pattern in a way that carried direct social consequences: it implied that tribal boundaries did not define religious obligation, that the Arab and the non-Arab stood in the same relation to it, and that no clan held a franchise over the sanctuary's meaning. For the Quraysh, who derived considerable standing from custodianship of a local shrine, the claim was not abstract. It is one thing to be told that your gods are false; it is another to be told that the replacement is not yours either. The Qur'an raises the same point about earlier prophets by contrast rather than by argument, noting of several that they were sent to their own people specifically, while reserving for this mission a description with no such limit attached. The difference is not presented as a promotion of one messenger over others but as a difference in the assignment, and the sūrah's placement of the claim here — in the middle of a local quarrel about local idols — is what gives it force.

**"Most People Do Not Know"**

The closing clause is deflationary and the sūrah uses it more than once, repeating it at (34:36) about provision. *Wa lākinna akthara al-nāsi lā yaʿlamūn* — but most people do not know. Coming directly after the announcement of a universal mission, it acknowledges the gap between the scope of the message and the size of its reception. The verb is *yaʿlamūn*, they do not know — not "they do not believe" and not "they refuse", which are stronger words the Qur'an uses elsewhere. What is being described is ignorance rather than defiance, and the diagnosis is consistent with the sūrah's treatment of its opponents as men operating on unexamined assumptions (34:20). The clause also functions as a caution against measuring truth by headcount. A claim addressed to everyone that most people have not grasped is exactly what the verse says it is, and the low uptake is reported without embarrassment.

**The Limit on the Office Is Also a Defence of It**

There is one more thing this verse accomplishes that its neighbours make clear. The Meccan opponents had accused the Prophet of fabricating a message and of madness (34:8), and both charges assume a man claiming more than he was. A messenger who announces and warns, who neither compels belief nor controls outcomes, is a smaller figure than the one being accused. The sūrah will press this defence further, having him disclaim any payment (34:47) and even accept that if he is astray the loss falls on him (34:50). Read against that background, *We did not send you except as* is not a reduction of the Prophet's standing but a description of it, and an accurate description is the best answer available to a caricature. The next verse gives the opponents their reply.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:29

> And they ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Designed Not to Be Answered**

*Matā hādhā al-waʿdu in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — when is this promise, if you are truthful? The sentence has the grammar of an enquiry and the function of a taunt, and the two halves of it work together to produce that effect. The first half asks for a date. The second half attaches a condition that turns the request into a test of the speaker's honesty rather than of the claim's truth. Nothing about the structure invites information. A man who genuinely wanted to know when something would happen would not append a clause implying that failure to name a date proves he is being lied to. This is one of the recurring voices in the Qur'an's record of its opponents, appearing at (10:48), (21:38), (27:71), (36:48) and (67:25) in nearly identical words. The consistency across chapters suggests a stock objection in actual circulation rather than a literary device.

**Why the Demand for a Date Is a Trap**

The logic the question relies on is worth exposing, because it looks stronger than it is. If no date is given, the questioner concludes the threat is empty. If a date were given and passed without event, he would have his refutation. And if a date were given far in the future, he would point out that nothing testable had been asserted. The demand is therefore unanswerable by design, and this is precisely why the Qur'an never meets it on its own terms. The Book states repeatedly that the timing of the Hour is withheld from everyone including the Prophet, which the questioners took as evasion and which the sūrah has already explained on other grounds: knowledge of the unseen belongs to God, as the failure of Solomon's jinn demonstrated (34:14). Refusing to supply a date is not a weakness in the position. It is a consequence of the position.

**"This Promise" — A Word That Cuts Both Ways**

The noun they use is *al-waʿd*, translated here as *threat*. Arabic distinguishes between *waʿd*, a promise of something good, and *waʿīd*, a promise of something bad, though *waʿd* covers both in general usage. Their choice of the neutral term is a small piece of evidence about how they heard the message. To them the coming Day was a threat and nothing else, an item of hostile rhetoric produced to frighten people into compliance. The sūrah has given a different account of the same event from its fourth verse onward, where the Day is first described as the occasion on which believers receive forgiveness and honourable provision (34:4). The opponents' word choice therefore reveals something they did not intend to disclose: they had understood only half of what was being said to them, and the half they missed was the half addressed to people unlike themselves.

**Who Is Being Asked**

The verb is plural and the condition is addressed to a group — *if you are truthful*, plural *ṣādiqīn*. The translation supplies the referent: they are asking the believers, not only the Prophet. This detail matters more than it seems. A challenge put to a small community rather than to its leader is a challenge aimed at the weakest point of a new movement, which is the confidence of ordinary members who have staked a great deal on a claim they cannot demonstrate. Every such member had relatives asking exactly this question at exactly this volume. The sūrah's answer in the following verse is accordingly given to the Prophet to relay, which supplies the community with a reply it could repeat. Scripture here is functioning as it often does in the Meccan period: not as abstract doctrine but as material for people under social pressure.

**Certainty About the Fact, Silence About the Hour**

The Qur'an's position on this question is unusual and consistent: maximum confidence that the Day will come, complete refusal to say when. Both halves are held at full strength, and the sūrah has already displayed the pattern in its third verse, where the Prophet is made to swear an oath that the Hour will certainly arrive while adding nothing at all about its timing (34:3). The combination is not a compromise between belief and ignorance. It reflects a claim about what kind of knowledge is available: the structure of the world is knowable, including the fact that it is the sort of place where accounts are settled, while the schedule is not. A questioner who treats the withheld schedule as evidence against the structure has confused two different kinds of claim.

**What the Question Reveals About the Questioner**

There is a diagnostic value in this sentence that the sūrah exploits. A man who asks when a danger is coming, and who treats the absence of a date as permission, has told you how he makes decisions: he responds to deadlines, not to risks. Anyone who has watched a person postpone a medical appointment because no symptom has yet arrived recognises the reasoning. It is not stupidity; it is a preference for the tangible over the probable, and it works well enough in ordinary life to feel like wisdom. The Qur'an's alternative is stated in the very next verse and is worth noting in advance only as the shape of a reply — the date exists and is fixed, and its being unknown to him changes nothing about it. Behind the taunt lies the assumption the sūrah named in its opening exchange, that the Hour will simply never come to these particular people (34:3), and the taunt is that assumption in its confident public form. Ridicule, as the chapter has shown before, is how an unexamined assumption defends itself when it cannot produce an argument.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:30

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “A Day has ˹already˺ been appointed for you, which you can neither delay nor advance by a ˹single˺ moment.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Answer That Refuses the Question's Terms**

They asked when. The reply does not say when, and it does not evade either. *Lakum mīʿādu yawm* — you have an appointment of a Day. The information supplied is not the date but the status of the date: it exists, it is fixed, and it is yours. This is a precise response to a question that was not really asking for a calendar entry. The taunt assumed that an unspecified threat is an empty one; the answer separates specification from certainty and hands back the half that matters. A man told that his court hearing is scheduled, and that the clerk will not tell him the day, has not been reassured. The verse works because it concedes the one thing the questioners wanted — no date — while removing the conclusion they meant to draw from it.

**mīʿād: The Vocabulary of an Appointment**

The noun is *mīʿād*, from the same root as *waʿd*, the promise the opponents mentioned in their question. A *mīʿād* is an appointed time or place of meeting — the word used for a rendezvous, a due date, a tryst. Arabic builds it on the pattern of nouns of place and instrument, which gives it a concrete feel: not merely a promise but a fixed point at which two parties come together. The choice answers their wording directly. They said *this promise*, using a vague noun; the reply upgrades it to an appointment already entered in the register. And the possessive is pointed: *lakum*, for **you**, the same personal address the sūrah used when they claimed the Hour would not come *to us* (34:3). What they tried to push away as a general doctrine is returned to them as a personal engagement.

**Neither Delayed Nor Advanced**

The double negative covers both directions, and the second one is the surprise. That a fixed Day cannot be postponed is the obvious half; anyone expecting a threat expects that. But it also cannot be brought forward, which means the arrangement is not at the disposal of the impatient any more than of the fearful. The Qur'an uses this pair often, usually of the term granted to a community: *"When their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it"* (7:34), and again at (10:49), (15:5), (16:61) and (23:43). Nūḥ states it of the divine term generally: *"But when Allah's appointed time comes, it cannot be delayed, if only you knew!"* (71:4). Applying the formula here extends it from the fate of nations to the Day itself. The same rule that governs how long a civilisation stands governs when the whole account closes.

**Not by a Single Hour**

The unit named is *sāʿah*, translated here as *a single moment*. This is the same word the sūrah has been using throughout for the Hour itself — *al-sāʿah*, the Day of Judgment, which the opponents said would never come to them (34:3). Using it now as a measure of time produces a deliberate resonance: they cannot shift the Hour by an hour. The choice of unit is also rhetorically shrewd. A verse saying the Day cannot be moved by a century would leave room for the thought that smaller adjustments are available, and human beings negotiate in exactly that space. Naming the smallest common unit of the working day closes it. The sūrah has done this before, measuring divine knowledge by the smallest weight on a merchant's scale (34:3) and now measuring divine scheduling by the smallest division of a merchant's day.

**Why Fixity Is the Point Rather Than Secrecy**

The verse could have replied by stressing that the timing is God's secret, and the Qur'an does say that elsewhere. Instead it stresses that the timing is fixed and immovable, which is a different emphasis with a different practical consequence. Secrecy alone invites speculation and the hope that the thing may be indefinitely deferred. Fixity forecloses both. An appointment that is already set is not affected by the ignorance of the party who must attend; his not knowing the hour is a fact about him, not about the schedule. This is why the verse does not treat the withheld date as a limitation on the claim. The Day is described with three properties — appointed, unmovable, theirs — and none of the three requires the date to be disclosed in order to hold.

**The Door Left Open by the Silence**

There is a mercy in the arrangement that the verse does not state but that follows from it. A man who knew the date would organise his life around it, and the organisation would be arithmetic rather than character: postpone the correction until the last feasible moment, then repent on schedule. A man who knows only that the appointment exists has no way to game it, which means every choice he makes has to be made on its own merits. The unknown hour is therefore not a gap in the information but a condition of the test the sūrah described a few verses earlier, where the arrangement exists so that belief and doubt become visible (34:21).

**The Reply Is Handed Over as Speech, Not Doctrine**

Like the verses around it, this one opens with *qul*, and the instruction matters for how the sentence should be read. It is a line given to the Prophet for use against a taunt delivered in public, and its usefulness in that setting depends on its brevity. A long theological reply to a heckler loses the room; a single sentence that concedes nothing and returns the challenge does not. This is the fourth of the chapter's consecutive *say* commands, and the sequence has a discernible shape: the accounts are separated (34:25), the judge is named (34:26), the evidence is demanded (34:27), the mission is defined (34:28), and now the schedule is asserted. Each reply is short, each answers a specific move, and none of them describes the punishment the opponents were daring the Prophet to describe. Having answered the taunt, the chapter turns from what the deniers say now to what they will say to each other later.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:31

> The disbelievers vow, “We will never believe in this Quran, nor in those ˹Scriptures˺ before it.” If only you could see when the wrongdoers will be detained before their Lord, throwing blame at each other! The lowly ˹followers˺ will say to the arrogant ˹leaders˺, “Had it not been for you, we would certainly have been believers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Refusal Announced in Advance**

The verse opens with a declaration of permanent policy: *lan nuʾmina bi-hādhā al-qurʾāni wa lā bi-lladhī bayna yadayh*. The particle *lan* is Arabic's strongest future negation — not "we do not believe" but "we never will", a door closed before any further evidence can be presented. What makes the statement remarkable is its scope. They reject the Qur'an and also the scriptures that came before it, which is a larger commitment than the argument required. Most of the sūrah's opponents had no quarrel with the Torah or the Gospel; those books were somebody else's business. Extending the refusal backwards makes sense only as a defensive move. If a man rejects one revelation he must explain why the others do not count against him, and the simplest way to avoid the question is to reject the category. The position follows logically from what the preceding verses exposed: men who deny God's unity, prophethood and the resurrection have nothing left to do with books that teach all three.

**A Sentence That Breaks Off Unfinished**

The second part of the verse begins *wa law tarā* — if only you could see — and never supplies its own conclusion. Arabic permits this construction, in which the consequence of a conditional is left out because no words would improve on the reader's own completion. You would see something; what you would then do or feel is not stated. The tradition fills it in variously — you would be astonished, you would see a terrible thing — and the omission is more effective than any of the fillings. The verse is describing a scene that its Meccan audience could not witness, and it declines to tell them how to react to it. Notice also the sudden change of grammatical time. The refusal was declared in the present; the scene is given as a future event described as though it were already visible, which is the Qur'an's usual way of collapsing the distance between a promise and its fulfilment.

**Detained, Then Arguing**

*Mawqūfūna ʿinda rabbihim* — made to stand before their Lord. The word carries the sense of being halted and held, stopped in place pending disposal, which is the posture of prisoners awaiting a hearing rather than guests awaiting an audience. And what they do while held is not plead, confess or fall silent. They argue: *yarjiʿu baʿḍuhum ilā baʿḍin al-qawl*, they throw speech back and forth at one another. The Arabic idiom is of statements being returned like objects. This is the detail that gives the scene its psychological accuracy. People caught in a joint failure do not, in the first instance, examine themselves; they establish who is responsible, and they do it loudly. The Qur'an lets this quarrel run for three full verses, which is a long time by its standards, and the length is part of the argument. What is being exhibited is not a punishment but a conversation, and the conversation is doing damage to both parties before any punishment arrives.

**Two Words for Two Classes**

The parties are named with terms drawn from social structure rather than from theology. *Alladhīna istuḍʿifū* — those who were made weak, a passive form, so the description includes the claim that their weakness was done to them. *Alladhīna istakbarū* — those who made themselves great, an active form built on the root of *kibr*, pride, so the description places the second group's status in their own hands. The grammar has already assigned a preliminary verdict before either party speaks. The sūrah has been building toward this exchange since it quoted the sneer of the comfortable at the poor who followed the Prophet, and it will name the type directly in three verses when it describes the luxury-dwellers of every warned town (34:34). What the Qur'an consistently declines to do is treat the division as merely economic. The arrogant are not condemned for being rich but for the use they made of position.

**The Followers' Case: A Real Grievance, Deployed Badly**

*Law lā antum la-kunnā muʾminīn* — had it not been for you, we would have been believers. The claim deserves to be taken seriously before it is dismissed, because it contains something true. Leaders do obstruct; a man born into a household that mocks the message hears it under conditions a wealthy independent man does not; the Qur'an itself recognises genuine oppression as a mitigating circumstance and holds out pardon for those truly unable to act, while refusing the excuse to those who merely claimed inability (4:97–99). So the followers are not inventing a category. What they are doing is inflating their own case, converting real pressure into total causation, and the inflation is exposed by the word they chose: *believers*. They do not say "we would have acted differently" but "we would have believed", and belief is the one thing no external party can install or prevent. A man can be stopped from praying in public. He cannot be stopped from being convinced.

**Why the Excuse Is Recorded Rather Than Refuted**

The verse does not answer the followers. It gives them their sentence and moves on, leaving the reply to the arrogant in the next verse and the counter-reply after that. This is a deliberate method. An excuse stated in the presence of the One who knows the facts refutes itself, and the Qur'an is content to let the transcript do the work. There is also a warning embedded in the timing. The excuse is offered after the moment when it could have made any difference, at a hearing where the evidence is already in, and everyone involved knows it. For the sūrah's original audience, many of them ordinary Meccans who followed their clan chiefs because that is what one did, the scene functions as a preview of an argument they were already preparing. The chapter's answer is not that the pressure was imaginary. It is that the pressure was survivable, and that a group is standing nearby who survived it.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:32

> The arrogant will respond to the lowly, “Did we ever hinder you from guidance after it came to you? In fact, you were wicked ˹on your own˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Defence Built on a Technicality That Happens to Be True**

The leaders answer with a question rather than a denial of fact, and the question is precisely framed: *a-naḥnu ṣadadnākum ʿani al-hudā baʿda idh jāʾakum* — did we turn you away from the guidance **after it had come to you**? The final clause is the whole defence. They are not claiming innocence of influence; they are pointing out that the guidance reached their followers directly, in the same words and the same streets, and that nothing they did prevented it from arriving. Once a message has been delivered to a man's own ears, the leaders argue, what happens next is inside him. This is a stronger case than the followers' and it is uncomfortable for exactly that reason. The Qur'an does not put a foolish argument in the mouths of the arrogant. It puts the best one available, and lets its strength stand.

**The Verb They Choose for What They Did Not Do**

*Ṣadda* means to block, to turn back, to divert something from its course — the word used for barring people from the sacred mosque and for turning others from the path of God. The leaders select it because it names a physical obstruction, and physical obstruction is the charge they can most safely deny. They did not seize anyone's ears. But the verb's narrowness is what exposes them, since the Qur'an uses *ṣadd* for more than blockade: the sūrah has already described people who *strive to discredit Our revelations* (34:5), which is obstruction by other means. What the leaders have done is answer a wide accusation with a narrow denial and hope the difference passes unnoticed. Anyone who has watched a powerful man respond to a charge of corruption by denying a specific transaction will recognise the technique.

**"In Fact, You Were Wicked"**

Having deflected the charge, they attack: *bal kuntum mujrimīn* — rather, you were criminals. The particle *bal* is corrective, and the noun is *mujrim*, from *jurm*, a crime — the same root the believers used of themselves in the disclaimer a few verses earlier (34:25). Notice the tense. *Kuntum* is the past continuous, describing a settled state rather than an act: you **were** criminals, all along, independently of us. The counter-charge is therefore not that the followers made a bad decision but that they were the kind of people who would. This is the arrogant man's characteristic move at the moment his authority stops protecting him. He does not defend his leadership; he disowns the people who accepted it, and reclassifies them as defective material that would have failed under any management.

**What the Qur'an Concedes to This Argument**

The Book does not treat the leaders' point as worthless, and the honest reading has to say so. It insists repeatedly that no one carries another's burden (6:164; 17:15; 39:7), which is the principle the leaders are invoking. It records that the followers chose whom to follow, and the choice is described elsewhere as a preference for wealth and influence over the messenger who came with proofs. Al-Aḥzāb has the followers make the same complaint in the next world — *"Our Lord! We obeyed our leaders and elite, but they led us astray"* (33:67) — and the Qur'an's answer there is not to cancel their responsibility but to add to the leaders': *"Our Lord! Give them double ˹our˺ punishment"* (33:68). So the accounting the Qur'an operates is not zero-sum. Guilt assigned to a leader is not subtracted from a follower, which means both sides of this quarrel can be substantially right and still both be convicted.

**Why Neither Party Mentions the Evidence**

There is something absent from this exchange that ought to be present. The dispute is about who caused a failure of belief, and neither party discusses whether the message was true. The leaders do not argue that the Qur'an was implausible; the followers do not say they examined it and were unconvinced. Both treat belief as a social outcome produced by pressure, influence and class, and they argue only about the distribution of the pressure. That shared assumption is the deeper failure the scene exposes, and it explains why the Qur'an lets the quarrel run at length instead of interrupting it. Two parties arguing about who is responsible for a decision that neither of them ever made on the merits have convicted themselves of something neither is accusing the other of. The sūrah will shortly prescribe the opposite procedure, telling people to stand apart from the crowd, singly and in pairs, and think (34:46).

**The Sound of Authority Withdrawing**

Read as speech rather than as doctrine, the leaders' reply has a recognisable tone: the cool, technically accurate self-defence of a man whose subordinates have become a liability. The relationship that held in the world does not survive the hearing. In Mecca these men had offered protection, standing and belonging, and their followers had traded compliance for it; here the protection evaporates in a sentence, and the followers are informed that they were criminals on their own account and always had been. The Qur'an makes this desertion a fixed feature of the next world's scenes, where alliances built on worldly advantage are shown collapsing precisely when they would be needed.

**A Question Asked by People Who Know the Answer**

One further feature of the reply deserves notice: it is phrased as a question rather than a statement. *Did we turn you away?* A man confident of his innocence generally asserts it; a man who puts it to his accuser as a question is inviting the accuser to produce proof, which is the move of someone who believes the proof is unavailable rather than someone who believes the charge is false. The distinction is exactly the one the followers will exploit in their next answer, when they stop describing a general atmosphere of discouragement and name a specific instruction they were given. Until that point the leaders' question stands unanswered, and it is worth registering how effective it is. Vagueness on one side and precision on the other decides most arguments, and at this stage in the exchange the precision belongs entirely to the arrogant.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:33

> The lowly will say to the arrogant, “No! It was your plotting by day and night—when you ordered us to disbelieve in Allah and to set up equals with Him.” They will ˹all˺ hide ˹their˺ remorse when they see the torment. And We will put shackles around the necks of the disbelievers. Will they be rewarded except for what they used to do?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Followers Produce the Detail Their First Answer Lacked**

Their opening plea had been vague — had it not been for you (34:31) — and the leaders had answered it easily by pointing out that nobody physically blocked the road. Now the followers come back with specifics, and the specifics change the case. *Bal makru al-layli wa-l-nahār* — rather, it was the scheming of night and day. Then the concrete charge: *idh taʾmurūnanā an nakfura bi-llāhi wa najʿala lahu andādā*, when you were ordering us to reject God and to set up rivals to Him. The verb *amara* means to command, not to suggest, and it names a relationship of authority rather than of influence. This is a materially different accusation from the first. The leaders had denied obstruction; they are now charged with instruction, which their denial did not touch.

**Scheming by Night and Day**

*Makr* is the Qur'an's word for a plan laid out of sight, the stratagem that works because its target cannot see it being made. Attaching it to *night and day* has been read two ways, and both are in the Arabic. It may describe duration — plotting that never stopped, running through every hour. Or it may describe method, with the night for laying plans and the day for executing them. The second reading gives a sharper picture of how the opposition in Mecca actually operated: agreements reached in private houses about what would be said in public, who would be boycotted, which poets would be paid. What the phrase establishes either way is continuity. A man exposed to a sustained, organised campaign is in a different position from one who heard a discouraging remark, and the followers are right to say so.

**The Detail That Convicts the Leaders**

The most damaging word in the followers' speech is *taʾmurūnanā*, you were commanding us. The leaders' defence rested on the claim that they only called and did not compel (34:32). A command issued by a man who controls a person's livelihood, protection and standing in a tribal society is not a suggestion, and the followers have found the one fact that survives the leaders' technicality. Note also what they were commanded to do: to disbelieve, and to set up *andād* — equals, rivals, peers. The word is significant because the Meccan elite did not describe their own religion in those terms; they spoke of intercessors who bring one nearer to God (39:3). The followers, at the hearing, use the accurate description rather than the polite one. Under judgment the euphemisms drop away, and the arrangement is named for what it was.

**Remorse Concealed at the Moment It Becomes Useless**

The quarrel ends without a verdict, and the narration takes over: *wa asarrū al-nadāmata lammā raʾawu al-ʿadhāb* — they concealed their regret when they saw the punishment. The verb *asarra* means to keep something inside, to hide it from view, and the subject is plural without distinction — both parties, leaders and led. The concealment is the strangest detail in the scene and the most human. Why hide regret at the point where nothing further can be lost? Because acknowledging it in front of the other party would be the final concession in an argument both have been fighting to win, and pride outlasts its own usefulness. Some have read the concealment as coming after an earlier open admission, comparing the moment when the guilty cry *"Our Lord! We have now seen and heard, so send us back and we will do good"* (32:12) and are refused. On that reading the regret is swallowed because it has already been voiced once and rejected. Yūnus describes the same silence: *"They will hide ˹their˺ remorse when they see the torment"* (10:54).

**Shackles on the Necks**

*Wa jaʿalnā al-aghlāla fī aʿnāqi alladhīna kafarū* — We will put the shackles on the necks of those who disbelieved. *Ghull* is a specific object, the iron collar fitted around a captive's neck, often joined to the hands. The image recurs across the Qur'an: al-Raʿd says the deniers will have shackles around their necks (13:5), Yā-Sīn describes collars reaching to the chin so the head is forced up (36:8), and Ghāfir shows them dragged by shackles and chains (40:71). What makes the image apt here is its relation to the argument just concluded. The two parties have spent three verses disputing who was free and who was constrained, each claiming the other had the liberty to choose differently. The collar settles the question in the only way it can now be settled: both are restrained, visibly and identically, and the difference they were arguing about has ceased to exist.

**A Closing Question That Answers the Whole Quarrel**

*Hal yujzawna illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — will they be repaid except for what they used to do? The rhetorical question is the verse's verdict on the entire exchange, and it cuts through the apportionment both sides were attempting. Neither is being punished for the other's share. Each is being repaid for his own conduct: the leaders for commanding, the followers for obeying, and the fact that these two are causally linked does not merge them into a single account. Al-Ṣāffāt states the rule in the same words as a general principle — *"and will only be rewarded for what you used to do"* (37:39) — and Ghāfir applies it to both directions of the ledger (40:40). The verb form *kānū yaʿmalūn* is continuous past: what they **used to** do, habitually, over a lifetime. Neither party is convicted on a single act. Both are convicted on a pattern, which is the one thing about a man that no leader installs and no follower can disown.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:34

> Whenever We sent a warner to a society, its elite would say, “We truly reject what you have been sent with.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Rule Stated in the Form of a History**

The sentence is constructed as a universal: *wa mā arsalnā fī qaryatin min nadhīrin illā qāla mutrafūhā* — We never sent a warner to a town except that its luxury-dwellers said. The double negative is the Qur'an's usual way of asserting an exceptionless pattern, and the tense covers all past instances at once. What is being offered to the Prophet's Meccan opponents is therefore not an analogy but a classification. They had treated their rejection as a considered judgment about a particular man and a particular message; the verse tells them it is the standard reaction of their social type, reproduced without variation across every community that ever received a warning. There is something deflating in this that no direct insult would achieve. To be told you are wrong is an argument. To be told your response was predictable before you made it is a different kind of blow.

**mutrafūn: The Word the Verse Turns On**

The rejecters are not called disbelievers, idolaters or leaders. They are *mutrafūn*, from the root *t-r-f*, which denotes ease, abundance and indulgence; a *mutraf* is someone softened by comfort, accustomed to having his wants met. The word could be rendered the pampered, the indulged, the luxury class. This is a sociological description rather than a theological one, and choosing it makes a claim: the obstacle in these people is not intellectual but circumstantial. A man whose life is comfortable has the least use for a message about a coming reckoning, because his present arrangement is working and any change threatens it. The Qur'an elsewhere names the rejecting class as *al-malaʾ*, the notables — the chiefs who speak for a people — and uses that word repeatedly in the stories of Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb and Moses. Sabaʾ chooses the other word, and the shift moves the focus from political office to material comfort.

**Rejection Announced as an Identity**

Their statement is short and emphatic: *innā bimā ursiltum bihi kāfirūn* — we are, in what you have been sent with, rejecters. The Arabic uses the emphatic particle *inna* and a participle rather than a verb, which in Arabic describes a settled state rather than an action. They do not say "we do not accept this" but "we are rejecters of it", making the refusal a description of themselves. Notice also what they reject: not the warner, and not God, but *what you have been sent with* — the content of the message. This is more honest than the charges of madness and fabrication quoted earlier in the sūrah (34:8), and in a sense more final. A man who calls the messenger insane leaves open the possibility of being shown otherwise. A man who declares himself a rejecter of the content has closed the file himself.

**Why Comfort Rather Than Poverty Produces Refusal**

The connection the verse asserts between luxury and rejection is worth examining, since the opposite might be expected — that the comfortable, having leisure and education, would weigh a claim more carefully. The Qur'an's account runs the other way and gives reasons elsewhere. Al-Muʾminūn describes the chiefs of a warned people as those *"spoiled by the worldly luxuries We had provided for them"* (23:33), and the spoiling is the point. A warning is only heard by someone who can imagine his situation changing. Al-Isrāʾ adds a second mechanism: when a society is due for destruction, its indulged class is commanded to obey and rebels instead (17:16). And there is a plain self-interest at work. A message that dissolves inherited status and puts a slave and a chief on the same footing is a message whose adoption costs the chief more than anyone. The sūrah will let this class speak again in the next verse, and what they say there makes the financial logic explicit.

**The Same Rejection Outside the Qur'an's Own Tradition**

The pattern the verse describes is not confined to the communities the Qur'an narrates, and the earlier Abrahamic scriptures record it in their own terms. The prophets of Israel are shown being opposed by the officials and priests of their own cities, and the Gospels state the principle as a proverb in the mouth of Jesus: no prophet is accepted in his hometown, a line preserved in Luke 4:24 and paralleled at Matthew 13:57, Mark 6:4 and John 4:44. That the same observation surfaces independently in these traditions strengthens rather than weakens the Qur'an's claim, since the claim is precisely that this is how established interests behave everywhere. The Prophet Muḥammad's own experience fits the pattern exactly: rejected by the leading families of the city he was born in, believed first by the young, the poor and the unprotected.

**What the Verse Does for the Prophet and for His Followers**

Read in its setting, the verse performs a specific service for a small, harassed community. Their situation looked, from inside Mecca, like a local failure: the best families would not listen, and the movement's adherents were people of no consequence. The verse reframes that failure as the normal opening condition of every prophetic mission on record. Nothing had gone wrong; the expected thing was happening. This is consolation, but it is not only consolation, because the classification also carries a threat for the other party. Every community in the series that produced this reaction has a known ending, and the sūrah has just spent four verses on one of them at Maʾrib. The elite are about to explain why they believe the ending will not apply to them.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:35

> Adding, “We are far superior ˹to the believers˺ in wealth and children, and we will never be punished.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Claims Joined by an Unstated Premise**

The sentence has two halves and the join between them is missing. *Naḥnu aktharu amwālan wa awlādan* — we have more wealth and more children. *Wa mā naḥnu bi-muʿadhdhabīn* — and we will not be punished. Nothing explicit connects the inventory to the conclusion, and yet the speakers move from one to the other without pausing, which tells us the premise was too obvious to them to need stating. That premise is the assumption this whole section of the sūrah exists to destroy: that material success is a divine verdict already delivered. If prosperity indicates approval, then the prosperous have their answer, and the warning they have just rejected (34:34) is addressed to someone else. The argument is not stupid. It is the oldest religious mistake there is, and the Qur'an treats it as a serious position because a great many serious people have held it.

**Why Wealth and Children Specifically**

The pairing *amwāl* and *awlād* is a fixed formula in the Qur'an and names the two forms of capital that mattered in a tribal society. Wealth is the obvious one. Children — specifically sons — were the other, and arguably the more important: manpower for raids and defence, hands for the caravan, standing in the assembly, and the only available form of continuity after death. A man rich in both was secure in this world in every sense his society recognised. The Qur'an's response is never to deny that these are goods. It is to reclassify them. Al-Anfāl and al-Taghābun both state the reclassification in the same words: *"Your wealth and children are only a test"* (8:28; 64:15). Al-Kahf allows them their beauty and then ranks them: *"Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord"* (18:46). A test and an ornament are both real. Neither is a verdict.

**The Boast Names a Comparison Without Naming the Compared**

*Aktharu* is a comparative — more, greater — and a comparative requires a second term. The verse does not supply it in the Arabic, and the translation fills in what the context demands: more than the believers. This is the sting of the sentence. The comparison is with the poor men and slaves who had accepted the message, and the elite are treating that visible gap in fortune as the decisive evidence in a theological dispute. The Qur'an records the same reasoning from earlier communities in almost identical form. Noah's opponents dismissed his followers as *"the lowliest among us"* (11:27); the people addressed by another messenger asked, *"How can we believe in you, when you are followed ˹only˺ by the lowest of the low?"* (26:111). In every case the argument is not about the message at all. It is about the social register of the people who accepted it.

**The Second Half Is the Real Claim**

Of the two clauses, only the first is a fact. That they had more wealth and more sons than the Muslims of early Mecca was simply true, and the verse does not dispute it. The second clause is where the error sits, and it is stated with the same confidence as the first — *mā naḥnu bi-muʿadhdhabīn*, constructed with a negation and an emphatic particle, meaning we are certainly not to be punished. Placing a verifiable fact and an unverifiable prediction in one breath is how the sentence achieves its persuasive force, and the technique is familiar from any confident assertion that smuggles a conclusion behind an observation. The sūrah has already recorded the same people making the same kind of prediction about the Hour (34:3), and the pattern is consistent: they are certain about the future in exactly the way they accuse the Prophet of pretending to be.

**What the Qur'an Says Abundance Actually Predicts**

If wealth signals anything in the Qur'an's account, it more often signals danger than approval. Al-Tawbah issues a direct instruction not to read it as favour: *"So let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this worldly life"* (9:55). Āl ʿImrān is blunter about the settlement day: *"neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah"* (3:10). And al-Shuʿarāʾ names the one asset that will transfer: the Day *"when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit. Only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺"* (26:88–89). The Qur'an's position is not ascetic. Al-Qaṣaṣ tells a man to seek the next world with what he has been given *"without forgetting your share of this world"* (28:77). Wealth is to be used, not renounced — and never read as a report card.

**A Boast the Sūrah Has Already Answered With a Story**

The most efficient refutation of this sentence is not an argument but the passage sitting twenty verses earlier. The people of Sheba had more of everything than these speakers: two irrigated plains, a road with way-stations in sight of one another, safe travel by day and night (34:15, 34:18). By the standard being proposed here, they were the most approved community in Arabia. They were scattered until nothing remained of them but a story told by passing traders (34:19). The sūrah constructed that account before quoting this boast, so that the boast arrives already refuted by an example its audience knew from their own trade route. The Prophet is nonetheless instructed to answer in words as well, and the reply in the next verse takes the argument apart at its foundation rather than at its conclusion.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:36

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Surely ˹it is˺ my Lord ˹Who˺ gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills. But most people do not know.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply Attacks the Premise, Not the Boast**

The elite had claimed more wealth and more sons, and concluded that they were safe (34:35). The answer does not dispute the inventory and does not argue that they will in fact be punished. It goes underneath both and addresses the assumption connecting them: *inna rabbī yabsuṭu al-rizqa li-man yashāʾu wa yaqdir* — my Lord expands provision for whom He wills, and restricts it. If the distribution is decided by a will that is not explained, then the amount in a man's hands carries no information about his standing. The boast collapses not because its second half is false but because its first half was never evidence. This is the more economical refutation, and the harder one to answer. A man told that he will be punished can dispute the prediction; a man told that his wealth was never a signal has nothing left to point at.

**Two Verbs From the Vocabulary of Measuring**

The Arabic pairs *yabsuṭu* and *yaqdiru*, and both are physical words before they are economic ones. *Basaṭa* means to spread out, to unroll — the same root gives *bisāṭ*, a carpet laid flat, and *basṭ*, extension. *Qadara* means to measure out, to apportion, to set a limit; it is the same verb used when David was told to get the gauge of his armour links right (34:11) and when the travel stages of Sheba were measured out (34:18). The second word is important because it is not a word for deprivation. To restrict provision, in this vocabulary, is to measure it — to give a determined amount rather than an unrolled one. Neither state is described as a punishment or a reward. Both are described as acts of apportionment by the same hand, which is exactly what the elite's reasoning cannot accommodate.

**"To Whoever He Wills" — The Clause That Does the Work**

*Li-man yashāʾ*, for whom He wills, appears in the middle of the sentence and severs the link the opponents relied on. Had the verse said God expands provision for the grateful and restricts it for the ungrateful, the boast would have been refuted by substituting one signalling system for another, and the wealthy would merely have needed a new theory about who is approved. By declining to name a criterion, the verse removes the possibility of reading the distribution at all. This is not a statement that the distribution is arbitrary; the Qur'an calls the same Lord All-Wise in this sūrah's first verse (34:1). It is a statement that the criterion has not been published, which is a different thing. What follows from it is a rule of interpretation: no one may infer a verdict from a balance sheet, in either direction.

**The Formula Recurs Across the Book**

This sentence is one of the Qur'an's fixed statements and appears in nearly identical wording at (13:26), (17:30), (28:82), (29:62), (30:37), (39:52) and (42:12), as well as twice in this sūrah. Tracking its settings shows what it is for. Al-Qaṣaṣ places it in the mouths of those who had envied Qārūn's treasure the day before the earth swallowed him. Al-Rūm places it alongside the observation that people despair when restricted and grow arrogant when expanded. Al-Isrāʾ attaches it to a warning against killing children for fear of poverty. In every case the formula is deployed against a false inference drawn from someone's material condition — envy, despair, arrogance, fear. Sabaʾ uses it against the last of the sūrah's targets, the man who reads his own prosperity as a certificate.

**Why the Prophet Says "My Lord"**

The possessive in *rabbī* is worth noticing, because the verse could have said "Allah" or "your Lord". The Prophet is made to attribute the doctrine to his own Lord in a dispute with men who were wealthier than he was. This places the claim in the mouth of someone who loses by it socially and gains nothing from it materially — a speaker with less wealth and fewer sons than his opponents, asserting that wealth and sons prove nothing. The same first-person possessive returns at the end of this passage when the formula is repeated with an addition (34:39), and it recurs throughout the sūrah's closing sequence of *say* commands. The effect is to keep the argument personal. It is not an anonymous theological proposition being announced; it is one man's stated relationship with the One who apportions, offered against another party's stated relationship with their own accounts.

**"But Most People Do Not Know"**

The verse closes with the same clause that ended the announcement of the universal mission eight verses earlier (34:28), and the repetition is not filler. What most people do not know, in this setting, is specifically the thing just said — that the distribution carries no message. This is an accurate observation about human beings in any period. The reflex that reads success as merit and hardship as fault is close to universal, survives every argument against it, and reasserts itself in each generation's preferred vocabulary. The word is *yaʿlamūn*, know, rather than believe, which frames the error as a gap in understanding rather than an act of defiance.

**What the Doctrine Asks of the Poor as Well as the Rich**

A statement that cuts the link between wealth and divine favour has two edges, and the sūrah's audience contained people on both of them. For the elite, it removes a certificate they were relying on. For the believing poor of Mecca — the men and women the elite had just dismissed by comparison — it removes something too: the reading of their own hardship as a sign of abandonment. If an expanded provision proves nothing, a restricted one proves nothing either, and the verse's refusal to publish a criterion protects both parties from misreading their circumstances. Mary is shown answering exactly this question inside the sanctuary, asked where her provision came from, and replying that it is from God, *"Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit"* (3:37). The sūrah now turns from the false inference to the true one, and states what actually brings a person near.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:37

> It is not your wealth or children that bring you closer to Us. But those who believe and do good—it is they who will have a multiplied reward for what they did, and they will be secure in ˹elevated˺ mansions.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Denial Is About Proximity, Not About Value**

The verse states its negative with precision: *wa mā amwālukum wa lā awlādukum bi-llatī tuqarribukum ʿindanā zulfā*. What is denied is a specific function — that wealth and children *bring near*. It is not said that they are worthless, forbidden, or spiritually dangerous in themselves. The Qur'an elsewhere calls them an adornment (18:46) and a test (8:28), and tells a wealthy man not to forget his share of this world (28:77). So the sentence is narrow and exact. These things do many things: they feed, protect, extend a name, buy influence. The one thing they do not do is close the distance between a person and God. The boast quoted two verses earlier had assumed precisely that function (34:35), and the reply removes it without conceding that the assets are unreal.

**zulfā: A Word for Court Access**

The final word of the denial is *zulfā*, nearness or a degree of proximity, from a root that gives *zalafa*, to draw close. In the political vocabulary of the ancient Near East, nearness to the throne was the currency of power — a courtier's rank was measured by how close he stood. The word is therefore precisely the one the Meccan elite would have reached for, since their whole model of religion was built on access: idols were valued as intermediaries who stood near and could carry a request inward (39:3). The verse uses their own concept and tells them their assets purchase none of it. This connects directly to the demolition of intercession earlier in the sūrah, where even the angels are shown waiting in fear for a decree they had no part in shaping (34:23). If proximity cannot be bought at that level, it certainly cannot be bought with camels and sons.

**The Exception Clause and Its Grammar**

After the denial comes *illā man āmana wa ʿamila ṣāliḥan* — except whoever believes and does good. Arabic readers have long noticed that the exception does not grammatically match what precedes it. The negated subjects were wealth and children, which are things; the exception introduces a person. The mismatch is deliberate and it carries the argument. Had the verse said "except the wealth spent in charity", the sentence would have kept the old framework and merely adjusted the qualifying conditions, leaving wealth as the operative agent. By switching from the asset to the person, the verse changes what the question is about. Nearness is not produced by any quantity of anything. It is produced by what someone is and does, and the sūrah has used this exact pairing of belief and repair before, in the promise of forgiveness and honourable provision (34:4).

**Multiplied — And the Qur'an Gives the Multipliers**

The reward is *jazāʾ al-ḍiʿf*, a doubled or multiplied recompense, and the Book is unusually specific about the factors elsewhere. Al-Anʿām sets the floor: *"Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold"* (6:160). Al-Baqarah raises it with an agricultural image — a grain that produces seven ears of a hundred grains each — and then declines to cap it: *"And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills"* (2:261). The contrast with the deniers' arithmetic is exact. They had counted their holdings and found themselves ahead; the verse describes a currency in which a small deposit is multiplied by a factor nobody set and nobody can predict. Note also what is multiplied: *bimā ʿamilū*, for what they did. The multiplication applies to action, not to intention or to status, which keeps the promise tied to the same ground the denial cleared.

**Secure, and Why Security Is the Reward Named**

The final clause promises that they will be *fī al-ghurufāti āminūn* — in the upper chambers, secure. *Ghurfah* is a room on an upper storey, the elevated part of a house, and in the Arabian context an upper room was the desirable one: cooler, private, defensible, a marker of standing. The word chosen for the state of those in them is *āminūn*, from *amn*, safety — the same root the sūrah used when Sheba was told to travel between their way-stations *safely* (34:18). The choice of security as the headline reward, rather than pleasure or splendour, answers the boast precisely. What wealth and sons actually purchased in a tribal society was security: protection from raids, from famine, from having no one to avenge you. The verse grants the objective and relocates it, promising the thing the elite were buying to people who could not afford it.

**Elevation Given as an Answer to Social Rank**

Two details in the closing clause work together. The rooms are high, and the occupants are the people the elite had just dismissed as their inferiors in wealth and children (34:35). The reversal is not stated and does not need to be; the architecture states it. A society that measured a man by the house he could hold is shown its own metric applied to a different population by a different assessor. The Qur'an has been building this contrast through the whole passage — the luxury-dwellers who reject every warner (34:34), the boast, the reply about apportionment (34:36), and now the assignment of upper rooms to the believers. What makes the sequence more than consolation is that nothing in it disparages the goods themselves. The believers in this verse are not promised poverty vindicated. They are promised the same things the elite valued, on terms the elite could not qualify for.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:38

> As for those who strive to discredit Our revelations, it is they who will be confined in punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Description, Returned With a Different Ending**

This verse repeats, word for word in the Arabic, the description the sūrah gave in its fifth verse — *alladhīna yasʿawna fī āyātinā muʿājizīn*, those who exert themselves against Our signs seeking to frustrate them. What changes is the second half. There the sentence ended with a torment of agonising pain (34:5); here it ends with confinement. A chapter that repeats a clause exactly and then alters its conclusion is not padding. It is completing a frame. The first occurrence opened the sūrah's case against the organised opposition; this one closes it, and everything between the two — the mockery of resurrection, the ruin of Sheba, the quarrel of leaders and followers, the boast about wealth — sits inside the brackets. Readers of the Arabic would hear the repetition immediately, the way a listener hears a musical phrase return at the end of a movement.

**Work Against the Signs, Once More**

The vocabulary was examined when the phrase first appeared, and the key terms deserve restating because the verse's force depends on them. *Saʿā* is a verb of physical exertion — hurrying, striving, labouring at something — not of holding an opinion. *Muʿājizīn* names the objective: to render the signs *ʿājiz*, incapable, unable to deliver. The people described are therefore not those who failed to be convinced but those who worked to ensure the message could not do its work. The sūrah has shown exactly what that work looked like in the verses between the two occurrences: the staged mockery inviting a crowd to look at a man (34:7), the coordinated plotting by night and day that the followers eventually named (34:33), the boast deployed to discredit the believers by comparison (34:35). The definition given in verse 5 has been filled in by twenty-three verses of illustration before being restated here.

**muḥḍarūn: Brought In and Held**

The new element is the closing word, *muḥḍarūn*, from *aḥḍara*, to bring someone into presence, to produce them before an authority. It is a legal and administrative term rather than a poetic one: the person *muḥḍar* is the one fetched and made to stand where he is required. Al-Rūm uses the same participle for those who denied the signs and the meeting with their Lord, saying they *"will be confined in punishment"* (30:16). The image is of custody rather than of burning. What this adds to the earlier verse is a change of category: verse 5 described what they will feel, and this one describes their legal status. They will be produced, held, and kept, which is a condition that begins before any sensation of pain and does not depend on it.

**Being Produced Is the General Rule; Where You Are Produced Is Not**

The Qur'an says everyone will be brought forward — Yā-Sīn states that all will be *"brought before Us"* — so the verb by itself does not distinguish these people from anyone else. What distinguishes them is the destination attached: they are brought **into the punishment** rather than merely into the presence. This distinction is worth drawing out because it clarifies what the verse actually threatens. Nobody escapes the summons; the universal appearance is not itself the penalty. The penalty is where the summons deposits you. Al-Ḥajj makes the point by pairing the same description with a location: those who strive against the revelations *"will be the residents of the Hellfire"* (22:51). Custody, in these verses, is not a stage on the way to a verdict. It is the verdict's execution.

**The Irony in the Word**

There is a reversal contained in *muḥḍarūn* that the Arabic makes audible. These are people characterised by exertion — running, striving, working at their opposition. The word that describes their end is a passive participle: they are brought. Every verb attached to them in verse 5 and in this verse's first half is active and effortful; the verb attached to their outcome removes their agency entirely. A man who spent his life making things happen is finally a man who is fetched. The sūrah has used this kind of grammatical reversal before, describing those who made themselves great and those who were made weak in the quarrel scene (34:31), where the forms of the verbs delivered a verdict before either party spoke.

**Why the Verse Is Placed Immediately After the Promise**

The two preceding verses described the multiplied reward and the secure upper rooms of those who believe and do good (34:37). This one supplies the alternative in a single line, and the brevity is itself an argument about proportion. There is also a precise contrast in the imagery of the two verses. The believers were placed in elevated chambers and called *secure*; these are brought in and held. One group is housed, the other is detained. The Arabic sets the two states against each other using the ordinary vocabulary of residence and custody, and the difference between an upper room and a cell is not primarily a difference in comfort but in whether the door is yours to open. The Qur'an frequently pairs the two destinies, and in this sūrah the pairing has appeared twice: at verses 4 and 5, and now at 37 and 38. In both pairs the promise comes first and receives more words than the threat. That ordering is consistent enough across the Book to count as a deliberate emphasis. A reader who takes the chapter's temperature from its severity would misread it; the severe sentences are short, and they follow longer sentences about forgiveness, honourable provision, multiplied reward and security. The next verse repeats the sūrah's statement about provision and adds something that has not yet been said.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:39

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Surely ˹it is˺ my Lord ˹Who˺ gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills of His servants. And whatever you spend in charity, He will compensate ˹you˺ for it. For He is the Best Provider.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Sentence, With Two Additions**

The opening clause repeats verse 36 word for word until it reaches a phrase that was not there: *min ʿibādih*, of His servants. Then the verse continues into entirely new territory with a promise about spending. A repetition that adds two things is doing something specific, and the additions are related. The first occurrence of the formula was an answer to men boasting of wealth; it told them the distribution carries no verdict (34:36). This occurrence is addressed to people who might act on that knowledge, and the addition of *His servants* narrows the frame from humanity in general to those in a relationship of service. What follows is accordingly practical rather than corrective. Having established that no one can read the ledger, the sūrah now tells those who accept this what to do with whatever is in their hands.

**The Fear That Stops Generosity**

*Wa mā anfaqtum min shayʾin fa-huwa yukhlifuh* — whatever you spend of anything, He replaces it. The verb *akhlafa* means to put something in the place of what has gone, to supply a successor; from the same root come *khalīfah*, a successor, and *khalaf*, what comes after. The promise is therefore of replacement rather than of reward, and the distinction matters because it addresses the actual obstacle. The reason people do not give is rarely that they doubt giving is virtuous. It is that they can see the gap the gift will leave. A promise of merit in the next world does not close a present-tense gap; a promise of replacement does. The Prophet described two angels who descend every day, one saying: O Allah, compensate the one who spends, and the other: O Allah, bring ruin to the one who withholds (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1442, from Abū Hurayrah). The wording of that supplication uses the same root as this verse.

**Replacement in What Currency?**

The verse does not specify what the replacement consists of, and the silence is worth respecting rather than filling. Commentators have read it as compensation in this world, in the next, or in both, and the Qur'an's related statements support a range. Al-Baqarah describes charity as a loan to God, *"which Allah will multiply many times over"* (2:245). Al-Rūm contrasts it with lending at interest and says of the charitable that *"it is they whose reward will be multiplied"* (30:39). Al-Naḥl states the general principle with a comparison: *"Whatever you have will end, but whatever Allah has is everlasting"* (16:96). What all these share is a refusal to treat the transfer as a loss. Whether the return arrives as money, as sufficiency, as protection from a cost that never materialises, or as something recorded and paid later, the verse's claim is that the outgoing entry is matched. A believer is therefore never in deficit from having given.

**Charity Placed Immediately After the Doctrine of Apportionment**

The two halves of this verse are joined for a reason that repays attention. If God expands and restricts provision as He wills, then two conclusions are available. The fatalistic one says that since the amounts are set, spending and withholding make no difference. The verse takes the other road: since the amounts are set by Someone who can also replace what leaves your hand, spending is safe. Belief in apportionment, on this account, is what makes generosity rational rather than what makes it pointless. The man most likely to hoard is the man who believes his balance is entirely his own achievement and entirely his own responsibility to defend. This is precisely the man the sūrah has been arguing with since the boast of wealth and children (34:35), and this verse quietly names the behavioural consequence of his theology.

**"Best of Providers" — A Title Applied to the One Being Trusted**

The closing name is *khayr al-rāziqīn*, the best of those who provide. The construction is comparative and therefore concedes that others provide too — an employer, a father, a patron, a market. What it claims is superiority within a shared category, and the ground of that superiority is supplied by the clause it follows. Other providers give and are depleted; this one replaces what is spent. The title appears elsewhere in the same practical register: Jesus asks for a table set down and calls on the best of providers (5:114); al-Jumuʿah uses it of what God has beyond the trade and amusement people rush toward (62:11). Placing it here converts the promise from an assertion into an argument. The reason spending is safe is not that generosity is magically rewarded. It is that the account it draws on belongs to a Provider whose supply does not run down.

**Two Kinds of Provision**

There is a distinction the verse's vocabulary invites, and the earlier Muslim commentators drew it explicitly. Al-Tustarī, the third-century ascetic of Basra, is reported to have said of this verse that provision is of two kinds: one is remembrance, which sustains the spirit, the intellect and the heart, as the angels live by remembrance and would perish without it; the other is what is eaten and drunk for the benefit of the body. The observation fits the verse's structure well, since a promise of replacement for what is spent makes most sense if the replacing supply is not limited to the same commodity. A man who gives money may be repaid in something that is not money and still be repaid. The sūrah now leaves the subject of provision and turns to a scene in which the objects of false worship are questioned directly.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:40

> And ˹consider˺ the Day He will gather them all together, and then ask the angels, “Was it you that these ˹polytheists˺ used to worship?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Put to the Wrong Party**

The scene is a courtroom and the first question is not addressed to the accused. God gathers everyone and then turns to the angels: *a-hāʾulāʾi iyyākum kānū yaʿbudūn* — was it you these people were worshipping? The interrogation of a third party in front of the defendants is a recognisable legal technique, and its purpose is not to obtain information. The One asking knows the answer, and so does everyone in the room. What the question does is force a public statement from the only party whose testimony the defendants cannot dispute. A worshipper can argue with his accuser; he cannot argue with the object of his own worship. By putting the question to the angels rather than to the idolaters, the scene removes any possibility of the defence continuing.

**Why the Angels and Not the Idols**

The Qur'an could have staged this with the stone figures of the Kaʿbah, and elsewhere it does describe the summoning of the partners (28:64). Choosing the angels here is deliberate, and the reason is that they are the strongest case for the other side. Stone cannot answer, which makes its silence ambiguous; a being with intelligence, standing and genuine proximity to God can answer, and is the sort of being for whom the intercession argument was actually made. Some among the Arabs held the angels to be daughters of God, a claim the Qur'an rejects sharply elsewhere. If the highest beings ever credited with divinity disown the worship offered to them, nothing below them can retain a claim. The sūrah has been narrowing toward this point since it denied the idols an atom's weight of ownership (34:22) and showed the angels themselves waiting in terror for a decree (34:23).

**The Emphatic Pronoun in the Middle of the Question**

Arabic word order carries the emphasis here in a way English struggles to reproduce. The question places *iyyākum*, you, before the verb — was it **you** they were worshipping? The isolated pronoun throws the weight onto the identity of the object rather than onto the act. This matters because the answer that follows will turn on exactly that point: the angels will not deny that worship occurred, only that they were its true recipient. The question is therefore precisely framed to elicit that distinction. It does not ask whether these people were idolaters, which is not in dispute, but whether the worship reached the addressee it named. A gift sent to the wrong address is still a gift sent, and it is still lost.

**The Verb Tense Describes a Habit**

*Kānū yaʿbudūn* is the continuous past — they **used to** worship, over and over, as a settled practice. The Qur'an consistently describes the conduct that is judged in this tense rather than in the simple past, and the sūrah has done so twice already in this passage, with those who were repaid for what they *used to do* (34:33) and those who denied what they *used to* deny. What is being examined at the reckoning is not an episode but a pattern. This has a practical implication for how the scene should be read. Nobody in it is being convicted of a moment of confusion or a single misdirected prayer. The defendants spent lifetimes in a practice, built institutions around it, and defended it in public, which is why the question can be put so simply and answered so briefly.

**A Parallel Interrogation Elsewhere**

Al-Furqān stages the same courtroom with a sharper question. There God gathers them along with what they worshipped besides Him and asks: *"Was it you who misled these servants of Mine, or did they stray from the Way ˹on their own˺?"* (25:17). That version puts responsibility directly in issue, asking whether the worshipped party recruited the worshippers. Sabaʾ's version asks only about the identity of the recipient, which is the narrower and, for this sūrah's purposes, the more useful question. The chapter has already settled responsibility in the quarrel between leaders and followers, where each was repaid for his own conduct (34:33). What remains to be established is whether the whole apparatus of intermediary worship ever connected to anything, and that is what this question is designed to expose.

**The Silence of the Accused**

One feature of the scene is easy to miss: the idolaters are present and say nothing. They are gathered, they are indicated — *these* — and they are discussed in the third person while standing there. The sūrah opened with a mockery in which the Prophet was talked about in the third person to a crowd, as *a man who claims* (34:7). The arrangement is now reversed, and the men who reduced him to an exhibit are themselves the exhibit, pointed at in a conversation they are not part of. Nothing in the verse comments on this. The reversal is delivered by the grammar and left there, which is how the Qur'an usually handles such symmetries.

**Gathering Before Questioning**

The sequence of the verse puts the assembly first and the interrogation second: *yaḥshuruhum jamīʿan thumma yaqūl* — He gathers them all, **then** He says. The particle *thumma* marks a gap between the two actions rather than a smooth continuation, so the scene includes a pause: everyone is present and nothing has yet been said. This is the third time the sūrah has mentioned the gathering, after the answer to the taunt about timing (34:26) and the Day appointed that cannot be moved (34:30), and each mention adds a detail the others lacked. Here the addition is the presence of every party at once — worshippers and worshipped, accusers and accused, in one place. The efficiency of the arrangement is part of its terror. Disputes that ran for lifetimes across separate communities are settled in a single session because, for the first time, everyone involved is in the room.

The angels' answer comes in the next verse and it is not a defence of the people who worshipped them.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:41

> They will say, “Glory be to You! Our loyalty is to You, not them. In fact, they ˹only˺ followed the ˹temptations of evil˺ jinn, in whom most of them had faith.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Word Is Not an Answer**

Asked whether they were the ones being worshipped, the angels do not begin with yes or no. They begin with *subḥānak* — glory be to You, or more literally, You are far above that. The word *subḥān* comes from a root meaning to swim or to move freely, and its sense in this formula is of declaring something utterly removed, transcendent, beyond the suggestion just made. So the first movement of their reply is not testimony about the defendants but a disclaimer about God. Before addressing the facts, they clear away the implication that anything could genuinely occupy the position the worshippers assigned. This is the instinctive response of beings who know exactly what they are: not offence at the insult to themselves, but the immediate refusal of any association placed near God. The Qur'an records the same opening in other scenes of questioned servants, where the response to a charge of receiving worship begins with the same word.

**Loyalty Declared, and the Direction It Runs**

*Anta waliyyunā min dūnihim* — You are our *walī*, not them. The word *walī* is one of the Qur'an's important terms and has no single English equivalent: it covers protector, ally, patron, close friend, and the one whose side you are on. What the angels assert is a relationship of allegiance running upward, and they deny any corresponding relationship with those who petitioned them. The phrase *min dūnihim*, apart from them, is the same construction used throughout the sūrah for the false gods worshipped *besides* God (34:22), and turning it around is pointed. The worshippers had treated the angels as figures standing between themselves and God; the angels describe themselves as standing entirely on one side, with no bond at all to the other. An intermediary who is not allied with the petitioner cannot function as an intermediary, which is the second time in this chapter the intercession doctrine has been dismantled from above.

**What They Do Not Say**

The angels never claim the worship did not happen, and they never say the worshippers were mistaken about the existence of angels. Both would have been available answers and neither is given. What they deny is the relationship, not the act — which is the precise point the question was framed to test (34:40). This restraint is worth noting because it makes the testimony credible in a way a broader denial would not. They concede the facts and reject the interpretation, and they add a correction that nobody asked for: the worshippers were not, in truth, dealing with angels at all. Having refused their own implication in the practice, they explain what the practice actually was.

**The Correction: It Was the Jinn**

*Bal kānū yaʿbudūna al-jinn* — rather, they were worshipping the jinn. This is the verse's substantive claim and it reframes the whole history of Arabian idolatry. The rites were conducted in the names of angels and deities; the beings actually involved, on this account, were of a different order entirely. The jinn appear throughout the Qur'an as a created species with will, capable of belief and disbelief, hidden from ordinary sight — and this sūrah has already shown their limits, when Solomon's conscripted workers failed to notice their master's death (34:14). Two readings of the claim are available and neither excludes the other. It may mean that jinn actually received and encouraged these devotions, or that the worshippers were following what the jinn suggested, in which case their obedience was a form of worship regardless of the name on the shrine. The second reading fits the sūrah's earlier statement that most people followed Iblīs' expectations (34:20).

**"Most of Them Had Faith in Them"**

The closing clause is *aktharuhum bihim muʾminūn*, most of them believed in them. The word is *muʾminūn* — believers — used here of people committed to the jinn, which is a startling application of the Qur'an's most positive religious term. Its use is not ironic but exact: faith is a disposition of trust and reliance, and these people had it, directed at the wrong object. The observation removes a comfortable assumption about idolatry, namely that it is a failure to believe. The sūrah's opponents were not men short of conviction. They were men of conviction, confident, publicly committed, and willing to argue — which is precisely how the chapter has portrayed them from its third verse onward. Misplaced faith is presented as a species of faith, not as its absence, and that is what makes it so hard to dislodge by argument.

**The Collapse of an Entire Religious Economy**

Taken with the question that prompted it, this reply ends the case. Worshipping a being that does not know it is being worshipped is one kind of error; worshipping in a direction where a different and hostile party is receiving the traffic is another, and the second is what the angels describe. The Meccan system rested on a chain: worshipper, then intermediary, then God. The sūrah has now cut the chain at both joints. Earlier it showed the upper joint failing, with even the angels waiting in fear for a decree they had no part in shaping (34:23). Here the lower joint fails too, as the supposed intermediaries state that no relationship of patronage ever existed and that the traffic was running to a different destination altogether. What remains of the practice is a lifetime of devotion delivered to an address its senders never intended. The verse does not gloat over this, and the angels' tone throughout is a servant's rather than a victor's. The verdict falls in the next verse, and it falls on both parties at once.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:42

> So Today neither of you can benefit or protect each other. And We will say to the wrongdoers, “Taste the torment of the Fire, which you used to deny.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Today" — A Word That Closes an Era**

The verse begins with *fa-l-yawma*, so today, and the adverb carries the weight of the sentence. Everything described before this belonged to a period in which arrangements could be made, alliances formed, favours banked. The word announces that the period has ended and a different set of rules now applies. The Qur'an uses this abrupt temporal marker at the decisive moments of its judgment scenes, and its effect is to make the reckoning present rather than predicted. Note also what the word implies about the day before. The relationships now declared void were functioning, or seemed to be, right up to the previous evening — patrons were petitioned, intermediaries were trusted, and the system returned enough apparent results to sustain belief in it. Nothing in the verse says the arrangements were always obviously worthless. It says they stop working today.

**Neither Benefit Nor Harm: The Two Halves of Any Power**

*Lā yamliku baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin nafʿan wa lā ḍarrā* — none of you holds for another any benefit or any harm. The pairing is the Qur'an's standard formula for the full extent of a power, and both halves are needed. A being that can help but not hurt is a patron; one that can hurt but not help is a threat; one that can do neither is neither, and has no claim on anyone's attention. The verb is *yamliku*, to own or have at one's disposal — the same verb used when the sūrah said the false gods do not own an atom's weight in the heavens or the earth (34:22). The repetition ties the two verses together. What was asserted as a doctrine in the middle of the chapter is now enacted as a fact at the reckoning, and the demonstration requires no argument because both parties are present and neither can produce anything.

**Who Is Being Addressed**

The pronoun is plural and ambiguous in the Arabic, and the ambiguity is productive. Read one way, the address is to the angels and their worshippers: the angels cannot help those who petitioned them, and the worshippers can do nothing for the angels either. Read another way, it is addressed to the idolaters among themselves — the leaders and followers who spent three verses earlier in this same sūrah blaming each other (34:31–33), and who now learn that neither faction can shift anything onto the other. Both readings are supported by the context, since both relationships have been dismantled in the preceding verses. The Qur'an states the general rule behind either reading in al-Baqarah: a Day *"when no soul will be of any help to another"* (2:48), with no intercession accepted and no ransom taken.

**From Third Person to Second: The Sentence Is Handed Down**

The verse's second half changes who is being spoken to. Until now the wrongdoers have been discussed — *these*, pointed at while the angels testified (34:40). Now they are addressed directly: *dhūqū ʿadhāba al-nāri* — taste the torment of the Fire. The shift from discussion to address is how a court moves from evidence to sentence, and the Qur'an marks it with the imperative *dhūqū*, taste. The verb is deliberately physical and somewhat coarse; it belongs to eating and drinking, not to suffering in the abstract. It also carries a note of first acquaintance, since tasting is how one finds out what something actually is. People who had only ever heard the Fire described in words are told to acquire the information directly.

**"Which You Used to Deny"**

The relative clause at the end is the whole verdict compressed into four words: *allatī kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn*, which you used to call a lie. The punishment is identified not by its properties but by its relation to the defendants' own speech. What they are entering is the specific thing they publicly denied — not a surprise, not an item they were never warned about, but the exact object of their mockery. This closes a circuit the sūrah opened in its seventh verse, where they staged a public joke about a man who claimed the scattered dead would be raised (34:7). The verb *kadhdhaba* means to declare something false, and it is the Qur'an's standard word for the response of every community that rejected a messenger. Its use here makes the punishment a consequence of a speech act rather than of an ideology. They said it was a lie; they are shown the thing they said it about.

**Why the Scene Ends Without Reply**

The idolaters are given no answer in this verse and no answer anywhere in the passage. They were silent while the angels testified, silent while the mutual disclaimers were announced, and they are silent when the sentence is delivered. Silence of that kind is not humility and the verse does not present it as such; it is what remains when every available line of defence has been closed off in advance by someone else's testimony. This is unusual for the Qur'an's judgment scenes, which often let the condemned speak — pleading to be sent back (32:12), cursing each other (7:38), or admitting their sins. The silence here is more effective than speech would be, because the preceding three verses have already established that there is nothing available for them to say. Their gods have disowned them, their leaders and followers have exhausted their quarrel, and the record of their own mockery has been read out. The sūrah now steps back from the Day of Judgment and returns to the present, to the words they were using while the argument was still open.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:43

> When Our clear revelations are recited to them, they say, “This is only a man who wishes to hinder you from what your forefathers used to worship.” They also say, “This ˹Quran˺ is no more than a fabricated lie.” And the disbelievers say of the truth when it has come to them, “This is nothing but pure magic.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Reactions to One Recitation**

The verse records three separate statements, each introduced by its own verb of speaking, and the accumulation is the point. First an attack on the man, then on the text, then on its effect. What connects them is that none is an argument. Each is a classification — he is a *mere* man with a motive, the book is a *mere* fabrication, the experience is *mere* magic — and each uses the Arabic restrictive construction *mā hādhā illā*, this is nothing but. The formula appears three times in one verse, which no Arabic listener would miss. A man reaching for the same dismissive frame three times in succession is not weighing a case; he is closing it repeatedly, and the repetition suggests the closing did not hold the first time. The verse also specifies the trigger: this happens when the revelations are recited *bayyināt*, as clear evidences. The clarity is what provokes the reaction.

**The First Charge Is About Ancestors, Not About God**

Their opening objection is worth reading carefully, because of what it does not mention. *Mā hādhā illā rajulun yurīdu an yaṣuddakum ʿammā kāna yaʿbudu ābāʾukum* — he only wants to turn you away from what your forefathers worshipped. No claim is made that the forefathers were right, that the old gods are real, or that the new message is false. The charge is disruption of inheritance. For a society organised around lineage, that was a serious accusation and did not need supplementing; a man who breaks with the fathers has broken with everything that makes a person legible. The verb they choose, *yaṣudd*, is the same one the arrogant leaders used when denying that they had blocked anyone from guidance (34:32). Here it is turned around and applied to the Prophet, who is accused of doing precisely what the leaders denied doing.

**"A Man" Again**

The sūrah opened its record of Meccan speech with the same indefinite noun — *a man who claims that when you have been utterly disintegrated you will be raised* (34:7) — and it recurs here in the same position with the same function. The strategy is consistent across thirty-six verses: strip the name, supply *rajul*, and the category of messenger has been denied without an argument being made against it. What has changed is the accusation attached. The earlier version made him ridiculous; this one makes him dangerous, with a motive and a target audience. That progression tracks something real about how opposition to a movement develops. A message that can still be laughed at is described as absurd. One that is gaining adherents is described as subversive.

**Fabrication and Magic Are Incompatible Charges**

The second and third statements cannot both be true, and putting them in one verse exposes the incoherence. *Ifk muftaran* is a fabricated lie — something composed, assembled, made up by a human being working at it. *Siḥr mubīn* is manifest sorcery — something with genuine power, operating on people beyond their ability to resist. A forgery is impotent; that is what makes it a forgery. Magic works; that is what makes it magic. The opponents needed both charges because each answers a different embarrassment. Fabrication explains where the text came from and denies it authority. Magic explains why it has such an effect on listeners, which fabrication cannot account for. Holding both at once is the mark of people working backwards from a conclusion, which the sūrah has already diagnosed in them when they offered the choice between liar and madman (34:8).

**Why Sorcery Was the Charge That Stuck**

Of the three, the accusation of magic is the most revealing about the Qur'an's reception. Sorcery was a recognised category in Arabia with a recognised social function, and to call something *siḥr* was not to call it ineffective. The word acknowledges power while denying legitimacy. What the Meccans were confronting was a text that changed people — that held listeners against their intention, that split households, that produced conversions among men who had come to mock. The Qur'an records more than one instance of opponents warning each other not to listen to it. Calling it magic was the only available explanation that accounted for the observed effects without conceding the source. That the charge is recorded here, at the end of a long argument in which every other objection has been answered, marks it as the last position available to them.

**The Phrase That Frames the Third Charge**

The final statement is introduced with a clause the others lack: *wa qāla alladhīna kafarū li-l-ḥaqqi lammā jāʾahum* — the disbelievers say of the truth, when it has come to them. Two elements are inserted before the quotation. The object is named as *the truth*, not as the Qur'an or the message, so the reader is told what is being dismissed before hearing the dismissal. And the timing is specified: *when it had come to them*, at the moment of arrival, with no interval for examination. Together these make the third statement the most damning of the three, because the verse has supplied the facts that the speakers omitted. They are calling a thing sorcery which is in fact the truth, and they are calling it that on arrival rather than after study. Al-Zukhruf records the identical reflex almost word for word, showing it as the standing reaction of the type rather than a considered local verdict. The next verse takes up the one thing that might have justified such confidence, and finds it absent.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:44

> ˹They say so even though˺ We had never given them any scriptures to study, nor did We ever send them a warner before you ˹O Prophet˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Objection Answered by Examining the Objector's Credentials**

The previous verse recorded three confident verdicts: he is a man with a motive, the book is a forgery, the experience is sorcery (34:43). This verse does not refute any of them directly. It asks on what basis they were issued. *Wa mā ātaynāhum min kutubin yadrusūnahā* — We gave them no books they were studying. *Wa mā arsalnā ilayhim qablaka min nadhīr* — nor did We send them any warner before you. The strategy is to examine the credentials of the court rather than the merits of the case, and it is a fair move in this instance because the verdicts were delivered with the confidence of expertise. A man who declares a text a forgery is implicitly claiming to know what a genuine revelation looks like. The verse asks where he acquired that knowledge.

**yadrusūnahā: The Verb for Sustained Study**

The word chosen for what they were not doing is precise. *Darasa* means to study a text repeatedly, to go over it until it is mastered; the same root gives *madrasah*, a school, and *dirāsah*, the systematic study of a subject. The verse does not merely say they had no scriptures — it says they had no scriptures **that they were studying**, in the continuous tense. This wording anticipates an obvious reply. The Meccans were not ignorant of the existence of earlier books; Jewish and Christian communities were known to them, their caravans passed through territories where those books were read, and individual Meccans had encountered their contents. What they lacked was a tradition of engagement with them. Having heard of a thing is not studying it, and the verse draws that line exactly where a critic's authority would have to rest.

**No Warner Before You**

The second clause concerns oral rather than written transmission. No messenger had been sent to this particular people before the Prophet, which is stated elsewhere in the Qur'an as the reason his coming mattered so much to them. This closes the other route by which a community might acquire a standard for judging revelation. They had neither a book to consult nor a living tradition of prophecy to compare against, and so possessed no basis at all — textual or inherited — for the confident classifications of the preceding verse. The Prophet is named as the first: *qablaka*, before you. That single word turns the verse from an accusation into a description of an opportunity. A people who had never received a warner were not being tested against a standard they had failed before; they were being addressed for the first time.

**The Same Argument Made Elsewhere**

The Qur'an uses this line of attack repeatedly and always against confident claims about unseen matters. Fāṭir asks: *"Or have We given them a Book—which they use as an authority?"* (35:40). Al-Ṣāffāt puts the challenge to those who assert things about the angels: *"Or do you have a compelling proof? Then bring your scripture, if what you say is true!"* (37:156–157). Al-Zukhruf and al-Aḥqāf press it in similar terms. In every case the demand is the same: produce the warrant. The consistency of this challenge across the Book indicates something about the Qur'an's own view of how religious claims should be settled. It does not ask its opponents to accept assertions on authority; it asks them to state the basis of theirs.

**The Sharpest Edge: Claims About the Unseen**

There is a particular target embedded in this verse that becomes visible when it is read against the rest of the sūrah. The Meccans had made a series of claims about unseen realities: that the Hour would never come to them (34:3), that they would never be punished (34:35), that their deities held standing with God (34:22). Those are not opinions about visible matters; they are assertions about the hidden order, and assertions of that kind can only be grounded in revelation. The verse points out that they had received none. Their confidence about the unseen therefore rests on nothing at all — which is precisely the charge they were levelling at the Prophet, who at least claimed a source. The sūrah has already used this reversal once, when it showed that even the jinn who served Solomon had no access to the unseen (34:14).

**What a People Without Either Actually Had**

It is fair to ask what the Meccans did possess, since the verse denies them two things without saying they had nothing. They had a sanctuary, a pilgrimage, an inherited cult, a rich oral poetry, and a detailed genealogical memory. None of these is a source of information about the unseen, and that is precisely the verse's point. A tradition can be ancient, dignified, socially binding and entirely silent on the questions in dispute. The Qur'an's challenge is not that their heritage was worthless but that it was the wrong kind of thing to settle the argument they were having. Ancestral practice tells you what your people did. It does not tell you whether the dead will be raised.

**A Verse That Removes an Excuse and Offers One at the Same Time**

Two readings of this verse pull in different directions, and both are legitimate. The grammar supports either, since the clause is attached to the preceding verse without a connective that would settle its function — it may explain why their verdicts were baseless, or it may explain why such a people needed a messenger at all. Read as a rebuke, it says they had no standing to judge and judged anyway, which aggravates the offence. Read as a mitigation, it describes a people who had genuinely been left without guidance for generations — which is why al-Yā-Sīn describes the Prophet's mission as being to a people *"whose forefathers were not warned, so they are heedless"*. The Qur'an holds both, and the tension is resolved by timing. Before the warner came, ignorance was their condition and not their fault. After he came, and after the revelations were recited as clear evidences, the ignorance became a choice. The verse sits exactly at that hinge, which is why it is placed immediately after their three dismissals rather than before them. The following verse turns from what they lacked to what earlier peoples possessed.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:45

> Those ˹destroyed˺ before them denied as well—and these ˹Meccans˺ have not attained even one-tenth of what We had given their predecessors. Yet ˹when˺ they denied My messengers, how severe was My response!

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Argument From Proportion**

The verse runs a comparison and draws a conclusion from the ratio. Earlier peoples denied their messengers and were destroyed. The present opponents have not reached a tenth of what those peoples possessed. Therefore the confidence of the present opponents is worth less than the confidence of people who had ten times the grounds for it and were still overtaken. This is a different move from the sūrah's earlier appeals to history, which pointed at Sheba's ruin as an example (34:19). Here the argument is quantitative. It does not merely say that a precedent exists; it says the precedent involved a stronger party, and reasons downward from there. Nothing about the argument requires the audience to accept any theological premise. It requires them only to compare two sets of resources, which is the kind of comparison a trading people made professionally.

**"Not a Tenth" — Of What, Exactly?**

The Arabic is *mā balaghū miʿshāra mā ātaynāhum*, they have not reached a tenth of what We gave them. The verse does not itemise what was given, and the commentators have filled it out in the obvious directions: power, wealth, numbers, length of life, the scale of what earlier civilisations built. The sūrah has supplied one of these examples itself, in detail, a few verses earlier — Sheba with its two irrigated plains, its chain of way-stations, its safe roads by day and night (34:15, 34:18). Against that, the Quraysh were a merchant community of moderate size in a barren valley, with no agriculture, no monuments, and wealth that depended on other people's goodwill along a trade route. The fraction is not flattery in reverse; it is roughly accurate, and the audience knew it. This is what gives the comparison its force. It is not telling them they are small in the eyes of God. It is telling them they are small by their own standards of measurement.

**Why a Fraction Rather Than a Comparison**

The verse could have said the earlier peoples were greater, or mightier, or longer-lived, and the sense would have carried. Choosing a numerical fraction instead does something a qualitative comparison cannot. It invites the hearer to perform a calculation, and the calculation has to be done with his own estimate of his own resources on one side of the ratio. A man told he is weaker than his predecessors can dispute the assessment. A man told he holds under a tenth of what they held has been handed an arithmetic problem, and working it honestly means first totalling what he actually has. This is the same technique the sūrah used when it measured divine knowledge by the smallest weight on a merchant's scale (34:3) and Solomon's wind by a month of caravan travel (34:12). Quantities drawn from the audience's working life are harder to argue with than adjectives.

**Denial Named as the Common Factor**

*Wa kadhdhaba alladhīna min qablihim* — those before them denied. The verb *kadhdhaba* means to declare something a lie, and it is the Qur'an's standard term for the response of every destroyed community. What the verse isolates is the one variable that stayed constant while everything else changed. The civilisations differed in wealth, technology, location and religion; their endings differed in method — flood, blast, stones, drowning (29:40). The denial was identical. This is the sūrah's second use of the classification argument in twelve verses, after the observation that every warned town's luxury class said the same thing (34:34). Both verses do the same work: they remove the opponent's sense of being a special case, which is the assumption every confident party operates on.

**The Exclamation That Ends the Verse**

*Fa-kayfa kāna nakīr* — how, then, was My rejection! The word *nakīr* comes from the root *n-k-r*, to disown, to refuse to recognise, to find something objectionable; the same root gives *munkar*, what is wrong or disavowed. So the divine response to their denial is named with a word that mirrors the offence: they refused to acknowledge, and they were refused in turn. The phrase can also be rendered *how was My reproach* or *how terrible was My punishment*, and the recurrence of the formula elsewhere shows it functioning as a fixed closing exclamation — at al-Ḥajj, at Fāṭir, and at al-Mulk, where it follows the reminder that earlier deniers were seized. Grammatically it is an interrogative used as an exclamation, and the effect is to invite the hearer to supply the answer from evidence he already has. The ruins were available for inspection along the roads these men travelled.

**Why the Question Is Left for the Audience to Answer**

The verse does not describe what happened to the earlier peoples. It asks how severe the response was and stops. This is the second time the sūrah has used an unfinished construction, after the *if only you could see* that broke off before naming what would be seen (34:31). The technique is consistent with the chapter's method throughout: the opponents are repeatedly handed the materials for a conclusion and left to assemble it. Told to call their gods and see what answers (34:22); told to produce the partners they attached (34:27); told to stand apart and think, two verses from now (34:46). A revelation that argued by assertion would have described the punishments in detail. This one relies on the audience's own knowledge of a landscape full of abandoned sites.

**The Logic Runs Backwards From Security**

There is a specific complacency this verse targets, and it was stated openly a few verses earlier: *we are far superior in wealth and children, and we will never be punished* (34:35). That sentence treats resources as insurance, and insurance is a claim about causation — that having more of something reduces exposure to a risk. The comparison here dismantles the assumption by running it backwards. If wealth protected people, the wealthiest communities in history would still be standing; they are not, and the men making the claim are poorer than the ones who fell. What remains of the argument after this verse is nothing at all, which is why the sūrah's next move abandons debate entirely and prescribes a procedure instead. Having shown that the opponents have no scripture, no prior warner, no precedent in their favour and less than a tenth of the resources of those who failed, the chapter turns to what a person should actually do.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:46

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I advise you to do ˹only˺ one thing: stand up for ˹the sake of˺ Allah—individually or in pairs—then reflect. Your fellow man is not insane. He is only a warner to you before ˹the coming of˺ a severe punishment.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Instruction, Not a Programme**

*Innamā aʿiẓukum bi-wāḥidah* — I advise you of one thing only. The restrictive particle *innamā* narrows the demand to a single item, and the narrowing is itself persuasive. A man told to change his religion, abandon his ancestors, redistribute his wealth and accept a new authority has been given a list he can refuse as a whole. A man told to do one thing has nothing to push against. What that one thing turns out to be is smaller still: not belief, not submission, not even attendance at a recitation. It is an act of mental attention performed in private. The Qur'an here reduces its entire demand, for the purposes of this argument, to the lowest possible threshold — and the reduction is a challenge, since a man who will not do even this has revealed that the obstacle was never evidence.

**Standing Up for God**

*An taqūmū lillāh* — that you stand for God. The verb is the ordinary word for rising to one's feet, and it carries a second sense in Arabic of taking up a matter, standing for a cause, the way English speaks of standing for something. Both senses work here and the tradition has read it both ways. Taken as physical rising, it means to get up out of one's seat in the gathering — to leave the circle of men who are confirming each other. Taken as commitment, it means to take up the question sincerely, for God's sake rather than for advantage. The qualifier *lillāh*, for God, is what distinguishes this from ordinary thinking. It specifies the motive: not to defend a position, not to find an answer that protects your standing, but to find out what is true. Most reasoning about religion in any society fails at exactly this point, and the verse names the failure by naming its cure.

**Singly or in Pairs — and Why Not in Groups**

*Mathnā wa furādā*, in twos and one by one. The exclusion is as important as the prescription: no crowds. The sūrah has been describing crowd behaviour for forty-five verses — a staged mockery for an audience (34:7), the luxury class speaking with one voice (34:34), leaders and followers whose positions were social before they were intellectual (34:31–33). A man in a group does not evaluate a claim; he monitors the room. The prescription breaks the group into the two smallest units in which thought is possible. Alone, because that is where a man has no audience to perform for. In pairs, because a single honest interlocutor can catch errors that solitude protects, and two people talking can reach conclusions neither would reach separately. What no gathering of forty can do is let any member change his mind without cost.

**Then Reflect**

*Thumma tatafakkarū* — then think. The particle *thumma* marks sequence with a gap, so the thinking comes after the separation, not during it; first leave the room, then reason. The verb *tafakkara* is reflexive in form, which in Arabic gives the sense of working something over in oneself, turning it about. The Qur'an uses it often and always for the same operation: examining available evidence rather than receiving a conclusion. What is remarkable is that the verse specifies no subject for the reflection. It does not say think about the Qur'an, or about God, or about death. The object is left open because the argument only requires that they think at all, under conditions where the outcome is not predetermined by the company.

**The Verdict the Thinking Will Produce**

Having prescribed the procedure, the verse states its expected result: *mā bi-ṣāḥibikum min jinnah* — there is no madness in your companion. The word *ṣāḥib* is the key term and it is chosen with care. It means a companion, an associate, someone you keep company with — a man known to you. The Meccans had called him *a man* twice in this sūrah, stripping the name to make him a specimen (34:7, 34:43). The reply restores the relationship: he is not some man, he is your associate, whom you have known for forty years. That is the evidence the reflection will consult, and it is why no reasoning is supplied. Anyone who had actually watched him could answer the question about madness from memory. Al-Aʿrāf makes the identical argument in the same words, asking whether they have not reflected, and stating that there is no madness in their companion (7:184).

**A Warner Before a Severe Punishment**

The verse closes with the Prophet's own description of his office: *in huwa illā nadhīrun lakum bayna yaday ʿadhābin shadīd* — he is only a warner to you before a severe punishment. The idiom *bayna yaday*, between the hands of, means in advance of, ahead of — the position of a scout or a herald. The reduction is deliberate and consistent with this sūrah's insistence that he was sent only as a bringer of good news and a warner (34:28). It is reported that he climbed al-Ṣafā, called out until the clans gathered, and asked whether they would believe him if he told them a cavalry was about to attack from behind the hill; they answered that they had never known him to lie. He then said: I am a warner to you before a severe punishment (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4770, from Ibn ʿAbbās). Their own testimony about his honesty, given before the claim was made, is exactly what this verse asks them to go away and remember.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:47

> Say, “If I had ever asked you for a reward, you could keep it. My reward is only from Allah. And He is a Witness over all things.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Answering the Charge of Motive**

Four verses earlier the opponents said he was a man who wanted to turn them from their fathers' religion (34:43), which is an accusation about motive. The previous verse answered the charge of madness; this one answers the charge of interest. *Qul mā saʾaltukum min ajrin fa-huwa lakum* — whatever payment I have asked you for, you may keep it. The sentence is constructed so that the hypothetical collapses on inspection. He had asked for nothing, so there is nothing to return, and the offer to hand it back is a way of saying that publicly without appearing to boast about it. This is a shrewder rhetorical form than a flat denial. A man who says "I never took anything from you" invites the reply that he wanted to; a man who tells his opponents to keep whatever he took has invited them to name the sum.

**The Test That Was Available to Them**

The argument works because it is checkable. Motive is normally the hardest thing to establish in a dispute, since it lives inside a person. Payment is not: it leaves traces in a small community where everybody knows what everybody has. After years of preaching, the Prophet's material position had not improved and was in most respects worse — his clan boycotted, his following drawn from people who could contribute nothing, his own trade neglected. The Meccans could have settled the question by producing a single transaction. The verse is confident enough to make the challenge explicit, and the silence that followed it is part of the record. This is why the Qur'an gives the argument to the Prophet as speech rather than stating it as doctrine. Its force depends on being said out loud, in the place where the facts were known.

**A Standing Formula Among the Messengers**

The disclaimer is not unique to this Prophet. The Qur'an puts it in the mouths of several: Noah tells his people he asks no payment and that his reward lies only with God, and the same words are repeated by Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot and Shuʿayb in a single sūrah, each delivering the identical line to his own community (26:109, 127, 145, 164, 180). The Prophet Muḥammad is instructed to say it at al-Anʿām, al-Furqān, Yā-Sīn, Ṣād and al-Shūrā as well as here. The repetition across so many figures marks it as a structural feature of the office rather than a personal virtue. A messenger who charges has entered a transaction, and a transaction gives his audience a way to evaluate him that has nothing to do with whether he is telling the truth. Removing the fee removes the distraction.

**Where the Payment Actually Comes From**

*In ajriya illā ʿalā Allāh* — my reward is only upon God. The preposition *ʿalā* is the one used for a debt owed, which is a striking choice; the Prophet describes his compensation as an obligation resting with God, not a hope or a gift. This does two things at once. It denies his audience any leverage, since a man whose income does not depend on his listeners cannot be influenced by withholding it. And it explains how he can continue under conditions that would have ended a commercial enterprise years earlier. The sūrah has been arguing since its thirty-sixth verse that provision comes from a source the beneficiary does not control (34:36, 34:39), and this verse applies that doctrine to the messenger's own situation. He is the first test case for the teaching he brought.

**The Witness Named at the End**

*Wa huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd* — He is a Witness over all things. *Shahīd* comes from the root *sh-h-d*, to be present at and to testify to; a *shāhid* is someone who was there and can speak to what happened. The name is precisely fitted to the claim it closes. A dispute about whether money changed hands is a dispute about facts, and facts require a witness. The Prophet has no way to prove a negative to men determined to disbelieve him, so he names a witness who was present at every transaction that never occurred. The Qur'an uses this name in similar evidentiary settings at al-Nisāʾ, al-Māʾidah, al-Ḥajj and al-Aḥzāb, and at Fuṣṣilat it is invoked against those who think their conduct is hidden.

**Why an Unpaid Messenger Is Harder to Dismiss**

The argument has a force that goes beyond clearing the Prophet's name, and it is worth drawing out. Every explanation his opponents had offered required a motive. Fabrication requires one — a forger forges for something. The charge in verse 43 that he wanted to turn them from their fathers' worship requires one, since a man does not undertake years of public hostility for no return. Even the accusation of sorcery, which is about method rather than motive, assumes a practitioner working for a purpose. Remove the payment and every one of these explanations loses its engine. What remains is a man doing something costly, sustained over years, with no visible return in a society where returns were the point of public action — and the only remaining explanation is the one his opponents refused to consider.

**The Shape of the Closing Sequence**

This verse is the second in a run that will carry the sūrah to its end, each opening with *qul* and each answering one specific charge with one short sentence. Madness is answered by a procedure for thinking (34:46). Greed is answered by an offer to return what was never taken. What follows will address the source of the message and the standing of the messenger himself. Read together, the sequence is a defence brief delivered in fragments, and its most notable feature is what it declines to include: no miracle is offered, no threat is repeated, no claim is made about the speaker's virtue beyond the facts his audience could verify. Everything rests on evidence already in their possession, which is the same method the chapter used when it told them to look at the sky and the ground around them (34:9).

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:48

> Say, “Surely my Lord hurls the truth ˹against falsehood˺. ˹He is˺ the Knower of all unseen.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb of Force, Not of Persuasion**

*Inna rabbī yaqdhifu bi-l-ḥaqq* — my Lord hurls the truth. The verb *qadhafa* is physical and violent: to throw something with force, to fling, to cast a projectile. Arabic uses it for hurling a stone, and in a different register for casting an accusation at someone's reputation. Nothing in it suggests gentle communication. This is a surprising word to describe revelation, which the sūrah has elsewhere presented as recitation, warning and good news. The choice indicates what kind of event the verse is describing. Truth here is not being offered for consideration; it is being launched at something, and the unstated target is supplied by the Qur'an's use of the same image elsewhere. The sudden shift in register is also a response to the argument's stalemate. Persuasion has been tried for forty-seven verses. This verse describes what happens when the matter passes out of the realm of argument.

**The Image Completed in Another Sūrah**

Al-Anbiyāʾ supplies the full picture with the same verb: *"In fact, We hurl the truth against falsehood, leaving it crushed, and it quickly vanishes"* (21:18). That verse gives the projectile a target and an outcome — falsehood does not withstand the impact, and its collapse is immediate. Read against it, the compressed statement in Sabaʾ is an abbreviation of a known image rather than a bare assertion. Al-Isrāʾ adds the declaration that goes with the impact: *"The truth has come and falsehood has vanished. Indeed, falsehood is bound to vanish"* (17:81). The three passages together describe a single doctrine about the relation between the two. Falsehood is not defeated by being out-argued; it is described as structurally unable to survive contact with truth, which means its persistence is always a function of the truth's absence rather than of its own strength.

**A Second Reading: What Is Cast Into Hearts**

The verb admits another application, and the commentators noticed it early. To hurl the truth may describe what God does to falsehood in the open, or what He does inside a person — casting the truth into a heart, where it lands without being sought and changes the one it strikes. Both senses are available in the Arabic and neither displaces the other. The second reading has considerable support from experience and from the Qur'an's accounts of sudden conversions, in which a man comes to mock a recitation and leaves having believed. Nothing in such an event resembles the conclusion of an argument; it resembles impact. This reading also explains why the verse follows the instruction to go away and reflect (34:46). A person who separates himself from the crowd and thinks honestly has put himself in the path of something.

**Knower of the Unseen, Named Here for a Reason**

The verse closes with *ʿallām al-ghuyūb*, the Knower of all hidden things — an intensive form, not merely one who knows the unseen but one whose knowledge of it is comprehensive. The sūrah has used a version of this title once before, in the oath sworn about the Hour, where it was *ʿālim al-ghayb* (34:3). Its appearance here is precisely placed. The whole dispute recorded in this chapter has been about hidden matters: whether a reckoning is coming, whether the message has a divine source, whether the beings petitioned at the sanctuary have standing. Every one of those questions is settled by information that neither party in Mecca possessed. Naming the One who does possess it, immediately after describing truth as a thrown object, identifies who is doing the throwing and why the projectile does not miss.

**A Third Sense of the Verb, Which the Sūrah Will Use**

There is a detail in the Arabic that becomes visible only five verses later, and it disappears entirely in translation. The same verb, *qadhafa*, returns at the end of this chapter to describe what the deniers did — casting or hurling at the unseen from a distant place (34:53). So the sūrah uses one word for two opposite operations. God hurls the truth, and it lands; they hurl guesses at the unseen, and nothing lands. The symmetry is deliberate and it defines the difference between the two parties in a single shared verb. Both are throwing. One is throwing something real at a target that cannot survive it; the other is throwing speculation at a target it cannot see. The Arabic reader who reaches verse 53 hears this verse again, and the echo does the work of an argument without stating one.

**Why This Follows the Disclaimer of Payment**

The sequence of these closing verses has a logic. The Prophet has just said his reward is with God and that God witnesses everything (34:47), which locates his support entirely outside his audience's control. This verse extends the same point to the message. Notice that the speaker again says *my Lord* rather than naming God in the third person, as he did when answering the boast about wealth (34:36) and again when promising replacement for what is spent (34:39). The possessive runs through all of these closing statements and keeps the argument personal: a man with no payment, no patron and no leverage, asserting that the source he is connected to does the throwing. If the truth is thrown by a Lord who knows every hidden thing, then its progress does not depend on the persuasion of any particular gathering, and the opposition of a city is not a defeat. For a community under pressure in Mecca, that was the practically important claim. The outcome of the argument had ceased to look promising by any ordinary measure, and this verse tells them the measure is wrong. The next verse states the result of the throw as an accomplished fact.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:49

> Say, “The truth has come, and falsehood will vanish, never to return.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Announcement, Not a Prediction**

*Jāʾa al-ḥaqq* — the truth has come. The verb is in the perfect tense, which in Arabic reports a completed event, and the choice of tense is the whole force of the opening clause. Nothing here is promised for later. Something has already arrived, and the sentence is a notification of a fact on the ground rather than a forecast of one. This matters because of what precedes it. The previous verse described truth being hurled like a projectile (34:48); this one reports the landing. Read in sequence, the two verses are the throw and the impact, and the tense marks the moment between them as already past. For an audience whose standard objection was that the promised events kept failing to arrive (34:29), a statement in the completed tense makes a claim they could check: whatever else is still pending, this has happened.

**Two Verbs That Describe What Falsehood Cannot Do**

The second clause is the difficult one and the more interesting: *wa mā yubdiʾu al-bāṭilu wa mā yuʿīd* — falsehood neither originates nor restores. The two verbs are technical terms in the Qur'an's vocabulary of divine action. *Abdaʾa* is to begin something, to bring it into existence for the first time; *aʿāda* is to bring it back, to restore or repeat. Together they name the two halves of creation and resurrection, and the Qur'an pairs them repeatedly when describing what only God does — He originates the creation, then repeats it. Applying the pair to falsehood, in the negative, is therefore a precise theological claim rather than a general insult. Whatever falsehood is, it does neither of the two things that define a real power.

**Why This Refers Back to the Idols**

The clause looks abstract until it is connected to the courtroom scene a few verses earlier, where the objects of worship were questioned and the whole apparatus of intermediary religion collapsed (34:40–42). Those beings were petitioned for outcomes: rain, victory, children, safety. The two verbs name exactly what would be required to deliver any of it. A power that cannot originate cannot produce anything new, and a power that cannot restore cannot bring back what is lost. Al-Furqān makes the identical charge against them in full: they have taken gods who *"create nothing but are themselves created"*, who hold no power over harm or benefit to themselves, and *"no control over death, life, or resurrection"* (25:3). The sentence in Sabaʾ is that verse compressed into four words.

**A Second Reading: Falsehood Has No Future**

The clause has been read another way, and the alternative is worth holding alongside the first. Taken as a statement about falsehood's own career rather than its productive capacity, it means that falsehood begins nothing and revives nothing — that once dispersed, it does not come back. The translation here takes this line with *never to return*. On this reading the sentence is about permanence: the arrival of truth is not one turn in a cycle that will reverse, and what has been displaced will not re-establish itself. Al-Isrāʾ supports this sense with the fullest version of the statement: *"The truth has come and falsehood has vanished. Indeed, falsehood is bound to vanish"* (17:81). Neither reading excludes the other. A thing that cannot originate anything is also a thing that cannot re-originate itself.

**The Word bāṭil**

*Bāṭil* is usually translated falsehood, but its root sense is closer to void, null, without effect. In Arabic legal usage a contract that is *bāṭil* is not a contract that says something untrue; it is one that has no force, that fails to produce the consequences it purports to produce. This is the sense that makes the verse's two verbs fit. Falsehood is not being described as a rival claim that happens to be incorrect but as a thing with no operative power — an empty form. Luqmān states the contrast in exactly this vocabulary: *"That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth, and whatever they invoke besides Him is false"* (31:30). The pairing there is between *al-ḥaqq*, what is real and established, and *al-bāṭil*, what is void. The dispute running through this whole sūrah has been between those two categories rather than between two religions.

**Why the Coming Is Announced Before the Vanishing Is Complete**

There is a difficulty a careful reader will notice. The truth has come, in the completed tense — and yet at the moment this verse was recited, the Meccan sanctuary was still full of idols, the opposition still held the city, and the community around the Prophet was still small and harassed. How can the arrival be reported as finished while the displacement it causes is visibly still under way? The answer is contained in the second clause. Falsehood is not described as being defeated in a contest whose result is pending; it is described as having no capacity to originate or restore. A thing with no productive power does not need to be defeated, only exposed, and exposure is what the arrival accomplishes. On that account the outcome is settled at the moment of arrival regardless of how long the visible consequences take to work through. The sūrah has made a similar claim about the Day itself, calling it an appointment already fixed and unmovable while its date remains unknown (34:30).

**The Shortest Verse in the Chapter, and Its Placement**

This is the briefest statement in Sabaʾ, and its brevity is functional. It arrives after forty-eight verses of argument — the record, the mockery, the kings, the ruined kingdom, the quarrel of leaders and followers, the boast of wealth, the interrogation of the angels, the demand that they stand apart and think. Everything has been said. What remains is a declaration, and a declaration that argued its own case would undercut itself. The Prophet is reported to have recited this verse while entering Mecca at the conquest, striking the idols around the Kaʿbah with a stick as they fell, and the scene fits the verse exactly: no argument, no explanation, a fact announced while it is visibly occurring. Three more *say* statements follow, and then the sūrah turns to the moment when the void becomes undeniable to those who invested in it.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:50

> Say, “If I am astray, the loss is only mine. And if I am guided, it is ˹only˺ because of what my Lord reveals to me. He is indeed All-Hearing, Ever Near.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Concession No Opponent Expected**

The Prophet is instructed to entertain, out loud, the possibility that he is wrong: *in ḍalaltu fa-innamā aḍillu ʿalā nafsī* — if I have gone astray, I go astray only against myself. Nothing in the preceding forty-nine verses prepares for this. The chapter has argued, challenged, threatened and testified, and it now hands its opponents a sentence they could not have extracted by pressure. The concession is not a weakening of the claim. It is a statement about where the risk lies, and it functions as the final answer to the accusation of motive that the sūrah has been dismantling since verse 43. A man who acknowledges that his error would cost him everything and gain him nothing has removed the last reason to suspect him of an angle.

**The Asymmetry Between the Two Halves**

The sentence has two conditional branches and they are not built the same way. If he is astray, the loss falls on himself — a closed circuit, no one else implicated. If he is guided, the credit goes elsewhere: *fa-bimā yūḥī ilayya rabbī*, it is by what my Lord reveals to me. He takes full ownership of the downside and none of the upside. This asymmetry is the verse's argument, and it is the opposite of how a man defending a position normally speaks. The ordinary pattern claims credit for what is right and distributes blame for what is wrong. Reversing it is either an extraordinary rhetorical calculation or an accurate description of how the speaker understands his own situation, and the sūrah has spent the preceding verses removing the grounds for the first explanation.

**Guidance Described as Received, Not Achieved**

The second branch contains a claim about the nature of guidance that the Qur'an makes repeatedly. It does not say "if I am guided, it is because I reasoned well" or "because I sought sincerely." It says the guidance consists in what is revealed to him — *yūḥī*, in the present tense, an ongoing supply rather than a single past delivery. This connects to the sūrah's treatment of knowledge at the other end of the chapter, where those who recognised the revelation as truth were described as people who had been **given** knowledge rather than people who had acquired it (34:6). The same passive stance appears in both places. On this account the difference between a guided man and a straying one is not primarily a difference in effort, which removes the last trace of self-congratulation from the Prophet's position.

**Why the Admission Is Safe to Make**

There is a hard-headed logic under the apparent vulnerability of this verse, and the Meccan audience would have felt it. Their charge was that he had gone astray and was dragging others with him. The first half of the sentence addresses only the first part of that and concedes it hypothetically; but the concession has a consequence they may not have wanted. If his error would harm only himself, then their opposition is not a public service, and the urgency of their campaign against him loses its justification. The Qur'an states the underlying principle in general terms elsewhere: *"Whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good. And whoever chooses to stray, it is only to their own loss"* (17:15). The sūrah applied it to both parties earlier, when neither side was to be questioned about the other's conduct (34:25). Here the Prophet applies it to himself first.

**Two Names, and One of Them Occurs Only Here**

The closing pair is *Samīʿun Qarīb* — All-Hearing, Ever Near. The first name is common in the Qur'an. The second, *Qarīb*, is used of God in several places, but searching this translation of the whole text shows that the two are joined in this combination at this verse alone. The pairing is exact for what precedes it. A man who has just said that his guidance depends entirely on what his Lord reveals is a man whose whole position rests on a line of communication, and the two names describe the two properties that line must have: someone listening, and someone close enough for the listening to matter. Hearing without nearness would be surveillance. Nearness without hearing would be presence without response.

**A Sentence That Models the Procedure It Recommends**

Four verses earlier the opponents were told to separate from the crowd and think honestly about the question in front of them (34:46). This verse shows what honest thinking sounds like when the speaker applies it to himself. He states both branches of the question, assigns the consequences of each without flinching, and declines to claim anything he cannot support. Nothing in the sentence is defensive. A man who can hold his own possible error in view while continuing to act is demonstrating exactly the disposition he asked his opponents to adopt, and the demonstration is more persuasive than the instruction was. This is also why the verse belongs in the closing sequence rather than earlier. It answers the last remaining version of the charge of madness: a man whose reason had been taken by the jinn could not construct a conditional about his own condition.

**What Nearness Means in the Qur'an's Vocabulary**

Al-Baqarah gives the fullest statement of this name's practical content, answering a question the Companions had asked: *"When My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me: I am truly near. I respond to one's prayer when they call upon Me"* (2:186). The nearness there is defined by responsiveness rather than by location, and it is offered as the reason petition works at all. Placing the same name at the end of this verse ties the Prophet's situation to every listener's. He has just described himself as a man entirely dependent on a channel he does not control — and the name that closes the sentence is the one the Qur'an elsewhere attaches to ordinary people's prayers. The sūrah now leaves the Prophet's speech and turns, for its last four verses, to the moment when the party that ignored all this discovers what it cost.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:51

> If only you could see when they will be horrified with no escape ˹on Judgment Day˺! And they will be seized from a nearby place.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Left Deliberately Unfinished**

*Wa law tarā idh fazīʿū* — and if you could see when they are terrified. As in the earlier verse that opened the same way (34:31), the conditional has no stated consequence. Arabic allows the apodosis to be omitted when the speaker judges that any completion would diminish the effect, and the Qur'an uses the device precisely at moments it wants the hearer to supply. You would see something, and what you would then feel is not named. The technique is more effective here than in its first appearance because the sūrah is now in its closing movement and the reader has been given all the materials. Note also the tense: *fazīʿū* is a perfect verb describing a future event, which is the Qur'an's habitual way of reporting the Day as something already accomplished rather than pending.

**Terror Without Exit**

*Fa-lā fawta* — and there is no escaping. The noun *fawt* means the slipping away of something, a missing or a passing beyond reach; the same root gives the verb used when an opportunity is lost. So the phrase does not merely say they will not escape. It says the category of escape does not apply — nothing can slip past, nothing gets away. The sūrah has been building toward this for fifty verses by closing exits one at a time: no hiding from the record that catches an atom's weight (34:3), no intercessor with standing (34:23), no protection in wealth (34:37), no help from one party to another (34:42). This verse states the cumulative result in three words. Terror in ordinary life is bearable partly because the mind is calculating a way out even while afraid. This is terror with the calculating removed.

**Seized From a Nearby Place**

*Wa ukhidhū min makānin qarīb* — and they are taken from a close place. The phrase is short and has attracted a range of readings, all of which sharpen the same point. Some take *a close place* as the grave, since the dead are seized directly from where they lie the moment they are raised, with no interval and no journey. Others read it as the earth itself, close beneath them. Others hear it as an idiom for immediacy: taken from right where they are, without pursuit, without needing to be found. The readings converge because they all describe the collapse of distance. The men in question had spent their lives operating at a comfortable remove from the thing they denied, and the seizure happens at arm's length.

**The Word "Near" Used Against Them**

There is a deliberate cruelty in the vocabulary that a reader of the Arabic cannot miss. The previous verse ended by naming God *Qarīb*, Ever Near, as the reassurance underwriting the Prophet's dependence on revelation (34:50). This verse uses the same root for the place from which the deniers are seized: *qarīb*, near. One word, two applications. Nearness is the ground of hope for the one who calls and the ground of terror for the one who does not, and the sūrah sets the two uses a single verse apart so that the contrast cannot be missed. The same proximity that makes a prayer answerable makes an arrest instantaneous. Nothing in the divine attribute has changed between the two verses; only the relationship of the person to it.

**Why Terror Rather Than Punishment**

The verse describes a psychological state and not a physical one. No fire is mentioned, no shackle, no sentence — only fright, the absence of escape, and the seizure. This is characteristic of how the Qur'an handles the opening moment of the Day, and it is more effective than a description of torment would be. Punishment can be imagined at a distance and discounted; the sensation of having no exit cannot be discounted by anyone who has ever felt a version of it. The sūrah has also earned this moment by recording, at length, the confidence these people expressed while the question was still open: the Hour will never come to us (34:3), we will never be punished (34:35). Terror is the exact reversal of a settled certainty, which is why it is the emotion named.

**Who Is Being Addressed by "If Only You Could See"**

The verb *tarā* is singular, so the address is to one person — the Prophet — rather than to the assembly. This detail changes the verse's function. It is not a warning shouted at the deniers about what awaits them; it is a scene shown privately to a man who has spent the chapter being mocked, accused of fabrication and called insane. What he is offered is not revenge but information: the argument he has been losing in public has an ending, and the ending is not in doubt. The Qur'an does this for him repeatedly in the Meccan period, and the sūrah has done it once already with the same construction at the quarrel of leaders and followers (34:31). Both scenes are given to the Prophet rather than to his opponents, which is worth noticing. Those who would most benefit from seeing the Day are precisely the ones who cannot be shown it, because being shown it would end the test the sūrah described in its twenty-first verse.

**The Structure of the Chapter's Closing Scene**

This verse opens the last movement of Sabaʾ, four verses that run without a break to the end of the chapter. The arrangement is chronological: seizure here, the belated declaration of faith next, the reason it cannot be accepted after that, and the barrier that closes the account. What the sequence does is answer, one final time, the question the opponents put in verse 29 — when will this promise come to pass? They wanted a date and were told only that the appointment was fixed and unmovable (34:30). Now the chapter describes the appointment being kept, in the present tense, with the audience still holding the delay they thought was safety. The next verse gives them their first and last words at the hearing.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:52

> They will ˹then˺ cry, “We do ˹now˺ believe in it ˹all˺.” But how could they ˹possibly˺ attain faith from a place so far-off ˹from the world˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words That Come Too Late**

*Wa qālū āmannā bih* — and they said, we believe in it. The declaration is the shortest possible statement of faith and it is grammatically flawless; nothing in the wording is defective. What has failed is the timing. The Qur'an does not accuse them of insincerity here, and the verse's reply does not suggest they are lying. They mean it. That is what makes the scene disturbing rather than merely just: belief has finally arrived, genuine and complete, at the one moment when it can accomplish nothing. The sūrah has recorded these same voices confidently denying that the Hour would reach them (34:3), mocking the claim of resurrection to a gathered crowd (34:7), and announcing that they would never believe in this Qur'an (34:31). The last of those was a *lan* — never. The never has expired.

**Belief That Is No Longer a Choice**

The reason the declaration fails is not arbitrary and the Qur'an supplies it consistently. Faith, in the Book's understanding, is trust extended to what has not been seen — which is why the believers are described at the opening of al-Baqarah as those who believe in the unseen. Once the unseen becomes visible, the act being performed is no longer faith but acknowledgement, and acknowledgement of a fact standing in front of you is not a moral achievement. The whole arrangement described earlier in this sūrah depended on that distinction: the world exists as a place where belief and doubt can become visible precisely because the outcome is not yet on display (34:21). A test that continues after the answers are published is not a test. What these speakers are offering is the right answer read off the board.

**"From a Place Far Off"**

*Wa annā lahum al-tanāwushu min makānin baʿīd* — how can they attain it from a distant place? The noun *tanāwush* means reaching for something, grasping at it, taking hold — a word with the physical sense of stretching out a hand. The image is of people reaching back toward something now out of range. The distance in question is not spatial. Faith was available in the life they have just left, at close quarters, cheaply; it is now being grasped at from the far side of death, and the reaching cannot span the gap. The rhetorical question *annā lahum*, how can they, is the Qur'an's standard form for an impossibility rather than a difficulty. Nothing is being refused to them out of severity. The thing they want is located somewhere they no longer are.

**The Distance That Reverses the Previous Verse**

One verse earlier they were seized *from a nearby place* (34:51); now they reach for faith *from a distant place*. The two phrases are set one line apart, using the paired opposites *qarīb* and *baʿīd*, and the reversal is the sūrah's most economical statement of their situation. Judgment reached them instantly, across no distance at all. The remedy is at a distance they cannot cross. This is exactly the inversion of their position during life, when the punishment seemed impossibly remote — they demanded a date for it and treated the absence of one as proof (34:29) — while repentance was available at any hour. The relative positions of the two have swapped, and nothing they do now affects either.

**Other Scenes of the Same Late Petition**

The Qur'an returns to this moment often and the request is always refused. Al-Sajdah shows the guilty with their heads bowed: *"Our Lord! We have now seen and heard, so send us back and we will do good. We truly have sure faith ˹now˺!"* (32:12). Ghāfir has them admitting their sins in the Fire. Al-Munāfiqūn records the wish of the man who dies holding his wealth: *"My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous"* (63:10). In every case the petition is well-formed, sincere, and unavailing. The consistency across these passages establishes it as a rule rather than a judgment on particular individuals. What all the petitioners have in common is that they are asking for a return to conditions under which their new conviction would have counted.

**What "It" Refers To**

The object of their belated belief is left as a bare pronoun — *āmannā bih*, we believe in it — and the Arabic does not say what *it* is. The candidates are all present in the sūrah. One is the truth, which the preceding verses described as thrown and landing (34:48–49). Another is the Qur'an, which they had sworn never to believe in (34:31). A third is the Hour itself, denied in the chapter's third verse and mocked in its seventh. A fourth is the Prophet, whom they called a man with a motive. Nothing forces a choice, and the ambiguity is probably the point: at that moment every one of these has become undeniable simultaneously, and a single pronoun covers the whole collapse. A man who has just been seized from the grave does not distinguish between the messenger, the message and the event. They arrive as one fact.

**What the Verse Asks of a Living Reader**

The scene is placed in the sūrah's final movement for an obvious reason, and it is aimed at people for whom the door is still open. Everything these speakers now believe was available to them at no cost the previous week, and the only difference between them and the sūrah's audience is a date. This is why the chapter ends here rather than with the arguments, the histories or the demolition of the idols. An argument can be answered and a history can be discounted; a scene of people who agree with you too late cannot be argued with at all. The following verse explains why the refusal is not merely a matter of timing but a consequence of how they had treated the unseen all along.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:53

> while they had already rejected it before, guessing blindly from a place ˹equally˺ far-away ˹from the Hereafter˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reason the Late Declaration Fails**

The previous verse showed them reaching for faith across a distance they could not cross. This one explains why the distance exists, and the explanation is not about the geography of the next world. *Wa qad kafarū bihi min qabl* — they had already rejected it before. The word *qabl*, before, is doing the work. Their present position is not an arbitrary penalty imposed after the fact; it is the continuation of a stance they took while the matter was open. A man who spends a lifetime declaring a thing false has, by the time it appears in front of him, already placed himself at the maximum distance from it. The verse presents the two moments as one trajectory rather than as a crime followed by a sentence.

**The Verb That Ties This Verse to the Prophet's Statement**

The phrase translated *guessing blindly* is *wa yaqdhifūna bi-l-ghaybi*, and the verb is the surprise. *Qadhafa* is the same word used five verses earlier when the Prophet was told to say that his Lord *hurls the truth* (34:48). One verb, two subjects, opposite results. God hurls the truth and it lands on falsehood and crushes it. They hurl at the unseen, and the throw goes nowhere. The Arabic also carries a second sense of *qadhafa* that English cannot hold in the same word: to cast an accusation at someone, to sling a charge. That sense is live here, since what they threw at the unseen was precisely accusation — the Qur'an is a fabrication, the messenger is mad, the resurrection is absurd. Both readings describe the same act. Something was thrown, and the thrower could not see the target.

**Throwing at What You Cannot See**

The image deserves to be taken literally for a moment, because it is exact. A man who hurls a stone into the dark may hit something or may not, but either way he did not aim, and his confidence about where it landed is worthless. This is the verse's charge against every claim the opponents made about hidden matters. They asserted that the Hour would not come (34:3), that they would not be punished (34:35), that the message was sorcery (34:43) — all statements about a region they had no access to. The sūrah demonstrated that lack of access twice: once by showing that even the jinn who served Solomon could not detect a death in front of them (34:14), and again by pointing out that this people had received neither scripture nor prior warner (34:44). They had no instrument and threw anyway.

**"From a Place Far Away" — Repeated Deliberately**

The phrase *min makānin baʿīd* closes this verse exactly as it closed the previous one, and the repetition is the passage's sharpest stroke. In verse 52 the distance was where they now stand, reaching uselessly for faith. Here the same distance describes where they stood when they were making their confident pronouncements. The sūrah is saying that their position has not changed. They were always far from the matter they spoke about; the only difference is that the remoteness was invisible to them then and is unbearable now. A man shouting judgments about something he cannot see is at exactly the same distance from it as a man reaching for something he cannot touch.

**Why Speculation Is Treated as a Moral Failure**

It would be possible to read the deniers' behaviour as an intellectual error — they guessed and guessed wrong. The verse frames it as something worse, and the framing is consistent with the whole sūrah. Guessing about the unseen is not a neutral activity when guidance about it has been delivered and is available for examination. These men had a warner in front of them whose honesty they had publicly attested, reciting a text they could evaluate, and they preferred conjecture. The chapter's alternative was stated plainly seven verses earlier: stand up, singly or in pairs, and think (34:46). That instruction was an offer of the only method by which a person without access to the unseen can responsibly proceed. Throwing in the dark is what remains when the offer is declined.

**The Pronoun Left Open Here Too**

As in the previous verse, the object of their earlier rejection is a bare pronoun: they had rejected *it* before. Commentators have read the referent variously as the truth discussed in verses 48 and 49, as the Qur'an, as the Prophet himself, or as God. The range is not a weakness in the text. Each candidate produces a sentence that is true of these speakers, and the Arabic construction allows a reader to hear more than one at once. There is also a small point of symmetry worth registering: verse 52 had them believing in *it* too late, and this verse has them rejecting *it* too early, with the same unspecified pronoun in both. Whatever the object was, their relation to it was wrong at both ends — dismissed when it could have been examined, accepted when acceptance had stopped meaning anything.

**Two Ways of Being Certain**

The sūrah closes its argument by placing two kinds of confidence side by side, and the contrast has been running since the third verse. The Prophet was made to swear an oath that the Hour would come, and the oath rested on a stated source: revelation from the Knower of the unseen (34:3). The deniers matched his certainty with their own and rested it on nothing — no book, no messenger, no instrument, nothing but the fact that the thing in question could not be seen. Both parties were equally sure. Only one could say where the certainty came from. The distinction survives the chapter's setting entirely, since confidence about hidden things is not a habit peculiar to seventh-century Mecca. One verse remains, and it describes what closes behind people who spent a life throwing in the dark.

## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:54

> They will be sealed off from whatever they desire, as was done to their counterparts before. Indeed, they were ˹all˺ in alarming doubt.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Barrier, Not a Refusal**

*Wa ḥīla baynahum wa bayna mā yashtahūn* — a separation was made between them and what they desire. The verb *ḥāla* means to come between, to intervene, to block a passage; the related noun *ḥāʾil* is a partition or screen. What the verse describes is therefore not a petition denied but a structure put in place. A refusal implies someone still listening and deciding; a barrier implies the matter has passed out of the realm of decision entirely. The passive construction reinforces this, since no agent is named — the separation simply exists between them and the object. The sūrah has spent its last three verses removing possibilities in sequence: no escape (34:51), no faith available at that distance (34:52), no basis for the confidence that brought them there (34:53). This verse makes the closure architectural.

**What Exactly They Want**

The verse does not specify the object of the desire, and the readings offered for it all fit the scene. Most obviously it is a return to the world in order to repent, which is the request the Qur'an records at al-Sajdah: *"send us back and we will do good"* (32:12). It may be repentance itself, wanted directly rather than as something to be enacted elsewhere. It may be escape from the punishment. Or it may be the pleasures they had actually spent their lives desiring — the wealth and sons they had boasted about (34:35), now cut off permanently from people who no longer have any use for them. The verb *ishtahā*, to crave or covet, supports the last reading as much as the first. Its ordinary range in Arabic is appetite rather than repentance, which gives the sentence a second and colder meaning: the barrier stands between them and everything they ever wanted.

**"As Was Done to Their Counterparts Before"**

*Kamā fuʿila bi-ashyāʿihim min qabl* — as was done with their like before. *Shīʿah* means a party, a faction, a group of the same kind; *ashyāʿ* is its plural. The word classifies rather than names, and the classification is the sūrah's final use of a device it has employed throughout. Every warned town's luxury class said the same thing (34:34). Those before them denied and had ten times their resources (34:45). Now their end is the same as the end of their type. What this removes is the last trace of exceptionalism — the assumption, stated openly in verse 35 and implied everywhere else, that these particular people would be the case that came out differently. The chapter has been dismantling that assumption since its first quotation of their speech, and it closes by filing them with the others.

**The Doubt That Was There All Along**

*Innahum kānū fī shakkin murīb* — they were in doubt, disquieting doubt. The construction is emphatic in a way English struggles to reproduce, because *murīb* comes from a second root for doubt, *rayb*, so the phrase stacks two doubt-words together: doubt that is itself doubt-inducing, unsettled and unsettling. What makes this the right note to end on is that it contradicts the public posture the sūrah has recorded for fifty-four verses. These men spoke with total confidence — the Hour will never come to us, we will never be punished, we will never believe in this Qur'an. The final verse says that underneath the certainty there was doubt, and that the doubt was never resolved, only managed. Loud assertion, on this diagnosis, was the management technique rather than the conviction.

**Doubt Named as the Condition, Not Disbelief**

It is worth registering what the chapter does not say here. It could have ended by calling them deniers, idolaters, or liars, and it has used all three descriptions earlier. It ends instead with *doubt* — the mildest available term, and the one the sūrah identified in the middle of the chapter as the line of division: the arrangement exists so that those who believe in the Hereafter may be distinguished from those *in doubt about it* (34:21). The two verses frame the whole book of the chapter. Doubt is where the separation was drawn at the start and doubt is the verdict at the end, which means the sūrah has been consistent about what it was actually arguing against. Not a rival theology. An unresolved uncertainty that was never examined, because examining it was uncomfortable and mocking it was easy.

**Doubt Described Elsewhere in the Same Words**

The expression *shakk murīb* is not unique to this verse, and tracking it shows what kind of condition the Qur'an means by it. Hūd uses it twice of communities that would not accept their prophets' message, and Ibrāhīm applies it to peoples who told their messengers they were in disquieting doubt about what they were called to. Fuṣṣilat and al-Shūrā both use it of those who dispute the Book. In every case the phrase describes not a temporary uncertainty on the way to a conclusion but a settled refusal to reach one — doubt maintained as a position, because reaching a conclusion would require acting on it. That is the condition the sūrah ends on, and it explains why the chapter's central prescription was an instruction to stand apart and think rather than an instruction to believe.

**A Chapter That Began With Praise and Ends With a Partition**

Sabaʾ opened by saying that all praise belongs to God in this world and the next, and that everything in the heavens and on the earth is His (34:1). It closes with a group of people sealed away from everything they wanted. The two ends of the chapter are connected by the argument that runs between them: if ownership is total, then nothing a person holds is held independently, and the only question is whether he acknowledged the terms. Sheba was given two orchards and a safe road and asked for one thing in return (34:15). David was given iron and told to make protection and work well (34:11). Solomon's household was told to work gratefully (34:13). The people in this final verse were given a warner, an argument, a history, and an instruction to stand apart and think (34:46), and the barrier at the end stands exactly where they stopped.
