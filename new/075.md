# Sūrah al-Qiyāmah (Chapter 75) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary


## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:1

> I do swear by the Day of Judgment!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Falls on the Day Itself**

The sūrah opens with an oath sworn by the resurrection, and it does so before a single line of evidence for the resurrection has been given. God swears by the Day of Judgment; the forty verses that follow unpack what that oath has already declared. Elsewhere the Qur'an swears by created things that a hearer can look at — *“By the sun and its brightness”* (91:1), *“By the night when it covers”* (92:1), *“By the fig and the olive ˹of Jerusalem˺”* (95:1). Those oaths ask the listener to reason from something present to something absent. Here the thing sworn by is absent as well, and the boldness of the verse lies exactly there: a future event is treated as though it were already an object, as settled as the sun overhead. The Makkan audience denied that any such Day was coming. The reply is not an argument built out of premises they would grant; it is an oath resting on a fact they have not conceded, which leaves them either to accept it or to call the Speaker a liar.

**Why the Oath Is Cast in a Negative Mould**

The Arabic reads *lā uqsimu bi-yawmi l-qiyāmah*, literally “I do not swear by the Day of Resurrection,” and no serious reading of this verse takes it as a denial. The grammarians divided over the particle *lā*, and both positions deserve their grounds. One party treats it as extra, a particle of emphasis that Arabic regularly attaches to an oath, so that the sense is simply “I swear.” The other gives *lā* its normal work of negation and supplies a suppressed clause before it: the matter is not as you claim, and then the oath. The first reading has economy on its side, since it assumes nothing missing. The second has the stronger claim on principle, because a particle that negates everywhere else in the language should be allowed to negate here. The dispute is genuine, but it changes nothing that the verse asserts: both roads end in an affirmation, which is why the sūrah can proceed on the sense without settling the grammar.

**What the Word Qiyāmah Carries**

*Qiyāmah* comes from the root *q-w-m*, to stand. The Day is named for standing: the dead stand up out of the earth, and then every creature stands before its Lord to answer. Both motions are held in the single noun, rising and accounting, and the sūrah will use each of them. Naming it “the standing” rather than “the day” or “the event” builds a claim into the name itself — this is an assembly, a summons, a reckoning conducted face to face, not merely a stretch of time. The Qur'an elsewhere names the same reality by what it does to those who meet it: *“The Inevitable Hour! What is the Inevitable Hour?”* (69:1–2). The one name describes the posture of those who are raised; the other describes the impossibility of escape. Together they rule out the reading the deniers preferred, that death is a door which opens onto nothing.

**An Oath as Argument, Not Ornament**

Short Makkan sūrahs lean on oaths because their hearers understood the form and what it cost. An oath stakes the speaker's credibility on the statement that follows, and in the Qur'an the thing sworn by is never neutral: it is chosen because its evidential weight is already conceded. What is unusual here is the inversion. No visible sign is offered as the ladder; the disputed Day is itself the rung. The effect is to relocate the burden of proof. The denier is no longer asked whether resurrection is conceivable in the abstract. He is told that it is a fact known to the One who swears, and the conversation moves from possibility to conduct. Against a position held on the strength of custom alone — *“There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time”* (45:24) — an oath speaks as the testimony of a witness. God swears as one who was present at the first creation and will be present at the second.

**The Answer the Oath Is Left Waiting For**

Every oath expects an answer clause: I swear by X that Y. Verse 1 supplies no such clause, and the commentators differed about where to find it. Some hold that the answer is the question in v. 3, since an interrogative placed under oath does the work of an assertion: *“Do people think We cannot reassemble their bones?”* (75:3) means, under the oath, that they are wrong to think it. Others hold that the answer is suppressed entirely and should be supplied from the sūrah's own conclusion, which makes the oath govern everything between. The first reading is stronger, because the abrupt turn from oath to interrogation in v. 3 is precisely the shape an oath's answer takes, and because it binds the opening triad into one movement rather than leaving the first verse to float over the whole chapter. Either way the sūrah announces its subject in a form that cannot be read as speculation.

**A Day Sworn To, and a Day Mocked**

The certainty asserted here is the certainty the Makkan audience ridiculed, and the Qur'an records both sides of that exchange without softening either. Against the oath stands the mockers' question, repeated across the Makkan and early Madinan sūrahs in nearly identical words. Against their laughter stands the flat declaration: *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves.”* (22:7), and the oath-form is used the same way elsewhere, *“Yes—by my Lord, the Knower of the unseen—it will certainly come to you!”* (34:3). The sūrah therefore begins where a preacher would normally end. Nothing is left to be proved at the level of whether; everything that follows concerns what the Day will do when it arrives, and who will be found standing.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:2

> And I do swear by the selfreproaching soul!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Oath, and a Stranger Object**

The first verse swore by a Day; this one swears by a soul. The pairing is the sūrah's first piece of reasoning, and it is compressed into a single conjunction. God swears by the Day of Resurrection and by the self-reproaching soul because the two perform the same work on different scales: each one summons a person, exposes what he has done, and refuses to let him plead ignorance. The Day does that once, publicly, at the end. The soul does it continuously, privately, throughout a life. An oath by such a soul is an oath by the faculty in a human being that already knows the difference between what he did and what he should have done, and that will not stop saying so. The object of the oath is therefore not a rarefied spiritual state but an ordinary one, familiar to anyone who has ever regretted something before anyone else found out.

**Three Souls, in Ascending Order**

The Qur'an names three conditions of the soul, and this verse supplies the middle one. The lowest is the soul that presses a person toward wrongdoing, named in the confession of the Egyptian minister's wife: *“And I do not seek to free myself from blame, for indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil, except those shown mercy by my Lord.”* (12:53). The highest is the soul addressed at the end of the Fajr sūrah: *“O tranquil soul! Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺.”* (89:27–28). Between them sits *al-nafs al-lawwāmah*, the blaming soul, which has not arrived at peace but has stopped being at ease with itself. The ordering matters: the middle term is not a stage one passes through and leaves behind so much as a condition of the struggle, the state of a person still in the fight. To swear by it is to swear by the unfinished, arguing, self-accusing human being rather than by the saint.

**What Makes the Blaming Soul Worth Swearing By**

The word *lawwāmah* is an intensive form: one who blames repeatedly, who returns to the charge. That persistence is the property being invoked. A single pang of regret could be dismissed as mood; a faculty that will not stop prosecuting is evidence of a moral constitution, of a standard inside the person that he did not install and cannot silence. The Qur'an locates this inward witness very precisely: *“Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them, and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein.”* (50:16). The soul's self-accusation is thus not a private psychological event only. It is the internal counterpart of the accounting that is coming, which is why one report has every soul, pious or profligate, reproaching itself on that Day — the good wondering why it did not do more, the wicked wishing it had stopped. On the Day the sūrah's first verse swears by, the prosecution that began inside a person simply continues in the open.

**The Minority Reading: an Oath Refused**

Because the verse opens with the same particle *lā* as v. 1, a minority of commentators read it as real negation: “I do *not* swear by the blaming soul.” Their ground is that an oath is normally sworn by something great — God, or a sign of His — and a human soul, however scrupulous, is not in that class. On this reading the sense is carried by what is suppressed, as though the text said: the matter is not as you suppose; I swear by the Day of Resurrection, and I do not need to swear by the blaming soul. The majority answer is that v. 1 and v. 2 stand or fall together, since the same particle introduces both, and a reading that turns the second into a denial while the first remains an oath has to explain the inconsistency. The majority reading is stronger: it keeps the parallel intact and gives the sūrah two witnesses instead of one.

**A Second Etymology: the Restless Soul**

An alternative derivation reads *lawwāmah* not from blame but from *talawwum*, constant turning, and takes the soul as fickle — happy then sad, obedient then heedless, never settled in one state. Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyyah argued for this sense in his book on the soul. The strength of the reading is that it fits what ordinary observation shows, a self that cannot hold a mood or a resolution for long. Its weakness is that the Qur'an's own vocabulary for blame is built on the same root, and the intensive form points to an agent doing the blaming rather than to instability. Both readings converge on a single picture of the human interior as a place of argument. The difference is whether the quarrel is with the self's conduct or with its own inconsistency, and the first is the more natural meaning of a word whose root appears throughout the Qur'an in the sense of censure.

**The Soul Sworn By in Another Sūrah**

The same oath-object appears in a sūrah that builds its whole argument on it: *“And by the soul and ˹the One˹ Who fashioned it, then with ˹the knowledge of˺ right and wrong inspired it!”* (91:7–8). There the soul is sworn by precisely as a thing made and then informed, which is what gives its self-reproach its authority: the standard it appeals to was put there by the One who fashioned it. A person who blames himself is not inventing the yardstick he is measured against. That is why the blaming soul can be joined, in a single oath, to the Day of Resurrection, and why the sūrah will later say that on that Day a man becomes a witness against himself despite the excuses he offers.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:3

> Do people think We cannot reassemble their bones?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is Already a Verdict**

The verse is shaped as a question but functions as a rebuke. *A-yaḥsabu* — does he suppose, does he reckon — is not the verb of inquiry but of ungrounded calculation: forming an opinion from what one can see and calling it knowledge. The Qur'an's reply, when it comes in the next line, is not an answer to a question but a correction of a method. Someone who concludes that God cannot gather bones has done arithmetic with decay and called the result theology. The interrogative form also serves a rhetorical purpose: it puts the denier's position into words so that he can hear it said aloud, stripped of the bravado with which it was first offered. Mockery sounds different when it is stated as a proposition about the divine power — *We cannot* — because the subject of that sentence is the Creator, and the audience is being asked to consider whether the claim is one they are prepared to own.

**Why Bones**

Bones are the denier's own chosen example, and the Qur'an quotes him choosing it more than once. In Yā Sīn the objector argues with his Creator *“forgetting they were created—saying, ‘Who will give life to decayed bones?’”* (36:78). The same challenge recurs in almost identical phrasing across the Makkan sūrahs: *“When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?”* (17:49); *“When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?”* (37:16); *“When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?”* (56:47); *“even after we have been reduced to decayed bones?”* (79:11). The choice is deliberate on the objector's part. Bone is the part of a body that outlasts everything else, the last thing to go, the remnant that shepherds find in an old graveyard. If even that cannot be reassembled, he reasons, nothing can. The argument is designed to look empirical, and its persuasiveness came from the sight of dry skeletons scattered on a hillside.

**The Report Behind the Verse**

The books of occasions of revelation name a particular interlocutor, ʿAdī ibn Rabīʿah, who is said to have asked the Prophet when the Hour would be and how it would happen, heard the answers, and then refused them, saying that even if he saw that Day with his own eyes he would not believe, and asking whether God could really gather these bones. The report fits the Makkan pattern of challenge and gives the verse a face. It is not, however, a hadith with a chain in the canonical collections; it belongs to the tafsīr literature's asbāb material. [UNVERIFIED: the ʿAdī ibn Rabīʿah account was not located this session in a chain-carrying source; it is cited here as a report preserved in tafsīr, not as an authenticated narration.] That limitation does not touch the verse's meaning. The Qur'an's question is addressed to *al-insān* — to the human being as such — and the argument it opens does not depend on which man happened to say it first.

**The Grammar of the Denial**

What is denied is not God's knowledge or His will but His power, and the verb makes that explicit: *jamʿ*, gathering what has been scattered. The objector's picture is of parts dispersed into soil, eaten by animals, carried on the wind, and the claim is that no one can collect them again. The Qur'an's counter is not to dispute the dispersal but to re-describe the collection. Gathering dispersed matter is not presented as a difficult feat reserved for the end of time; it is presented as an ordinary description of what the Creator does. *“We certainly know what the earth consumes of them ˹after their death˺, and with us is a well-preserved Record.”* (50:4). Nothing of the body is lost to the One who is keeping account of it, which relocates the problem from power to record-keeping — and the record, in this sūrah's logic, was never in question.

**An Objection About Matter, Answered About the Maker**

The deeper flaw in the challenge is that it treats resurrection as a physical process that must be extrapolated from other physical processes, when the claim being made is about the Agent who set those processes running. The Qur'an consistently refuses to argue on the objector's ground. Where he demands a mechanism, it points at the first creation: *“They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being.”* (36:79), and *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him.”* (30:27). The audacity of that reply is worth stating plainly: making a man from nothing in the first place is not presented as easier than remaking him from his own remains, but the second is presented as the smaller task by comparison. *“Have they not seen how Allah originates the creation then resurrects it? That is certainly easy for Allah.”* (29:19). The denier's difficulty was never with the bones; it was with the premise that the world has a Maker who addresses it.

**The Scale of the Claim**

One further consideration closes the gap the objector thought he had found. He imagined the task as large because he counted the bodies. The Qur'an counts differently: *“The creation and resurrection of you ˹all˺ is as simple ˹for Him˺ as that of a single soul.”* (31:28). Multiplicity is what makes collection hard for creatures who must pick things up one at a time; it is not what makes anything hard for the One who sustains every atom at once. The verse thus sets up the answer that follows, which will not argue in general but will descend to the smallest particular the objector's own example contained.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:4

> Yes ˹indeed˺! We are ˹most˺ capable of restoring ˹even˺ their very fingertips.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Outruns the Objection**

*Bālā* opens the verse as a flat reversal: no, the matter is not as you supposed, and yes, it will happen. What follows is the striking part. The objector had said bones; the reply says fingertips. The Qur'an does not meet the challenge on its own level but moves beneath it, to a smaller and finer detail than the one that was raised, and in doing so makes two points at once. First, the power in question reaches down to the least part of the least remnant, so the example the denier thought was his strongest is nowhere near the limit. Second, the choice of the fingertip is not arbitrary: it is the part of the human body that carries a person's unique mark, the detail by which one individual is distinguished from every other who has ever lived. The sūrah is not promising a generic reassembly of human material. It is promising this person, with these hands.

**Bānān: the Word and What It Carries**

*Bānān* in classical Arabic denotes the fingertips, and by extension the fingers, and lexical authorities also record its use for the extremities generally. The commentators took it here in its fine sense, and the grammar supports them: the object of the sentence is not merely the existence of fingers but their *taswiyah*, their being made proportionate and shaped. The verb is the one the Qur'an uses elsewhere for God's act of forming and levelling a creation into its proper measure. So the verse is not saying that God can reattach parts. It is saying that He can reproduce the design — the ridges, the proportions, the exact configuration that makes a hand the hand of one man rather than of another. Theological precision and biological precision meet in that single word, and the claim is more demanding than the objector's, not less.

**Why the Fingertip Is the Better Proof**

Reasoning from the smaller to the greater runs through the Qur'an's answers to this challenge, and it works because the human imagination is being engaged rather than the human intellect alone. A man can picture a heap of bones being collected; the task feels large but conceivable. What resists the imagination is the recovery of the minute: the pattern on a fingertip, the individual lineaments of a face once the flesh is gone. By choosing the part that seems most hopeless, the verse removes the last place the denier could stand. If the finest detail is within reach, the coarse one never needed arguing. The sūrah has thus converted the objector's example into a demonstration. He brought up bones because he thought they were the most ruined thing he could name, and the reply accepted his premise, went one step smaller, and turned it.

**Restoring the Form, Not Only the Matter**

The argument also clarifies what resurrection is being claimed. It is not reassembly of matter into a lump, and it is not the survival of a disembodied essence. It is the reconstitution of a person in his own form, body included, with the particularities that made him who he was. That is why the Qur'an's creation passages insist on the shaping stages and not merely on the raw material: *“then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation.”* (23:14). The same hand that clothed the bones with flesh the first time is the hand being described here. Nothing in the second act is new except the fact that the material already existed once, and it is hard to see why a second use of one's own material should be harder than the first use of no material at all.

**The Maker of the Extremities Elsewhere**

The sūrah is consistent with the rest of the Qur'an about where the evidence lies. Divine power is demonstrated not by the vastness of the heavens alone but by the finish of small things: *“And He created the pairs—males and females—from a sperm-drop when it is emitted.”* (53:45–46); *“˹They were˺ created from a spurting fluid, stemming from between the backbone and the ribcage.”* (86:6–7). A drop too small to see becomes a creature with hearing, sight, and a mind, and the stages are described in detail precisely so that the reader will not treat the minute as beneath the notice of God. The fingertip argument belongs to that same body of reasoning. Whoever formed a human being out of a fluid emitted in the dark has not lost the pattern when the bones are scattered. The argument does not depend on any particular account of what happens to matter after death. The Qur'an never asks its hearer to believe that the particles of a body stay somewhere retrievable, and it never needs to. What it asks is whether the One who originated the form out of no form at all can originate it a second time out of what remains, or out of nothing again. Once the first creation is granted, the second is the smaller claim, and the denier's own body is the argument standing in front of him.

**Where the Argument Is Heading**

The verse is the first move in a proof the sūrah will complete at its close. Having shown that the extremity can be restored, it will return at the end to the origins of the human being — a sperm-drop, then a clot, then a formed body — and ask the same question in the form of a challenge about the Creator's power over death. What is offered here as a promise of restoration will there be offered as a demonstration from what everyone already admits about how a person came to exist. The reasoning is a single arc, and this verse is its opening premise: the One who proportioned the fingertip in the first place has not been defeated by the grave.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:5

> Still people want to deny what is yet to come,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Unbelief Described as Appetite**

The sūrah has just answered the objector's challenge about bones and fingertips, and the answer was decisive. What follows is therefore remarkable: the very next statement is not that he was unconvinced but that he wants something. *Yurīdu* — he wills, he desires. Unbelief is here located in the will rather than the intellect. A person who had been shown the argument and found it thin would be described as mistaken; this verse describes him as wanting. The distinction runs through the Qur'an's diagnosis of rejection, which seldom treats denial as the conclusion of an inquiry and regularly treats it as the pressure of a preference. The objector cannot be argued out of a position he did not reason his way into, and the sūrah is candid about that: the evidence was given in v. 4, and nothing in v. 5 suggests it failed to land. It was unwelcome.

**What the Word Means: the Root f-j-r**

The verb *yafjuru* carries a family of senses that all descend from one image: breaking through. Dawn is *fajr* because it splits the dark. *Fujūr* is transgression because it breaks the bound that should hold a person in. From that root the commentators took two readings of this verse. The first: man wants to go on breaking the limit in the time that lies ahead of him, meaning he persists in sin for the rest of his life. The second: he denies the Hereafter that lies before him, spoiling it in advance by refusing it. The first reading has the root's ordinary moral sense on its side; the second has the sūrah's argument on its side, since the whole chapter is about refusing the Day. Neither requires the other to be discarded — a person who wants to keep breaking the bound and a person who denies the reckoning are doing the same thing from the inside out, because the prospect of being answered for it is exactly what makes the bound feel like a bound.

**The Time That Is Always Ahead**

The phrase *amāmahu* — what is in front of him — is where the verse does its sharpest work. Sin is placed in front of the person, and so is his repentance, and the two are never reached at the same moment. Al-Qushayrī described the condition precisely: a man resolves to turn back and then defers it, saying he will repent later, and dies before he does. He sets down the intention and never unties the knot, and the condition of genuine turning is not regret alone but a resolved intention not to return to what he did. If the sweetness of the lapse is still in the heart and the thought of repeating it is still pleasant, then what he calls repentance is only a wish about himself. The verse is short, but it names the most successful of all self-deceptions: the future in which one is finally going to change.

**Why Delay Feels Safe**

Nothing in the texture of ordinary life warns a person that the delay is dangerous. The goods keep arriving, the warnings lose their urgency, and the silence of heaven starts to look like approval. The Qur'an names this and refuses to let it be read as a verdict: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise.”* (6:44). Extended time is not a certificate; *“They are only given more time to increase in sin”* (3:178), and the One who grants the extension is the One who said *“I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless.”* (7:183). A person who treats his own survival as evidence that nothing is wrong has misread the situation entirely. Delay is the substance he is being tested with, and he is spending it.

**The Reversal at the Edge of Death**

What the deferrer cannot imagine is how the same request will sound in his own mouth at the end. The Qur'an records the plea several times, always in the same grammar of belatedness: *“When death approaches any of them, they cry, ‘My Lord! Let me go back, so I may do good in what I left behind.’”* (23:99–100). The answer is a barrier, not a negotiation. Others ask for the smallest of extensions: *“My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous.”* (63:10), and the reply is flat — *“But Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes.”* (63:11). The cruelty is not in the refusal. It is that the plea is identical to the one the same person declined a hundred times while it was still possible to act on it.

**Where the Sūrah Places the Blame**

By naming desire rather than doubt, the verse settles a practical question about how the message is to be delivered. If the obstacle were intellectual, the remedy would be more argument, and the Qur'an would be obliged to keep producing proofs until the last objection was met. Since the obstacle is appetite, the remedy is a warning that breaks the trance of *later*. That is why the sūrah moves at once from the denial to the state of the denier when the Day arrives, and why the door is held open until the breath stops: *“O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53). The offer runs right up to the moment the request becomes impossible.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:6

> asking ˹mockingly˺, “When is this Day of Judgment?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is Not an Inquiry**

The verse gives the denier's words directly, and the form is interrogative. The content is not a request for information. *Ayyāna* asks after time — at what moment? — and the question is asked in a tone the Qur'an describes elsewhere as ridicule, since its purpose is not to learn but to display the asker's confidence that there is nothing to learn. A person who genuinely wants to know whether an event will happen asks *whether*; this question concedes nothing and demands a date. The move is a familiar one in argument: convert a claim you cannot refute into a logistical detail, and then treat the missing detail as the refutation. Whoever has no answer about the calendar appears, to the crowd, to have no answer at all.

**The Question Rehearsed Across the Qur'an**

The wording recurs so often, and in such similar form, that it reads as a fixed piece of the Makkan repertoire. *“They ask ˹mockingly˺, ‘When is this Day of Judgment?’”* (51:12). *“˹Still˺ they ask ˹the believers˺, ‘When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?’”* (67:25). The same sentence appears with small variations in 10:48, 21:38, 27:71, 34:29 and 36:48. The repetition tells us something about the audience rather than about the Day. This was not an objection raised once by a thoughtful questioner; it was a slogan, repeated in assemblies, whose function was social — to mark the believers as people making claims they could not substantiate. That is why the Qur'an answers it so many times and in so many different ways: not because the question was hard, but because it kept being asked in bad faith.

**Why the Date Is Withheld**

The reply given to this question elsewhere in the Qur'an is consistent and unusually direct: the time belongs to God, and the Messenger was not permitted to disclose it. *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, ‘When will it be?’ Say, ‘That knowledge is only with my Lord. He alone will reveal it when the time comes. It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will only take you by surprise.’”* (7:187). The same instruction is repeated in 79:42–43, and the list of five hidden things includes the Hour alongside knowledge of what the wombs carry and what a soul will earn tomorrow (31:34). If the date were published, the life lived in front of it would be transformed into a countdown, and conduct would be governed by arithmetic rather than by love, fear, and loyalty. The withholding is the condition of the test, not a gap in the revelation. A second wisdom sits behind it. A published date would change conduct, but it would also end the condition in which conduct means anything, since a person who knows exactly when the audit falls can arrange his affairs for the day before it and live as he pleases until then. The hiddenness of the hour is what makes every hour the possible one. It is no accident that the Qur'an names knowledge of the Hour alongside the sending of rain and the contents of the wombs: these are things no creature manages, and not managing them is what keeps a creature dependent.

**What Was Actually Being Demanded**

Behind the demand for a date sat a demand for a different kind of proof. The Makkan audience wanted the threat cashed immediately, in this world, on a schedule they could check. *“And if We delay their punishment until an appointed time, they will definitely say, ‘What is holding it back?’”* (11:8). A prophecy that arrives late looks, to someone keeping score, like a prophecy that failed, and the delay was therefore taken as evidence. The Qur'an's response is to re-locate the whole matter: the Hour is not late, it is near in a sense that has nothing to do with clocks, *“Bringing about the Hour would only take the blink of an eye, or even less.”* (16:77). Nearness here belongs to the One who will bring it, not to the interval that remains.

**How the Qur'an Answers a Question It Refuses to Answer**

The sūrah does not date the Day; it describes what the Day will do to the people who asked. That is the shape of the answer throughout the Qur'an, and it is worth stating why. A date would have settled a dispute about information and left the dispositions of the disputants untouched. A description of the Day addresses the will, which is where this sūrah has already located the problem in v. 5. Seen in that light, the mockery is not dodged but answered on its own ground: the questioner wanted to know when, and he is told, in effect, that by the time he knows, the knowing will be part of the terror. *“In fact, the Hour will take them by surprise, leaving them stunned. So they will not be able to avert it, nor will it be delayed from them.”* (21:40).

**Hastening, and the Discipline Against It**

The impulse behind the question — the demand that God produce His threat on the asker's timetable — is one the Qur'an treats as a recurring disease, and it prescribes patience as its cure: *“And do not ˹seek to˺ hasten ˹the torment˺ for the deniers. On the Day they see what they have been threatened with, it will be as if they had only stayed ˹in this world˺ for an hour of a day.”* (46:35). The warning about the Day is not softened by the delay, and it is not strengthened by it either. What the delay does is expose the questioner: whether he is waiting to be convinced or waiting to be caught. Asked in that tone, the question also performs a second function: it shields the asker from the content of the claim. A date can be laughed at, while a claim about justice and accountability can only be answered in conduct. The sūrah will now show what being caught looks like, beginning not with the ground or the sky but with the eyes of the man who asked.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:7

> But when the sight is stunned,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Begins With the Eye**

The sūrah's reply to the scoffer starts in an unexpected place: not with the earth splitting or the Trumpet sounding, but with a faculty failing. *Baṣar* is sight, and more narrowly the act of looking with comprehension. On that Day, the first thing to go is the organ by which a human being normally takes hold of his surroundings. The choice is deliberate. Throughout the Qur'an, seeing is the master-metaphor for certainty — a thing is known when it is seen, and the person who will not accept a truth is described as one who does not see. So the collapse of sight at the opening of the Hour is not one catastrophe among many. It is the undoing of the instrument the denier had been relying on to judge what was real.

**Dazzled, or Confounded**

The verb *bariqa* carries two related senses, and the commentators divided between them. The first is being dazzled, struck by a flash so that the eye is blinded and cannot function; the root belongs to *barq*, lightning, and the picture is of an organ overwhelmed by light. The second is being confounded — the eye open and moving but unable to settle on anything, because terror will not let it rest. Ibn Kathīr took the verse in the sense that the disbelievers, at the terror of that Day, cannot fix their gaze on anything at all; al-Jalālayn read it as amazement at seeing the things they used to deny. The two are not exclusive. A dazzled eye and a panicked eye produce the same symptom from different causes, and the verse may be taken to hold both: light too great to bear, and a scene too frightening to take in.

**The Stare Recorded Elsewhere**

The same fixed gaze is described in several passages, and the details agree. In Ibrāhīm the guilty come forward *“rushing forth, heads raised, never blinking, hearts void.”* (14:43). In al-Qamar, *“With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts, rushing towards the caller. The disbelievers will cry, ‘This is a difficult Day!’”* (54:7–8). In al-Anbiyāʾ the disbelievers are portrayed as *“staring ˹in horror, crying,˺ ‘Oh, woe to us! We have truly been heedless of this.’”* (21:97). The consistency of the detail is instructive. These are not people looking for a way out or scanning the horizon for help; sight has stopped being a tool of action and has become pure reception. What they refused to look at in life is now the only thing in front of them.

**Why the Eye Fails First**

There is a logic in the sequence. The Qur'an's complaint against the deniers was never that they lacked evidence but that they had eyes and did not use them: *“They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with.”* (7:179). The faculty was intact; its employment was refused. The Hour accordingly begins by disabling the instrument that was misused, and the reversal is exact. Sight that would not read the signs is left with nothing to read but the thing it denied, and this time it cannot look away. The punishment fits the fault without any need for a separate machinery of retribution: the organ that was made for recognising the truth is simply handed the truth at a moment when recognition no longer helps.

**A List Without Connectors**

The sūrah strings these signs together with almost no connectors. The clauses fall one after another with the barest of connectors, giving the passage the quality of a list delivered quickly rather than a narrative unfolding in order. The effect is compression: the events are piled rather than sequenced, and the reader is left with the impression of a world coming apart faster than it can be described. Other sūrahs use the same device for the same subject — *“When the sky splits open, and when the stars fall away, and when the seas burst forth, and when the graves spill out.”* (82:1–4). The rhetorical point of the piling is that the distinction between first and next has stopped mattering. For the person living through it, catastrophe is not a series; it is a single event with many edges.

**The Collapse Comes Before the Answer**

The scoffer had asked when. The sūrah answers by describing the state of the man who asked, which is a way of saying that the question has been overtaken by its subject. By the time the date is known, the knowing is no longer information but experience, and the eyes that demanded proof have been rendered incapable of examining anything. The same principle governs what happens to the other faculties on that Day. Hearing, speech and the power to argue are not removed; they are left intact and turned against their owner, because a person who would not use an instrument for its purpose must eventually meet the truth the instrument was built to receive. The Qur'an describes mouths sealed while hands and feet speak (36:65), and ears, eyes and skin testifying to what was done through them (41:20). Sight is handled in exactly this way: not destroyed but overloaded, handed at last the full object it spent a life avoiding, and left without the capacity to process what it receives. Nothing in the description is cruelty for its own sake. The correspondence between the fault and its consequence is the argument, and the sūrah will go on from here to the moon, the sun, and the cry for escape. It begins with the eye because the eye is where the refusal began.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:8

> and the moon is dimmed,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Lesser Light Goes First**

Having opened with the failure of human sight, the sūrah turns to the lights in the sky, and it takes them in descending order: the moon, then the sun. The verb used of the moon, *khasafa*, belongs to a root meaning to sink, to be swallowed, or to lose light — the same word from which the term for a lunar eclipse is derived. The moon does not crash and it does not explode in this verse. It goes dark. That detail matters more than it first appears. A body that is extinguished rather than destroyed has had something withdrawn from it, and the withdrawal is the point: the moon was never the source of its own light, and on that Day whatever arrangement supplied it is switched off at the source.

**Two Lights With Two Different Jobs**

The Qur'an is precise about the difference between the two great lights, and the distinction is repeated wherever they appear together. *“He is the One Who made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases, so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺.”* (10:5). The sun is a lamp; the moon is a measure. The moon's function is to mark out time — *“As for the moon, We have ordained ˹precise˺ phases for it, until it ends up like an old, curved palm stalk.”* (36:39) — and human beings were given it as a calendar. The two are also described as running on tracks that never interfere: *“It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own.”* (36:40). Order, measure, and reliability are the qualities being emphasised every time.

**What Is Being Undone: the Clock, Not the Lamp**

If the moon is the instrument of timekeeping, then darkening it is the removal of the world's clock before the removal of the world. That reading fits the sūrah's argument, which is about a people who asked *when* and were given no date. The object they used to measure their intervals is the first thing dismantled. Time in this life is what makes delay possible; it is the substance the denier was spending while he put off his repentance. Taking the measure away is therefore not incidental scenery. It is the end of the postponement, stated physically: the interval in which one might have changed has closed, and the instrument that counted it has gone dark.

**The Calendar the Deniers Relied On**

The first audience lived by a lunar calendar. Months were tracked by the moon's phases, and the same moon regulated the times of pilgrimage, the waiting periods of divorce and inheritance, and the season of the fast. A community that ordered its commercial and ritual life by that light could not have missed the force of the image. The thing they treated as the fixed frame of their affairs was itself a creature with an appointed term, and the Qur'an had already said so: the sun and moon are *“each travelling in an orbit”* (21:33), *“each orbiting for an appointed term”* (13:2), *“each orbiting for an appointed term”* (39:5). A term is a thing that ends. The stability people assume when they look up at the sky is the stability of a schedule, not of a necessity. Nor is the point that timekeeping was somehow wrong — the Qur'an itself instructs believers to reckon their appointed times by the moon. The point is that a measured thing is a created thing. Living by the phases is lawful; assuming that the phases guarantee themselves is to mistake a schedule for a necessity.

**Eclipse, or Erasure**

The commentators glossed this verse in company with the next, and many understood the moon's fate as part of the enfolding described in the sūrah of the Rolling Up: *“When the sun is put out, and when the stars fall down.”* (81:1–2). On that reading the moon is not merely eclipsed but folded away, its light taken and its form undone. The difference between the two readings is a difference of severity rather than of kind. An eclipse implies a light temporarily blocked; an enfolding implies an arrangement brought to its end. The sūrah's own wording sits deliberately between them, and the reader is left holding both meanings at once, which is the effect the whole passage is aiming for: the world's most reliable object has become unrecognisable.

**The Order Was Never Self-Sustaining**

The theological point underneath the imagery is that the regularity of the heavens is not a machine left running. Every description of the sun and moon in the Qur'an attaches them to a term and to a Will that set them on their courses, and the purpose of their regularity is stated as a sign: *“He makes the signs clear so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord.”* (13:2). The order in the sky was installed as an argument for the Day that is now arriving, and its dissolution is that argument's conclusion. The One who split the moon as a sign to a generation that demanded one (54:1) is the One who now puts its light out, and nothing in between was ever held in place by anything but His command. For the denier, this is the closing of the case he had been arguing. He had treated the regularity of the world as the strongest evidence against the Hour, since nothing in his observation suggested that any day would differ from any other. The darkening answers him in his own terms: the pattern was never a proof of permanence but a sign, and a sign is a thing that points beyond itself to the One who set it.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:9

> and the sun and the moon are brought together,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Orbits, One Ending**

The moon was darkened in the previous verse; here the sun is joined to it. *Jumiʿa* is a passive of gathering, and the two bodies that had been kept apart for the whole of created time are brought into one place and one condition. The sūrah does not say they collide, and the commentators did not read it that way — they read it, with the parallel passage in 81:1–2, as an enfolding in which both lights are extinguished and rolled up together. The joining is therefore not a crash but an assembly: two separate things are made to share a single fate. What had been the most reliable pair of objects in the sky becomes, in one clause, a pair of undone things.

**What the Gathering Is Not**

Care is needed about what the verse is claiming, because the imagination runs ahead of the text. Nothing here describes the sun falling on the earth, or the two bodies striking one another, or a physical catastrophe of a kind that could be modelled. The verb is the ordinary verb of collection, the same verb used elsewhere for assembling people and for assembling scattered parts. Its force is relational: two things which were defined by their separation are now defined by their conjunction. The created order was built out of distinctions — light and dark, sun and moon, day and night — and the undoing of that order is described as the collapse of the distinctions rather than as the destruction of the material. The reader is not being given physics. He is being told that the arrangement is over.

**The Order Was Built on Separation**

The Qur'an is emphatic that the two lights are kept apart by command, and it says so in the same terms in several sūrahs. *“It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own.”* (36:40). *“And He is the One Who created the day and the night, the sun and the moon—each travelling in an orbit.”* (21:33). *“The sun and the moon ˹travel˺ with precision.”* (55:5). The pattern in these verses is always the same: distinctness, measure, and a term. Two bodies on separate tracks, each with its appointed course, never interfering. That arrangement is what the present verse terminates. Gathering the two is the exact negation of the description that has been repeated throughout the Book, which is why the image lands as hard as it does.

**The Same Verb That Was Used for the Bones**

The most striking feature of the wording is one that translation hides. The verb here — *jumiʿa*, they are gathered — is from the same root as the verb in v. 3, where the denier asks whether God can *gather* his bones. The sūrah has thus answered the objection by using its own word against a harder object. The man who thought that gathering scattered bones was beyond the Creator is now told that the sun and the moon are gathered, and the sentence is not presented as difficult. If the greatest bodies in the sky can be collected into one place, the collection of a skeleton is not the difficult case it was made out to be. The sūrah's argument has come full circle in six verses, and it closes by making the denier's own vocabulary describe the undoing of his world.

**Both Lights Are Servants, Not Powers**

Nothing in the Qur'an permits the sun or the moon to be treated as an independent power. They are described repeatedly as *musakhkharāt*, subjected, made to serve by command, and always for a stated purpose: *“He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.”* (6:96). The regularity that impresses an observer is the regularity of something obeying, not of something ruling. This is also why their dissolution requires no contest. Nothing is being defeated here, and no rival power is being overthrown; a servant's commission has simply reached its end. The universe does not end in a battle but in a discharge. The sun's own term is stated in this vocabulary elsewhere: *“The sun travels for its fixed term. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.”* (36:38), and the same note is struck in the creation accounts, where the sun, moon and stars are created *“all subjected by His command.”* (7:54). A fixed term is the opposite of a necessity. Whatever has been given a term is a thing that stops, and the regularity that looked like independence was only the reliability of obedience. The Qur'an even anticipates the religious use people would make of these bodies: *“Do not prostrate to the sun or the moon, but prostrate to Allah, Who created them ˹all˺, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺.”* (41:37). Servants cannot shelter anyone.

**The World That Made Postponement Possible**

Taken with the previous verse, the couplet removes both of the instruments that made delay feel safe. The moon was the measure of intervals; the sun was the marker of days. With the measure darkened and the marker gathered in, the world in which a person could say *later* has closed. That is the thread running from the mockery of v. 6 to this point, and it explains why the sūrah spends two of its shortest verses on the sky rather than on the earth. The denier's objection depended on the stability of the order above him. The sūrah answers by turning out its lights. It is a small detail that the two lights are named in the order of their usefulness to a person postponing something: the measure first, then the day. The sequence reads like the dismantling of a schedule, and the sūrah's hearers, who had asked for the date in mockery, are being shown the date's precondition being removed.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:10

> on that Day one will cry, “Where is the escape?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Description to a Human Voice**

Six verses of cosmic signs end here, and the passage turns without warning to direct speech. Up to this point the sūrah has described what will happen to the sky and the eyes; now a person speaks, and the sentence is a cry rather than a statement. The shift is the point of the whole sequence. The collapse of the heavens matters in the Qur'an not as spectacle but because of what it does to the people standing under it, and the sūrah moves from the event to the voice in a single conjunction. Everything described so far was preparation for this one sentence. It is also the answer to the mockery in v. 6: the man who asked *when* now asks *where*, and the two questions could not be more different in tone.

**Al-Mafarr: a Place, Not a Plan**

The word cried out is *al-mafarr*, from a root meaning to flee. The noun is a place-noun: not the act of escaping but somewhere to escape *to*. That distinction carries the desperation of the sentence. The speaker is not asking for advice, a strategy, or a way of buying time, which is what a person in danger normally wants. He is asking for a destination, and the form of the question concedes that flight itself is assumed — only the destination is missing. The same root produces the description of that Day elsewhere, when people are portrayed running from the people they love most: *“on that Day every person will flee from their own siblings, and ˹even˺ their mother and father, and ˹even˺ their spouse and children.”* (80:34–36). Flight is universal there, and it is useless there too.

**A Question With No Addressee**

Nobody is named as the person being asked. The cry is not directed at God, at the angels, or at the other people in the scene, and that anonymity is part of its horror. A question addressed to someone presupposes that someone might answer and that an answer would help. This question is addressed to the situation. The Qur'an uses the device elsewhere to show a person who has passed beyond negotiation — the disbelievers detained before the Fire cry out to be sent back (6:27), and the wrongdoers ask for a short delay (14:44), and in both cases the plea is met with the fact that the time for pleading has closed. What distinguishes this cry is that it is not even a plea. The Qur'an addresses the same cry to people who have not yet reached that Day, and the preposition changes everything: *“Respond to your Lord before the coming of a Day from Allah that cannot be averted. There will be no refuge for you then, nor ˹grounds for˺ denial ˹of sins˺.”* (42:47). The word for refuge there is of the same family as the one in the next verse, and the notice is served in advance. Nothing about that Day is presented as a trap sprung on the unprepared; the exits are described as closing long before they close.

**The Escape Routes That Used to Exist**

In ordinary life there is always somewhere to go. A man under pressure can leave a city, change his company, occupy himself with work, or simply refuse to think about the thing that frightens him. The Qur'an treats the availability of these exits as the real content of the test, and it names the day they run out in several places: *“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it.”* (18:53); *“If only you could see when they will be horrified with no escape ˹on Judgment Day˺! And they will be seized from a nearby place.”* (34:51); *“Whatever ˹idols˺ they used to invoke besides Allah will fail them. And they will realize that they will have no escape.”* (41:48). The repetition is deliberate. The Qur'an is training its hearers to recognise that the exits they rely on are borrowed, not owned.

**What Is Actually Being Fled**

The object of the flight deserves attention, because the sūrah is precise about it. What arrives on that Day is not a place of punishment but a record and a reckoning, and what the speaker wants to escape is being seen. This is consistent with the Qur'an's account of why people resisted the message in the first place: not because the argument was weak but because accepting it would require them to answer for how they had lived. The horror is therefore not physical. It is exposure. A person who has spent a life arranging to be thought well of is presented with the only audience whose opinion he could not manage, and there is nowhere to stand where he is not visible.

**The Reversal of the Two Questions**

The structure the sūrah has built is a reversal, and it is worth laying out plainly. In v. 6 the denier asked a question he did not want answered, in a tone of contempt, about a date he had no intention of preparing for. Here he asks a question he needs answered, in a tone of panic, about a place he would give everything to find. The first question was a way of avoiding the subject; the second is the subject. Between them stand four verses in which the world he trusted comes apart, and the sūrah's insistence on the suddenness of that collapse is the answer to his original demand for a timetable. He wanted to know when, and the answer is: sooner than his arrangements assume. There is a final irony in the pairing. The question he asked in contempt was designed to make the Day look distant and unreal; the question he asks in terror is designed to make it stop. Both questions are about time, and the sūrah has spent the verses in between showing that time is precisely what is being taken away.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:11

> But no! There will be no refuge.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Is Three Words Long**

The cry of the previous verse is answered immediately, and the answer is shorter than the question. *Kallā* rejects what has gone before — no, the matter is not as the speaker hopes — and then comes the negation: *lā wazar*. There is no refuge. The economy of the reply is part of its force. No reason is given, no alternative is offered, no condition is attached, and the absence of qualification is what makes the sentence final. A promise of escape conditioned on something would have left the cry alive; an unqualified negative closes it. The sūrah has spent four verses dismantling the world and now spends one dismantling the last thing a person in terror reaches for.

**Wazar: Shelter, Stronghold, and Burden**

The word being negated is worth unpacking, because English *refuge* flattens it. *Wazar* is the place a person runs to when something is after him — a fortress, a mountain's protection, the shelter of a protector's tent. The same root yields *wizr*, the burden of sin a person carries, and *wazīr*, the minister who carries a ruler's weight. The family of meanings hangs together: a refuge is what bears the weight you cannot bear yourself. To say there is no *wazar* is therefore to say something stronger than *there is nowhere to run*. It is to say that nothing will carry the load for you. Every arrangement by which a person transfers his accountability to someone or something else has been voided in a single noun.

**Every Exit the Qur'an Has Already Closed**

The denial is not unique to this verse; it is the Qur'an's standard account of that Day, and the consistency is the point. *“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it.”* (18:53). *“˹Imagine˺ how many peoples We destroyed before them, and they cried out when it was too late to escape.”* (38:3). *“so those who dispute about Our signs may know that they have no refuge.”* (42:35). The same verdict is delivered in 34:51 and 41:48. What changes across these passages is the identity of the thing that fails — idols, protectors, distance, time — and what never changes is that the failure is total. The Qur'an is not describing a difficult situation from which the clever might slip. It is describing the end of slipping.

**Why There Is Nowhere Left to Go**

The refuges of this life are worth naming, because each of them is a good thing in its place and each of them fails here. Time was a refuge: while it lasted, nothing was settled. Company was a refuge: among people who shared his assumptions, a man's account of himself went unchallenged. Status, wealth, and the ability to move were refuges, and so was the simple power of not thinking about something. Every one of these is a created thing being used as though it were independent, and the Day arrives as the removal of that pretence. What is left is the person and what he did, and the Qur'an is insistent that the two cannot be separated. The record is not a file kept somewhere and produced at the hearing; it is what the person has become. *“And We have everything recorded precisely.”* (78:29), and the precision is the problem: nothing small is omitted, because nothing small was ever without weight. Nothing is taken from anyone; the intermediaries simply stop functioning, and the person is left alone with what he did, which is what he had spent his life arranging to avoid.

**The One Direction That Still Works**

The Qur'an does acknowledge a flight, and it is the only one, and its direction is the opposite of what the cry in v. 10 imagines. Of three men who had failed badly and knew it, the text says: *“their souls were torn in anguish. They knew there was no refuge from Allah except in Him. Then He turned to them in mercy so that they might repent.”* (9:118). The preposition does all the work. There is no refuge *from* God except *in* God, which means the flight that succeeds is the one that stops running. That is available now, in the interval the sūrah keeps insisting is closing, and the Qur'an's offer is stated without conditions attached to the size of the offence: *“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53).

**A Warning Delivered Before, Not After**

The verse is addressed to people who are still alive, and that is what makes it a warning rather than a report. Nothing is gained by telling the damned that they are damned; the Qur'an's purpose in describing the closure of every exit is to make the hearer look at the exits he is currently using. The man who has been putting something off on the strength of time, or hiding behind the approval of people who think as he does, is being told that these are borrowed arrangements with a fixed term. The negative in this verse is severe, and while breath remains it is also entirely avoidable. Nothing about the reckoning comes as a surprise to the person who has been warned about it. That combination is the shape of the Qur'an's warning throughout: the sentence is real, and the door in front of it is open. The verse can therefore be read twice, and it should be. Read as report, it describes the closure of every exit on a Day that is coming. Read as address, it asks the hearer which exit he is standing in now, and how long he intends to stay there.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:12

> On that Day all will end up before your Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Mustaqarr: Where a Thing Finally Settles**

The word rendered as destination is *al-mustaqarr*, from a root meaning to settle, to become still, to come to rest. It is the noun of arrival after movement, and it carries the sense of a thing that has stopped travelling because it has reached the place it was travelling toward. The word appears in the Qur'an for the resting-place of the embryo in the womb and for the term appointed to the world itself, and in every case the idea is the same: movement with an end built into it. Applied to a human being on that Day, it means the wandering is over. Not the body only, and not the soul only, but the whole person, at rest because there is nowhere further to go.

**The Destination Is a Person**

What makes the sentence severe is its preposition. The settling is not *in* a place but *to* the Lord: *ilā rabbika*. The Qur'an could have said that everyone comes to rest in the grave, or in the Fire, or in the Garden, and each of those would have been true. It says instead that the terminus is God, which relocates the whole matter from geography to audience. A person's end is not a location he reaches but a Person he meets, and every description of that Day in the Qur'an is arranged around this: the record is opened, the witnesses arrive, and the judgment is pronounced in the presence of the One who was present all along. The horror and the comfort of the verse are the same fact seen from two sides. The same idea is put the other way round in the last verse of the sūrah of the Morning Brightness, where the terminus is named in the same preposition: *“and that to your Lord ˹alone˺ is the ultimate return ˹of all things˺.”* (53:42). A person's life is movement with an end built into it, and the end is not a place on a map but an audience. That is why the Qur'an's descriptions of the Hereafter are saturated with the language of meeting, presence, and being seen. The Garden is not described as a location so much as a nearness, and the Fire is not described as a place so much as a separation from the One before whom everyone has arrived.

**To Your Lord: Why the Prophet Is Addressed**

The possessive is singular and it is addressed to the Messenger. That choice does two things at once. It universalises the statement, since the Lord of the Prophet is the Lord of everyone, and it personalises it, since the one hearing it first is the man who has been told to warn his people. Every human being will come to rest before the Lord whom Muhammad ﷺ proclaimed, which is the sūrah's answer to the mockery in the opening movement. The people who asked *when* are now told *before whom*. The question of the date, which the Qur'an consistently declines to answer, is replaced by the question of the identity of the Judge, which it answers constantly.

**Sovereignty Without Intermediaries**

The scene the Qur'an builds for that Day strips away every other claim to authority. *“the Day all will appear ˹before Allah˺. Nothing about them will be hidden from Him. ˹He will ask,˺ ‘Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme!’”* (40:16). *“All authority on that Day is for Allah ˹alone˺. He will judge between them.”* (22:56). Everything that mediated power in this life — tribe, wealth, office, the protection of a patron, the opinion of a crowd — is absent from these descriptions, and the absence is the argument. A creature who has arranged his standing through intermediaries is brought into a court where no intermediary is recognised, and the verse's plainness is deliberate: no advocate is mentioned, no negotiation is described, and no one speaks except by permission.

**One Verb, Two Outcomes**

The noun is neutral, and that neutrality is theologically important. *Al-mustaqarr* names the arrival, not the accommodation, and the arrival is common to everyone; what differs is where a person is sent from the audience. The sūrah itself will divide the arrivals two verses later into faces that are bright and faces that are gloomy, and the Qur'an's descriptions of the two destinations are equally concrete. Both are called a settlement, both are permanent, and both are described as the fitting outcome of a life. The verse thus refuses to let its hearer decide in advance which side of the division he occupies. The neutrality also guards against a lazy assumption. A person may tell himself that arriving before the Lord is automatically good news, since God is merciful; the Qur'an refuses to let mercy be used as a solvent for the reckoning. The same Lord whose mercy is invoked is the Lord who judges, and the two are not in tension in these texts. Mercy is what makes the warning possible in the first place, since a God who did not warn would not be merciful at all.

**The Close of the Sūrah's First Movement**

Verses 1 through 12 form a single arc, and this line is its closing statement. The sūrah opened with an oath by the Day of Resurrection and by the soul that accuses itself; it named the denial, answered it from the body's own extremities, described the collapse of the sky, and recorded the cry of a person looking for somewhere to go. Each of those steps moved toward this one sentence, which supplies what the whole passage was missing: a destination. What follows will not be more scenery. Having established that everyone arrives before the Lord, the sūrah turns to what happens at the arrival, beginning with the account a person is given of his own life.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:13

> All will then be informed of what they have sent forth and left behind.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Informed, Not Interrogated**

The verb is *yunabbaʾu* — he is told, he is given news — and it belongs to the family of *nabaʾ*, the weighty report. The passive matters: the human being on that Day is not asked to supply information, because the information is already complete and comes from the One who kept it. A trial in this world exists to establish facts that are in dispute; a notification presupposes that nothing is in dispute. That is why the Qur'an's scenes of the Judgment so often skip the investigation and go straight to the disclosure. The purpose of the occasion is not to find out what happened but to have the person face it. He arrives suspecting, hoping, and half-believing his own account of himself, and the first thing that happens to him is that he is told.

**What Was Sent Ahead**

The first half of the account is what a person sent on before him. The image is of a traveller dispatching his goods ahead to the place he is going, so that they arrive before he does. Every deed in the Qur'an is described as travelling: *“It is certainly We Who resurrect the dead, and write what they send forth and what they leave behind. Everything is listed by Us in a perfect Record.”* (36:12); *“˹then˺ each soul will know what it has sent forth or left behind.”* (82:5). The point of the metaphor is that a deed does not vanish when the moment of doing it passes. It goes somewhere and waits. A life is therefore not a series of events that are over, but a convoy arriving at a destination ahead of its owner, and the owner walks into a room already furnished with what he did.

**What Was Left Behind: Two Readings**

The second half of the pair is read in two ways, and both have strong support. On the first reading, *what he left behind* is the good he did not do — the omissions, the prayers not prayed, the money not given, the reconciliation not attempted. On the second, it is the trace that outlasts him: the practice he began that others continued, the knowledge he taught, the child he raised, the harm he set in motion that kept moving after he stopped. The first reading has the parallelism of the verse on its side, since sent-forth and left-behind then divide neatly into acts and omissions. The second has the Arabic on its side, since the verb can carry the sense of what a person leaves after him. The second is also the heavier claim, and the sunnah supports it: the Prophet said that when a person dies his deeds end except for three — ongoing charity, beneficial knowledge, and a righteous child who prays for him (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1631). A man's account therefore does not close when he stops breathing, which is exactly the sort of thing a person arranging his affairs should want to know in advance.

**Nothing Too Small to Record**

Both halves of the account are kept with a precision the Qur'an describes in some detail. Two angels record, one on the right and one on the left, and *“not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺.”* (50:18). Secret conversations are included: *“Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺.”* (43:80). The same precision is stated of creation at large — *“Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge”* (6:59) — which means the record is not a special surveillance installed for human beings but a consequence of the kind of world this is. Nothing is too small to be counted, and the smallest item on the list is what the Qur'an singles out elsewhere: *“So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it.”* (99:7).

**The Record Is Not Separable From the Person**

What is being disclosed is not a file produced from outside. The record is what the person has made of himself, and the Qur'an's language consistently binds the two. *“And We have everything recorded precisely.”* (78:29) is a statement about the keeping, but the content kept is the man's own history, held in a form he cannot now edit. That is why the disclosure is described as a recognition rather than a revelation: the person reads and knows. *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it. They will cry, ‘Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?’”* (18:49). The cry is not about unfairness. It is the shock of completeness.

**Why the Telling Is the Judgment**

Once the disclosure has happened, surprisingly little remains to be done. There is no cross-examination in these scenes, no dispute about mitigating circumstances, no argument about whether the record is accurate. The person's own knowledge of what he did is the evidence, and the Qur'an states the principle directly: *“Read your record. You ˹alone˺ are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself.”* (17:14). The judgment therefore does not need to be imposed from outside. It is the encounter between what a person is and what he always knew he was, conducted in the open, in front of the One whose knowledge was never in question. The disclosure is also strictly individual. *“˹Consider˺ the Day ˹when˺ every soul will come pleading for itself, and each will be paid in full for what it did, and none will be wronged.”* (16:111), and no soul bears another's load (17:15). The company a person kept, the crowd whose approval he shared, and the community whose habits he absorbed will all be present, but they will not dilute his account. The convoy he sent ahead contained his deeds and no one else's.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:14

> In fact, people will testify against their own souls,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Witness Is the Self**

The natural arrangement in a court is that the accused defends himself and someone else prosecutes. This verse reverses the arrangement: the defendant is the witness, and he testifies against himself. The Arabic is unusually dense here, since the word rendered as insight or clear witness (*baṣīrah*) belongs to the same root as sight, which the sūrah used in v. 7 for the faculty that failed at the Hour. There, sight was overloaded and could not take in what was in front of it. Here it has become the instrument of the confession. The organ that was misused in life is restored for the one purpose it can now serve: seeing the truth about the person it belongs to.

**Why No Interrogation Is Needed**

The Qur'an's scenes of the reckoning dispense with witnesses in the ordinary sense, because the admission comes from the party himself. The limbs supply what the mouth will not: *“When they reach it, their ears, eyes, and skin will testify against what they used to do.”* (41:20); *“on the Day their tongues, hands, and feet will testify against them for what they used to do.”* (24:24); *“On this Day We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to commit.”* (36:65). These are not external informants brought in to defeat a denial. They are parts of the person's own history, and their testimony is simply the record of what was done through them. The confession in this verse is the last step in the same process: the man stops disputing and says it himself.

**The Blaming Soul Reappears**

The sūrah swore in v. 2 by the self-reproaching soul, and the connection to this verse is direct. The faculty that accused a man in private throughout his life is the faculty that now accuses him in public. Nothing new has been installed; something has been unblocked. In life, self-accusation was one voice among many, competing with appetite, reputation, company, and the constant availability of delay. On that Day those competitors are gone. What remains is the knowledge he always had, and the Qur'an presents its emergence as testimony rather than as torture, which suggests that the pain lies in the truth rather than in any additional machinery attached to it.

**Conscience Becomes Evidence**

The Qur'an locates this inward knowledge with precision: *“Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them, and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein.”* (50:16). The whisper of the soul is known before it is spoken, which means the self-knowledge described in this verse was never hidden from God; it was hidden from the person, or from the parts of him he permitted himself to consult. The Day is thus a removal of the veil rather than an acquisition of evidence. What a person has been carrying inside him all along — the unexamined knowledge of what he has done — is produced, and he hears himself say it.

**Confession Without Coercion**

The confessions recorded elsewhere in the Qur'an have a striking quality: they are volunteered. *“O assembly of jinn and humans! Did messengers not come from among you, proclaiming My revelations and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours?” They will say, “We confess against ourselves!”* (6:130). The same admission is put in the mouths of those whom the angels take at death (7:37). Nothing in these passages suggests a confession extracted under threat. What has changed is the situation, not the person's knowledge. In life, denial was inexpensive because the interests that made it attractive were still in place; on that Day those interests have been removed, and the same man who found denial easy now finds it impossible. Two passages fill in how the confession actually arrives. When the enemies of God are gathered, their own skins speak against them, and they turn on their skins in outrage, only to be told that they never bothered to hide anything from their ears, eyes and skin (41:21–22). The reply given to them locates the real failure: *“It was that ˹false˺ assumption you entertained about your Lord that has brought about your doom, so you have become losers.”* (41:23). Their account of themselves was never a dispute about evidence. It was a false assumption about the One who kept the record, and once that assumption collapses, the confession follows on its own. That is the most economical explanation of the confession, and it is the one the Qur'an supports.

**What This Means for the Living**

The practical implication is plain enough to state without embellishment. If the witness at the reckoning is a person's own self, then the thing to attend to now is not the strength of one's arguments but the state of one's self-knowledge. The man who has practised accusing himself, in the manner the sūrah swore by in v. 2, arrives at the hearing with the testimony already given and the account already settled. The Qur'an also records the moment at which denial becomes impossible, and it is not a moment of duress but of unveiling: *“You were totally heedless of this. Now We have lifted this veil of yours, so Today your sight is sharp!”* (50:22). The veil was never a shortage of information. It was the arrangement of interests that made ignoring the information comfortable. Removing it adds no knowledge to the person; it removes his reasons for not using what he already knew, and what remains is testimony. The man who has spent his life managing his own version of events arrives with the entire procedure still ahead of him. The Qur'an's advice is not to silence the inward voice but to agree with it while agreement still costs something, since that is the only time it is worth anything.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:15

> despite the excuses they come up with.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Excuses Survive Even This**

The previous verse established that a person will testify against himself, and this one adds something that stops the reader from treating that confession as easy or automatic. Even then, with the record opened and his own soul bearing witness against him, he produces excuses. The persistence is the point. A person's defence of himself is not a position he argues because it is strong; it is a reflex that survives the collapse of every argument he ever made for it. The Qur'an's realism about human beings is consistent here: the same man who denied the Day without evidence will go on explaining himself in front of the Day without prospects, because defending himself is what he does. The excuses are not a defence strategy. They are a symptom that has outlasted its usefulness. The Qur'an's realism here matches its account of human beings elsewhere, in this life as on that Day. Wherever a fault is named, the same reflex is visible: the account is adjusted, the circumstance is widened, the responsibility is distributed among other people, and the speaker emerges as the person who had least to do with what happened. The sūrah is saying that the reflex does not break at the grave. That is why the confession in v. 14 needs no external pressure to explain it — the pressure comes from the collapse of the habit's usefulness, not from any change in the habit itself.

**The Word Being Used**

The Arabic term is the plural of an intensive form built on the root for excuse, and its shape suggests something done repeatedly and with effort. This is not a single plea but a stream of them, produced as each previous one fails. English renderings that flatten it into *excuses* lose that element of ongoing manufacture. The same word appears in the Qur'an on the lips of people in this life, offering reasons to the Prophet for what they had failed to do, and the response they receive is instructive: *“They will make excuses to you ˹believers˺ when you return to them. Say, ‘Make no excuses, ˹for˺ we will not believe you. Allah has already informed us about your ˹true˺ state.’”* (9:94). Excuses are addressed here to a party who cannot be deceived by them and who does not need to investigate them, which is precisely the situation on the Day the sūrah is describing.

**Two Ways of Reading the Clause**

The commentators divided over how this verse attaches to the one before it. On the first reading it is a concession: he testifies against himself *even though* he has his excuses ready, meaning the testimony is so strong that it overrides his defence. On the second it is a rejection: he casts about for excuses, and none of them will be accepted. The first reading gives the verse a rhetorical force, since it measures the strength of the self-witness by what it overcomes. The second gives the excuses their own clause and makes the verse a verdict on pleading. The second is the stronger reading, because the Qur'an says the same thing elsewhere in explicit terms — *“the Day the wrongdoers’ excuses will be of no benefit to them.”* (40:52) — and because the sūrah has already closed every avenue of escape in v. 11. Either way, the outcome is identical: the plea fails.

**What Excuses Are Made Of**

The materials are always the same, and they are worth naming because they are recognisable from ordinary life. Circumstance is the most common: the times were hard, the pressure was real, the options were narrow. Company comes next — everyone was doing it, and the man who held back was the odd one. Upbringing, temperament, ignorance, and necessity follow, and behind all of them sits the same structural move: the transfer of responsibility from the agent to something else. Every excuse is a re-description of a choice as an event that happened to someone. That is why no excuse can be answered by more information; the dispute is never about the facts. It is about whether the person who did the thing is the person who owns it, and on that Day the answer is given by the person himself.

**The Plea the Qur'an Records**

Scripture is specific about what the pleading sounds like, and the examples are sobering. Some will swear that they were never associators at all: *“Their only argument will be: ‘By Allah, our Lord! We were never polytheists.’”* (6:23). Others will swear to God as they used to swear to people, *“thinking they have something to stand on. Indeed, it is they who are the ˹total˺ liars.”* (58:18). What these scenes share is that the lie is not tactical. A person who has spent decades telling a story about himself has come to believe it, and the exposure of the story is experienced as an assault rather than as a correction. The Qur'an diagnoses the failure at its root elsewhere: the assumption that God did not really know much of what they were doing (41:22). Fix that assumption and the excuses lose their function.

**Why No Plea Can Work There**

The failure of the excuses is not a refusal to hear them. It is that the conditions which made them plausible have been removed. An excuse works in this life because it is offered to someone who cannot see the whole picture, or who can be worn down, or whose opinion matters more to the speaker than the truth does. None of those conditions obtain at the reckoning. The Witness is the One whose knowledge was never in question, the record is complete, and the person's own soul is on the side of the prosecution. The Qur'an warns the living about this in the plainest possible terms: *“There will be no refuge for you then, nor ˹grounds for˺ denial ˹of sins˺.”* (42:47). The time for the plea that works is the time before the Day arrives.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:16

> Do not rush your tongue trying to memorize ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sudden Change of Addressee**

Twelve verses of eschatology, and then the sūrah turns without transition to the Prophet and instructs him about his own tongue. The shift is abrupt by design. The passage that follows — vv. 16 through 19 — is a parenthesis inside the argument about the Day, and its placement is its meaning: the revelation being received is itself part of the case the sūrah is making. The God who will raise the dead and reckon with them is the God who is speaking now, and the manner in which His speech is received is therefore not an incidental matter. Reciting, listening, and being explained to are acts due to that Speaker, and the sūrah interrupts its own theme to establish how His words are to be handled.

**What Prompted the Instruction**

The circumstance is preserved in the most reliable collection of the Prophet's sayings. Ibn ʿAbbās reported that when revelation came down, the Prophet used to move his tongue and lips along with it, hurrying to fix the words before they escaped him, and this was hard for him; so God revealed *“Move not your tongue concerning the Quran to make haste therewith.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4927). A second narration in the same collection adds the explanation of what the following verses promise: that God would gather it in his chest and enable him to recite it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5, 4928). The wording of the instruction is therefore precise: the thing prohibited is not effort but haste, and the haste came from fear of loss. God's answer addresses the fear rather than the diligence.

**The Fear Behind the Haste**

The anxiety is entirely intelligible. A man receiving scripture in an oral culture, with no written text in front of him and no means of checking what he had heard, would reasonably press to secure every syllable before it slipped. The Qur'an answers that anxiety elsewhere with a promise rather than a technique: *“We will have you recite ˹the Quran, O Prophet,˺ so you will not forget ˹any of it˺, unless Allah wills otherwise.”* (87:6). The guarantee is attached to the Giver, not to the recipient's precautions. The Prophet's haste was not disobedience; it was the last remnant of relying on his own management, and the verse removes it. The report carries a further detail. The sub-narrator demonstrated the movement with his own lips so that his students could see it, and the chain — Mūsā ibn Abī ʿĀʾisha from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr from Ibn ʿAbbās — transmitted the gesture along with the words. Al-Bukhārī placed a version of this report at the head of his Book of Revelation, which indicates how the community read it: not as an anecdote about the Prophet's habits but as the charter for how scripture is received. The instruction belongs to the first moment of the revelation's descent and governs every recitation after it.

**Revelation Is Received, Not Seized**

There is a principle here that reaches beyond the historical moment. The Qur'an repeatedly presents revelation as something given at a pace and in an order chosen by its Author, and it treats the recipient's desire to accelerate as a category error. The same instruction is given in Sūrah Ṭā Hā in almost identical terms: *“Do not rush to recite ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran ˹O Prophet˺ before it is ˹properly˺ conveyed to you, and pray, ‘My Lord! Increase me in knowledge.’”* (20:114). The prescribed response to not knowing is not to grab but to ask for more. That ordering — listen, then ask — is the discipline being installed, and it applies wherever a person stands before something he did not compose and cannot control.

**The Reciter's Manner**

The sūrah's instruction also settles a question about how the Qur'an is to be recited. Haste of the tongue is prohibited, and the opposite is prescribed: *“or a little more—and recite the Quran ˹properly˺ in a measured way.”* (73:4). The word there is *tartīl*, recitation that is distinct, unhurried, and given its full measure of sound. A text that is recited slowly is a text the reciter is attending to, and the attention is part of the act. The opposite of rushing is not slowness for its own sake; it is allowing the words to arrive in the order and at the speed at which they were sent. Since the Qur'an describes itself as revealed in stages for exactly this reason — *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace.”* (17:106) — the manner of recitation reproduces the manner of revelation.

**The Student's Discipline**

The early commentators drew out a further application that the wording supports. One who hurries to master the words before their meaning has been settled will form conclusions on partial evidence, and a text revealed over twenty-three years could not be understood in any other way than by waiting for the whole. The instruction is therefore a rule for anyone reading: hold the question open until the passage is complete. The Qur'an makes the same point about itself in several places, insisting that it is a Book whose parts interpret one another and whose verses were perfected and then explained in detail (11:1). The rule reaches the listener as well. If the Prophet was told not to move his tongue ahead of the revelation, the obligation on those who hear it is no lighter: attend while it is recited, and let it finish before drawing conclusions. The Qur'an names the opposite practice and complains about it — *“The Messenger has cried, ‘O my Lord! My people have indeed received this Quran with neglect.’”* (25:30) — and it describes the proper response elsewhere as attending silence. Understanding is promised in the following verses as God's own work, which means the hearer's task is not to outpace the text but to stay with it. Haste in the tongue is only the visible form of a deeper impatience, and the cure for both is the same: let the Speaker finish.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:17

> It is certainly upon Us to ˹make you˺ memorize and recite it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Burden Is Transferred**

Having prohibited haste, the verse immediately supplies the reason why haste is unnecessary. The two tasks the Prophet was straining at — fixing the words in memory and producing them correctly — are taken over by the Speaker. *Inna ʿalaynā* is emphatic: upon Us, and no one else. The guarantee is not phrased as help offered to someone who is doing his best; it is phrased as a transfer of responsibility. The Prophet's anxiety about losing the revelation is answered by reassignment rather than reassurance, which is the stronger response. A promise of assistance leaves the person still carrying the thing; a declaration that it falls upon God removes it from him altogether.

**Gathering It in the Chest**

The first of the two tasks is *jamʿuhu*, its gathering, and the early authorities were specific about what was meant. Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as God's promise to gather the Qur'an in the Prophet's breast (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4928), and the same collection narrates him saying that the Messenger used to bear the revelation with difficulty and move his lips with it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5). The image is of scattered pieces being brought into one place inside a man, and it suits a revelation that arrived in fragments over twenty-three years — a few verses at a time, in response to events, questions, and circumstances no one could have scheduled. Two features of that gathering deserve attention. First, it occurred inside a man, not on a page: the primary vessel of the Qur'an in the first generation was the chest, and the writing down of the text ran alongside a memorisation that was never treated as secondary. Second, it was completed across the whole period of revelation, which means the promise covers the intervals as well as the moments of descent. A revelation that arrived in fragments required a gathering that ran continuously, and the verse promises that nothing was lost in the gaps between the fragments.

**Qurʾān: a Word That Is Also an Act**

The second term, *qurʾānahu*, is a verbal noun used here in its active sense: its reciting, the act of bringing it out. The word *qurʾān* is built on the root meaning to gather and to read aloud, and both senses are present in this one short verse — the gathering inside and the reciting outside. The grammatical point carries theological weight. The text is not called a book that must then be performed; it is called a recitation, an event of the mouth and the ear, which is why the Qur'an's own self-description insists on its oral character: *“Indeed, you ˹O Prophet˺ are receiving the Quran from the One ˹Who is˺ All-Wise, All-Knowing.”* (27:6). A scripture defined as recitation requires a guarantee that the recitation will be right, and that is exactly what this verse gives.

**The Promise of Preservation**

The assurance given here is stated elsewhere as a general promise covering the whole text: *“We will have you recite ˹the Quran, O Prophet,˺ so you will not forget ˹any of it˺, unless Allah wills otherwise.”* (87:6). The exception clause is important, since it prevents the promise from being read as mechanical; the guarantee is personal and continuing, not a once-for-all deposit. The same protection is claimed for the scripture itself: *“It is certainly We Who have revealed the Reminder, and it is certainly We Who will preserve it.”* (15:9). Taken together, the two verses describe a single arrangement in which the preservation of the text and the preservation of the reciter are the same divine act, and neither is left to the resources of the man who received it. The two promises also answer the two forms of loss that a revealed text can suffer. It can be lost through forgetting, which the gathering in the chest addresses, and it can be lost through alteration, which the recitation addresses, since a text that is constantly recited in public by many memorisers cannot be quietly revised by anyone. The Qur'an's own account of its preservation takes in both risks.

**What the Verse Removes**

Anxiety about the revelation did not end with the Prophet, and the verse speaks to everyone who has ever worried about retaining what he has learned. Read against its occasion, it establishes a principle about where the outcome of religious effort rests. The Prophet's diligence was not rebuked — he was hurrying to secure something precious, and diligence is not the thing being corrected. What was corrected was the assumption that the security of the text depended on his grip. The same relocation applies to the student who fears forgetfulness and the teacher who fears distortion. Effort belongs to the servant; the result is placed elsewhere, and the Qur'an is explicit about which party carries it.

**How the Community Understood It**

Muslim practice from the first generation onward has treated this verse as the basis of an institution rather than only as a comfort. If the collection of the Qur'an rests with God, then its preservation among human beings is maintained through human effort rather than in competition with it, and the community organised itself accordingly: recitation from memory, transmission by chain, and the writing down of what had been recited and checked. The Qur'an describes the text as held in honoured, purified pages in the hands of angel-scribes (80:13–16), and as *“a well-preserved Record”* (56:78). Those descriptions are not poetry about an ideal scripture. They are the theological account of why the copy in a child's hands and the copy in a museum in Samarkand are recognisably the same book. The practical consequence is that the community has never treated memorisation as a specialism. The science of variant readings, the chains of transmission, and the certificate a student receives to teach what he heard are all elaborations of one conviction: the gathering that this verse places with God is real, and human effort cooperates with it rather than replacing it.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:18

> So once We have recited a revelation ˹through Gabriel˺, follow its recitation ˹closely˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Follow Before You Recite**

The instruction establishes an order, and the order is the whole point. *Fa-ittabiʿ qurʾānah* — so follow its reciting — puts the Prophet in the position of follower rather than speaker. He is not to run alongside the revelation or ahead of it; he is to come after it. The sequence reverses what a person might expect from a messenger, since the natural assumption is that a prophet recites and others follow. Here the Prophet is told to follow, and only then to recite. His role at the moment of descent is to receive, and his role as reciter begins when the descent is finished. The text he will later deliver is something he first heard.

**Who Is Doing the Reciting**

The subject of the verb is God: *when We recite it*. The Qur'an attributes the recitation to its Author and treats the angel and the Prophet as the means through which it reaches human ears, and the early exegesis follows the text closely. Ibn ʿAbbās glossed this verse as an instruction to listen and be silent, and then explained that afterwards the Messenger would recite it as Gabriel had recited it to him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4928). The same narration fills in what the listening looked like in practice: whenever Gabriel came, the Prophet would listen, and when he departed he would recite as Gabriel had recited. The discipline described here became a yearly institution. Abū Hurayra reported that Gabriel used to repeat the recitation of the Qur'an with the Prophet once every year, and twice in the year in which he died, and a second narration in the same collection has Gabriel meeting him every night of Ramaḍān to review the Qur'an with him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4998; 6). The review was a listening as much as a reciting, with each party checking the other's reading. That is the discipline of this verse written into the community's calendar, and it explains why the annual revision of the text became a standing practice rather than an occasional one.

**Silence as the First Obedience**

The instruction to follow implies silence, and the implication was drawn out by the earliest commentators. To follow a recitation means not to contribute to it while it is happening — no finishing of phrases, no anticipation of endings, no simultaneous rehearsal. The prohibition in v. 16 and the instruction here are two halves of one discipline: the tongue is stopped so that the ear can work. The Qur'an states the same obligation to the believing community in a Madinan verse, where listening during recitation is commanded and silence is prescribed, with mercy attached to the practice. The principle reaches beyond the ritual setting. Before a person can respond to revelation, he has to have let it finish, and the willingness to let something finish is itself a form of obedience.

**Why the Sequence Matters Theologically**

There is a reason the order is fixed rather than incidental. If the Prophet had been permitted to recite along with the revelation, the distinction between what was revealed and what he produced would have been harder to maintain in practice, and the community's confidence in the text would have rested on his care rather than on the promise in v. 17. The sequence protects the text's origin. It also protects the Prophet from a burden he could not discharge, since a man cannot simultaneously attend to what is being said to him and secure it against loss. The divine recitation comes first, the human recitation second, and the second is described in the following verse as having its explanation supplied by the same Speaker.

**The Pace of the Descent**

The same principle governs how the revelation reached the community. It was not sent down in a single delivery but in stages, and the reason given for that arrangement is the reason given in this verse: *“˹We have sent it˺ as such ˹in stages˺ so We may reassure your heart with it. And We have revealed it at a deliberate pace.”* (25:32). The objectors wanted it all at once, on the ground that a scripture should arrive complete; the reply is that the pace was chosen for the recipient's sake. *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace.”* (17:106). The objectors who wanted the whole scripture at once were asking, in effect, for a text they could control — a document complete on delivery, available for consultation, usable on their own timetable. What they were given instead was a recitation that arrived in instalments and had to be followed. Listening is therefore built into the structure of the revelation rather than demanded only of its first hearer.

**A Rule for Reading**

The verse has always been read as a rule for anyone who opens the text, and the rule is simple to state and hard to keep. Follow the recitation: let the passage say what it says before deciding what it means, and let the whole of it be heard before any part is put to work. The opposite practice is the one the Qur'an complains about — a people who received the recitation with neglect (25:30) — and its most respectable version is the hurry of the student who reaches conclusions on partial evidence. The next verse completes the arrangement by assigning the explanation to God, which leaves the reader with the one task he can actually perform: attending until the Speaker has finished. Nothing in that instruction diminishes the reader's effort. It places the effort where it can do some good, which is after the text has been heard rather than during it.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:19

> Then it is surely upon Us to make it clear ˹to you˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Third ‘Upon Us’**

The passage has now made three promises in the same emphatic form, and the repetition is deliberate. The gathering of the text belongs to God, its recitation belongs to God, and its explanation belongs to God. Nothing in the transaction between the Speaker and the Messenger is left to the Messenger's resources. The third promise is also the one that completes the others, since a text perfectly preserved and perfectly recited would still be useless if its meaning were unavailable. The sūrah therefore ends this parenthesis by guaranteeing the thing that preservation alone cannot supply: understanding. The sequence — gather, recite, explain — describes the whole life of a revelation among human beings, and every stage of it is claimed by its Author.

**What Bayān Covers**

The word rendered as clarification is *bayān*, and it carries a wide range: making something distinct, separating it out, and stating it so that it is no longer ambiguous. It covers the meaning of a word, the ruling contained in a verse, and the application of a general principle to a particular case. The early authorities took it in this breadth. Ibn ʿAbbās explained that God would clarify the Qur'an through the Prophet's own tongue (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4928), which assigns the explanation to the same channel as the revelation without collapsing the two into one act. The verse thus supports the view that the Prophet's exposition of the text was itself part of what was promised, and the Qur'an states that office explicitly: *“And We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Reminder, so that you may explain to people what has been revealed for them, and perhaps they will reflect.”* (16:44).

**The Prophet as Exponent**

The guarantee in this verse is one reason the community has always treated the Prophet's explanation as authoritative rather than merely useful. If the clarification of the text was promised by the One who sent it, then the clarification is not one scholarly opinion among many. The Qur'an describes the Prophet's teaching office in the vocabulary of the same gift: he was sent to recite the revelations, to purify, and to teach the Book and wisdom (3:164; 62:2). The pairing is significant. The text is one thing and the wisdom of its application another, and both are attributed to instruction rather than to independent reasoning. That is the scriptural basis for the weight the sunnah carries in every school of Islamic law.

**Explanation Follows the Text, Not the Reverse**

The order of the three verbs matters again here. Explanation comes last, after the gathering and the recitation have been secured. A person cannot clarify what he has not first received and fixed, and the Qur'an never presents understanding as something that precedes submission to the text. The same ordering appears in its account of how the revelation reached its audience: it was sent down in stages and recited deliberately, and comprehension grew with the community's life under it. The Qur'an also warns against the reverse procedure, in which a part is seized and worked before the whole is known, and it treats that practice as a mark of those whose hearts are crooked rather than as a legitimate method. Explanation is a thing received in order, which is why the sūrah places it last. The Qur'an also describes itself as a book whose verses were made firm and then set out in detail (11:1), which is the same sequence stated as a property of the text rather than as a promise to the Prophet.

**Limits of the Promise**

The guarantee of clarification should not be read as a promise that every question will be answered, and the Qur'an prevents that misreading. Some matters are left undisclosed, and the withholding is presented as mercy rather than as a defect: *“O believers! Do not ask about any matter which, if made clear to you, may disturb you.”* (5:101). Knowledge of the Hour, of what the wombs carry, and of what a soul will earn tomorrow is withheld by name (31:34). What is promised in this verse is therefore not exhaustive exposition but sufficient clarity: the text will be explained to the extent that its guidance depends on explanation. A reader who treats every silence in the revelation as a gap to be filled by speculation has misunderstood the promise as much as one who treats it as licence to ignore the meaning.

**Why the Meaning Descends Too**

The verse settles a question that matters more than it first appears. If the text had been preserved but its meaning left to whoever came after, the scripture would have become whatever the most persuasive reader said it was, and the community's disputes would have had no court of appeal. By placing the *bayān* with the One who sent the text, the sūrah establishes that meaning is part of the revelation and not an addition to it. That is why the Qur'an insists on its own internal consistency as a mark of its origin (4:82), and why its description of itself as guidance is never separated from its description as a clarification. One further consequence follows. Because the clarification is promised by the Speaker, the text is not at the mercy of its readers' ingenuity, and the Qur'an's repeated challenge to produce a sūrah like it (2:23; 10:38) is not a challenge about style alone. A scripture whose meaning is guaranteed is a scripture that can be held to a standard, and the community has always read it that way: as a text that judges its interpreters rather than the reverse. The parenthesis closes here, and the sūrah returns to its subject: the same God who guarantees the meaning of His words will require an account of how they were received.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:20

> But no! In fact, you love this fleeting world,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Swings Back**

Four verses were spent instructing the Prophet about how to receive revelation, and now, with the particle *kallā*, the sūrah turns away from him and speaks to people in the plural. The shift is abrupt and it is meaningful. The parenthesis established that God guarantees His text; the accusation that follows is that the hearers do not want what the text offers. The two belong together. A people who loved what the revelation promised would not need to be told that they love something else, and the sūrah's structure puts the guarantee and the refusal side by side so that the refusal cannot be blamed on obscurity. The message has been made clear. The objection is not that it is unclear. The particle *kallā* appears three times in this short sūrah, and each appearance marks a turn in the argument rather than a simple negation. In v. 11 it closed the cry for escape; here it dismisses the whole posture of the person who receives the revelation and ignores it; later it will introduce the moment of death. The recurrence gives the sūrah its rhythm: a claim stated, a refusal marked, and the argument moved forward over the objection rather than around it.

**What the Word al-ʿĀjila Carries**

The word translated as fleeting is *al-ʿājila*, from a root meaning to hurry. The present world is named here for its speed rather than for its wickedness, and that is the whole diagnosis in a single adjective. What defines this life in the Qur'an's vocabulary is not that it is corrupt but that it is passing — it arrives quickly, it presses, it demands an answer now, and then it is gone. The opposite term, used throughout the Book for the next life, is *al-ākhira*, the later, the last. The pairing is temporal rather than moral. One is the thing in front of you that will not last; the other is the thing coming that will. To prefer the first is to prefer what is near over what is permanent, and the sūrah is accusing its hearers of exactly that calculation.

**The Charge Is Love, Not Preference**

The verb is *tuḥibbūna* — you love. Not you prefer, not you choose, not you overestimate. Love is the strongest word available, and it relocates the problem from judgement to affection. A person who miscalculates can be corrected with better information; a person who loves the wrong thing cannot, because the attachment is the obstacle rather than a consequence of it. This is consistent with the sūrah's earlier diagnosis. In v. 5 unbelief was traced to what a person *wants*, and here it is traced to what he loves. The argument of the sūrah runs through the will rather than through the intellect, which is why its appeals are almost entirely to what a person should fear and hope for rather than to what he should infer.

**The World Is Not Condemned Here**

Nothing in this verse, or in the Qur'an generally, requires contempt for created things. The lawful provisions and adornments God brought forth are declared to be for His servants in this life (7:32), and the sūrah of the Poets warns against the opposite error of withholding them. What is condemned is the ordering: *“Surely those ˹pagans˺ love this fleeting world, ˹totally˺ neglecting a weighty Day ahead of them.”* (76:27). The parallel verse in Sūrah al-Insān makes the structure explicit — loving the hasty thing and neglecting the weighty thing are two descriptions of one movement. A person does not have to hate the world to be ruined by it. He has to love it in the wrong position, ahead of the thing that outlasts it.

**Why Haste Is Fatal**

The danger in the adjective is that the hasty world does not feel temporary. It presses with the urgency of things that need deciding today, and the Hereafter, being absent, never presses at all. That asymmetry is the test. It is not a fair contest between two objects of equal presence, and the Qur'an's remedy is to make the absent thing present through remembrance, revelation, and the constant reminder of death. The sūrah has been doing precisely that for twenty verses — collapsing the sky, opening the record, closing the exits — so that the weighty Day acquires some of the urgency the hasty world has by default. The warning here is the conclusion of that effort: you are being hurried by a thing that is itself hurrying away. The Qur'an states the comparison in the plainest terms available: *“But you ˹deniers only˺ prefer the life of this world, even though the Hereafter is far better and more lasting.”* (87:16–17). Better and more lasting — the two adjectives are the argument. A person is not being asked to choose a worse thing out of duty. He is being told that he is choosing the smaller and the shorter, and the sūrah of Iron spells out why the near thing cannot bear the weight put on it: *“this worldly life is no more than the delusion of enjoyment.”* (57:20).

**Reordering, Not Renunciation**

If the disease is misordered love, the cure is not the destruction of the object but the reordering of the heart, and the Qur'an's prescriptions take that shape. Wealth is not abolished; it is put to work — *“You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish.”* (3:92) — and the test is precisely that the thing given is the thing loved. Time is not abolished; it is filled with remembrance at its two ends. The world is turned from a rival into a resource by being spent rather than accumulated. That is why the sūrah's complaint is followed immediately by a description of two faces: the ordering a person lives by now is the ordering that will be visible on him then.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:21

> and neglect the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Neglect, Not Denial**

The verb is decisive and it is not the verb of rejection. These are not people who have examined the claim and found it false; they are people who have left it aside. The distinction is the sūrah's most practical insight, because it relocates the danger from the ranks of the convinced to the ranks of the distracted. A declared disbeliever has at least taken a position on the question, and a position can be argued with. Neglect takes no position at all. It simply never gets around to the matter, and it is compatible with every outward observance, since a person can affirm the Day of Judgment in words and never once let the affirmation alter an arrangement in his life.

**The Anatomy of Neglect**

Neglect is an omission rather than an act, and that is what makes it so difficult to detect in oneself. One does not neglect something by attacking it; one neglects it by filling the space it would have occupied with other things. The diary fills up, the obligations multiply, and the question of how one stands before God is never answered because it is never quite reached. The Qur'an describes the same condition from the other end, in the words of those who arrive at the reckoning having left the Day behind them: *“those who took this faith ˹of Islam˺ as mere amusement and play and were deluded by ˹their˺ worldly life.” ˹Allah will say,˺ “Today We will ignore them just as they ignored the coming of this Day of theirs.”* (7:51). The correspondence is exact. The person who leaves God out of his account is left out of God's. The most severe statement of this exchange is put in God's own words: *“So taste ˹the punishment˺ for neglecting the meeting of this Day of yours. We ˹too˺ will certainly neglect you.”* (32:14). The symmetry is deliberate and it should be read carefully, because it identifies the precise fault. These are not people who denied the meeting; they neglected it, and what is returned to them is not a new penalty invented for the occasion but the same neglect, now fixed and irreversible.

**Behind, Not Ahead**

The parallel verse in Sūrah al-Insān sharpens the image, since the verb used there is the one for placing something behind one's back: *“Surely those ˹pagans˺ love this fleeting world, ˹totally˺ neglecting a weighty Day ahead of them.”* (76:27). They have not lost the Day; they have relocated it to a position where it cannot affect their sightline. The placement is deliberate rather than accidental, and it is maintained by effort, since the Day has a habit of reappearing at funerals, in illness, and in the quiet hours. Neglect is therefore not passivity but a low-grade, continuous activity: the work of keeping something at the back of the mind where it cannot be consulted.

**Why the Hereafter Loses the Contest**

The competition is inherently unequal, and the Qur'an is honest about the reason. The world is present, sensory, and shared by everyone around; the Hereafter is absent and known only by report. Under those conditions, attention will always drift toward what can be seen unless something compensates, and the compensating mechanism in the Qur'an is revelation plus remembrance. That is the practical function of the daily prayers, the recitation, and the constant return to these descriptions. The sūrah's own twenty-verse campaign to make the Day vivid is itself the counterweight being supplied. A person who stops feeding his attention on the absent will find, without any decision having been made, that he has stopped believing in it. The Qur'an describes this outcome as a form of knowledge that is truncated rather than false: *“They ˹only˺ know the worldly affairs of this life, but are ˹totally˺ oblivious to the Hereafter.”* (30:7). The remedy it prescribes is equally concrete, and it is not withdrawal from the world. Wealth and children are called the adornment of this life, *“but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope.”* (18:46).

**What Neglect Costs**

The cost is not only the loss of a future benefit; it is the deformation of the present. A person who never reckons with an end to his account lives without a standard by which to order his desires, and the result is that the nearest desire governs. The Qur'an's analysis of this drift is unsparing: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise.”* (6:44). Prosperity then confirms the neglect rather than curing it, because the neglect is invisible from inside a life that is going well. And the consequence on the other side is stated without softening: *“Undoubtedly, they will be sealed off from their Lord on that Day.”* (83:15).

**Two Loves Cannot Share One Place**

The verse is a clause attached to the previous one, and the grammar matters. Neglect of the Hereafter is presented as the consequence of love for the fleeting world, not as a second and separate fault. The heart has a fixed capacity for attachment, and what occupies it determines what it ignores. That is why the Qur'an never asks a person to hate the world and then to love God as a separate exercise. It asks him to spend what he loves (3:92), to remember at the ends of the day, and to put the weighty Day in front of him until it acquires the urgency it deserves. The sūrah will now show what the two orderings look like when they are finished: one face bright, the other gloomy.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:22

> On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why the Face**

The Qur'an summarises a person's whole destiny on his face, and the convention is used so consistently that it must be deliberate. The face is what a human being presents to others and cannot hide; it is where honour and humiliation are registered in every culture; and in Arabic usage the word also carries the sense of the person himself, the self that turns toward something. So when the sūrah divides humanity on that Day, it does not divide them by inventory of deeds or by location. It divides them by face, which is to say by what they have become. The division is stated rather than argued, because by this point in the sūrah there is nothing left to argue.

**Nāḍira: Freshness Rather Than Illumination**

The word rendered as bright is *nāḍira*, and its root is the one used for verdant, fresh, flourishing growth — a meadow after rain, skin at its best. It is not the ordinary word for shining, and the difference matters. A lamp shines from a source outside itself; a face that is *nāḍira* has the look of something in its own best condition. The brightness described here is therefore not light cast upon the person but the visible state of the person, which is why the same root appears in the Qur'an for the freshness of Paradise itself. The reward is not applied like paint. It is the appearance of a creature restored to what it was made to be, and the face is where the restoration shows.

**The Face as Standing**

Honour and disgrace are described on the face throughout the Qur'an, and the passages are consistent enough to fix the meaning. Of the guilty: *“Humiliation will cover them—with no one to protect them from Allah—as if their faces were covered with patches of the night’s deep darkness.”* (10:27). Of the same people in the sūrah of the Overwhelming, *“On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be downcast,”* (88:2). And the two classes are named together in one verse: *“On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy.”* (3:106). In each case the face registers what a person is worth in the only court that matters. The Qur'an is not describing cosmetics. It is describing dignity and its loss, visible at last without the disguises that status, wealth, and reputation supply in this life.

**Recognition, Not Transformation**

What happens on that Day is not the alteration of a face into something foreign to its owner. The Qur'an says elsewhere that the radiance is recognisable — *“You will recognize on their faces the glow of delight.”* (83:24) — and that those granted joy show it in the same place where everyone has always shown what he feels. Nothing here suggests a mask fitted to a face at the last moment. The sūrah of the Heights gives the same principle from the other side, where the people of the Garden describe what was removed before they entered: *“We will remove whatever bitterness they had in their hearts.”* (7:43). The removal of what corrupts and the appearance of what flourishes are one event, and the face is where it is legible. The face had been trained all along: by what it looked at, by what it was ashamed of, by the things it lit up at and the things it turned away from. The sūrah of the Night names the training in a sentence: *“But the righteous will be spared from it — who donate ˹some of˺ their wealth only to purify themselves,”* seeking *“the pleasure of their Lord, the Most High. They will certainly be pleased.”* (92:17–21). Spending for the sake of a countenance one has not seen is the discipline, and seeing it is the wage. A person who spent his life brightening at the mention of his Lord arrives with a face that does that naturally, and the Day removes the last thing preventing the correspondence between the inside and the outside.

**The Division the Sūrah Announces**

The sūrah introduces the two classes without preamble, and the brevity itself says something: no mechanics of the sorting are given, because by this point in the argument there is nothing procedural left to settle. No criterion is stated here beyond what has already been said: these are the people who loved the hasty thing and neglected the weighty one, and those are the people who did not. The Qur'an elsewhere names three rather than two groups — *“the people of the right, how ˹blessed˺ will they be; the people of the left, how ˹miserable˺ will they be; and the foremost ˹in faith˺ will be the foremost ˹in Paradise˺.”* (56:8–10) — which is a reminder that the division is not coarse. What the sūrah needs at this point is the pairing, because its next verse is about what the first group sees.

**Where the Radiance Comes From**

The Qur'an is careful to attribute the brightness to God rather than to the person's own achievement, and the parallel in Sūrah al-Insān states it directly: *“So Allah will deliver them from the horror of that Day, and grant them radiance and joy,”* (76:11). Radiance is granted. That is not a detail of phrasing but the theology of the whole passage: the face is bright because it has been turned toward its Lord and then looked upon favourably, not because it earned a good complexion. The same correspondence is stated from the other direction in the sūrah of the Overwhelming: *“On that Day ˹other˺ faces will be glowing with bliss, ˹fully˺ pleased with their striving, in an elevated Garden.”* (88:8–10). The faces are pleased because the striving was accepted, and the acceptance is God's act throughout. One's deeds are real, and the record is exact, but the radiance is a gift, and the sūrah's next verse will name the gift in a single clause that the commentators have argued over ever since.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:23

> looking at their Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reward Named in Two Words**

The sūrah has described the blessed faces, and the reward it names is neither a garden nor a drink nor companionship. It is a gaze: *ilā rabbihā nāẓira*, looking toward their Lord. The construction is explicit — the verb of looking followed by the preposition *ilā*, toward — and it is this preposition that has carried the weight of fourteen centuries of argument. The placement is what should strike a reader first. The Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise elsewhere are full of sensory detail, and the sūrah of al-Insān, revealed around the same period, gives silk, couches, silver vessels and springs in abundance. Here all of that is passed over in order to say that the faces are looking at their Lord, which makes the vision, on any reading, the summit of what is being promised.

**The Majority Reading and Its Evidence**

The great majority of commentators understood this as the literal vision of God in the Hereafter, and they did so on the strength of reports that are among the most securely transmitted in the corpus. Jābir's narration is explicit: the Prophet came out to his companions on the night of the full moon and said, *“You will see your Lord on the Day of Resurrection as you see this ˹full moon˺ and you will have no difficulty in seeing Him.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7436). A longer version in the same collection adds the comparison to the sun and moon when no cloud obscures them and the instruction to guard the prayer before sunrise and before sunset, which ties the vision to the practice of worship (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 554; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 633). The wording of the verse and the wording of the reports converge, and the preposition is doing ordinary work in both.

**The Grammatical Dispute**

The minority reading turns on that preposition. *Naẓara* with *ilā* can mean looking at, but the verb also means to wait, to expect, and to consider, and a reader who takes it that way renders the clause as *waiting for their Lord*, understood as awaiting His reward. The strength of this reading is lexical: the verb does carry that sense elsewhere in Arabic. Its weakness is contextual. The clause stands in apposition to *nāḍira*, radiant, which describes a face in a state of flourishing, and a face waiting for something does not explain its radiance, whereas a face seeing its Lord does. A second weakness is that the waiting reading has to supply an object — waiting *for* the reward — that the verse does not mention.

**The Argument From 6:103**

The stronger objection is theological rather than lexical. *“No vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision.”* (6:103) denies that sight comprehends God, and a reader who treats that verse as definitive must treat this one as figurative. Al-Zamakhsharī argued along these lines, and the discussion belongs to the larger question of how verses about God's attributes are to be handled. The majority answer distinguishes the predicates: the verb in 6:103 is *idrāk*, comprehending or overtaking, which is a claim about grasp, whereas the claim here is about sight. The hadith's own simile makes the same distinction, since the companions were not told they would understand the moon — only that seeing it involves no crowding and no doubt. Vision without encompassment is exactly what the reports describe. That reading is stronger, because it honours both texts without weakening either, and because a third passage settles where the vision is *not* available. When Moses asked to see God, the answer was *“You cannot see Me! But look at the mountain. If it remains firm in its place, only then will you see Me.”* (7:143). The mountain was levelled and Moses fell unconscious. A text that denies the vision in this life and promises it in the next is making a distinction the two parties both need: the limitation belongs to the creature in his present state, not to the object seen.

**Why the Vision Is the Highest Reward**

The spiritual commentators drew out why this should be the summit. The reward for obedience is Paradise; the reward for the realisation of God's oneness is the sight of Him, as one early authority put it, and the same writers connect the sight to love: whoever has been slain by love of God is repaid with the vision of Him. These statements are reports preserved in the tafsīr literature rather than hadith, and they are cited as such. What they capture is genuine to the Qur'an's own logic. Everything else promised to the blessed is a thing given; the vision is a presence, and the difference between the two is the difference between a gift and the Giver. A text that locates the highest reward in nearness rather than in possessions has told the reader what the whole religion is for.

**Correspondence Between the Life and the Sight**

The reward also fits the life that prepared for it, and the fit is the Qur'an's usual way of describing outcomes. Those who acted for the sake of God's countenance rather than for the thanks or repayment of people (76:9) are given the countenance itself. The sūrah opened by swearing by the self-reproaching soul, the faculty that keeps a person's account in front of him; it closes this passage with the people who kept that account honestly being brought into the presence of the One who was keeping it all along. The faces are radiant because they are looking at what they had been looking for, and the looking is described as continuing rather than as a single glance. Two conditions are named elsewhere and both fit. The first is awe: *“And whoever is in awe of standing before their Lord will have two Gardens.”* (55:46). The second is soundness of the organ itself: *“Only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺.”* (26:89). A heart that has been trained to look toward its Lord in absence is the heart that will look at Him in presence.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:24

> And ˹other˺ faces will be gloomy,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Face, and the Word for It**

The sūrah gives the two classes in the briefest possible form: faces radiant, and here, faces *bāsira*. The root of the second adjective is the one used for a contracted, scowling face — the face a person makes when something has gone badly wrong and cannot be undone. The two words are placed two verses apart with nothing in between, and the effect is a single image held up twice: the same humanity, divided by what shows on the face. Nothing is said about which group is larger. The Qur'an's interest here is not in census figures but in the fact that the division is real, visible, and permanent.

**Two Images: Dust and Darkness**

Other passages supply the detail that this verse abbreviates, and they use two different pictures. One is dust: *“while ˹other˺ faces will be dusty, cast in gloom”* (80:40–41). Dust is what covers a person who has been dragged, and in the Qur'an's idiom it is the mark of defeat and disgrace. The other picture is darkness: *“Humiliation will cover them—with no one to protect them from Allah—as if their faces were covered with patches of the night’s deep darkness.”* (10:27). Darkness is the loss of light rather than the addition of filth. Together the two images say that this face has both lost what lit it and acquired what disfigures it, and neither is applied from outside. The defilement and the darkening are what the person has become. A third passage adds the posture of the same people once the sentence is carried out: *“And whoever comes with an evil deed will be hurled face-first into the Fire. Are you rewarded except for what you used to do?”* (27:90). The face leads, and the question attached to it rules out complaint. What is happening to these faces is what they did, returned to them in a form they can no longer edit.

**What the Face Registers**

A face on that Day is registering a verdict, and the Qur'an is consistent about which verdict it is. These are people who had standing — tribe, wealth, followers, reputation — and none of it has been recognised. *“And all faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining. And those burdened with wrongdoing will be in loss.”* (20:111). The humiliation is not an additional punishment imposed on top of a sentence; it is the sentence, seen from the inside. A person who built his worth out of things that do not survive discovers, in a single moment and in public, that he has nothing to stand on, and the discovery is legible on his face before a word is spoken.

**Why the Face and Not the Body**

The Qur'an could have divided humanity by destination, by deed, or by the record placed in the right or the left hand — and elsewhere it does all of these. The choice of the face here is deliberate, because in Arabic usage the face is the part of a person that turns toward something. It is the organ of attention: what you face is what you are for. A life spent turned toward the hasty world produces a face that, when the world is gone, has nothing left to turn to. A life spent turned toward God produces a face that is at last looking at what it had been looking toward. The division in these verses is therefore not arbitrary. It is the visible form of the orientation a person lived by. The Qur'an states the same principle in the language of light and sight elsewhere: the blind and the seeing are not equal, nor are darkness and light, nor shade and heat, nor the dead and the living (35:19–22). Those pairings are not poetry about two groups of people. They are a claim about what a life does to the person living it, and the face is where the result becomes visible.

**The Faces of This Life Cannot Be Managed There**

One of the functions of status in this world is to control what other people see. A man can surround himself with people who will not contradict him, buy the appearance of composure, and ensure that his account of himself is the only one in circulation. Every one of those arrangements depends on an audience that can be managed, and the removal of that audience is part of what the Day does. *“On that Day all will follow the caller ˹for assembly˺, ˹and˺ none will dare to deviate. All voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate.”* (20:108). No crowd is available to be worked, and no face can be held in an expression it does not have. Pleading is removed as well: *“Then the disbelievers will neither be allowed to plead nor appease ˹their Lord˺.”* (16:84), and the witnesses who could have spoken for them are speaking against them (4:41). The management of appearances was never going to survive an occasion designed to end it, and the Qur'an is careful to say so in advance.

**A Warning Delivered in Advance**

The verse is a description of a future scene, and it is also the sūrah's most compact warning. The faces being described are not yet fixed, which is the entire reason for telling people about them now. The man who is reading this has a face that is still being formed by what he turns toward, and the formation is not complete until the breath stops. That is why the Qur'an pairs every description of these two classes with practical instruction somewhere nearby, and why the sūrah will follow this pair with a scene at a deathbed rather than with more scenery. The division is decided somewhere ordinary, in a life that looks like any other.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:25

> anticipating something devastating to befall them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Expectation Is Part of the Punishment**

The gloomy faces are not merely dark; they are described as knowing what is coming. The verb is one of reckoning and expectation, and in the Qur'an's eschatological vocabulary it is used for a confidence about the future rather than for a guess. These faces are fixed in an expression because their owners have already worked out what is about to happen to them. Nothing is left to hope for and nothing is left to find out. The significance of this detail is easy to miss: the punishment described here begins before it is administered. The interval between the certainty and the event is itself part of the sentence, which is why the verse attaches the anticipation to the faces rather than to the sentence that follows.

**Ẓann: Reckoning, Not Conjecture**

The word deserves a note, because in ordinary usage it can mean opinion or suspicion, and a reader might take it to mean that these people are merely worried. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the confidence of the blessed, which settles the sense: when the context is the Hereafter, the verb describes a settled expectation, whether of good or of harm. The people of the record given into the right hand say, *“I surely knew I would face my reckoning.”* (69:20). The same certainty, the same verb, applied in the other direction, is what darkens the faces in this verse. What distinguishes the two groups is not that one knows and the other guesses. It is what each has become certain about. There is a further detail in the grammar: the expectation is attributed to the faces themselves, in the feminine singular, matching the singular noun for face used in the previous verse. The dread belongs to the person, not to the scene. This is not a description of a frightening situation viewed from outside but of a condition the sufferer carries with him, which is the only way the Qur'an ever describes punishment: as something a person has become rather than something happening around him.

**Al-Fāqira: the Back-Breaking Calamity**

The noun is built on a root meaning to break the spine, and it belongs to the vocabulary of catastrophic disaster rather than of ordinary misfortune. The image is of a blow that leaves a person unable to stand, and its application here is to something that will befall the faces rather than merely be seen by them. The Qur'an uses physical severity in this way deliberately: an abstract penalty would not convey the total character of the event. This is not a setback to be absorbed and survived. It is the kind of catastrophe after which the person who suffers it is not the person he was, and the faces in this verse are turned toward it in advance. The spine is what holds a person upright, both physically and in the Qur'an's idiom of dignity, so a blow that breaks the back is a blow that removes the ability to stand. The same people describe their condition once the calamity has landed, and their account of it is instructive: *“My wealth has not benefited me! My authority has been stripped from me.”* (69:28–29). What breaks is precisely what they had been standing on.

**The Pain of Knowing Beforehand**

Scripture is candid that the interval is part of the suffering, and it describes the physiological shape of the dread in more than one place: *“Warn them ˹O Prophet˺ of the approaching Day when the hearts will jump into the throats, suppressing distress. The wrongdoers will have neither a close friend nor intercessor to be heard.”* (40:18). A second passage gives the same moment from the standpoint of those who can see where the path ends: *“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it.”* (18:53). Knowing, in these scenes, is not a mitigation. It is the beginning of the thing known, and there is no longer any route by which the knowledge can be put to use.

**Why Certainty Is Not Mercy Here**

A person might suppose that certainty would be kinder than suspense, since at least the waiting would be over. The Qur'an's answer is that certainty is only a relief when it can be acted on. Knowledge of a verdict one cannot appeal, cannot flee, and cannot bargain with does not shorten anything; it removes the last occupation the mind had, which was the arrangement of arguments and hopes. The faces in this verse have run out of those. *“And warn the people of the Day when the punishment will overtake ˹the wicked among˺ them, and the wrongdoers will cry, ‘Our Lord! Delay us for a little while. We will respond to Your call and follow the messengers!’”* (14:44) — and the reply they receive is a reminder that they had once sworn none of this would happen.

**The Two Certainties Side by Side**

The sūrah has now put the two classes in front of the reader with their inward states attached, and the parallel is exact. The radiant faces are looking at their Lord; the gloomy faces are looking at what is about to break them. Both groups possess certainty, both are past the stage of argument, and both have arrived at the state produced by what they did. The difference is everything, and it was settled long before the Day. The pairing also rules out a sentimental reading of either side. The radiant faces were not spared the reckoning — they were radiant *at* it, and the sūrah of the Heights shows them saying that the promise was true and that they had been given an inheritance for what they did (7:43). The gloomy faces are not unfortunate. They are accurate. That is the last thing the sūrah will say about the Hereafter before turning to the deathbed, where the same two destinies are decided one person at a time.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:26

> But no! ˹Beware of the day˺ when the soul reaches the collar bone ˹as it leaves˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third kallā, and a Change of Scale**

The sūrah has used this particle twice already, each time to mark a refusal and turn the argument. Here it introduces something new in kind: after forty-odd verses about the end of the world, the scale collapses to a single room and a single dying body. The movement is characteristic of the Qur'an. Eschatology is never allowed to remain a subject about the distant future, because every human being meets his own version of it at death, and the sūrah has been building toward that convergence. What the Hour does to the cosmos, death does to the person — and for the person, the second is the one that comes first.

**Tarāqī: the Point of Exit**

The word rendered as collarbones is *al-tarāqī*, the plural of a noun for the bone at the top of the chest, and it denotes the hollow above the collarbone where the throat meets the torso. The picture is precise: the soul has risen through the body to the point of leaving. The Qur'an uses the same image in the sūrah of the Inevitable Event — *“Why then ˹are you helpless˺ when the soul ˹of a dying person˺ reaches ˹their˺ throat,”* (56:83) — and the two passages share the assumption that dying is a process with a describable anatomy rather than a switch. Nothing in the verse requires the reader to locate the soul physically. The point is the threshold: the moment at which the person is still here and about not to be. The early commentators described this as the point at which the soul is drawn out of the body, and they disagreed about the manner of the drawing rather than about its reality: the soul of the believer leaving easily, like water from a waterskin, and the soul of the denier dragged out as a skewer is pulled through wet wool. Those descriptions are reports preserved in the tafsīr literature rather than hadith with secured chains, and they are cited here as such. What they agree on is the verse's own claim: death is a transition that happens to a person, not a state he simply enters.

**Why the Description Is Physical**

There is a reason the Qur'an describes death in this register instead of speaking only of the soul's departure. A death is an event that happens in a room, at a known hour, in front of people who can describe it, and the Qur'an anchors its teaching in events that can be witnessed rather than in abstractions. The angels are described taking the soul at exactly this point in another sūrah — *“When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul, never neglecting this duty.”* (6:61) — which places the visible moment and the invisible agent in the same sentence. The dying person is not alone in the room, though the people around him cannot see who else is present.

**Two Readings of kallā Here**

The commentators divided over the particle's force in this verse, and the division is not trivial. Read as negation, it dismisses something said or assumed just before: the matter is not as the deniers suppose, and the soul will reach the throat. Read as affirmation, it is an emphatic *indeed*, opening a new scene: truly, when the soul reaches the collarbones, such and such will happen. The negation reading fits the particle's use earlier in the sūrah, where it answers a claim. The affirmative reading fits the grammar of a conditional sentence that follows it. The negation reading is stronger here, since the sūrah has spent its preceding verses describing people who deny the very thing this scene will force on them, and the particle has been the instrument of that refusal each time.

**The Moment Denial Becomes Impossible**

What happens at that threshold, on either reading, is the end of the argument the sūrah has been conducting since v. 3. The denier's position depended on the absence of the thing he denied; at death the thing arrives, and the Qur'an describes the arrival as an unveiling rather than as a revelation of new facts: *“You were totally heedless of this. Now We have lifted this veil of yours, so Today your sight is sharp!”* (50:22). Nothing is added to the person's knowledge. What is removed is the arrangement that allowed him to live as though he did not have it. That is why death is called a certainty rather than a risk, and why the Qur'an treats preparation for it as the whole of practical religion.

**The Hour and the Private Hour**

The sūrah's structure now becomes visible. It opened with an oath by the Day of Resurrection, moved through the collapse of the sky, the opening of the record, and the two faces, and it has arrived at the deathbed of one person. The two subjects are not separate. For the dying man, his own death and the Hour are the same event, since the interval between them will not be experienced. The same logic governs the interval between the two. The Qur'an states that there is a barrier set behind the dead until the Day they are raised (23:100), so the period between a person's death and the general resurrection is real, but it is not an interval he experiences as waiting. He dies, and then it is the Day. That collapse of distance is why the sūrah can move in four verses from the gathering of the sun and moon to a man's collarbone, and why its argument never needed to apologise for the transition.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:27

> and it will be said, “Is there any healer ˹who can save this life˺?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Asked in a House**

The scale has changed completely. Five verses ago the sūrah was dividing humanity at the Resurrection; now it reports a sentence spoken in a room where someone is dying. The sentence is short, ordinary, and desperate: is there anyone who can heal him? The Qur'an's choice to record a domestic cry rather than a pronouncement is part of its method. The end of the world is taught through the end of a person, and the end of a person is taught through the words his family actually say. Nobody in that room is thinking about theology. They are looking for help, and the question is the form that looking takes when the ordinary options have run out.

**What the Word Carries**

The term rendered here as healer is built on the root for incantation, and in the Arabic of the period it covered the practices of the healer who recited charms as well as the physician. That breadth is what makes the question so stark. It is not asking whether a particular treatment has been tried; it is asking whether there is any route, physical or otherwise, by which this death can be averted. The implied answer is no, and the form of the question supplies it: a question asked aloud in a house where everyone already knows the answer is not an inquiry. It is the last sound of hope, and the sūrah records it without comment because no comment would improve on it.

**A Second Reading: the Angels Speak**

Several commentators took the sentence differently and read it as the question of the angels rather than of the bystanders, on the basis that the same root can carry the sense of ascending: *who will take his soul up?* The grounds for this reading are lexical and contextual — the next verses describe a departure and a driving to the Lord, and the angels are described elsewhere as ascending with the soul. Some specified that the question is asked by the angels of mercy in the case of a believer and the angels of punishment in the case of a denier, which would make the sentence a division between two kinds of death. The reading is coherent, but the majority position is stronger, because the verb as used here is the ordinary word for healing, because the question fits the scene of a deathbed, and because the following verse's certainty about the parting reads more naturally as the dying person's awareness than as an angel's report.

**Everything That Could Be Tried Has Been Tried**

The pathos of the question lies in what precedes it. By the time anyone asks, the medicine, the money, the physicians, and the prayers of the household have all been exhausted. The Qur'an is precise elsewhere about who actually rescues people from the edge: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Who rescues you from the darkest times on land and at sea? He ˹alone˺ you call upon with humility, openly and secretly: “If You rescue us from this, we will be ever grateful.”’”* (6:63). The verse identifies the Rescuer, and it also identifies the pattern — a vow made at the edge and forgotten once the shore is reached. The question in this sūrah is asked at the one edge from which no one is brought back. What distinguishes that edge is not the failure of medicine but the arrival of the agents who take the soul: *“When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul, never neglecting this duty. Then they are ˹all˺ returned to Allah—their True Master.”* (6:61–62). Every death is thus described as a handing over, and the question in the room is being asked of the wrong party. The people who love the dying man have no jurisdiction over the thing that is happening, which is what makes the scene unbearable to read.

**Why the Sūrah Descends to This Scale**

The movement from cosmic catastrophe to a bedroom is not a change of subject but a tightening of the same one. A person can hold the Resurrection at arm's length because it is distant, public, and shared; he cannot do that with his own death, which is near, private, and his alone. By placing the deathbed inside the argument about the Day, the sūrah removes the distance that made denial comfortable. The sun and moon gathered, the record opened, and the faces divided are not a separate story from the man on the bed. For him they follow immediately, and the sūrah's next three verses will trace the sequence from the question to the arrival without a pause.

**Where the Help Actually Was**

The question is asked at the point where it can no longer be answered, and the Qur'an's purpose in recording it is to relocate the asking. Everything that could have been done for this man is a thing that had to be done earlier: the belief, the prayer, the giving, and the turning back that the sūrah has been urging from its opening verses. The door is not closed by God at the deathbed; it closes because the interval in which it was open has ended. That is the plain sense of the sūrah's warning, and it is why the Qur'an keeps returning to the same instruction while breath remains: *“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53). The verse of Yā Sīn's successors puts the same point as an inventory of what will not help on the day it matters: *“the Day when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit, only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺.”* (26:88–89). Wealth and children are the two things a household can bring to a deathbed. Neither can be brought further than the door.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:28

> And the dying person realizes it is ˹their˺ time to depart,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Certainty Arrives at Last**

The verb is the strongest available in Arabic for knowing: he became certain, with the settled knowledge that excludes doubt. After a sūrah full of people who mocked, supposed, reckoned, and asked when, the dying man arrives at *yaqīn*. The irony is complete and it is not gentle. Certainty was the one thing that would have changed everything, and it is granted at the exact moment when it can no longer be spent. The sūrah's whole diagnosis was that the obstacle lay in the will rather than in the evidence; here the evidence becomes unanswerable, and the will is given nothing left to do with it.

**What He Is Certain About**

The object of the certainty is *al-firāq*, the parting — the separation of what has been joined. The commentators took this in two directions, and both are true at once. The dying person knows that he is parting from the world: from the property he assembled, the people he loved, the body he lived in, and the version of himself that existed only among others. He also knows that the parting is the separation of soul from body, which the previous verse located at the collarbones. The word holds the two senses together without strain, and that is part of why the Qur'an chose it. Death is one event described from two sides, and the person undergoing it is the only one who experiences both. What makes the parting bitter is also stated plainly: the things being left are the things that were made to look desirable — *“The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people.”* (3:14). A person does not part easily from what he has spent his life being attracted to, and the Qur'an's remedy is not indifference to those things but the knowledge that the finest destination lies elsewhere.

**The Knowledge That Comes Too Late**

The Qur'an is unsparing about the timing, and the point is repeated often enough to be a principle. A person at the threshold knows, asks to be returned, promises to act differently, and is not returned: *“If only you could see when they will be detained before the Fire! They will cry, ‘Oh! If only we could be sent back, we would never deny the signs of our Lord.”* (6:27). The knowledge is real and the desire is real, and neither is of any use, because the thing that made them useful — a span of time in which to act — is precisely what has ended. The Qur'an's practical conclusion is obvious and it is stated elsewhere with equal plainness: whatever certainty a person is waiting for, he should get it while it can still be lived out.

**The Will and the Intellect Finally Agree**

The sūrah traced unbelief in v. 5 to what a person wants, and it has spent twenty-three verses describing the consequences of that wanting. At the deathbed, wanting and knowing coincide for the first time — the man no longer wants to deny, and he no longer can. That convergence is worth noticing, because it shows that the sūrah was never describing an intellectual failure that more evidence could have cured. Evidence arrives here in overwhelming quantity, and what it produces is regret rather than conversion. Faith, in the Qur'an's account, is a thing a person does with the interval he has been given, not a conclusion he reaches when the interval closes. The request made at that moment is always for a little more of it: *“My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous.”* (63:10), and the answer is that no soul is delayed once its appointed time arrives (63:11). The man in this verse is not asking for more time because he has ceased to believe. He is asking because he believes perfectly well, and has discovered that belief without a remaining interval is not worth having.

**Clarity at the Threshold**

The scene also matches what the Qur'an says about the removal of the veil. *“You were totally heedless of this. Now We have lifted this veil of yours, so Today your sight is sharp!”* (50:22). The dying person's perception is described as sharpened rather than as newly informed, which means the clarity is not an addition of data but the removal of the interests that had been blurring it. Reports in the tafsīr literature describe the dying seeing the angels who have come for them, and these are cited in that literature rather than as authenticated narrations. What is safe to say from the Qur'an itself is that the moment is one of unveiled sight, and that the sight is not given as a gift. It is the absence of everything that had been blocking it.

**The Same Certainty, the Other Face**

Certainty at death is not always a terror, and the Qur'an gives the counterpart so that the reader does not take this verse as a statement about dying as such. To the tranquil soul the address runs: *“Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺.”* (89:28). And of those whom the angels take in a good state: *“Peace be upon you! Enter Paradise for what you used to do.”* (16:32). The same *yaqīn*, the same parting, the same threshold — and the content of the certainty is what a life has made it. The sūrah's next move is to follow the body from the bed to the grave and then to the driving, and it does so without pausing for comment.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:29

> and ˹then˺ their feet are tied together ˹in a shroud˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Leg Joined to Leg**

The verse is four words in Arabic and it has produced a long list of readings. *Al-sāq* is the shank, the part of the leg between knee and ankle, and *iltaffa* means to become entwined or wrapped around. The plainest picture is of the two legs brought together and bound, and the question the commentators argued over is what that binding means. The sūrah gives no explanatory clause, which is why the readings multiply: every interpreter has to supply a context, and each supplies the one his reading of the surrounding verses suggests.

**The Reading That Fits the Sequence**

The majority took it as the shroud. The legs of the dead are brought together and wrapped in the winding sheet, and the verse then belongs to a sequence that is easy to follow: the soul reaches the collarbones, the question is asked, the certainty arrives, and the body is wrapped. This reading has the immediate context on its side, since the sūrah has been describing the physical progress of a death from v. 26 onward, and the binding of the legs is the next physical thing that happens to that body. It also has the idiom on its side: Arabic poetry uses the phrase for the wrapping of the corpse, and the early authorities reported that sense without hesitation. On this reading the verse completes the deathbed scene and hands the body over to burial.

**The Readings That Read It as Hardship**

A second group of commentators took *al-sāq* in its idiomatic sense of severity, since Arabic uses the baring of the shank for a matter that has become grave, and read the verse as the joining of one hardship to another: the agony of leaving this world entwined with the agony of entering the next. Variations on this reading make it the last day of this life joined to the first day of the next, or death joined to life, or the general confusion of a day on which people's legs are tangled and they cannot move. The strength of this family of readings is that it takes the idiom seriously and gives the verse a moral rather than a merely physical content. Its weakness is that it must supply the hardships being joined, since the text names none, whereas the shroud reading needs nothing supplied.

**Weighing Them**

The shroud reading should be preferred, and the reason is contextual rather than lexical. Every verse from 26 onward has been tracking the body: collarbones, the question at the bedside, the certainty, and then, three verses later, the driving to the Lord. A description of the corpse being wrapped sits naturally in that series, while a moral abstraction about two hardships arriving together does not. The objection to discarding the second reading entirely is that the Qur'an itself uses *sāq* in the sense of a grave and unavoidable matter: *“˹Beware of˺ the Day the Shin ˹of Allah˺ will be bared, and the wicked will be asked to prostrate, but they will not be able to do so,”* (68:42). The lexicographers connect that usage to the image of a man rolling up his garment to his shank for a task that has become serious, which is the same idiom the commentators invoked here. That said, the two are not exclusive in effect. The Qur'an frequently uses a physical detail that carries a moral charge, and the binding of a dead man's legs in a shroud is not an image anyone finds neutral. The body is being prepared for the grave at the very moment the sūrah is insisting that the grave is not the end.

**The Body Is Not Discarded**

This is the point the sūrah's argument has been building since v. 3. The objector asked whether the bones could be gathered, and the reply was that even the fingertips could be restored. Everything since then has assumed the importance of the body: the soul leaves it at the collarbones, the certainty is located in the dying person, and the body is wrapped with care and buried. The Qur'an does not treat the corpse as a husk to be shed. It is the same body that will be raised, which is why the sūrah can move without embarrassment from a man's legs being tied in a sheet to his being driven to his Lord. Resurrection is not the replacement of a body but the reassembly of one. The sūrah said as much at the outset, when it met the objection about crumbled bones with the claim that even the fingertips can be restored, and the Qur'an shows the same principle enacted for a man who doubted it: *“And look at the bones ˹of the donkey˺, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh!”* (2:259). The corpse being wrapped in this verse is therefore not a discarded shell. It is the material of the argument the sūrah opened with, and the body that will answer for the life lived in it.

**Burial, and What Follows Immediately**

The sequence also compresses time in the way the Qur'an does elsewhere. Between the shroud and the driving there is, in ordinary experience, an interval that may last centuries; in the sūrah's account the two are joined by *on that Day*, and the barrier that is set behind the dead until the resurrection (23:100) is not an interval the dead experience. The grammar is doing theology here. A person dies, is wrapped, and is driven to his Lord, and nothing in the sūrah's telling allows the reader to insert a comfortable delay between the grave and the reckoning.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:30

> On that day they will be driven to your Lord ˹alone˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Driven, Not Asked**

The noun rendered as driving, *al-masāq*, comes from a verb meaning to urge animals forward, and the choice of verb is deliberate. Nobody in this scene is walking voluntarily. The dying man who became certain in v. 28 did not then get up and go; he is conducted. That is consistent with everything the sūrah has said about the closure of options: no refuge, no escape, no plea, and now no choice about the journey. The verb is not inherently violent — it is the ordinary word for driving a mount — but it does place the person in the position of the one being moved rather than the one deciding.

**The Same Verb for Both Destinies**

What makes the word interesting is that the Qur'an uses it for the saved as well. The disbelievers are *“driven to Hell in ˹successive˺ groups”* (39:71), and the mindful are described in the parallel verse with the same verb: *“And those who were mindful of their Lord will be led to Paradise in ˹successive˺ groups.”* (39:73). The verb is identical in both. It describes the conduct of the soul to its destination and says nothing about which destination that is, which is exactly the point of using it here. The sūrah is not yet saying where this person is being taken. It is saying that he is being taken, and that the taking is not his to arrange. The verb also carries a claim about deserts. The groups in those two passages are not processed anonymously: the disbelievers arrive to opened gates and a question about whether messengers had come to them (39:71), while the mindful arrive to gates already open and a greeting of peace (39:73). The driving is the same; what differs is what the person has made of the interval before it, and the sūrah has spent thirty verses describing how that interval is spent.

**To Your Lord, and Not to a Place**

The preposition is the one the sūrah used in v. 12 — *ilā rabbika*, to your Lord — and the repetition brackets the death scene inside the same claim about the end of the journey. Both times the destination is named as a Person rather than a location, and both times the address is to the Prophet, whose Lord is everyone's Lord. The effect of the repetition is to fold death and resurrection into one movement. What the Hour does to the whole of creation and what death does to one man are the same event in two scales, and both end at the same place: not at a gate or a garden or a fire, but in front of the Lord who then assigns.

**Why the Prophet Is Addressed Again**

The possessive matters for the sūrah's polemic. The people who mocked the message are now being driven to the Lord of the man they mocked, and the Qur'an loses no opportunity to make that identification. It is the same strategy as the mockery question itself: the objectors asked when, and were told before whom. Nothing in the verse softens what it says, and the sūrah does not pause to gloat. The point is simply that the argument the Prophet's opponents were having was never really with him. It was with his Lord, and the driving settles which of the two parties was in a position to set terms.

**On That Day: the Compression Continues**

The phrase *on that day* refers most immediately to the day of the death just described, and it is the same phrase the sūrah used in v. 12 for the Day of Resurrection. The double reference is not carelessness. The Qur'an states plainly that a barrier is set behind the dead until they are raised (23:100), so the interval between a person's death and the general resurrection is real; but it is not an interval the dead person experiences, and the sūrah's grammar reflects that. For the dying man, the shroud and the arrival are separated by nothing at all. The driving also has agents, and the Qur'an names them: *“Each soul will come forth with an angel to drive it and another to testify.”* (50:21). One conducts and one bears witness, which means the journey described in this verse is neither solitary nor unrecorded. The person being driven is accompanied by the record of what he did, and the two angels who were assigned to him in life are the two who present him at the end. Nothing in the scene is improvised, and nothing about it is being decided on the way.

**The Answer to the Cry for Escape**

The sūrah asked in v. 10 where escape might be found, and answered in v. 11 that there is none. This verse completes the thought by saying where the person is going instead. There is no refuge, and there is no alternative destination; the driving is to the Lord, and the only question left is the one the sūrah's final verses will put: whether the Lord admits a person to His mercy or has prepared for him a painful punishment. Everything between the bedside and that decision is conducted by Someone else, which is the last thing a person has to reckon with before the journey begins. One further note on the word: *masāq* is a noun of place and time as well as of action, so the phrase names not only the driving but the place driven to and the day of it. The sūrah has used the same economy of language before — *mustaqarr* in v. 12 named the settling and the place of settling at once — and the effect is to compress the event, its location and its hour into a single word. Nothing about the end is described at length, and nothing about it is uncertain.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:31

> This denier neither believed nor prayed,

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Deathbed to a Case Study**

The sūrah has followed one death from the collarbones to the driving, and now it names the kind of person whose death that was. The shift to the third person is significant: the previous scene was a particular deathbed, and this verse generalises it into a type. Anyone who matches the description should recognise himself in it. The Qur'an uses this device repeatedly — a concrete scene, then the category it instantiates — because a particular death can be contemplated with detachment, whereas a description one might fit cannot. The sūrah is moving from reporting to diagnosis.

**Two Failures, Not One**

The charge has two halves, and both are needed. *He did not affirm the truth*, and *he did not pray*. The first is the inward act — accepting the message as true, trusting the Messenger, assenting to what the revelation said about the Day. The second is the outward act — the worship that belongs to the One whose truth has been accepted. The verse thereby excludes half-measures in both directions. A person who prays while denying the Day has not done the first; a person who concedes the Day in words and never turns to his Lord has not done the second. The sūrah has already insisted that unbelief is a matter of what a person wants, and here it adds that what a person wants shows up in what he does with his body at the appointed times.

**A Second Reading of the First Verb**

Some commentators took the first verb in its other sense and read the verse as *he did not give in charity*, since the root that means to affirm the truth also means to give alms — the connection being that almsgiving is the verification of faith. The grounds for this reading are strong. The Qur'an uses the root in both senses, and the parallel passage in Sūrah al-Muddaththir names exactly these two failures as the self-description of the people in the Fire: *“They will reply, ‘We were not of those who prayed, nor did we feed the poor.’”* (74:43–44). On that reading the verse pairs worship of God with care for His creatures, which is the pairing the Qur'an uses constantly. The reading is coherent and well supported; the majority held the first sense on the ground that the sūrah's context is denial rather than stinginess, and that is the stronger reading here, though the two converge in practice, since a person who affirms the truth gives. The convergence is visible in the Qur'an's own definitions. Righteousness is described as believing, *“and give charity out of ˹their˺ cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives; who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make”* (2:177). Belief, worship, and giving appear in one sentence because the Qur'an does not treat them as separate departments.

**The Confession of the Inhabitants of the Fire**

The parallel in al-Muddaththir is worth following, because it supplies the full catalogue that this verse abbreviates. The inhabitants of the Fire are asked what put them there, and they answer with four items: *“We were not of those who prayed, nor did we feed the poor. We used to indulge ˹in falsehood˺ along with others, and deny the Day of Judgment, until the inevitable came to us.”* (74:43–47). Neglect of worship, neglect of the poor, absorption in the talk of the crowd, and denial of the Day — and then the arrival of the certainty that ends the argument. The sūrah's two failures are the first two items in that list, and the order is the same in both passages.

**Why Denial Takes This Shape**

It is worth asking why disbelief should be described through worship and charity rather than through doctrine. The answer is that the Qur'an does not treat the Day of Judgment as a hypothesis to be evaluated. It treats it as a claim that, if true, reorganises a life: a person who believes he will be asked about what he owns gives, and a person who believes he will stand before his Lord prays. Where those two are absent, the belief is not present either, whatever the mouth says. The sūrah has already located unbelief in the will; here it locates the evidence of that unbelief in the two acts that a living faith produces as a matter of course. The opposite profile is drawn just as concretely, and it is drawn in terms of what a person is not distracted from: *“by men who are not distracted—either by buying or selling—from Allah’s remembrance, or performing prayer, or paying alms-tax.”* (24:37). The difference between the two types is not intelligence, temperament, or opportunity. It is what each has allowed to occupy him, and the occupation is visible long before the deathbed.

**A Profile to Be Checked, Not a Curse**

The verse is addressed to the living, and its purpose is diagnostic rather than merely condemnatory. A person can examine the two items honestly and find out where he stands, which is the use the Qur'an intends for every description of the doomed. Nothing in the wording suggests that these failures are beyond repair before death; the sūrah has said the opposite, holding out forgiveness while breath remains (39:53). What the verse rules out is the comfort of thinking that a person can neglect both worship and his obligations to others and still be counted among those who affirmed the truth. The next verse will add the disposition behind the neglect. What this verse establishes on its own is that the reckoning turns on two ordinary things rather than on anything exotic: a person's assent to the truth and his attendance on his Lord. No one will be asked at the driving whether he had mastered an argument. The question will be about what he did with the two items named here, and the Qur'an has stated the question in advance so that no one arrives surprised by it.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:32

> but persisted in denial and turned away,

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Omissions to Commissions**

The previous verse named two things this person did not do: he did not affirm the truth, and he did not pray. This verse names two things he did do. The movement from omission to commission is deliberate, and it traces the ordinary progress of unbelief. Neglect is where it starts — a person simply never gets around to the matter — but neglect does not stay still. Once the reminders have been ignored often enough, ignoring them hardens into a verdict about them, and the person moves from not attending to actively rejecting. The sūrah is describing a trajectory rather than a pair of faults, and the next verse will carry it one step further. The distinction also settles a question about responsibility. Omissions can be excused by weakness, forgetfulness, or pressure; commissions cannot, because they require a person to have acted. By pairing the two, the sūrah removes the excuse before it can be offered. This man is not being condemned for failing to be attentive. He is being condemned for having reached a conclusion and then conducted himself in accordance with it.

**Takdhīb: Declaring It False**

The first verb is from the root that means to lie and, in this form, to declare something a lie. It is stronger than disbelief and different in kind. A person who does not believe may simply have never considered the claim; a person who declares it false has considered it and passed judgement. That is the significance of the root's constant appearance in the Qur'an's accounts of the messengers' audiences: what was rejected was not an unexamined proposition but a demonstrated one, and the rejection took the form of a counter-claim. *“Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say, ‘Ancient fables!’”* (83:13). Calling the message old stories is a verdict on it, not an absence of information about it. The Qur'an goes further and states that the verdict can be reached against the person's own conviction: *“And, although their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly.”* (27:14). Denial here is not the absence of belief but its suppression, and the motive named is arrogance rather than doubt. That single verse settles the sūrah's underlying claim: what the denier lacks is not evidence but the willingness to be the kind of person the evidence would make him.

**Tawallā: the Turn of the Back**

The second verb is physical. *Tawallā* means to turn away, to give something one's back, and the Qur'an uses it wherever it wants to describe a deliberate withdrawal rather than a gradual drift. The pairing of the two verbs is fixed in the Qur'an's vocabulary — *“who deny and turn away”* (92:16) — and the order is always the same: a verdict is formed, and then the person turns his back on what he has judged. The image is of a man who has decided and now physically removes himself from the place where the thing is being said. The decision comes first and the departure follows, which is why the Qur'an treats rejection as an act of the will rather than a failure of the evidence. The same point is made to the Prophet in a verse of consolation, and it identifies what was actually being rejected: *“It is not your honesty they question—it is Allah’s signs that the wrongdoers deny.”* (6:33). The objection was never to the messenger's character, which was not in dispute; it was to the claim being made on their lives. Turning away is the physical form of that refusal.

**Why Denial Is Not Ignorance**

The sūrah's whole account rests on this distinction, and it is worth stating plainly because the alternative reading is common. If unbelief were ignorance, the remedy would be instruction, and a person could plead that he had never been told. The Qur'an forecloses that plea in the strongest terms: a man who has heard the message, understood it, and declared it a lie has no such defence, and his responsibility is precisely located in the verdict he reached. The Qur'an describes the hardening that produces such verdicts as something a person does to himself over time — *“their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit!”* (83:14) — and the staining is the accumulated effect of repeated refusals.

**The Escalation Across Three Verses**

The sequence the sūrah has now laid out is worth reading as a whole. Verse 31: he neither believed nor prayed — the private life, empty of what should fill it. Verse 32: he declared the message false and turned away — the verdict formed and the back turned. Verse 33 will show him going home and swaggering about it — the verdict performed in front of an audience. This is how a position becomes an identity. What began as not attending ends as a public self, and the distance between the first stage and the last is covered without any single decision that felt decisive at the time.

**What Turning Away Looks Like Now**

The description has a contemporary shape that the Qur'an itself supplies elsewhere. A person can be told the same truth repeatedly and move further from it each time: *“We have surely varied ˹the signs˺ in this Quran so perhaps they may be mindful, but it only drives them farther away.”* (17:41). The remedy is not to vary the message more cleverly, since the variation was already there. The obstruction is in the will of the hearer, and the sūrah's answer to it is the one it has been giving since the first verse: the Day is coming whether or not he turns his back on it, and turning away does not alter what waits at the end of the road.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:33

> then went to their own people, walking boastfully.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Stage: Public Performance**

The trajectory the sūrah has been tracing arrives at its last stage. First the empty private life, then the verdict and the turned back, and now the performance: he goes to his own people and swaggers. The destination is significant. He does not go to the believers to argue with them, since argument would imply that the question is open. He goes home, to the people who already agree with him, and performs the version of himself that they will admire. The swagger is not a celebration of an idea. It is a celebration of an identity, and it requires an audience that will not challenge it.

**Yatamaṭṭā: the Word for the Walk**

The verb rendered as walking boastfully is *yatamaṭṭā*, built on a root meaning to stretch and draw oneself out, and the classical lexicons gloss it as a swaggering, strutting walk in which a man lengthens his step and stretches his sides. Some commentators took it in the related sense of lounging at ease in luxury, since the same root is used for stretching oneself out in comfort. The first reading has the context on its side: the sūrah is describing a man going out among his people, and the Qur'an's habit is to show a moral condition in a bodily posture. Pride, on this reading, is not described as an opinion the man holds but as the way he walks, which is far more cutting. The Qur'an prescribes the opposite gait in several places, which confirms that the walk is being read as a moral statement: *“And do not turn your nose up to people, nor walk pridefully upon the earth. Surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful.”* (31:18). The instruction is not about etiquette. It is about the body being made to say what the heart has decided, and the prescription is a shortening of the step: *“Be moderate in your pace. And lower your voice, for the ugliest of all voices is certainly the braying of donkeys.”* (31:19).

**Why the Household Matters**

The choice of *ahl* — his own people, his household, his circle — identifies where the performance is aimed. A man who has just refused a message does not go off alone to enjoy his refusal; he goes to the people whose regard he lives on and lets them see that he is unimpressed. The Qur'an treats this as one of the engines of unbelief, and it describes the same mechanism from the other side: *“When they meet the believers they say, ‘We believe.’ But when alone with their evil associates they say, ‘We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.’”* (2:14). The opinion that counts is the opinion of the group, and the group decides what a man can afford to believe.

**The Parallel in Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn**

The same figure appears in a later Makkan sūrah, drawn in more detail and with the same destination. *“wink to one another whenever they passed by, and muse ˹over these exploits˺ upon returning to their own people. And when they saw the faithful, they would say, ‘These ˹people˺ are truly astray,’”* (83:30–32). The three movements are identical: the mockery performed in passing, the return home to laugh about it, and the verdict pronounced on the believers. The repetition across two sūrahs suggests that the Qur'an regarded this as a recurring and identifiable social type rather than as a single man's behaviour. The denier is sustained by a circle, and the circle is sustained by mockery. The reason given elsewhere for the arrogance is physical and it is worth quoting, since it is an argument rather than a rebuke: *“And do not walk on the earth arrogantly. Surely you can neither crack the earth nor stretch to the height of the mountains.”* (17:37). A creature of that size, with that reach, has no material for a swagger. The strut is a claim about importance made by a body that cannot support it.

**Pride Behind the Posture**

A further verse supplies the interior state that produces the gait, and it is worth quoting because it names the link the sūrah is relying on: *“For they used to be prideful among their people, thinking they would never return ˹to Allah˺.”* (84:13–14). The swagger is not incidental to the unbelief; it is the bodily form of the assumption underneath it. A man who walks like that has concluded, at the level at which he actually lives, that he is not going to be called to account. The sūrah has spent thirty-two verses arguing the opposite, and it now places the conclusion in front of the reader as a posture rather than as a proposition.

**The Answer to the Swagger**

What follows in the sūrah is not an argument but a threat, and the form of the threat answers the posture exactly. The man who draws himself up and lengthens his stride is told, twice, that woe is nearer to him than he thinks. The Qur'an's response to pride is not to reason with it but to name what is coming, since pride has already refused reason. The Qur'an's counter-portrait supplies the alternative in the same register: *“The ˹true˺ servants of the Most Compassionate are those who walk on the earth humbly, and when the foolish address them ˹improperly˺, they only respond with peace.”* (25:63). Two gaits, two communities, and the difference between them is what each believes about its own standing before God. The sūrah will then turn, in its final movement, from this man's walk to his origin — a sperm-drop, a clot — which is the most complete answer available to a creature who has begun to swagger.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:34

> Woe to you, and more woe!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Threat in an Idiom**

The sūrah has finished describing the man — neither believing nor praying, declaring the message false, turning his back, and swaggering home to his people — and it answers him with a phrase rather than an argument. That is the first thing to notice. Argument was offered earlier in the sūrah and was refused; what is offered now is a threat, and the Qur'an's movement from proof to threat is itself a diagnosis. A person who has declared the signs false is not short of reasons. He is short of fear, and the sūrah addresses what is actually missing. The sequence matters for how the Qur'an is to be understood generally: proof is given first, refusal follows, and only then does the threat arrive. Anyone who reads the threat without the preceding thirty-three verses will mistake warning for vindictiveness, and the sūrah has taken care that this cannot be done honestly.

**What the Phrase Means**

The Arabic is *awlā laka fa-awlā*, and it is an idiom the grammarians discussed at length because its literal sense and its usage pull in different directions. The word *awlā* is an elative meaning nearer or more fitting, so the literal sense is *nearer to you, and nearer still* — nearer, that is, than anything you are expecting. As used by the Arabs, however, the phrase functioned as a threat, and the early authorities recorded that this was its force: a warning that the thing dreaded is close at hand rather than distant. Sībawayhi and the Basran grammarians treated it as an idiom of menace. The two readings converge rather than compete. What is near is the woe, so a rendering that makes the nearness explicit and a rendering that makes the woe explicit are describing the same sentence from two sides.

**Why the Threat Is Not Filled In**

No punishment is described. There is no fire here, no chain, no duration, no description of what the person will undergo, and the absence is deliberate. A specified punishment can be measured against a person's estimate of his own deserts and found excessive; an unspecified one cannot be argued with. The Qur'an uses the same economy elsewhere, and its threats are frequently at their heaviest when they are at their vaguest. A man told that woe is nearer to him than his own next step is left to supply the content, and what he supplies is shaped by everything he knows about what he has done. The Qur'an uses the explicit word for woe elsewhere, and the objects of it are instructive: *“Woe on that Day to the deniers—those who deny Judgment Day!”* (83:10–11). The woe attaches to the denial of the Day rather than to a list of particular sins, which is the same emphasis the sūrah has maintained since its opening oath. The man being threatened here is not a monster. He is someone who declined to believe that this Day would arrive.

**Who Is Being Addressed**

The address is to a single person in the second person singular, and the reports preserved in the tafsīr literature name him as Abū Jahl, one of the Prophet's most determined opponents in Makkah. A related report assigns the four occurrences of the phrase across these two verses to four stages: defeat at Badr, the grave, the Resurrection, and the Fire. These are exegetical reports rather than authenticated narrations, and they are cited as such. [UNVERIFIED: the identification of the addressee with Abū Jahl and the fourfold assignment were not located this session in a chain-carrying source; they belong to the asbāb material of the tafsīr tradition.] The generalising function is unaffected. Whoever matches the description in vv. 31–33 is the person being addressed, which is why the Qur'an names the type rather than restricting the threat to one man.

**The Answer to the Swagger**

The placement of the threat is exact, and the Qur'an's sense of fitness here should not be missed. The man in v. 33 drew himself up, lengthened his stride, and went home to be admired. The reply tells him that something is nearer to him than his own stride and that it is coming for him. Pride is met with nearness, because the one thing arrogance cannot survive is the collapse of distance. A man can shrug off a punishment he regards as remote; he cannot shrug off one he is told is at the door. The nearness is not a rhetorical flourish invented for this verse. It is the Qur'an's standing description of the situation: *“˹The time of˺ people’s judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away.”* (21:1), and *“The Hour has drawn near and the moon was split ˹in two˺.”* (54:1). Every person alive is closer to his own reckoning than he was yesterday, which is what makes the phrase *nearer to you* literally rather than merely rhetorically true. A man can shrug off a punishment he regards as remote; he cannot shrug off one he is told is at the door. That is why the sūrah frames the threat in the vocabulary of proximity rather than in the vocabulary of severity.

**A Threat Is Also an Address**

The hardest thing to hold on to in a verse like this is that it is spoken to someone still alive. A threat delivered to a man in this life is a warning, and a warning presupposes that the thing warned against can be avoided. The Qur'an never threatens the already-damned; its threats are always served in advance, and the sūrah has already stated the alternative in the plainest terms available: *“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53). The severity of the phrase is the measure of what is at stake, not of what has been decided.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:35

> Again, woe to you, and even more woe!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Woe Is Not an Echo**

The sūrah repeats the threat almost word for word, and the repetition is the point rather than the redundancy. The Arabic opens with *thumma*, then or moreover, which in this context indicates sequence with an interval rather than simple addition. A second woe is being announced after the first, not alongside it. The effect is of a punishment that arrives in stages, each one complete in itself and each one followed by another. The Qur'an's rhetoric here is doing something a single intensified threat could not do: it is denying the listener the comfort of a bounded disaster. Whatever he imagines when he hears the first phrase, the second tells him there is more.

**How Many Woes Are Contained Here**

The phrase in the previous verse holds two occurrences of *awlā*, and this verse holds two more, which gives four across the two verses. Exegetes who counted them assigned the four to four distinct stages: the battlefield of Badr, the grave, the Resurrection, and the Fire. That assignment is a homiletical reading preserved in the tafsīr literature rather than a report with a chain, and it should be cited as such. What gives the reading its value is not the specific four but the principle underneath it: the punishment is not a single event but a condition that unfolds, and each stage is real rather than symbolic. The second possibility is simpler and should be stated alongside it: in Arabic rhetoric, repetition of this kind is a standard device of emphasis, and the doubled phrase may intend nothing more than to make the threat unmistakable. The two explanations are compatible. Emphasis and escalation both deny the hearer the comfort of a limit, and both leave him with the sense of something that has begun and is not going to stop. The Qur'an's own account supports the principle even where it does not license the count.

**A Pattern of Escalation Across the Sūrah**

This doubling is the last step in a rising series that began much earlier. Verse 11 stated flatly that there would be no refuge. Verses 22 through 25 described the two faces and attached to the second of them the anticipation of a back-breaking calamity. Verses 34 and 35 now address a single figure directly and double the threat against him. The sūrah's tone has been intensifying steadily, and the intensification is not rhetorical decoration. It mirrors the argument: as the evidence accumulates, the refusal becomes less excusable, and the language appropriate to it becomes heavier. A sūrah that opens with an oath and closes with a threat has moved through every register available to it.

**The Fitness of the Response**

The Qur'an's punishments are always described in terms that correspond to the fault, and the correspondence here is unusually tight. The man in v. 33 was swaggering on his way home to an audience that admired him. What is threatened to him now is the loss of every one of those supports: the woe at Badr removed his standing among his people, the grave removed his audience, and the Resurrection and the Fire removed his self-possession. The principle is stated elsewhere as a rule: *“Whoever does an evil deed will only be paid back with its equivalent.”* (40:40). Nobody is punished for something other than what he did, and the shape of the punishment is the shape of the act.

**Why the Punishment Is Still Not Described**

The sūrah could easily have named the fire, the chain, or the boiling drink, all of which appear elsewhere in the Qur'an in far more detail. It does not, and the omission is worth accounting for. A threat that names its content can be assessed, and assessment is the first step toward argument; a threat that names only its nearness cannot be assessed at all. The sūrah is addressing a man who has spent his life managing what other people see and think, and the one thing he cannot manage is an unspecified disaster he is told is close. The silence is the severity. There is a second reason for it, which the Qur'an supplies elsewhere: the thing being threatened is not a penalty imposed from outside but the consequence of the man's own state. *“Undoubtedly, they will be sealed off from their Lord on that Day.”* (83:15) names the punishment as separation, and separation admits no description, because it is the absence of everything rather than the presence of something. A threat that names a fire gives the imagination somewhere to stand. A threat that names only an exclusion leaves it nowhere at all. The silence is the severity.

**Why the Sūrah Does Not End Here**

It is worth asking why the sūrah does not close on this note, since a doubled threat would make an effective ending. The reason is that a threat only moves a person who already accepts the premise — that there is a Day on which the threat will be executed. The denier of this sūrah does not accept that premise; he asked in v. 6 when the Day would be, in a tone that suggested it would never come. A threat built on an unaccepted premise is wasted. This is also why the threat is not softened in the telling. The sūrah is not trying to frighten a man into a convenient belief; it is establishing that the refusal has a cost, and it will then establish that the claim being refused is true. Fear and proof appear in the Qur'an in that order more often than readers expect, since fear is what makes a person willing to look at the argument he has been refusing. So the sūrah turns, in its final five verses, from threatening the man to proving the thing he denies, and it does so by an argument he cannot dismiss: his own origin.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:36

> Do people think they will be left without purpose?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Returns to Its Opening Question**

The movement away from threat and back to argument is marked by a repeated verb. Verse 3 asked whether a man supposes that God will not gather his bones, and this verse asks whether he supposes he will be left to no purpose. The verb is the same — *yaḥsabu*, to reckon, to suppose without grounds — and the two questions between them frame the entire sūrah. The first raised an objection about power: can scattered bones be reassembled? This one raises an objection about purpose: is a human being answerable for anything? The sūrah answered the first in v. 4 and will answer this one in the four verses that follow, using the same method in both cases — a premise the denier already accepts.

**What the Word Means**

The term rendered as without purpose is *sudan*, and it belongs to a root used of a camel left untended, wandering without a herdsman and without a destination. The image is not of something deliberately destroyed but of something carelessly abandoned, and that is exactly the claim being examined. Would God make a creature of this complexity and then leave it to wander? The choice of word places the burden where the sūrah wants it. The alternative to purpose is not hostility but negligence, and a negligent creator is not a credible description of the One whose power the sūrah has spent forty verses describing. The word also carries a social sense in its root usage: a thing left *sudan* is a thing without an owner to answer for it. The claim being tested is therefore not that God is cruel but that He is indifferent, and that is the more dangerous idea, since a cruel god could at least be appeased. An indifferent one offers nothing to do.

**What Being Left Would Entail**

If a human being were truly abandoned in that sense, several things would follow, and all of them are false on the Qur'an's account. There would be no command and no prohibition, since a creature with no purpose cannot be directed. There would be no messengers, since there would be nothing to say. There would be no record, no reckoning, and no return, since an aimless creature cannot be called to account for a journey it was never sent on. The Qur'an poses the same absurdity in a direct question: *“Did you then think that We had created you without purpose, and that you would never be returned to Us?”* (23:115). The two halves are inseparable: purpose and return stand or fall together. The purpose is named elsewhere in a single verse, and it is not a vague invitation to be good: *“˹He is the One˺ Who created death and life in order to test which of you is best in deeds.”* (67:2). A test requires an end at which the result is read, which is why the same Qur'an that insists on purpose insists with equal force on the reckoning. The sūrah of the Spider puts the consequence of purpose in the form of a question every believer is asked: *“Do people think once they say, ‘We believe,’ that they will be left without being put to the test?”* (29:2).

**A Different Argument From the One Before**

It is worth marking the shift in the kind of proof. Verses 3 and 4 argued from power — can the bones be gathered, can the fingertips be restored? — and the answer was that the Creator's power has no difficulty with either. This verse argues from purpose, which is a different claim altogether. Power answers the objection that resurrection is impossible; purpose answers the objection that it is pointless. A person can concede the first and still deny the second, and many have: the Makkan materialists did not doubt that a powerful god could raise the dead if he wished, and they argued only that nothing of the sort was going to happen. The sūrah closes that door separately.

**The Deniers Had Purposes of Their Own**

There is a subtlety in what is being denied. The people addressed here were not nihilists; they had projects, ambitions, trades, and family obligations, and they pursued them with real energy. What they denied was that any purpose had been *assigned* to them. The verse rejects that division. A creature made by a wise Maker has a purpose whether or not he acknowledges it, and the purpose is not up for negotiation: *“We only created them for a purpose, but most of these ˹pagans˺ do not know.”* (44:39). The ignorance is theirs, not the absence of the thing. The same movement appears in the Qur'an's account of what these people actually said: *“There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time.”* (45:24). That is not a claim that life has no purpose; it is a claim that the species continues. The individual, on their account, is expendable, and the Qur'an replies that the individual is exactly what the Creator is concerned with. A man is not a specimen of a continuing kind. He is the one who will be asked. A man who chooses his own aim instead of the one he was made for has not escaped purpose; he has substituted a smaller one for the real one.

**The Answer the Last Verses Supply**

The question is left hanging for exactly one verse, and the answer begins immediately afterwards. The sūrah will not argue about purpose in the abstract. It will point at the process by which each human being comes into existence — a drop, then a clot, then a formed and proportioned body, then the division into the two sexes — and it will ask whether a Maker who did all of that did it for nothing. The argument is from workmanship to intention: deliberate, staged, intricate work implies a worker with an end in view. That is why the sūrah ends with the question it ends on, and why the question is addressed to the same people who began by asking for a date.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:37

> Were they not ˹once˺ a sperm-drop emitted?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Moves to Origins**

The sūrah's answer to its own question begins where human life begins. Rather than argue about whether a purposeless creation is conceivable, it points at something no one in the audience disputed: every human being started as a drop of fluid. The interrogative here is not one of doubt but of reminder — *was he not*, expecting the answer *yes, he was*. The Qur'an uses this form constantly, and its force is rhetorical rather than informational. Nobody is being told something new. People are being asked to consider what they already know and to notice that they have never drawn the conclusion that follows from it. Every human being has passed through this beginning, and none of them chose it or supervised it. That combination — universal, involuntary, and intricately ordered — is what the Qur'an treats as the signature of a Maker who has not finished with what He made.

**The Vocabulary of the Drop**

Two words carry the verse. *Nuṭfa* denotes a small quantity of liquid, a drop, and the Qur'an uses it consistently for the beginning of a human being. The second word is a passive participle from a root meaning to pour or emit, and it locates the drop in the moment of its emission. The same vocabulary appears across the Qur'an in the passages describing human origins: from a drop when it is emitted (53:46), from a spurting fluid (86:6), from a drop of mixed fluids (76:2), and, in the most pointed of them all, *“He created humans from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Him˺.”* (16:4). That last verse is the argument of this sūrah in one line: the origin is admitted and the conclusion is refused, and the two cannot both stand.

**Arguing From What the Opponent Already Concedes**

This is the distinctive feature of the Qur'an's method here, and it deserves to be stated plainly, because it explains why the sūrah bothers with an argument at all. The Makkan audience did not deny that God was the Creator. Asked who made them, they answered *God*: *“If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created them, they will certainly say, ‘Allah!’”* (43:87), and the same admission is recorded in 29:61, 31:25 and 39:38. The Qur'an therefore does not need to establish that there is a Maker. It takes the premise its opponents already hold and asks them to follow it to its end. A person who admits that God made him from a drop cannot then argue that the same God has no further claim on him, and that is the contradiction the sūrah is pressing.

**The Answer to the Swagger**

The origin also answers the posture described a few verses earlier. The man who went home swaggering began as this. The Qur'an makes the connection explicit in more than one place: *“Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Us˺?”* (36:77). Pride is not merely a fault here; it is a category error, because the materials do not support it. A creature whose beginning is a quantity of fluid emitted in the dark has nothing on which to build a swagger, and the sūrah's move from the strut to the drop is the most economical demolition of pride available.

**The Stages Are the Point, Not the Embryology**

The Qur'an's interest in origins is not scientific curiosity, and its descriptions are given as signs rather than as data. The stages are listed in several sūrahs with slight variations — a drop, then a clinging clot, then a lump, then bones, then flesh, then a new creation (23:14) — and the purpose of the listing is stated in the passage itself: *“in order to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you.”* (22:5). The same point is made about the signs generally: *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth.”* (41:53). The sūrah is using the origin as evidence of an Agent who acts with intention over time, and the staged character of the process is what makes the intention visible. That intention is spelled out in the account of the same stages in Sūrah al-Sajdah, where the sequence ends with the faculties that make a moral life possible: *“then He fashioned them and had a spirit of His Own ˹creation˺ breathed into them. And He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect.”* (32:9). Hearing to receive the message, sight to read the signs, and intellect to judge between them — a creature equipped in that way has not been left wandering.

**From This Beginning to the Ending in Question**

The argument is still building, and its shape should be visible by now. If a human being comes into existence through a sequence of transformations directed by Someone, then the sequence has not ended with his birth, and the One who directed it has not lost interest in the result. The Qur'an states the principle as a comparison of difficulty: *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him.”* (30:27). Bringing a person into being from a drop is the harder act; bringing him back from what remains of him is the lesser one. The Qur'an presses the same comparison from the other direction as well, using the creation of the whole universe as the measure: *“The creation of the heavens and the earth is certainly greater than the recreation of humankind, but most people do not know.”* (40:57), and *“Do they not realize that Allah, Who created the heavens and the earth and did not tire in creating them, is able to give life to the dead?”* (46:33). If the greater work is admitted, the lesser cannot be denied without abandoning the premise that produced it. The sūrah is two verses from that conclusion, and every step toward it has used only what the denier already knew.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:38

> Then they became a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He developed and perfected their form,

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿAlaqa: a Word With a Wide Range**

The second stage is named with a word whose meanings do not fit neatly into one English term. *ʿAlaqa* belongs to a root meaning to cling, to hang, to be suspended, and the classical lexicons give it three senses that all bear on this verse: the thick, clotted blood that adheres; the thing that clings to the wall of the womb; and the leech, an animal named for its clinging. The word therefore describes at once the substance, the behaviour, and an object the Arabs knew well. No exegete of the classical period read it as anything other than the early stage of the embryo, and the range of the word is part of the Qur'an's economy: one noun carries a physical description and a functional one.

**Why That Word and Not Another**

The richness of the term has attracted attention in every period, and it is worth being precise about what can and cannot be claimed. The three senses are all attested in the classical lexicons, which is where the commentators took them from, and each of them describes something true of the embryo: it is a small mass of blood, it clings to the uterine wall, and it has a shape that resembles the leech. What should not be claimed is that the Qur'an's point is to anticipate a discipline that did not exist in the seventh century. The verse is not offering information. It is naming a stage in order to make an argument, and the argument is about the One who took the embryo through it. The reason the word matters for that argument is its dependency. A thing that clings is a thing that requires somewhere to cling, and the clinging is not the embryo's achievement but the condition in which it exists. The Qur'an's choice of noun therefore embeds a claim about contingency at the very point where a person is most self-sufficient in the ordinary imagination — the beginning of his own life, which he did not arrange and could not have survived without.

**From Clot to Proportioned Form**

Two verbs follow, and their order is deliberate. *Khalaqa* is to bring into being, to determine; *sawwā* is to proportion, to level, to give each part its right measure and relation to the others. The sequence moves from existence to form, and the second verb is the one the sūrah used in v. 4, where God declared Himself able to restore the fingertips — to proportion them. The echo is exact and it is certainly intended. The God who proportioned the extremities at the first creation is the God who will proportion them again, and the sūrah has now placed the two claims forty verses apart in order to close the circle.

**The Full Sequence, Given Elsewhere**

This verse abbreviates a process that other sūrahs set out at greater length, and the fuller accounts are worth placing beside it. *“then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation.”* (23:14). Sūrah al-Ḥajj adds the purpose explicitly — *“in order to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you”* (22:5) — and Sūrah Ghāfir repeats the same sequence with the same conclusion (40:67). The variations between the lists are not inconsistencies; each passage selects the stages that serve the point it is making, and the sūrah of the Prostration adds the element this sūrah leaves implicit: that the forming is done well by design. *“Who has perfected everything He created. And He originated the creation of humankind from clay.”* (32:7). Perfection here is not an aesthetic judgement but a functional one — each thing fitted to what it is for — and a creature fitted in that way has been fitted for something. The sūrah is about to say that he has not been left without purpose.

**Each Verb Marks a Distinct Act**

The grammar of the sequence is as important as the stages themselves. The Qur'an does not say that the drop became a clot and then, in the ordinary course of things, a body. It attributes each transition to an act: We created, We proportioned, We made. The insistence on separate verbs for separate transitions rules out any reading of the process as self-directing. Matter does not organise itself in this account; it is organised, at every step, by an Agent whose action is continuous rather than confined to the beginning. That is the theological claim the sūrah needs, because the same continuity is what will raise the dead. It also excludes a reading that has caused difficulty in every period: that the Qur'an describes a process set in motion once and left to run. The verbs refuse that. A drop does not become a clot because drops do that; it becomes one because it is made to. Whoever reads the sequence as a description of autonomous development has not read the grammar, whatever he makes of the biology.

**What Has Been Established**

By the end of this verse the sūrah has assembled its proof. A human being is the product of a sequence of transformations, each one an act, carried out on material that had no life and no form, and brought to a proportioned body of a particular kind. What is being denied by the sūrah's opponents is not that this happened — they conceded it — but that the Agent who did it can do anything further. The next verse will name the last of the transformations, and the verse after it will ask the question the whole sūrah has been moving toward.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:39

> producing from it both sexes, male and female.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Third Verb, a Third Act**

The sequence has moved from drop to clot to proportioned form, and now a further verb introduces the last stage named in this sūrah: *fa-jaʿala*, and He made. The change of verb is worth noticing, since the sūrah has already used *khalaqa* for bringing into being and *sawwā* for proportioning. *Jaʿala* is the verb of assigning, of making one thing into another, of instituting a function. What is being described here is not a new material but a new determination of the material already formed: from that single origin, two kinds of human being, complementary and different, each with its own role in the continuation of the species. The grammar also settles a question about the pronoun. The two sexes are made *from him* — from that single human source — which is the Qur'an's consistent account of the origin of the pair (4:1) and the point on which this verse turns. The differentiation is not a second creation out of nothing. It is a determination imposed on what was already there.

**Why Sexuality Belongs in This Argument**

There are two reasons, and they work together. The first is that the production of two coordinated sexes out of one source is a further instance of the design the sūrah has been describing. A single drop does not contain two kinds of person by accident; the differentiation is imposed, and the complementarity that makes reproduction possible is not the sort of thing that arises from undifferentiated matter. The second reason is the one the sūrah needs: if the continuation of the human race depends on a mechanism the Creator instituted, then creation is not a single event in the past but an activity He is carrying on now. Every birth is His act, and the argument from origins is therefore not an argument about something remote. This is why the Qur'an's appeals to human origins are so often in the present tense and addressed to people who were not present at the beginning of anything. The proof is not an event in the distant past that must be accepted on authority. It is happening now, in every pregnancy, and a person who has watched a child being born has watched the argument he is refusing.

**The Pairing Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The same point is made repeatedly and in several registers. *“And He created the pairs—males and females—from a sperm-drop when it is emitted.”* (53:45–46). The pairing is extended to the whole created order: *“And We created pairs of all things so perhaps you would be mindful.”* (51:49). It is stated as a sign accompanied by affection rather than only by function: *“And one of His signs is that He created for you spouses from among yourselves so that you may find comfort in them. And He has placed between you compassion and mercy.”* (30:21). And it is given as the origin of human diversity: *“Indeed, We created you from a male and a female, and made you into peoples and tribes so that you may ˹get to˺ know one another.”* (49:13).

**Reproduction as a Standing Sign**

The argumentative value of reproduction is that it puts the proof in front of everyone. A person does not have to accept a report about the beginning of the human race, because the beginning of every human being is observable. *“One of His signs is that He created you from dust, then—behold!—you are human beings spreading over ˹the earth˺.”* (30:20), and: *“And He is the One Who originated you all from a single soul, then assigned you a place to live and another to ˹be laid to˺ rest.”* (6:98). The Qur'an's constant question in these passages is the one it asks in Sūrah al-Naḥl: given that you concede the origin, why do you then argue with the One who originated you (16:4)?

**The Multiplication Is Not the Difficulty**

One of the underlying assumptions of the objector in v. 3 was that the sheer number of the dead makes resurrection implausible — billions of bodies, dispersed over the whole earth and throughout all of history. The production of the two sexes answers that assumption rather than merely repeating an answer to it. Human multiplication is itself part of the divine arrangement described in this verse, and the God who instituted a mechanism for filling the earth with people is not going to be defeated by the result of His own institution. The Qur'an states the principle in a single line: *“The creation and resurrection of you ˹all˺ is as simple ˹for Him˺ as that of a single soul.”* (31:28). Multiplicity is what makes a task hard for a creature, since a creature must deal with things one at a time. It is not what makes anything hard for a Creator who sustains every particle at once, and the sūrah of Yā Sīn makes the same point by asking about the origin of the whole order: *“Can the One Who created the heavens and the earth not ˹easily˺ resurrect these ˹deniers˺?” Yes ˹He can˺!* (36:81).

**Where the Sūrah Has Arrived**

The sūrah opened with the objection that bones cannot be gathered, and it has now answered it with the stages of a single life: a drop emitted, a clinging clot, a proportioned form, and from it the two sexes, from whom every human being since has come. Nothing in that list was disputed by the people being addressed. The last verse will ask the question that the whole sequence was assembled to make unanswerable, and it will ask it in a form that expects the hearer to supply the answer himself.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:40

> Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Supplies Its Own Answer**

The last verse is a question, and it is the kind of question that supplies its own answer. *A-laysa dhālika bi-qādirin* — is not that One able to give life to the dead? — expects *yes, He is*, and the expectation is part of the grammar rather than an addition to it. The sūrah that opened with an oath by the Day of Resurrection closes by asking whether the Creator can raise the dead, and it leaves the hearer to say the word. Nothing is spelled out and nothing needs to be. After forty verses of argument, the conclusion is being placed in the reader's mouth.

**The Response the Prophet Taught**

The community did not treat that question as rhetorical only; they answered it out loud in prayer. A report in the Sunan collections describes a man reciting this verse on his rooftop and saying, *“Glory be to You, then, why not?”* — and when he was asked about it, he replied that he had heard it from the Messenger of God (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 863, graded ḥasan by al-Albānī). A related narration in the same chapter gives a fuller formula for the reciter of this sūrah: that when he reaches the question about raising the dead, he should say that he is among those who testify to it. The practice turns the closing verse into an act of worship: the reader does not merely concede the conclusion, he confesses it. That is a point about the nature of faith in this sūrah. Belief is not presented anywhere in it as the acceptance of a proposition after weighing the evidence. It is presented as assent given to a Speaker, and the assent is the thing being asked for from the first verse onward. The argument exists to make the assent possible; the assent is not the argument's by-product.

**How the Logic Runs**

The argument constructed over the last five verses is a single syllogism, and it rests entirely on premises the audience granted. The One who made a human being from an emitted drop, took him through the stage of the clinging clot, proportioned him, and brought forth from him the two sexes from whom the earth was populated — is that One unable to bring the dead back? The Qur'an states the comparison of difficulty in the same terms elsewhere: *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him.”* (30:27). Bringing a person into being from nothing is the greater act; reconstituting him from what remains is the lesser, and the sūrah's closing question is unanswerable on any other premise. The Qur'an adds the guarantee in the plainest terms elsewhere: *“Just as We produced the first creation, ˹so˺ shall We reproduce it. That is a promise binding on Us.”* (21:104). A promise binding on God is not a hope, and the sūrah has built its entire case on a premise the objectors never disputed — that the God who made them is the God who made everything.

**The Circle Closed With Verse 3**

The structure deserves to be stated, because it is the sūrah's design rather than an accident of arrangement. Verse 3 asked whether a man supposes that God cannot gather his bones; verse 40 asks whether that God is unable to give life to the dead. The two questions use different verbs but make the same challenge, and between them the sūrah has supplied the answer to both: the fingertips, the cosmic collapse, the record, the self-witness, the deathbed, and the stages of a single human life. A reader who reaches the last verse has been given everything he needs to answer it, and the answer he gives is the same one the objector refused thirty-seven verses earlier.

**Assent as an Act**

Why does the sūrah end on a question rather than on a declaration? Because the point of the whole argument is to produce something in the hearer, and a declaration would have produced nothing. A question obliges the reader to answer, and the answer — *yes, He is able* — is itself the faith the sūrah has been asking for since v. 1. That is why the Prophet's taught response matters: the confession is not a comment on the argument but its intended conclusion. The sūrah is not finished when it is read. It is finished when the reader says the word. There is a version of this in the practice of the community: the reciter who reaches this verse in prayer does not pass over it in silence but answers it, because a question addressed to a reader is not complete until the reader responds. The sūrah that began by swearing by the Day ends by putting the same Day's possibility into the mouth of whoever is reading, and the pause between the question and the answer is the whole of a person's life.

**The Whole Arc**

The sūrah opened by swearing by the Day of Resurrection and by the self that accuses itself. It named the objection to resurrection and answered it from the body's own extremities. It located unbelief in what a person wants rather than in what he knows. It described the collapse of the sky, the opening of the record, the testimony of the soul against itself, and the two faces. It turned aside to instruct the Prophet about receiving revelation, then returned to rebuke a people who love the hasty world and leave the weighty Day behind. It followed one death from the collarbone to the driving, threatened the swaggerer, and closed by asking whether the God who made him from a drop can raise him from the grave. The question is not rhetorical for the person reading it. It is the reason the sūrah exists.
