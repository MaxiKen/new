# Sūrah al-Maʿārij (Chapter 70) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:1

> A challenger has demanded a punishment bound to come

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mockery Disguised as a Question**

The sūrah opens mid-confrontation, with an unnamed figure demanding torment as though placing a wager. Arabic saʾala with the preposition bi- can mean either asking about something or calling for it, and the verse exploits that seam. Some exegetes read a question — someone asked the Prophet who would be punished and when — while most hear a taunt, a supplication hurled upward in contempt. The majority view fits the sūrah's arc better, since everything that follows answers swagger rather than curiosity. Tradition names the mocker: al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, who is reported to have challenged God to rain stones from the sky if the message were true (8:32), or by another report Abū Jahl himself. Both identifications agree on the essential point — this was no sincere inquiry. Sarcasm dressed as prayer reveals a heart that has mistaken divine patience for divine absence. The sūrah will spend its first breaths correcting that arithmetic.

**A Punishment Described as Already Falling**

Grammar preaches before theology begins. "Bound to come" renders wāqiʿ, an active participle meaning falling, occurring, befalling — the event gets cast as already in motion. Arabic participles lend ongoing certainty: this punishment is not merely possible but underway, a stone already dropped though not yet landed. The indefinite form adds dread, leaving the torment unnamed and therefore unlimited; specific threats can be mentally managed, but an unspecified falling doom colonizes the imagination. Such wording also answers the challenger's dare on its own terms. He demanded spectacle, and the verse replies that spectacle is en route — though it will arrive as catastrophe rather than theater. Tense here carries mercy too. Between the demand and the descent stretches a window for repentance, and the sūrah holds that window open even while announcing its frame. Certainty of arrival need not mean immediacy.

**Badr Answered What Makkah Asked**

Occasion-of-revelation reports tie the verse to the battlefield of Badr in the second year after the Hijrah. Al-Naḍr, who had demanded heaven's stones, was taken captive there and executed — his petition granted in a form he never intended. The irony did not escape the early community: the man who hurried punishment received it ahead of schedule, while the believers he mocked stood vindicated around him. Those who name Abū Jahl instead preserve the same moral with a different corpse, since he too fell at Badr after years of taunting. Disagreement over the name leaves the lesson untouched, and the stronger scholarly instinct refuses to force a choice — both men embodied the mocking demand, and Badr buried both. Yet the verse outgrows its occasion. Badr was an installment; the Day of Resurrection remains the full payment. History's preview guarantees eschatology's feature, and the sūrah now pivots from the preview to the main event.

**Hastening Doom as Proof of Blindness**

Psychology explains why anyone would dare heaven to strike. The challenger treats God's delay as God's defeat, converting mercy into evidence against mercy. Scripture catalogs this disease across many passages: deniers bidding the Prophet to hasten torment (22:47), demanding the Hour's date as though scheduling would domesticate it (10:50-51), treating the threat's postponement as its refutation (16:1). Each instance shares one structure — impatience weaponized against patience. The Qur'anic reply never hurries; it warns that hurrying proves nothing except the hurrier's folly. Punishment delayed is not punishment denied but respite extended, and respite is the very mercy the mocker mistakes for weakness. This verse therefore diagnoses before it threatens. Behind the demand for doom stands a refusal to read time theologically, to see in each undisturbed morning a gift rather than an alibi. Blindness to mercy and blindness to wrath are one blindness.

**The Sūrah Continues Where al-Ḥāqqah Ended**

Placement illumines purpose. Al-Maʿārij belongs to the middle Makkan period and likely followed its predecessor closely, extending the Resurrection scenes of 69:18-37 into fresh tableaux of terror and duty. Where the previous sūrah closed by vindicating the Book, this one opens by confronting those who mocked it — defense turning to counter-charge. Its very titles witness to the opening scene: Saʾala, "Someone Asked," preserves the challenger's verb, while al-Mawāqiʿ recalls the impending matters he summoned. The canonical title, al-Maʿārij, waits two verses ahead, naming the stairways of ascent that frame God's majesty. Such continuity rebukes any reading of sūrahs as loose anthologies. The challenger of verse 1 stands in the rubble of ʿĀd and Thamūd, inherits Pharaoh's drowned arrogance, and still dares to demand. The Book answers sustained defiance with sustained description, surrounding the mocker with witnesses.

**Demanded Punishment Still Allows Repentance**

Mercy hides inside the dare's aftermath. The challenger demanded doom, yet doom did not strike mid-sentence; heaven recorded the wager and let the gambler walk home. That walk home was grace — hours and days in which repentance stayed possible, the demand retractable, the heart changeable. Scripture often shows this patience toward provocation: Pharaoh got signs before the sea, Quraysh got years before Badr, and every mocker gets mornings to reconsider. The falling punishment of the verse falls slowly precisely so that its targets might step aside. Only persistence turns respite into ruin. This transforms how hearers should receive the threat: not as a slammed door but as a door held open with visible effort. The sūrah will later command beautiful patience from the Prophet, and God's own patience models it first. Heaven absorbs insults without hurry, answering taunts with time. What the challenger mistook for absence was actually mercy, extended toward the very tongue that mocked it.

**The Sentence Runs Into the Next Verse**

Syntax refuses to stop where the verse number does. The demand's object — punishment bound to come — spills into verse 2, which will name its owners and seal its certainty. Enjambment across the verse boundary quickens the recitation, denying the challenger even a pause between his dare and its doom. Reciters feel this pull bodily: the breath carries forward because the grammar does, and meaning arrives as pursuit. The device also binds the passage's logic visibly. Who demands, what descends, upon whom, from whom, and through what heavens — the sūrah will answer in cascading clauses across verses 1-4, one grammatical avalanche. Modern punctuation obscures this momentum with full stops, but the Arabic flows. Form follows content: just as the punishment cannot be halted, the sentence describing it cannot rest. The next verse completes the thought by identifying exactly for whom this falling torment falls.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:2

> for the disbelievers—to be averted by none—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Punishment Has Named Owners**

Verse 1 announced a falling torment; verse 2 delivers its address label. "For the disbelievers" — lil-kāfirīna — assigns the demanded punishment to those who demanded it, closing the circle with judicial neatness. The definite article matters: not some hypothetical deniers but the disbelievers, the known party of mockers surrounding the Prophet. Exegetes keep the reference concrete, hearing the Quraysh scoffers who treated resurrection-talk as entertainment. Yet the label outgrows its first wearers, as Qur'anic categories always do, extending to everyone who replicates their stance toward warning. Grammar reinforces ownership through the lām of entitlement: this punishment belongs to them the way wages belong to earners. No transfer, no substitution, no clerical error will redirect it. The challenger asked for whom the torment was; the verse answers with a mirror. It was, all along, for him.

**No Hand Can Turn It Back**

"To be averted by none" renders lā dāfiʿa lahu — no defender, no repeller, no shield exists for it. The noun dāfiʿ evokes someone who pushes back an attacker, and its negation leaves the punishment's path utterly clear. Wealth cannot bribe it, armies cannot block it, intercessors cannot intercept it, and the idols so confidently invoked will stand as helpless as their worshippers. This absoluteness distinguishes divine punishment from every human threat, which some power can always deflect. Here the mighty and the sheltered discover a common nakedness. Theologians hear in the phrase a pure note of divine sovereignty: to decree torment that none can lift belongs to God alone, and claiming such power for any other is itself the unbelief being punished. Helplessness before heaven is the human condition; this verse simply removes the anesthesia. When the demand of verse 1 meets the irreversibility of verse 2, bravado curdles into dread.

**Certainty Doubled in a Single Line**

Two locks fasten this verse shut. The punishment is wāqiʿ, already falling, and now also undeterrable, past all rescue. Falling plus unstoppable equals arrived: the outcome stands so assured that only the timetable stays hidden. Rhetoric of this kind — paired certainties in one breath — recurs wherever the Book confronts delay-based doubt. Deniers reasoned that postponement meant cancellation; the verse replies that descent plus irreversibility makes postponement mere scheduling. Each half of the line guards the other: "for the disbelievers" prevents the threat from floating abstractly, while "to be averted by none" prevents it from softening hopefully. Together they form a sealed chamber with the mocker inside. Translators capture the compression with em-dashes, setting the second clause as a grim aside. But the Arabic needs no punctuation to convey finality. Nine syllables close every exit, and the door they close is the Day itself.

**A Challenge Thrown Back at the Challenger**

Irony crowns the verse. The man who demanded punishment to prove its absence learns that his demand has been entered as evidence — and that the sentence allows no appeal. Mockers imagined themselves prosecutors cross-examining the Prophet; the Book reveals them as defendants sentencing themselves. This reversal follows a deep Qur'anic pattern in which the weapons of denial turn in their wielders' hands. Those who demanded angels receive instead the terror of angels; those who begged for hastening get hastening as wrath. The mechanism is moral rather than magical: persistent scorn hardens into destiny, and destiny then wears scorn's own face. Verse 2 thus answers verse 1 the way an echo answers a shout, except the echo carries farther and returns heavier. What left Makkah as laughter comes back from heaven as law. The challenger wanted a sign; he has become one, exhibited across centuries to everyone tempted by his wager.

**Unbelief Chooses Its Own Destination**

Agency threads through the grammar. The disbelievers are not random victims of a falling sky but travelers who selected their road, demanded its destination, and now find the ticket nonrefundable. "For the disbelievers" therefore reads as consequence rather than caprice: persistent denial earns its own harvest, and the harvest is gathered without interference. This preserves both divine justice and human freedom in one stroke. God sends the punishment, yet the punished chose the camp in which it falls; sovereignty and responsibility meet without collision. Pastoral wisdom hides here too. Hearers tempted by the mockers' swagger get shown its terminus plainly, while there is still time to change roads. The verse threatens, but its threat functions as rescue-signal — severity in service of mercy. Only those who insist on the label need fear its postage. Everyone else may read the address, shudder, and step back from the company of the doomed.

**Severity Serves as a Final Mercy**

Threats this absolute can sound cruel until their purpose surfaces. The verse seals every exit not to torment the living but to redirect them — terror now prevents ruin later. Physicians inflict pain to prevent death, and the Book's severest passages work the same way, wounding complacency to save souls. Had the punishment been described as escapable, the mockers would have calculated odds and hired defenses; "averted by none" forecloses the arithmetic entirely. Despair of escape becomes hope of another kind: since the Day cannot be dodged, it must be prepared for, and preparation remains open. This is severity as kindness, the fence at the cliff's edge rather than the cliff itself. Hearers who shudder at the verse have already begun benefiting from it. Only hearts that hear "none" and shrug have truly grasped neither the threat nor the mercy folded inside its severity.

**The Day Lurks Behind the Words**

"Punishment" here points chiefly to the Last Day, though Badr supplied its down payment. The demand of verse 1 concerned eschatology — the Hour the Prophet preached — and verse 2 keeps that horizon fixed. Worldly calamities can sometimes be averted: walls hold, ransoms pay, storms pass. Only the final reckoning carries the absolute "none," because only there does every buffer dissolve. Exegetes therefore hear the eschatological register beneath the historical occasion, reading Badr as installment and Resurrection as sum. The verse's brevity suits that vastness; a line this short can hold the Day only by refusing detail. Detail will come soon enough — skies like molten brass, mountains like wool, children offered as ransom. For now the sūrah contents itself with ownership and irreversibility, two nails sufficient to fasten attention. The next verse will then name the height from which this unstoppable sentence descends.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:3

> from Allah, Lord of pathways of ˹heavenly˺ ascent,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Punishment Comes From Allah Himself**

Verse 2 sealed every exit; verse 3 names the power that sealed them. "From Allah" — min Allāh — traces the falling torment to its uttermost origin, past angels, past causes, past all intermediaries. The preposition matters: punishment does not merely occur with God's permission but proceeds from His presence, bearing His signature. Such directness answers the challenger's dare precisely. He had invoked heaven as though heaven were empty; the verse replies that heaven's Lord heard, and the falling punishment carries His return address. Theology and terror merge here. Because the torment comes from Allah, no lesser power can intercept it — verse 2's "none" now gets its explanation. Idols cannot shield, patrons cannot ransom, and nature cannot absorb what the Almighty sends. The verse therefore completes a small ladder of dread: demanded in verse 1, assigned and sealed in verse 2, and now sourced in the One whom none can resist.

**Stairways to the Sky**

"Lord of pathways of ascent" renders dhī al-maʿārij, one of the Book's most evocative titles. Maʿārij, plural of miʿrāj, means stairways, ladders, routes of rising — the courses by which angels ascend, deeds rise, and prayers climb toward the throne. Commentators picture ranked heavens with appointed paths, trafficked by angels on missions of command and record. The image conveys ordered majesty: heaven is no empty void but a governed height with roads and ranks. Some exegetes widen the sense to degrees of favor, the ascending stations God bestows on His servants, so that maʿārij names both cosmic architecture and spiritual promotion. Both readings magnify the same Lord — high above all height, yet ordering every step that rises. The bracketed "heavenly" in translation rightly guides the sense: these are not mountain trails but the sky's own thoroughfares. Punishment descends from the height that prayers ascend to, and the symmetry is deliberate.

**Why This Name Appears Here**

Divine titles in the Book always suit their scenes, and this one suits a scene of unstoppable descent. The Lord of ascents commands every vertical road; what He sends down, none below can send back. Height imagery also answers the challenger's posture. Earthbound mockers looked up and saw nothing, concluding nothing looked down; the title corrects their astronomy by populating the sky with pathways and their Governor. Majesty and menace intertwine: the same elevation that receives the prayers of the righteous releases the ruin of the scornful. Exegetes note the consolation folded inside the threat — a Lord with stairways is a Lord who can raise the lowly as surely as He can strike the proud. Ascent remains available to those who climb in obedience; only the defiant must fear the downward traffic. The title thus preaches to two audiences at once, lifting one while leveling the other.

**Only He Opens the Way Up**

Sovereignty over ascent carries a sharp corollary: nobody rises without leave. Angels climb by command, souls rise by mercy, and prayers ascend by grace — every upward movement runs on permission from the Lord of the ways. This monopoly quietly demolishes the ladder-claims of every rival power. No idol operates an elevator, no sorcerer keeps a stair, no patron owns a route past death. The challenger's demand assumed a heaven open to taunts but closed to traffic; the verse reveals the reverse, a heaven trafficked by angels yet deaf to mockery. Contrast sharpens the doctrine: deniers cannot rise to escape, while punishment descends unhindered to arrive. Verticality becomes verdict. Those who bowed in this life will find the pathways open; those who straightened their backs in scorn will find the sky a ceiling. One title teaches the whole economy of rising and falling.

**Degrees of Favor Behind the Architecture**

The minority reading deserves its hearing. Some commentators take maʿārij as ranks of blessing — the ascending degrees of nearness, honor, and reward that God assigns among creatures. On this view the title praises distributive wisdom: the Lord arranges creation in stations, elevating whom He wills by measures only He knows. Evidence favors keeping both senses together rather than choosing. The next verse will describe angels physically ascending, supporting the architectural reading, yet the sūrah's later passage on the righteous climbing through prayer and charity supports the moral one. Strong exegesis often layers rather than selects, hearing cosmic roads and spiritual ranks in one phrase. Majesty gets the last word either way. Whether pathways mean angel-routes or favor-degrees, they belong to Him, and belonging to Him means escaping no one. The God of many stairs sees every step.

**Height Puts Mockery in Its Place**

Mockery shrinks when heaven's scale appears. The challenger stood on desert sand and hurled words upward, imagining an empty sky; the title Lord of the ascents fills that sky with roads, ranks, and rising angels. Smallness is the verse's silent lesson — not human worthlessness but human proportion. Creatures who see themselves truly find worship natural and defiance absurd; creatures who see themselves falsely reverse the two. The sūrah therefore practices cosmic perspective as therapy, lifting eyes from tribal quarrels to stairways of light. Ancient cosmology pictured layered heavens with immense distances, and whatever the physics, the theology holds: God transcends every creature's reach while remaining nearer than any vein. That twin truth steadies believers and unnerves mockers in equal measure. The challenger demanded proof from on high; the verse answers by describing the High itself. Mockery that survives such description is no longer ignorance but resolve.

**A Title That Names the Whole Sūrah**

No accident placed the sūrah's canonical name in its third verse. Al-Maʿārij — the Ascensions — lifts the chapter's gaze from the challenger's pit to God's heights in the space of three lines. Titles drawn from opening passages usually announce governing themes, and ascent governs here: angels will rise in verse 4, the righteous will rise through eight duties in verses 22-34, and the wicked will sink precisely because they refused to climb. The middle Makkan setting sharpens the summons. Believers then were few and pressed, needing to know that heaven's roads stayed open however narrow earth's alleys grew. Naming the sūrah for stairways told them to look up. For today's reciter the title works identically, turning a chapter that opens with doom toward a God who raises. The next verse describes who climbs those ways and how long their Day endures.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:4

> ˹through which˺ the angels and the ˹holy˺ spirit will ascend to Him on a Day fifty thousand years in length.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Angels Climbing Toward Their Lord**

The verse opens heaven's traffic to view: angels ascending to Him along the pathways verse 3 named. The verb taʿruju pictures steady rising — ranks mounting through ranked skies toward the throne. Such scenes recur across the Book wherever majesty needs displaying: the night of decree fills with descending angels (97:4), while here the emphasis reverses into ascent on the Day of gathering. Direction carries doctrine. Upward movement means return to origin, completion of errands, delivery of records — creation's couriers coming home. The plural "angels" keeps their number vast and unnamed, an uncounted host streaming skyward. Against the lone challenger of verse 1, the verse arrays heaven's multitudes; against earth's flat horizons, it stacks height upon height. What the mocker demanded as spectacle has become spectacle beyond his imagining. Punishment will not crawl toward him across the sand. It will descend along roads crowded with ascending light.

**The Spirit Beside the Angels**

"The spirit" — al-rūḥ — stands distinguished from the angels though marching with them, and the distinction has exercised exegetes for centuries. Most identify the Spirit as Gabriel, the Trustworthy Spirit of Sūrah 26 (26:193), singled out by rank as a commander gets named apart from the troops. The singling honors rather than separates: Gabriel leads the ascent as he led revelation, first among bearers of divine business. Others propose a mighty angel distinct from Gabriel, or the collective spirits of the dead rising for judgment, or a created grandeur beyond angelic kind. Each minority view strains against the plain parallel with Sūrah 97, where the Spirit descends alongside angels in identical pairing. The strongest reading therefore keeps Gabriel, while admitting the verse's reserve — the Book names him by office here rather than by name, letting function outshine person. Whatever precision scholars debate, the scene's force survives: heaven's greatest, unnamed or named, climbs with heaven's many.

**Fifty Thousand Years in a Single Day**

Duration staggers the imagination deliberately. A Day whose measure runs fifty thousand years collapses every human schedule; the longest life becomes a morning, the mightiest empire an afternoon. Exegetes take the Day as Resurrection, experienced at full crushing length by those who denied it. Numbers here work as awe rather than arithmetic — fifty thousand means "beyond your counting," the way "seventy" can mean multitudes elsewhere. Yet the figure also answers the challenger's impatience with proportion. He who could not wait a lifetime for doom must now face a Day longer than all lifetimes stacked. Some commentators alternatively read the span as the road's length, the distance between earth and the throne measured in years of travel. The Day-reading holds the majority and fits the context better, since punishment and gathering frame the verse. Either way the mathematics humbles. Time itself becomes an instrument of majesty, stretched like a rack for the defiant.

**Time Moves Differently Above the Sky**

The Book elsewhere confirms that heaven keeps its own clock. A day with your Lord measures like a thousand years of earthly counting (22:47), and cosmic affairs ascend to Him through days of that millennial scale (32:5). Such verses relativize every human timetable without dissolving accountability — the Day comes slowly by our impatience and suddenly by our readiness. The fifty thousand years here extend the same principle to its eschatological extreme: the final Day dwarfs even the thousand-year days of providence. Physics offers no objection, since time was always a creature rather than a master; the Lord of ascents stretches and folds it at will. Pastoral fruit grows from this doctrine. Believers crushed by long affliction learn that heaven's delays are not heaven's denials, while mockers demanding instant proof learn that their stopwatch binds nothing. Eternity will swallow every schedule. Wisdom lies in preparing by the slow years for the Day that outruns them.

**Length as Mercy and as Terror**

One Day, two experiences: that is the settled teaching gathered around this verse. For deniers the fifty thousand years drag as exposure — standing, sweating, waiting, with every dodge exhausted and every record open. For believers the same Day passes lightly, shortened by mercy into something endurable, even brief. The contrast needs no mechanism beyond divine will; time bends for the righteous as surely as it racks the wicked. This duality explains why the sūrah will soon command beautiful patience (70:5): endurance now purchases ease then, trading short years of strain for a long Day made short. Commentators press the bargain further, urging hearers to spend present hours buying future lightness through prayer, charity, and chastity — the very duties verses 22-34 will list. The Day's length therefore preaches both ways at once, terrifying the scornful and comforting the strained. Dread and hope share one calendar, differing only in how its hours will feel.

**The Scene Before the Command to Endure**

Verse 4 closes the sūrah's opening tableau, and its placement is deliberate. Before commanding patience, the Book displays what patience must outlast: sneering challengers below, ascending myriads above, and a Day vaster than history between. Such sequencing follows prophetic pedagogy — show the scale, then summon the stamina. The Prophet, facing daily mockery, gets first a vision of vindication's machinery: heaven's roads are busy, the Spirit himself is rising, and the deniers' Day already casts its fifty-millennium shadow. Only then will verse 5 speak its gentle imperative. Readers should therefore receive verses 1-4 as one chamber of a larger argument, moving from dare to doom to height to Day. Each line answers the challenger at a higher altitude. Earth asked its question; heaven has now fully replied. What remains is the instruction for living between the dare and the Day, which the next verse delivers as beautiful patience.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:5

> So endure ˹this denial, O Prophet,˺ with beautiful patience.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Patience Commanded at the Flashpoint**

Fa-ṣbir — "so endure" — lands immediately after the challenger's dare and heaven's four-verse reply. The sequence matters: first the Book answers the mocker with doom, then it steadies the Prophet with discipline. Polemics without patience curdle into bitterness, so the sūrah prescribes composure at the exact moment provocation peaks. The fa- prefix draws the consequence tight: because punishment is certain and rescue impossible, the Prophet need not hurry, answer, or despair. Vindication travels on schedule; his task is stamina till it arrives. Such commands punctuate the Makkan years, where daily scorn made endurance the Prophet's primary warfare. Revelation managed his heart the way generals manage troops — with orders fitted to the battle's phase. Here the order is stillness. Let heaven's machinery grind; the Messenger's portion is beautiful patience.

**Beauty Qualifies the Endurance**

Ṣabran jamīlan — "beautiful patience" — refuses grim resignation. Jamīl means lovely, comely, gracious: patience worn the way fine garments are worn, without tearing. The phrase echoes Jacob's resolve when his sons brought Joseph's bloodied shirt — "beautiful patience!" he cried, twice over (12:18, 83). His model defines the term: no wailing to men, no complaint against heaven, grief carried upright before God alone. Exegetes therefore gloss beautiful patience as endurance without grievance aired to creatures, suffering that neither performs nor curdles. The adjective transforms a burden into an adornment. Anyone can wait; the Prophet must wait beautifully, his composure itself a sermon to watching Makkah. Strain will show — he was human — but strain need not sour. What the verse commands is not numbness but nobility under pressure.

**The Prophet Is the First Addressee**

The singular imperative speaks to Muḥammad in his shop of sorrows. Mockery dogged his steps, boycott had starved his clan, and men like the challenger of verse 1 demanded miracles of doom as sport. Into that furnace the verse pours cool oil: endure, and endure beautifully. Personal address carries personal tenderness — heaven notices the Prophet's wound and binds it with command. Yet the singular widens by example, as prophetic orders always do. Believers facing their own mockers inherit the same imperative; the command's grammar may be singular but its congregation is plural. Every Muslim scorned for faith stands addressed here, summoned to Jacob's standard. The verse thus pastors two flocks at once — the Messenger in his extremity, and his followers in theirs. What steadied him in Makkah steadies them everywhere.

**Patience Was Never Abrogated**

A famous dispute surrounds this verse. Some early commentators held that commands to endure unbelievers were later abrogated by permission to fight — patience as wartime tactic, superseded when swords were unsheathed. Others, led by al-Ṭabarī, insisted the command holds at all times and in all situations; still others classed it as definitive (muḥkam), unabrogatable by nature. The majority instinct favors permanence, and for principled reasons: abrogation touches specific legal rulings, not universal virtues (2:106). Patience names a character, not a tactic — and characters do not expire with circumstances. Moreover the Prophet practiced beautiful patience after power came, forgiving Makkah at its conquest when vengeance lay ready to hand. The stronger view therefore keeps the verse permanently open. Swords may answer armies, but only patience answers scorn.

**Delay Belongs to Wisdom, Not Weakness**

The command answers the sūrah's opening tension. Verses 1-4 announced certain, unstoppable punishment — yet the challenger walked home unstruck. Why the wait? Beautiful patience supplies heaven's half of the answer: delay is policy, not paralysis. God postpones doom to ripen mercy, giving mockers mornings for repentance and believers years for witness. The Prophet's patience mirrors God's own — heaven absorbs insults without hurry, and the Messenger must reflect that calm. Impatience would imply doubt, as though vindication needed hurrying; patience confesses confidence that the Day keeps its appointment. This reframes every unanswered taunt. Silence from heaven is not absence but forbearance, the fifty-thousand-year Day approaching at its appointed pace. Those who grasp this can smile at scoffers the way farmers smile at winter — knowing spring's date is fixed.

**Endurance as the Believer's Craft**

Beyond its occasion, the verse founds a discipline. Every generation produces challengers — new taunts, same dare — and every believer must learn the beautiful wait. The craft has techniques: answering scorn with dignity rather than heat, measuring vindication in eternities rather than news cycles, and grieving privately before God while standing publicly upright. Jacob's precedent guides each step, proving that patience can coexist with tears. The sūrah will later list prayer's constancy among the righteous (70:22-23), and patience fuels that constancy — worship repeated across unrelieved years. Communities absorb the lesson corporately too: minorities under pressure survive by beautiful patience where retaliation would destroy them. The verse is thus less a moment's comfort than a lifetime's curriculum. Makkah's furnace forged the formula; every furnace since reuses it.

**Command Prepares the Contrast**

Verse 5 pivots the passage from threat to temperament, and what follows explains why patience is rational. The next two verses will contrast two visions of the Day — deniers seeing it impossibly far, God seeing it decisively near. Patience stands justified between those visions: the Prophet waits because heaven's sight, not earth's, tells true. Structure serves psychology here. Commands unsupported feel arbitrary; the sūrah therefore grounds its imperative in cosmology to come. Endurance is not whistling in the dark but walking by a nearer light. Readers should carry the command forward as a lens: everything the sūrah next describes — melting skies, ransomed children, churning fire — arrives prefaced by this call to beautiful patience. Terror and tenderness share the chapter. The next verses justify the wait by contrasting human farness with divine nearness.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:6

> They truly see this ˹Day˺ as impossible,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Distance Measured by Denial**

Baʿīdan — "far off" — diagnoses more than geography. The deniers deem the Day remote, and remoteness in their dialect means impossibility: what stands far enough away need never be faced. Exegetes gloss the term both ways — distant in time or impossible in fact — and the ambiguity is the point, since delay and denial feed each other. A threat postponed feels a threat dissolved; each quiet sunrise votes against the warnings. Such reasoning mistakes the map for the territory, treating personal unreadiness as cosmic unlikelihood. The verse exposes the maneuver with a single adjective. Farness here is not measured but manufactured — a moral verdict disguised as a calendar estimate. They call the Day far because nearness would demand repentance, and repentance is the one journey they refuse. Distance becomes disobedience spelled in miles.

**The Day They Deem Too Far to Fear**

"It" points back to the punishment of verses 1-4 and forward to the Day of verses 8-18 — the Hour under both its names. The deniers survey that horizon and shrug: too distant to dread, too doubtful to deter. Complacency of this kind needs no arguments; it feeds on routine, on harvests that arrive and deadlines that pass without apocalypse. Each ordinary day becomes evidence against the Extraordinary Day, though logically it evidences nothing but mercy's patience. The verse's "truly" (innahum) underscores the settled nature of their estimate — this is conviction, not passing doubt. They have weighed the warnings and found them light. What they cannot see is that weightlessness was the scale's fault, not the warnings'. Their farness is a cataract, and the sūrah writes as its surgeon.

**Farness as a Moral Verdict**

Seeing-far reveals the seer. In the Book's psychology, perception follows posture: the humble see truly, the arrogant see dimly, and distance judgments expose hearts. Those who call the Day far announce their own remoteness from God rather than the Day's remoteness from them. Projection inverts reality — the far thing is their own return, the near thing their own end. Other passages confirm the pattern: deniers deem resurrection unlikely precisely because they deem themselves unaccountable, twin denials from one root. The verse therefore reads sight as symptom. Ophthalmology of the soul would prescribe humility as corrective lens; without it, horizons stay hazy however bright the warnings. This moralizes epistemology without obscuring it. Knowing the Day is near requires not data but dread, and dread requires honesty. They see far because they stand far, and verse 7 will supply heaven's corrective prescription.

**Mockery Feeds on Imagined Distance**

The opening dare makes sense only against this backdrop of farness. Why did the challenger demand punishment sportively? Because he deemed it distant enough to joke about — thunder too far to fear becomes theater. Mockery always needs safety, real or imagined; nobody taunts a lion at arm's length. Perceived remoteness supplied the mockers' courage, turning eschatology into entertainment. The sūrah thus connects verse 1's dare with verse 6's diagnosis: the demand for doom and the dismissal of doom share one root in felt distance. Exposure of that root deflates the dare retroactively — the challenger looks less brave and more blind. His swagger depended on fog. Once heaven's nearness clears the air, the joke curdles. Readers tempted by sophisticated dismissal should note the mechanism: every generation's scorn feeds on the same imagined miles.

**Scripture Repeats the Warning**

The Book returns to hastening-mockery across many chapters, building a dossier on delay-based doubt. One passage records deniers bidding the Prophet to hasten the punishment (22:47); another warns that the Hour comes suddenly while they dispute (36:49-50, in sense); still others note the demand for instant doom as a standing symptom (16:1; 29:53-54). Each instance shares the structure verse 6 distills: distance assumed, urgency mocked, patience mistaken for impotence. Repetition across sūrahs signals typicality — this is not Makkah's quirk but humanity's reflex. Every age discounts futures it cannot invoice, treating postponement as permission. The Qur'an's persistence matches the disease's: warning restated till heeded or till arrival ends debate. Verse 6 condenses the whole file into one line. Behind its brevity stands a library of unheeded alarms, each now echoed in this single verdict of farness.

**Their Sight Will Sharpen Too Late**

Irony awaits the far-seers. The Day they deem distant will arrive with corrective lenses — suddenly every denier will see clearly, estimate accurately, and believe firmly. But clarity after arrival purchases nothing; the exam ends when the answers appear. Other passages stage this reversal vividly: eyes humbled, abasement covering, wishes for return all denied. Present farness guarantees future shock, since the unprepared suffer most what the prepared absorb. The verse thus functions as both diagnosis and forecast — you see far now, you will see near then, and the interval between is your only mercy. Urgency hides inside description. Each reading shortens the interval, bringing the far Day one verse nearer. Hearers who adjust their sight in time join the Prophet's patience; those who keep their cataracts keep their comedy until the trumpet removes both.

**Half of a Divine Antithesis**

Verse 6 deliberately dangles, ending with a comma in sense as well as sound. "They see it far" demands its counterweight, and verse 7 will supply it: "but We see it near." Antithesis is the sūrah's engine here — two sights, one Day, with truth residing entirely in heaven's estimate. The half-verse structure forces reciters forward; breath cannot rest on human farness but must complete the thought in divine nearness. Rhetoric mirrors theology: man's word never gets the last word. Readers should feel the incompleteness as invitation, leaning into the next line for resolution. What follows is not mere contrast but correction — the "but" that overturns worlds. The next verse completes the antithesis by declaring how near the Day stands.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:7

> but We see it as inevitable.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs of Seeing Collide**

Yarawnahu against narāhu — "they see it" against "We see it" — the sūrah stages a collision of visions. Same verb, same object, opposite estimates: farness versus nearness, impossibility versus inevitability. The parallel grammar spotlights the only variable that matters — the seer. Deniers peer through cataracts of desire; God beholds through perfect knowledge. One sight guesses, the other knows. Such antithetical pairing concentrates the whole passage's argument into two lines: verses 6-7 together form a scale, and every reader must choose which pan holds truth. The adversative "but" carries the verse's entire force, overturning human estimate with divine fact. No argument follows because none is needed — heaven's sight settles what earth's disputes. When God and mockers disagree about distance, only one of them has ever measured the road.

**Nearness Means Certainty, Not Schedule**

Qarīban — "near" or "imminent" — needs careful hearing. Divine nearness does not publish a date; the fifty-thousand-year Day may still tarry by human clocks. Nearness here means inevitability: the event stands so certain that, from eternity's viewpoint, it has effectively arrived. Scripture uses "near" this way consistently — the Hour is near though its hour stays hidden, judgment approaches though calendars cannot trap it. Such language reframes waiting entirely. Deniers count sunrises and feel safe; believers count certainties and feel urgency. The same interval looks endless to one sight and momentary to the other. This explains how the verse can call "near" a Day elsewhere measured in fifty millennia: length and nearness coexist once certainty replaces schedule as the measure. What cannot fail to come is already, in the only sense that matters, here.

**The Royal We of Majesty**

"We" speaks in heaven's plural, and most exegetes hear God majestic in first person — the royal We of power and grandeur. Al-Ṭabarī leads this majority, noting that only divine sight could correct human blindness with authority. A minority, represented by Ibn Kathīr's report, allows "the believers" as the seers — the righteous who fear the Day as though it stood at hand. Grounds divide accordingly: the majority stresses the verse's corrective force, which needs God's own voice; the minority stresses pastoral imitation, believers learning to see with heaven's eyes. The majority holds stronger, since verse 6's "they" demands a divine "We" for true antithesis — creatures against Creator, not faction against faction. Yet the minority preserves a devotional fruit: believers should adopt God's estimate, seeing near what deniers call far. Both readings converge in practice. Heaven sees near; earth's wise agree.

**Time Looks Different From the Throne**

Eternity revises every timetable. From the throne's perspective, the fifty thousand years of verse 4 compress to imminence — history's whole scroll lies open at once, its end visible beside its beginning. Human impatience measures from below, counting the Day far because many sunrises intervene; divine knowledge measures from above, counting it near because none can intervene to stop it. The disparity consoles the afflicted and unnerves the defiant in equal measure. Martyrs waiting for vindication learn their wait is short by heaven's clock; tyrants counting on delay learn their remainder is shorter still. Other passages strike the same note: a thousand earthly years pass as one day with your Lord (22:47), and the coming Hour is described as blinking near. Time is a creature, and creatures do not bind their Maker. Nearness is not haste; it is certainty viewed from eternity.

**Inevitable Comforts as It Threatens**

Qarīb cuts two ways, and the sūrah wields both edges. For deniers, nearness threatens — the doom they mocked approaches with quiet feet. For believers, nearness comforts — vindication they awaited stands at the door. Same Day, same nearness, opposite receptions: the difference lies entirely in preparation. This duality explains the verse's placement after the command to beautiful patience (70:5). Patience needs a horizon, and "near" supplies it — endure beautifully, for relief approaches. Without nearness, patience sags into indefinite gloom; with it, endurance gains a finish line. The Prophet's hardest years thus received their deepest consolation in one adjective. Every subsequent generation of the strained inherits the same syllable of hope. What threatens the scoffer steadies the sufferer, and the Book's economy wastes no word — one "near" serves two congregations.

**The Pivot of the Whole Passage**

Verses 6-7 form the hinge on which the sūrah's opening turns. Behind them stands the challenger's dare with heaven's four-verse reply; ahead stretch the Day's horrors in verses 8-18. The hinge works by contrast: human farness dismissed, divine nearness declared — and then the nearness gets displayed in melting skies and ransomed children. Structure serves conviction here. Abstract correction ("you misjudge distance") gains flesh through concrete scenes ("behold what approaches"). Readers pass from epistemology to imagery, from being told they err to being shown what nears. The pivot also reframes everything before it: the dare of verse 1 now looks doubly foolish, launched against a Day already at the threshold. Antithesis complete, the sūrah can proceed to description. Deniers see far; God sees near; the coming verses will show near.

**Vision Gives Way to Description**

Theology now yields to tableau. Having corrected the deniers' estimate, the Book will illustrate the nearness with scenes — skies like molten brass, mountains like tufts of wool, friends abandoning friends. Each image answers verse 6's "far" with visible imminence: look how detailed the approaching Day already appears. Detail implies proximity; what stands remote stays vague, while what nears gains features. The coming passage therefore functions as proof-by-portrait, nearness made imaginable. Readers should receive verses 8-18 as verse 7's exhibition hall, each horror a brushstroke in the near Day's likeness. The pivot from seeing to showing also quickens the pulse — argument walks, but imagery runs. What follows will run for eleven verses without pausing. The next verse opens the gallery with a sky turned to molten brass.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:8

> On that Day the sky will be like molten brass

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cosmos Begins to Melt**

Yawma takūnu — "on a Day when" — opens the gallery of horrors verse 7 promised. After estimates of distance, the sūrah displays the Day itself, beginning overhead. The sky, humanity's firmest ceiling, turns fluid first: solidity was always conditional, blueness a temporary lease. Cosmic unmaking follows a deliberate order across the Book's eschatology — heavens split, stars fall, mountains march, seas boil — and this passage contributes its own sequence from sky through mountains to abandoned friendships. Starting above the head maximizes dread; no shelter stands when shelter itself dissolves. The definite "that Day" points back to the punishment of verses 1-4 and the nearness of verse 7, binding description to warning. What deniers called far now gains a sky, a color, a texture. Vagueness was their refuge; detail evicts them.

**Brass, Dregs, or Burning Oil**

Muhl, rendered "molten brass," has exercised lexicographers for centuries. Some hear molten metal — copper, brass, or lead liquefied by furnace heat, glowing and viscous. Others hear the dregs of boiled oil, filthy residue scorching at the pot's bottom. Still others, following Qatādah, hear oozing blood or pus — bodily horror writ across the sky. Each reading draws support from usage elsewhere, and the strongest approach layers them: muhl names any molten, defiled fluid that burns whatever it touches. The common thread matters more than the choice — liquidity, heat, and loathsomeness combined. Translation must pick one edge; "molten brass" captures the glow and flow while muting the filth. The Arabic keeps all three. Whatever muhl precisely is, no one would wish it overhead. The sky becomes what the damned drink, and the universe's roof turns into torment's mirror.

**Hell's Drink Becomes Heaven's Roof**

A parallel passage sharpens the terror. Elsewhere the tree of punishment feeds sinners food like muhl, boiling in their bellies like scalding water (44:45). Same noun, same burning fluid — but there it is swallowed, while here it hangs overhead. The sūrah thus merges two torments: hell's interior menu becomes the sky's exterior canopy. Above and within, the damned meet the same substance; no direction offers relief. Such cross-linking rewards readers who hold the Book whole — muhl in one chapter illuminates muhl in another, and the Day's sky gains its full horror only beside hell's cauldron. Craftsmanship of this kind weaves eschatology into a single fabric rather than scattered threats. The fluid damned souls will drink already glowers above unrepentant heads. Judgment's kitchen and judgment's ceiling stock the same brew.

**The Familiar Blue Turned to Terror**

Inversion drives the image. Skies signify shelter, permanence, and divine calm — the tent spread by mercy, the blue proof of order. Turning that tent to molten brass unmakes the most basic human trust: that above means safe. The Day will teach, in one glance upward, that every shelter was lent and every lease expires. Colors preach here too. Blue distances and calms; molten brass looms and scorches. The sky's transformation thus reverses its daily sermon — from "rest under me" to "flee, and find no cover." Such reversals structure the whole passage: mountains soften to wool, friends abandon friends, children become currency. The world will un-teach everything it taught, and the sky leads the recantation. Hearers who loved clear heavens must imagine them curdled. What the eye trusted most betrays first.

**The Day Gets Its First Color**

Visual horror needs palette, and muhl supplies the passage's first pigment — the orange-yellow glare of liquefied metal. Coming verses will add wool-white mountains and the flushed desperation of offered ransoms, but brass-glare leads. Color makes the Day imaginable where abstraction failed; deniers who shrugged at "punishment" must now picture a sky. Imagination enlisted becomes conscience's ally — what the mind's eye sees, the heart starts fearing. The sūrah understands this psychology and paints accordingly, moving from doctrine through duration to sheer spectacle. Each sense will get its verse: sight here, then the social horror of severed friendships, then fire's appetite. The gallery hangs its first canvas overhead, where every eye must look. No one chooses whether to see the sky. On that Day, no one will choose what the sky shows.

**Makkah's Sky Will Not Stay Blue**

The image targets local complacency with precision. Makkah's traders navigated by stars, scheduled caravans by seasons, and trusted the heavens' routine as deeply as any people. Their sky was a business partner — predictable, contractual, safe. The verse voids the contract: the partner will melt, the routine will curdle, the blue will burn. What the merchant class deemed most permanent becomes the first exhibit of impermanence. Prophetic warnings often struck this way, toppling whatever each audience trusted most — harvests for farmers, walls for builders, skies for caravans. Universality hides inside locality: every age trusts some blue — markets, medicines, treaties — and every blue will brass over. The verse is therefore less meteorology than idolatry-smashing. Whatever stands between a heart and its Maker, however sky-high, melts first. Only the Maker remains solid when ceilings flow.

**The Eye Drops From Sky to Mountain**

Verse 8 fixes the gaze upward; verse 9 will lower it to the mountains, completing the vertical collapse. Sky melts above, peaks soften beside — between them no refuge stands. The sūrah paces its unmaking deliberately, one cosmic fixture per verse, letting each dissolve before dissolving the next. Readers descend the ladder of solidity rung by rung: heavens, then mountains, then friendships, then families offered as ransom. Each step removes a deeper trust. Mountains deserve their verse because they symbolize exactly what skies do not — earthly permanence, the proverbial immovable. If brass can flow and peaks can drift, nothing created holds still. The gallery has hung its first canvas; the second waits one verse below. The next verse softens the mountains into tufts of drifting wool.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:9

> and the mountains like ˹tufts of˺ wool.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mountains Soften to Wool**

Verse 8 melted the sky; verse 9 teases the mountains into fibers. Al-jibāl ka-l-ʿihn — mountains like carded wool — inverts the most fixed simile in human speech. Mountains mean permanence in every tongue; wool means lightness, drift, the handful that scatters at a breath. The Book stages this reversal identically in Sūrah 101 (101:5), where the same noun ʿihn describes the same unmaking. Doubled witness, single doom. ʿIhn denotes wool fluffed and teased for spinning — fibers separated, airy, adrift. What tectonics raised over eons, the Day unmakes in a moment. The conjunction "and" binds sky and peak into one collapse: above melts, beside dissolves, and between them no refuge stands. Geology itself will testify that only one permanence ever existed. When mountains go woolly, every lesser solidity has already failed.

**The Immovable Learns to Drift**

Proverbial firmness makes the mountains heaven's chosen exhibit. Humans swear by their stability — "firm as mountains" — and the Book elsewhere shows them passing like clouds on that Day (27:88), reduced to shifting sand-heaps (73:14), scattered to dust (77:10). Each image attacks a different facet of trust: motion against fixity, sand against rock, dust against mass. Wool adds texture to the sequence — not merely moved but transformed, solidity unmade into softness. The progression teaches by accumulation: no single metaphor exhausts the unmaking, so metaphors multiply. What resists one image falls to the next. Readers who trust geography must watch every landmark dissolve in turn. The Day does not relocate the mountains; it redefines them. Immovable becomes ungraspable, and proverbs collapse with their referents.

**Carded, Dyed, Scattered**

ʿIhn repays lexical lingering. The noun evokes wool carded — teased apart by combs into separate strands — and some lexicographers add dyed in various colors, suggesting peaks loosened into drifting hues. Either way lightness dominates: fibers that winds toss, fingers part, and children scatter. Mountains will hang in the Day's air the way thistledown hangs in summer — visible, touchable, utterly without weight. The image also inverts creation's order, where mountains were set as pegs to steady the earth; pegs pulled, the tent collapses. Such reversal runs through the whole passage: skies melt, peaks drift, friendships freeze, children become currency. Every created trust gets un-created in sequence. Wool-mountains preach what molten skies began — permanence was always loan, never possession. The Day repossesses everything, starting with the metaphors.

**Sky Melts Above, Peaks Dissolve Beside**

Verses 8-9 compose a diptych of vertical collapse. Above, the ceiling flows; beside, the pillars soften. Between melting roof and drifting wall, the human figure stands exposed — the passage's true subject, though unnamed till verse 10. The sūrah paces its unmaking one fixture per verse, letting each dissolve fully before dissolving the next. Readers descend solidity's ladder rung by rung, discovering each rung was mist. Order matters: heaven first, then earth, then society, then family — the Day strips shelter from outside in, like an auctioneer selling a house room by room. What remains when roof and wall go? Only the soul and its ledger, the subjects of coming verses. Cosmic demolition clears the stage for moral reckoning. Scenery removed, actors stand judged.

**What Permanence Was Always Worth**

Theology hides inside topography. Mountains symbolize whatever each age deems unshakable — empires, markets, dynasties, dogmas of progress. Their wooling announces that every such trust was misplaced from the start. Only the Maker outlasts His mountains; all else holds a lease the Day terminates. This relativizes without despising: mountains served nobly as pegs, watersheds, and landmarks, but service is not sovereignty. Mistaking servants for masters is idolatry's oldest form, whether the servant is stone or stock. The verse smashes the confusion with a simile. Wool cannot be worshipped, leaned on, or fled toward — and that is precisely the point. When the Day reveals mountains as fibers, it reveals every earthly refuge as threadbare. One shelter stands un-wooled, and the sūrah's later verses will name its address: prayer, charity, chastity, trust.

**Geology Undone in a Line**

Brevity is the verse's hammer. Seven words unmake the planet's skeleton — no process described, no mechanism offered, only the accomplished simile. Such compression assumes omnipotence the way everyday speech assumes sunrise: the Day does not struggle with mountains but redefines them effortlessly. The Book's eschatology consistently refuses mechanics, declining to explain how skies melt or peaks drift. Means belong to the Commander; results belong to the warned. This reticence also quickens dread — unexplained unmaking frightens more than explained, since explanation domesticates. Readers get the finished horror without the comforting physics. What remains is pure spectacle: look up at brass, look across at wool, and understand that nature kept no promises. The verse's shortness mimics the suddenness it announces. Mountains fall faster than sentences.

**Color Completes the Collapse**

Some lexicographers hear dyed hues inside ʿihn — wool teased and tinted, fibers of many colors drifting together. If mountains keep their varied strata while losing all solidity, the Day's sky will hang with slow ribbons of red, white, and dust, geology become aurora. Spectacle compounds dread: beauty itself enlisted as terror's palette. The Book elsewhere drapes the Day in striking colors — skies like molten brass, faces brightened or blackened — and tinted peaks would join that gallery. Whether or not hues are intended, lightness is certain, and lightness suffices to unmake every trust. Readers should picture the familiar ridgelines — gray granite, green slope, white cap — loosened into floating threads. What eyes verified daily as solid dissolves between glances. Color makes the collapse memorable; memorability makes it persuasive. Even the mountains' beauty will testify against reliance on them.

**Nature Fails, Then Society**

Cosmos dissolved, the sūrah turns to company. Verses 8-9 unmade shelter; verses 10-11 will unmake friendship — the Day strips refuges from outside in. This sequencing follows dread's logic: first the world fails, then the people in it. Readers who survive the mountains' wooling must next survive abandonment, discovering that fellow creatures offer no firmer hold than melting skies. The passage thus moves from geology to sociology, each collapse deeper than the last. What good are firm friends under a flowing sky? The next verse answers: none will even ask. With peaks turned to drifting fibers, the sūrah severs the bonds between the exposed souls below.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:10

> And no close friend will ask ˹about˺ their friends,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Friendship Freezes on That Day**

Lā yasʾalu ḥamīmun ḥamīman — no warm friend will ask after a warm friend. The doubled ḥamīm frames mutual intimacy: each was the other's confidant, table-companion, keeper of secrets. On that Day neither inquires. Asking after someone is friendship's smallest currency — a question costing nothing — yet even this copper goes unspent. Terror bankrupts affection entirely. The verse's absoluteness startles: not "few will ask" but none, loves dissolved universally. Exegetes stress the contrast with tribal solidarity, where kinsmen ransomed kinsmen and friends vouched for friends; the Day voids every such bond. What melts skies and wools mountains also freezes hearts. Social warmth was always weather, and the Day changes the climate. No inquiry, no greeting, no glance of recognition spared — each soul audits itself alone.

**Terror Privatizes Every Soul**

The Book explains this solitude across several passages. On that Day each person's own affair will suffice and swallow them (80:37); no kinship will function between them, nor will they ask after one another (23:101); no bearer will carry another's burden (35:18); parent and child will find the reckoning unshareable (31:33). Together these texts draw one portrait: dread so total it monopolizes attention. Drowning swimmers cannot save each other; the Day drowns every soul separately. Preoccupation is not cruelty but incapacity — hearts have no surplus left for courtesy. Such privatization reverses earthly life, where attention overflows toward others. The verse thus measures terror by its social cost: whatever unmakes friendship has unmade everything smaller too. When ḥamīm ignores ḥamīm, no bond on earth has survived. Solitude is the Day's universal uniform.

**The Warm Friend Goes Cold**

Ḥ-m-m, the root of ḥamīm, means heat — boiling water, fevered brow, passionate concern. The close friend is literally the warm one, heated with loyalty. The verse chills that warmth absolutely: the hot go cold, the near go strange. Etymology sharpens the pathos — language itself testifies what the Day destroys. Intimacy's vocabulary survives while intimacy perishes; ḥamīm still means warm friend, but no warm friends remain to bear the name. The Book loves such hollowed words, letting familiar terms echo in emptied halls. Readers feel the cold through contrast: today's warmth remembered against tomorrow's frost. What turns on the root is the completeness of loss. Not formal alliances but felt affections freeze — the verse targets love's core, not diplomacy's shell. Warmth itself is the casualty.

**Contrast With Earth's Loyalties**

Arabian society ran on ḥamiyyah — fierypartisanship, the kinsman's rush to his kinsman's aid. Blood feuds, tribal wars, and sanctuary customs all expressed one creed: never abandon your own. The verse voids that creed in a line. Where poets celebrated friends who stood firm in battle's dust, the Day stages friends who will not even speak. The contrast is deliberate and devastating — the highest earthly loyalty dissolves first. Such targeting universalizes: every culture sacralizes some bond — family, friendship, party, nation — and the verse dissolves each in turn. Whatever humans deem unbreakable, the Day breaks as prelude. This is not cynicism about love but theology of ultimacy: penultimate bonds serve their season, then yield to the only bond that stands — the soul's tie to its Lord. All else was rehearsal. The Day performs the reality.

**Self-Absorption as Judgment**

Incapacity to ask is itself punishment. The friend who ignores his ḥamīm suffers his own terror; neglect flows from dread, not malice. Yet the neglected suffers doubly — abandoned at the hour of greatest need. Mutual abandonment thus multiplies torment: each soul endures both its own fear and others' indifference. The verse's passive construction blurs agency appropriately — none asks, none is asked after; all inflict and all endure. Such symmetry defines the Day's social hell: isolation chosen on earth becomes isolation imposed in eternity. Those who refused the Prophets' fellowship find human fellowship withdrawn. Poetic justice operates socially here as it operated cosmically with melting skies. What souls did to truth, souls now do to each other. Abandonment answering abandonment, silence answering silence — the Day echoes earth.

**The Day Unmakes Society Last**

Order in the passage climbs from cosmos to company: skies melt (verse 8), mountains drift (verse 9), friendships freeze (verse 10). Society unmakes last because humans trust it most — when nature fails, we flee to each other. The Day removes even that refuge, leaving the soul truly alone for the first time. Sequencing serves dread's crescendo: each verse takes a deeper shelter. Readers descend from geology through sociology toward the self, discovering no floor beneath the final verse's friendlessness. What remains when friends fall silent? Only the bargaining of verses 11-14, where family itself becomes currency. The sūrah strips systematically — roof, wall, companion, kin — until nothing stands between the soul and its ledger. Architecture of abandonment, built one verse per wall. The next course removes sight's comfort.

**Courtesy Costs Too Much Then**

Asking after someone costs nothing on earth — a breath, a glance, a passing phrase. On that Day the same question costs more than any soul can pay. Attention is the currency of the Day, and terror spends it all on the self. This economics of dread explains the verse's absoluteness: not reluctance but bankruptcy. Manners are civilization's surplus, affordable only when survival feels secure; the Day removes the surplus by removing security. Even the eminent stand absorbed in their own reckoning until mercy releases them. If the righteous ration attention, the wicked have none to spare at all. Readers who find this cold should consider its mirror: earthly indifference to God now becomes mutual indifference then. Courtesy extended to truth today purchases company tomorrow. What friendship sows in this life, the Day's silence reaps.

**Sight Without Sympathy Follows**

Verse 10 denies asking; verse 11 will grant seeing — they will behold each other yet still abandon. The pairing is surgical: first remove inquiry, then remove even the comfort of shared blindness. To see the friend and stay silent wounds deeper than never meeting. The sūrah thus escalates solitude from absence to presence-without-aid, the loneliest condition imaginable. Readers should feel the vise tightening. Each verse narrows mercy's space: cosmos gone, friends mute, and next — eyes open but hands closed. The progression trains dread upward toward the fire's own appetite. With friendship frozen silent, the sūrah grants vision only to sharpen abandonment's edge.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:11

> ˹although˺ they will be made to see each other. The wicked will wish to ransom themselves from the punishment of that Day by their children,

**Expanded Commentary**

**They Will See and Still Abandon**

Yuṣṣarūnahum — "they will be made to see them" — grants vision while withholding mercy. Friends behold friends, kin recognize kin, yet verse 10's silence holds. Exegetes debate the tableau's arrangement: most hear mutual sight, all humanity exposed to all, recognition without rescue. Some report God displaying the damned to the believers as vindication; others hear angels shown the states of souls for witness. The majority reading carries the verse's main terror — seeing and abandoning together. Blindness would have been kinder; sight sharpens solitude into spectacle. Each soul watches beloved faces pass toward ruin, hands lowered, voice stilled. The passive verb assigns heaven's agency: God shows them to each other, staging recognition as part of reckoning. What eyes meet, hearts cannot help. Vision becomes a new faculty of torment.

**Vision Becomes Accusation**

Sight on that Day accuses. The wicked see the righteous ascending and read their own failure; the righteous see the wicked sinking and read mercy's magnitude. Believers behold mockers they once feared, now begging; mockers behold warners they once scorned, now serene. Every glance testifies. This mutual exposure reverses earthly privacy, where hearts hid behind faces and destinies stayed veiled. The Day flays all covering — states visible, ranks manifest, no pretense possible. Such transparency itself punishes: shame needs spectators, and heaven supplies multitudes. The verse's first clause therefore functions as sentence, not scenery. To be seen truly, by all, forever — this is exposure no curtain can soften. Eyes become courtrooms. What the wicked hid from neighbors, neighbors now witness; what they flaunted as strength stands confessed as folly. Seeing is judging.

**The Wicked Bid Their Children**

Al-mujrim — "the wicked" or "the guilty" — names the Day's defendants by their verdict. Mujrim denotes the criminal cut off from good, the convicted felon of eternity. Such a soul, beholding ruin, opens bargaining with its dearest currency: banīhi, his sons, his children. Ransom (yaftadī) means buying oneself free by substitute payment — a prisoner offering gold for his own neck. Here the gold is offspring. That children lead the bidding measures both love and terror: love, because nothing exceeds their worth; terror, because even that worth gets tendered. The verse's "will wish" (yawaddu) stresses futility — wishing, not transacting. No counterparty accepts; heaven's justice takes no substitutes. Yet the wish itself testifies, revealing what the wicked valued and how ruin revalues all. Love's summit becomes terror's tender.

**Love Inverted by Terror**

Parental instinct counts among nature's deepest laws — mothers shield young with their bodies, fathers spend lives for children's bread. The verse inverts that law absolutely: the parent offers the child as shield for himself. Such reversal gauges horror beyond description; whatever unmakes parenthood has unmade everything. **Ransom Refused in Advance**

**The Auction Opens With the Priceless**

**The Bidding Climbs Higher**

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:12

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ransom Widens to the Household**

Wa-ṣāḥibatihi wa-akhīh — "his spouse and his brother" — continues the auction verse 11 opened. Children failed as tender; now the wicked bid the household's remaining pillars. Only the lots grow, and the growing is the torment.

Ṣāḥibah — "spouse" or "female companion" — derives from ṣ-ḥ-b, companionship, the root of fellowship and friendship. The wife is literally the consort, the sharer of life's road. Tendering her means offering the soul's nearest companion, the witness of private years. The Book elsewhere honors this bond — spouses as garments to one another, mercy placed between them — which makes its tendering here doubly appalling. Readers should feel the tenderness inverted: hands that once clasped now push forward as payment. Love's sanctuary becomes terror's treasury. What the wicked cherished closest, they spend fastest — proof that dread unmakes even devotion's deepest chamber.

**Brother: Blood Put on the Block**

Akh — "brother" — carries tribal Arabia's heaviest freight. The brother was defender, avenger, co-owner of honor; abandoning him meant disgrace worse than death. Tendering him therefore inverts the culture's supreme loyalty — blood offered to save blood, kinship spent like coin. The singular generalizes: brothers, kin, the whole fraternity of birth. Such tendering confesses bankruptcy absolute — when a man bids his brother, no asset remains unspent. The verse's order also climbs intimacy's ladder in reverse: children (one's future), spouse (one's present), brother (one's past and blood). Each lot reaches deeper into identity. **Each Verse Raises the Stakes**

Auction-architecture deserves study. Verse 11 offered children; verse 12 adds spouse and sibling; verse 13 will tender the sheltering clan; verse 14 will bid all earth's inhabitants together. Rhetoric of escalation ensures dread compounds — readers grasp at each ring that the previous insufficiency repeats at grander scale. If children failed, households fail; if households fail, tribes fail; if tribes fail, worlds fail. Logic of futility, staged theatrically. The sūrah could have listed all lots in one line; instead it spends four verses, forcing reciters to pronounce each failure separately. **Terror Unmakes the Home**

Household is humanity's last fortress — when tribes scatter and markets crash, the family table still gathers. The verse storms even that keep. Spouse and sibling, the two bonds no storm should sever, get tendered like furniture at an estate sale. Such unmaking completes the passage's inward march: cosmos melted, mountains drifted, friends silenced, and now the home's hearth auctioned. Each collapse struck deeper shelter; this one strikes deepest. What the Day leaves its victims is instructive — nothing. No roof, no peak, no friend, no spouse, no brother: the soul stands denuded of every belonging, clutching only its record. Stripping is the Day's method and message. Everything the wicked called "mine" gets tendered, refused, and removed. Only what they gave for God — never tendered because never owned that way — will stand beside them. ## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:13

> their clan that sheltered them,

**The Clan That Sheltered Gets Spent**

Faṣīlatihi-llatī tuʾwīh — "his clan that sheltered him." Faṣīlah names the kin-circle — extended family, subtribe, the sheltering ring beyond the household. Tuʾwīh, "gave him refuge," recalls years of protection: blood-money paid, enemies deterred, honor upheld. Now the wicked tender that whole sheltering circle as ransom. Gratitude inverts to expenditure — protectors become payment. The relative clause twists the knife: the clan is bid precisely as shelterer, its sheltering cited on the auction block like provenance on goods. Such is terror's ingratitude. What refuge did for decades gets spent in a verse. Readers should feel loyalty's last fortress fall: household already tendered, now the tribe. Protection purchased with devotion gets sold for survival. The bid will fail, but the bidding itself convicts. Even shelter gets spent by the unsheltered soul.

**Shelter Offered as Sacrifice**

Refuge becomes ransom — the inversion crowns the auction's pathos. On earth the clan absorbed blows meant for its member; on the Day the member offers the clan to absorb his. Direction of sacrifice reverses: protectors protected, now protectors proffered. Such reversal measures dread absolutely — whatever unmakes gratitude has unmade everything. The verse's economy is exact: tuʾwīh (sheltering, imperfect, habitual) describes years of cover in one verb. Years of cover, tendered in three words. Ingratitude on this scale needs no commentary; the grammar convicts. Yet the tendering also confesses — the bidder admits, by bidding, that clan-shelter always pointed beyond itself. True refuge was never tribal. Tribes sheltered bodies for decades; only God shelters souls forever. The auction teaches by failing: every spent shelter proves unsheltering. What cannot save gets spent; what could save was never sought.

**Tribe or Mother**

Faṣīlah's sense divides exegetes. Most — Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī — hear the tribe or kin-group, the outer ring of belonging. Some — also reported by Ibn Kathīr and Qatādah — hear the mother, the nearest shelter of infancy. Grounds pull both ways. Tribe fits the auction's widening geometry: children, household, clan, world — each ring larger. Mother fits tuʾwīh's intimacy: none shelter like a mother. Context favors tribe decisively: verse 14's "all on earth" completes an outward expansion, and mother would narrow what must widen. Yet the minority preserves pathos worth keeping — the bidder would spend even her. Strong reading keeps tribe as sense and mother as echo: the clan tendered, maternal love implied in its sheltering. Both agree on the essential — nearest refuge spent. The auction's third ring holds whatever sheltered most.

**The Third Ring of Belonging**

Geometry governs the auction: children (verse 11), spouse and sibling (verse 12), clan (verse 13), all earth (verse 14). Concentric circles, each wider — self's loves rippling outward to humanity's sum. The clan-ring matters structurally: it bridges intimate household and abstract world. Without it the leap from sibling to planet would strain; with it escalation breathes naturally. Each ring also widens futility's proof: if dearest loves failed, wider ones fail doubly. Readers traverse the rings like Dante's circles inverted — ascending attachment, descending hope. The sūrah spends one verse per ring, pacing desperation's sprawl. Pronunciation participates: every tongue voicing the lots rehearses their voidance. Third ring tendered, third refusal echoing. One ring remains — the widest, the world — and then the gavel verse 15 will bring down.

**Tribalism Meets Its Terminus**

Arabian pride ran on faṣīlah-loyalty — the clan's honor defended unto death, its members ransomed at any price. Earthly auctions ran the opposite direction: clans spent wealth to free members. The Day reverses the market — members spend clans to free themselves. Such inversion terminates tribalism as soteriology: blood cannot buy what blood never earned. The Book elsewhere voids kinship on that Day — no lineage honored, no intercession unpermitted (23:101) — and this verse stages the voiding. Clan-solidarity, noblest of earthly bonds, dissolves into currency. This is not contempt for kinship but theology of ultimacy: penultimate shelters serve their season, then yield. Souls that made tribes their trust discover the trust denominated in vanishing coin. Only the shelter that never needed sheltering stands. Tribes fall with all flesh; God remains unsheltered need.

**Protection That Cannot Protect**

Irony seals the verse: the sheltering clan cannot shelter. Tuʾwīh describes past protection precisely to expose present impotence — shelterers who need no shelter spent by one who does. False refuge is the auction's whole theme: children, spouses, clans tendered as though beloveds could ransom. Each lot confesses the same error — trusting creatures for what only the Creator grants. The clan's failure teaches generally: every earthly shelter leaks at the last. Fortresses fall, immunities expire, patrons perish. Only one refuge never failed a refugee, and the wicked seek it never. Their bidding anatomizes misplaced trust lot by lot. Readers still choosing shelters should choose advisedly. Spend devotion on the Unsheltered Shelter now, and no clan need be tendered then. Protection that cannot protect is poetry; protection that cannot fail is God.

**Shelter Inverted Throughout**

Tuʾwīh — sheltering — runs through the Book as mercy's verb: God shelters the orphan, believers shelter the fugitive, and the cavern sheltered the sleepers. Here alone sheltering gets spent — refuge tendered as ransom. The inversion is absolute: mercy's verb drafted into terror's auction. Such reversal measures the Day's power to unmake even language's pieties. Shelterers become currency; refuge becomes tender. Yet the verb's holy history also judges the bidder — he spends in panic what saints gave in love. Those who sheltered prophets earned eternity's shelter; he who auctions his shelterers earns Laẓā. The same deed-word divides destinies by direction: given upward in charity, shelter saves; tendered outward in terror, it voids. Readers still holding roofs over others should hold them tighter. Shelter given freely today is shelter kept eternally.

**The World Comes Last**

Clan tendered, one lot remains — all who dwell on earth, bid together. The auction's widening nears its limit: from children to planet in four verses. Readers should feel totality approaching — every belonging spent, every love liquidated, the whole world weighed against one skin. Desperation's geometry completes its circle. Then heaven speaks. Verse 15's single syllable will void the entire proceedings, and verses 16-18 will describe the fire awaiting the bidders. The next verse tenders the whole earth at once, closing the auction's final lot.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:15

> But no! There will certainly be a raging Flame

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Syllable Closes the Auction**

Kallā — "nay," "no," "never." One syllable voids four verses of bidding. Most exegetes hear denial of the ransom: no ransom accepted, no bargaining permitted, the auction void. A minority, following Qatādah, hears affirmation — "truly" or "indeed" — pivoting to the fire's certainty. Grounds favor denial: the auction context demands refusal, and kallā typically rebuts what precedes. Yet the minority preserves truth — the fire is indeed certain — folding affirmation into the denial's aftermath. Strong reading keeps denial primary: NO to ransom, then behold the flame. The syllable's brevity is its force; heaven answers planetary bidding with two consonants. Length of pleading meets shortness of verdict. Readers feel the gavel in the gutturals — kallā cracks like a slammed door. Four verses knocked; one syllable bars. Bargaining ends forever.

**Laẓā: The Fire That Churns**

Laẓā names hell by its motion — blazing, churning, leaping fire. The root l-ẓ-y evokes flame's restless flicker; al-Jalālayn derives it from talaẓẓā, to blaze fiercely. This is fire as verb rather than noun — not a place but a devouring. Innahā laẓā, "it is Laẓā," identifies the threat with terrible particularity: not fire generally but this fire, the churning one. Definiteness personalizes torment — Laẓā awaits its own the way hosts await guests. The Book names hell's chambers distinctly (jahannam, saʿīr, saqar, laẓā), and each name preaches: Gehenna the deep, Blaze the fierce, Scorch the searing, Churn the restless. Laẓā's restlessness suits the bidders — their desperation churned through lots; the fire churns through them. Appetite answers appetite. What they fed with loves, the flame feeds with them.

**Hell Gets a Proper Name**

Proper names particularize dread. "Punishment" threatens abstractly; "Laẓā" threatens personally, the way named storms frighten more than "bad weather." The sūrah withholds the name across fourteen verses — punishment, Day, torment unnamed — then pronounces it at the auction's close. Timing maximizes impact: bidders who named every love now hear the one name that matters. Naming also dignifies the warning — Laẓā is no metaphor but destination, chambers prepared and hungry. Other passages summon Laẓā's voice: the fire that calls its own (verse 17 will develop this), flames that strip and summon. Readers should receive the name as address — the auction's losers now know where they are going. Vagueness was mercy's last veil; naming removes it. Laẓā stands pronounced, churning, certain.

**The Answer Fits the Bidder**

Poetic justice links lot to flame. The wicked bid loves to escape burning; burning takes them because they bid instead of repenting. Their churning desperation — lot after lot, higher and hollower — mirrors Laẓā's churning appetite. What they did with affections, the fire does with them: spends rapidly, accepts nothing. The mockers of verse 1 demanded punishment sportively; Laẓā answers the demand literally. Dare for doom, receive doom — heaven grants the wager's letter while voiding its spirit. Such fittingness runs through every eschatological passage: hoarded wealth brands hoarders, neglected prayers accuse neglecters, demanded doom devours demanders. The verse's innahā ("indeed it") underscores inevitability — no appeal from Laẓā's certainty. Fire fits bidder the way sentence fits crime. The auctioneer's gavel was never coming; the fire's embrace always was.

**Certainty Replaces Bargaining**

Grammar shifts from wishing to being. Verses 11-14 ran on yawaddu, "he will wish" — optative, futile, unanswered. Verse 15 runs on innahā, "indeed it is" — declarative, certain, final. Mood-change marks regime-change: human bargaining ends, divine fact begins. Wishing occupied four verses; being occupies the rest. Such pivoting teaches prayer's limits — wishes expire at death, facts commence after. The living wish usefully (repentance, charity, prayer all count as wishing rightly); the damned wish vainly (ransom, return, relief all void). Timing divides fruitful from futile: wish now, receive then; wish then, receive Laẓā. Readers stand on wishing's frontier — every breath still converts wishes to deeds. The verse's certainty should terrify only the procrastinating. For the prepared, Laẓā's certainty confirms mercy's: both promises keep. Bargaining closed; being begins.

**Refusal Preached as Mercy**

Kallā wounds to heal — the living, that is. The damned hear refusal as sentence; the living hear it as warning, and warning is mercy. Every terror in the passage serves the unsealed ear: learn ransom's futility now, while charity still ransoms. The Book's severest verses are thus its kindest, scaring souls toward safety. Preachers have long wielded Laẓā this way — not to despair hearers but to detach them from auction-thinking. What will you tender on that Day? Nothing avails — so tender devotion today. The verse's "but no" also guards divine honor: heaven cannot be bought, justice cannot be bid for, and the attempt insults. Refusal protects God's transcendence as well as souls. A purchasable paradise would be no paradise. Kallā preserves both grace and grandeur — salvation free, never for sale.

**Denial Echoes the Deniers**

Kallā answers kallā. The wicked spent their lives saying no — no resurrection, no reckoning, no Lord — and heaven returns their syllable with interest: no ransom, no escape, no appeal. Negation boomerangs; the word they wielded becomes the word that seals them. Such echo-justice runs through the Book: mockers mocked, forgetters forgotten, deniers denied. Readers should weigh their own noes carefully. Every refusal of truth rehearses this kallā; every acceptance rehearses mercy's yes. The auction's bidders said no to warnings across lifetimes, then heard one No across eternity. Syllables outlive speakers. Choose yours as though heaven will quote them — it will.

**The Flame Gets Hungry**

Verse 15 names Laẓā; verses 16-18 will feed it — scalps stripped, summoners called, hoarders claimed. The sūrah moves from naming to appetite, refusing to let the noun rest abstract. Readers should brace for intensification: fire named, then fire feeding. Each coming verse adds a horror — flaying, calling, claiming — until the auction's bidders stand fully described as fuel. Naming was the sentence; appetite is its execution. The passage's dread thus compounds verse by verse, Laẓā growing more vivid as its company assembles. ## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:16

> ripping off scalps.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fire Strips Its Prey**

Nazzāʿatan li-l-shawā — "a stripper of scalps." Nazzāʿah intensifies nazʿ, to pull off, tear away, strip — the form (faʿʿālah) denotes habitual, professional action. Laẓā does not merely burn; it flays, tears, scalps by trade. The feminine agrees with fire (nār), grammatically female, appetite grammatically fixed. Stripping exceeds burning in horror: burns destroy, but stripping removes — skin from skull, covering from core. Exposure compounds pain; the flayed suffer nakedness with searing. Such personification makes fire a butcher rather than element — skilled, repeated, relentless. The verse's three Arabic words hit like hammer-blows: stripper, of, scalps. No verb wasted, no mercy admitted. Readers meet Laẓā at work, not at rest. Naming ended; feeding begins. What the flame does habitually, this verse exhibits once — and once suffices forever.

**Scalps, Skins, or All**

Shawā's scope divides exegetes. Most — Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī — hear the scalp: skin of the head, hair's foundation, honor's crown. Some — also al-Ṭabarī — widen to all skin: the body's whole covering stripped. Others, via Ibn Kathīr, widen further: everything stripped except one part — variously bones, fingertips, or facial features. Grounds accumulate with widening: scalp fits the noun's primary sense; skin fits flaying's logic; all-but-one fits intensiveness. Strong reading layers them — scalp certainly, skin probably, all-but-remnant possibly. The verse's horror survives every option; precision would only localize what widening universalizes. Heads matter symbolically: scalps crown the proud, and pride crowned the damned. What the arrogant raised highest, Laẓā removes first. Flaying starts at the top — literally. Whatever shawā's edge, its center is the head.

**The Heart Left Crying**

Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's reading pierces deepest: the fire burns all, leaving only the heart — and the heart cries out. Flesh stripped, frame consumed, yet the core conscious and wailing. This concentrates torment exquisitely: whatever can suffer remains, whatever shields is gone. The heart that refused to soften on earth stays tender in fire — sensitivity preserved as capacity for pain. Reports attribute the image to al-Ḥasan via Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī, and its power needs no chain: every conscience recognizes itself. Hearts that would not break in repentance break in ruin. The reading also theologizes pain's purpose — not annihilation but awareness. Laẓā keeps its victims sentient the way furnaces keep metal molten: suffering requires subject. Annihilation would be mercy; persistence is the sentence. The crying heart preaches to unbroken ones: break now in sorrow, or break then in fire.

**Pain Described by Removal**

Stripping differs from burning, and the difference terrifies. Burns assault; stripping undresses — each layer removed exposes deeper sensitivity. The verse chooses removal's vocabulary (nazʿ) over combustion's, picturing Laẓā as unraveling its victims. Such imagery suits the auction's aftermath: bidders who stripped themselves of loves get stripped of skin. Poetic justice flays: self-denuding answered by denuding. Removal also prolongs — stripping proceeds layer by layer, pain renewed with each. Burns might numb; flaying never does. The Book's hell-imagery varies torments deliberately — boiling, freezing, crushing, stripping — each targeting a different dread. Nazzāʿah targets the dread of exposure: to be uncovered utterly, inside displayed. What verse 18 of Sūrah 69 threatened (no secrets hidden), Laẓā executes on flesh. Stripped souls, stripped bodies — exposure complete.

**The Body Testifies Against Itself**

Flayed flesh preaches fitted recompense. Bodies that served defiance — strong hands hoarding, proud heads scorning, swift feet fleeing truth — get dismantled piece by piece. The Book elsewhere stages limbs testifying: hands speaking, feet witnessing, skins confessing (36:65; 41:20). Laẓā's stripping anticipates that testimony, unmaking the instruments of sin. Hands that withheld charity lose their skin; scalps raised in pride lose their covering. Dismantling is thus a moral audit: each removed part answers a misused gift. Readers should inventory their own members — every faculty a trust, every trust an audit. What the fire removes piecemeal, obedience would have preserved whole. The verse anatomizes accountability: bodies are not selves' property but loans. Misused loans get repossessed violently. Keep the scalp by bowing the head; Laẓā scalps only the unbowed.

**Intensive Form, Intensive Torment**

Arabic morphology preaches here. Nazzāʿah's faʿʿālah pattern denotes professional intensity — the butcher slaughters, the tailor stitches, the stripper strips. Laẓā flays by occupation, expertly, endlessly. The intensive form forbids imagining accidental burns or incidental pain; this is torment's trade, practiced with craftsman's consistency. Grammar also implies repetition: faʿʿāl does its deed again and again. Stripped skin regrows for re-stripping — other passages confirm skins renewed for renewed tasting. Eternity needs such morphology; one-time verbs would expire. Nazzāʿah never finishes because finishing would end the sentence. Readers meet in one noun the whole doctrine of perpetuated pain. What the intensive promises, eternity performs. The stripper strips without rest, by grammatical profession and divine decree.

**Honor Stripped With Skin**

Scalps crown more than skulls — they crown honor. Shaving heads disgraced captives in ancient custom; exposing crowns humbled kings. Laẓā's stripping therefore shames as it sears, pairing pain with public disgrace. The proud who raised their heads against warning lose the heads' covering before multitudes. Such doubling is deliberate: hell punishes the sin's relish as well as its deed, and pride's relish was display. Display reversed becomes exposure — the audience remains, the dignity departs. Other torments hide their victims (depths, darkness, bellies); stripping exhibits them, flayed gooseflesh under heaven's gaze. Shame needs spectators, and the Day supplies all humanity. Readers who guard reputation above righteousness should note the exchange: guard humility now, or guard wounds then. What pride lifts, Laẓā lowers — skinning honor with hide.

**The Fire Calls Its Own**

Stripping described, summoning follows. Verse 17 will show Laẓā calling — inviting, claiming, summoning its destined company. Appetite plus voice completes personification: the fire feeds and speaks. Readers should brace for the guest-list: back-turners and hoarders, named and claimed. The sūrah moves from flame's teeth to flame's tongue, dread compounding. What strips scalps also summons souls — Laẓā wants its own, and wants them loudly. The next verse gives the fire its voice, calling all who turned away.

## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:18

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wealth Gathered, Mercy Scattered**

Jamaʿa fa-awʿā — "gathered and stored." Jamaʿa piles up; awʿā stows away — accumulation plus retention, earning plus embalming. Wiʿāʾ, the noun behind the verb, means vessel, container: the hoarder turns wealth into contents sealed from circulation. Gathering serves life; hoarding serves fear — fear of poverty, of future, of dependence on God. The fa- links piling to storing as habit: gathered, therefore stowed; earned, therefore entombed. Such pairing indicts not possession but posture — hands open to receive, closed to release. Wealth's purpose is flow: provision through the rich to the poor. Hoarding dams the flow, flooding the hoarder while parching neighbors. Readers audit their vessels: does wealth pass through toward need, or pool behind fear? Containers confess. What you store against God, Laẓā counts as fuel.

**The Twin Verse in Sūrah 104**

Sūrah 104 opens on the same figure: the slandering backbiter who amasses wealth and tallies it (104:1-2). Twin portraits, twin dooms — gathering-plus-counting there, gathering-plus-storing here. The pairing instructs: hoarding's sociology (slander guards wealth; wealth funds pride) meets hoarding's eschatology (Laẓā summons both). Counting deserves note — the 104 hoarder numbers his piles, affection quantified. Numbers replace neighbors; tallies replace ties. Together the verses anatomize miserliness whole: mouth wounding (slander), hands clutching (hoarding), heart counting (tallying). Laẓā claims the complete figure — back turned on truth (verse 17), hands closed to poor (verse 18), tongue loose on fellows (104). Readers should hold both portraits together: wealth's disease infects speech, fists, and arithmetic alike. The cure is one — open hands. Give, and Laẓā's summons never names you.

**Hoarding Means Withholding Zakāh**

Exegetes specify the crime: refusing the obligatory alms, zakāh — the poor's due portion in the rich's wealth. Hoarding here is not mere saving but withholding what God assigned others. The poor own a share of every fortune; the hoarder expropriates it. A blistering tableau elsewhere details the wage: hoarded gold and silver heated in hellfire, branding foreheads, sides, and backs — "taste what you hoarded" (9:34-35). Branding fits hoarding part by part: foreheads that frowned at beggars, sides turned away, backs presented. Such fittingness preaches proportion — members that withheld get marked. Readers should translate doctrine to dirhams: zakāh calculated, zakāh paid, conscience clear. The verse threatens not wealth but theft-from-God. What He earmarked for the poor was never yours. Withhold it, and Laẓā collects with interest.

**Containers Against the Day**

Awʿiyah — vessels, storages — multiply in vain. Vaults, accounts, portfolios: containers against contingency. Yet the Day opens all vessels, audits all stores, voids all hedging. What containers kept from the poor, fire claims from the keeper. Futility crowns the verse: stored wealth saves no storer. The auction of verses 11-14 already proved it — children, clans, worlds tendered and refused. Hoards join the refused lots. Such economics invert earth's: saving secures here, spending secures there. The wise transpose currencies — earthly stores into heavenly credit via charity. Fools maximize the wrong account, arriving rich in voided coin. Readers choose containers daily: vaults that Laẓā opens, or charities that mercy keeps. One storage survives the Day — what was given. Vaults burn with their keepers; alms await them.

**Gathering Is Not the Sin — Clutching Is**

Balance guards the verse against misuse. Earning is lawful, provision praiseworthy, wealth itself a potential blessing — the Book honors lawful acquisition and commands seeking bounty. Jamaʿa alone never damns; fa-awʿā damns — gathering plus sealing, earning plus embalming. The sin is clutching: receiving without releasing, holding without heeding. Such precision protects merchants and farmers while warning misers. Hands may fill; they must also pour. The prophets owned, traded, and inherited — yet their stores flowed. Readers should neither romanticize poverty nor demonize profit; the verse targets neither. It targets the sealed vessel, the stopped flow, the dammed mercy. Earn freely, spend dutifully, and Laẓā's summons passes over. Wealth is water: life in motion, rot in stagnation. Keep it moving toward need.

**Backs and Bank Accounts Together**

Verses 17-18 pair creed with deed: backs turned (belief's desertion), hands closed (mercy's refusal). Laẓā's company holds both defects — wrong facing, wrong fisting. The pairing completes the portrait: truth declined, poor denied. Neither defect alone tells the whole story; together they convict the whole person. Such pairing recurs across the Book — belief-plus-deeds as salvation's twin wings. Right facing without open hands limps; open hands without right facing wanders. Laẓā summons those lacking both. Readers audit both wings: face toward truth? hands toward poor? The verses stage judgment's two questions in order. Answer each rightly, and the fire's call never sounds. The sūrah will soon list the opposite company — praying, giving, believing (verses 22-35). Two guest-lists: Laẓā's and the Garden's. Choose yours by posture and purse.

**Vessels Sealed, Hearts Sealed**

Awʿā seals vessels; sealing vessels seals hearts. The hoarder's wiʿāʾ (container) and qalb (heart) harden together — closed fists, closed sympathies. Wealth dammed stops mercy flowing: the sealed vault teaches the sealed breast. Such is miserliness's physiology — avarice as cardiac condition. Giving reverses both sealings at once: opened hands open hearts, outflows restore circulation. The Book's charity-therapy works medically — spending cures clutching by exercising release. Each gift loosens the next; generosity compounds like interest. Readers should treat giving as cardiology: prescribe regular doses, increase gradually, monitor warmth returning. Hoards harden; alms soften. What awʿā closed, ṣadaqah reopens — vessels first, then hearts. Laẓā claims the sealed; mercy claims the opened. Break one seal today, and both begin healing.

**Human Nature Gets Diagnosed Next**

Back-turners summoned, hoarders claimed — now the sūrah diagnoses the disease beneath both. Verses 19-21 will anatomize anxious humanity: fretful in hardship, grudging in ease. Turning and hoarding prove symptoms; impatience is the syndrome. Readers should receive the coming diagnosis personally — the passage aims inward. Laẓā's fuel is not alien stuff but familiar frailty. The next verses expose the anxious heart that turns backs and seals vessels.
