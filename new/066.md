# Sūrah al-Taḥrīm (Chapter 66) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Named for a Prohibition**

Al-Taḥrīm, "the Prohibition," is a Madinan sūrah of twelve verses, generally placed around the seventh year after the migration, though a minority of scholars have held that only its first nine verses are Madinan. It takes its name from its opening line, in which the Prophet ﷺ is asked why he has forbidden himself something God made lawful. Because it deals so directly with his household, the early scholars also called it Sūrat al-Nabī, "the sūrah of the Prophet," and it is sometimes referred to by its opening words, *Limā tuḥarrimu* — "Why do you forbid?"

That a chapter of scripture opens by correcting the man through whom the scripture arrived is the first thing to understand about it. The correction concerns a small domestic matter: an oath sworn to settle friction among his wives. It is recorded, preserved, and recited, together with the state of two of those wives' hearts, the warning they received, and the month in which the Prophet ﷺ withdrew from his household while Madina speculated. A text designed to build an untouchable authority would not contain this material.

**From One Household to Every Household**

The sūrah moves outward in stages. Verses 1–5 deal with the Prophet's home — the oath and its release, a confidence broken between two wives, the diagnosis that their hearts had tilted, and a warning framed as a possibility about replacement. Verse 6 turns to all believers with the command to guard themselves and their families from a Fire, and verse 7 quotes what will be said to those who arrive with excuses. Verse 8 calls the whole community to sincere repentance and describes the light the believers will carry. Verse 9 turns outward again, to the struggle against deniers and hypocrites.

The final three verses supply the chapter's conclusion in the form of four women. Two of them — the wives of Noah and Lot — lived in prophetic households and were counted with the deniers. One, the wife of Pharaoh, lived in a tyrant's palace and asked for a house near her Lord. The last, Mary the daughter of ʿImrān, had no husband at all and is described as among the devoutly obedient. Arranged in that order, the examples establish that a household is where salvation is worked on and not the thing that delivers it.

**Why It Follows al-Ṭalāq**

The two chapters form a natural pair and are placed together. The previous sūrah legislated divorce for the community at large; this one turns to the marriage of the Prophet ﷺ himself, and to the principle that closeness to a righteous man confers nothing automatically. It also complements the sixtieth sūrah, whose closing verses record women making their own pledge to the Prophet ﷺ — a pledge given in their own names rather than through their husbands.

What holds the chapter together is a single claim applied at every level: each soul carries its own file. The Prophet ﷺ is corrected in his; his wives are addressed in theirs; every believer is told to guard his family and warned that excuses will not be received; and the closing examples show two women ruined despite their marriages and two vindicated without them. The sūrah that opens inside a marriage ends by establishing that the unit finally weighed is the person.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:1

> O Prophet! Why do you prohibit ˹yourself˺ from what Allah has made lawful to you, seeking to please your wives? And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Functions as a Correction**

The sūrah opens by asking the Prophet ﷺ a question to which the questioner knows the answer. *“Why do you prohibit ˹yourself˺ from what Allah has made lawful to you”* (66:1) is not a request for information; it is the form a correction takes when it is addressed to someone honoured. The interrogative softens nothing about the substance, but it does place the Prophet ﷺ in the position of a man invited to examine his own reasoning rather than a defendant receiving a verdict.

That a scripture preserves the correction of its own founder is worth registering. A text composed to build the authority of a leader would not open a chapter by questioning his judgement in a domestic matter, name the cause as a wish to please his wives, and then leave the exchange in the permanent record for every generation to recite. The early community found the chapter difficult for exactly this reason, and it has been called Sūrat al-Nabī, the sūrah of the Prophet, precisely because it deals so directly with his household. Its preservation is an argument about the nature of the Book: the Messenger is a servant under instruction, and the instruction is published.

**What Was Forbidden and Why**

The occasion is reported with two main accounts. In the widely transmitted version, the Prophet ﷺ had been with Māriya, the Coptic woman who had come into his household, in the apartment of Ḥafṣa on Ḥafṣa's appointed day. His wives took their turns in rotation, and the breach of that rotation upset her. To settle the matter he asked her to keep it private and declared that Māriya was henceforth forbidden to him. The second account has him swearing off a drink of honey after two of his wives told him it had left an odour on him. Some commentators keep both, noting that the verse would apply to any case in which he barred himself from something God had permitted.

The details differ; the correction does not. In either version the Prophet ﷺ responded to friction in his household by removing something lawful from his own life, and in either version the response was disallowed. The verse's wording is careful about where the fault lies. Nothing wrong was done in the act he renounced — it is described as *“what Allah has made lawful to you”* (66:1). The error was in the renunciation itself.

**Peacemaking Is Not a Licence to Alter the Law**

*“seeking to please your wives”* (66:1) names the motive without condemning it. A husband trying to keep peace in a house where several women share a life is doing something decent, and the Qur'an nowhere criticises the impulse. What it refuses is the method. A personal oath that turns a permitted thing into a forbidden one has crossed from managing a household into legislating, and legislation is not among the things a man may offer his family as a concession.

The same principle is addressed to the whole community elsewhere, and in almost the same words: *“O believers! Do not forbid the good things which Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not like transgressors”* (5:87). The Qur'an also condemns those who invented prohibitions on their own livestock and food, *“falsely attributing lies to Allah”* (6:140). Read together with this verse, the policy is consistent: the boundary between lawful and forbidden belongs to God alone, and the sincerity of the person who moves it is not a defence. If the Prophet ﷺ himself is corrected for shifting that line in his own favour of domestic peace, nobody after him has standing to shift it out of piety, scrupulousness or a wish to be agreeable.

**Renunciation Is Not Automatically Piety**

Behind the ruling sits a broader argument about religious temperament. Many traditions treat self-denial as a reliable indicator of devotion, so that the person who gives up more is assumed to be nearer God. This verse checks that assumption at its source. A man denied himself something permitted, for a motive that was not selfish, and was asked why.

The correction is consistent with the Prophet's recorded impatience toward exaggerated abstinence among his Companions, and with a sūrah that will shortly define the excellent believing woman without a single reference to what she has renounced — she is described as submissive, faithful, devout, repentant, given to worship and fasting (66:5). Fasting appears there as a prescribed practice, not an invented one, and that distinction is the whole point. Discipline within the limits God set is worship; discipline that redraws the limits is presumption wearing the clothes of worship. A religion that cannot make this distinction ends by treating its most severe adherents as its most reliable ones.

**Forgiveness Named Before Any Repentance Is Reported**

The verse ends where a rebuke would not: *“And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”* (66:1). No apology has been offered yet, no expiation performed, no remorse described. The two names arrive immediately after the question, closing the correction with an assurance rather than a threat.

The placement establishes the register for the entire chapter, which will go on to correct two of the Prophet's wives, warn them about the state of their hearts, address believers about protecting their households from the Fire, and call for sincere repentance. All of that is severe, and all of it is read in the light of the two names placed here at the start. The order also carries a practical lesson about how error is handled in the Book: the fault is named precisely and without euphemism, and the door back is shown in the same breath. What the Qur'an will not do is either pretend nothing happened or leave the corrected party without a route forward. The next verse turns to that route.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:2

> Allah has already ordained for you ˹believers˺ the way to absolve yourselves from your oaths. For Allah is your Guardian. And He is the All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Remedy That Existed Before the Mistake**

*“Allah has already ordained for you ˹believers˺ the way to absolve yourselves from your oaths”* (66:2) uses the perfect tense, and the timing is the point. The provision for releasing a person from an oath had been legislated earlier; it was not devised in response to this episode. A man who has bound himself by a rash vow is not being rescued by a special dispensation, and the Prophet ﷺ is not being handed a personal exemption. He is being directed to a standing rule that was already part of the law when the oath was sworn.

The address also widens without warning. The previous verse spoke to the Prophet ﷺ in the singular; this one uses the plural, *lakum*, gathering the whole community into the ruling. What began as a correction of one household becomes general legislation within a single line. The effect is to make the episode instructive rather than merely biographical: every believer who has ever tied himself to something foolish under oath is included in the provision being pointed out here.

**The Expiation the Verse Points To**

The mechanism referred to is set out in the fifth sūrah. Thoughtless oaths carry no liability, but deliberate ones do, and the penalty for breaking them is specified: *“The penalty for a broken oath is to feed ten poor people from what you normally feed your own family, or to clothe them, or to free a bondsperson. But if none of this is affordable, then you must fast three days”* (5:89). Three options, graded by capacity, with fasting as the fallback for anyone who can afford none of the others.

The design tells us what an oath is worth in Islamic law. It is serious enough that breaking it costs something real — feeding ten households is not a formality — and it is not so absolute that a person must remain trapped in a vow that has turned out to be wrong. Commentators divide over what the Prophet ﷺ actually did in this case. Some report that he made expiation by freeing a slave. Others hold that no expiation was required, because this verse itself released him and there was nothing left to expiate. The disagreement is honest and unresolved; what both positions share is the understanding that the oath was not permitted to stand.

**Sincerity Is No Reason to Keep a Bad Vow**

The deeper ruling here is one that runs against a natural moral instinct. A person who has sworn something feels that keeping his word is integrity, even when the thing sworn has turned out to be a mistake. The Qur'an disagrees, and says so in a comparable case: people who had sworn never again to help relatives who had wronged them were told, *“Let them pardon and forgive. Do you not love to be forgiven by Allah?”* (24:22). The oath had to yield to the better act.

The principle stands out clearly in the verse before this one. The Prophet ﷺ swore off something lawful for a motive nobody could call selfish, and the oath still had to go. Fidelity to one's own word is a virtue only while the word points somewhere legitimate; a vow that maintains a prohibition God never issued does not become holy through being kept. This is why the law provides a priced exit rather than a free one. The expiation prevents oaths from being taken lightly, and the existence of an exit prevents a moment of anger or embarrassment from binding a life.

**Guardianship as the Reason the Exit Exists**

*“For Allah is your Guardian”* (66:2) explains the ruling rather than decorating it. The word *mawlā* denotes the one who holds responsibility for another's interests — a protector, patron and manager of affairs. Naming Him as such immediately after supplying a release from a rash vow identifies that release as an act of guardianship. A guardian does not let his ward remain caught in a trap the ward built himself.

Reading the chapter as a unit, the term also answers the household situation directly. A Prophet under pressure from his wives, negotiating peace with a self-imposed prohibition, is told who his actual patron is. The next two verses will reinforce this when the wives are warned that God, Gabriel, the righteous believers and the angels are all his support. The correction of verse one and the relief of verse two come from the same source, and both are described as the acts of a protector rather than of a critic.

**Knowledge and Wisdom Applied to a Domestic Muddle**

The verse closes with two names: *“And He is the All-Knowing, All-Wise”* (66:2). Both are chosen for the case at hand. Knowledge is relevant because the episode turned on private matters inside a household — a confidence, a rotation of days, an oath sworn in a moment nobody else witnessed. Wisdom is relevant because the resolution had to hold several interests at once: the Prophet's dignity, the feelings of his wives, the integrity of the line between lawful and forbidden, and the community's need for a general rule about oaths.

What the pairing rules out is the suspicion that this ruling was an improvisation. The oath is not simply cancelled by decree, which would make vows worthless; nor is it enforced, which would leave a false prohibition standing in the Prophet's own life; a priced release already on the books is applied instead. That is a wise solution to a real conflict rather than a convenient one, and the names at the end of the verse invite the reader to notice the difference. The sūrah now turns from the oath to the conversation that produced it.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:3

> ˹Remember˺ when the Prophet had ˹once˺ confided something to one of his wives, then when she disclosed it ˹to another wife˺ and Allah made it known to him, he presented ˹to her˺ part of what was disclosed and overlooked a part. So when he informed her of it, she exclaimed, “Who told you this?” He replied, “I was informed by the All-Knowing, All-Aware.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Confidence Given and Broken**

The verse narrates a small domestic event with unusual precision. Something was told to one wife in confidence; she passed it to another; the Prophet ﷺ was informed of the breach from above. The commentators identify the parties: Ḥafṣa was the wife who had been asked to keep the matter private, and ʿĀʾisha was the one she told. The subject was the episode behind the sūrah's opening rebuke. When she was later asked whether she had spoken of it, she denied it.

What makes the narration unusual is how little it moralises. No adjective is attached to the disclosure, no denunciation follows, and the woman is not named in the text. The Qur'an states the sequence and moves on to what the Prophet ﷺ did about it. That restraint is itself a comment on how such matters should be handled: the offence is recorded accurately and left without embellishment, which is close to the opposite of how a betrayed confidence is usually discussed inside a family.

**Two Wives, and the Ordinary Pressures of a Shared House**

The event is trivial in its content and instructive in its setting. A household in which several wives share one husband on a fixed rotation generates exactly this sort of friction, and the Qur'an declines to pretend otherwise about the Prophet's home. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, asked years later who the two women were that had backed each other against the Prophet ﷺ, answered without hesitation: ʿĀʾisha and Ḥafṣa (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4913). The same reports describe him warning his own daughter about arguing with the Prophet ﷺ, and describe the community's alarm when the Prophet ﷺ withdrew from his wives for a month.

Preserving this material served a purpose beyond history. It established that the Prophet's wives were not a category of being above scrutiny, which is precisely the conclusion the chapter will drive home in its closing verses with the wives of Noah and Lot. It also gave the community a realistic picture of the man they were to imitate. A household presented as frictionless would have been useless as a model and, to anyone who knew the participants, obviously false.

**Disclosure That Comes From Outside the House**

*“and Allah made it known to him”* (66:3) is the hinge of the verse. The Prophet ﷺ did not learn of the breach through gossip, a servant or his own suspicion; the information was given to him. This is stated plainly and it changes the character of the episode from a domestic quarrel into a demonstration.

The demonstration concerns the nature of prophecy itself. A man who is receiving legislation about marriage, inheritance and war from beyond himself is here shown receiving something small and verifiable — a private conversation in his own house, repeated back to a woman who knew for certain whether he could have discovered it any other way. Her reaction, *“Who told you this?”* (66:3), is the response of someone who has just tested a claim and found it holds. The Qur'an offers grand evidence for revelation elsewhere; here it offers a piece of evidence that only one person in the world was positioned to check, and records her checking it.

**Telling Part and Withholding Part**

*“he presented ˹to her˺ part of what was disclosed and overlooked a part”* (66:3) is the detail the commentators dwell on, and rightly. The Prophet ﷺ had been given the full account and chose to confront her with some of it. The restraint is read in two ways. Most take it as kindness: he spared her the full exposure because there was no purpose in it beyond humiliation. Others read the partial disclosure as God's, so that the Prophet ﷺ was given a portion and the rest was withheld from him.

The first reading is stronger, because the sentence attributes the selection to him — he made part known and turned away from part. It also matches the Qur'an's picture of his character in dealing with those who had wronged him: *“Had you been cruel or hard-hearted, they would have certainly abandoned you”* (3:159). The lesson generalises easily and is rarely practised. Knowing everything about another person's fault does not oblige a man to say everything. Enough must be said for the wrong to be named and stopped; the remainder is his to leave unspoken, and leaving it unspoken is a decision about the relationship's future rather than about the past.

**Two Names That Answer Her Question**

She asks who informed him, and the reply names no one in the house: *“I was informed by the All-Knowing, All-Aware”* (66:3). The pairing of *al-ʿAlīm* with *al-Khabīr* is deliberate. The first denotes knowledge in its full extent; the second denotes acquaintance with the inner detail of things, the hidden particulars that leave no outward trace. A private remark passed between two women in a Madinan apartment is exactly the kind of thing *khabīr* covers.

The answer also settles the question of what kind of failing a broken confidence is. It could have been treated as a social lapse, dealt with by apology and forgotten. Naming God as the source of the disclosure moves it into a different register: what was said in a room where nobody else was present was heard, and reported, and came back. The chapter will build on this steadily — the wives are told their hearts have inclined, believers are told to guard their families, and everyone is called to a repentance that must be sincere rather than performed. A God described as *al-Khabīr* is one before whom the distinction between sincere and performed is not available to be blurred.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:4

> ˹It will be better˺ if you ˹wives˺ both turn to Allah in repentance, for your hearts have certainly faltered. But if you ˹continue to˺ collaborate against him, then ˹know that˺ Allah Himself is his Guardian. And Gabriel, the righteous believers, and the angels are ˹all˺ his supporters as well.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Left Deliberately Unfinished**

The Arabic opens with a conditional whose answer is missing: if you two turn to God in repentance — and then nothing. Grammarians call this an ellipsis, and the translation supplies the sense with *“˹It will be better˺”*, while the commentators complete it as "it will be accepted of you both." The omission is not a defect in the text. A conditional left hanging invites the hearer to finish it, and what a person supplies reveals what they expect.

The rhetorical effect is gentleness rather than vagueness. The alternative branch, in contrast, is spelled out in full and at length: if you continue to back one another against him, here is the list of who stands with him. One road is left open with its ending unstated; the other is described in detail and is plainly not an invitation. The asymmetry is the argument, and it is made without a single word of abuse toward either woman.

**"Your Hearts Have Faltered"**

The diagnosis is placed in the middle of the verse and given as a fact: *“for your hearts have certainly faltered”* (66:4). The Arabic verb *ṣaghat* means to lean, tilt or swerve from true, and the commentators read the inclination in two connected ways. One takes it as the hearts having leaned toward approving the prohibition of what God had made lawful — that is, toward the error the sūrah opened by correcting. The other reads it more generally as a deviation from what is right.

Both readings identify the same underlying problem, and it is subtler than disobedience. Nothing is alleged here about an action. What is named is a tilt, a heart that has come slightly off true and now finds a wrong position comfortable. That is how most serious failures begin, and it is also why the remedy prescribed is repentance rather than restitution — there is nothing to give back. The Qur'an's willingness to address the inner posture of two named women in the Prophet's own household, and preserve it for recitation, is also a statement about who is subject to this kind of scrutiny. Proximity to the Messenger conferred no exemption from having one's heart examined.

**Collaboration Described Without Euphemism**

*“But if you ˹continue to˺ collaborate against him”* (66:4) uses a verb of mutual assistance, *taẓāharā*, from the root for backing someone up, putting one's back against theirs. It describes two people forming a bloc. This is the element that raises a private disagreement into something the Qur'an treats seriously: not that one woman was upset, but that two coordinated.

The distinction is worth drawing out, since it has application far beyond this house. A single person's complaint remains a complaint and can be answered. An alliance changes the situation structurally, because each party now has an interest in maintaining the other's position and neither can retreat without losing face before the other. Grievances that would have dissolved on their own are preserved by the partnership that formed around them. The verse names the alliance rather than the grievance, and offers repentance as the way out — which, for two people who have bound themselves together in a position, is the hardest possible exit and the only clean one.

**The Support Listed Against the Alliance**

Then comes the answer to the bloc, and its scale is extraordinary for a domestic dispute: *“then ˹know that˺ Allah Himself is his Guardian. And Gabriel, the righteous believers, and the angels are ˹all˺ his supporters as well”* (66:4). Four names, ascending and then widening. God is named first as *mawlā*, the same title given to the believers in the previous sūrah of this pair, and the point of listing anyone after Him is not that He needs help. The point is the disparity being set out.

The rhetorical strategy resembles what happens elsewhere when a small human coalition is confronted with what actually stands on the other side. Two people had made an arrangement between themselves; the reply enumerates the Messenger's backing in a sentence that runs from the Lord of the worlds through the greatest of the angels and out to the whole body of the righteous. Nothing in the verse threatens either woman with a penalty. It simply describes the field, and leaves the arithmetic to them.

**Who the Righteous Believers Are**

The phrase *ṣāliḥ al-muʾminīn* — rendered *“the righteous believers”* (66:4) — is grammatically singular in form and has attracted a long history of identification. Some commentators take it as a collective, meaning the best of the believers generally. Others name Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, which carries a certain edge, since they were the fathers of the two women being addressed. In Shia commentary the phrase is referred to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, supported by a report that the Prophet ﷺ took ʿAlī by the hand and identified him with the words of this verse.

The collective reading is the most defensible on the wording alone, since the singular form is regularly used for a class in Arabic and the verse is already listing categories rather than individuals. The identification with the two fathers is exegetically attractive for a different reason: it would mean that the two women's own families stood on the Prophet's side of the dispute, and the reports about ʿUmar's reaction when he learned what his daughter had done support exactly that. Whichever reading is preferred, the function of the phrase in the sentence is unaffected. Between Gabriel and the angels, the verse inserts human beings — ordinary believers, whose loyalty is counted alongside the support of heaven.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:5

> Perhaps, if he were to divorce you ˹all˺, his Lord would replace you with better wives who are submissive ˹to Allah˺, faithful ˹to Him˺, devout, repentant, dedicated to worship and fasting—previously married or virgins.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Warning Framed as a Possibility**

The verse opens with *ʿasā*, perhaps — a word that keeps the statement short of a prediction. No divorce is announced, and no replacement is promised; a scenario is set out and left standing. This is a particular kind of rebuke, one that works by describing what could happen rather than what will. The women addressed are asked to consider their position from the outside.

The context is preserved in the reports. The Prophet ﷺ had withdrawn from his wives for a month, and Madina was unsettled by rumours that he had divorced them all. ʿUmar recounted that the wives had pressed the Prophet ﷺ and that he told them it might be that if he divorced them, God would give him better wives in their place — and then this verse came down with the same content (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4916 records ʿUmar saying exactly this). The verse therefore confirmed a warning already spoken by a human being, which is a striking sequence: a father's blunt words to his daughter and her co-wives were endorsed from above.

**"Better Than You" and What the Comparison Means**

The claim that God could supply better wives is not an assessment of the women's worth as persons. It is a statement about a specific relationship and specific conduct, and the qualities listed immediately afterwards define the standard of comparison. Every one of them concerns the woman's relation to God rather than her beauty, lineage, wealth or usefulness to a household.

That list is the verse's real content. Submissive here renders *muslimāt*, meaning given over to God; *muʾmināt*, believing; *qānitāt*, devoutly obedient; *tāʾibāt*, repeatedly returning in repentance; *ʿābidāt*, given to worship; and then the disputed term rendered *fasting*. Set beside the situation that produced the sūrah — a confidence broken, an alliance formed, a husband pressured into forbidding what God allowed — the list reads as a direct answer. What was lacking was not affection or intelligence but orientation. The women being addressed were faithful and devout; the warning is that these qualities can coexist with a heart that has tilted, and that God is not short of people in whom they do not.

**Devotion Owed to God and Not to a Husband**

The third quality, *qānitāt*, deserves separating out, because it has been misused. Words from the root *q-n-t* in the Qur'an refer consistently to obedience and constancy toward God — in standing at night, in worship, in steadiness of religious commitment. They are not used for a wife's obedience to a husband. The same term is applied to men in the great list of believers who are promised forgiveness and a mighty reward: *“devout men and women”* (33:35), and it is applied to Mary at the close of this very chapter, where she is described as among the devoutly obedient (66:12). Mary had no husband.

Reading the word correctly matters for how the whole verse is understood. If *qānitāt* meant compliant wives, this verse would be a threat that disobedient women are replaceable with obedient ones, and the chapter would be about domestic authority. Read accurately, the standard held up is God-directed in every item, and the women who close the sūrah as models — Pharaoh's wife and Mary — are commended precisely for a devotion that ran against their households rather than serving them. The hierarchy the chapter teaches places a woman's relationship with her Lord above her relationship with anyone in her house, including a prophet.

**The Word That Divides the Commentators**

The sixth quality, *sāʾiḥāt*, is the interpretive crux of the verse. Its root means to travel or roam. The majority read it here as given to fasting, on the basis of reports that the term was used this way, and some extend the sense to a general restraint of the limbs from disobedience — a fasting of the whole body, not just the stomach. A second group reads it literally as those who travel for the sake of their religion, which in a Madinan context means the women who emigrated. A third reading takes the travelling as spiritual: those who journey on the path toward God.

There is reason to hold the emigration reading in view rather than defaulting to the majority. The word's plain sense is movement, and the women of the early community had in fact made the journey from Mecca at real cost, a sacrifice the Qur'an praises elsewhere in strong terms. Later exegesis, working in settings where women's public movement had narrowed, had an obvious incentive to convert a term about travel into a term about fasting performed at home. Nothing forces a choice between the readings, since both describe real practices of real believing women. What should not happen is the quiet disappearance of the more demanding sense.

**Two Categories, One Standard**

The list ends with a pairing that is not a quality at all: *“previously married or virgins”* (66:5). After six spiritual descriptions, the verse adds a purely factual distinction and declines to rank it. Both categories appear on the same side of the comparison, both among the better wives God could supply.

The refusal to rank is deliberate and worth stating plainly, because communities have rarely managed it. A previously married woman — widowed or divorced — carries in most societies a diminished standing in the marriage market, whatever her character. This verse puts her beside the never-married woman without comment, having just spent a line and a half establishing that the qualities which actually distinguish women are submission, faith, devotion, repentance and worship. The Prophet's own household demonstrated the principle before the verse stated it: his first marriage was to a widow, and most of the women he married had been married before. The sūrah that opens by correcting him for an oath about his wives closes this passage by defining what a good wife is, and marital history is not on the list.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:6

> O believers! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, overseen by formidable and severe angels, who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Widens From One House to Every House**

Five verses have dealt with the Prophet's household — an oath, a broken confidence, two hearts that tilted, a warning about replacement. Now the vocative changes: *“O believers!”* (66:6). The particular family drops away and every family in the community is addressed with an instruction built on what has just been shown. If the household of the Messenger required correction, no household is beyond needing it.

The command is also unusual in its direction. Across the Qur'an, believers are normally described asking God to shield them from the Fire — *“Our Lord! Shield us from the punishment of the Fire”* runs through the supplications recorded in the Book. Here they are told to do the shielding themselves. Protection from the Fire becomes a task assigned rather than only a favour requested, and the responsibility extends past the individual to the people in his care.

**Guarding a Family Means Teaching It**

The verse says protect, and it does not say how, which has left commentators to supply the content from the rest of revelation. The consistent answer is that one shields a family by obedience to God and by cultivating that obedience in them — instruction, correction, and the ordinary example of a household where God's commands are visibly kept. There is no other mechanism available. Nobody can stand between a grown son and the consequences of his own choices on the Day of Judgement, which is exactly the lesson the wives of Noah and Lot will illustrate at the end of this chapter.

Placing this command immediately after the household narrative gives it a sharp edge. The Prophet ﷺ had responded to friction in his home by taking an oath, and was corrected for it. What the verse requires instead is the harder and slower work: forming the people in the house rather than managing their moods. A man who indulges his family in everything they want and neglects what they need to know has not protected them; he has made himself popular. The sūrah has already shown that seeking to please one's household is not a sufficient standard even for a prophet.

**Fuel That Is People and Stones**

The description is deliberately hard: *“a Fire whose fuel is people and stones”* (66:6). The same pairing appears in the challenge issued early in the second sūrah: *“then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers”* (2:24). What makes the image disturbing is the category error at its centre. Fuel is ordinarily something gathered and consumed to produce heat for someone's benefit; here human beings occupy the position of the firewood.

The stones have been explained most persuasively as the idols. They were carved from stone, they were worshipped, and the Qur'an elsewhere describes false objects of worship accompanying their devotees into the Fire, where they are of no use whatever. If that reading is right, the image contains a complete argument: the thing a person gave his life to, and the person who gave it, burn together in the same furnace. Others read the stones as a particular kind of stone that burns with extraordinary heat. Either way, the verse describes a fire that does not need feeding from outside, because what is thrown into it is what sustains it.

**Angels Who Do Not Negotiate**

The Fire is *“overseen by formidable and severe angels”* (66:6), described with two adjectives — *ghilāẓ shidād*, harsh and strong — and then with a clause about their obedience: *“who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded”* (66:6). The Qur'an specifies their number elsewhere: *“It is overseen by nineteen ˹keepers˺”* (74:30).

The detail about their obedience is the functionally important one, and it removes a hope people do not admit to holding. Every human system of punishment has slack in it, because the people administering it are people — they can be moved by a face, worn down by pleading, bribed, or simply tired. The verse closes that gap in advance. These keepers have no independent will to appeal to and no sympathy that can be worked on; they execute precisely what was commanded, and nothing else. A person hoping to find a soft place in the machinery is being told that the machinery has none. The next verse will show what happens when the attempt to make excuses is finally made.

**Why This Command Sits in a Chapter About Marriage**

The placement of a warning about Hellfire in the middle of a sūrah dealing with the Prophet's wives is not a change of subject. It supplies the reason the earlier corrections mattered. A quarrel about a rotation of days, a confidence carried to a co-wife, an oath sworn to keep the peace — these are small matters by any worldly measure, and the natural response to them is to keep them small. The verse refuses that scaling by naming what a household is ultimately for.

Read this way, the chapter has an architecture. It begins with the management of a marriage, moves to the state of hearts inside it, and then states that the family is a unit with a destination, which every member is responsible for helping the others reach. The instruction to guard oneself *and* one's family makes the household the smallest religious community, with duties running in both directions inside it. This is also why the sūrah will end with four women whose fates were decided independently of the men they lived with: the family is the arena in which salvation is worked on, and it is not the thing that delivers it.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:7

> ˹The deniers will then be told,˺ “O disbelievers! Make no excuses this Day! You are only rewarded for what you used to do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Scene Rather Than a Statement**

The verse does not describe the disbelievers; it quotes what is said to them. The reader is placed at the moment of arrival, and the words are addressed to people who are already there. The commentators locate the scene precisely: this is spoken to the deniers as they enter the Fire, and *“this Day”* is the Day of Resurrection. The previous verse described the keepers of that Fire, harsh and strong and incapable of disobedience, so the voice here carries no possibility of appeal.

Presenting judgement as reported speech rather than as narration does something a description could not. The reader overhears a sentence that will one day be addressed to somebody, and the identity of that somebody is not fixed by the text in front of him. The chapter has been building toward this: it has just told every believer to guard his family from a Fire, and now it lets him listen to the reception of those who were not guarded.

**The Excuse Forbidden Before It Is Offered**

*“Make no excuses this Day!”* (66:7) is a prohibition on an act that has not yet been attempted. The prohibition implies the impulse: people arriving at that moment will want to explain themselves, as people do in every other setting where they are held to account. Excuse-making is the most reliable human response to exposure, and it works, in this world, surprisingly often. Circumstances were difficult, the information was unclear, others were worse, the times were against us.

What removes the excuse is not a refusal to hear it but the conditions under which it would be offered. When the reality of a life's deeds is laid out in full, an excuse requires a gap between what happened and what is known to have happened, and there is no gap. The Qur'an describes people at that moment recognising the truth of their own record, and the recognition is what silences them. This is also why the verse does not bother to refute any particular excuse. It simply forbids the category, the way one forbids something that has become pointless rather than something that is dangerous.

**Requital Exactly Proportioned to Conduct**

*“You are only rewarded for what you used to do”* (66:7) contains the justification for everything in the verse. The restrictive particle is doing the work: only for what you did, nothing added, nothing invented. The identical sentence is addressed to the deniers elsewhere, in the middle of a passage about the Fire: *“You are only rewarded for what you used to do”* (52:16).

The principle behind it is stated as a purpose of creation itself: *“For Allah created the heavens and the earth for a purpose, so that every soul may be paid back for what it has committed. And none will be wronged”* (45:22). Two things follow. First, the punishment is not an excess of divine anger but a return of what was sent; the recipient is receiving his own consignment. Second, the phrase *mā kuntum taʿmalūn* uses a construction denoting habitual action — what you used to do, repeatedly, as a settled practice. The judgement is on a pattern of life rather than on an isolated failure, which is consistent with a Book that assigns absolution for lesser faults to those who avoid the major ones.

**Who the Verse Is Actually Addressed To**

A sentence spoken to disbelievers in the Fire cannot be heard by disbelievers in the Fire, since the matter is settled for them. Its audience is the one reading it now, with the term still running. This is the pattern for Qur'anic scenes of judgement generally: they are addressed in the second person to people who no longer have choices, in order to be read by people who do.

The chapter's arrangement makes this unusually clear. Verse six commanded believers to guard themselves and their families; verse seven shows the outcome for those who were not guarded; verse eight will call the believers to sincere repentance and describe their light spreading before them on the same Day. Three consecutive verses, one Day, two receptions. Placing the deniers' arrival between the command and the promise is not an interruption of the argument but its evidence. What makes the call to repentance urgent is that the alternative has just been quoted aloud, and the excuse that everyone privately intends to make has been ruled out in advance.

**The Word "Disbelievers" as a Description of Conduct**

The address names its audience by what they refused rather than by tribe, nation or era. *Kafara* in Arabic carries the sense of covering something over, and the Qur'an uses it for people who had the truth put before them and buried it. That is a description of behaviour, and it explains why the second half of the verse speaks about deeds rather than beliefs: the requital is for *“what you used to do”* (66:7), because what they did is what their refusal amounted to in practice.

The chapter's arrangement supports reading the term this way. Two verses earlier, believers were told to guard their families, and the guarding described was a matter of conduct — teaching, correcting, keeping commands in a house where children watch. Nothing in this sūrah treats faith as a position held privately and separately from behaviour. The wives corrected in the opening verses were believers whose hearts had tilted; the disbelievers addressed here are people whose covering-over ran all the way through their lives. Between those two cases lies the whole audience of the chapter, which is why the call to repentance follows immediately.

**Silence as the Final Condition**

What the verse ultimately describes is a state in which speech has lost its function. Excuses are forbidden, the record is complete, and the only thing left to say has already been said by the deeds themselves. For creatures who spend their lives explaining, negotiating and presenting themselves, this is a strange and specific deprivation.

It also clarifies what the Qur'an means by a Day of accounting. The accounting is not an argument in which two sides present cases; it is the reading out of a ledger that both parties can see. Every ordinary human tribunal leaves room for advocacy precisely because the facts are never fully known, and advocacy is how the gaps get filled. Where nothing is unknown, advocacy has no material to work with. The verse thus closes the door that the rest of the chapter keeps holding open: while the term runs, a person may turn, repent, correct a tilted heart and guard his household. At the point described here, all of that has passed into the record, and the record speaks instead of him.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:8

> O believers! Turn to Allah in sincere repentance, so your Lord may absolve you of your sins and admit you into Gardens, under which rivers flow, on the Day Allah will not disgrace the Prophet or the believers with him. Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right. They will say, “Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us. ˹For˺ You are truly Most Capable of everything.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Repentance as Turning Rather Than Regretting**

The verb *tūbū* means to turn back, and the direction is specified: *“Turn to Allah”* (66:8). This is the second time the chapter has called for repentance — the two wives were offered it in the fourth verse, and now the whole community is. What the word describes is a change of orientation rather than a feeling. A person who is sorry has an emotion; a person who repents has changed the way he is facing.

The distinction is practical. Remorse is easy to generate, pleasant in its own way, and compatible with repeating the act next month; the Qur'an's term requires a reversal of course. This is also why the sūrah puts the call here, immediately after quoting what is said to those who arrive with excuses. The alternative to making an excuse later is turning now, and the chapter has just demonstrated that the first option will not be available.

**What Makes Repentance "Sincere"**

The qualifier *naṣūḥ* has been explained by the commentators in terms of durability. Sincere repentance is not a turning away from the sin that leaves the door open behind it, but one accompanied by genuine contrition and a settled resolve never to return to what was repented of. The word's root carries the sense of purity and of sincere counsel, so that the repentance is honest advice a person gives himself and acts on.

Placed in this chapter, the adjective is doing corrective work. The sūrah has been dealing with failures of exactly the kind that produce insincere repentance — a broken confidence followed by a denial, an alliance maintained after the correction, an oath sworn to settle a quarrel rather than to solve it. Against that background, the demand for *naṣūḥ* asks for a repentance that is not another item of household diplomacy. The verse then attaches *ʿasā*, it may be, to the outcome: *“so your Lord may absolve you”* (66:8). The word conveys hope, and hope from God is certainty; what it removes is any sense that repentance is a transaction which obliges Him.

**Absolution and the Company It Keeps**

*“absolve you of your sins and admit you into Gardens, under which rivers flow”* (66:8) joins the two promises the Qur'an habitually pairs. The covering of sins is linked in different verses to different things — to reverence, as in the previous sūrah's *“He will absolve them of their sins and reward them immensely”* (65:5) and in *“If you are mindful of Allah... absolve you of your sins, and forgive you”* (8:29); to faith and good work, as in *“As for those who believe and do good, We will certainly absolve them of their sins”* (29:7); and to avoiding the gravest wrongs, as in *“If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your ˹lesser˺ misdeeds”* (4:31).

The pattern across these verses is that absolution is never free-standing. It is always attached to something a person does, and the something varies with the context in which the promise is made. Here it is attached to a turning, which is the appropriate condition for a chapter about people whose hearts had tilted. What is being covered over, moreover, is not merely punishment but the record itself — the Arabic image is of concealment, a fault put out of sight. For a community that had just watched a private conversation in the Prophet's house become public revelation, a promise that God covers things over is not a small one.

**The Day the Prophet Is Not Disgraced**

The timing clause is unexpected: *“on the Day Allah will not disgrace the Prophet or the believers with him”* (66:8). A verse about personal repentance suddenly names the Prophet's honour as the marker of the occasion. The connection runs back through the chapter. Its opening verses recorded a correction, a broken confidence and a household in difficulty, and the enemies of the community had material there to work with. The sūrah closes that account by fixing the day on which nothing of the kind will be possible.

The believers are included in the same clause — the Prophet *and those who believe with him* — which links their standing to his. This is one of the Qur'an's recurring moves: the community's fate is described in the company of its Messenger rather than separately. For an audience that had just been warned about the Fire, about excuses, and about hearts leaning off true, the promise of a day without disgrace addresses a fear distinct from punishment. Many people fear exposure more than pain.

**Light That Runs Ahead and a Prayer to Complete It**

*“Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right”* (66:8) describes the believers moving with their own illumination. The fuller scene appears elsewhere: *“On that Day you will see believing men and women with their light shining ahead of them and on their right”* (57:12), and the verses following it show the hypocrites calling out to borrow some of that light and being told to go back and look for it where light is acquired (57:13). Light on that Day is not distributed at the gate; it is carried in from the life before.

That context explains the prayer. *“Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us”* (66:8) is understood by the commentators as spoken when the believers see the light of the hypocrites put out around them, and the sight makes them ask for their own to be completed rather than merely maintained. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī read the supplication differently and more hopefully: God does complete their light, and they go on calling to Him anyway, seeking nearness. Both readings fit the words, and both say something about the state described. People walking in their own light are still asking for more of it, and still asking for forgiveness — which is, for a chapter that began with an oath sworn to keep peace in a household, an unusually precise picture of what a settled soul looks like.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:9

> O Prophet! Struggle against the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be firm with them. Hell will be their home. What an evil destination!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse Repeated Word for Word**

This line is identical to one in the ninth sūrah: *“O Prophet! Struggle against the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be firm with them. Hell will be their home. What an evil destination!”* (9:73). The Qur'an repeats material often, but exact repetition of a full verse is less common, and it invites a question about what the second placement adds.

The answer lies in the surroundings. In the earlier sūrah the command sits among passages about military campaigns, the treatment of those who stayed behind, and the exposure of the hypocrites of Madina. Here it sits between a call to sincere repentance and a set of examples drawn from the households of prophets. The same words, dropped into a chapter about family life and inner states, acquire a different function. They mark the boundary of the chapter's gentleness: the sūrah has corrected a prophet with a question, invited two women to repent, and called believers to turn — and it now states that not everyone is being addressed this way.

**Two Adversaries Who Require Different Handling**

The command names two groups, and the commentators have consistently read the striving as taking different forms against each. Against open deniers it was understood to include armed struggle where that was called for, alongside argument and the call to God. Against the hypocrites — who professed faith publicly and worked against the community privately — it was understood as applying the law to them, since they could not be fought as enemies while they identified themselves as Muslims.

The distinction is legally important and has been widely ignored by later readers of both kinds. Hypocrisy was never treated in the Prophet's practice as grounds for execution or expulsion, which is why the known hypocrites of Madina lived, prayed and traded in the city until they died. What the verse requires against them is firmness rather than tolerance of their conduct: their schemes exposed, their claims not accepted at face value, and the community's business not entrusted to them. That is a sustainable policy for a society that contains people whose loyalty is uncertain, and it does not require identifying who they are with any certainty.

**Firmness as the Absence of Accommodation**

*“and be firm with them”* (66:9) uses a verb built on harshness, and the word deserves care. The Qur'an that issues this command also describes the Prophet ﷺ as lenient by God's mercy and warns him that hardness would have scattered the people around him (3:159). The two statements are addressed to different objects. Gentleness is the standing posture toward the community, including its faltering members; firmness is the posture toward those whose project is the community's undoing.

What the verse forbids, in practice, is accommodation — the softening of a position to avoid a confrontation, the quiet dropping of a demand because pressing it would be awkward. Read against the chapter's opening, this is a pointed placement. The sūrah began with a prohibition adopted *“seeking to please your wives”* (66:1), an accommodation made for the sake of peace and corrected from above. The same instinct, applied to the community's opponents, is the failure this verse forecloses. A man who cannot hold a line inside his household is unlikely to hold one outside it, and the chapter has treated both as the same discipline.

**Why the Struggle Verse Sits Here**

Placing this command immediately before the examples of the wives of Noah and Lot produces a deliberate sequence. Those two women were inside the households of prophets and were counted with the deniers; they are introduced in the very next verse, after a line about disbelievers and hypocrites whose home is Hell. The chapter is arranging its categories so that the reader cannot draw the boundary of the community along the lines of family, proximity or public profession.

This also completes the chapter's account of what a believer's life involves. Its earlier verses dealt with the inner work: hearts that tilt, confidences kept, repentance that is genuine rather than performed, families taught rather than indulged. This verse turns outward and names a second obligation that the inner work does not replace. A person can be scrupulous at home and entirely passive about what surrounds him, and the sūrah declines to accept that as sufficient. The word *jāhid* covers effort of every legitimate kind, and it is the only imperative in the chapter addressed to the Prophet ﷺ alone that concerns anything outside his house.

**The Destination Named Without Argument**

*“Hell will be their home. What an evil destination!”* (66:9) closes the verse with a statement and an exclamation rather than a case. The word rendered *home* is *maʾwā*, a place of shelter and return — the place one goes back to at the end of the day. Using it of the Fire is bitterly precise: what these people spent their lives building toward turns out to be the place that receives them.

The exclamation that follows, *biʾsa al-maṣīr*, is a formula the Qur'an uses to close such statements, and its brevity is the point. No description of the punishment is offered here, no detail added to what the chapter already gave three verses earlier about fuel and keepers. The destination is named and the sentence stops. For a chapter that has been unusually generous with explanation — completing an elliptical conditional, listing the Prophet's supporters, itemising the qualities of better wives — the refusal to elaborate at this particular point reads as deliberate. Some things are set out at length because the reader is meant to weigh them. This one is stated because it is settled.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:10

> Allah sets forth an example for the disbelievers: the wife of Noah and the wife of Lot. Each was married to one of Our righteous servants, yet betrayed them. So their husbands were of no benefit to them against Allah whatsoever. Both were told, “Enter the Fire, along with the others!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Women Chosen to Make a Point About Everyone**

The chapter's closing section presents four women, and it opens with the two negative cases. The choice is pointed. Of all the disbelievers in the Qur'an's repertoire — Pharaoh, Abū Lahab, the chiefs of ʿĀd and Thamūd — the example held up for those who reject faith is a pair of women married to prophets. Nothing about their situation suggested that outcome. They lived in prophetic households, heard revelation at first hand, and shared a table with men God calls *“Our righteous servants”* (66:10).

That is exactly why they were selected. An example works by removing excuses, and these two remove the most respectable one available: that a person's religious fate is largely set by their circumstances. These women had the best circumstances imaginable for belief. The verse states the outcome without softening it, and the address is to disbelievers generally rather than to women — the point being made concerns proximity to righteousness, not gender. Placed at the end of a chapter that corrected the Prophet's own wives, the example is unmistakable in its local application and larger than that application in its meaning.

**The Betrayal Was Religious, Not Marital**

*“yet betrayed them”* (66:10) has been misread in a particular direction often enough that the commentators addressed it explicitly, and the classical consensus is firm: the betrayal was not sexual infidelity. No prophet's wife committed adultery. What these two did was work against their husbands' mission from inside their homes. Noah's wife is reported to have told the people that he was mad. Lot's wife is reported to have informed the townsmen when guests arrived at the house — the guests the men of Sodom came to assault.

Read this way, the word *khānatāhumā* describes a breach of trust of the gravest practical kind. Each woman possessed something only a spouse possesses: private knowledge, daily access, and the credibility that comes from being the person closest to the man. Each used it against him. Lot's wife's disclosure in particular converted the household from a refuge into a trap, which is the exact inversion of what a home is supposed to be in a chapter that has just told believers to guard their families from the Fire.

**Marriage to a Prophet Saved Neither of Them**

*“So their husbands were of no benefit to them against Allah whatsoever”* (66:10) states the doctrine the examples exist to establish. The Arabic ends with an absolute negation: nothing at all, in no respect. Two men whose intercession mattered enormously could do nothing for the women in their own houses.

The case of Lot's wife is the more documented of the two, and the Qur'an returns to it repeatedly. When the angels instruct Lot to leave by night, they specify the exception: *“do not let any of you look back, except your wife. She will certainly suffer the fate of the others”* (11:81). The same exclusion appears when he is rescued — *“We saved him and his family except his wife, who was one of the doomed”* (7:83) — and again in the negotiation over Sodom: *“We will certainly save him and his family—except his wife, who is one of the doomed”* (29:32). Noah's wife has no comparable dossier; this verse is the only explicit Qur'anic statement of her betrayal, which is one reason it is cited so often. The rescue of Noah's household likewise excluded a member, and the Book is consistent across both stories: the ark and the escape were extended to a family, and membership in the family was not the qualification.

**A Principle That Cuts Both Ways**

The commentators draw from these two examples a rule stated in both directions: the righteousness of one person does not benefit someone who works corruption, and the corruption of one person does not harm someone who is righteous. The second half of that rule is what the next verse will illustrate with Pharaoh's wife, and the pairing is the reason the chapter presents its examples in this order.

Stated so baldly, the principle is harder than it first sounds. It dissolves the entire economy of inherited religious standing — the assumption that a family, a lineage, a community or a marriage carries its members along with it. The Qur'an is dismantling something that every traditional society runs on, and it does so using the most sympathetic possible cases. These were not women who rejected an obscure message from a stranger; they rejected the men they lived with. If that relationship did not transfer merit, no relationship does. What the individual is left with is a file in their own name, which is the arrangement the chapter's warnings about hearts and repentance have assumed throughout.

**The Sentence Delivered in the Plural**

*“Both were told, ‘Enter the Fire, along with the others!’”* (66:10) ends the verse with a command that contains its own commentary. The two are not given a separate destination reflecting their unusual position; they are sent in *with those who enter*. Whatever distinction their marriages had conferred in the world evaporates at the threshold.

The phrasing answers an unspoken expectation. A reader following the story might assume that a prophet's wife, even a treacherous one, would be handled as a special case — judged more severely because she knew more, or more leniently because of her household. Neither happens. She joins the general body of those entering, sorted by what she did rather than by whom she married. This is the same principle the previous verses applied to the living: excuses are not accepted, requital matches conduct, and the light a person walks by on that Day is the light they arrived with. The chapter now turns to a woman whose household offered her every reason to fail.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:11

> And Allah sets forth an example for the believers: the wife of Pharaoh, who prayed, “My Lord! Build me a house in Paradise near You, deliver me from Pharaoh and his ˹evil˺ doing, and save me from the wrongdoing people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exact Reverse of the Previous Case**

The formula is repeated deliberately: God sets forth an example, as in the verse before, but now *“for the believers”* (66:11). Two women married to righteous men were ruined; one woman married to the most notorious tyrant in the Qur'an is held up as the model. The symmetry is precise and the lesson is the completion of the one just given. If a prophet's household could not save a woman who betrayed the mission, a tyrant's household could not destroy a woman who did not.

Her name is not in the Qur'an, though the reports give it as Āsiya bint Muzāḥim. What the text supplies instead of biography is her prayer, quoted in full, which is an unusual distinction. The Qur'an names few women and quotes fewer. Preserving her words rather than her story suggests what the example is for: the reader is not being told about a historical figure so much as given something to say.

**A House Requested Before a Rescue**

The order of her three requests repays attention. She asks first for a house in Paradise near her Lord, and only then for deliverance from Pharaoh and from the wrongdoing people. A woman in immediate danger puts the eternal request first and the urgent one second.

The phrasing of the first request is also precise: *“Build me a house in Paradise near You”* (66:11). The Arabic places the nearness before the location — near You, in the Garden — so that proximity to God is the specification and the Garden is where it happens. She was, by any material measure, already housed better than almost anyone alive. Palaces were the one thing Pharaoh's wife did not lack, and the Qur'an elsewhere records Pharaoh boasting of his kingdom and its rivers. The prayer asks for the one kind of dwelling that his wealth could not commission, and it asks for it in the vocabulary of building, as if to set the two construction projects side by side.

**Deliverance From a Man and From His Deeds**

The second request separates two things that could have been said as one: *“deliver me from Pharaoh and his ˹evil˺ doing”* (66:11). Escaping the man would not have been enough. What she asks to be free of is also what he did — the system of oppression she lived inside, benefited from, and could not have wholly avoided as the wife of the person running it.

That distinction is the most searching part of the prayer, and it is what makes her a model rather than a victim. A person embedded in an unjust arrangement is implicated by it even when they did not design it, and the honest prayer is not merely to be rescued but to be cleared of the whole business. She then widens it once more, asking to be saved from *“the wrongdoing people”* (66:11) — not just the tyrant but the court, the officials, the whole apparatus that made his rule possible. Tyranny in the Qur'an is rarely the work of one man; it requires a society of people willing to carry out what he wants.

**What the Reports Add About Her End**

The commentators report that Āsiya was tortured by Pharaoh for believing in Moses, and that she spoke these words during that torture, after which she was taken to Paradise. The detail explains the shape of the prayer as nothing else does. A woman under torture asking first for a house near her Lord is describing what she can see; the deliverance she asks for second arrives in the same moment as the house.

Her standing in the tradition is high. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have named the best of the women of Paradise as Khadīja bint Khuwaylid, Fāṭima bint Muḥammad, Maryam bint ʿImrān and Āsiya bint Muzāḥim, the wife of Pharaoh (transmitted in Musnad Aḥmad and cited by Ibn Kathīr in his commentary on this verse). Two of the four named are in this passage, and the chapter closes with the second of them. The pairing is not accidental: the sūrah selects, from all the believing women available, the two whose fidelity to God was worked out entirely without the support of a believing household.

**A Prayer Given to People Who Are Not in Palaces**

The example is set forth *for the believers*, plural and general, which means it is meant to be used by readers whose situations bear no resemblance to hers. That is possible because the prayer is built around a structure that transfers. Ask for nearness to God as the primary thing; ask to be extracted from the specific wrong you are inside; ask to be kept clear of the people who sustain it.

Read against the chapter's earlier verses, the placement becomes sharper still. Believers were told to guard themselves *and* their families from the Fire (66:6), and the obvious objection is the case where the family is the problem. Āsiya answers it. A person whose household is hostile to faith is not thereby excused from faith and not thereby doomed by association; the duty contracts to what remains possible, which is the state of one's own soul and the direction of one's own prayer. Nothing in her prayer asks for her husband to be changed, and nothing asks for the court around him to be reformed. She asks to be taken out. That is a narrower request than the one a believer in a healthy household might make, and the Qur'an records it as sufficient — indeed as exemplary — which tells a reader in a comparable position what is actually being required of them. The chapter has one more example to give, and it concerns a woman with no household at all.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:12

> ˹There is˺ also ˹the example of˺ Mary, the daughter of ’Imrân, who guarded her chastity, so We breathed into her ˹womb˺ through Our angel ˹Gabriel˺. She testified to the words of her Lord and His Scriptures, and was one of the ˹sincerely˺ devout.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Model, and a Woman With No Husband at All**

The verse continues the sentence begun in the previous one, so that Mary is joined to Pharaoh's wife as the second example set forth for the believers. The progression across the chapter's four women is deliberate. Two were married to righteous men and were lost. One was married to a tyrant and was saved. The fourth was married to nobody.

That last step completes the argument. Having shown that a good household does not save and a bad one does not condemn, the chapter produces a case in which the household is absent entirely, and the woman is commended in her own name. For a sūrah that opened inside a marriage, spent five verses on marital friction, and warned about replacement wives, closing on an unmarried woman held up as a model for all believers is a considered ending. The marital frame that organised the first half is set aside in the last line.

**Guarding What Was Publicly Doubted**

*“who guarded her chastity”* (66:12) is the first thing said of her, and it is said in answer to something. Mary's people accused her when she returned carrying a child, and the Qur'an preserves their words and her vindication in the nineteenth sūrah. The same description appears there in the parallel verse: *“And ˹remember˺ the one who guarded her chastity, so We breathed into her through Our angel, ˹Gabriel,˺ making her and her son a sign for all peoples”* (21:91).

The repetition of the description across sūrahs is a defence entered into the permanent record. What her community concluded about her was wrong, and the correction is issued not once but wherever she is mentioned. This has a bearing on the chapter that contains it. The sūrah began with a private matter in the Prophet's house becoming public, and ends with a woman whose private life was publicly misjudged and publicly cleared. In both cases the verdict that stands is the one God issued, not the one the neighbours reached.

**The Spirit Breathed In, and What the Commentators Dispute**

*“so We breathed into her ˹womb˺ through Our angel ˹Gabriel˺”* (66:12) states the conception without explaining it, and the Arabic says literally that We breathed into it of Our Spirit. What *Our Spirit* refers to has been read three ways. Many take it as Gabriel, the angel who appeared to her, and the translation follows this reading. Others understand it as a spirit among the spirits God creates, here the spirit of Jesus himself. A third reading takes the phrase at its most literal, as the Spirit of God.

The disagreement is old and the stakes in it are doctrinal rather than merely linguistic, since the Qur'an is emphatic that Jesus was a servant and messenger and warns against going to extremes in exactly this matter: *“The Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary, was no more than a messenger of Allah and the fulfilment of His Word through Mary and a spirit ˹created by a command˺ from Him”* (4:171). The Gabriel reading has the advantage of fitting the narrative of the annunciation and of avoiding any suggestion of a divine component entering a human body. What all three readings preserve is the grammar of the sentence: the action is God's, Mary is its recipient, and the possessive *Our* attaches the breath to Him without making the child anything other than created.

**Belief in Words and in Books**

*“She testified to the words of her Lord and His Scriptures”* (66:12) describes her faith in two registers. The commentators read the scriptures as the revealed books generally, so that she believed in all that had been sent down before her. The words of her Lord are usually read as His commands and decrees, including the ones addressed personally to her, which in her case were extraordinarily demanding.

The distinction matters because the two are not the same test. Believing the Books is a general commitment shared by every person of faith in her tradition. Accepting the word spoken directly to her required something else: she was told she would bear a child without a husband, in a society certain to draw the obvious conclusion, and her recorded response is a question about how this could be rather than a refusal. The verse credits her with both — the general faith and the particular submission — and puts the words before the Books, which is the order in which she actually met them.

**"Among the Devoutly Obedient"**

The final phrase, *min al-qānitīn*, closes the chapter. The word is the same one used earlier in the sūrah for the better wives God might supply — devoutly obedient, constant, standing in worship (66:5) — and applying it to Mary settles how it should be read there. A woman with no husband is described by the term, so the obedience it names is owed to God and to nobody else.

Two further details are worth drawing out. The form used is masculine plural, *al-qānitīn* rather than *al-qānitāt*, which the grammarians explain as placing her among the devout as a class rather than among devout women specifically; some read the phrase as indicating that she came from a devout lineage, the family of ʿImrān whom the Qur'an names among those God chose above all people (3:33). Either way, the chapter ends by locating a woman inside the general body of the faithful rather than in a category of her own. That is the conclusion the whole passage has been building toward. The sūrah that began by correcting a household ends by establishing that the unit which is finally weighed is the person.
