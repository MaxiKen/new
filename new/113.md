# Sūrah al-Falaq (Chapter 113) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:1

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Shelter, Four Threats**

The architecture of this chapter is a single shelter with four doors of danger. The preposition *bi-* attaches the speaker to one thing — the Lord of the daybreak — and the preposition *min* attaches him to four: what He created, the night when it darkens, those who blow on knots, and the envier when he envies. The refuge is singular; the list of what is fled from is open-ended enough to cover a life.

That proportion is the argument. A prayer structured this way says that the number of harms is not matched by the number of protections. There is one place to stand, and it holds against everything named in the following four verses and everything not named in them.

The verb is first person singular: *aʿūdhu*, I seek refuge. The command *qul* at the head puts the words in the Prophet's mouth, but the form belongs to whoever recites them. Every Muslim who says this sūrah is not reporting a prayer the Prophet made; he is making it. That is why the two chapters at the end of the codex are used as supplication and not only as recitation.

**ʿĀdha Is the Verb of Someone Who Cannot Win**

To seek refuge — *ʿādha*, *istaʿādha* — is to place yourself under a stronger party because you cannot hold your own position. The word carries inability in its meaning; nobody takes refuge from something he can defeat.

The Qur'an commands this verb rather than permitting it, and commands it to the Prophet ﷺ himself: *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah. Surely He is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”* (7:200); *“And if you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah. Indeed, He ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”* (41:36); *“When you recite the Quran, seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed.”* (16:98). A man protected from error is told to seek protection from temptation, before prayer and during it.

That instruction settles the status of the danger. If the harms named in this sūrah were superstition, the Qur'an would not order its own prophet to ask for shelter from them. The command is the evidence: the request for refuge is rational because the exposure is real.

**Falaq Is What Gets Split Open**

*Falaq* comes from a root meaning to cleave, to split something lengthwise. The dawn is a *falaq* because the night appears to be split by it — the dark is opened and light comes through the opening. The Qur'an uses the same root for God's own action: *“He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision.”* (6:96). God is *fāliq al-iṣbāḥ*, the Cleaver of the morning.

The word also carries a metaphorical sense in the Arabic lexicographical tradition: the emergence of clarity after uncertainty, of truth after confusion. On that reading the verse asks for shelter in the Lord who splits open confusion, and the request becomes a request for understanding as much as for safety. The Qur'an supplies the same idea elsewhere: *“And declare, ‘The truth has come and falsehood has vanished. Indeed, falsehood is bound to vanish.’”* (17:81).

A minority reading takes *falaq* to be a proper name — a well, a prison, a tree, or a mountain in Hell. It was recorded and it has no support in the verse's own grammar, since "Lord of the daybreak" pairs naturally with the night named two verses later. The exegetes kept the reading for completeness; the plain sense is the one the chapter uses.

**Lord of an Event, Not of a Thing**

The title is not "Lord of the light" or "Lord of the sun." It is Lord of the *falaq* — of the splitting, the moment of transition. The verse names God by what He does at the turn of night into day, and the choice is deliberate: that turn happens every morning, everywhere, to everyone, and nobody causes it.

A refuge invoked by this title is invoked by a daily demonstration. The person praying has seen the dark split open that morning and knows it was not his doing. The argument for trusting the shelter is in the evidence of the last twenty-four hours.

This is also why the sūrah moves from the daybreak to the night. The same Lord governs both, and the prayer does not ask Him to abolish the night; it asks Him to be its Lord while it lasts. Danger is not removed by the verse — it is placed under management.

**A Chapter Revealed to Undo a Spell**

The occasion most often given for these two sūrahs is the report that magic was worked on the Prophet ﷺ. ʿĀʾisha narrated that he began to imagine he was doing things he was not doing; that two men came to him in a dream, one at his head and one at his feet; that one said he had been bewitched; that the magician was Labīd ibn al-Aʿṣam; that the material used was a comb, the hair gathered on it, and the outer covering of the pollen of a male date-palm; and that it lay in the well of Dharwān. He went to the well, returned, and refused to have the objects brought out, saying he had been cured by Allah and feared the matter would spread evil among the people; the well was then filled in. That is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3268.

Bukhārī's report does not name these two sūrahs. The connection comes through the exegetical tradition, which records that Gabriel identified a knotted string on which the spell had been whispered, that ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib was sent to fetch it, and that the fourteen verses of the two chapters were recited over its fourteen knots. [UNVERIFIED: the detail about ʿAlī and the fourteen knots is transmitted in the classical commentaries without a chain grade I could check against a ḥadīth collection; the bewitchment itself is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3268.]

**The Two Protectors**

Together with the next chapter this one is called *al-Muʿawwidhatān*, the Two Protectors, from the verb of seeking refuge that opens both. Their standing is stated in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections. ʿUqba ibn ʿĀmir reported the Messenger of Allah ﷺ saying: "What wonderful verses have been sent down this night, the like of which has never been seen — *Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of the dawn*, and *Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of men*" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 814a).

The same narrator described their use. Travelling with the Prophet ﷺ between al-Juḥfa and al-Abwāʾ, when a wind and intense darkness overtook them, he began to seek refuge reciting the two chapters, and said: "O ʿUqba, use them when seeking refuge, for no one has ever sought refuge with anything comparable to them." ʿUqba added that he heard him leading the congregation in prayer with them. That is Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1463, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī.

A report from Zirr ibn Hubaysh preserves an early hesitation: Ibn Masʿūd is said to have treated the two chapters as supplication given to the Prophet ﷺ rather than as Qur'an, and Ubayy ibn Kaʿb answered that he had asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ about them and been told they had been revealed to him and recited by him as Qur'an. The hesitation is not found in any surviving text of the revelation and was settled by the community's practice.

**Refuge Comes Before Everything Else in This Book**

The placement of these two chapters at the end of the codex is not an accident of length. The Book closes with a man asking for protection, and the request is the last word of the revelation.

That order is already prescribed inside the text: *“When you recite the Quran, seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed.”* (16:98). Recitation is preceded by refuge, and the Qur'an ends by supplying the two formulas in full. Whoever finishes the codex finishes in the posture in which he is required to begin reading it.

The following chapter repeats the verb and changes the title of God invoked; the movement from daybreak to humankind is the subject of the next chapter, and it is the only connection this verse needs to make.

## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:2

> from the evil of whatever He has created,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Widest Category in the Qur'an's List of Fears**

The word that does the work is *mā* — what, whatever. It is the general relative, and it makes the object of the refuge unlimited: from the evil of whatever He has created. No class is specified, no exception is admitted, and no boundary is drawn around the request.

The four protections in this chapter are arranged so that the widest comes first. After it come three named cases: the night when it darkens, the blowers on knots, the envier when he envies. Each of the three is a subset of the first. If the general clause stood at the end it would look like a summary; standing at the beginning it looks like a covering blanket under which the specific petitions are placed.

That arrangement makes the verse the most comprehensive prayer for safety in the Qur'an. Nothing created is excluded, which means nothing that can harm a person is excluded. The reciter is not asking to be protected from a list but from a category, and the list that follows is offered as illustration rather than as limit.

**The Evil Belongs to the Created Thing, Not to the Creating**

The construction is exact: *sharr mā khalaqa* — the evil of what He created. It is not *sharr khalaqa*, the evil He created. The genitive attaches evil to the creature, and the act of creation stays with God.

That distinction is not decorative. It reflects a consistent convention in the Qur'an, which attributes good to God without qualification and handles evil through other grammars. The jinn in their own chapter say of themselves: *“We do not know whether evil is intended for those on earth, or whether their Lord intends for them what is right.”* (72:10) — even the possibility of evil is spoken of passively, without naming its author.

The verse in this chapter does the same thing in two words. God is the creator of everything, including the things from whose evil refuge is sought; the evil is theirs, not His. A reader who wanted a doctrine of evil from the Qur'an would find the grammar settled before the argument begins: creation is divine, harm is a property of creatures, and the response to harm is not to renegotiate the first claim but to seek shelter under it.

**What the Category Is Understood to Contain**

Four readings are recorded, and they differ mainly in scope. Some take the phrase to mean Satan and his kind, on the ground that the greatest evil among created things is the one that works on the heart. Others take it to mean the Fire, the created evil that is final. A third group reads it generally — all evil, whatever its source. A fourth restricts it to the evil committed or manifested by creatures: the harm human beings and jinn do.

The general reading is the strongest, for a structural reason. If the verse meant Satan alone, the following three verses would be additions to a list; since the verse says "whatever He has created," the following three are instances of it. The chapter then reads as one large petition followed by three examples, which is how the Qur'an usually handles such lists.

The reading that takes the phrase as the Fire is not incompatible with that. It narrows the reference to the worst created thing, and the Qur'an does elsewhere describe the Fire as the evil that is prepared. But it costs the verse its function in this chapter, which is to cover everything the reciter cannot name.

**Creation as a Veil That Also Reveals**

A further interpretation treats the phrase as referring to creation itself, on the ground that the created world is a veil: it hides the Creator by occupying attention, and reveals Him by displaying His qualities. On this reading the refuge sought is from the veil as veil — from being stopped by the world at the world.

The reading belongs to the contemplative strand of commentary rather than to the plain sense, and it is preserved in the tradition alongside the others. Its support in the Qur'an is the language of light and sign: *“Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth.”* (24:35), and the constant claim that created things are *āyāt*, signs, whose function is to be seen through.

The plain reading remains the one that fits the sūrah. What follows this verse are harms, not distractions: darkness, sorcery, envy. A chapter that lists those three is a chapter about danger, and the general clause at its head is best understood as the totality of danger rather than as the totality of existence.

**Asking Protection From What God Made**

An objection has been pressed against this verse since the earliest centuries: if God created the thing, how can one ask God for protection from it? The answer is inside the Qur'an's own account of harm, which makes created evil real and never independent.

The clearest statement is in the verse about magic at Babylon: *“Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will.”* (2:102). The harm is genuine — it broke marriages — and it operates only within a permission it does not control.

Refuge in the Lord is therefore refuge in the owner of the thing that threatens you. The prayer does not set God against His creation; it appeals from a creature to its Creator, on the ground that the Creator's authority over it is total. That is why the title used in verse one is *Lord* — *rabb*, the one who governs and disposes — rather than a name of power or of mercy. Lordship over the daybreak includes lordship over everything the daybreak reveals.

**A Clause That Covers What Has Not Been Invented Yet**

Because the petition is general, it does not age. The three specific evils named in this chapter belong to a world of darkness, cords, and neighbours' envy; the general clause covers harms that had no name when it was revealed and have names now.

That is a deliberate feature of a text meant to be recited for as long as the religion lasts. A list would have been exhausted by its own contents. A category is not.

The verse therefore does two things at once, and the reciter may use either. He may ask for protection from everything created, in which case the rest of the chapter adds emphasis. Or he may find that the rest of the chapter has named three of his actual fears — the hour he cannot see through, the person working against him in secret, the friend who resents his good fortune — and that the general clause at the head covers whatever he has not thought to name.

## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:3

> and from the evil of the night when it grows dark,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ghāsiq and Waqaba Describe a Darkness That Arrives**

Two words carry the verse, and both were argued over. *Ghāsiq* is the active participle of a root meaning to be dark or black; as a noun it names the dark one, the darkening thing. *Waqaba* means to enter, to sink in, to spread over — of the sun, to set; of night, to come down and cover.

The phrase *al-ghāsiq idhā waqab* therefore describes a darkness in the act of arriving: the darkening one when it settles. Most commentators take the referent to be the night, and the evil sought against is the evil of the hours when it has fully closed in.

A second reading takes *waqaba* from a different sense of the same root — to grow cool — and understands the verse as the night when it turns cold. The grounds are lexical: the root is used of cold as well as of descent, and the night's cooling is as much a feature of it as its darkening. The dominant reading is stronger, because the participle *ghāsiq* is a word about darkness and not about temperature, and because the pairing of a dark thing with a darkening verb is the natural construction. The cooling reading survives as a legitimate minority gloss rather than as a rival interpretation.

**Why Night Is Named as a Source of Harm**

The verse is practical before it is anything else. Night is when harm arrives unseen: when the road cannot be read, when the intruder cannot be identified, when the animal in the brush is heard rather than watched. A person's capacity to guard himself falls as the light falls, and the sūrah marks the fact.

The Qur'an observes elsewhere that concealment is the condition of evil plotting. Those who oppose the messenger are described as agreeing by day and scheming when the meeting breaks up at night, and the text says that God records what they contrive in the dark. Evil that depends on being unseen depends on the night, and a prayer against the night's evil is a prayer against the operations that require it.

There is a second, less physical layer. Night is the time when the mind is least defended — when sleep approaches, when solitude is complete, when thought has no company to correct it. The tradition's nightly practice of reciting this chapter treats that vulnerability as real. The refuge asked for is not only from what happens outside the house but from what the hours do to a person inside it.

**The Moon Reading Rests on a Report From ʿĀʾisha**

A substantial body of commentary identifies *al-ghāsiq* with the moon. The evidence is a Prophetic statement, not a lexical inference. ʿĀʾisha reported: the Prophet ﷺ looked at the moon and said, "O ʿĀʾisha, seek refuge in Allah from the evil of this, for this is *al-ghāsiq idhā waqab*" — the darkening one when it darkens or sets. The report is in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3366, which al-Tirmidhī called *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ* and Dārussalām grades *ḥasan*.

That report settles a second referent for the word. It does not abolish the first. The moon is *ghāsiq* precisely when it sets and the night takes over, so the two readings describe successive moments of the same darkness: the night darkens, and within it the moon darkens.

The exegetical method here is worth noting, because it is the method the tradition uses throughout. Where a word in the Qur'an admits two referents and a report from the Prophet ﷺ attaches one of them to the word itself, both are kept. The report does not narrow the verse; it fills it out. A reciter who thinks of the setting moon and a reciter who thinks of the closing night are both inside the phrase.

**The Pleiades Reading Is the Weakest of the Three**

A minority identified *al-ghāsiq* with the Pleiades, *al-Thurayyā*, at their setting, on the ground that disease and pestilence were held to become prevalent in the season when that cluster declines.

The grounds are not textual. They rest on the medical and astronomical beliefs of the period, in which the risings and settings of particular stars were taken to govern seasons of sickness. Nothing in the verse requires the identification, no report supports it, and the word *ghāsiq* is an ordinary Arabic term for what darkens rather than a name for a constellation.

The reading is preserved in the commentaries because the tradition records minority glosses even when it does not adopt them, and because the phenomenon it appeals to was real: seasonal epidemics did cluster, and people did mark them by the stars. It is the clearest example in this chapter of an interpretation that tells the historian about the commentators rather than about the verse.

**The Sūrah Is Recited at the Hour It Names**

The practice attached to these verses places them in the dark. ʿĀʾisha reported that every night, when the Prophet ﷺ went to his bed, he joined his hands, breathed into them, recited into them this chapter, the next one, and *Say: He is Allah—One*, and then wiped over as much of his body as he could, beginning with the head, the face and the front of the body, doing that three times. That is Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī.

The other report puts the same chapters outdoors. Travelling between al-Juḥfa and al-Abwāʾ, when a wind and intense darkness came over the party, the Prophet ﷺ sought refuge reciting the two chapters and told ʿUqba ibn ʿĀmir to do the same, adding that no one has ever sought refuge with anything comparable to them (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1463).

In both cases the prayer is used at the moment its own third verse describes. The sūrah is not a text about nightfall so much as an instrument for it, and the tradition treats it that way — recited at bed, on a dark road, in wind, before sleep, at the two ends of the day.

**Three of the Four Evils Cannot Be Seen**

The list this verse belongs to has a shape. The night's darkness is a condition rather than an agent; the blowers on knots work out of sight; the envier's evil is inside him. Only the first is a natural phenomenon, and even it is named for what it hides.

That is the common feature. Each of the three specific harms operates where observation fails: in the dark, at a distance, in another person's heart. None of them can be watched for, none can be litigated, and none can be answered by force. A refuge is the only available response, which is why the sūrah is a refuge and not a set of instructions.

The first verse supplies the reason the refuge works. The Lord invoked there is the Lord who splits the night open every morning — the one who ends the condition under which all three harms operate. The prayer does not claim the darkness will be lifted at once; it claims that the darkness has a Lord.

**Night Is Also His, and That Is Why the Prayer Works**

Verse one names God as Lord of the daybreak and verse three asks for protection from the night. The two are the same governance seen from two ends, and the Qur'an says so directly: *“He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.”* (6:96).

Night in that verse is not an invasion but an appointment — made for rest, measured, part of a design. The evil sought against in this chapter is not the night's existence but what happens inside it. The distinction keeps the prayer from becoming a complaint about creation.

The structure of the whole chapter follows from it. Every harm named is a harm that occurs within a created order God governs, and the refuge is taken in the governor. The following verse turns from the natural order to the human one.

## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:4

> and from the evil of those ˹witches casting spells by˺ blowing onto knots,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Technique Is Described, Not a Type of Person**

The verse names an activity. *Al-naffāthāt fī l-ʿuqad* — those who blow onto knots — is a phrase built entirely out of verbs and objects: a blowing, a place to blow on, and the people who do it. Nothing is said about who they are by birth, by tribe, or by temperament, and nothing is said about what the blowing achieves. The description is of a method.

That method was a recognizable trade in the Arabia of the revelation. Knots were tied in a cord one at a time; over each knot an incantation was spoken or breathed; the finished cord was kept, hidden, or buried; and the spell was released by untying the knots in the same order. The operation had a beginning, a set of instruments, and a known way of being reversed, which is why the exegetical accounts of the Prophet's own bewitchment describe a string being located and the knots being undone by recitation.

Naming a craft rather than a person has an effect on the prayer. The reciter is not asking to be protected from a category of people he might identify in his street. He is asking to be protected from something done to him at a distance, by someone he may never meet, using a method he cannot detect. Detection is precisely what the verse gives up on: the harm is described in terms of its technique because its author is unavailable.

**Nafatha Is a Blow With Almost Nothing in It**

The verb distinguishes three kinds of blowing in Arabic, and the distinction is fine enough that the commentators drew it out. *Nafakha* is to blow air; *tafaḷa* is to spit; *nafatha* is between them — a blowing accompanied by a small amount of moisture, or by none at all. The intensive form used here, *naffātha*, denotes someone who does it repeatedly and as a practice.

The choice of the middle term is not incidental. A spell performed by breathing on knots requires no material, leaves no mark, and can be done anywhere in silence. It is the cheapest and least detectable form of the craft, which is why it became its characteristic gesture and why the verse uses it to stand for the whole practice.

The feminine plural form is the ordinary way Arabic makes a class out of an agent-noun. The intensive active participle in the feminine plural here denotes those whose function is this blowing, and the grammar says nothing about whether the practitioners in question were women. That question was argued separately, on the evidence of reports rather than of the word.

**What the Reports Say About Who Did It**

The authenticated account of the Prophet's bewitchment names a man: Labīd ibn al-Aʿṣam, of the Jews of Medina, who used a comb, the hair gathered on it, and the covering of a male date-palm's pollen, and hid the material in the well of Dharwān (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3268). Other accounts, preserved in the exegetical literature, describe women of his household performing the knot-work, and the early commentators connected the feminine plural of this verse to them.

The two are not in conflict. A spell could be commissioned by one person and worked by others, and the tradition records both the employer and the operators. What the verse settles is neither: it describes the work, and leaves the workers unnamed.

Commentators from al-Rāzī onward made the same point from the grammar. The feminine plural in Arabic is used for a class in general, not only for women, so nothing in the wording restricts the refuge sought to harms caused by female practitioners. The reading that takes the form as generic is the one that fits the sūrah's purpose, which is to cover an evil the reciter cannot identify.

**Magic Is Real in the Qur'an, and It Is Never Independent**

The Qur'an's treatment of sorcery has two fixed points, and this verse sits between them. The first is that the practice exists and does damage. Al-Baqarah describes what people learned at Babylon: *“Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will. They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter.”* (2:102). Marriages broke; the buyer of the craft lost his share in what comes next.

The second point is in the same sentence: no harm occurs except by God's will. The craft has effect and no authority. It operates inside a permission it does not control, and the one who uses it trades his Hereafter for that permission.

This chapter's contribution is the correct response. Neither contempt nor terror is offered. Contempt would leave a person unguarded against something the Qur'an says can break a marriage; terror would grant the craft an independence the same Qur'an denies. The response is a request for refuge — the recognition that the danger is real and that it belongs to God.

**ʿUqda Is the Qur'an's Word for a Bind Only Someone Else Can Loose**

The noun in the verse, *ʿuqda*, appears elsewhere in the Qur'an in a personal rather than a magical sense. Moses, sent to Pharaoh, asks: *“and remove the impediment from my tongue”* (20:27) — in Arabic, *wa-ḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī*, untie a knot from my tongue. The word is the same, and so is the logic: a knot is something tied that the one tied by it cannot untie himself.

That is what makes the image right for the harm in this verse. The person affected by knot-work is, by definition, not the one who tied the knot and cannot find it. His tongue, his marriage, his health, his mind may be bound from a place he cannot reach, and the only remedy is an agency greater than both parties.

The Qur'an uses the same idea for the social bond at 2:102 — the rift between husband and wife is a loosening of a knot that was tied in the marriage contract. Tying and untying are the book's ordinary language for what joins people and what separates them. The blowers on knots are working at that level, in the dark, with a cord.

**The Same Gesture, Used in the Opposite Direction**

The tradition preserves an inversion of this verse's image, and the inversion is exact. ʿĀʾisha reported that every night the Prophet ﷺ joined his hands, breathed into them, recited into them this chapter and the next and *Say: He is Allah—One*, and wiped over as much of his body as he could, beginning with the head and face, three times — Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī.

The gesture is the same one the verse condemns: blowing, in a repeated act, directed at the person who needs help. What differs is the content. Over the knots went an incantation addressed to powers below God; into the palms went the revelation of God, and the breath carried it across the body rather than binding it into a cord.

That is the practical theology of the two chapters at the end of the codex. They do not argue that the world contains no hidden harm. They supply a counter-practice for it, using the same human instruments — breath, repetition, hands — pointed at a different object. The reciter is told to expect the blowing and to do some breathing of his own.

## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:5

> and from the evil of an envier when they envy.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Evil Named Is Inside a Human Heart**

The chapter's list ends where it began to point all along: not in the dark, not at a distance, but in a person who knows you. The envier is usually a neighbour, a relative, a colleague, a friend. Of the four protections requested in this sūrah, this is the only one whose source may be sitting in the same room.

The order of the list is instructive. It moves outward to inward — from all created things, to the night that hides them, to a craft worked in secret, to a feeling no one can see. Each step narrows the distance between the reciter and the harm. The last item is the closest, and it is the one he is least able to guard against, because the guard would have to be inside the other person.

That is why the chapter closes here rather than on sorcery. A person can avoid a dark road and can refuse the services of a knot-blower. He cannot decline the envy of someone who is pleased to see him and unhappy at his good fortune. The final refuge is asked for the harm that has no practical defense.

**"When They Envy" Fixes the Moment of Danger**

The phrase *idhā ḥasada* — when he envies — attaches a time to the evil. The refuge is not from the envier as a person but from him in the act of envying, and the commentators took that restriction seriously.

Two readings follow from it. On the first, the qualifier distinguishes disposition from operation: many people feel the sting of another's advantage without doing anything about it, and the verse asks protection from the moment when the feeling becomes active, when it looks, speaks, or plots. On the second, the qualifier identifies the condition under which the evil works — envy is harmful when it is being exercised, in the same way that darkness is harmful when it has closed in and a knot is harmful while it is tied.

The first reading is the stronger, because it explains why the verse needed the clause at all. Without it, the prayer would be against a class of people, defined by a permanent trait, which would make every uncomfortable person an enemy. With it, the prayer is against an event: the moment at which someone's resentment of your good becomes an operation against you.

**Envy Is Not Admiration, and It Is Not Ambition**

Arabic separates three states that English often blurs. *Ḥasad* is the wish that another's blessing be removed — the pain is at his having it, and the desire is that he stop. *Ghibṭa* is the wish for the like of what he has, without his losing it; the tradition treats this as legitimate and even praiseworthy. Between them lies ordinary ambition, which is directed at a thing rather than at a person.

The Qur'an legislates the difference directly: *“And do not crave what Allah has given some of you over others. Men will be rewarded according to their deeds and women ˹equally˺ according to theirs. Rather, ask Allah for His bounties.”* (4:32). The instruction is structural: the craving is redirected from the other person's possession to a request addressed to God. Wanting what another has becomes wanting from the One who gives.

The same word appears in the description of those who oppose the revelation: *“Or do they envy the people for Allah's bounties? Indeed, We have given the descendants of Abraham the Book and wisdom, along with great authority.”* (4:54). Envy there is not a private feeling but a public position — a refusal to accept that a blessing has been given where it was given.

**The First Two Sins in the Qur'an Were Envy**

The tradition reads the chapter's last verse as an appeal against the oldest evil on record. The first sin in the heavens was Iblīs's refusal, and the Qur'an gives his reason in his own words: *“I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.”* (7:12), after he alone refused to prostrate (7:11). The comparison that produced the refusal is the structure of envy — another's advantage experienced as one's own diminishment.

The first sin on earth was the killing of Abel. *“So Cain threatened, ‘I will kill you!’ His brother replied, ‘Allah only accepts ˹the offering˺ of the sincerely devout.’”* (5:27), and the accepted offering was Abel's. The verse records the outcome: *“Yet Cain convinced himself to kill his brother, so he killed him—becoming a loser.”* (5:30). The motive the commentators give is the same one: his brother's acceptance.

Placing envy last in a list of harms is therefore not a descent but an ascent. Darkness, sorcery, and envy are ordered by antiquity, and the final item is the one that has been operating since before human beings existed. Those who envy a favor given to another are, on this account, opposing the giving itself.

**The Envier Is Wronged Before He Wrongs Anyone**

ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, the Umayyad caliph who ruled from 717 to 720, is reported to have said that he could think of no wrongdoer more likely to be wronged than one who envies another. The remark is a summary of the mechanism rather than a moral platitude.

The envier suffers the blessing twice. Once in the possession of the person he envies, where it is a fact he cannot change; and once in himself, as a loss he cannot stop feeling, since its cause is not his own condition but someone else's. Nothing he does improves his position: gaining the same thing does not remove the other's, and the other's having it is precisely what hurts.

That is why the verse asks refuge from the envier rather than advice about him. There is no strategy available against a harm whose source is another person's pain at your good. The only response recorded in the Qur'an is patience and pardon — *“Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision.”* (2:109) — addressed to people being envied, with the decision left to God.

**The Evil Eye Is Treated as Real**

The tradition has read this verse against the evil eye from the earliest period, and the reading rests on a Prophetic statement rather than on inference. Abū Hurayrah reported the Prophet ﷺ saying: "The effect of an evil eye is a fact" — Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5740, in the Book of Medicine, in a chapter headed by that claim.

The report explains the continuous use of these two chapters. From the first centuries to the present, *al-Muʿawwidhatān* have been recited for protection against the evil eye and against spells, over children, over the sick, at night, and on journeys. The practice is not a folk addition to the religion but the application of a verse whose last item is a look accompanied by resentment.

The connection also clarifies the previous verse. Sorcery worked through a cord and an incantation; the eye works through a glance. Both are invisible operations against a person, both are named in the same chapter, and both are answered by the same request. What the sūrah treats as one problem is the harm that arrives without a weapon.

**Envy Inside the Community, Not Only Outside It**

The verse is often read as protection against outsiders, and the Qur'an itself supplies the correction. The envy described in al-Baqarah belongs to people who had received a scripture: *“Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them.”* (2:109).

The same sūrah locates the origin of religious division inside a religious community: *“Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them.”* (3:19). Division followed knowledge, and its cause was not ignorance but resentment of where the blessing had landed.

So the envier in this verse may hold the same creed as the person who recites it. That possibility is what makes the petition general rather than polemical: it asks for protection from a state of heart that no community is free of, and it does so in a chapter recited nightly by people whose neighbours are mostly not their enemies.
