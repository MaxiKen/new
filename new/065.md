# Sūrah al-Ṭalāq (Chapter 65) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shorter Sūrah About Women**

Al-Ṭalāq, "Divorce," is a Madinan chapter of twelve verses that takes its name from the subject of its opening half. The early community also called it Sūrat al-Nisāʾ al-Qusrā, "the shorter sūrah concerning women," setting it beside the fourth chapter, al-Nisāʾ, which handles the same field at far greater length. The nickname is accurate about both its scope and its function: this is a supplement, filling gaps that the earlier legislation in the second and fourth sūrahs had left open, and it reads throughout like law written in response to cases that had actually arisen.

Divorce in Islam is permitted and discouraged at once. The well-known saying that the most hated of permitted things before God is divorce is transmitted through Sunan Abī Dāwūd 2178 and Sunan Ibn Mājah 2018, and hadith scholars have long noted that the stronger chains for it are mursal, so that its wording cannot be firmly attributed to the Prophet ﷺ even though its sense is supported by the wider tradition. What is beyond dispute is the Qur'an's attitude in this chapter: divorce is legislated in detail precisely because it is a disaster, and every ruling here is designed either to delay it, to soften it, or to prevent the weaker party from being destroyed by it.

**Two Halves and One Argument**

The sūrah divides cleanly. Verses 1–7 are legislation: the timing of a pronouncement, the counting of the waiting period, the ban on evicting a woman from her home, the terms for women past menopause and women who are pregnant, housing and maintenance, payment for nursing, and the scaling of all obligations to the payer's means. Verses 8–12 are warning and consolation: the record of communities that treated God's commands as optional, the reminder sent down to this community, the messenger reciting clear signs, and finally the architecture of the heavens and the earth through which the divine command descends.

The join between the halves is not decorative. Every ruling in the first section transfers something from the stronger party to the weaker one, and rules of that shape are eroded quietly rather than rejected openly. The second section supplies the reason to keep them when nobody is checking. Three times in four verses the chapter attaches a promise to reverence — a way out, provision from an unreckoned quarter, ease in one's affairs, absolution and an immense reward — and it closes by naming the power and the knowledge that make those promises worth anything.

**What the Chapter Changed**

Read against the practice it replaced, the sūrah's rulings are radical in a specific direction. A pronouncement must be timed so that a clean term can begin, which means a man must wait weeks before he can lawfully speak. The divorced woman keeps her home for the duration of the term, at her husband's expense and to his standard. Pressuring her to leave counts as eviction. A pregnancy must be maintained to delivery. Nursing is paid labour, not an assumed duty. Every obligation is measured against the payer's actual capacity, so that nobody is crushed and nobody is exempt.

The chapter also legislates for people the earlier revelation had not covered, after a Companion raised the omission directly with the Prophet ﷺ — women past menstruation, girls who had not begun, and women carrying a child. Nothing in the sūrah is abstract. It reads as the record of a community working out, case by case, what it means to end a marriage without ending a person.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:1

> O Prophet! ˹Instruct the believers:˺ When you ˹intend to˺ divorce women, then divorce them with concern for their waiting period, and count it accurately. And fear Allah, your Lord. Do not force them out of their homes ˹during the waiting period˺, nor should they leave—unless they commit a blatant misconduct. These are the limits set by Allah. And whoever transgresses Allah’s limits has truly wronged his own soul. You never know, perhaps Allah will bring about a change ˹of heart˺ later.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Man Addressed, a Whole Community Bound**

The sūrah opens by naming the Prophet ﷺ alone, then immediately shifts to the plural: *“O Prophet! ˹Instruct the believers:˺ When you ˹intend to˺ divorce women”* (65:1). The grammar does the legal work. A singular vocative gathers the community's attention around its teacher, and the plural verb that follows tells every husband in Madina that the ruling is his. The Qur'an uses this hinge elsewhere, addressing the Prophet ﷺ in the singular while the content reaches everyone: *“Whatever good befalls you is from Allah and whatever evil befalls you is from yourself”* (4:79).

Placing the law of divorce under a prophetic address also sets its tone. Divorce here is not a private grievance settled by shouting in a courtyard; it is a regulated act performed under the eye of revelation, with a timetable, witnesses, housing rights and maintenance attached. The reports say the verse came down in the aftermath of a hard moment in the Prophet's own household — he had divorced Ḥafṣah and was told to take her back, since she fasted often, prayed long into the night, and would be his wife in the Garden (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 2283 records the divorce and the return; the wording about her fasting and night prayer is transmitted through al-Ḥākim and other collections). Whatever the exact occasion, the ruling arrives with the authority of a man who had felt its weight.

**Divorce Must Be Aimed at the Waiting Period**

The phrase rendered *“with concern for their waiting period”* (65:1) translates *li-ʿiddatihinna*, and the preposition carries the whole procedure. It means the pronouncement must be timed so that a clean, countable term can begin at once. In practice this rules out two things. A man may not divorce his wife while she is menstruating, because that cycle cannot be counted and the term would be stretched without cause. He also may not divorce her in a period of purity in which the couple have had relations, because a pregnancy may already exist that nobody yet knows about.

This is not a later juristic invention; it is how the Prophet ﷺ himself applied the verse. When ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar divorced his wife during her menses, his father asked the Prophet ﷺ about it, and the answer was to take her back, keep her until she becomes clean, let a further cycle pass, and only then either keep her or release her before touching her — *“and that is the prescribed period which Allah has fixed for the women meant to be divorced”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5251; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1471). Al-Bukhārī placed that report under a chapter headed with this very verse. The delay is deliberate: a man who must wait weeks before he can lawfully speak the word often finds that he no longer wants to speak it.

**Counting the Term Is Itself an Act of Worship**

*“And count it accurately”* (65:1) turns arithmetic into obedience. The instruction is addressed to the men, which is striking, since the term runs in the woman's body and she is the one who reports its end. Both parties are made responsible for the same number, and neither is left to guess. What hangs on the count is substantial: the husband's right to take her back, her freedom to marry someone else, the paternity of any child, and the maintenance owed in the meantime. A sloppy count is not a clerical slip but a door opened to injustice.

The base term is the one set in the earlier Madinan legislation: *“Divorced women must wait three monthly cycles ˹before they can remarry˺”* (2:228). Where there has been no consummation, no term is owed at all, and the Qur'an says so plainly: *“If you marry believing women and then divorce them before you touch them, they will have no waiting period for you to count”* (33:49). The command to count is immediately followed by *“And fear Allah, your Lord”* (65:1), pinning the calendar to conscience. Two people who have stopped trusting each other are told that the same Lord is watching the ledger.

**The House Remains Hers Until the Term Runs Out**

Then comes the sharpest social reversal in the verse: *“Do not force them out of their homes ˹during the waiting period˺, nor should they leave”* (65:1). Notice that the dwelling is called *their* homes, not his, even though the man is paying for it. Pre-Islamic practice in Arabia allowed a husband to end a marriage with a sentence and a woman to be back at her father's door the same evening, stripped of shelter and standing. The verse forbids both the expulsion and the walk-out, and the two prohibitions protect different people. She keeps her right to housing; he keeps his right to a return that is still physically possible.

The arrangement is also psychologically shrewd. Reconciliation almost never survives distance and the intervention of two offended families. Keeping the couple under one roof, without conjugal relations, preserves the ordinary contact through which anger cools. The same logic runs through the Qur'an's wider marital counsel: *“If you happen to dislike them, you may hate something which Allah turns into a great blessing”* (4:19). A right to stay is worth more than any amount of advice about patience.

**Only a Proven Indecency Breaks the Shelter**

The single exception — *“unless they commit a blatant misconduct”* (65:1) — has been read narrowly by the commentators, and rightly so. The word *fāḥisha mubayyina* carries the sense of something open and established, not whispered. Suspicion is not a ground, jealousy is not a ground, and an accusation of adultery is worth nothing in itself: the Qur'an demands four witnesses before such a charge can stand, and punishes those who bring it without them. Some jurists extended the exception to persistent, aggressive abuse of the husband's family during the term, since that too is an open wrong rather than a private doubt.

Reading the clause narrowly matters because a loosely worded exception would swallow the rule. If a man could evict his wife by alleging misconduct, the housing right created two sentences earlier would exist only on paper. The verse's structure protects itself: the shelter is the norm, the exception is defined by proof, and the burden of proof sits with the one who wants the exception.

**Limits a Man Can Only Cross Against Himself**

*“These are the limits set by Allah. And whoever transgresses Allah’s limits has truly wronged his own soul”* (65:1). The term *ḥudūd Allāh* in the Qur'an usually marks the outer edge of lawful conduct in family life, and the same phrase closes the divorce legislation in the second sūrah: *“These are the limits set by Allah, so do not transgress them”* (2:229). What is unusual here is where the damage is said to land. The man who bullies his wife out of the house has certainly harmed her; the verse says he has wronged *himself*. God's law needs no defending, and the injured woman has her redress in this world. The transgressor, meanwhile, has quietly spent something of his own that he cannot recover.

**The Clause That Refuses to Close the Door**

*“You never know, perhaps Allah will bring about a change ˹of heart˺ later”* (65:1) is the reason the whole procedure is slow. The waiting period is not a cooling-off formality but a space in which God may do something neither party can presently imagine — soften a grievance, produce a pregnancy, or simply let exhaustion pass. This applies to the first and second pronouncements, while return is still lawful; after the third, the door described in 2:230 is shut and the couple cannot simply change their minds. The verse addresses the man's certainty, not his circumstances. He is sure the marriage is finished; he is told, in the plainest words, that he does not know.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:2

> Then when they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period, either retain them honourably or separate from them honourably. And call two of your reliable men to witness ˹either way˺—and ˹let the witnesses˺ bear true testimony for ˹the sake of˺ Allah. This is enjoined on whoever has faith in Allah and the Last Day. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will make a way out for them,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Decision Forced at the Last Moment**

The Arabic *balaghna ajalahunna* means the term has been reached, and the bracketed *˹almost˺* captures how the jurists read it here: the deadline is close but has not yet expired, because once it expires there is nothing left to decide. The verse therefore fixes a point on the calendar where drifting becomes impossible. A man cannot let the clock run out by inattention and then claim he had meant to reconcile. He must act while action still has legal force.

Timing the choice this way also exposes motive. Anyone can be gracious in the first week of a separation, when feelings are hot and gestures are cheap. What the verse wants is a decision made when the counting is nearly done and the consequences are immediate. The same instruction appears in the earlier legislation with the same deadline: *“When you divorce women and they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period, either retain them honourably or let them go honourably”* (2:231). Repetition in a later sūrah is not redundancy. The Madinan community had by then seen the rule evaded in practice, and the reminder arrives with witnesses attached.

**Two Exits, One Standard of Conduct**

The word *bi-maʿrūf* — decently, by what is recognised as right — governs both branches of the sentence, and the balance is the point. Taking her back honourably means doing it because the marriage is wanted, not as a manoeuvre. The abuse the earlier sūrah names is exactly this: a husband who revokes the divorce days before the term ends, holds his wife for another cycle, divorces her again, and repeats the exercise until she is worn down. Of that man the Qur'an says, *“But do not retain them ˹only˺ to harm them ˹or˺ to take advantage ˹of them˺. Whoever does that surely wrongs his own soul”* (2:231).

Separating honourably has its own content, and it is concrete rather than sentimental: the dowry is not clawed back, maintenance for the remaining term is paid, her possessions leave with her, and her reputation is not damaged on the way out. *“It is not lawful for husbands to take back anything of the dowry given to their wives”* (2:229) closes the most common route of petty revenge. What the verse forbids in both directions is using the legal machinery as a weapon. A marriage may end; the standard of behaviour may not.

**Witnesses Make a Private Act Public Property**

*“And call two of your reliable men to witness ˹either way˺”* (65:2) introduces something the pre-Islamic practice had no use for: a record. Divorce by unilateral word is easy to deny, easy to misremember and easy to re-describe months later when property or a child is at stake. Two upright witnesses convert the event into a fact the community can confirm. The jurists divide over whether the summons is obligatory or strongly recommended, and both readings have a basis — the imperative form points to obligation, while the absence of any stated penalty and the silence of the earlier divorce verses on witnessing point to emphatic recommendation.

The stronger position treats witnessing as at minimum a binding safeguard wherever rights could be lost, because the harm it prevents is precisely the harm the sūrah legislates against. A woman who cannot prove she was taken back may find her marriage denied; a woman who cannot prove she was released may find herself neither married nor free. The Qur'an's general policy in matters of contract runs the same way, requiring documentation and witnesses for debts so that memory is never the only evidence. What is left undocumented is what later becomes disputed.

**Testimony Owed to God Before the Parties**

The witnesses are told to *“bear true testimony for ˹the sake of˺ Allah”* (65:2), and the phrase quietly anticipates how witnesses actually fail. They are usually relatives or neighbours of one household, and the pressure on them is not to lie outright but to shade the account toward the family that invited them. Naming God as the beneficiary of the testimony cuts that line of pressure. The witness is not there to help the man who called him.

This is the Qur'an's standing rule for anyone asked to speak in a dispute, and it is stated most sharply where it costs most: testimony must be upheld for God even against oneself, one's parents and one's relatives. In a divorce the stakes are ordinary and domestic — who said what, and when — which is exactly why the temptation to be agreeable is strong. The verse makes the witness a servant of the record rather than of the host. A community that takes this seriously produces documents that can be relied on decades later, which is the only reason the procedure protects anyone at all.

**Faith Is the Only Enforcement Mechanism Offered**

*“This is enjoined on whoever has faith in Allah and the Last Day”* (65:2) tells the reader who is expected to comply and why. There is no court named in the verse, no official inspecting the count, no penalty specified for the man who ignores the housing rule or skips the witnesses. What the sūrah offers instead is a description of the kind of person the instruction is for. The rules of divorce are placed inside the category of belief, so that keeping them is evidence of faith and evading them is evidence of something else.

That is a realistic strategy rather than a pious one. Family law is enforced in the home long before it reaches a judge, and most of what happens in a collapsing marriage is never seen by anyone with authority. A husband who genuinely expects to stand before God will count the term honestly when nobody is checking. This is also why the sūrah will shortly turn from legislation to the fate of communities that treated God's commands as optional — the law and the reckoning are argued as one subject.

**Mindfulness Opens an Exit Nobody Can See**

The verse ends mid-thought: *“And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will make a way out for them”* (65:2). The promise is attached, in context, to men and women who have just been told to do several difficult things — delay the pronouncement, keep a former wife in the house, pay for a marriage that is ending, submit a private humiliation to two witnesses. The natural objection is that obedience here is expensive. The answer is that God undertakes to provide the exit, *makhraj*, from the constriction that obedience creates.

The word is spatial. A *makhraj* is the way out of a place with no visible door, which is how a failing marriage feels from inside. The relief promised is not necessarily reconciliation; it may be a clean separation that leaves both parties able to live, or a settlement neither side could have negotiated. Elsewhere the Qur'an gives the same guarantee for calamity in general: *“And whoever has faith in Allah, He will ˹rightly˺ guide their hearts ˹through adversity˺”* (64:11). The next verse will name what accompanies that exit.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:3

> and provide for them from sources they could never imagine. And whoever puts their trust in Allah, then He ˹alone˺ is sufficient for them. Certainly Allah achieves His Will. Allah has already set a destiny for everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Provision From a Direction Nobody Was Watching**

*“and provide for them from sources they could never imagine”* (65:3) answers the practical fear that runs under the whole chapter. Divorce is expensive. A man facing housing costs for a woman who is no longer his wife, maintenance until she delivers, and payment for nursing a child he may rarely see, has every reason to calculate that obedience will ruin him. The verse does not deny the arithmetic. It denies that the arithmetic is complete, because the ledger he is consulting contains only the income streams he already knows about.

The Arabic *min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasib* means from a quarter he does not reckon or count on. The emphasis falls on the unexpectedness rather than the size. Provision is not promised as a windfall matching the loss; it is promised as arriving through a channel the person had not been monitoring — an opening at work, a debt repaid, an inheritance, help from someone who owed nothing. The commentators attach an occasion to this promise: a poor man with many children came to the Prophet ﷺ in distress and was told to fear God and be patient; not long afterwards his captured son escaped and returned driving a large flock before him, and the Prophet ﷺ told him to keep it. The point of the story is the direction of the relief, which was the one nobody was facing.

**Trust Is Not a Substitute for Effort**

*“And whoever puts their trust in Allah, then He ˹alone˺ is sufficient for them”* (65:3) uses *tawakkul*, a word that has been misread in both directions across Islamic history. It does not mean abandoning means, and the Prophet ﷺ settled the question with an image drawn from the sky: if people relied upon God as He deserves to be relied upon, they would be provided for as the birds are — *“they go out in the morning with empty stomachs and return full”* (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2344, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Tirmidhī). The birds fly out. Nothing is delivered to the nest. What the hadith removes is not labour but anxiety about the outcome of labour.

Nor is trust a mood that replaces planning. The Qur'an pairs consultation with reliance in a single sentence: *“Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah”* (3:159) — deliberation first, then reliance. Applied to this sūrah, *tawakkul* is what allows a husband to pay what he owes, hand over the house for the duration of the term, and still sleep. The word translated *“sufficient”* is *ḥasbuhu*, meaning He is enough as a guarantor. A man who believes that has one creditor to satisfy rather than a hundred fears to manage.

**Nothing God Intends Is Left Unfinished**

*“Certainly Allah achieves His Will”* (65:3) reads, in the Arabic, as a statement about completion: God carries His command through to its end. In a chapter about people whose plans have just collapsed, this is a deliberately placed sentence. Every marriage that ends was a plan that failed. The couple intended something and could not bring it about, either because circumstances refused them or because their own characters did. Human intention is chronically unfinished, and divorce is one of the clearest illustrations of that.

Set against this, the verse states the one case where intention and outcome never separate. God's command does not stall, get renegotiated, or run out of resources. The practical consequence is a reordering of where a person invests confidence. Promises made by a spouse, an employer or a relative may be sincere and still fail, because the one promising does not control the conditions. The guarantee of provision in the first half of this verse is worth something only because of the clause that follows it: the One promising can finish what He starts.

**A Measure Assigned to Every Single Thing**

*“Allah has already set a destiny for everything”* (65:3) closes the passage with *qadar* — measure, proportion, an amount fixed in advance. The Qur'an uses the term for creation at large: *“Indeed, We have created everything, perfectly preordained”* (54:49), and *“He has created everything, ordaining it precisely”* (25:2). Belief in this measuring is one of the pillars named in the well-known report of Gabriel's questioning, where faith is defined as belief in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and the divine decree, its good and its bad (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 8).

Placed at the end of a divorce ruling, the doctrine does specific work rather than general work. The waiting period is a measure. The three cycles, the months, the birth that ends a pregnant woman's term, the amount a wealthy man pays and the smaller amount an poor man pays — each is a quantity fixed by God rather than negotiated by the stronger party. Observing the count is therefore not merely legal compliance; it is an acknowledgement that the timing of this marriage's end is not the husband's to set. He may choose to divorce. He does not get to choose the shape of the aftermath.

**Why Decree Consoles Rather Than Paralyses**

The obvious objection to *qadar* is that it invites passivity: if everything is measured, why struggle? The Qur'an's own usage answers this, because it places the doctrine beside commands rather than instead of them. Here it follows an instruction to count accurately, retain or release honourably, call witnesses and pay maintenance. A decree that arrives at the end of a list of duties is plainly not being offered as an excuse for skipping them.

What it offers instead is relief from a particular kind of torment. A person emerging from a failed marriage replays the decisions endlessly, hunting for the moment when a different word would have produced a different life. Belief in a fixed measure does not excuse the wrongs actually committed — those remain, and the verse before this one made the wrongdoer answerable. It removes the fantasy that the whole course of events was ever under one person's control. Provision was measured, the term was measured, the separation fell within a measure. What remains to be judged is conduct, which is exactly the part the sūrah keeps legislating.

**Reverence as the Hinge of the Whole Passage**

Three consecutive promises in this sūrah hang on one condition. Whoever is mindful of God gets a way out and unimagined provision here; the next verse will promise ease in his affairs; the one after that will promise the erasure of sins and a great reward. The repetition is structural rather than decorative. The legislation demands behaviour that costs money and pride, and each demand is answered immediately by a benefit that is not money.

The logic is worth stating plainly. A man who fears only the court will do the minimum the court can detect, which in domestic life is very little. A man who fears God will observe the term he alone can verify. The sūrah is not merely recommending piety as a virtue; it is identifying reverence as the only mechanism capable of enforcing family law in the places where families actually live. That is why the promises are addressed to the pious and not to the compliant.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:4

> As for your women past the age of menstruation, in case you do not know, their waiting period is three months, and those who have not menstruated as well. As for those who are pregnant, their waiting period ends with delivery. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will make their matters easy for them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cases the Earlier Rule Left Unanswered**

The general law had already been given: *“Divorced women must wait three monthly cycles ˹before they can remarry˺”* (2:228). That rule works only for women whose cycles can be counted, and it left three groups without an answer — women past menopause, women who had not yet begun to menstruate, and women who were pregnant. The gap was noticed and raised. It is reported that Ubayy ibn Kaʿb put it to the Prophet ﷺ that people were saying certain women had been left unmentioned in the Qur'an, and this verse came down in response.

The episode is worth pausing over for what it shows about how the community engaged revelation. A Companion identified a class of people whom an existing ruling could not accommodate, said so openly, and received legislation. The Qur'an's family law was not handed down as a finished code to be applied mechanically; it was built in contact with cases, with the awkward instances named rather than quietly ignored. A legal system that leaves edge cases undefined is a system in which the weakest parties fall through, since the strong will always find an interpretation that suits them.

**Three Months Where There Is Nothing to Count**

For women whose cycles have ended and for those who have not begun, the term is fixed at three calendar months. The substitution is exact in function: the purpose of three cycles was to let enough time pass to establish that no pregnancy exists and to give room for return, and three months achieves both without requiring a biological signal that is not going to arrive. Where a natural measure is unavailable, a calendar measure takes its place.

The phrase *“in case you do not know”* (65:4) has been read two ways, and the difference is not trivial. Most take it as addressing uncertainty about the ruling itself — if you are unsure what her term should be, here it is. Others read it as addressing uncertainty about the woman's condition, covering the case of a woman whose cycles have stopped but who may not yet be past childbearing, so that three months settles the doubt. A related reading connects the phrase back to the housing rule of the first verse, warning a man not to expel a woman while he is still unsure whether her term has actually ended. All three readings converge on one policy: doubt is resolved in favour of waiting, never in favour of the man who wants the matter closed.

**Delivery Ends the Term, However Long or Short**

*“As for those who are pregnant, their waiting period ends with delivery”* (65:4) replaces a period of time with an event. The consequences are dramatic at both extremes. A woman who is eight months pregnant when divorced waits a few weeks; a woman who conceived days before waits the better part of a year. The principle overrides the calendar in both directions, because the purpose of the term here is not the passage of time but the establishment of paternity and the protection of the child, and birth settles that question completely.

The Prophet's practice ﷺ confirmed how far this rule reaches. Subayʿa al-Aslamiyya was widowed while pregnant and gave birth shortly afterwards. When a suitor told her she must still complete the four months and ten days that the Qur'an assigns to widows in *“let them observe a waiting period of four months and ten days”* (2:234), she went to the Prophet ﷺ, who ruled that she was free to marry, since she had delivered (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5318; the fuller version is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Ibn ʿAbbās initially held that such a woman must complete the longer of the two terms, and changed his position when Umm Salama reported the Prophet's ruling. The disagreement was resolved by evidence rather than by preference, and the delivery rule was established as general — it governs the widow as well as the divorcee.

**A Term That Belongs to the Body, Not to the Husband**

Read together, the three cases in this verse reveal what the waiting period actually is. It is not a punishment, not a probation, and not a period during which the husband retains ownership. It is a measurement taken from the woman's own condition, and the law simply reads it off her. Menstruating women are counted by cycles; women without cycles are counted by months; pregnant women are counted by their pregnancy. In every case the term is set by something happening in her life rather than by anything the man decides.

This has a practical edge in disputes. Because the count is anchored in her body, the Qur'an elsewhere warns against the one manipulation available: *“It is not lawful for them to conceal what Allah has created in their wombs”* (2:228). That prohibition is the mirror image of the husband's duty to count accurately. Both sides can distort the term, and both are forbidden to. The law assumes honest reporting from a woman and honest counting from a man, and it makes each of them answerable to God rather than to the other, since at this stage in a marriage neither will take the other's word for anything.

**Ease Promised to Those Who Keep the Measure**

*“And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will make their matters easy for them”* (65:4) is the third promise attached to reverence in four verses, and each is tailored to what the ruling just given actually costs. Here the cost is friction: months of enforced proximity, awkward questions about a pregnancy, relatives pressing for an answer, a term that may stretch far past what either party expected. The relief offered is *yusr*, ease — the smoothing of an affair that has every reason to be difficult.

Commentators read the ease in two ways that are not in conflict. One takes it as ease toward reconciliation: a couple who observe the rules carefully during the term are far likelier to find their way back, since nothing irreversible has been said or done. The other takes it as ease in the separation itself, so that what could have become a feud between two families ends as a settled matter. Which one a given couple receives is not theirs to choose. The Qur'an's general assurance stands behind both: *“So, surely with hardship comes ease”* (94:5). What the verse guarantees is that obedience is not the thing making the situation harder, though it will often feel that way in the third month.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:5

> This is the commandment of Allah, which He has revealed to you. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will absolve them of their sins and reward them immensely.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Seal Placed on the Rulings Already Given**

*“This is the commandment of Allah, which He has revealed to you”* (65:5) points backwards. Four verses of detailed instruction have just been delivered — how to time a pronouncement, how to count a term, who keeps the house, what happens to a woman past menopause or carrying a child — and the passage now stamps all of it as revealed law rather than communal custom. The demonstrative *dhālika* gathers the whole block; the verb *anzalahu*, sent down, insists on its origin.

The insistence was needed. Every ruling in this passage cut against something the pre-Islamic Arabs had taken for granted: that a husband could end a marriage in a sentence and be finished with it, that a woman had no claim on the house once the word was spoken, that a term could be stretched or collapsed at his convenience. Rules of that kind are not accepted because they are reasonable. They are accepted because of who issued them. By naming the source before the audience has time to weigh the cost, the verse forecloses the argument that these are merely the Prophet's preferences about domestic manners.

**Reverence Named for the Fourth Time**

The structure of this passage has been relentless. A ruling, then *“whoever is mindful of Allah”*; another ruling, then the same clause; and now a third repetition within a few lines. Each occurrence has carried a different reward: a way out and unimagined provision (65:2–3), ease in one's affairs (65:4), and now the covering of sins together with a reward described as immense. No other short sūrah stacks the phrase this way.

The reason lies in what the legislation asks. Divorce law is enforced almost entirely in private. Nobody is standing in the house to verify that a man counted the cycles honestly, refrained from evicting his wife, or paid what he owed for a child he will see rarely. Courts arrive late and see only what the parties choose to present. The Qur'an therefore attaches the enforcement to *taqwā*, which operates where no official can reach, and then makes that attachment worth accepting by naming what the pious man gets in return. The rhetoric is not padding; it is the legislation's enforcement clause.

**Sins Covered, Not Merely Excused**

The verb *yukaffir* comes from a root meaning to cover over, which is why the expiation paid for a broken oath is called *kaffāra*. The image is of something spread across a fault so that it no longer shows. The Qur'an promises this covering repeatedly, and what it is tied to varies in each case. Sometimes it follows reverence, as here and in *“If you are mindful of Allah, He will grant you a standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺, absolve you of your sins, and forgive you”* (8:29). Sometimes it follows faith and good works: *“As for those who believe and do good, We will certainly absolve them of their sins”* (29:7). Sometimes it follows the avoidance of the gravest wrongs: *“If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your ˹lesser˺ misdeeds”* (4:31).

Placed at the close of a divorce passage, the promise lands on people who need it in a specific way. A marriage rarely ends without both parties having said and done things they would rather not answer for. The verse does not pretend those months were innocent. It offers, to the one who kept God's limits through them, a covering over what went wrong — and the ordering is instructive, since absolution is named before reward. What a person leaving a failed marriage most wants is not a prize but the erasure of a record.

**A Reward Left Deliberately Unmeasured**

*“and reward them immensely”* (65:5) ends the passage with the one quantity in the sūrah that is not specified. Everything else here has been counted: three cycles, three months, the term ending at delivery, spending measured against means. Against that background, an unquantified reward stands apart. The Arabic *aʿẓama lahu ajran*, magnified for him a reward, states the scale without giving a figure.

The contrast is the argument. The obligations of this chapter are finite and can be listed; a man can work out exactly what obedience will cost him in months and in money. What he receives cannot be worked out at all, which means the calculation he is being asked to make is one he cannot lose. This is the same logic the Qur'an uses whenever it asks for expenditure that looks unrecoverable. The sūrah will shortly turn to the other side of the ledger — communities that weighed the same choice and refused — and the two halves of the chapter meet exactly here, at the hinge between what obedience earns and what defiance costs.

**A Short Verse Carrying the Weight of a Chapter**

Brevity here is functional. The verse says almost nothing new and settles a great deal, because its job is to convert a list of instructions into a single object that can be accepted or refused as a whole. A man may find the housing rule reasonable and the nursing fee excessive, or accept the waiting period and resent the ban on eviction. This verse denies him the option of sorting the rulings into the ones he finds sensible and the ones he does not. They arrived together, from one source, in one revelation.

The same technique appears wherever the Qur'an legislates in blocks. Family rulings are closed with a statement of their origin and a statement of their consequence, so that the block is received as law rather than as advice about how a decent man might behave. What follows in this sūrah confirms that reading: the chapter turns from instruction to the record of communities that treated a command as negotiable. A verse naming these rulings as revealed, and promising the pious both absolution and reward, stands exactly at the join between the two.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:6

> Let them live where you live ˹during their waiting period˺, according to your means. And do not harass them to make their stay unbearable. If they are pregnant, then maintain them until they deliver. And if they nurse your child, compensate them, and consult together courteously. But if you fail to reach an agreement, then another woman will nurse ˹the child˺ for the father.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Housing Measured Against the Husband's Actual Means**

The first verse of the sūrah forbade eviction; this one specifies what must be provided instead. *“Let them live where you live ˹during their waiting period˺, according to your means”* (65:6) sets the standard by a comparison rather than a figure. She is not owed a house matching some communal average, nor the minimum shelter that will keep rain off. She is owed accommodation of the kind the man himself occupies, scaled to what he can afford. A wealthy man cannot move a divorced wife into an outbuilding while he keeps the main house; a poor man is not required to rent something he could never rent for himself.

The jurists divided over how far the obligation extends. Some read the verse as covering shelter alone, on the ground that housing is all it names. Others hold that food and clothing come with it, since a woman kept in a house during her term is forbidden to leave and marry, and shelter without maintenance would starve the very right the verse creates. The second position is stronger, because it reads the ruling in light of its purpose. A right to stay in a house you cannot afford to eat in is not a right at all, and the prohibition on eviction that opened the sūrah would be easily evaded by a man who simply stopped paying.

**Pressure Is Treated as a Form of Eviction**

*“And do not harass them to make their stay unbearable”* (65:6) closes the obvious loophole. A man forbidden to put his wife out of the door can still make the door the only tolerable exit. The Arabic *lā tuḍārrūhunna li-tuḍayyiqū ʿalayhinna* — do not harm them so as to put them in straits — names the tactic precisely: not violence, but constriction, the steady narrowing of a life until the person leaves by choice and the husband can claim she departed freely.

Treating pressure as equivalent to expulsion is a mature legal move. It measures the offence by its effect rather than by its form, which is the only way to regulate conduct inside a household where every individual act can be explained away. Cutting the household budget, filling the house with hostile relatives, withholding ordinary civility, complaining loudly of the expense — none of these is eviction, and all of them are. The Qur'an uses the same verb of harm in the parallel rule about children: *“No mother or father should be made to suffer for their child”* (2:233). In both places the prohibition is aimed at the party with the power to make ordinary life unlivable.

**Maintenance for a Pregnancy That May Outlast the Marriage**

*“If they are pregnant, then maintain them until they deliver”* (65:6) removes any argument about how long the obligation runs. Since a pregnant woman's term ends with delivery, her maintenance runs to the same point, and it does so whether the divorce is revocable or final. This is the most expensive clause in the sūrah. A man who divorces a woman early in a pregnancy is bound to house and support her for months, at a point when the marriage is legally over in every other respect.

The reasoning behind it is unsentimental. The child in her body is his, and its welfare cannot be separated from hers during those months. To support the child is to support the woman carrying it, which means his responsibility to his offspring begins before the offspring is visible. The Qur'an's wider framing of provision applies directly here: *“There is no moving creature on earth whose provision is not guaranteed by Allah”* (11:6). The man paying is not, on this view, the source of the provision, only the appointed channel for it — which is also the answer to his fear that the cost will break him.

**Nursing Is Paid Work, Not an Assumed Duty**

*“And if they nurse your child, compensate them”* (65:6) states something that most legal systems took centuries to concede: the care a mother gives her infant has economic value and can be owed as a debt. The divorced woman is under no obligation to nurse. If she does, she is entitled to payment, and the payment is not charity or a sweetener attached to the settlement — it is compensation for labour performed, owed by the father because the child's maintenance is his responsibility.

The related legislation sets the normal span and the surrounding conditions: *“˹Divorced˺ mothers will breastfeed their offspring for two whole years, for those who wish to complete the nursing ˹of their child˺. The child’s father will provide reasonable maintenance and clothing for the mother ˹during that period˺”* (2:233). The same passage authorises hiring a wet-nurse as long as fair payment is made. What emerges is a system in which the father is financially liable for his child's early years regardless of whether the marriage survived, and the mother's involvement is contracted rather than conscripted. Her milk is not a resource he owns by virtue of having been her husband.

**Consultation Between People Who No Longer Wish to Speak**

*“and consult together courteously”* (65:6) asks a genuinely hard thing. Two people who have just ended a marriage are told to sit down and negotiate feeding arrangements, duration and payment for an infant. The word *iʾtamirū* implies mutual deliberation, each party putting a view and taking the other's seriously, and *bi-maʿrūf* requires that it be done decently. The Qur'an does not ask them to be reconciled. It asks them to be functional on the one subject where the child's welfare depends on it.

This is where the sūrah's repeated demand for *maʿrūf* finally bites. Retaining honourably and releasing honourably were single acts; consulting courteously is a relationship that must be maintained for up to two years across a permanent separation. Most of the bitterness in the aftermath of divorce is generated exactly here, in arrangements about children, because it is the one arena where each party retains leverage over the other. The instruction is realistic about that, which is why the verse does not stop at the command but goes on to provide for its failure.

**When Agreement Fails, the Child Is Not the Battleground**

*“But if you fail to reach an agreement, then another woman will nurse ˹the child˺ for the father”* (65:6) supplies the fallback. Deadlock is anticipated rather than condemned. The father may be refusing to pay what the mother asks; the mother may be unwilling to nurse at any price. In either case, the verse does not adjudicate who is being unreasonable. It removes the child from the dispute by authorising the father to engage someone else, and the burden stays on him because the child's maintenance was always his.

Two things follow. A mother cannot be compelled to nurse, which protects her against a court or a family ordering her body into service. And a father cannot escape the cost by pointing out that she refused, since hiring a substitute is his obligation, not an option he may decline. Islamic law attached a further consequence: a woman who nurses an infant becomes a milk-relation to it, and the marriage bar that follows is the same as for blood kinship. Arranging a wet-nurse is therefore a decision with lifelong effects on the child's family map, which is one more reason the verse would rather the parents managed to agree.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:7

> Let the man of wealth provide according to his means. As for the one with limited resources, let him provide according to whatever Allah has given him. Allah does not require of any soul beyond what He has given it. After hardship, Allah will bring about ease.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Standard That Moves With the Payer**

The previous verse tied housing to the husband's means; this one generalises the principle into a rule of maintenance. *“Let the man of wealth provide according to his means”* (65:7), and the man whose provision is straitened pays out of what God has actually given him. No figure is named anywhere in the passage, and the omission is deliberate. A fixed sum would be crushing for a labourer and contemptible from a merchant, and it would freeze into law an amount that inflation or famine would render meaningless within a generation.

What the verse fixes instead is a ratio. The obligation is proportional to capacity, which means the burden is felt roughly equally by rich and poor while the amounts differ enormously. Two questions divided the jurists here: whether the sum is set by the woman's needs, by the man's wealth, or by both. Those who argue for both have the better of it, since the verse explicitly names the man's capacity while the surrounding legislation repeatedly invokes *maʿrūf*, what is recognised as decent, which cannot be assessed without reference to what she actually requires. A rich man does not discharge his duty by covering bare necessities, and a poor man is not in breach for failing to match his former wife's expectations.

**Poverty Is Not an Exemption**

The clause about the man of limited resources is easy to read as relief, and it is — but only partly. He is told to spend *“according to whatever Allah has given him”* (65:7), which means he is still told to spend. There is no threshold below which the obligation disappears. A man with almost nothing owes almost nothing, and he owes it. This matters because poverty is the most common excuse offered for non-payment, and it is frequently sincere.

The phrasing quietly reframes his position. His provision is described as what *God* gave him, not what he earned, which cuts in two directions at once. It removes the grievance of a man who feels he is being made to hand over the fruits of his own labour, and it removes the shame of a man who can hand over very little. He is a trustee distributing an allocation rather than a proprietor being taxed. The Qur'an makes the same point about wealth generally, and the sūrah has already promised that the one who fears God is provided for from an unreckoned direction (65:3). A man told to give from a small allocation has just been told who controls the size of allocations.

**No Soul Is Charged Beyond Its Capacity**

*“Allah does not require of any soul beyond what He has given it”* (65:7) states a principle far larger than maintenance payments, and the Qur'an applies it across the whole of religious obligation: *“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford”* (2:286). In this context it functions as a defence against two opposite abuses. It protects the impoverished husband from a demand he cannot meet, and it protects the ruling itself from being dismissed as impractical, since nothing impractical has been asked of anyone.

The specific wording repays attention. The measure of liability is *mā ātāhā* — what God has given the soul — rather than an abstract assessment of what a person of that class ought to manage. Liability is therefore individual and current, not typical and historical. A merchant whose caravan is lost owes according to what he now has, not according to what he had last year. A judge applying this verse must look at the actual man in front of him. That is slower than applying a table of rates, and it is the reason the Qur'an refused to supply the table.

**Hardship Named Honestly Before Relief Is Promised**

*“After hardship, Allah will bring about ease”* (65:7) closes the legal half of the sūrah, and its first achievement is the admission contained in the word *ʿusr*. The Qur'an does not claim that obeying this law is easy. It has just required a man to house a woman who is no longer his wife, maintain her through a pregnancy, pay her for nursing his child, and negotiate courteously with someone he may no longer respect. That is hardship, and naming it is more persuasive than denying it.

The promise that follows is stated twice in another sūrah for emphasis: *“So, surely with hardship comes ease. Surely with ˹that˺ hardship comes ˹more˺ ease”* (94:5–6). Commentators divide the relief between the two worlds, and both readings are defensible from the wording. Some read it as material: God will widen the circumstances of the man who pays what he owes while he is struggling to pay it. Others read it as eschatological: the ease is his reception in the Hereafter, earned by keeping a command that cost him. The sūrah's structure supports both, since it has already promised provision from an unexpected quarter in this life and an immense reward in the next. Either way, the straitened man is told that his condition is a stage rather than a verdict.

**Why the Legal Section Ends on a Promise**

The seven verses of legislation finish not with a penalty clause but with an assurance, and the placement is worth noticing. Every one of these rulings transfers a resource from the stronger party to the weaker one: the house, the maintenance, the nursing fee, the delay before a pronouncement takes effect. Law of that shape is resisted, and it is resisted most effectively not by open defiance but by the quiet insistence that it cannot be afforded.

The sūrah answers that resistance on its own ground. It concedes the cost, scales the obligation to capacity so that nobody is asked for the impossible, and then guarantees relief to those who pay while it hurts. What follows in the next verse turns to the alternative — what has historically happened to communities that decided God's commands were negotiable — so the reassurance here is the last word spoken to the willing before the chapter addresses the unwilling. Those who accept the burden are sent off with a promise; those who reject it are shown a record.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:8

> ˹Imagine˺ how many societies rebelled against the commandments of their Lord and His messengers, so We called each ˹society˺ to a severe account and subjected them to a horrible punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sudden Turn From Household Law to Ruined Cities**

Seven verses of maintenance payments and waiting periods end, and the sūrah abruptly asks the reader to count destroyed towns. The shift looks like a change of subject and is not. Everything legislated so far transferred power away from the party who held it, and laws of that kind are broken quietly rather than denounced openly. A man does not announce that he rejects the Qur'an's housing rule; he finds reasons why his case is different. The sūrah answers that habit by widening the frame until private evasion sits in the same category as the defiance that flattened cities.

The connection is made through the word *amr*, the command. Towns were destroyed for rebelling against *“the commandments of their Lord and His messengers”* (65:8), and the rulings just given were sealed as *“the commandment of Allah, which He has revealed to you”* (65:5). The same noun binds the two halves of the chapter. Nothing in the verse says that a man who evicts his divorced wife will have his town levelled. What it says is that the category of offence is one the reader already knows the history of.

**Rebellion as Arrogant Overstepping**

The verb *ʿatat* is stronger than disobedience. Its root carries insolence, exceeding a bound with a raised chin, and the Qur'an reserves it for defiance that has become a posture rather than a lapse. This distinguishes the communities described here from ordinary sinners. They did not fail to keep a command; they treated the command as beneath them, which is why the sentence names both *“their Lord”* and *“His messengers”* — the vertical relationship and the human envoy who delivered the terms were rejected together.

That pairing is the usual pattern in the Qur'an's accounts of destroyed peoples. The messenger arrives, the leading men find his authority insulting, and the rejection of the message becomes inseparable from contempt for the man carrying it. The relevance to this sūrah is direct, since its own legislation arrived through a messenger and was addressed to him by name in the opening words. A community that will take rulings from God but not from the Prophet ﷺ who transmits them has not understood how the commands reach it.

**Counting Towns Rather Than Naming Them**

*“˹Imagine˺ how many societies”* (65:8) uses the rhetorical *kam*, how many, and the choice not to name a single one is deliberate. Elsewhere the Qur'an gives the dossiers in full — ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, Pharaoh's court — with their prophets, their arguments and their particular ends. Here it gives only a quantity, and the effect is to present the evidence as overwhelming rather than anecdotal. Named cases invite the reader to distinguish himself from them. A count leaves nowhere to stand.

The same formula appears at the head of the account of destroyed peoples elsewhere: *“˹Imagine˺ how many societies We have destroyed! Our torment took them by surprise ˹while sleeping˺ at night or midday”* (7:4), and again over the wreckage of settlements long abandoned: *“Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving them in total ruin. ˹Many are˺ also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces!”* (22:45). These verses assume an audience that travels and sees ruins. The Arabian trade routes ran past dead settlements whose names had been forgotten, and the Qur'an treats them as an argument standing in the landscape.

**A Reckoning Before the Punishment**

The verse specifies two stages, and the order is significant: *“We called each ˹society˺ to a severe account and subjected them to a horrible punishment”* (65:8). Accounting comes first. A *ḥisāb shadīd* is an audit, an itemised confrontation with what was done, and its position before the punishment rules out any picture of arbitrary destruction. Nothing was swept away without the charges being put.

Commentators have read the pair as covering both worlds: the severe accounting as what these communities met in history, and the horrible punishment as what awaits them afterwards. The reading fits the grammar and fits the sūrah, which will state in the next breath that a further punishment has been prepared. It also fits the Qur'an's general refusal to let worldly ruin count as the whole penalty. A town that fell to famine or invasion has not thereby settled its account; the collapse was the reckoning, and the sentence follows. This is the reverse of the assumption that prosperity indicates approval and disaster indicates a closed case.

**Why This Warning Follows Legislation About Women**

The placement of a destruction narrative directly after family law is not unique to this sūrah. The same move follows the inheritance rulings in the fourth sūrah, where shares newly assigned to daughters, widows and mothers are immediately sealed with limits, a warning about transgressing them, and the promise of a humiliating punishment for those who do. Both passages legislate rights for women that cut hard against established practice, and both are followed at once by the language of boundaries and consequences.

The pattern suggests a diagnosis rather than a coincidence. Rules that redistribute power within a household are the rules a community is most likely to erode by custom while professing to accept them in principle — not repealed, simply not applied. The Qur'an's response is to attach the heaviest available sanction to precisely these rulings, so that a man tempted to treat his wife's housing right as a technicality is reading, four lines later, about towns that took the same view of a command and were audited for it. The chapter's next verse will describe what those communities found waiting at the end.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:9

> So they tasted the evil consequences of their doings, and the outcome of their doings was ˹total˺ loss.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Punishment Described as Something Swallowed**

The verse chooses a verb from the kitchen rather than the courtroom: *dhāqat*, they tasted. Nothing is said about a sentence being imposed from outside; what these communities met is presented as something they consumed. Tasting is intimate, unavoidable and personal — a person cannot taste on someone else's behalf, and cannot appeal against a flavour. The metaphor closes the distance between the deed and its result in a way that the language of penalty never quite does.

The Qur'an uses this verb of consequences often enough that the usage is a deliberate policy. It is applied to the punishment of the Fire and to the disasters that overtake communities in this world, and in both settings it removes the suggestion of an arbitrary imposition. A person who tastes something has taken it in. Applied to the towns of the previous verse, the image says that their ruin was not an external blow that happened to land on them but the internal completion of what they had been doing. The *wabāl* they swallowed was the heaviness of their own conduct, a word whose root carries the sense of something indigestible, harmful to the stomach that receives it.

**The Grammar Makes the Deed Its Own Sentence**

Both halves of the verse turn on the same word: they tasted the evil consequence of *their affair*, and the end of *their affair* was loss. The repetition of *amrihā* welds cause to outcome so tightly that no third element is needed between them. What befell these communities is described as the tail end of their own project, not as a separate event introduced by God in response to it.

This is not a denial of divine agency, which the previous verse asserted plainly in the first person — *“We called each ˹society˺ to a severe account”* (65:8). The two verses together give both accounts of the same thing: God acts, and what He brings about is precisely the ripening of what the people chose. The Qur'an states the principle for whole societies in terms of their inner condition: *“Indeed, Allah would never change a people’s state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺”* (13:11). Judgement in this picture is not a foreign body introduced into history. It is the conclusion of a sentence the community began writing itself.

**Regret That Arrives Too Late to Work**

Tasting implies a moment of recognition, and that moment is the cruelty in the image. These communities eventually understood exactly what they had been doing — the consequence made it unmistakable — but the understanding arrived after the only period in which it could have changed anything. Regret is useful while options remain. Once the account has been called in, it becomes simply another part of the suffering.

The Qur'an returns to this timing repeatedly, showing the rejecters of the messengers clear-eyed at last and asking for a return they will not be given. The sūrah's placement of the theme is pointed. Its first half spoke to people in the middle of a marital crisis, where every ruling assumed that the situation was still open: the term is still running, the return is still possible, *“You never know, perhaps Allah will bring about a change ˹of heart˺ later”* (65:1). Here the reader is shown the other end of that road, where nothing is open and the only remaining act is tasting.

**Loss as the Final Accounting Term**

*“the outcome of their doings was ˹total˺ loss”* (65:9) ends with *khusr*, the vocabulary of trade. A loss is what a merchant records when the return is less than the outlay, and the word implies that something real was invested. These communities had worked. They built the lofty palaces and dug the wells the Qur'an elsewhere leaves standing empty; they organised, accumulated and defended what they had. The verdict does not call their effort lazy. It calls it unprofitable.

That is a harder judgement than condemnation, because it engages people on the ground they themselves chose. A community that measures itself by output is told that its output has been audited by the only accountant who matters and found to be a net deficit. The sūrah's second half is arguing, in commercial terms, the same case its first half argued in domestic ones. The pious man who pays maintenance he can barely afford is promised provision from an unreckoned direction and an immense reward; the community that kept everything and conceded nothing is shown its final balance. Both are described as transactions, and only one of them closes in profit.

**Why the Verse Repeats Instead of Advancing**

Read quickly, this verse says nothing the previous one had not already said. Communities defied, were audited, were punished; now they tasted the consequence and ended in loss. The apparent redundancy is the Qur'an's usual method with judgement passages, and it works by changing the camera angle rather than the content. The previous verse gave the view from above — a divine actor calling towns to account. This one gives the view from inside the town, where nothing is seen of the reckoning and everything is felt of the result.

That shift is what makes the passage useful to the audience actually being addressed. A believer in Madina is unlikely to picture himself as a defiant civilisation; the scale is too large and the comparison too flattering to refuse. He can picture eating something that turns out to be poison, which is what the verse describes. The two verses together assemble a complete account of the same event from the outside and the inside, and the chapter then moves on to state that even this is not the end of it.

**A Warning Positioned Where People Still Have Choices**

The value of this verse lies in who is reading it. Those who tasted are finished; their affair has closed in loss and nothing further is required of them. The reader, by contrast, is mid-affair. His own conduct — including the domestic conduct legislated in the first half of this sūrah — is at the stage where it can still be altered, and the record of the destroyed communities functions as advance information about where a certain kind of behaviour terminates.

This is why the Qur'an narrates ruin rather than merely threatening it. A threat asks for fear; a record asks for judgement, and judgement is the faculty the next verse will address when it calls on people of reason. The two halves of the sūrah are joined by that appeal. A man weighing whether to keep an expensive ruling about housing and maintenance is invited to consult the historical record of what happens to those who decide that God's commands are for other people, and then to do the arithmetic himself.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:10

> Allah has ˹also˺ prepared for them a severe punishment. So fear Allah, O people of reason and faith. Allah has indeed revealed to you a Reminder,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Punishment Held in Reserve**

The previous verse closed the worldly file: those communities tasted the consequence and their enterprise ended in loss. This verse reopens it. *“Allah has ˹also˺ prepared for them a severe punishment”* (65:10) uses the perfect tense of preparation, *aʿadda*, meaning the thing is already made ready and waiting. What ruined them in history was therefore not the settlement of the account but an instalment of it.

The verb matters because it rules out a particular consolation. It is natural to assume that a community which has already been destroyed has paid, and that catastrophe in this world discharges what is owed in the next. The Qur'an refuses that equation in both directions — worldly ease is no proof of acceptance, and worldly ruin is no proof of atonement. Preparation language is used elsewhere for what awaits the righteous as well, so the same grammar describes both destinies: rewards and punishments alike exist now, complete, waiting for their recipients rather than being assembled when they arrive.

**An Address to Those Who Can Actually Follow the Argument**

*“So fear Allah, O people of reason and faith”* (65:10) picks out a specific audience by a specific faculty. The phrase *ūlū al-albāb* — literally possessors of the kernel, the inner core of a thing — appears across the Qur'an for those who reach the meaning behind what they observe rather than stopping at the surface. They are the ones who look at the sky and the alternation of night and day and find an argument there: *“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the day and night there are signs for people of reason”* (3:190). They are the ones told that good and evil are not equal even when evil is abundant and impressive (5:100).

Two features of the address deserve attention. First, the summons to fear God is aimed at intelligence, not at nerves. Nothing in the previous verses was an attempt to frighten; they were a record of what happened to communities that treated commands as optional, and the reader who can read a record is the reader being called. Second, the translation's *“and faith”* renders the clause that follows in the Arabic, identifying these people of reason as those who have believed. Reason alone reaches the doorway; the Qur'an consistently pairs the faculty with the commitment, because a person can perceive the pattern of history perfectly and still decline to place himself inside it.

**The Reminder as the Thing That Breaks the Pattern**

*“Allah has indeed revealed to you a Reminder”* (65:10) shifts from the destroyed communities to the one being addressed, and the shift is the most hopeful sentence in the second half of the sūrah. Those towns had messengers and rejected them. This audience has a Reminder sent down to it, in the present, while the term is still running. The word *dhikr* — a reminder, a recollection — assumes that what is being conveyed is not foreign information but something the hearer has reason to know already and has let slip.

Most commentators read the Reminder here as the Qur'an itself, which the Book calls by this name frequently. A minority read it as the Prophet ﷺ, on the grounds that the next verse immediately describes a messenger reciting clear signs, so that the Reminder is the man and the recitation is what he brings. The two readings are not far apart in practice, since the community received the Book only through the messenger, and the verse's structure — a Reminder revealed, then a messenger reciting — can be taken either as two descriptions of one gift or as a gift and its bearer. What neither reading allows is any excuse of ignorance. The information that saved nobody in those ruined towns because they refused it has been delivered here in full.

**Why the Warning Is Addressed to Believers and Not to Deniers**

The command to fear God in this verse is not aimed at the rejecters described three lines earlier. They are spoken about; the believers are spoken to. This is a consistent feature of Qur'anic warning passages and it changes how they should be read. The destroyed communities function as evidence presented to the faithful about the seriousness of commands, rather than as an insult thrown at outsiders.

The reason becomes clear when the sūrah is read as a whole. Its legislation was addressed to believing households, and its rulings about housing, maintenance and the waiting period are ones only a believing community would ever be asked to keep. Nobody outside that community is being asked to count a term honestly when no court can check. The warning therefore lands where the obligation landed. A believer who has just been told that these rulings are God's revealed command, and who has then been shown what happened to peoples who treated commands as advice, is being asked to draw the obvious conclusion about the file sitting on his own household. The next verse will describe what the messenger's recitation is meant to accomplish in those who accept it.

**The Sequence That Runs Through the Verse**

Three moves are made in a single line, and their order is the argument. First a punishment already prepared, then a command to fear God, then the announcement of a Reminder sent down. Fear sits in the middle, framed on one side by what happened to others and on the other by what has been given to this community. It is neither the starting point nor the conclusion.

That placement is a corrective to two familiar distortions. Religion reduced to fear alone produces people who are frightened and not instructed, and fear without a text to act on has nowhere to go; it degenerates into anxiety or superstition. Religion reduced to information alone produces people who know the rulings on the waiting period and ignore them, because nothing is at stake in the knowing. The verse refuses both by binding the emotion to the evidence behind it and the instruction in front of it. A reader who has taken in the ruined towns and holds the Reminder in his hands has both the motive and the means, and the sūrah has spent seven verses specifying exactly what he is supposed to do with them.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:11

> ˹and sent˺ a messenger reciting to you Allah’s revelations, making things clear so that He may bring those who believe and do good out of darkness and into light. And whoever believes in Allah and does good will be admitted by Him into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever. Allah will have indeed granted them an excellent provision.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Revelation Delivered by a Human Voice**

The Reminder named at the end of the previous verse is now given a carrier: *“a messenger reciting to you Allah’s revelations”* (65:11). The Qur'an could have been described as a book that appeared, a text discovered, an inscription found. It is described instead as something recited aloud by a man who lived among the people hearing it. That method has consequences the sūrah has already relied on. The rulings on divorce reached Madina through someone who could be questioned, whose own household had felt the strain of the subject, and whose practice showed the community what the words meant when applied — as when Ibn ʿUmar's mistimed divorce was corrected by the Prophet's instruction to take his wife back and wait for a clean term (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5251).

The participle *yatlū*, reciting, is in the continuous form, describing an activity rather than a single delivery. Revelation in this community arrived in instalments across two decades, responding to events, answering questions, correcting practice. The verse describes the signs as *mubayyināt*, made clear, and clarity of this kind is not merely a property of the wording. A text becomes clear when someone applies it in public to a real case, which is what a reciting messenger does and a discovered document cannot.

**Out of Darkness and Into Light**

The purpose clause states the whole project in a single image: to bring those who believe and do good *“out of darkness and into light”* (65:11). The Arabic keeps darkness plural and light singular, and the asymmetry is consistent throughout the Qur'an. There are many darknesses — ignorance, superstition, inherited custom, self-interest dressed as principle — and one light. The plural describes the situation accurately: people do not get lost in a single uniform way, and a community can be sunk in a darkness that another community has never heard of.

The formula recurs across the Book with the agent varying in an instructive pattern. Sometimes God is the one who brings people out: *“Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light”* (2:257). Sometimes it is done through His blessing and the prayers of angels (33:43). Sometimes the Book is the instrument, addressed to the Prophet ﷺ as *“a Book which We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ so that you may lead people out of darkness and into light”* (14:1). And sometimes the messenger himself is the one leading, as here. The variation is not confusion about who does the work; it is a description of a single act with several agents, in which the extraction is God's, the instrument is revelation, and the hand that takes hold is a human one.

**Belief Never Left Standing Alone**

Four times in this verse the pairing appears: those who believe and do good, whoever believes in God and does good. The Qur'an almost never lets faith stand unaccompanied when it describes who is saved, and the insistence is structural rather than stylistic. Belief that changes nothing about how a person conducts a divorce, pays maintenance or houses a woman during her term is precisely the belief this sūrah has spent seven verses testing.

Reading the chapter as a unit makes the point sharp. The good deeds relevant to its original audience were not abstract acts of piety; they were counting a waiting period honestly, refusing to make a woman's stay unbearable, paying a nursing fee, consulting courteously about an infant with someone no longer loved. That is what *ʿamila ṣāliḥan* looked like in the households of Madina when this passage was recited. The Gardens promised in the next clause are attached to belief expressed in exactly those unglamorous obligations, which is why the promise appears at the end of this chapter rather than somewhere more elevated.

**Permanence as the Distinguishing Feature**

*“to stay there for ever and ever”* (65:11) uses a doubled construction in the Arabic — abiding therein forever — and the emphasis answers a specific anxiety rather than decorating the description. Everything the sūrah has dealt with is temporary and precarious. A marriage ends. A waiting period runs out. A house is provided for three months. Maintenance continues until a delivery. Even the punishments of the earlier verses fell on communities that had themselves been temporary, and their palaces stand empty.

Against that background, permanence is the substantive promise. Rivers running beneath the Gardens carry the same weight in a desert idiom: not scenery, but a supply that does not depend on rainfall, seasons or the goodwill of whoever controls the well. The final clause measures the whole exchange — *“Allah will have indeed granted them an excellent provision”* (65:11) — and the word *rizq* deliberately repeats the vocabulary of the chapter's opening promises, where the man who fears God is provided for from a direction he never reckoned (65:3). The provision offered here is the same word at a different scale. What began as a guarantee that obedience would not bankrupt a household closes as a guarantee about where the household is finally going.

**Provision Named Twice in One Chapter**

The word that closes this verse, *rizq*, has already appeared at its opening, where the man who fears God is promised provision from a quarter he never reckoned on (65:3). Bringing it back at the end is not accidental repetition. The sūrah began by answering a financial fear and ends by answering the same fear at the largest possible scale, and the vocabulary makes the two answers a single argument.

The effect is to place the whole of a believer's economy under one heading. What arrives unexpectedly during a hard month and what awaits in the Gardens are described by the same noun, which quietly rejects any division between material sustenance and spiritual reward. A man who understands that his income and his destination come from the same source, under the same name, is not being asked to trade the first for the second. He is being told that both are allocations from an estate he does not own, and that the terms on which he holds the first are the terms on which he will receive the second.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:12

> Allah is the One Who created seven heavens ˹in layers˺, and likewise for the earth. The ˹divine˺ command descends between them so you may know that Allah is Most Capable of everything and that Allah certainly encompasses all things in ˹His˺ knowledge.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Cosmology Placed at the End of a Divorce Chapter**

A sūrah that opened with waiting periods and nursing fees closes with the architecture of the universe. The move is not a change of subject but an argument about authority. Every ruling in this chapter asked someone to give up leverage — to delay a pronouncement, keep paying for a house, hand over a fee for milk. The reasonable question is who has standing to demand this. The final verse answers by identifying the legislator as the One who built the heavens in layers and the earths beneath them, and whose knowledge leaves nothing outside it.

The same logic explains the closing phrase. A man who evades the housing rule succeeds only if nobody sees; the verse ends by stating that God *“certainly encompasses all things in ˹His˺ knowledge”* (65:12). A man who fears that obedience will ruin him doubts that anything can compensate the loss; the verse states that God is *“Most Capable of everything”* (65:12). Power and knowledge are exactly the two attributes on which compliance with unenforceable domestic law depends, and the chapter names them last so that the legislation is read backwards through them.

**Seven Heavens, and an Earth Like Them**

The creation of seven heavens is stated many times in the Qur'an — *“˹He is the One˺ Who created seven heavens, one above the other”* (67:3), *“Do you not see how Allah created seven heavens, one above the other”* (71:15), *“So He formed the heaven into seven heavens in two Days”* (41:12). This verse alone adds the counterpart: *“and likewise for the earth”* (65:12). Nothing in the Qur'an elsewhere speaks of seven earths, which makes the clause the most discussed sentence in the chapter.

Two readings compete, and both have support. The first takes the parallel literally as seven earths arranged in layers, matching the seven heavens above, and cites the Prophet's warning about land seized unjustly: *“If anyone takes a span of land unjustly, his neck will be encircled with it down seven earths on the Day of Resurrection”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3198; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1610). That report speaks of depth and stacking, which fits a layered reading naturally. The second treats the seven as regions spread across a single earth — the inhabited climes known to the geographers. The first reading has the stronger textual anchor, since the hadith is explicit about descending through seven, while the second explains why no other verse describes the arrangement. What both agree on is the symmetry: whatever is above is matched below, and the same Maker built each.

**Ibn ʿAbbās and the Interpretation He Withheld**

Among the reports on this verse, one stands out for what it refuses to say. Ibn ʿAbbās, the Companion most associated with Qur'anic exegesis, is reported to have said of this verse that were he to tell people its interpretation, they would disbelieve, and their disbelief would be their denial of it. Another report from him describes each land as containing a prophet like the Prophet, an Adam like Adam, a Noah like Noah, an Abraham like Abraham, a Jesus like Jesus.

Whatever is made of the second report, the first is a lesson in exegetical restraint from the man least likely to be accused of ignorance. Some meanings in scripture are held back not because they are secret but because an audience unprepared for them will reject the verse itself rather than revise its assumptions. The instinct behind that reticence is worth transplanting. A commentator's duty is to explain what the verse establishes, mark clearly where the evidence stops, and resist the pressure to supply a confident picture of what has not been disclosed. The verse states that seven heavens and their counterpart exist; it does not describe them, and neither should anyone else.

**The Command Coming Down Through the Layers**

*“The ˹divine˺ command descends between them”* (65:12) uses the continuous form: the descent is ongoing, not a single past event. This is the sentence that stitches the cosmology back to the chapter's legal half. The rulings on divorce that the community had just received came down through this same structure, by the same continuous traffic of command that governs the universe at every level. Nothing in the sūrah's first seven verses was local custom; it belonged to the same order as the layered heavens.

Read alongside the Qur'an's statement that *“We surely sent a messenger to every community”* (16:36), the descending command also carries a universal implication. Revelation is not presented as an anomaly granted to one people at one moment but as a standing feature of how the created order is governed — instruction issued downward, wherever there are creatures to receive it. The verse then names the purpose of the whole arrangement in one clause: *“so you may know”* (65:12). The heavens were built in layers, the earth was made to match, and the command travels between them, all for the sake of knowledge in a creature small enough to be worried about a three-month waiting period.

**Power and Knowledge as the Chapter's Last Word**

The sūrah ends on two attributes, and each answers one half of what it has demanded. Capability over everything answers the man who has calculated that he cannot afford to obey. He was promised a way out, unimagined provision, ease in his affairs and an immense reward; all four promises are worthless unless the One making them can deliver, and the verse closes that gap by stating His power in unrestricted terms.

Encompassing knowledge answers the other half. Family law fails in the dark, and this chapter legislated almost entirely for the dark — for what a man does with a woman's housing when no neighbour is watching, for whether he counted a term he alone could verify, for whether the harassment that drove her out was deliberate. No human system reaches into those rooms. The chapter's final claim is that they are not in fact unobserved, and that the same knowledge which encompasses the layered heavens encompasses a household in Madina. The legislation at the start of the sūrah becomes enforceable only on that assumption, which is why the chapter ends by stating it rather than by repeating the rules.
