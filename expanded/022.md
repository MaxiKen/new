# Sūrah al-Ḥajj (Chapter 22) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sūrah Revealed on Every Terrain of Revelation**

Sūrah al-Ḥajj, "The Pilgrimage," is the twenty-second chapter of the Qur'an, comprising seventy-eight verses. The classical sources preserve no unanimous verdict on its place of revelation. Some authorities, cited by Ibn Kathīr, hold it to be Madinan except for verses 52–55, which are Makkan; others, cited by al-Qurṭubī, hold the reverse — Makkan except for verses 19–22. The majority view is that it is a mixed sūrah, containing both Makkan and Madinan passages. One tradition calls it among the most remarkable (ʿajīb) of sūrahs because it was revealed by night and by day, at home and on journey, in war and in peace, and because it contains abrogating and abrogated verses, as well as both muḥkam (clear) and mutashābih (ambiguous) passages.

The sūrah takes its name from the command to proclaim the ḥajj in verse 27. Yet its scope is far wider than pilgrimage law. It opens with the earthquake of the Hour (vv. 1–2), argues for the Resurrection from embryology and the revival of dead earth (vv. 5–7), contrasts the arrogant disputant with the wavering worshipper on the brink and the firm believer (vv. 3–16), affirms God's judgment over all religious communities (v. 17), describes the cosmic prostration of all creatures (v. 18), juxtaposes the two adversaries and their eternal ends (vv. 19–24), legislates the sanctity of the Sacred Mosque and the Abrahamic pilgrimage (vv. 25–37), grants the first permission for defensive fighting (vv. 39–41), consoles the Prophet with the history of denied messengers (vv. 42–48), clarifies the nature of revelation and Satanic insinuation (vv. 49–57), honors emigration and just retaliation (vv. 58–60), displays God's power in nature and providence (vv. 61–66), regulates religious disagreement (vv. 67–72), strikes the parable of the fly (vv. 73–74), affirms God's choice of messengers (vv. 75–76), and closes with the charter of worship, striving, and Abrahamic identity (vv. 77–78).

**Why This Sūrah Matters for the Modern Reader**

Three threads make al-Ḥajj urgently contemporary. First, it is a sūrah of certainty against doubt: it meets the skeptic who disputes without knowledge, the opportunist who worships on condition, and the materialist who denies resurrection, each with evidence drawn from the self, the womb, and the soil. Second, it is a sūrah of worship with a heart: again and again it insists that flesh and blood do not reach God, but reverence does. Third, it is a sūrah of community under pressure: it was revealed as Muslims moved from persecution to power, and it teaches when to fight, when to forgive, how to protect houses of worship of all faiths, and how to build a just order through prayer, alms, and enjoining right.

What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary. Each verse is treated as a complete, independent discourse, drawing upon Arabic language, classical commentators, authenticated Hadith with sources identified, and the reasoning that connects revelation to human psychology, society, and modern life.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:1

> **O mankind! Reverence your Lord. Truly the quaking of the Hour is a tremendous thing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Summons Addressed to the Entire Species**

The sūrah opens not with "O you who believe" but with *yā ayyuhā al-nās* — "O mankind." Al-Ṭabarī notes that this universal address signals a universal subject: the Hour concerns every human being, believer and disbeliever alike, and no one is exempt from its terror or its reckoning. The command *ittaqū rabbakum* — "revere your Lord" — places *taqwā* at the gateway of the sūrah. *Taqwā*, from the root *w-q-y* meaning to shield or protect, is reverent caution: to place a shield between oneself and God's displeasure by obedience. Ibn Kathīr explains that reverence here means obeying commands and avoiding prohibitions out of awe of the Lord who nurtures (*rabb*, from *r-b-b*, to nurture to maturity). The logic is intimate: He who raised you from dust to maturity deserves your vigilance, and vigilance is your own protection.

**The Earthquake That Dwarfs Every Earthquake**

*Zalzalat al-sāʿah* — "the quaking of the Hour" — derives from *zalzala*, a doubled verb suggesting violent, repeated shaking. The Qur'an elsewhere describes this convulsion in apocalyptic detail:

> *"When the earth is shaken with its [mighty] shaking, and the earth brings forth its burdens."* (Qur'an 99:1–2)

> *"On the Day the quaking quakes, followed by the successor."* (Qur'an 79:6–7)

Ibn ʿAbbās understood *al-sāʿah* (literally "the Hour") as the appointed time of resurrection, so near in God's measure that it is called an hour. Al-Qurṭubī records the disagreement over whether this quaking precedes the world's destruction or occurs upon resurrection itself; he favors the view that it encompasses the great convulsion that ends this world and inaugurates the next. The word *ʿaẓīm* — "tremendous" — is the Qur'an's own scale: what God calls tremendous no human instrument can measure. Modern seismology gives a faint analogy: a magnitude-9 earthquake can shift the earth's axis and unleash tsunamis across oceans. The Hour's quaking will shift not coastlines but the order of existence — mountains as carded wool, seas set ablaze, graves overturned.

**Why Begin Pilgrimage with the End of the World**

Al-Rāzī asks why a sūrah named for ḥajj opens with eschatology. His answer: ḥajj itself is a rehearsal of the Hour. Pilgrims leave wealth and status, wear identical unstitched cloth like shrouds, stand together on the plain of ʿArafah as humanity will stand on the plain of resurrection, and hasten at *nafar* as souls will hasten to judgment. To command *taqwā* before describing either ḥajj or the Hour teaches that both have one key: conscious reverence. The Prophet ﷺ drew the same link when he recited this verse to his Companions on a journey and they wept; he then said:

> *"Do you know what Day that is? That is the Day when Adam will be called... 'Send forth those of your progeny bound for the Fire.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3348; Muslim 222, in the narration of Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, describing nine hundred ninety-nine of every thousand)

The narration continues that the Companions were distressed until the Prophet ﷺ reassured them that with Gog and Magog and the nations, they would be a small honored company among many. The psychology is deliberate: fear that awakens, then hope that steadies.

**Taqwā as Driving Through a Mountain Pass at Night**

Taqwā is often translated as "fear," but the verse's logic shows it is closer to alert driving through a dangerous mountain pass at night. The driver who knows the cliff's drop does not abandon the journey; he grips the wheel, watches the signs, slows at curves, and arrives safely. Heedlessness, not the road, destroys. So the Hour is "tremendous" not to paralyze but to concentrate attention. The modern reader, surrounded by distractions engineered to capture every second, hears a counter-call: revere the Lord who made time itself, before the Hour when time ends. Every prayer, every honest transaction, every restrained glance is a small act of reverence that prepares the traveler for the great quaking.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:2

> **On the day you see it, every nursing woman will forget what she nurses, and every pregnant woman will deliver her burden, and you will see mankind drunk, though drunk they will not be. Rather, the Punishment of God is severe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Images of Unraveling**

This verse paints the Hour through three human collapses, ascending in intimacy. *Tadhhalu kullu murḍiʿatin ʿammā arḍaʿat* — "every nursing woman will forget what she nurses." *Dhuhūl* is not ordinary forgetting but absent-minded abandonment under shock. Al-Qurṭubī observes that the nursing mother is the Qur'an's supreme emblem of attachment; if she abandons her infant at the breast, every lesser bond has already snapped. The second image, *wa taḍaʿu kullu dhāti ḥamlin ḥamlahā* — "every pregnant woman will deliver her burden" — depicts miscarriage induced by terror, the body surrendering what it most protects. The third, *wa tarā al-nāsa sukārā wa mā hum bi-sukārā* — "you will see mankind drunk, though they are not drunk" — extends the shock to all: staggering, dazed, senseless, without wine. The cause is stated with severity: *wa lākinna ʿadhāba Allāhi shadīd* — "rather, the punishment of God is severe."

**What "It" Refers to and When It Occurs**

The pronoun in *yawma tarawnahā* ("the day you see it") refers, most commentators hold, to the *zalzalah* of verse 1 (al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī). Al-Ṭabarī preserves reports placing these horrors at the world's destruction, while others place them on the Day of Resurrection itself. Ibn Kathīr reconciles them: terror has stages — at the blast that ends the world, in the grave, and at the gathering — and this verse captures the consummate terror that makes all previous fears seem small. The Qur'an corroborates the staggering image elsewhere:

> *"The Day they see it, it will be as though they had tarried but an evening or its morning."* (Qur'an 79:46)

> *"And you will see the mountains, which you deem solid, passing as the passing of clouds."* (Qur'an 27:88)

The point is not cartography of the unseen but certainty: what is coming is real, and its signs in this world — earthquakes that orphan thousands in seconds — are rehearsals.

**The Abandoned Infant and the Argument from Nature**

Classical scholars drew a theological inference from the nursing mother. Maternal attachment is the strongest instinct God planted in human nature; only a reality greater than nature can override it. Al-Rāzī argues: if God tells us that the Hour will suspend the deepest instinct, then denial of the Hour is denial of God's power over the instincts He created. The verse also answers the skeptic gently: you already witness lesser "hours" — wars, famines, disasters — in which mothers lose children and minds reel. If secondary causes can do this, how can the Primary Cause not do greater? The Prophet ﷺ described the Day's terror in terms his Companions could feel:

> *"The people will sweat on the Day of Resurrection until their sweat sinks seventy cubits into the earth, and it will rise to their mouths and ears."* (al-Bukhārī 6532; Muslim 2863, from Abū Hurayrah)

**The Sobriety of Terror and the Intoxications of Today**

The image of sober drunkenness carries a psychological mirror. The Qur'an elsewhere calls heedlessness itself a kind of intoxication: men walk through life drunk on wealth, status, and desire, staggering from craving to craving. On that Day the intoxication of delusion will be replaced by the sobriety of terror — they will finally see clearly, but clarity without preparation is itself a punishment. The modern analogy is the moment of sudden catastrophe — the diagnosis, the crash, the market collapse — when habitual confidence evaporates and people move as if drugged. The verse asks the reader to borrow that clarity in advance. Whoever lets reverence sober him today will not stagger then; whoever insists on numbness now will find the awakening severe. Severity (*shiddah*) here is not cruelty but proportion: a tremendous Hour demands tremendous seriousness, and God warns before He punishes.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:3

> **And among mankind are those who dispute concerning God, without knowledge, and follow every defiant satan,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Disputation Without Knowledge**

After the cosmic terror of the Hour, the sūrah turns to its human denier. *Wa min al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bi-ghayri ʿilm* — "among mankind are those who dispute concerning God, without knowledge." *Jidāl*, from *j-d-l*, originally means to twist a rope; disputation twists words against truth. Al-Rāzī stresses the qualifier: the verse does not condemn theological discussion as such — the Qur'an itself argues, reasons, and invites reflection — but disputation *without knowledge*, without evidence, guidance, or revelation. Ibn Kathīr identifies the immediate referent as those who denied resurrection and the Hour just described, mocking it as "fables of the ancients," but the wording is general: anyone who speaks about God, His attributes, His decree, or the Hereafter from conjecture and desire falls under it.

The Qur'an distinguishes blameworthy disputation from praiseworthy dialogue:

> *"And dispute with them in the most beautiful manner."* (Qur'an 16:125)

> *"And they disputed with falsehood, that they might refute the truth thereby."* (Qur'an 40:5)

The first is disciplined inquiry seeking truth; the second is weaponized speech seeking victory. Al-Qurṭubī draws the legal corollary: the verse implicitly praises the scholar who speaks about God with knowledge — scripture, sound reason, and transmitted tradition — and censures the autodidact of desire.

**Following Every Defiant Satan**

*Wa yattabiʿu kulla shayṭānin marīd* — "and follows every defiant satan." *Marīd*, from *m-r-d*, describes one stripped of good, rebellious and defiant — a smooth rock holds nothing, and the defiant one holds no truth. Commentators note that *shayṭān* here includes jinn devils and human devils: every tempter, demagogue, and deceiver who rebels against God. Al-Ṭabarī explains the sequence: empty disputation creates a vacuum, and Satan rushes into vacuums. Whoever abandons knowledge becomes a follower — not a leader — trailing every new doubt, conspiracy, and fashionable denial. The universality of *kulla* ("every") is chilling: such a person has no filter; whatever rebellion is proposed, he joins it.

The Prophet ﷺ warned of this intellectual fragility:

> *"Satan comes to one of you and says, 'Who created this? Who created that?' until he says, 'Who created your Lord?' When it reaches that, let him seek refuge in God and stop."* (al-Bukhārī 3276; Muslim 134, from Abū Hurayrah)

**The Psychology of Arrogant Ignorance**

Why would anyone dispute about the greatest of subjects with the least of preparation? The verse implies three motives. First, pride: to speak about God is to claim stature, and the ignorant disputant borrows God's grandeur for his own ego. Second, evasion: denying the Hour cancels accountability, and canceling accountability licenses appetite. Third, imitation: following "every" Satan is easier than the labor of learning. Modern life supplies vivid examples: the commentator who dismisses scripture without reading it, the influencer who pronounces on the soul, God, and eternity from a thread of slogans, the debater who seeks followers rather than truth. Socially, such disputation corrodes discourse: when speech about ultimate things requires no knowledge, all speech is cheapened, and the community loses the difference between an argument and a jeer.

**Knowledge as Armor and Humility as Method**

The remedy is embedded in the censure. Speak about God only with knowledge, and let silence be worship where knowledge ends. The righteous predecessors modeled this: Imām Mālik, asked about the *how* of God's establishment on the Throne, answered that the establishment is known, the how is unknown, belief in it is obligatory, and questioning it is innovation — knowledge paired with humility. Practically, the verse teaches a threefold discipline: learn before speaking, transmit from trustworthy sources, and examine motives before debating. Whoever does so is guided; whoever disputes to display, to dominate, or to dissolve accountability has already begun following a defiant Satan, and the next verse will show where that road ends.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:4

> **for whom it is decreed that, should anyone take him as a protector, he will cause him to go astray and guide him unto the punishment of the Blaze.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Decreed Consequence, Not an Arbitrary Curse**

*Kutiba ʿalayhi* — "it is decreed upon him" — opens with divine writing. Al-Rāzī explains that *kitābah* here means an unalterable divine judgment, inscribed in the Preserved Tablet and announced in revelation: whoever allies with Satan will be misled by him. The grammar admits two readings, both preserved by the tradition: the decree may fall on the defiant Satan (whoever takes *him* as protector will be misled by *him*), or on the follower (it is decreed for the follower that Satan will mislead him). Both converge on one law: alliance determines destination. The word *walī* ("protector," "ally," "guardian") is pivotal: to take Satan as *walī* is to accept his suggestions as guidance, his promises as true, and his enmity toward God as one's own cause.

**Misguidance as Satan's Only Guidance**

The verse's irony is precise: *yuḍilluhu wa yahdīhi ilā ʿadhābi al-saʿīr* — "he will cause him to go astray and guide him unto the punishment of the Blaze." Satan's *iḍlāl* (leading astray) is his only *hidāyah* (guidance): he guides, but only toward fire. *Al-saʿīr*, from *s-ʿ-r*, denotes a blazing, kindled fire — the Qur'an's name for Hell in its raging aspect. Al-Qurṭubī notes the polyptoton: the same verb "to guide" that describes God's mercy here describes Satan's malice, exposing false guidance as real misguidance. The Qur'an elsewhere records Satan's own confession of his program:

> *"He promises them and fills them with vain hopes, and Satan promises them naught but delusion."* (Qur'an 4:120)

> *"And Satan will say, when the matter is concluded, 'Truly God promised you the promise of truth, and I promised you and failed you. I had no authority over you, save that I called you and you responded to me.'"* (Qur'an 14:22)

**Free Will, Divine Decree, and Human Responsibility**

This verse touches the delicate doctrine of decree (*qadar*) and responsibility. "It is decreed" might suggest compulsion, but the condition *man tawallāhu* — "whoever takes him as protector" — restores agency: the decree describes the consequence of a chosen alliance, not a forced one. Ibn Taymiyyah's principle illuminates the verse: God's universal decree (*qadar kaw-nī*) encompasses Satan's existence and whispering, but God's religious command (*amr sharʿī*) forbids following him; whoever follows does so by will and deserves the decreed outcome. Al-Ṭabarī cites Ibn ʿAbbās: God decreed for Satan that whoever follows him, Satan will mislead him — the follower's act triggers the written consequence, as fire's nature burns whoever grasps it by choice.

**The Alliance Test in Everyday Life**

Practically, the verse offers a diagnostic: examine your *walāyah* (allegiance) by examining your direction. Every person takes protectors — mentors, media, appetites, ideologies. The test is where they lead: toward prayer or away from it, toward honesty or toward fraud, toward modesty or toward shamelessness, toward remembrance or toward oblivion. Satan's alliance rarely announces itself as Satanism; it arrives as "everyone does it," "God is forgiving, so indulge," "religion is backward." The Prophet ﷺ taught refuge as daily armor:

> *"Whoever says, 'There is no god but God alone, without partner; His is the dominion and His is the praise, and He is Powerful over all things,' one hundred times a day... will be protected from Satan that day until evening."* (al-Bukhārī 3293; Muslim 2691, from Abū Hurayrah)

The modern believer surrounded by algorithmic tempters — feeds engineered to inflame envy, lust, and rage — needs this verse's clarity: behind every defiant whisper is a guide to the Blaze, and behind every refusal is a door to the Garden. Choose your protector, and you choose your path.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:5

> **O mankind! If you are in doubt concerning the Resurrection, [remember] We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a blood clot, then from a lump of flesh, formed and unformed, that We may make clear for you. And We cause what We will to remain in the wombs for a term appointed. Then We bring you forth as an infant, then that you may reach maturity. And some are taken in death, and some are consigned to the most abject life, so that after having known they may know nothing. And thou seest the earth desiccated, but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and produces every delightful kind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Doubt to Demonstration**

*Yā ayyuhā al-nās in kuntum fī raybin min al-baʿth* — "O mankind, if you are in doubt concerning the Resurrection." *Rayb* is unsettling doubt, the kind that agitates the heart. The verse does not rebuke the doubter but invites him to evidence: *fa-innā khalaqnākum* — "then [remember] We created you." Al-Rāzī calls this the argument from first creation to second: whoever grants the harder beginning cannot deny the easier return. If God originated you from nothing, reproducing you from remains is no difficulty. The Qur'an states the principle explicitly elsewhere:

> *"And He it is Who originates creation, then brings it back, and that is easier for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27)

**Dust, Drop, Clot, Lump: The Stages of the Self**

The embryological sequence — *turāb* (dust), *nuṭfah* (drop), *ʿalaqah* (blood clot, clinging thing), *muḍghah* (lump of flesh, chewed morsel), *mukhallqah wa ghayr mukhallaqah* (formed and unformed) — compresses human origins into four stations. Commentators explain *turāb* as Adam's creation and, by extension, every human's material basis, since food chains return to soil (al-Rāzī). *Nuṭfah* is the mingled drop of male and female fluids; *ʿalaqah* evokes both congealed blood and a clinging leech, matching the embryo's clinging and nourishment; *muḍghah* evokes a chewed lump, matching somite formation. *Formed and unformed* is understood as complete and miscarried pregnancies (al-Ṭabarī) — God's will detailing each womb: *wa nuqirru fī al-arḥāmi mā nashāʾu ilā ajalin musammā* ("We cause what We will to remain in the wombs for a term appointed").

The Prophet ﷺ detailed these stages and their appointed terms:

> *"Your creation is gathered in your mother's belly for forty days, then he is a blood clot for the same, then a lump of flesh for the same. Then God sends an angel who is commanded with four things: his provision, his lifespan, his deeds, and whether wretched or felicitous."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643, from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd)

Ibn Kathīr cites this Hadith to show that divine knowledge and decree govern the hidden world of the womb as surely as the visible world of birth.

**Infancy, Maturity, and the Return to Helplessness**

*Thumma nukhrijukum ṭiflan thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum* — "Then We bring you forth as an infant, then that you may reach maturity." *Ashudd* denotes full strength, roughly between thirty and forty. Then divergence: *wa minkum man yutawaffā wa minkum man yuraddu ilā ardhali al-ʿumur* — "some are taken in death, and some are consigned to the most abject life." *Ardhal al-ʿumur* ("the most abject life") is extreme old age, when faculties dissolve: *li-kaylā yaʿlama min baʿdi ʿilmin shayʾā* — "so that after having known they may know nothing." The Qur'an parallels this elsewhere:

> *"And whomsoever We give long life, We cause him to regress in creation. Will they not understand?"* (Qur'an 36:68)

Al-Qurṭubī notes the mercy within the warning: the believer's regression is softened by recorded faith, while the denier's knowledge — degrees, skills, memories — proves how easily God gives and withdraws. Psychologically, witnessing aging parents is itself a proof of resurrection: the One who reversed strength to weakness can reverse death to life.

**The Desiccated Earth and the Logic of Revival**

*Wa tarā al-arḍa hāmidatan* — "thou seest the earth desiccated." *Hāmidah* is lifeless, still, ashen. *Fa-idhā anzalnā ʿalayhā al-māʾa ihtazzat wa rabat wa anbatat min kulli zawjin bahīj* — "when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and produces every delightful kind." Three verbs — stirring, swelling, sprouting — mirror conception, gestation, and birth. Al-Rāzī calls this the macrocosm answering the microcosm: the womb and the soil obey one law of revival. Modern observers see it annually: deserts bloom after rain, volcanic ash becomes forest. If water revives dead land before your eyes each spring, why doubt that God's command revives dead bones? The verse thus closes doubt from both sides — from within the self and from beneath the feet — leaving denial with no refuge but heedlessness.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:6

> **That is because God is the Truth, and because He gives life to the dead, and because He is Powerful over all things,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The "Because" That Grounds Everything**

*Dhālika bi-anna Allāha huwa al-Ḥaqq* — "That is because God is the Truth." The demonstrative *dhālika* points back to the entire demonstration of verse 5: creation from dust, gestation, aging, and the earth's revival. All of it is true and coherent *because* its Author is *al-Ḥaqq* — the Truth, the Real, the Necessarily Existent whose being is firm while all else flickers. Al-Qurṭubī explains that *al-Ḥaqq* is among God's greatest Names, signifying the One whose existence is undeniable, whose word is true, whose promise is binding, and whose worship is rightfully due. False gods are *bāṭil* — null, perishing; God alone is *ḥaqq* — abiding, effective.

The Qur'an pairs this Name with exclusive worship:

> *"That is because God is the Truth, and what they call upon apart from Him is falsehood."* (Qur'an 31:30; cf. 22:62)

**He Gives Life to the Dead**

*Wa annahu yuḥyī al-mawtā* — "and because He gives life to the dead." The present-tense verb *yuḥyī* indicates continuous divine action: God is even now giving life — to wombs, to seeds, to hearts deadened by sin that awaken in repentance. Al-Ṭabarī argues that the revival of earth and embryo, witnessed constantly, is the visible limb of the same power that will revive graves. Ibn Kathīr adds that this clause answers the doubter's hidden objection: "bones have decayed; how can they live?" The answer: the One who gives life without precedent gives life without difficulty. The Qur'an elsewhere cites Abraham's request for tranquil certainty:

> *"My Lord, show me how Thou givest life to the dead."* (Qur'an 2:260)

God's response — directing Abraham to train birds, dismember them upon hills, then call them — demonstrates that resurrection is not a riddle for God but instruction for man.

**Powerful Over All Things**

*Wa annahu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr* — "and because He is Powerful over all things." *Qadīr*, from *q-d-r*, denotes perfect, effortless power proportioned to wisdom. Al-Rāzī notes the logical chain: because God is the Real (His nature is perfection), because He acts (He gives life), and because nothing limits Him (He is Powerful over all), therefore resurrection is necessary, possible, and certain. The three clauses correspond to essence, act, and scope — a complete theology in one verse. Nothing is excluded by *kulli shayʾ* ("all things") except what contradicts His perfection, for inability never touches Him and absurdity never describes Him.

**Why This Matters Beyond Eschatology**

The verse quietly transforms daily reliance. If God is *al-Ḥaqq*, then truthfulness participates in divinity: the honest word, the kept promise, the just judgment all reflect the Real. If He gives life to the dead, then no heart is too dead, no habit too entrenched, no community too decayed for revival — despair is theological error. If He is Powerful over all things, then anxiety over provision, illness, or enemies must be weighed against omnipotence. The Prophet ﷺ taught the destitute and the fearful to anchor in these Names:

> *"O Ever-Living, O Self-Subsisting, by Thy mercy I seek help."* (al-Tirmidhī 3524, from Anas; graded fair)

Modern despair — the sense that ruined lives, ruined ecologies, ruined societies cannot be remade — dissolves before *al-Ḥaqq* who revives. The verse that proves resurrection also proves hope.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:7

> **and because the Hour is coming, in which there is no doubt, and because God will resurrect whosoever is in the graves.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Certainty Against the Culture of Doubt**

*Wa anna al-sāʿata ātiyatun lā rayba fīhā* — "and because the Hour is coming, in which there is no doubt." The particle *inna* with the emphatic *lā rayba* ("no doubt") delivers absolute certainty. Al-Qurṭubī observes that *rayb* (doubt) was the doubters' own word in verse 5 — "if you are in doubt" — and here God expels it: the matter you doubt is the matter most certain. The Hour is *ātiyah* — coming, approaching, already on its way. The Qur'an stresses its imminence despite delay:

> *"The Hour has drawn nigh, and the moon is split."* (Qur'an 54:1)

> *"They see it as distant, and We see it as near."* (Qur'an 70:6–7)

Ibn Taymiyyah explains that nearness here is both temporal (every approaching thing is near) and personal (each person's Hour begins at death). The Prophet ﷺ joined two fingers and said:

> *"I and the Hour have been sent like these two."* (al-Bukhārī 4936; Muslim 2950, from Sahl ibn Saʿd)

**Resurrection of Whosoever Is in the Graves**

*Wa anna Allāha yabʿathu man fī al-qubūr* — "and because God will resurrect whosoever is in the graves." *Baʿth*, from *b-ʿ-th*, means to send forth, to raise up — as sleepers are raised, as messengers are sent. Al-Ṭabarī notes the universality of *man* ("whosoever"): kings and slaves, prophets and tyrants, the buried and the drowned, the cremated and the scattered — all graves, literal and metaphorical, will give up their inhabitants. The Qur'an details the summons:

> *"And the Trumpet shall be blown, and behold, from the graves they shall hasten unto their Lord."* (Qur'an 36:51)

Ibn Kathīr cites the Hadith of the questioning in the grave to show that resurrection begins before resurrection: the grave is the first station of the Hereafter, a garden or a pit, anticipating the final raising.

**The Grave as Evidence and Admonition**

Classical scholars drew practical fruit from *man fī al-qubūr*. First, visiting graves softens hearts precisely because the verse is true: those silent mounds contain conscious souls awaiting the Blast. The Prophet ﷺ first forbade grave-visiting, then permitted it with reason:

> *"I had forbidden you from visiting graves, but now visit them, for they remind you of the Hereafter."* (Muslim 977, from Buraydah)

Second, belief in bodily resurrection — not mere spiritual survival — affirms divine justice and human dignity: the hands that stole, the tongues that lied, the knees that bowed will all testify and be recompensed. The Qur'an insists:

> *"Does man suppose We shall not gather his bones? Yea, We are able to restore his very fingertips."* (Qur'an 75:3–4)

Third, certainty about graves cures the modern illusion of permanence. Wealth managers, architects, and influencers build as though graves were for others. The verse returns each reader to the plot of earth awaiting him — two meters of soil where rank dissolves and only deeds accompany. Whoever remembers that "God will resurrect whosoever is in the graves" prepares provisions that graves cannot decay: faith, prayer, charity, and a sound heart.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:8

> **And among mankind are those who dispute concerning God without knowledge, without guidance, and without an illuminating Book,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return of the Disputant — Now Triply Empty**

This verse echoes verse 3 — *wa min al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bi-ghayri ʿilm* — but intensifies it with a threefold negation: *bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa lā hudan wa lā kitābin munīr* — "without knowledge, without guidance, and without an illuminating Book." Al-Rāzī asks why the repetition. His answer: verse 3 described the follower of Satan in belief; verse 8 describes the misleader of others in action, as verse 9 will show ("turning aside to cause others to stray"). Repetition here is escalation, not redundancy. Others hold that verse 3 concerned the denier of resurrection, while verse 8 concerns the arrogant chief who disputes God's unity and the Prophet's message. Both share one disease: speech without foundation.

**Three Lights He Lacks**

The three negations form a hierarchy. *ʿIlm* (knowledge) is sound rational evidence; *hudā* (guidance) is the innate and prophetic direction of the heart; *kitāb munīr* (illuminating Book) is revealed scripture that gives light. Al-Qurṭubī explains: the disputant has neither intellect rightly used, nor heart rightly directed, nor text rightly followed. *Munīr*, from *n-w-r*, means giving light — scripture is not merely a book but a lamp. The Qur'an describes itself and previous revelations in these terms:

> *"There has come unto you from God a light and a clear Book."* (Qur'an 5:15)

> *"And We gave him the Gospel, wherein is guidance and light."* (Qur'an 5:46)

Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that the verse refers to those who argue from whim while abandoning all three sources of truth — reason, fitrah, and revelation — leaving them in triple darkness.

**Disputation as a Social Epidemic**

The singular *man yujādilu* ("those who dispute") describes a type that recurs in every age: the confident amateur in ultimate things. Al-Ṭabarī preserves reports linking the verse to specific Makkan chiefs like Abū Jahl and al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, who disputed the Prophet's call without evidence. But the wording's generality (*min al-nāsi*) extends it to every era's equivalents — today, the polemicist who has never read the Qur'an yet pronounces upon it, the materialist who denies the soul without studying consciousness, the sectarian who declares others misguided without mastering a single science. The Prophet ﷺ warned that such disputation is itself a sign of misguidance:

> *"No people went astray after guidance except that they were given disputation." Then he recited: 'They cite him not to thee save for disputation. Nay, they are a contentious people.'"* (al-Tirmidhī 3253; Ibn Mājah 48, from Abū Umāmah; graded fair)

**The Discipline of Legitimate Speech About God**

The verse implicitly legislates an ethic of religious speech. Whoever wishes to speak about God must possess at least one of the three: sound knowledge (mastery of evidence), guidance (a heart purified by worship and sincerity), or an illuminating Book (scripture rightly understood through qualified scholarship). Ideally all three together. Practically, this means: study before posting, ask scholars before ruling, purify intention before debating, and prefer silence to speculation. Ibn Taymiyyah's lifelong method embodied this: fierce argument, but always from Qur'an, Sunnah, and rigorous reason — never from whim. The modern Muslim, drowning in hot takes, should make verse 8 a filter: "Do I have knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating text for what I am about to say about God? If not, I will learn first."

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:9

> **turning aside to cause others to stray from the way of God. Such shall have disgrace in this world, and We shall make him taste the punishment of the burning on the Day of Resurrection.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Arrogant Turn of the Neck**

*Thāniya ʿiṭfihi* — "turning aside" — literally means bending or folding his side, turning his neck away in pride. Al-Ṭabarī explains the idiom: the arrogant disputant twists his shoulder and averts his face when truth is presented, a bodily theater of contempt. *Li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīli Allāh* — "to cause others to stray from the way of God" — reveals that his pride is not private; it is missionary. He does not merely stray but leads astray, diverting seekers from God's path through mockery, sophistry, and obstruction. The Qur'an elsewhere describes this type:

> *"And when it is said unto him, 'Revere God,' pride takes hold of him in sin."* (Qur'an 2:206)

Ibn Kathīr notes the sequence: empty disputation (v. 8) hardens into arrogant aversion (v. 9a), which hardens into active misguidance (v. 9b). Sin, unrepented, always seeks company.

**Disgrace in This World Before Fire in the Next**

*Lahu fī al-dunyā khizy* — "Such shall have disgrace in this world." *Khizy* is exposure, humiliation, the stripping of false honor. Al-Qurṭubī records that this was fulfilled for the Makkan chiefs at Badr: Abū Jahl, who swaggered in Makkah, was slain and dragged; al-Naḍr, who boasted Persian tales against the Qur'an, was executed. But the promise is general: every misleader's disgrace surfaces — through refutation, through scandal, through the collapse of his movement, or at minimum through the inner disgrace of a conscience that knows it fights truth. The Qur'an repeatedly pairs worldly disgrace with otherworldly fire for such figures:

> *"Disgrace in the life of this world, and on the Day of Resurrection they shall be returned to the most severe punishment."* (Qur'an 2:85)

*Wa nudhīquhu yawma al-qiyāmati ʿadhāba al-ḥarīq* — "We shall make him taste the punishment of the burning." *Taste* (*dhawq*) makes punishment intimate and inescapable: fire is not merely seen but savored in agony. *Al-ḥarīq* is the blazing, consuming fire.

**Why Misguiding Others Multiplies Punishment**

Classical scholars derive from this verse the principle of compounded responsibility: whoever misleads bears his own sin plus the like of his followers' sins, without diminishing theirs. The Prophet ﷺ stated:

> *"Whoever calls to misguidance bears its sin and the sins of whoever follows him, without diminishing their sins in the least."* (Muslim 2674, from Abū Hurayrah)

Conversely, whoever guides to good shares its reward. The logic is social: ideas propagate, and their originators own their harvest. A single mocking book, a viral falsehood about Islam, a curriculum that normalizes immorality — each continues misleading after its author's death, accumulating disgrace. Al-Rāzī warns that *khizy* in this world often takes the form of intellectual exposure: the misleader's arguments, once fashionable, are refuted and abandoned, leaving his name as a cautionary tale.

**Pride, Posture, and the Remedy of Listening**

The verse's bodily image — the twisted neck — invites self-examination. Everyone has felt the impulse to turn aside: to scroll past a reminder, to mock a sincere adviser, to leave the gathering where truth is spoken. The remedy is the prophetic posture of listening: the Prophet ﷺ listened even to enemies, then answered with wisdom. Practically, the believer should watch his own *ʿiṭf* (side): when religious truth arrives, does he face it or twist away? Humility is not weakness but the courage to be corrected. Whoever turns toward God's way, however slowly, is promised honor in both worlds; whoever turns his side to mislead others is promised disgrace in this world and tasting of burning in the next.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:10

> **That is because of what your hands have sent forth, and because God wrongs not His servants.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Punishment as Harvest, Not Arbitrariness**

*Dhālika bi-mā qaddamat yadāk* — "That is because of what your hands have sent forth." The burning of verse 9 is here explained: it is not arbitrary infliction but the return of dispatched deeds. *Qaddamat* (sent forth) evokes sending goods ahead on a journey: every word and act is freight forwarded to the Hereafter, where its owner will meet it. *Yadāk* ("your two hands") is idiomatic for the whole person's agency, since hands execute most deeds. Al-Rāzī notes the singular address (*your* hands) within a passage about a type: on Judgment Day each misleader will be confronted individually — no crowd to hide in, no followers to shield him. The Qur'an repeats this formula almost verbatim elsewhere:

> *"That is because of what your hands sent forth, and because God wrongs not His servants."* (Qur'an 3:182; cf. 8:51)

The repetition across sūrahs shows a universal law: eschatological outcomes track worldly deeds.

**God Wrongs Not His Servants**

*Wa anna Allāha laysa bi-ẓallāmin li-l-ʿabīd* — "and because God wrongs not His servants." *Ẓallām* is an intensive form: not merely "unjust" but utterly, habitually far from injustice — God never wrongs in the least. Al-Ṭabarī explains the emphasis: lest anyone imagine the burning excessive, God declares His justice absolute. Every punishment is deserved, measured, and preceded by warning, evidence, and respite. The Qur'an reinforces this from multiple angles:

> *"And thy Lord wrongs no one."* (Qur'an 18:49)

> *"Truly God wrongs not mankind in aught, but mankind wrong themselves."* (Qur'an 10:44)

Ibn Taymiyyah grounded this in God's Names: He is *al-ʿAdl* (the Just) and *al-Ḥakam* (the Judge); injustice (*ẓulm*) is placing things out of place, and the All-Wise never misplaces. What appears as severity is precision — each soul reaping exactly what it sowed.

**The Book That Records Hands**

Commentators link "what hands sent forth" to the record every soul will read:

> *"And every man's augury have We fastened to his neck, and We shall bring forth for him on the Day of Resurrection a book which he will find spread open: 'Read thy book! Thou art sufficient this day as reckoner against thyself.'"* (Qur'an 17:13–14)

Al-Qurṭubī notes that hands themselves will testify, as will skin, hearing, and sight (41:20–21). The Prophet ﷺ described this with Homely precision:

> *"The servants' deeds are presented... and I was amazed at a man whose book flew to his left, crying, 'Would that I had not been given my book!'"* (paraphrasing Qur'an 69:25; cf. al-Tirmidhī 2427 on the weighing)

Modern technology offers a faint parable: every keystroke is logged, every transaction archived, every camera records. If human systems preserve so much, how shall the All-Knowing lose anything? The believer who internalizes this lives as though recorded — because he is.

**Responsibility as Dignity**

The verse's logic dignifies man: he is not a puppet punished for another's script but an agent whose hands shape his eternity. Psychologically, this cures both despair and blame-shifting. Despair says "I am doomed regardless"; the verse answers "your future is in your hands' sending." Blame-shifting says "society, Satan, fate wronged me"; the verse answers "God wrongs no one — examine what you sent forth." Practically, each evening becomes an audit: what did my hands forward today — charity or cruelty, prayer or neglect, truth or slander? The freight is already en route; repentance can still intercept it before arrival, for God effaces what He wills of evil with good.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:11

> **And among mankind some worship God upon a brink: if good befalls him, he is content thereby, but if a trial befalls him, he is turned over upon his face, losing this world and the Hereafter. That is the manifest loss.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Worship on the Edge of a Cliff**

*Wa min al-nāsi man yaʿbudu Allāha ʿalā ḥarf* — "among mankind some worship God upon a brink." *Ḥarf* is an edge, a margin, a cliff's lip — also the "letter" at a word's edge. Al-Rāzī explains the metaphor: just as one standing on a cliff's edge is unsteady, ready to fall either way, so the conditional believer stands on faith's margin, ready to topple into disbelief at the first tremor. He has not entered faith's interior; he camps at its border to see which way the wind blows. Ibn ʿAbbās, as transmitted by al-Bukhārī, gave the historical occasion: Bedouins would come to Madinah and embrace Islam; if their wives bore sons and their mares foaled, they said "this religion is good"; if hardship struck, they demanded to annul their pledge, saying "this religion is evil." The verse was revealed about such conditional converts (al-Bukhārī 4742).

**Contentment with Gain, Apostasy at Loss**

*Fa-in aṣābahu khayrun iṭmaʾanna bihi* — "if good befalls him, he is content thereby." His tranquility (*iṭmiʾnān*) is pegged not to God but to gain: health, wealth, sons, victory. *Wa in aṣābathu fitnatun inqalaba ʿalā wajhihi* — "but if a trial befalls him, he is turned over upon his face." *Fitnah* here is testing affliction — illness, poverty, defeat, bereavement. *Inqalaba ʿalā wajhihi* ("turned over upon his face") depicts total reversal: he falls forward off the brink, face-first, returning to disbelief or hypocrisy. Al-Qurṭubī notes the symmetry: good was a test he misread as validation; trial was a test he misread as refutation. Both were examinations; he failed both by worshipping outcomes instead of God.

The Prophet ﷺ described the opposite — the believer who profits from both states:

> *"Wondrous is the affair of the believer: all of it is good for him, and that is for none but the believer. If prosperity befalls him he gives thanks, and it is good for him; and if adversity befalls him he is patient, and it is good for him."* (Muslim 2999, from Ṣuhayb)

**Losing Both Worlds**

*Khasira al-dunyā wa al-ākhirah* — "losing this world and the Hereafter." Al-Ṭabarī explains the double loss: in this world, his apostasy forfeits the honor, tranquility, and divine aid of faith, while gaining him no lasting worldly security either — for trials afflict disbelievers too; in the Hereafter, he forfeits Paradise and earns punishment. *Dhālika huwa al-khusrānu al-mubīn* — "That is the manifest loss": *mubīn* means evident, needing no proof. The Qur'an elsewhere contrasts this with the profitable trade of steadfast faith:

> *"Shall I guide you to a trade that shall deliver you from a painful punishment? Believe in God and His Messenger and strive..."* (Qur'an 61:10–11)

Ibn Kathīr cites the report that when a man complained his Islam had brought him no worldly good, the Prophet ﷺ replied that Islam refines like fire purifying iron, gold, and silver — trials are not refutation but refinement.

**Conditional Faith in an Age of Transaction**

The brink-worshipper is perennially modern. His theology is transactional: "I pray, so I deserve promotion; I wear hijab, so I deserve marriage; I donate, so I deserve health." When the transaction fails, faith collapses — or curdles into resentment toward God. Socially, this produces congregations of fair-weather believers who crowd mosques in prosperity and vanish in hardship, and online apostasies announced after personal tragedies. The verse prescribes interior relocation: move from the *ḥarf* (edge) to the *qalb* (heart) of worship. Worship God because He is worthy — Lord in bounty and in trial — and let good produce gratitude and trial produce patience. The one who worships the Giver rather than the gift can never be bankrupted, for the Giver remains when gifts depart.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:12

> **He calls, apart from God, upon that which neither harms him nor benefits him. That is extreme error.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Futility of False Invocation**

*Yadʿū min dūni Allāhi mā lā yaḍurruhu wa mā lā yanfaʿuh* — "He calls, apart from God, upon that which neither harms him nor benefits him." *Duʿāʾ* (calling) here encompasses worship, supplication, and reliance. The object — idols, false gods, and by extension every powerless intermediary — is defined by double impotence: it cannot harm (*ḍarr*) nor benefit (*nafʿ*). Al-Rāzī notes the order: harm is mentioned first because the brink-worshipper of verse 11 fears trial most; the verse assures him his idols cannot shield him from it. Ibn Kathīr cross-references the Qur'an's constant refrain about idols:

> *"And they worship apart from God that which neither harms them nor benefits them."* (Qur'an 10:18; cf. 6:71; 20:89; 25:55)

The repetition is deliberate: the Qur'an's central polemic against idolatry is not aesthetic but functional — false gods *do nothing*.

**Extreme Error**

*Dhālika huwa al-ḍalālu al-baʿīd* — "That is extreme error." *Baʿīd* (far, distant) depicts straying as distance from the road: the idolater has not merely stumbled but wandered miles into trackless desert. Al-Qurṭubī explains that error (*ḍalāl*) admits degrees: minor error is remediable deviation, but *al-ḍalālu al-baʿīd* is fundamental misdirection — seeking from the helpless what only the Omnipotent gives. The Qur'an uses the same phrase for those who prefer this life to the next and bar others from God's way (14:3), linking idolatry with misguidance of others.

**Why Powerless Gods Attract the Brink-Worshipper**

The sequence from verse 11 is psychologically acute. The conditional believer wants a god who guarantees gain and prevents trial — a cosmic insurer. When the True God tests him, he turns to alternatives that promise control: idols he can flatter, priests he can pay, horoscopes he can consult, ideologies he can master. Al-Rāzī observes the irony: he abandons the All-Powerful because He tests, for the powerless who cannot even test. False gods attract precisely because they demand nothing and threaten nothing — they are projections of appetite, not objects of awe. The Prophet ﷺ captured this in his supplication:

> *"O God, I seek refuge in Thee from knowledge that does not benefit, a heart that is not humble, a soul that is never sated, and a supplication that is not heard."* (Muslim 2722, from Zayd ibn Arqam)

**Modern Idols That Neither Harm Nor Benefit**

The verse extends beyond stone statues. Modernity's idols — celebrity approval, market algorithms, political saviors, self-help gurus, AI oracles — equally "neither harm nor benefit" in ultimate terms: they cannot forgive sin, avert decree, or grant resurrection. Yet millions "call upon" them — shaping lives around their approval, sacrificing prayer, honesty, and family for their favor. The test of verse 12 is functional: "Can what I rely upon independently benefit or harm me?" Only God passes. Practically, the believer audits his *duʿāʾ*: whom does he beg in crisis, whom does he thank in bounty, whom does he fear in decision? Whoever reserves calling for God has left extreme error; whoever distributes it among the helpless remains far astray, however sophisticated his idols appear.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:13

> **He calls upon one whose harm is likelier than his benefit. What an evil ally, and what an evil friend!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Useless to Actively Harmful**

Verse 12 called idols powerless; verse 13 calls them pernicious. *Yadʿū la-man ḍarruhu aqrabu min nafʿih* — "He calls upon one whose harm is likelier than his benefit." Al-Rāzī reconciles the apparent tension: idols are powerless in themselves (v. 12), but worshipping them is positively harmful (v. 13), because idolatry corrupts the soul, incurs divine wrath, and severs the worshipper from true aid. The *lām* of emphasis (*la-man*) underscores certainty: this is no gamble but a losing wager — harm is *aqrab* (nearer, likelier) than benefit. Some commentators understand the verse as referring to human tyrants and worldly patrons whose alliance brings more danger than advantage; others restrict it to idols whose worship itself is the harm. Both readings converge: false reliance costs more than it pays.

**The Worst Alliance and the Worst Companionship**

*La-biʾsa al-mawlā wa la-biʾsa al-ʿashīr* — "What an evil ally (*mawlā*), and what an evil friend (*ʿashīr*)!" *Mawlā* denotes a protector, patron, or master — one expected to defend; *ʿashīr* denotes an intimate associate, a companion of close company (*ʿishrah*). Al-Ṭabarī explains the pairing: the false god fails both as defender in crisis and as companion in life. The exclamation *biʾsa* ("how evil!") is the Qur'an's verdict of contempt, elsewhere directed at Hell as a resting place (18:29) and at the price for which souls are sold (2:90). The Qur'an elsewhere contrasts this with the true *Mawlā*:

> *"God is the Protector of those who believe; He brings them forth from darkness into light. And those who disbelieve — their protectors are false gods."* (Qur'an 2:257)

**The Arithmetic of False Reliance**

The verse invites a cost-benefit analysis the idolater never performs. Benefits of idolatry: fleeting psychological comfort, social conformity, the illusion of control. Harms: wasted worship, hardened heart, divine anger, eternal loss. The arithmetic is so lopsided that the Qur'an calls the idolater's choice not merely wrong but unintelligent. Al-Qurṭubī extends the reasoning to worldly alliances: whoever allies with tyrants, flatters the corrupt for advantage, or obeys creatures in disobedience to the Creator will find, as the verse says, harm nearer than benefit — betrayal in this world, disavowal in the next:

> *"When those who were followed disown those who followed them, and they see the punishment..."* (Qur'an 2:166)

The Prophet ﷺ warned against mercenary companionship:

> *"A man follows the religion of his close friend, so let each of you consider whom he befriends."* (Abū Dāwūd 4833; al-Tirmidhī 2378, from Abū Hurayrah; graded fair)

**Choosing Protectors and Friends Today**

Practically, verse 13 is a filter for every *mawlā* and *ʿashīr* in modern life: the political patron demanding silence about injustice, the business partner demanding fraud, the friend demanding compromise of modesty or prayer, the ideology demanding hatred. Ask: "Is his harm likelier than his benefit — to my faith, my family, my Hereafter?" If yes, he is *biʾsa al-mawlā*. Conversely, the believer cultivates the best *mawlā* — God — through reliance (*tawakkul*), and the best *ʿashīr* — the righteous — through companionship. The one who chooses his allies by this verse's arithmetic will find, on the Day when false friends disown one another, that his Protector never abandons and his friends intercede.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:14

> **Truly God will cause those who believe and perform righteous deeds to enter Gardens with rivers running below. Truly God does whatsoever He desires.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counter-Destination of the Steadfast**

After the brink-worshipper's double loss (v. 11) and the idolater's evil alliance (vv. 12–13), the sūrah presents the opposite company: *inna Allāha yudkhilu alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — "Truly God will cause those who believe and perform righteous deeds to enter Gardens with rivers running below." The pairing of *īmān* (belief) with *ʿamal ṣāliḥ* (righteous deeds) is the Qur'an's constant formula — faith interior, deeds exterior, each validating the other. Al-Rāzī notes the contrast with verse 11's conditional worship: the steadfast believe in bounty and trial alike, and act righteously in both, so their reward is unconditional. *Jannāt* (Gardens) in the plural suggests multiplicity of delights; *rivers running below* evokes perpetual freshness, shade, and abundance — the desert Arab's supreme image of bliss, universalized for all.

The Qur'an details these Gardens elsewhere:

> *"Gardens of Eden, with rivers running below, abiding therein forever."* (Qur'an 98:8)

> *"In them are rivers of water unpolluted, rivers of milk whose taste never changes, rivers of wine delicious to drinkers, and rivers of purified honey."* (Qur'an 47:15)

**God Does Whatsoever He Desires**

*Inna Allāha yafʿalu mā yurīd* — "Truly God does whatsoever He desires." Al-Ṭabarī explains this as the seal of divine freedom: admission to Paradise is God's act, God's gift, God's will — not compelled by creatures, not constrained by their calculations. The brink-worshipper presumed to dictate terms to God ("give me good or I leave"); this clause restores proper order: God disposes as He wills, and His will is wisdom and mercy. Ibn Kathīr links the phrase to God's power to honor the despised believers and abase the arrogant chiefs — the afflicted Bilāl in Paradise, the mighty Abū Jahl in fire — demonstrating that divine desire, not worldly status, determines ends.

**Faith and Deeds as the Two Wings**

Classical scholars derive from the verse's pairing the doctrine that salvation requires both conviction and conduct. Faith without deeds is the brink-worshipper's lip-service; deeds without faith are the idolater's wasted labor. The Prophet ﷺ illustrated their union:

> *"Say, 'I believe in God,' then be steadfast."* (Muslim 38, from Sufyān ibn ʿAbdullāh al-Thaqafī)

> *"The most beloved deeds to God are the most consistent, even if small."* (al-Bukhārī 6464; Muslim 783, from ʿĀʾishah)

Al-Qurṭubī notes that *yudkhilu* ("He will cause to enter") attributes entry to God, not to deeds alone — deeds are the path and the plea, but entry is mercy. This balances hope and humility: strive as though salvation depends on deeds, and trust as though it depends on mercy — for both are true.

**Rivers Below and Desires Above**

Psychologically, the verse heals the transactional wound of verse 11. The brink-worshipper wanted rivers in this world or nothing; God promises rivers in the next for steadfastness in this. The delay is not denial but purification: worldly rivers stagnate and are fought over, while Paradisal rivers flow eternally without envy. Modern believers facing unanswered prayers, prolonged illness, or righteous poverty should hear *yafʿalu mā yurīd* as comfort: God's will is not randomness but perfect wisdom — He withholds the lesser river to grant the greater, and His desire for the steadfast is Gardens beneath which rivers flow. The task is to keep believing and keep doing good until the Gardener admits His gardeners.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:15

> **Whosoever thinks that God will not help him in this world and the Hereafter, let him stretch out a rope to Heaven. Then let him sever it and see if his scheming removes that which enrages.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Challenge to the Despairing and the Hostile**

*Man kāna yaẓunnu an lan yanṣurahu Allāhu fī al-dunyā wa al-ākhirah* — "Whosoever thinks that God will not help him in this world and the Hereafter." The pronoun *him* is debated. The majority, including al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr, hold it refers to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ: whoever imagines God will abandon His Messenger, denying the promised victory, is challenged to act. Others extend it to any believer despaired of by enemies, or to the brink-worshipper enraged that Islam advances despite his apostasy. Reports mention the tribes of Asad and Ghaṭafān, who doubted the Prophet's triumph, and believers impatient for victory. The wording's generality embraces all: whoever doubts divine help for God's cause is invited to test his power against God's.

**The Rope to Heaven**

*Fal-yamdud bi-sababin ilā al-samāʾi thumma li-yaqṭaʿ* — "let him stretch out a rope to Heaven. Then let him sever it." *Sabab* means rope, cord, or means of access; *al-samāʾ* means heaven, sky, or ceiling. Al-Qurṭubī preserves three classical readings. First, the hanging interpretation: let the enraged one tie a rope to his ceiling and hang himself, then see if suicide removes his rage — the implied answer being no. Second, the severing-help interpretation: let him ascend by ropes to heaven and try to cut off God's revelation and aid to the Prophet, then see if his scheme succeeds. Third, the earthly-scheme interpretation: let him exhaust every stratagem, then observe its futility. Al-Ṭabarī favors the first as most literal, while al-Rāzī shows all three share one logic: human scheming cannot sever divine help.

The Qur'an elsewhere mocks such celestial ambitions:

> *"Or have they a ladder by which they listen? Then let their listener bring clear authority."* (Qur'an 52:38)

**Rage That Consumes Its Bearer**

*Fal-yanẓur hal yudhhibanna kayduhu mā yaghīẓ* — "and see if his scheming removes that which enrages." *Kayd* is scheming, plotting; *ghayẓ* is choking rage. The verse diagnoses the doubter's emotion: not intellectual doubt but furious envy — rage that God helps whom he hates. Al-Rāzī notes the medical precision: rage harms its bearer, not its object. The Prophet's victory proceeded regardless of their fury; their plots recoiled upon themselves, as at Badr, Uḥud's aftermath, and the Conquest. The Qur'an promises this pattern:

> *"They plot, and God plots, and God is the best of plotters."* (Qur'an 8:30)

The Prophet ﷺ taught the enraged a prophetic alternative to hanging or plotting:

> *"Anger is from Satan... and when one of you becomes angry, let him perform ablution."* (Abū Dāwūd 4784, from ʿAṭiyyah al-Saʿdī)

**Despair, Envy, and Trust in Divine Help**

The verse speaks to two modern hearts. The despairing believer, watching falsehood advance and truth retreat, whispers "God will not help." To him the verse says: your despair changes nothing in heaven; only steadfastness invites help. The hostile envier, enraged at Islam's persistence or a righteous person's success, schemes to sever it. To him the verse says: exhaust your ropes and knives — divine help is unseverable. Psychologically, both need *tawakkul* (reliance): the believer by trusting God's timing, the envier by repenting before rage becomes self-destruction. History confirms the verse: every generation's "rope to heaven" — propaganda, persecution, mockery — has snapped, while God's help to His cause continues. Let the schemer look: has his scheming ever removed what enrages him, or only exposed him?

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:16

> **Thus do We send it down as clear signs, and thus does God guide whomsoever He desires.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Revelation as Clear Signs**

*Wa kadhālika anzalnāhu āyātin bayyināt* — "Thus do We send it down as clear signs." *Kadhālika* ("thus") links this Qur'an to the patterns just described: just as God distinguished the steadfast from the wavering and promised help to His cause, so He sends down clarity. *Āyāt bayyināt* — "clear signs" — denotes verses luminous in meaning, decisive in proof, evident to the sincere. Al-Rāzī notes the plural of majesty (*anzalnāhu*, "We sent it down") emphasizes divine authorship against those who called the Qur'an fables. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms this clarity:

> *"We have sent down unto thee clear signs, and none disbelieve in them save the iniquitous."* (Qur'an 2:99)

> *"A Book whose signs are made firm, then elaborated, from One All-Wise, All-Aware."* (Qur'an 11:1)

Ibn Kathīr explains that clarity does not mean every verse is simple — some are *mutashābih* requiring scholarship — but that the Qur'an's core message (God's unity, the Hour, resurrection, accountability) is unmistakable to whoever approaches without the twisted neck of verse 9.

**Guidance Follows Desire — Whose?**

*Wa anna Allāha yahdī man yurīd* — "and thus does God guide whomsoever He desires." The clause pairs divine sending with divine guiding: revelation is offered to all, but guidance penetrates whom God wills. Al-Ṭabarī stresses that God's will here is not arbitrary favoritism but wise response: He guides whoever turns toward guidance and abandons whoever turns away. The Qur'an states both sides:

> *"God guides unto Himself whomsoever turns to Him."* (Qur'an 13:27)

> *"And God guides not the wrongdoing people."* (Qur'an 2:258)

Ibn Taymiyyah distinguished two guidances: *hidāyat al-bayān* (guidance of exposition, offered universally through messengers) and *hidāyat al-tawfīq* (guidance of success, granted to the receptive). Verse 16 encompasses both: the Qur'an expounds clearly to all, and God grants success to whoever desires truth. The brink-worshipper and the disputant received exposition but forfeited success by their insincerity.

**Why the Same Verses Guide and Misguide**

Classical scholars observed that identical verses harden some hearts and soften others — the Qur'an itself affirms:

> *"And We send down of the Qur'an that which is a healing and a mercy for the believers, and it increases the wrongdoers in naught but loss."* (Qur'an 17:82)

The difference lies not in the light but in the eye. Al-Qurṭubī compares the Qur'an to rain: it nourishes fertile soil and floods barren rock. The Prophet ﷺ illustrated receptivity:

> *"The parable of the guidance and knowledge with which God sent me is like rain falling on land: fertile land absorbs and sprouts; barren land holds water for others; and rocky land absorbs nothing."* (al-Bukhārī 79; Muslim 2282, from Abū Mūsā)

**Approaching Clear Signs with a Clear Heart**

Practically, verse 16 teaches the etiquette of reception. Before opening the Qur'an, the believer should purify intention ("guide me, not my argument"), seek refuge from Satan, and pray for *tawfīq*. During recitation, he should reflect (*tadabbur*), not merely decode. After recitation, he should act — for guidance increases with obedience:

> *"And those who are guided, He increases them in guidance and gives them their reverence."* (Qur'an 47:17)

Modern readers drowning in information need this verse's distinction: clarity of text avails nothing without sincerity of heart. Whoever desires truth, God will guide him through clear signs; whoever desires pretext will find, in the same signs, only what he brought — loss upon loss.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:17

> **As for those who believe, and those who are Jews, the Sabeans, the Christians, the Magians, and the idolaters, indeed God will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection. Truly God is Witness over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Six Communities Before One Judge**

*Inna alladhīna āmanū wa alladhīna hādū wa al-ṣābiʾīna wa al-naṣārā wa al-majūs wa alladhīna ashrakū* — "As for those who believe, and those who are Jews, the Sabeans, the Christians, the Magians, and the idolaters." This is the Qur'an's most comprehensive catalogue of religious communities, unique in including the *Majūs* (Magians/Zoroastrians). Al-Ṭabarī explains each: believers are Muhammad's followers; Jews (*hādū*, "those who repented/returned") are Moses' community; Sabeans (*ṣābiʾīn*) are a monotheistic group between Judaism and Christianity (or star-worshippers, by other reports — see 2:62); Christians (*naṣārā*) are Jesus' followers; Magians are Persia's Zoroastrians, followers of Zoroaster, revering fire and professing dual principles; idolaters (*ashrakū*) are the polytheists. The order, Ibn Kathīr notes, is not ranking but comprehensiveness: every claim to truth is summoned.

*Inna Allāha yafṣilu baynahum yawma al-qiyāmah* — "indeed God will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection." *Faṣl* is decisive separation — distinguishing truth from falsehood, requiting each accordingly. Judgment is deferred, not denied: God does not compel uniformity in this world but will disclose it in the next.

**Why Judgment Is Deferred**

The verse answers a perennial question: if truth is one, why does God permit religious plurality? The Qur'an's answer is consistent — this world is an abode of testing, the next of disclosure:

> *"And had thy Lord willed, He would have made mankind one community, but they continue to differ."* (Qur'an 11:118)

> *"For each among you We have appointed a law and a way. And had God willed, He would have made you one community, but that He might try you in what He has given you. So vie in good deeds."* (Qur'an 5:48)

Al-Rāzī derives the ethic of coexistence: since God reserved final judgment for Himself, believers must not usurp it through compulsion. Invitation (*daʿwah*) is commanded; coercion is forbidden:

> *"There is no compulsion in religion."* (Qur'an 2:256)

The Prophet ﷺ embodied this, guaranteeing the Christians of Najrān their churches and the Jews of Madinah their law in the Charter, while judging finally only by revelation.

**God Is Witness Over All Things**

*Inna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd* — "Truly God is Witness over all things." *Shahīd* denotes present, observing, testifying knowledge — nothing escapes. Al-Qurṭubī notes this as both comfort and warning: comfort for oppressed believers whose sincerity God witnesses, warning for all communities that claims without deeds are witnessed too. The Magians' inclusion is significant: classical jurists like al-Shāfiʿī debated whether they rank as People of the Book; the Prophet ﷺ accepted jizyah from them:

> *"Treat them as you treat the People of the Book."* (Mālik, Muwaṭṭaʾ 617, from ʿUmar's practice regarding the Magians of Hajar)

**Pluralism of Judgment, Not of Truth**

Modern readers sometimes misread this verse as relativism — all paths equally valid. Classical scholars unanimously rejected this: the verse promises *judgment* between communities, which presupposes real difference between truth and falsehood. Islam's finality remains:

> *"Whosoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it shall not be accepted from him."* (Qur'an 3:85)

Yet judgment's deferral mandates civility now: protect houses of worship (as v. 40 will command), debate beautifully, cooperate in good, and leave final verdicts to the Witness. Practically, the believer holds two truths together: confident conviction that Islam is God's final message, and humble restraint that judgment belongs to God. He invites without insulting, disagrees without dehumanizing, and trusts that the Day of Separation will vindicate truth more eloquently than any polemic.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:18

> **Hast thou not considered that unto God prostrates whosoever is in the heavens and whosoever is on the earth, the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, and the beasts, and many among mankind? But for many the punishment has come due. Whomsoever God disgraces, none can ennoble. Truly God does whatsoever He will.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cosmic Prostration**

*A-lam tara anna Allāha yasjudu lahu man fī al-samāwāti wa man fī al-arḍ* — "Hast thou not considered that unto God prostrates whosoever is in the heavens and whosoever is on the earth." *Raʾā* here means reflective seeing — to consider, to perceive with insight. *Sujūd* (prostration) for non-human creatures is their submission to divine law: angels in worship, celestial bodies in orbit, mountains in stability, trees in growth, beasts in instinct. The verse then enumerates: *al-shamsu wa al-qamaru wa al-nujūmu wa al-jibālu wa al-shajaru wa al-dawābb* — "the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, and the beasts." Al-Rāzī calls this descending order — from highest heaven to crawling earth — proof that nothing is exempt from servitude. The Qur'an elsewhere universalizes this worship:

> *"The seven heavens and the earth and whatsoever is therein glorify Him, and there is naught but that it glorifies His praise, though you understand not their glorification."* (Qur'an 17:44)

> *"And unto God prostrates whosoever is in the heavens and on the earth, willingly or unwillingly, as do their shadows in mornings and evenings."* (Qur'an 13:15)

**Many Among Mankind — and Many Otherwise**

*Wa kathīrun min al-nās* — "and many among mankind" — prostrate willingly in prayer. *Wa kathīrun ḥaqqa ʿalayhi al-ʿadhāb* — "But for many the punishment has come due." *Ḥaqqa* means became deserved, binding, due. Al-Ṭabarī notes the asymmetry: all non-humans submit by nature, but humans divide — some join the cosmic choir, others defect. This division is man's dignity and danger: alone among creatures enumerated, he can refuse. Ibn Kathīr cites the Prophet's ﷺ weeping at this verse's implication — that creatures without choice obey, while creatures with choice rebel.

The Prophet ﷺ described the heavens' own submission:

> *"The heaven groans, and it has a right to groan: there is no space of four fingers in it but that an angel has placed his forehead in prostration to God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2312; Ibn Mājah 4190, from Abū Dharr; graded fair)

**Whomsoever God Disgraces, None Can Ennoble**

*Wa man yuhini Allāhu fa-mā lahu min mukrim* — "Whomsoever God disgraces, none can ennoble." *Ihānah* is abasement, disgrace; *ikrām* is honoring, ennobling. Al-Qurṭubī explains the context: the Makkan chiefs sought honor through idols, wealth, and lineage while refusing prostration to God; the verse declares such honor hollow — true dignity flows only from divine honoring, and divine honoring flows from submission. The Qur'an states the positive:

> *"Whosoever desires honor, then unto God belongs all honor."* (Qur'an 35:10)

*Inna Allāha yafʿalu mā yashāʾ* — "Truly God does whatsoever He will" — seals the verse with absolute freedom: He honors the prostrating slave and abases the refusing chief, exalts whom He wills and humbles whom He wills.

**Joining the Universe's Prayer**

This verse contains a prostration of recitation (*sajdat al-tilāwah*): the believer who reads it prostrates, physically joining the cosmic prostration it describes. Psychologically, the verse cures loneliness: the worshipper at dawn is not solitary but synchronized — while he bows, stars bow in orbit, trees bow in shade, mountains bow in stillness. Socially, it relativizes human pride: the executive who refuses prayer is the sole rebel in a universe of worshippers. Modern science deepens the wonder: galaxies spiral in lawful obedience, atoms hold in precise submission, ecosystems balance in appointed measure — all *sujūd* in the language of law. The intelligent response is to prostrate willingly what all else prostrates necessarily, and to seek honor where honor resides: with God, who does whatsoever He wills.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:19

> **These two adversaries dispute concerning their Lord. As for those who disbelieve, garments of fire shall be cut for them, and boiling liquid shall be poured over their heads,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Adversaries at the Threshold**

*Hādhāni khaṣmāni ikhtaṣamū fī rabbihim* — "These two adversaries dispute concerning their Lord." *Khaṣm* denotes a disputing party, singular or collective. Classical reports offer three identifications. First, the occasion at Badr: three Muslim champions — Ḥamzah, ʿAlī, and ʿUbaydah ibn al-Ḥārith — faced three Makkan warriors — ʿUtbah, Shaybah, and al-Walīd — in single combat before the battle; this verse was revealed about them (al-Bukhārī 4744, from Qays ibn ʿUbād reporting Abū Dharr's oath that the verse concerned those six). Second, believers versus People of the Book disputing nearness to God. Third, believers versus disbelievers generally, or even Paradise versus Hell disputing their inhabitants. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr favor the Badr occasion as the revelation's trigger while affirming the wording's generality: every age stages the same two adversaries — submission versus defiance — disputing about the Lord.

**Garments Cut from Fire**

*Fa-alladhīna kafarū quṭṭiʿat lahum thiyābun min nār* — "As for those who disbelieve, garments of fire shall be cut for them." *Quṭṭiʿat* (cut, tailored) evokes deliberate tailoring: just as garments are measured to bodies in this world, fire will be measured to disbelievers in the next. Al-Rāzī notes the inversion: clothing in this world protects and beautifies; there it burns and exposes. The Qur'an elsewhere describes this wardrobe:

> *"Their garments shall be of pitch, and fire shall cover their faces."* (Qur'an 14:50)

Ibn ʿAbbās explained that *thiyāb min nār* means sheets of fire wrapped around them like clothing. The image answers worldly vanity: those who draped themselves in silk, gold, and pride while draping their hearts in denial will wear what their deeds wove.

**Boiling Liquid Over Heads**

*Yuṣabbu min fawqi ruʾūsihimu al-ḥamīm* — "boiling liquid shall be poured over their heads." *Ṣabb* is pouring from above; *fawq al-ruʾūs* (over heads) emphasizes inescapability — the torment descends from the only direction with no shield. *Ḥamīm* is intensely boiling water. Al-Qurṭubī cites the Hadith detailing its effect:

> *"Boiling liquid will be poured over their heads, penetrating their skulls until it reaches their bellies, melting what is within, then emerging from their feet. Then they will be restored as they were."* (al-Tirmidhī 2582, from Abū Hurayrah; graded fair)

The Qur'an parallels this:

> *"And if they cry for help, they will be helped with water like molten metal, scalding faces."* (Qur'an 18:29)

**Disputation's Destination**

The verse's structure teaches that disputes about the Lord are not academic exercises but eternal wagers. The two adversaries argued in time; their arguments terminate in timeless abodes. The disbelieving party, who disputed without knowledge (vv. 3, 8), twisted away in pride (v. 9), and allied with the powerless (vv. 12–13), now wears the consequence. Psychologically, the verse warns the polemicist: every argument against God is a thread in a garment being tailored. Socially, it steadied the outnumbered believers at Badr: their three champions faced three pagans, but behind them stood God who cuts garments for oppressors. Modern readers should hear both warning and hope: abandon the adversary's path before its wardrobe is fitted, and stand with the party whose garments, as verse 23 will show, are silk.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:20

> **by which their innards and their skin will be melted.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Melting from Within and Without**

*Yuṣharu bihi mā fī buṭūnihim wa al-julūd* — "by which their innards and their skin will be melted." *Ṣahr*, from *ṣ-h-r*, means to melt, to dissolve — as fat melts in heat. The subject is the *ḥamīm* (boiling liquid) of verse 19: poured over heads, it penetrates inward, dissolving *mā fī al-buṭūn* (what is in bellies — intestines, organs, entrails) and outward, dissolving *al-julūd* (skins). Al-Ṭabarī explains the comprehensiveness: neither interior nor exterior escapes; the whole person liquefies. The Qur'an elsewhere describes skins as renewable fuel:

> *"As often as their skins are consumed, We shall replace them with other skins, that they may taste the punishment."* (Qur'an 4:56)

Ibn Kathīr notes the correspondence: bellies that devoured unlawful wealth and skins that displayed vanity are melted together — inner greed and outer pride dissolved by one pouring.

**The Belly and the Skin as Moral Records**

Classical scholars read anatomy morally. The belly (*baṭn*) symbolizes hidden appetite — gluttony, bribery consumed, usury devoured, orphans' wealth swallowed. The Qur'an warns:

> *"Those who consume the wealth of orphans wrongfully consume only fire in their bellies."* (Qur'an 4:10)

The skin (*jild*) symbolizes exposed identity — the face presented to society, the body adorned for display, the limbs that executed sin. That both melt teaches that hidden and manifest sins share one reckoning. Al-Rāzī adds a subtle point: melting reverses creation. God formed man from dust into flesh and skin (v. 5); denial unmakes him, returning solidity to fluidity — an undoing of the favor he denied.

**A Punishment Fitted to Denial**

Why such graphic severity? Commentators answer with proportionality. The disbelievers denied resurrection of bodies; their bodies therefore become the locus of proof — resurrected, melted, renewed, forever testifying that He who formed can reform. They mocked the Prophet's warnings as fables; the warnings materialize with physical precision. The Prophet ﷺ described Hell's depths to awaken, not to entertain:

> *"The least punished of Hell's people wears two sandals of fire that boil his brain."* (al-Bukhārī 6562; Muslim 213, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī in meaning; similar from al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr)

If the least is so, the verse's melting is for the leaders of denial — proportionate to misguidance multiplied across followers.

**Reflecting Without Despairing**

Such verses invite two errors: numbness ("I have heard this before") and despair ("there is no hope for sinners"). Both miss the purpose. The Qur'an describes Hell in visceral detail as mercy — a surgeon's warning before the disease becomes terminal. Whoever reads of melting bellies while his own belly still digests has time: repentance cools what sin heated, charity shades what greed exposed, fasting disciplines the belly before the belly is disciplined. The believer does not read verse 20 as a spectator of others' fate but as a physician of his own appetites — melting his hardness through tears now, that God may firm his softness then.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:21

> **And for them shall be hooked iron rods.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rods of Iron, Hooks of Return**

*Wa lahum maqāmiʿu min ḥadīd* — "And for them shall be hooked iron rods." *Maqāmiʿ*, plural of *miqmaʿah*, denotes a mace or rod of iron, often hooked — a tool for striking and dragging back. *Ḥadīd* (iron) is the Qur'an's emblem of severe strength:

> *"And We sent down iron, wherein is great might and benefits for mankind."* (Qur'an 57:25)

In this world iron serves man — plows, buildings, instruments. In Hell it serves justice — beating the defiant who in life wielded iron against believers. Al-Ṭabarī explains that angels strike them with these rods; Ibn Kathīr cites the Hadith of the grave's hammer as a foretaste:

> *"Then he is struck with a hammer of iron between his ears, and he cries a cry heard by all around him except men and jinn."* (al-Bukhārī 1338, from Anas, on the punishment of the grave)

**Beating Down Every Ascent**

The rods' function is revealed in the next verse: whenever the damned try to escape, they are struck back. Al-Rāzī notes the symbolism: in life they beat down truth and its bearers — mocking preachers, persecuting converts, legislating against prayer — so in death they are beaten down from every rise. The Qur'an elsewhere pairs these beatings with angelic rebuke:

> *"And if thou couldst see when the angels take those who disbelieve, striking their faces and their backs: 'Taste the punishment of the burning!'"* (Qur'an 8:50)

The symmetry is exact: faces that twisted away (v. 9) are struck on faces; backs that turned are struck on backs.

**Iron's Testimony Against Ingratitude**

Classical scholars drew a lesson from *min ḥadīd* specifically. Iron is among God's greatest material gifts — without it no civilization, no surgery, no shelter. The disbelievers enjoyed iron's benefits while denying its Giver, and worse, forged it into swords against His allies. The rods of Hell are thus iron testifying against its abusers: the blessing becomes the beating. Al-Qurṭubī generalizes: every blessing twisted into disobedience becomes a rod — wealth hoarded becomes molten branding (9:35), knowledge concealed becomes bridles of fire, tongues that lied become instruments of shame. Gratitude converts blessings into gardens; ingratitude converts them into iron.

**The Only Escape Is Before the Rods**

Psychologically, verse 21 closes the fantasy of enduring Hell lightly or escaping it cleverly. There is no tunneling out, no bribing the guards, no waiting out the sentence through stoicism — hooked rods drag back every climber. The Qur'an stresses finality:

> *"They will wish to exit the Fire, but they shall not exit from it."* (Qur'an 5:37)

Yet the verse's severity is itself an escape route: it is revealed before death, not after. The believer who fears hooked rods straightens his own crookedness now — returning stolen wealth, abandoning mockery of the righteous, breaking the idols of pride — and finds that repentance dissolves iron. The Prophet ﷺ promised:

> *"Regret is repentance."* (Ibn Mājah 4252; Aḥmad 3568, from Ibn Masʿūd; graded authentic)

Whoever regrets before the rods needs never meet them.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:22

> **Whensoever they desire, in their grief, to leave it, they shall be returned unto it, while [being told], “Taste the punishment of the burning!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cycle of Futile Escape**

*Kullamā arādū an yakhrujū minhā min ghammin uʿīdū fīhā* — "Whensoever they desire, in their grief, to leave it, they shall be returned unto it." *Kullamā* (whensoever) denotes repetition: every escape attempt, without exception, fails. *Ghamm* is choking grief, anguish that constricts the chest — the damned do not merely suffer pain but suffocating despair. *Uʿīdū fīhā* ("they shall be returned unto it") is passive: angels hurl them back with the rods of verse 21. Al-Ṭabarī describes the scene: flames lift them toward the rim, hope flickers, then hooked rods crash and they plunge again. The Qur'an elsewhere confirms this rhythm:

> *"Whenever it subsides, We shall increase for them the blaze."* (Qur'an 17:97)

Ibn Kathīr notes the contrast with Paradise's permanence: bliss is uninterrupted increase, torment is uninterrupted return — eternity mirrors choice.

**"Taste the Punishment of the Burning"**

*Wa dhūqū ʿadhāba al-ḥarīq* — "Taste the punishment of the burning!" The imperative is spoken, commentators hold, by the custodians of Hell (*zabāniyah*), voicing divine command. *Dhūqū* (taste) recalls verse 9's *nudhīquhu* — the arrogant who "tasted" power by misleading others now taste fire. *Al-ḥarīq* (the burning) is total combustion. Al-Rāzī observes the taunt's justice: in life they said to believers "taste our mockery, our boycott, our torture"; in death the sentence is reversed. The Qur'an records similar reversals:

> *"Taste! Truly thou art the mighty, the noble!"* (Qur'an 44:49 — said ironically to the tyrant who claimed might)

**Grief as the Deepest Torment**

Classical scholars distinguish *ghamm* (grief) from physical pain: pain afflicts the body, grief afflicts the soul. The damned grieve over wasted life, rejected warnings, and followers they misled — grief that no analgesic dulls. Al-Qurṭubī cites the Prophet's ﷺ description of Hell's regret:

> *"None enters Paradise but is shown his seat in Hell had he sinned, that he may be more grateful; and none enters Hell but is shown his seat in Paradise had he believed, that he may grieve more."* (al-Bukhārī 6569, from Abū Hurayrah)

Thus *ghamm* is perpetual: every moment recalls the Garden forfeited. Modern psychology glimpses this in chronic remorse — the prisoner replaying the crime, the addict mourning lost years — but earthly grief ends in death or repentance; Hell's grief has neither exit.

**Breaking the Cycle Before It Begins**

The verse's *kullamā... uʿīdū* (whenever... returned) mirrors a worldly cycle: sin, fleeting escape through distraction, return to sin — the addict's relapse, the liar's fresh lie, the oppressor's fresh victim. Hell eternalizes what the soul practiced: attempted escapes without true exit. The exit in this world is repentance (*tawbah*), which alone breaks cycles:

> *"O you who believe! Repent unto God with sincere repentance."* (Qur'an 66:8)

Practically, whoever feels trapped in a sin-cycle should hear verse 22 as both warning and hope: continue, and the cycle becomes endless return; repent, and the cycle becomes a testimony of mercy. The custodians' "Taste!" will be spoken to the unrepentant; the angels' "Peace!" (v. 24's glad tidings) awaits whoever tastes humility now.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:23

> **Truly God will cause those who believe and perform righteous deeds to enter Gardens with rivers running below, adorned therein with bracelets of gold and pearl, and therein their clothes will be of silk.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Believers' Counterpart to Garments of Fire**

*Inna Allāha yudkhilu alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — "Truly God will cause those who believe and perform righteous deeds to enter Gardens with rivers running below." The verse mirrors verse 19's structure exactly — *fa-alladhīna kafarū* (as for those who disbelieve) answered by *inna Allāha yudkhilu alladhīna āmanū* (truly God will admit those who believe) — staging the two adversaries' opposite ends. Al-Rāzī calls this *muqābalah* (contrastive parallelism): fire-garments versus Gardens, boiling over heads versus rivers below feet. The repetition of verse 14's opening here is not redundancy but judicial symmetry: each adversary's verdict is pronounced in full.

**Bracelets of Gold and Pearl**

*Yuḥallawna fīhā min asāwira min dhahabin wa luʾluʾā* — "adorned therein with bracelets of gold and pearl." *Taḥliyah* is adorning with jewelry; *asāwir* (bracelets) were insignia of kingship in ancient Arabia and Persia. Al-Qurṭubī notes the honor: those who shunned gold and silk for God's sake in this world — men forbidden gold vessels and silk garments — are adorned with them eternally. The Prophet ﷺ promised:

> *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him not wear silk or gold."* (Abū Dāwūd 4057, in meaning; the prohibition of gold and silk for men is in al-Bukhārī 5832; Muslim 2069, from ʿUmar and others)

> *"Gold and silk are permitted for the females of my community and forbidden for its males."* (al-Tirmidhī 1720; al-Nasāʾī 5148, from Abū Mūsā)

Paradise reverses the restriction into reward: restraint becomes ornament. *Luʾluʾ* (pearl) adds purity's luster — beauty without ostentation.

**Garments of Silk**

*Wa libāsuhum fīhā ḥarīr* — "and therein their clothes will be of silk." *Ḥarīr* is fine silk — soft, luminous, cool. The Qur'an elsewhere details Paradisal wardrobes:

> *"They shall wear green garments of fine silk and brocade."* (Qur'an 18:31; cf. 44:53; 76:21)

Al-Ṭabarī explains the contrast: disbelievers wear fire that exposes; believers wear silk that honors. Ibn Kathīr adds that silk's softness suits hearts softened by faith — the rough sackcloth of worldly trial becomes smooth silk of eternal ease. The Prophet ﷺ described Paradise's splendor beyond imagining:

> *"God says: I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has conceived."* (al-Bukhārī 4779; Muslim 2824, from Abū Hurayrah)

**Adornment as Theology**

Why does the Qur'an dwell on jewelry and clothing? Commentators answer: because man is embodied, and resurrection honors the body, not merely the soul. Worldly asceticism is not world-denial but world-discipline: the believer restrains adornment within God's limits now to enjoy it without limits then. Psychologically, the verse dignifies beauty: Islam does not abolish the love of finery but redirects it — from competing display to promised gift. Socially, it comforts the poor believer who owns one patched garment: his wardrobe awaits tailoring by God. Practically, every choice of modest dress, every refusal of forbidden gold, every garment given to the naked is a thread in silk being woven — and the Tailor never forgets a stitch.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:24

> **And they shall be guided unto that which is good in speech, and be guided unto the path of the Praised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guidance unto Good Speech**

*Wa hudū ilā al-ṭayyibi min al-qawl* — "And they shall be guided unto that which is good in speech." *Hudū* (they shall be guided/inspired) is passive: God Himself directs their tongues. *Al-ṭayyib min al-qawl* (the good/pure of speech) is explained diversely: the testimony *lā ilāha illa Allāh*, praise *al-ḥamdu li-Allāh*, the Qur'an itself, or the believers' mutual greetings of peace in Paradise. Al-Ṭabarī favors the comprehensive view: all pure speech — truthful, kind, glorifying — is their inspired portion. The Qur'an describes Paradise's soundscape:

> *"They hear therein neither idle talk nor lying."* (Qur'an 78:35; cf. 19:62; 56:25–26)

> *"Their greeting therein shall be 'Peace.'"* (Qur'an 10:10)

Al-Rāzī notes the contrast with Hell's soundscape — groaning, blaming, taunting ("Taste!") — while Paradise resonates with purity. Speech, the faculty most abused in life, becomes most beautified in eternity.

**Guidance unto the Path of the Praised**

*Wa hudū ilā ṣirāṭi al-ḥamīd* — "and be guided unto the path of the Praised." *Al-Ḥamīd* (the Praised, the Praiseworthy) is God's Name — the One praised in every tongue, worthy of praise in every state. His *ṣirāṭ* (path) is the way to Him and to His Garden. Al-Qurṭubī explains the pairing: good speech is the fruit, the Praised's path is the root — whoever walks God's path speaks God's praise. The Qur'an elsewhere identifies this path:

> *"And this is the straight path of thy Lord."* (Qur'an 6:126)

Ibn Kathīr adds that guidance here is consummated guidance: in life they were guided to faith's path through struggle; in death they are guided to its destination without effort — angels receiving them with glad tidings:

> *"Peace be upon you; you have done well; so enter it, to abide therein."* (Qur'an 39:73)

**The Tongue's Two Eternities**

Classical scholars derive from this verse the eschatology of speech. The tongue that uttered testimony, reconciled disputants, taught knowledge, and restrained from backbiting is guided to eternal good speech. The tongue that mocked, lied, slandered, and disputed without knowledge is left to Hell's recriminations. The Prophet ﷺ warned:

> *"A servant may utter a word pleasing to God, not considering it significant, and God raises him by it many degrees; and he may utter a word displeasing to God, not considering it significant, and he plunges thereby into Hell."* (al-Bukhārī 6478, from Abū Hurayrah)

Al-Qurṭubī therefore called guarding the tongue half of faith: prayer disciplines limbs five times daily, but the tongue requires lifelong vigilance.

**Cultivating Paradisal Speech Now**

Practically, verse 24 is a curriculum for the tongue. The believer rehearses Paradise's speech in this world: replacing idle talk with remembrance, complaint with praise, gossip with supplication for others, harshness with gentleness. Each *subḥān Allāh*, each reconciling word, each truthful testimony is a rehearsal guided by God toward the day when good speech becomes nature. Modern believers, whose tongues have multiplied through keyboards and microphones, need the verse urgently: every post, comment, and message is *qawl* (speech) being recorded toward one of two soundscapes. Whoever is guided to the good of speech now will be guided to the path of the Praised then — and will hear, from the Praised Himself, the best of speech: *"Peace — a word from a Merciful Lord."* (36:58)

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:25

> **Truly those who disbelieve and turn from the way of God, and the Sacred Mosque which We have appointed for mankind—equal are those who dwell there and those who come from abroad—whosoever desires to deviate wrongfully therein, We shall cause him to taste a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Barring from God's Way and God's House**

*Inna alladhīna kafarū wa yaṣuddūna ʿan sabīli Allāhi wa al-masjidi al-ḥarām* — "Truly those who disbelieve and turn from the way of God, and the Sacred Mosque." *Ṣadd* (*ṣ-d-d*) means both to turn away oneself and to turn others away — the Makkan chiefs did both: refusing Islam and barring believers from the Kaʿbah. Al-Ṭabarī records the occasion: Quraysh prevented the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions from performing ʿumrah (notably at Ḥudaybiyah), claiming custodianship entitled them to exclude. The verse answers: the Sacred Mosque belongs not to a tribe but to God, appointed for all mankind. The Qur'an elsewhere condemns such barring:

> *"And who is more unjust than he who bars God's mosques, that His Name be mentioned therein, and strives to ruin them?"* (Qur'an 2:114)

**Equal Rights of Resident and Visitor**

*Alladhī jaʿalnāhu li-l-nāsi sawāʾan al-ʿākifu fīhi wa al-bād* — "which We have appointed for mankind — equal are those who dwell there and those who come from abroad." *Al-ʿākif* is the resident devotee; *al-bād* (from *b-d-w*, to appear from the desert) is the Bedouin visitor, the outsider. Al-Qurṭubī explains the legal magnitude: unlike ordinary property, Makkah's sanctuary admits no monopoly — the Qurayshī resident and the distant pilgrim stand equal in access, in prayer-space, even (by some jurists' extension) in lodging during ḥajj. Ibn Kathīr cites ʿUmar's practice: Makkans could not charge rents that excluded pilgrims nor close doors during the season. The principle radiates outward: mosques are God's houses, not private clubs — no ethnicity, class, or lineage may claim privileged pews.

The Prophet ﷺ affirmed Makkah's universality at its conquest:

> *"Makkah is sacred... its game is not to be frightened, its trees not cut, its lost property taken only by one announcing it."* (al-Bukhārī 1831; Muslim 1353, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

**Deviation Within the Sanctuary**

*Wa man yurid fīhi bi-ilḥādin bi-ẓulm* — "whosoever desires to deviate wrongfully therein." *Ilḥād* (*l-ḥ-d*) means deviation, swerving — from which *laḥd* (the side-niche grave) derives, as it deviates from center. Coupled with *ẓulm* (wrongdoing), it encompasses idolatry, violence, oppression, and even (by some reports) prohibited commerce or monopolistic rents within the sanctuary. Al-Ṭabarī notes the striking word *yurid* (desires/intends): mere intention of deviation in the sanctuary incurs liability — unlike elsewhere where intentions unacted are forgiven — because the sanctuary's sanctity magnifies even resolve. *Nudhiqhu min ʿadhābin alīm* — "We shall cause him to taste a painful punishment": *alīm* (painful) pierces body and soul.

**Sanctuary as Trust, Not Privilege**

The verse legislates a theology of sacred space. Sacredness does not confer ownership but imposes stewardship: Quraysh's custodianship (*sidānah*) became condemnation when weaponized for exclusion. Modern parallels abound: mosque boards that exclude youth or converts, regimes that politicize ḥajj quotas, wealthy residents who price pilgrims out of the sanctuary's vicinity. The verse's equality clause judges all such monopolies. Practically, every believer should treat mosques as verse 25 treats the Ḥaram: open doors, equal rows, no reserved places, no barring of sincere worshippers. And every visitor to Makkah should remember *ilḥād*: the sanctuary that multiplies reward multiplies accountability — even an ill intention there is tasted. Reverence for the House means reverence for its Lord and equality for its guests.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:26

> **And [remember] when We assigned for Abraham the place of the House, [saying], “Ascribe no partners unto Me, and purify My House for those who circumambulate, and those who stand, and those who bow and prostrate.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Assigning the Place of the House**

*Wa idh bawwaʾnā li-Ibrāhīma makāna al-bayt* — "And [remember] when We assigned for Abraham the place of the House." *Tabwīʾah* (*b-w-ʾ*) means to settle, to designate a lodging — God Himself showed Abraham where to build. Al-Ṭabarī preserves reports that the Kaʿbah's site, effaced since Adam's time or Noah's Flood, was indicated to Abraham by divine sign — a cloud's shade, a wind uncovering the foundation — so that he built not by guess but by designation. The Qur'an elsewhere records Abraham and Ishmael raising its foundations:

> *"And when Abraham raised the foundations of the House, with Ishmael: 'Our Lord, accept from us; truly Thou art the Hearing, the Knowing.'"* (Qur'an 2:127)

Ibn Kathīr emphasizes the antiquity: the House is *al-bayt al-ʿatīq* (the Ancient House, v. 29), first established for mankind (3:96) — Abraham restored, not invented, its worship. The lesson: true religion is rediscovery of primordial truth, not fabrication of novelty.

**The Charter: No Partners, Pure House**

*An lā tushrik bī shayʾā* — "[saying], Ascribe no partners unto Me." The first covenant of the House is pure monotheism (*tawḥīd*): no idol within, no intermediary beside, no devotion divided. *Wa ṭahhir baytī* — "and purify My House": *taṭhīr* encompasses physical cleansing and spiritual purging — from idols, blood, impurity, and from the filth of polytheism (see v. 30). Al-Qurṭubī notes the possessive *baytī* ("My House"): God honors the Kaʿbah by attribution, as He honors the soul by breathing "My spirit" — attribution conferring sanctity. The Prophet's ﷺ conquest of Makkah enacted this purification literally:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah with 360 idols around the Kaʿbah. He struck them with his staff, reciting: 'Truth has come, and falsehood has vanished.'"* (al-Bukhārī 4287; Muslim 1781, from Ibn Masʿūd; cf. Qur'an 17:81)

**For Those Who Circumambulate, Stand, Bow, Prostrate**

*Li-l-ṭāʾifīna wa al-qāʾimīna wa al-rukkaʿi al-sujūd* — "for those who circumambulate, and those who stand, and those who bow and prostrate." Four worships define the House's purpose: *ṭawāf* (circumambulation, unique to the Kaʿbah), *qiyām* (standing in prayer and devotion), *rukūʿ* (bowing), and *sujūd* (prostration). Al-Rāzī observes the progression from Kaʿbah-specific (circumambulation) to universal (prayer-postures): the House serves both its unique rite and the universal prayer facing it. The verse thus dedicates the sanctuary exclusively to worship — not to commerce, politics, or tourism as ends. The Qur'an pairs this dedication with Abraham's supplication:

> *"My Lord, make this a secure land."* (Qur'an 2:126; 14:35)

**Purification as Perpetual Duty**

The command to Abraham extends to every custodian after him: keep God's houses pure. Physical purity means clean mosques, maintained facilities, dignified prayer-spaces. Spiritual purity means mosques free from showmanship, sectarian hatred, backbiting circles, and worldly haggling — devoted to the four worships. The Prophet ﷺ forbade raising voices, conducting business, or reciting lost-property announcements in mosques (Muslim 568; al-Tirmidhī 1321). Modern mosque communities should audit by verse 26: does our mosque exist for circumambulators of the heart — those who circle toward God — and for standers, bowers, prostraters? Or has it become a social club with a prayer-hall attached? Abraham's assignment endures: purify the House, and the House will purify you.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:27

> **And proclaim the ḥajj among mankind: they shall come to thee on foot and upon all [manner of] lean beast, coming from all deep and distant mountain highways,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham's Call Across the Ages**

*Wa adhdhin fī al-nāsi bi-l-ḥajj* — "And proclaim the ḥajj among mankind." *Adhān* (*a-dh-n*) means to announce, to call out — the same root as the call to prayer. Al-Ṭabarī preserves the beloved report: when commanded, Abraham asked, "O Lord, how shall my voice reach them?" God replied, "Proclaim, and upon Us is the conveyance." Abraham ascended (Mount Abū Qubays, by some reports), called "O mankind, God has prescribed ḥajj for you, so perform it," and God conveyed his voice to every soul — those in loins and wombs — so that whoever responded *labbayk* (here I am) performs ḥajj once for each response. Whether taken literally or as spiritual parable, the report teaches: the caller calls, but God conveys. No preacher reaches hearts by volume; conveyance belongs to God.

**On Foot and Upon Lean Beasts**

*Yaʾtūka rijālan wa ʿalā kulli ḍāmir* — "they shall come to thee on foot and upon all [manner of] lean beast." *Rijāl* here means pedestrians, those on foot (from *rijl*, foot) — not "men" exclusively; women and men walk alike. *Ḍāmir* denotes a lean, slender animal — a camel thinned by long travel. Al-Qurṭubī notes the order: pedestrians first, honoring those who walk — some jurists held walking to ḥajj more meritorious than riding, citing the Prophet's ﷺ companions who walked from distant lands. Ibn Kathīr records that the early generations prized arriving lean and dusty:

> *"Islam began as dust..."* — and the pilgrim's dust is honored: *"No dust of God's path and smoke of Hell gather in a servant's nostrils."* (al-Nasāʾī 3111, from Abū Hurayrah; authentic)

The leanness testifies to sacrifice: comfort is left behind, bodies thinned for God.

**From Every Deep and Distant Highway**

*Yaʾtīna min kulli fajjin ʿamīq* — "coming from all deep and distant mountain highways." *Fajj* is a wide mountain pass, a highway through rugged terrain; *ʿamīq* is deep, distant. The phrase evokes caravans emerging from remote valleys — Yemen, Persia, Andalusia, China — converging on one valley without crops. The Qur'an elsewhere marvels at this gathering:

> *"And thou seest people entering God's religion in multitudes."* (Qur'an 110:2)

Modern ḥajj fulfills the verse spectacularly: jumbo jets replace camels, yet the *fajj ʿamīq* remains — pilgrims from a hundred nations, crossing oceans and continents, converging in identical white. The transportation changed; the convergence did not.

**The Call That Still Sounds**

Abraham's proclamation never ended; every adhān, every ḥajj sermon, every returned pilgrim's tears re-echo it. The verse invites two responses. First, whoever is able must answer: ḥajj is obligatory once in a lifetime:

> *"O mankind, God has prescribed ḥajj for you, so perform it."* (Muslim 1337, from Abū Hurayrah)

Second, whoever calls to good participates in Abraham's office: teach, invite, announce — and trust God for conveyance. The parent calling children to prayer, the friend inviting to the mosque, the scholar proclaiming truth — each echoes *adhdhin*, and God conveys beyond their voice's range. Come on foot if you must, lean if you must, from distant passes if you must — but come, for the Caller is God and the conveyer is God.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:28

> **that they may witness benefits for them and mention the Name of God, during known days, over the four-legged cattle He has provided them. So eat thereof, and feed the wretched poor.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Witnessing Benefits of Both Worlds**

*Li-yashhadū manāfiʿa lahum* — "that they may witness benefits for them." *Manāfiʿ* (benefits) is plural and unrestricted: commentators include worldly benefits (trade, markets, fellowship, learning, livelihood during the season) and otherworldly benefits (forgiveness, reward, spiritual renewal, answered supplication). Al-Ṭabarī cites Ibn ʿAbbās: benefits include the markets of the jāhiliyyah seasons Islam permitted to continue without exploitation, and the far greater benefit of God's pardon. The Qur'an permits worldly seeking during ḥajj within devotion:

> *"There is no blame upon you in seeking bounty from your Lord."* (Qur'an 2:198)

Al-Rāzī notes the wisdom: God yokes commerce to worship so that livelihood does not prevent devotion nor devotion prevent livelihood — the pilgrimage season sustains Makkah's economy to this day while sustaining pilgrims' souls.

**Mentioning God's Name Over Provided Cattle**

*Wa yadhkurū isma Allāhi fī ayyāmin maʿlūmātin ʿalā mā razaqahum min bahīmati al-anʿām* — "and mention the Name of God, during known days, over the four-legged cattle He has provided them." *Ayyām maʿlūmāt* (known days) are, by majority view, the first ten days of Dhū al-Ḥijjah (or the days of sacrifice and tashrīq, by others) — days so known they need no naming. *Bahīmat al-anʿām* (four-legged cattle) denotes camels, cattle, sheep, goats — lawful sacrificial animals. *ʿAlā mā razaqahum* ("over what He provided them") frames sacrifice as gratitude: the animal is God's provision (*rizq*) before it is man's offering. Mentioning God's Name (*tasmiyah*) at slaughter consecrates taking life:

> *"So eat of that over which God's Name has been mentioned, if you believe in His signs."* (Qur'an 6:118)

The Prophet ﷺ slaughtered with his own hand, saying *bismillāh wa Allāhu akbar* (al-Bukhārī 5558; Muslim 1966, from Anas).

**Eat and Feed the Wretched Poor**

*Fa-kulū minhā wa aṭʿimū al-bāʾisa al-faqīr* — "So eat thereof, and feed the wretched poor." *Al-bāʾis* is the distressed, afflicted; *al-faqīr* the poor, needy — the doubling emphasizes severe want. Al-Qurṭubī explains the ruling: eating from one's sacrifice is permitted and recommended (contrary to jāhiliyyah custom that forbade it), while feeding the poor is obligatory in spirit — sacrifice unshared is devotion halved. Jurists derive the tripartite division (eat, gift, charity) from this and similar verses, though proportions are flexible. The Prophet ﷺ distributed sacrificial meat generously:

> *"I used to forbid you from storing sacrificial meat beyond three days... now eat, feed, and store."* (Muslim 1977, from Buraydah)

**Sacrifice as Social Security**

Verse 28 designs worship as welfare: millions of animals sacrificed become millions of meals for the wretched — the only meat many poor families taste yearly. Modern ḥajj's massive distribution networks (canning, freezing, shipping to famine regions) fulfill *aṭʿimū al-bāʾisa al-faqīr* on a global scale. Spiritually, the verse teaches that devotion must be edible — faith that feeds. The pilgrim who slaughters but ignores the poor has performed ritual without *manfaʿah* (benefit); the believer at home who shares ʿīd meat participates in ḥajj's spirit. Every meal shared with the wretched re-enacts verse 28: God's Name mentioned, God's provision distributed, God's poor fed.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:29

> **Then let them be done with their untidiness, and fulfill their vows, and circumambulate the Ancient House.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ending Untidiness: The Return to Cleanliness**

*Thumma li-yaqḍū tafathahum* — "Then let them be done with their untidiness." *Tafath* is a rare word — al-Rāzī notes it is unknown outside exegesis; Bedouins equated it with filth. Commentators explain it as the unkempt state of the pilgrim in iḥrām: uncut nails, unshaven hair, dust-covered body. *Qaḍāʾ* here means to remove, to complete — shaving or trimming hair, clipping nails, bathing, exiting iḥrām's restrictions. Al-Ṭabarī links this to the Day of Sacrifice's sequence: stoning, slaughtering, shaving, then *ṭawāf al-ifāḍah*. The Prophet ﷺ modeled the order:

> *"He stoned the Jamrah, then slaughtered, then shaved."* (al-Bukhārī 1737; Muslim 1306, from Anas, describing the Farewell Ḥajj)

The symbolism is transparent: sins are filth, and ḥajj's culmination is cleanliness — outer grooming mirroring inner purification. The pilgrim who arrived dusty departs radiant.

**Fulfilling Vows**

*Wa li-yūfū nudhūrahum* — "and fulfill their vows." *Nudhūr* (vows) encompasses pledged acts — vowed sacrifices, vowed worship — and, by extension (Ibn Kathīr), all remaining ḥajj obligations: completing rites faithfully, not cutting them short. Al-Qurṭubī extends further: whoever vows obedience to God anywhere must fulfill it, for vows are debts to God:

> *"They fulfill their vows and fear a Day whose evil is widespread."* (Qur'an 76:7)

The Prophet ﷺ warned against vowing as bargaining yet commanded fulfilling vows of obedience:

> *"Whoever vows to obey God, let him obey Him."* (al-Bukhārī 6696, from ʿĀʾishah)

Ḥajj itself is a vow writ large — the pilgrim pledged *labbayk* (here I am) at iḥrām; verse 29 commands completing what was pledged.

**Circumambulating the Ancient House**

*Wa li-yaṭṭawwafū bi-l-bayti al-ʿatīq* — "and circumambulate the Ancient House." *Al-ʿatīq* means ancient, venerable — and, by other derivations, freed (from tyrants' domination) or precious/beautiful. Al-Ṭabarī cites the Hadith: "It was named *al-ʿatīq* because God freed it from tyrants — no tyrant ever triumphed over it" (cf. Sūrah 105, the Elephant). The circumambulation here is *ṭawāf al-ifāḍah*, ḥajj's pillar without which ḥajj is incomplete. The Qur'an elsewhere commands:

> *"Then let their circuit end at the Ancient House."* (cf. 22:33)

Ibn Kathīr notes the honor: God calls it ancient because it is the first house established for mankind (3:96) — older than empires, surviving them all.

**Cleanliness, Fidelity, and Circling the Center**

The verse's triad — remove filth, fulfill vows, circle the House — maps the spiritual life. First, purification: strip sins as pilgrims strip unkempt hair. Second, fidelity: complete what you pledged — prayers, fasts, promises — without truncation. Third, centering: circumambulate life around God, not self — every orbit returning to Him. Modern believers who cannot attend ḥajj practice the triad at home: ritual bathing before Friday, fulfilling oaths, and "circling" the divine center through regular prayer facing the Ancient House. The House freed from tyrants frees its circumambulators from tyranny of self — and whoever circles it sincerely once circles in its orbit forever.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:30

> **Thus it is. And whosoever magnifies the sacred things of God, that is better for him in the eyes of his Lord. Cattle are permitted you, save that which is recited unto you. So shun the filth of idols, and shun false speech,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Magnifying God's Sacred Things**

*Dhālika wa man yuʿaẓẓim ḥurumāti Allāhi fa-huwa khayrun lahu ʿinda rabbih* — "Thus it is. And whosoever magnifies the sacred things of God, that is better for him in the eyes of his Lord." *Ḥurumāt* (plural of *ḥurmah*) denotes inviolable, sacred things — prohibitions to be respected, sanctities to be honored: the ḥajj rites, the sanctuary, the sacred months, and by extension all divine commands. *Taʿẓīm* is to deem great, to honor. Al-Ṭabarī explains: whoever treats God's boundaries as weighty — performing rites with reverence, avoiding violations with seriousness — earns goodness (*khayr*) with his Lord. Al-Rāzī notes the comparativeness of *khayrun* (better): better than the alternative of negligence, better than worldly gains sought through violation, better in both worlds.

The Prophet ﷺ embodied such magnification even under provocation:

> *"By Him in Whose Hand is my soul, they will not ask me for any matter magnifying God's sacred things but that I shall grant it to them."* (al-Bukhārī 2731, his words at Ḥudaybiyah)

**Cattle Permitted, Save What Is Recited**

*Wa uḥillat lakumu al-anʿāmu illā mā yutlā ʿalaykum* — "Cattle are permitted you, save that which is recited unto you." *Al-anʿām* (camels, cattle, sheep, goats) are lawful as food and sacrifice, excepting what revelation prohibits — carrion, blood, pork, animals dedicated to other than God (5:3; 6:145). Al-Qurṭubī notes the abrogation of jāhiliyyah taboos (*baḥīrah, sāʾibah, waṣīlah* — 5:103): pagans had forbidden wholesome animals by superstition; Islam restores permission bounded only by revelation. The principle is liberating: the default in foods is lawfulness; prohibition requires divine text, not human whim. Ibn Kathīr links this to ḥajj's context: pilgrims may eat sacrificial meat freely within God's bounds — reverence does not mean ascetic refusal of lawful bounty.

**Shun the Filth of Idols and False Speech**

*Fa-ijtanibū al-rijsa min al-awthāni wa ijtanibū qawla al-zūr* — "So shun the filth of idols, and shun false speech." *Rijs* is filth, impurity — idols are spiritual sewage. *Ijtināb* (shunning) demands distance, not mere non-worship: avoid their festivals, oaths, and venues. Strikingly, *qawl al-zūr* (false speech — lying, false testimony, slander, and the idolaters' claims about their gods) is paired with idolatry itself. Al-Rāzī explains: idolatry is falsehood in belief, false speech is falsehood in word — twins of untruth. The Prophet ﷺ stressed this pairing:

> *"Shall I inform you of the gravest of major sins? Associating partners with God, disobedience to parents..." — then he sat up and said, "and indeed false speech and false testimony" — repeating it until we wished he would stop.* (al-Bukhārī 2654; Muslim 87, from Abū Bakrah)

**Reverence in Deed, Truth in Word**

The verse yokes three devotions: magnify sacred things (reverent action), eat lawfully (grateful consumption), shun falsehood (pure belief and speech). Modern applications are direct: magnify prayer-times, mosques, and ḥajj by punctuality and presence; eat ḥalāl without superstition or excess; purge life of idols (status, desire, ideology worshipped beside God) and of *zūr* — fake news shared, false reviews posted, exaggerated résumés, perjured excuses. The believer who honors God's boundaries, thanks Him for cattle, and cleanses belief and tongue finds, as promised, that it is *better for him with his Lord* — better than every alternative, in this world and the next.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:31

> **as ḥanīfs before God, not ascribing partners unto Him. And whosoever ascribes partners unto God, it is as if he fell from the sky and the birds snatched him, or the wind swept him away to a place far off.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥanīfs Before God**

*Ḥunafāʾa li-Allāhi ghayra mushrikīna bihi* — "as ḥanīfs before God, not ascribing partners unto Him." *Ḥanīf* (plural *ḥunafāʾ*), from *ḥ-n-f* (to incline away from crookedness toward straightness), denotes the primordial monotheist — one who inclines from all false ways to the straight worship of God alone, following Abraham's creed. Al-Qurṭubī explains *li-Allāh* (before/for God) as sincerity of direction: the ḥanīf's entire inclination is Godward. The Qur'an identifies Abraham as the archetype:

> *"Truly Abraham was a community, devout to God, a ḥanīf, and he was not of the idolaters."* (Qur'an 16:120; cf. 2:135; 3:67)

Ibn Kathīr notes the verse continues verse 30's commands (shun idols, shun falsehood) with their positive form: be ḥanīfs. Negation of falsehood and affirmation of purity are one devotion's two faces.

**Falling from the Sky**

*Wa man yushrik bi-Allāhi fa-ka-annamā kharra min al-samāʾ* — "And whosoever ascribes partners unto God, it is as if he fell from the sky." *Kharr* denotes a heavy, headlong fall. Al-Rāzī unpacks the parable: faith is elevation — the believer ascends through tawḥīd toward heaven's tranquillity; idolatry is a plunge from that height into disintegration. The fall is total and accelerating: each compromise with polytheism — trusting idols, fearing them, sacrificing to them — increases velocity. The Qur'an elsewhere describes disbelief as a fall:

> *"And whoever disbelieves... his deeds fall, and in the Hereafter he is among the losers."* (Qur'an 5:5, in meaning of *ḥabṭ* — nullification)

**Snatched by Birds or Swept by Wind**

*Fa-takhṭafuhu al-ṭayru aw tahwī bihi al-rīḥu fī makānin saḥīq* — "and the birds snatched him, or the wind swept him away to a place far off." Two fates for the fallen: dismemberment (*takhtaf* — snatching, tearing) by birds of prey, or dispersal by violent wind (*tahwī* — carrying off, dropping) into *makān saḥīq* (a far-off, remote place). Al-Ṭabarī explains both as images of helplessness: the idolater's soul, fallen from faith's height, is torn by devils and desires (birds) or scattered by caprice and conjecture (wind) into remoteness from God. Al-Rāzī adds the spiritual reading: birds are Satanic whisperings that snatch fragments of resolve; wind is passion that sweeps the heart from one craving to another until it lands nowhere. The Qur'an elsewhere compares falsehood's scattering:

> *"And We shall turn to what deeds they did and make them scattered dust."* (Qur'an 25:23)

**Elevation Through Unity, Disintegration Through Division**

The parable's psychology is precise: tawḥīd integrates — one God, one direction, one purpose — gathering the heart's fragments into wholeness; shirk disintegrates — many gods, many fears, many masters — tearing the self apart. Modern polytheisms confirm it: whoever worships wealth, approval, pleasure, and ideology together falls from inner unity, snatched by anxieties and swept by trends into remoteness from peace. The Prophet ﷺ taught integration through one devotion:

> *"Whoever makes his concerns one concern — the Hereafter — God suffices him for his worldly concerns."* (Ibn Mājah 4106, from Ibn Masʿūd; graded fair)

Practically, the believer guards elevation by guarding tawḥīd: one Lord feared, one Lord hoped in, one Lord obeyed — and refuses the fall, for what falls from heaven finds only beaks and winds below.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:32

> **Thus it is. And whosoever magnifies the rituals of God, truly that comes from the reverence of hearts.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Magnifying God's Rituals**

*Dhālika wa man yuʿaẓẓim shaʿāʾira Allāhi fa-innahā min taqwā al-qulūb* — "Thus it is. And whosoever magnifies the rituals of God, truly that comes from the reverence of hearts." *Shaʿāʾir* (plural of *shaʿīrah*), from *sh-ʿ-r* (to know, to perceive), denotes perceptible signs through which God is known — rites, symbols, sacred places and times: ḥajj stations, sacrificial animals, the Kaʿbah, Ṣafā and Marwah. Al-Qurṭubī defines them as "that in which God has placed something by which He makes known." *Taʿẓīm* (magnifying) here means honoring them as weighty: choosing fine sacrificial animals (not the sick or scrawny), performing rites with presence, respecting sacred places. Ibn Kathīr cites Mujāhid: magnification includes selecting large, healthy, costly offerings — generosity toward God reflecting esteem for Him.

The Qur'an names such symbols:

> *"Truly Ṣafā and Marwah are among God's rituals."* (Qur'an 2:158)

**Reverence of Hearts as the Source**

*Fa-innahā min taqwā al-qulūb* — "truly that comes from the reverence of hearts." The pronoun refers to magnification: honoring rites springs from hearts' *taqwā*. Al-Rāzī reverses the common assumption: rites do not manufacture reverence mechanically; reverence produces true honoring of rites. The outward act reveals the inward state — a magnificent sacrifice from a heedless heart is display, while a modest offering from a reverent heart is magnification. The Prophet ﷺ located *taqwā* precisely:

> *"Taqwā is here" — pointing to his chest three times.* (Muslim 2564, from Abū Hurayrah)

Al-Qurṭubī therefore warns against two errors: neglecting rites as "mere externals" (for reverent hearts honor them), and performing them ostentatiously (for ostentation proves hearts lack what rites require).

**The Sacrificial Animal as Mirror of the Soul**

Commentators develop the verse's pastoral imagery: just as verse 2:71's unblemished cow symbolized the soul made worthy, so the pilgrim's choice of offering mirrors his soul's preparation. The scrawny, blemished animal betrays a scrawny devotion; the fine, well-fed beast reflects a nourished heart. Some Sufis, like al-Qushayrī, read the verse as commanding magnification of all divine symbols — scholars, mosques, prayer-times, the Qur'an's pages — since contempt for any of them betrays cardiac irreverence. The Qur'an warns:

> *"And whoever honors God's rituals — truly that is from the reverence of hearts."* (this verse, oft-cited as proof that outward honor evidences inward faith)

**Honoring Symbols in an Age of Irony**

Modern culture trains irony toward the sacred: rites mocked as superstition, mosques treated casually, sacrifices ordered by phone without witnessing, prayer rushed like a chore. Verse 32 diagnoses such casualness as cardiac, not cultural: hearts lacking *taqwā* cannot magnify. The remedy is deliberate honoring: arrive early for prayer, dress well for the mosque, choose the better charity, learn ḥajj's meanings before performing its motions, stand in awe at sacred places. Each act of *taʿẓīm* both evidences and increases *taqwā* — a virtuous circle: reverence honors rites, and honored rites deepen reverence. The believer who magnifies God's symbols finds God magnifying him — for whoever honors the King's insignia is honored by the King.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:33

> **You shall have benefits in them, for a term appointed. Thereafter their lawful place of sacrifice shall be at the Ancient House.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Benefits in Sacrificial Animals for a Term**

*Lakum fīhā manāfiʿu ilā ajalin musammā* — "You shall have benefits in them, for a term appointed." The pronoun refers to sacrificial animals (*budn*, camels and cattle designated for offering). *Manāfiʿ* (benefits) includes milk, wool, offspring, and riding — utility before sacrifice. *Ilā ajalin musammā* ("for a term appointed") bounds this utility: until slaughter during ḥajj. Al-Ṭabarī explains the context: jāhiliyyah Arabs, deeming sacrificial animals taboo, refused their milk, wool, or backs — superstition depriving owners of lawful benefit. Islam restores balance: dedication to God does not prohibit interim use. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms animals' manifold utility:

> *"And cattle He created for you: in them is warmth and benefits, and of them you eat. And in them is beauty for you when you bring them home and when you drive them forth."* (Qur'an 16:5–6)

Al-Qurṭubī derives the juristic rule: riding or milking the offering-animal is permitted when needed without harming it — dedication does not demand waste.

**Lawful Place of Sacrifice at the Ancient House**

*Thumma maḥilluhā ilā al-bayti al-ʿatīq* — "Thereafter their lawful place of sacrifice shall be at the Ancient House." *Maḥill* (*ḥ-l-l*) denotes the place/time of becoming lawful — where slaughter legitimates consumption and completes dedication. *Ilā al-bayti al-ʿatīq* (at/near the Ancient House) designates the Ḥaram environs — Minā, Makkah — as the ḥajj offering's venue. Al-Rāzī notes the convergence: benefits enjoyed across lands culminate at one House, teaching that provision's journey ends in devotion. Ibn Kathīr links this to Abraham's precedent: his intended sacrifice of his son (thence the ram) occurred at Minā — every subsequent offering re-enacts that submission at its station.

The Prophet ﷺ sacrificed at the appointed place:

> *"I slaughter here, and all of Minā is a slaughter-place."* (Muslim 1218, from Jābir, in the Farewell Ḥajj)

**Dedication Without Superstition**

The verse corrects two opposite errors. Pagan superstition consecrated animals through deprivation — the dedicated camel neither ridden nor milked, left to idols (5:103). Modern wastefulness dedicates nothing — animals consumed without gratitude, slaughtered without God's Name, sacrificed without the poor. Islam's middle way: dedicate to God, benefit gratefully, slaughter punctually, distribute generously. Al-Qurṭubī generalizes the principle to all endowments (*waqf*): a mosque's courtyard may host community benefit, a charity's capital may be invested — dedication to God does not freeze utility but directs it. The "term appointed" further teaches temporality: every benefit has its *ajal* (term) — milk dries, wool sheds, life ends — and wisdom uses benefits before terms expire.

**Using Blessings Before Their Terms**

Practically, verse 33 counsels stewardship with deadlines. Youth, health, wealth, time — each is a *manfaʿah ilā ajalin musammā* (benefit for an appointed term). The wise believer milks them for good before their *maḥill* (appointed end) arrives: learning before memory fades, giving before wealth passes, worshipping before strength wanes. And he directs their culmination to "the Ancient House" — symbolically, to God: every career, harvest, and project should terminate in devotion, not dissipation. Whoever benefits lawfully and sacrifices punctually finds his whole life become an offering whose lawful place is with God.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:34

> **For every community We have appointed a rite, that they might mention the Name of God over the four-legged cattle He has provided them. Your God is one God; so submit unto Him, and give glad tidings to the humble,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Every Community's Appointed Rite**

*Wa li-kulli ummatin jaʿalnā mansakan* — "For every community We have appointed a rite." *Mansak* (*n-s-k*), related to *nusuk* (devotion, sacrifice), denotes a ritual act, a sacrificial rite, or a place of ritual. Al-Qurṭubī preserves all three: each nation received divinely appointed worship — sacrifices, prayers, festivals — suited to its time. The Qur'an affirms this universality:

> *"For each among you We have appointed a law and a way."* (Qur'an 5:48)

> *"And We sent a messenger unto every community."* (Qur'an 16:36)

Al-Rāzī notes the implication: ritual diversity across prophets reflects divine wisdom, not divine inconsistency — forms differ, but the Essence worshipped is one. Ibn Kathīr adds that *mansak* here specifically includes sacrifice, linking all nations' offerings to one purpose: *li-yadhkurū isma Allāhi ʿalā mā razaqahum min bahīmati al-anʿām* — "that they might mention the Name of God over the four-legged cattle He has provided them." Every nation's slaughter was to be consecrated by God's Name — gratitude universalized.

**Your God Is One God — So Submit**

*Fa-ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥidun fa-lahu aslimū* — "Your God is one God; so submit unto Him." *Wāḥid* means both numerically one and uniquely the same — one God for all communities, the same God across all rites. The command *aslimū* (submit, surrender) derives Islam's very name: all prophets brought *islām* (submission), though laws (*sharāʾiʿ*) varied. The Qur'an elsewhere unites them:

> *"He has prescribed for you of religion what He enjoined upon Noah, and what We revealed to thee, and what We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus: establish religion and be not divided therein."* (Qur'an 42:13)

Al-Ṭabarī explains the argument: since the God is one and the provision is His, submission must be to Him alone — sacrifice to idols contradicts both unity and gratitude.

**Glad Tidings to the Humble**

*Wa bashshiri al-mukhbitīn* — "and give glad tidings to the humble." *Mukhbitīn* (*kh-b-t*), from *khabt* (low, level land), denotes the lowly, humble, tranquil — hearts leveled before God, free from pride's peaks. Al-Rāzī contrasts them with verse 9's neck-twisters: the arrogant turn aside, the humble bow down. The command addresses the Prophet ﷺ (*bashshir* — give glad tidings): announce to the humble God's acceptance, Paradise, and approval. The next verse will define them — hearts quivering at God's mention, patient in trial, prayerful, generous. The Prophet ﷺ described their reward:

> *"Paradise... its inhabitants are the weak and humble."* (al-Bukhārī 6657; Muslim 2853, from Ḥārithah ibn Wahb, in meaning)

**Unity Beneath Diversity**

The verse offers Islam's theology of religious history: one God, many communities, varied rites, single submission. This grounds both confidence and tolerance: confidence that Islam consummates prior rites (not contradicting but completing them), tolerance that prior communities received genuine divine appointments (not human inventions). Modern believers navigating plural societies draw guidance: affirm common Abrahamic roots (one God, sacrifice, submission) while inviting to final completeness. And personally, the verse summons to *ikhbāt* (humility): level the heart's pride, mention God's Name over every provision — meals, income, harvests — and submit wholly to the One God. To such humble ones, through every prophet's mouth, come glad tidings.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:35

> **whose hearts quiver when God is mentioned, and who bear patiently what befalls them, who perform the prayer, and who spend of that which We have provided them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hearts That Quiver at God's Mention**

*Alladhīna idhā dhukira Allāhu wajilat qulūbuhum* — "whose hearts quiver when God is mentioned." *Wajal* is trembling awe — the heart's shiver before Majesty. This is the first mark of the *mukhbitīn* (humble) of verse 34. Al-Qushayrī notes that quivering intensifies with awareness: the more one knows God's grandeur and one's own shortcomings, the more the heart trembles at His Name. The Qur'an elsewhere describes faith's physical imprint:

> *"Their skins and their hearts soften unto the remembrance of God."* (Qur'an 39:23)

> *"The believers are only those whose hearts quiver when God is mentioned."* (Qur'an 8:2)

Ibn Kathīr explains that *wajal* here is reverent fear producing obedience — not despairing terror but the trembling that restrains from sin and hastens to good. The Prophet ﷺ embodied this: his heart quivered, his eyes wept, his limbs prayed through the night until his feet swelled (al-Bukhārī 4837; Muslim 2820, from al-Mughīrah).

**Patience with What Befalls**

*Wa al-ṣābirīna ʿalā mā aṣābahum* — "and who bear patiently what befalls them." The second mark is *ṣabr* (patient endurance) over afflictions — illness, loss, persecution, poverty. Al-Rāzī notes the passive *aṣābahum* (befalls them): trials come from beyond, but patience comes from within. Unlike verse 11's brink-worshipper who topples at trial, the humble stand firm, trusting God's wisdom. The Qur'an promises patience's wage without measure:

> *"Truly the patient shall be paid their reward without reckoning."* (Qur'an 39:10)

The Prophet ﷺ defined patience's scope:

> *"Patience is at the first shock."* (al-Bukhārī 1302; Muslim 926, from Anas)

Al-Qurṭubī adds that patience here includes patience in obedience (persevering in prayer) and against disobedience (resisting temptation) — a threefold patience adorning the humble.

**Prayer Performed and Provision Spent**

*Wa al-muqīmī al-ṣalāti wa mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn* — "who perform the prayer, and who spend of that which We have provided them." The final two marks pair God's right (*ṣalāh*) with creatures' right (*nafaqah*). *Iqāmah* (establishing prayer) means performing it completely — timely, humble, congregational — not merely discharging it. Spending (*infāq*) is qualified by *mimmā razaqnāhum* ("of what We provided them"): charity is returning God's provision to God's dependents. Al-Ṭabarī notes the balance: prayer connects upward, charity connects outward — humility before God expresses itself in generosity toward men. The Qur'an elsewhere joins them:

> *"And perform the prayer and give the alms."* (Qur'an 2:43, and throughout)

**The Fourfold Portrait of Humility**

Verse 35 paints humility's anatomy: a quivering heart (inward awe), an enduring soul (steadfast patience), a praying body (disciplined worship), and an open hand (generous spending). Each corrects a counterfeit: emotion without endurance is sentimentality; endurance without prayer is stoicism; prayer without charity is ritualism; charity without awe is philanthropy. Together they form *ikhbāt* — leveled, tranquil humility. Modern believers can self-diagnose by the four: does God's mention move my heart or bore it? Do trials topple or temper me? Is my prayer established or neglected? Does my provision flow or stagnate? Whoever cultivates the four joins the company to whom the Prophet ﷺ was commanded: give glad tidings.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:36

> **And We have placed the sacrificial camels for you among God’s rituals. There is good for you in them. So mention the Name of God over them as they line up. Then when they have fallen upon their flanks, eat of them, and feed the needy who solicit and those who do not. Thus have We made them subservient unto you, that haply you may give thanks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sacrificial Camels Among God's Rituals**

*Wa al-budna jaʿalnāhā lakum min shaʿāʾiri Allāh* — "And We have placed the sacrificial camels for you among God's rituals." *Budn* (plural of *badanah*) denotes large sacrificial animals — camels primarily, cattle by extension. Their designation *min shaʿāʾiri Allāh* (among God's rituals/symbols) elevates livestock to liturgy: these animals are not mere meat but divine signs. *Lakum fīhā khayr* — "There is good for you in them": worldly good (meat, sharing, sustenance) and otherworldly good (reward, forgiveness, nearness). Al-Qurṭubī notes the grace: God designates animals for man's benefit, then rewards man for offering them back — bounty upon bounty. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"And cattle... you eat of them, and of them you eat."* (Qur'an 36:72–73, on manifold benefits)

**Mentioning God's Name as They Line Up**

*Fa-dhkurū isma Allāhi ʿalayhā ṣawāff* — "So mention the Name of God over them as they line up." *Ṣawāff* (plural of *ṣāffah*) describes camels standing in rows, lined up — traditionally slaughtered standing, one foreleg tethered. The *tasmiyah* (*bismillāh, Allāhu akbar*) consecrates slaughter, distinguishing Islamic offering from pagan stabbing and secular butchery. Al-Ṭabarī explains the procedure: the slaughterer faces the qiblah, mentions God's Name, and pierces (*naḥr*) the camel's lower neck. The Prophet ﷺ performed this with his own hand at the Farewell Ḥajj:

> *"He slaughtered sixty-three camels with his own hand, then gave the rest to ʿAlī."* (Muslim 1218, from Jābir)

*Thumma idhā wajabat junūbuhā* — "Then when they have fallen upon their flanks": *wujūb* here means falling, collapsing in death — only after confirmed death may meat be taken, ensuring mercy and lawfulness.

**Eat, Feed the Soliciting and the Restrained**

*Fa-kulū minhā wa aṭʿimū al-qāniʿa wa al-muʿtarr* — "eat of them, and feed the needy who solicit (*al-qāniʿ*) and those who do not (*al-muʿtarr*)." Commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr) explain: *al-qāniʿ* is the contented poor who asks (or who is content with what he is given), *al-muʿtarr* is the visitor/observer who comes hoping without begging — or vice versa by other readings. Either way, the verse commands feeding both the vocal and the silent poor. Al-Qurṭubī links this to 2:273's praise of the poor "who do not beg importunately": true generosity seeks the dignified silent, not only the clamorous. The Prophet ﷺ commanded:

> *"Eat, feed, and store."* (Muslim 1977, from Buraydah, on sacrificial meat)

**Subservience for Gratitude**

*Kadhālika sakhkharnāhā lakum laʿallakum tashkurūn* — "Thus have We made them subservient unto you, that haply you may give thanks." *Taskhīr* (subjugation) is the verse's theology: mighty camels — capable of crushing men — submit to a child's lead by God's arrangement. The Qur'an marvels:

> *"And We made them subservient to them: of them some they ride, and of them they eat."* (Qur'an 36:72)

Al-Rāzī argues: subservience without gratitude is ingratitude doubled — God tamed beasts for man, and man must tame himself for God. *Laʿallakum tashkurūn* ("that haply you may give thanks") makes gratitude sacrifice's purpose: every slaughtered camel preaches that provision is gift, not entitlement. Modern meat-consumers, detached from slaughter by packaging, need this verse: mention God's Name, share with seen and unseen poor, and thank the Subduer — lest subservient animals testify against ungrateful masters.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:37

> **Neither their flesh nor their blood will reach God, but the reverence from you reaches Him. Thus has He made them subservient unto you, that you might magnify God for having guided you. And give glad tidings to the virtuous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Flesh and Blood Do Not Reach God**

*Lan yanāla Allāha luḥūmuhā wa lā dimāʾuhā* — "Neither their flesh nor their blood will reach God." *Nayl* (*n-y-l*) means to reach, to attain. The verse demolishes pagan literalism: idolaters smeared sacrificial blood on the Kaʿbah and idols, imagining deities consumed it; some early Muslims, habituated to this, wished to continue. God declares: He is beyond ingestion — flesh decays, blood spills, but nothing corporeal ascends to the Incorporeal. Al-Ṭabarī cites the occasion: when Muslims asked about smearing blood on the Kaʿbah, this verse forbade it. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms God's transcendence over need:

> *"And I did not create jinn and mankind save to worship Me. I desire no provision from them, nor do I desire that they feed Me."* (Qur'an 51:56–57)

Ibn Kathīr notes the universality: no offering enriches God — prayer, fasting, charity ascend as obedience, not as commodities.

**But Reverence Reaches Him**

*Wa lākin yanāluhu al-taqwā minkum* — "but the reverence from you reaches Him." *Taqwā* — reverent consciousness, sincere Godwardness — is the only offering that ascends. Al-Rāzī calls this the verse's revolution: worship's value lies not in its material but its motive. A camel sacrificed for show reaches no higher than spectators' eyes; a sheep offered with trembling sincerity reaches the Throne. The Prophet ﷺ stated the principle governing all deeds:

> *"God looks not at your forms nor your wealth, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds."* (Muslim 2564, from Abū Hurayrah)

Al-Qurṭubī extends the principle: prayer's reaching is humility, fasting's reaching is restraint, charity's reaching is compassion, ḥajj's reaching is transformation. Rituals are vessels; *taqwā* is the wine.

**Magnifying God for Guidance**

*Kadhālika sakhkharahā lakum li-tukabbirū Allāha ʿalā mā hadākum* — "Thus has He made them subservient unto you, that you might magnify God for having guided you." Subservience's purpose is *takbīr* (magnification: *Allāhu akbar*) *for guidance* — gratitude for being guided to tawḥīd's sacrifice rather than idolatry's. Al-Ṭabarī explains: pagans sacrificed to idols; Muslims sacrifice to God — the difference is guidance, and guidance demands magnification. Hence the *takbīrāt* of ʿīd and tashrīq days echo this verse: every sacrifice concludes with "God is Greater." Ibn Kathīr notes the address shifts to the Prophet ﷺ in the final clause: *wa bashshiri al-muḥsinīn* — "And give glad tidings to the virtuous." *Muḥsinīn* are those who worship with excellence (*iḥsān*) — "as though you see Him" (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8, the Hadith of Gabriel).

**The Inner Sacrifice**

The verse's enduring lesson is interiorization: every outer sacrifice must slaughter an inner idol — stinginess, ostentation, heedlessness. The knife cuts the camel's throat; *taqwā* cuts the soul's greed. Modern believers who delegate sacrifice to agencies should heed: convenience must not sever the heart's participation — witness if possible, intend sincerely, distribute personally, magnify audibly. And beyond ʿīd, every act is sacrifice: wealth spent, time given, desires slaughtered. None of it — coins, hours, carcasses — reaches God as matter; all of it reaches Him as *taqwā*. Whoever sacrifices with excellence joins the *muḥsinīn* — and to them, through the Prophet's mouth, come glad tidings.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:38

> **Truly God defends those who believe. Truly God loves not any ungrateful traitor.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Defends Those Who Believe**

*Inna Allāha yudāfiʿu ʿan alladhīna āmanū* — "Truly God defends those who believe." *Dafʿ* (*d-f-ʿ*) means to push back, to repel — God Himself repels enemies, plots, and harms from believers. The present tense (*yudāfiʿ*) indicates continuous defense: in every age, against every adversary. Al-Ṭabarī explains the context: persecuted Makkan believers, barred from worship and tortured for faith, are assured that God repels what they cannot. The Qur'an elsewhere details this defense:

> *"And God repelled those who disbelieved in their rage, attaining no good. And God sufficed the believers in battle."* (Qur'an 33:25, on the Confederates)

> *"Truly We shall help Our messengers and those who believe in the life of this world and on the Day witnesses arise."* (Qur'an 40:51)

Ibn Kathīr notes the condition: defense attaches to *alladhīna āmanū* (those who believe) — genuine faith, not mere affiliation. The more complete the faith, the more comprehensive the defense.

**God Loves Not Any Ungrateful Traitor**

*Inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla khawwānin kafūr* — "Truly God loves not any ungrateful traitor." *Khawwān* (*kh-w-n*) is an intensive traitor — habitually treacherous, perfidious; *kafūr* (*k-f-r*) is intensely ungrateful, disbelieving — covering blessings. The pairing describes the Makkan persecutors: ungrateful for God's blessings (security, provision, the Prophet himself) and treacherous toward covenants and kinship. Al-Qurṭubī reports that the verse also restrained some believers who, suffering persecution, wished to retaliate through treachery and assassination; God forbids believers from mirroring traitors even under oppression. The Prophet ﷺ warned:

> *"A flag will be hoisted for every treacherous person on the Day of Resurrection, reading: 'This is the treachery of so-and-so.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6178; Muslim 1735, from Ibn ʿUmar)

The Qur'an elsewhere pairs the same traits:

> *"Surely God loves not the one given to treachery and sin."* (Qur'an 4:107)

**Defense Without Treachery**

The verse's juxtaposition legislates an ethic of conflict: God defends believers, but believers must not defend themselves through treachery. Al-Rāzī explains the wisdom: treachery (*khiyānah*) — breaking covenants, assassinating emissaries, betraying trusts — may win battles but forfeits divine love, and without divine love no victory endures. Hence Islam's war-ethics: no perfidy, no mutilation, no killing of non-combatants, no breaking treaties — even against traitors. The Qur'an commands:

> *"And if thou fearest treachery from a people, cast back to them [their treaty] on equal terms. Truly God loves not the treacherous."* (Qur'an 8:58)

Ibn Taymiyyah held that this verse proves God's defense is conditional upon believers' righteousness: when Muslims abandon faith's requirements — unity, justice, trustworthiness — they forfeit the defense they invoke.

**Trusting the Defender Today**

Practically, verse 38 counsels believers facing hostility — discrimination, defamation, persecution — to seek God's defense through faith rather than treachery's shortcuts. The believer does not lie to counter lies, betray to counter betrayal, or abandon gratitude in hardship. He prays, trusts, acts justly, and awaits *dafʿ* — God's repelling, which may come as unexpected allies, exposed plots, strengthened hearts, or deferred recompense in the Hereafter. The Prophet ﷺ, boycotted, stoned, and expelled, never broke a covenant nor ordered treachery — and God defended him until his enemies sought his protection. Whoever believes purely and acts trustworthily will find, as promised, that God defends — for He loves the grateful faithful and loves not any ungrateful traitor.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:39

> **Permission is granted to those who are fought, because they have been wronged—and truly God is able to help them—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Permission to Fight**

*Udhina li-alladhīna yuqātalūna bi-annahum ẓulimū* — "Permission is granted to those who are fought, because they have been wronged." This is widely held to be the first verse permitting armed struggle (*jihād*) after thirteen years of Makkan patience under torture, boycott, and expulsion. *Udhina* (permission is granted) is passive and gentle: not yet a command but a lifting of restraint. Al-Ṭabarī preserves Ibn ʿAbbās's report that upon its revelation, Abū Bakr said, "I knew fighting would come." The cause (*bi-annahum ẓulimū* — "because they have been wronged") is explicitly defensive: persecution, expulsion, and aggression — not conquest or compulsion. The Qur'an elsewhere bounds fighting by this cause:

> *"And fight in God's way those who fight you, but aggress not. Truly God loves not aggressors."* (Qur'an 2:190)

Variant readings (*yuqātilūna* — "who fight" vs. *yuqātalūna* — "who are fought") both point to defensive engagement: permission for the wronged to resist wrong.

**Thirteen Years of Patience Before Permission**

Commentators stress the chronology: Muslims endured Sumayyah's martyrdom, Bilāl's torture, the boycott's starvation, and the emigrations to Abyssinia and Madinah — responding with patience, prayer, and migration, never arms. Al-Qurṭubī explains the wisdom: premature fighting would have annihilated the fragile community and confused invitation with coercion; permission came only after a polity (Madinah) existed and wrongs became intolerable. The Prophet's ﷺ Makkan policy embodied this:

> *"I have been commanded to pardon, so do not fight."* (cf. Qur'an 4:77: "Restrain your hands and perform prayer" — the Makkan command)

Ibn Kathīr notes that permission escalated gradually: first permission (22:39), then command against aggressors (2:190), then general confrontation with hostile polytheists (9:5) — each stage conditioned by circumstance, never licensing aggression.

**Truly God Is Able to Help Them**

*Wa inna Allāha ʿalā naṣrihim la-qadīr* — "and truly God is able to help them." The emphatic *inna... la-qadīr* (truly... Most Able) assures the outnumbered: permission is accompanied by power. Al-Rāzī notes the consolation: quantity favors Quraysh, but *qudrah* (power) belongs to God — Badr's 313 against 1000 would soon prove it. The Qur'an elsewhere conditions victory:

> *"O you who believe! If you help God, He will help you and make firm your feet."* (Qur'an 47:7)

Help is able, but also conditional — upon faith, unity, and justice. History confirms both: aided when faithful at Badr, disciplined when disobedient at Uḥud.

**Defensive Struggle and Its Modern Misreadings**

Verse 39 is Islam's charter of just war misread by both extremes. Extremists ignore its defensiveness (*because they have been wronged*), waging aggression God forbids. Islamophobes ignore its context (thirteen years of persecution), portraying Islam as inherently militant. Classical scholars unanimously bounded *jihād* by wrong-removal, proportionality, non-combatant immunity, and treaty-fidelity — the next verse will add protection of all houses of worship. The greater struggle (*jihād akbar*) against the soul remains perpetual, as the Prophet ﷺ said returning from campaign:

> *"We have returned from the lesser struggle to the greater struggle."* (al-Bayhaqī, Zuḥd; al-Khaṭīb; graded weak in chain but sound in meaning per scholars)

Practically, believers today invoke verse 39 against oppression lawfully and justly — through courts, advocacy, self-defense within law — remembering that permission to resist wrong never permits committing it. The wronged may fight back, but only as the wronged, never as wrongdoers.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:40

> **who were expelled from their homes without right, only for saying, “Our Lord is God.” Were it not for God’s repelling people, some by means of others, monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques, wherein God’s Name is mentioned much, would have been destroyed. And God will surely help those who help Him—truly God is Strong, Mighty—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Expelled Only for Saying "Our Lord Is God"**

*Alladhīna ukhrijū min diyārihim bi-ghayri ḥaqqin illā an yaqūlū rabbunā Allāh* — "who were expelled from their homes without right, only for saying, 'Our Lord is God.'" The verse defines the wrong of verse 39: expulsion (*ikhrāj*) from homes and homeland — among the gravest injustices, since home is shelter, memory, and livelihood. *Bi-ghayri ḥaqq* (without right) denies any lawful cause; *illā an yaqūlū* (only for saying) identifies the sole "crime": monotheistic confession. Al-Ṭabarī notes the parallel with Pharaoh's threat to believing magicians and every tyrant's persecution of conscience. The Qur'an elsewhere lists expulsion among Quraysh's chief crimes:

> *"And expulsion from the Sacred Mosque is greater with God than killing."* (Qur'an 2:217, in meaning)

Ibn Kathīr emphasizes: the Muslims had committed no violence, theft, or treason — their only offense was creed. This establishes religious persecution as *casus belli* in Islamic law: conscience forcibly exiled may resist.

**Repelling People by Means of Others**

*Wa lawlā dafʿu Allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍ* — "Were it not for God's repelling people, some by means of others." *Dafʿ* (repelling) here mirrors verse 38's *yudāfiʿ* (He defends): God's defense operates through human instruments — the wronged resisting wrongdoers, just powers checking tyrants. Al-Rāzī calls this the sociology of restraint: without divinely enabled resistance, the strong would devour the weak universally and extinguish worship. The Qur'an states the same law elsewhere:

> *"Were it not for God's repelling people, some by means of others, the earth would have been corrupted."* (Qur'an 2:251)

History confirms: unchecked Hulagus burn Baghdad's libraries; unchecked Hitlers build crematoria; resistance — military, legal, moral — preserves civilization.

**Protection of All Houses of Worship**

*La-huddimat ṣawāmiʿu wa biyaʿun wa ṣalawātun wa masājidu yudhkaru fīhā ismu Allāhi kathīrā* — "monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques, wherein God's Name is mentioned much, would have been destroyed." This is the Qur'an's most explicit protection of non-Muslim worship: *ṣawāmiʿ* (monasteries, monks' cells), *biyaʿ* (churches), *ṣalawāt* (synagogues, places of Jewish prayer), *masājid* (mosques) — all shielded by lawful resistance. Al-Qurṭubī notes the condition *yudhkaru fīhā ismu Allāhi kathīrā* (wherein God's Name is mentioned much): houses of genuine worship deserve defense. Classical jurists extended this to prohibiting destruction of dhimmī houses of worship in Muslim lands. The Prophet's ﷺ covenants embodied it:

> *"Whoever kills a covenanted person (muʿāhad) shall not smell Paradise's fragrance."* (al-Bukhārī 3166, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr)

ʿUmar's pact for Jerusalem guaranteed churches unharmed — verse 40 in policy.

**God Helps Those Who Help Him**

*Wa la-yanṣuranna Allāhu man yanṣuruh* — "And God will surely help those who help Him." *Naṣr* (help) for God means helping His religion, His Prophet, His oppressed servants, His truth. The emphatic *la-yanṣuranna* (will surely help) is oath-like assurance. *Inna Allāha la-qawiyyun ʿazīz* — "truly God is Strong, Mighty": *Qawī* needs no allies, *ʿAzīz* is never thwarted — yet He enlists believers as instruments, honoring them with participation. Al-Rāzī notes the condition's direction: divine help follows human helping — prayer, unity, justice, courage — not the reverse. The next verse defines such helpers: prayer-establishers, alms-givers, right-enjoiners. Modern believers seeking divine help must therefore help God's causes — defending the persecuted of all faiths, preserving worship's freedom, resisting tyranny justly — and the Strong, the Mighty will surely help them.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:41

> **who, were We to establish them upon the earth, would perform the prayer, give the alms, and enjoin right and forbid wrong. And unto God is the end of all affairs.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Portrait of the Worthy Empowered**

*Alladhīna in makkannāhum fī al-arḍi aqāmū al-ṣalāta wa ātaw al-zakāta* — "who, were We to establish them upon the earth, would perform the prayer, give the alms." *Tamkīn* (*m-k-n*) means to establish, to grant power and stability. The verse defines those who help God (v. 40): empowerment does not corrupt them but manifests devotion. *Iqāmat al-ṣalāh* (establishing prayer) and *ītāʾ al-zakāh* (giving alms) are Islam's twin pillars — God's right and the poor's right. Al-Ṭabarī identifies them as the Muhājirūn and Anṣār empowered in Madinah, then in Persia and Byzantium — and every righteous community after. Unlike tyrants who, empowered, build palaces and prisons, God's helpers build mosques and treasuries for the poor. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same pair governance's mark:

> *"And perform prayer and give alms."* (Qur'an 2:43, addressed to empowered communities)

**Enjoining Right and Forbidding Wrong**

*Wa amarū bi-l-maʿrūfi wa nahaw ʿan al-munkar* — "and enjoin right and forbid wrong." *Maʿrūf* is the known-good — acknowledged by revelation, reason, and sound custom; *munkar* is the rejected-evil — denied by all three. Al-Qurṭubī explains that this is the empowered community's public duty: not merely private piety but societal reform — legislating justice, preventing vice, protecting virtue. The Prophet ﷺ made this the community's lifeline:

> *"By Him in Whose Hand is my soul, you shall enjoin right and forbid wrong, or God will soon send punishment upon you, then you will supplicate and not be answered."* (al-Tirmidhī 2169, from Ḥudhayfah; graded fair)

Ibn Taymiyyah held that *tamkīn* (empowerment) is conditional upon these four: prayer, alms, enjoining, forbidding — communities abandoning them forfeit establishment, however strong their armies.

**Unto God Is the End of All Affairs**

*Wa li-Allāhi ʿāqibatu al-umūr* — "And unto God is the end of all affairs." *ʿĀqibah* is the final outcome, the ultimate return. Al-Rāzī notes the consolation and the warning: consolation for the oppressed (tyrants' ends belong to God — Pharaoh drowned, Quraysh fell at Badr), warning for the empowered (their rule will be audited by God — justice rewarded, tyranny punished). The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"And the [good] end belongs to the reverent."* (Qur'an 7:128)

Ibn Kathīr cites ʿUthmān's era and beyond: dynasties rose and fell, but the verse's criterion endured — whoever established prayer, alms, and reform was vindicated, whoever established oppression was overturned.

**Empowerment as Test, Not Prize**

The verse reframes power: not a reward to enjoy but a trust to discharge. Modern Muslim polities, organizations, and even households granted *tamkīn* — authority, wealth, influence — face verse 41's audit: does our empowerment increase prayer or neglect it? Does our wealth flow as zakāh or stagnate? Does our authority enjoin good or enforce whim? The same applies to individuals: promotion, platform, parenthood are mini-*tamkīn* demanding the four duties. History's verdict confirms the verse: the Rightly-Guided Caliphs, who prayed, gave, and reformed, are honored across centuries; tyrants who prayed never and forbade only dissent are footnotes of shame. Unto God is the end — and he is wisest who rules each small dominion as though the audit were tomorrow.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:42

> **And if they deny thee, the people of Noah denied before them, as did ʿĀd and Thamūd,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Consolation Through Precedent**

*Wa in yukadhdhibūka fa-qad kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥin wa ʿĀdun wa Thamūd* — "And if they deny thee, the people of Noah denied before them, as did ʿĀd and Thamūd." *Takdhīb* (denial, belying) wounded the Prophet ﷺ deeply — his own kin calling him liar. God consoles: denial is not novel but perennial; the greatest messengers faced it. *Qawm Nūḥ* (Noah's people) denied across 950 years of preaching (29:14); *ʿĀd* (Hūd's people, mighty builders of pillars — 89:7) denied; *Thamūd* (Ṣāliḥ's people, carvers of mountain-homes — 7:74) denied. Al-Ṭabarī notes the order is chronological: Noah's flood-folk first, then ʿĀd's wind-destroyed giants, then Thamūd's earthquake-seized carvers. The Qur'an elsewhere assures:

> *"And messengers were denied before thee, but they bore patiently their denial and persecution until Our help came to them."* (Qur'an 6:34)

**Noah, ʿĀd, and Thamūd as Archetypes**

Each people embodies a mode of denial. Noah's folk mocked longevity's preacher — "old man with an old message" — drowning in the flood they ridiculed. ʿĀd boasted physical might — "who is greater than us in strength?" (41:15) — and were uprooted by a barren wind. Thamūd demanded signs then hamstrung the sign (the she-camel — 7:77), and were seized by the quaking. Al-Rāzī observes the escalation: mockery, arrogance, then aggression against miracles themselves. Ibn Kathīr details their ends as warnings to Quraysh, who combined all three: mocking the Prophet, boasting power, demanding signs. The Prophet ﷺ himself drew the parallel when rejected at Ṭāʾif, praying the famous supplication of the oppressed rather than invoking destruction.

**Denial's Uniformity Across Ages**

The verse teaches that denial's arguments never innovate: "you are only a man like us" (to Noah — 11:27), "who is mightier?" (ʿĀd), "bring the punishment if you are truthful" (Thamūd — 7:77). Modern denials echo: "religion is outdated" (mockery of longevity), "science makes us mighty" (ʿĀd's boast), "show us miracles" then explaining them away (Thamūd's hamstringing). The Qur'an elsewhere notes this recycling:

> *"Thus there came not unto those before them any messenger but that they said, 'A sorcerer or a madman!' Did they bequeath it to one another?"* (Qur'an 51:52–53)

Al-Qurṭubī derives comfort for every caller to truth: rejection reflects the message's weight, not the messenger's failure. Noah preached nearly a millennium with few believers — success is fidelity, not numbers.

**Bearing Denial Like the Messengers**

Practically, verse 42 steadies everyone denied for truth: the parent mocked for raising children Islamically, the youth ridiculed for modesty, the scholar dismissed for orthodoxy, the activist slandered for justice. The prophetic response is threefold: patience (*ṣabr*) without retaliation, persistence without despair, and trust that God's help follows its appointed term. The Prophet ﷺ, denied for thirteen Makkan years, answered denial with more invitation, more prayer, more character — until deniers became disciples. Whoever is denied today stands in Noah's, Hūd's, and Ṣāliḥ's company — and their ends, recorded in rubble from Iraq to Arabia, testify where denial leads and where patience arrives.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:43

> **and the people of Abraham, and the people of Lot,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham's and Lot's Peoples as Further Witnesses**

*Wa qawmu Ibrāhīma wa qawmu Lūṭ* — "and the people of Abraham, and the people of Lot." The catalogue of deniers continues: Abraham's Chaldean folk (Nimrod's Babylon) and Lot's Sodomite cities. Al-Ṭabarī notes the pairing's significance: Abraham and Lot were kin (nephew) and contemporaries, preaching simultaneously in Mesopotamia and the Jordan valley — two lights rejected in one generation. Abraham's people denied despite cosmic reasoning (the star, moon, sun argument — 6:76–79) and the miracle of cool fire (21:69); Lot's people denied despite his hospitality's plea, persisting in unprecedented immorality. The Qur'an records their ends:

> *"And We delivered him and Lot unto the land which We blessed for the worlds."* (Qur'an 21:71 — Abraham's deliverance)

> *"And We rained upon them a rain [of stones]. See how was the end of the guilty!"* (Qur'an 7:84 — Lot's people's destruction)

**Two Modes of Rejection**

Abraham's folk represent intellectual denial: they debated, demanded proofs, then resorted to the catapult when arguments failed — reason abandoned for violence. Lot's folk represent moral denial: they did not merely disbelieve but criminalized virtue, threatening to expel Lot's household for "purifying themselves" (27:56–57) — vice persecuting chastity. Al-Rāzī observes that Quraysh combined both: debating the Prophet by day (like Nimrod's court) and mocking his morals as backwardness (like Sodom). Ibn Kathīr notes the verse's brevity is eloquence: two names evoke entire narratives the audience knew — no elaboration needed, for ruins and scriptures testified.

The Prophet ﷺ invoked Abraham's precedent when his own kin threatened:

> *"The best speech I and the prophets before me have said..."* — and at Uḥud's wounds he prayed for his folk's guidance rather than Abraham's fire upon them (al-Bukhārī 3477, on the Prophet wiping blood and praying "forgive my people, for they know not").

**Intimacy of the Rejected**

Both Abraham and Lot were rejected by their nearest: Abraham by his father Āzar ("I will stone thee" — 19:46), Lot by his wife (who betrayed him — 66:10). Al-Qurṭubī draws the consolation for the Prophet ﷺ, rejected by uncles and clansmen: prophethood often divides households before uniting nations. The Qur'an elsewhere acknowledges this pain:

> *"And warn thy nearest kin."* (Qur'an 26:214 — the hardest audience)

Modern callers find solace: parental opposition, spousal mockery, familial boycott for faith's sake place them in Abraham's and Lot's households — honored company. Persistence with kindness, not rupture, is the prophetic way: Abraham prayed for his father (19:47), Lot sheltered his guests despite his wife, and Muhammad ﷺ honored his persecuting kin until they embraced or perished.

**Ruins as Reminders**

Archaeology and scripture converge: Babylon's mounds and the Dead Sea's desolation remain visible warnings. The Qur'an commands traveling to witness them (v. 46 will elaborate). For the modern reader, Abraham's people warn against idolizing heritage ("we found our fathers worshipping" — 21:53) and power (Nimrod's "I give life and death" — 2:258); Lot's people warn against normalizing immorality and persecuting purity. Both warn Quraysh — and every subsequent denier — that rejecting a merciful warner invites a severe reckoning, while the warner himself is delivered and blessed.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:44

> **and the inhabitants of Midian, and Moses too was denied. But I granted the disbelievers a respite; then I seized them in punishment. And how, then, was the change I wrought!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Midian and Moses Complete the Catalogue**

*Wa aṣḥābu Madyana wa kudhdhiba Mūsā* — "and the inhabitants of Midian, and Moses too was denied." Midian (Shuʿayb's people) were traders who cheated measures and robbed highways — economic corruption wedded to idolatry. Moses was denied by Pharaoh's Egypt — imperial arrogance wedded to deification ("I am your lord most high" — 79:24). Al-Ṭabarī notes the catalogue's span: from Noah's flood to Moses' sea, every major messenger tasted denial — seven communities in three verses (Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Abraham, Lot, Midian, Moses). The implication for Quraysh: you are the eighth in a sequence whose every prior member was destroyed. The Qur'an elsewhere summarizes:

> *"And how many a generation have We destroyed before them!"* (Qur'an 19:74)

Ibn Kathīr observes that Moses' mention without his people's name ("Moses was denied" rather than "Pharaoh's folk") universalizes: all who denied Moses — Pharaoh, Hāmān, Qārūn, and rebellious Israelites — share the verdict.

**Respite Before Seizure**

*Fa-amlaytu li-l-kāfirīna thumma akhadhtuhum* — "But I granted the disbelievers a respite; then I seized them." *Imlāʾ* (*m-l-w*) means to extend time, to grant delay — God prolonged their prosperity despite denial. *Akhdh* (seizure) is sudden gripping — flood, wind, quake, cry, sea. Al-Rāzī explains the wisdom of delay: respite completes proof (no excuse of haste), allows repentance (mercy's door), and lets the persists fill their measure (justice's fullness). The Qur'an elsewhere warns against misreading delay as approval:

> *"And let not those who disbelieve suppose that Our granting them respite is good for them. We grant them respite only that they may increase in sin."* (Qur'an 3:178)

The Prophet ﷺ illustrated:

> *"God grants respite to the wrongdoer, but when He seizes him, He does not release him." Then he recited: 'Thus is thy Lord's seizing when He seizes towns while they do wrong.'"* (al-Bukhārī 4686; Muslim 2583, from Abū Mūsā)

**How Was the Change I Wrought!**

*Fa-kayfa kāna nakīr* — "And how, then, was the change I wrought!" *Nakīr* (*n-k-r*) denotes forceful rejection, punitive alteration — God's "disapproval" manifested as overturning: seas drowning armies, earth swallowing cities, winds leveling palaces. The interrogative *kayfa* (how) is rhetorical amazement: how total, how sudden, how exemplary was the requital! Al-Qurṭubī notes the verse invites witnessing: travel and see Pharaoh's drowned host, Thamūd's carved emptiness, Midian's desolation — *nakīr* written in stone. The Qur'an elsewhere challenges:

> *"Have they not journeyed upon the earth and seen how was the end of those before them?"* (Qur'an 30:9; cf. v. 46)

**Respite as Mercy and as Test**

The verse speaks to every age of apparent impunity: tyrants prospering, mockers thriving, oppressors unpunished. Respite is not absence but patience — God's *ḥilm* (forbearance) giving room for return. For the oppressed, this counsels hope without vigilantism: God will seize in His time. For the prosperous disobedient, it counsels fear without complacency: delay increases either repentance's reward or persistence's punishment. Practically, believers should use their own respite — health, wealth, time — for repentance before seizure, and interpret others' respite as mercy's delay, not justice's failure. *Nakīr* came to seven peoples; it awaits every persistent eighth — unless they heed the warner their predecessors denied.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:45

> **How many a town have We destroyed while it did wrong, laying thus fallen upon their roofs! And [how many] an abandoned well and lofty palace!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Towns Destroyed While Doing Wrong**

*Fa-kaʾayyin min qaryatin ahlaknāhā wa hiya ẓālimah* — "How many a town have We destroyed while it did wrong." *Kaʾayyin* (how many) signals multitude beyond counting — the catalogue of verses 42–44 was illustrative, not exhaustive. *Qaryah* (town, city) denotes settled civilization — markets, courts, temples — destroyed *wa hiya ẓālimah* (while it did wrong): destruction met them mid-injustice, mid-idolatry, mid-arrogance. Al-Ṭabarī stresses the causative link: wrongdoing (*ẓulm*) invited destruction; prosperity without justice is a town awaiting its term. The Qur'an elsewhere states the law:

> *"And We never destroy towns save that their people are wrongdoers."* (Qur'an 28:59)

Ibn Kathīr lists the wrongs: idolatry, cheating, oppression, mocking messengers — each town specialized, all shared ruin.

**Fallen Upon Their Roofs**

*Fa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā* — "laying thus fallen upon their roofs!" *Khāwiyah* means empty, desolate, collapsed — walls caved, roofs pancaked, streets silent. *ʿAlā ʿurūshihā* (upon their roofs) depicts inversion: roofs that sheltered now buried — ceilings become floors of rubble. Al-Rāzī notes the image's vividness for Arabian travelers who passed ruined Nabatean, Sabaean, and Thamūdic cities — columns prostrate, lintels kissing dust. The Qur'an elsewhere evokes the same desolation:

> *"Or like him who passed by a town fallen upon its roofs..."* (Qur'an 2:259)

Archaeology confirms: Pompeii's casts, Babylon's mounds, Petra's emptiness — *khāwiyah ʿalā ʿurūshihā* in stone and ash.

**Abandoned Wells and Lofty Palaces**

*Wa biʾrin muʿaṭṭalah wa qaṣrin mashīd* — "And [how many] an abandoned well and lofty palace!" *Biʾr muʿaṭṭalah* is a deserted well — once life's source, now unvisited; *qaṣr mashīd* is a towering, plastered palace — once power's seat, now uninhabited. Al-Qurṭubī explains the pairing: wells symbolize sustenance-economy, palaces symbolize authority-polity — both abandoned when their people perished. Wells without drawers, palaces without princes: provision and power outlast their abusers as mute witnesses. The Qur'an elsewhere ironizes such abandonment:

> *"How many a garden and spring they left behind, and sown fields and noble stations!"* (Qur'an 44:25–26, on Pharaoh's folk)

**Ruins as Sermons**

The verse transforms tourism into theology: every ruin preaches. The abandoned well asks: "Where are those who drank?" The lofty palace asks: "Where is he who commanded?" The collapsed roof answers: "Wrongdoing felled us." Modern travelers photographing Petra, Palmyra, or rust-belt factories should hear verse 45: civilizations are mortal, and injustice accelerates death. For believers, the lesson is civic: build towns on justice lest they become *qaryah ẓālimah* — feed the poor (lest wells be abandoned), shelter the weak (lest palaces mock), heed warners (lest roofs collapse). And personally: the body is a town, the heart its well, ambition its palace — wrongdoing desolates all three, while faith maintains them for eternity.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:46

> **Have they not journeyed upon the earth, that they might have hearts by which to understand or ears by which to hear? Truly it is not the eyes that go blind, but it is hearts within breasts that go blind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Journeying for Hearts and Ears**

*A-fa-lam yasīrū fī al-arḍi fa-takūna lahum qulūbun yaʿqilūna bihā aw ādhānun yasmaʿūna bihā* — "Have they not journeyed upon the earth, that they might have hearts by which to understand or ears by which to hear?" *Sayr* (journeying) here means purposeful travel — trade-caravans passing ruins, pilgrims crossing histories — not tourism for selfies but inquiry for insight. The purpose clause (*fa-takūna* — "that they might have") makes travel an epistemology: hearts that *yaʿqilūn* (understand, literally "bind" knowledge) and ears that *yasmaʿūn* (hear receptively). Al-Rāzī notes the Qur'anic physiology: understanding is attributed to hearts (*qulūb*), not merely brains — the heart is the seat of comprehension, resolve, and moral perception. The Qur'an elsewhere commands such travel:

> *"Say: Journey upon the earth and see how was the end of the guilty."* (Qur'an 27:69)

Quraysh's own caravans to Syria passed Thamūd's carved mountains and Lot's drowned valley — evidence beneath their camels' feet.

**It Is Not Eyes That Go Blind**

*Fa-innahā lā taʿmā al-abṣār* — "Truly it is not the eyes that go blind." Physical eyesight, however keen, perceives only surfaces — ruins as stones, history as dates. Many blind men (like Ibn Umm Maktūm, honored in Sūrah 80) saw truth clearly, while many sighted chiefs stumbled. Al-Ṭabarī explains the consolation: the Prophet ﷺ should not grieve that deniers "see" miracles yet disbelieve — their eyes function, but sight without insight is blindness. The Prophet ﷺ prayed for true seeing:

> *"O God, show us truth as truth and grant us its following, and show us falsehood as falsehood and grant us its avoidance."* (cf. Duʿāʾ attributed in tafsīr literature; meaning supported by Qur'an 8:29 on discernment)

**But Hearts Within Breasts Go Blind**

*Wa lākin taʿmā al-qulūbu allatī fī al-ṣudūr* — "but it is hearts within breasts that go blind." *ʿAmā al-qalb* (heart-blindness) is the inability to perceive truth, heed warnings, or feel awe — a cataract of arrogance, desire, and habit. *Allatī fī al-ṣudūr* (within breasts) locates it precisely: not in texts (which are clear) but in chests (which are sealed). Al-Qurṭubī lists its causes: persistent sin rusting the heart (83:14), arrogance sealing it (40:35), and worldliness distracting it. Ibn Kathīr cites the Hadith of the heart's polish:

> *"When the servant sins, a black spot appears on his heart; if he repents, it is polished away, but if he persists, it spreads until it covers the heart — that is the rust God mentions."* (al-Tirmidhī 3334; Ibn Mājah 4244, from Abū Hurayrah; graded fair)

**Ophthalmology of the Soul**

Verse 46 is the Qur'an's diagnosis of modern blindness: eyes scanning screens 20/20, hearts unable to see purpose, death, or God. Information abounds; insight starves. The cure is journeying — literally (visiting ruins, graves, hospitals, battlefields that preach) and metaphorically (traveling through scripture, history, self-examination) — until hearts understand and ears hear. Practically, the believer prescribes himself heart-optics: daily Qur'an with reflection (polish), weekly grave or hospital visits (perspective), nightly self-audit (diagnosis), and constant supplication for *baṣīrah* (insight). For eyes will close in death, but hearts seeing now will see forever — and hearts blind now will be raised blind then (20:124–125).

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:47

> **They bid thee to hasten the punishment, though God will not fail His Promise. And truly a day with your Lord is as a thousand years of that which you reckon.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bidding to Hasten Punishment**

*Wa yastaʿjilūnaka bi-l-ʿadhāb* — "They bid thee to hasten the punishment." The deniers, hearing warnings of seizure (vv. 44–45), mocked: "Bring the punishment now, if you are truthful!" — mistaking delay for falsehood. Al-Ṭabarī records Quraysh's taunts at the Prophet ﷺ, paralleling Thamūd's "bring what thou threatenest" (7:77) and Noah's folk's impatience. The Qur'an elsewhere captures their challenge:

> *"And they say, 'When is this promise, if you are truthful?'"* (Qur'an 10:48, and throughout)

Al-Rāzī notes the folly: they hasten what they cannot bear — like children demanding fire to prove it burns. God's response is twofold: certainty of promise and difference of timing.

**God Will Not Fail His Promise**

*Wa lan yukhlifa Allāhu waʿdah* — "though God will not fail His Promise." *Ikhlāf* (failing, breaking) is impossible for God — His promise of requital, like His promise of mercy, is unbreakable. *Lan* (never) is absolute negation: no delay, intercession, or obstacle can void it. Al-Qurṭubī explains the theology: divine promises differ from human ones — men break promises from inability, forgetfulness, or regret; God is Able, All-Knowing, and All-Wise, so His promise is certain, only its *when* is wise. The Qur'an affirms:

> *"Truly God fails not His promise."* (Qur'an 3:9; 13:31; 39:20)

Badr fulfilled the promise for Quraysh's mockers within years; the Hour will fulfill it for all mockers in time.

**A Day as a Thousand Years**

*Wa inna yawman ʿinda rabbika ka-alfi sanatin mimmā taʿuddūn* — "And truly a day with your Lord is as a thousand years of that which you reckon." *ʿInda rabbik* (with your Lord) signals divine measure versus human reckoning (*taʿuddūn* — you count). Al-Ṭabarī explains: what seems long delay to men (years of respite) is brief in God's span — punishment's "tomorrow" is nearly "today." Other verses mention fifty thousand years (70:4) for the Day's length — different measures for different contexts (respite's day vs. reckoning's Day), all teaching temporal relativity. Ibn Kathīr notes the consolation: believers need not despair at injustice's persistence — a thousand years of tyranny are a day to God, and His promise outlasts all tyrants.

**Impatience as Disbelief's Symptom**

Hastening punishment reveals two diseases: doubt (demanding immediacy as proof) and arrogance (dictating God's schedule). Believers suffer the inverse impatience — hastening relief — and the verse treats both: God's timing is perfect, His promise certain. The Prophet ﷺ counseled the hasty:

> *"You will be answered so long as you do not hasten, saying, 'I supplicated and was not answered.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6340; Muslim 2735, from Abū Hurayrah)

Modern believers watching oppression's longevity — occupations enduring decades, tyrants dying in beds — should reckon by God's calendar: a day as a thousand years means no crime escapes, no tear is forgotten, and no promise fails. The mockers' "when?" will be answered; the believers' "how long?" will be comforted — for with the Lord, justice is never late, only appointed.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:48

> **And how many a town did I grant respite while it did wrong. Then I seized it [in punishment], and unto Me is the journey’s end!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Respite Granted to Wrongdoing Towns**

*Wa kaʾayyin min qaryatin amlaytu lahā wa hiya ẓālimah* — "And how many a town did I grant respite while it did wrong." The verse echoes verse 45's *kaʾayyin* (how many) and verse 44's *amlaytu* (I granted respite), weaving prior threads: many towns, prolonged delay, persistent wrongdoing. The first-person *amlaytu* (I granted) personalizes respite as God's direct act — not nature's indifference but mercy's extension. Al-Rāzī asks why respite accompanies wrongdoing rather than ending it. His answer: respite tests both the wrongdoers (will prosperity soften or harden?) and the believers (will delay shake or strengthen faith?). The Qur'an elsewhere explains:

> *"And We grant them respite only that they may increase in sin."* (Qur'an 3:178 — for the persistent)

> *"And thy Lord is the Forgiving, Possessed of Mercy. Were He to seize them for what they earned, He would hasten the punishment for them. But they have an appointment."* (Qur'an 18:58)

**Then I Seized It**

*Thumma akhadhtuhā* — "Then I seized it." *Thumma* (then) marks respite's end: delay terminates in gripping. *Akhdh* (seizure) is sudden, inescapable — the flood at dawn, the cry at morning, the quake at night. Al-Ṭabarī notes the singular pronoun (*-hā*, it) treating each town as a unit: collective wrongdoing invites collective seizure, though individuals are judged singly in the Hereafter. Ibn Kathīr lists the seizures: Noah's drowning, ʿĀd's wind, Thamūd's blast, Lot's overturning, Midian's quake, Pharaoh's sea — each *akhdh* tailored to its town's arrogance (sea-folk drowned, star-gazers blasted, builders buried).

The Prophet ﷺ warned against mistaking respite for safety:

> *"When you see God granting a servant what he loves of this world despite his sins, it is only enticement (istidrāj)." Then he recited: 'When they forgot what they were reminded of, We opened for them the gates of all things... then We seized them suddenly.'"* (Aḥmad 17311, from ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir; graded fair; cf. Qur'an 6:44)

**Unto Me Is the Journey's End**

*Wa ilayya al-maṣīr* — "and unto Me is the journey's end!" *Maṣīr* (*ṣ-y-r*) is the destination, the final becoming — all journeys terminate with God. Al-Qurṭubī explains the comfort and the threat: comfort for exiled believers (their hijrah ends with God), threat for seized towns (their ruin is not annihilation but transfer to judgment). The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"And unto God is the journey's end."* (Qur'an 3:28; 5:18; 24:42)

Every town's history — rise, wrongdoing, respite, seizure — concludes before God, where mayors and mobs, prophets and persecutors stand equal.

**Reading Our Towns by This Verse**

Modern cities mirror verse 48's towns: wrongdoing normalized (usury, exploitation, immorality), respite enjoyed (skylines rising, markets booming), seizure unimagined. Believers should read urban prosperity with two eyes: gratitude for provision, vigilance against *istidrāj*. Civic duty follows: warn towns through reform before seizure — enjoin justice, forbid corruption, shelter the oppressed — for collective repentance can avert collective fate (as Nineveh's did — 10:98). And personally: each soul is a town granted respite while wronging itself; *thumma akhadhtuhā* awaits every unrepentant life, and *ilayya al-maṣīr* summons all. Use respite for return, before seizure makes return impossible.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:49

> **Say, “O mankind! I am only a clear warner unto you!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warner's Commission**

*Qul yā ayyuhā al-nāsu innamā anā lakum nadhīrun mubīn* — "Say, 'O mankind! I am only a clear warner unto you!'" *Qul* (say) commissions the Prophet ﷺ to announce his office. *Yā ayyuhā al-nās* (O mankind) universalizes it: his warning addresses not Quraysh alone but humanity — the Makkan merchant and the distant farmer, the first century and the last. *Innamā* (only, merely) delimits: his role is warning, not compelling, enriching, or hastening punishment. *Nadhīr* (*n-dh-r*) is a warner — one who alerts of impending danger; *mubīn* (clear, manifest) describes both his clarity (plain Arabic, evident proofs) and his openness (public, unhidden). Al-Ṭabarī notes the verse answers those who demanded punishment's hastening (v. 47) or miracles on order: the Prophet brings warning, not inventory — belief is yours, requital is God's.

The Qur'an elsewhere defines this office:

> *"Thou art only a warner, and for every people there is a guide."* (Qur'an 13:7)

> *"And I am only a clear warner."* (Qur'an 15:89; 29:50; 67:26 — repeated across sūrahs)

**Warning as Mercy**

Worldly minds hear "warner" as threat; revelation means it as mercy. The warner (*nadhīr*) is like a lighthouse-keeper, a physician diagnosing cancer early, a sentry spotting raiders — his alarm is salvation for whoever heeds. Al-Rāzī asks why prophets are called warners more than bearers of glad tidings here. His answer: the context is denial and hastening — those racing toward cliffs need warnings before congratulations. Yet warning implies rescue: God warns because He wills to save, not to destroy. The Prophet ﷺ illustrated his mission:

> *"My parable and yours is like a man who kindled a fire, and moths fell into it, and he pushes them back — and I am grasping your waistbands from the Fire."* (al-Bukhārī 6483; Muslim 2284, from Jābir and Abū Hurayrah)

Ibn Kathīr notes the clarity (*mubīn*): the Prophet ﷺ warned plainly — describing Hell's garments (v. 19), boiling (v. 20), and eternity — leaving no ambiguity about stakes.

**"Only" as Liberation**

*Innamā* (only) liberates both warner and warned. It liberates the Prophet ﷺ from responsibility for results: he delivers, God guides — rejection does not indict him (as vv. 42–44 proved through predecessors). It liberates hearers from intermediaries: no priesthood, no secrecy, no esoteric rites — a clear warner in plain speech. And it liberates callers after him: every parent, teacher, and preacher inherits *nadhīr mubīn* in miniature — warn clearly, then trust God. The Prophet ﷺ charged his community:

> *"Convey from me, even a verse."* (al-Bukhārī 3461, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr)

Al-Qurṭubī derives the ethic: warning must be *mubīn* — clear in content (scripture, not opinion), clear in language (understandable, not obscure), clear in motive (compassion, not contempt).

**Heeding the Clear Warner Today**

Modern mankind faces warners of lesser perils — climate scientists, physicians, economists — heeded urgently, while the clear warner's warnings of eternal loss are dismissed as premodern. Verse 49 reverses the priority: the greatest danger deserves the clearest warning and the swiftest heeding. Practically, believers should amplify the warning lovingly — sharing verses of reckoning with family, reminding friends of accountability, warning gently without harshness — and heed it personally: every *nadhīr* verse is addressed *lakum* (unto you). The warner has warned clearly; the Fire he described is real; the Gardens he promised are near — and mankind has no excuse after a *nadhīrun mubīn*.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:50

> **And as for those who believe and perform righteous deeds, theirs shall be forgiveness and a generous provision.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faith and Righteous Deeds Rewarded**

*Fa-alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti lahum maghfiratun wa rizqun karīm* — "And as for those who believe and perform righteous deeds, theirs shall be forgiveness and a generous provision." The warner's message (v. 49) bifurcates: this verse for responders, the next for resisters. *Īmān* (belief) plus *ʿamal ṣāliḥ* (righteous deeds) — the Qur'an's constant pair — yields two rewards: *maghfirah* (forgiveness) covering past sins, and *rizq karīm* (generous provision) bestowing future bounty. Al-Ṭabarī explains the order: forgiveness first (clearing debts), provision second (granting gifts) — pardon precedes paradise. *Karīm* (generous, noble) describes provision honorable in quantity, quality, and manner — without stint, toil, or humiliation. The Qur'an elsewhere details:

> *"And forgiveness from thy Lord and a Garden whose breadth is the heavens and the earth."* (Qur'an 3:133)

> *"And therein they shall have pure spouses and abide forever."* (Qur'an 2:25)

Ibn Kathīr notes *rizq karīm* encompasses Paradise's food, drink, dwellings, and supreme provision — God's pleasure and vision.

**Forgiveness as the First Provision**

*Maghfirah* (*gh-f-r*), from the helmet (*mighfar*) covering the head, denotes God's covering sins from exposure and shielding from punishment. Al-Rāzī observes that forgiveness is mentioned before provision because even Paradise would be joyless with guilt — the forgiven enter delight unburdened. The Prophet ﷺ quantified this mercy:

> *"God extends His Hand by night for the sinner of the day, and by day for the sinner of the night, until the sun rises from the west."* (Muslim 2759, from Abū Mūsā)

> *"None enters Paradise by his deeds." They said, "Not even thou?" He said, "Not even I, unless God covers me with mercy."* (al-Bukhārī 5673; Muslim 2816, from Abū Hurayrah)

Thus *maghfirah* is not earned wages but gifted covering — deeds are the plea, mercy the verdict.

**Generous Provision Versus Worldly Wages**

*Rizq karīm* contrasts with worldly provision (*rizq*) that is meager, mixed, and mortal: wages delayed, food spoiling, wealth envied, health fading. Paradisal provision is *karīm* — noble in source (God's hand), abundant in measure, pure in pleasure, eternal in duration. Al-Qurṭubī cites the Hadith:

> *"The first group entering Paradise... their food's flavor never changes, their vessels are gold and silver, their sweat is musk."* (al-Bukhārī 3245; Muslim 2834, from Abū Hurayrah)

The Qur'an elsewhere promises:

> *"A generous provision" for the sincere.* (Qur'an 37:40–42; 8:74 — the same phrase for emigrants and strivers)

**Working for Forgiveness and Generosity**

Practically, verse 50 orders priorities: seek forgiveness before provision — repent daily, for pardon precedes plenty. Then work righteously: prayer, charity, honesty, kindness — each deed a seed of *rizq karīm*. The verse also comforts the impoverished righteous: worldly *rizq* may be scant, but *karīm* provision awaits — God's generosity is deferred, not denied. And it warns the wealthy wicked: worldly abundance without faith is not *karīm* but *istidrāj* (enticement). The believer's economics are eschatological: invest deeds now, harvest forgiveness and generosity then — a trade whose profit is guaranteed by the clear warner's Lord.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:51

> **But those who endeavor to thwart Our signs, they shall be the inhabitants of Hellfire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Endeavoring to Thwart God's Signs**

*Wa alladhīna saʿaw fī āyātinā muʿājizīn* — "But those who endeavor to thwart Our signs." *Saʿā* (*s-ʿ-y*) means to strive, to endeavor — the same verb as ḥajj's *saʿy* between Ṣafā and Marwah. Here striving is inverted: effort against God rather than for Him. *Muʿājizīn* (*ʿ-j-z*) denotes those trying to incapacitate, to outstrip, to render God's signs powerless — as though they could outrun revelation. Al-Ṭabarī explains: they strive to refute the Qur'an, mock its reciters, obstruct its spread, and "defeat" its arguments — imagining God's signs can be disabled. The Qur'an elsewhere describes their tactics:

> *"And those who disbelieve say, 'Listen not to this Qur'an, and make noise therein, that haply you may overcome.'"* (Qur'an 41:26)

Ibn Kathīr notes the verse follows verse 50's promise as its antithesis: believers act righteously toward reward; thwarters strive hostilely toward fire.

**Companions of Hellfire**

*Ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-jaḥīm* — "they shall be the inhabitants of Hellfire." *Aṣḥāb* (companions) implies intimate, enduring association — Hell is not their visit but their company, their home, their fellowship. *Al-Jaḥīm* (*j-ḥ-m*), from blazing fiercely, is Hell's raging aspect — fire fully stoked. Al-Rāzī notes the contrast with verse 50's *lahum* (theirs shall be): believers *receive* forgiveness as gift; thwarters *belong* to Hell as companions — reward is granted, punishment is earned through companionship with hostility. The Qur'an elsewhere pairs striving with Hell:

> *"And those who strive against Our signs, seeking to thwart — for them is a painful punishment of filth."* (Qur'an 34:5)

**Why Thwarting Deserves Hell**

Classical scholars explain the proportionality: thwarting God's signs is not private disbelief but public warfare — misleading others (v. 9), barring mosques (v. 25), persecuting believers (v. 39). Such striving multiplies sin across victims: every obstructed convert, every mocked verse, every burned scripture accrues. The Prophet ﷺ warned:

> *"Whoever calls to misguidance bears its sin and the sins of whoever follows him."* (Muslim 2674, from Abū Hurayrah)

Al-Qurṭubī adds that *muʿājizīn* includes those who declare the Qur'an outdated, who legislate against its rulings, who persecute its teachers — every age's thwarters share one companionship. Conversely, those who strive *for* God's signs — teaching, defending, embodying them — share the opposite company: the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs (4:69).

**From Thwarting to Serving Signs**

The verse invites self-audit: does my striving serve or thwart God's signs? The tongue mocking a bearded brother or veiled sister thwarts; the hand sharing doubts without seeking answers thwarts; the wealth funding falsehood thwarts; the vote criminalizing worship thwarts. Repentance reverses striving: the former mocker becomes a defender, the former obstructor a facilitator — as ʿUmar transformed from burying Islam to carrying it. Practically, believers should strive *fī āyātinā* (in Our signs) positively: memorizing, understanding, teaching, funding Qur'an schools, beautifying recitation. The same *saʿy* (endeavor) that earns Hell when directed against signs earns Paradise when directed for them — effort is neutral, direction is destiny.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:52

> **And no messenger or prophet did We send before thee, but that when he had a longing, Satan would cast into his longing, whereupon God effaces what Satan cast. Then God makes firm His signs—and God is Knowing, Wise—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Every Prophet's Longing and Satan's Casting**

*Wa mā arsalnā min qablika min rasūlin wa lā nabiyyin illā idhā tamannā alqā al-shayṭānu fī umniyyatih* — "And no messenger or prophet did We send before thee, but that when he had a longing, Satan would cast into his longing." *Tamannā* (*m-n-y*) means to wish, to long — also, secondarily, to recite. *Umniyyah* is the wish, desire, hope — classically, the prophet's longing that his people believe and be guided. *Alqā al-shayṭān* (Satan cast) denotes insinuation: whispering doubts into hearers' hearts, interpolating falsehoods into audiences' perceptions, or suggesting compromises to the prophet's human hopes. Al-Rāzī and the majority explain: prophets longed for their peoples' guidance; Satan exploited such longing by casting doubts, distractions, and false interpretations among listeners — not by corrupting revelation itself, which God guards (15:9).

**The "Story of the Cranes" and Its Rejection**

Some histories attach to this verse the so-called "story of the cranes" (*gharānīq*) — claiming Satan interpolated idol-praising verses into the Prophet's ﷺ recitation of Sūrah 53, later effaced. Mainstream scholars — al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Māturīdī — rejected this decisively. Al-Rāzī argued: God declares prophets protected in conveyance (*mablagh*), Satan has no authority over God's servants (15:42), and the alleged interpolation contradicts Sūrah 53's own condemnation of intercession (53:26) and daughters (53:21–23). Ibn Kathīr ruled its chains weak and its content impossible (*munkar*), violating prophetic infallibility (*ʿiṣmah*) in revelation. Al-Qurṭubī held that *tamannā* here means "longed" (for guidance), not "recited" — Satan casts into listeners' understanding, not into the text. The verse's own sequence confirms: God effaces Satan's casting *then* firms His signs — revelation remains pristine throughout.

**God Effaces and Makes Firm**

*Fa-yansakhu Allāhu mā yulqī al-shayṭān* — "whereupon God effaces what Satan casts." *Naskh* (*n-s-kh*) here means to remove, to wipe away — linguistic effacing, not legal abrogation (though the same root). *Thumma yuḥkimu Allāhu āyātih* — "Then God makes firm His signs": *iḥkām* is to fortify, to perfect — revelation emerges clarified, dúvidas dispelled, falsehoods exposed. Al-Ṭabarī explains the divine pattern: Satanic insinuation becomes the occasion for greater clarity — doubts raised are doubts answered, confusions sown are distinctions reaped. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms this fortification:

> *"A Book whose signs are made firm (uḥkimat), then elaborated."* (Qur'an 11:1)

*Wa Allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm* — "and God is Knowing, Wise": Knowing Satan's castings and servants' hearts, Wise in permitting trial then manifesting truth.

**Longing, Whispering, and Firmness Today**

The verse consoles every sincere caller: longing for others' guidance is prophetic; encountering Satanic interference — doubts whispered to your audience, distortions of your words, compromises suggested to your hopes — is universal. The response is prophetic too: trust God's effacing and firming. Do not dilute truth to satisfy longing (the temptation Satan exploits); proclaim it purely, and God will efface distortions and firm understanding in receptive hearts. The Prophet ﷺ, grieved by rejection, was comforted similarly:

> *"Perhaps thou wouldst kill thyself with grief that they believe not."* (Qur'an 26:3)

Practically, when your good words are twisted, your intentions doubted, your message resisted — remember every prophet tasted this, Satan casts into every longing, and God effaces and firms. Long purely, speak truthfully, and leave effacing and firming to the Knowing, the Wise.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:53

> **that He might make what Satan casts to be a trial for those in whose hearts is a disease, and those whose hearts are hard—and truly the wrongdoers are in extreme schism—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Satanic Casting as Divine Trial**

*Li-yajʿala mā yulqī al-shayṭānu fitnatan li-alladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍun* — "that He might make what Satan casts to be a trial for those in whose hearts is a disease." *Fitnah* here is a sifting test — exposing hidden states. Al-Rāzī explains the wisdom: God permits Satan's whispering not from need but for discrimination — to distinguish sound hearts from sick ones. *Maraḍ al-qalb* (heart-disease) denotes hypocrisy, doubt, envy, lust — spiritual ailments distorting perception. The Qur'an elsewhere catalogues such hearts:

> *"In their hearts is a disease, so God increased their disease."* (Qur'an 2:10)

> *"And as for those in whose hearts is a disease, it increases them in filth upon filth."* (Qur'an 9:125)

Ibn Kathīr notes the mechanism: the same insinuation that the sound heart rejects as whispering, the diseased heart embraces as insight — trial reveals constitution.

**Hard Hearts and Extreme Schism**

*Wa al-qāsiyati qulūbuhum* — "and those whose hearts are hard." *Qaswah* (*q-s-w*) is hardness — hearts like stone, unmoved by warnings, unsoftened by mercy. The Qur'an elsewhere laments:

> *"Woe unto those whose hearts are hardened to the remembrance of God!"* (Qur'an 39:22)

> *"Then your hearts hardened after that, being like stones or even harder."* (Qur'an 2:74)

Al-Qurṭubī distinguishes disease from hardness: disease is remediable affliction (doubt treatable by knowledge), hardness is advanced petrifaction (truth bouncing off). Both fail Satan's trial — the diseased by infection, the hard by impenetrability. *Wa inna al-ẓālimīna la-fī shiqāqin baʿīd* — "and truly the wrongdoers are in extreme schism": *shiqāq* (*sh-q-q*) is splitting, fracturing — wrongdoers are split from truth, from believers, from their own fitrah — and *baʿīd* (far, extreme) marks the fracture's depth. The Qur'an uses the phrase for entrenched opposition (2:137; 41:52).

**Why God Permits the Trial**

Al-Ṭabarī addresses the underlying question: why permit Satan to cast at all? His answer, echoing the Qur'an's theodicy: without trial, faith and hypocrisy would be indistinguishable — assembly-lines of identical claimants. Satan's casting is the assayer's acid: gold gleams, dross blackens. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"Do men suppose they will be left saying 'We believe' without being tried?"* (Qur'an 29:2)

> *"And We tried them with good and evil, that haply they might return."* (Qur'an 7:168)

Ibn Taymiyyah added: trials also elevate the steadfast — resisting whispering increases reward, knowledge, and humility. The Prophet ﷺ reassured the whisper-afflicted:

> *"That is pure faith" — finding Satan's whispering grievous.* (Muslim 132, from Ibn Masʿūd: the Companions complained of unutterable thoughts, and he declared their distress itself faith)

**Diagnosing Hearts Before Trial Diagnoses Them**

Practically, verse 53 prescribes cardiac self-examination. Does my heart harbor disease — envy of the righteous, doubt about revelation, lust resisting restraint, hypocrisy performing for eyes? Is it hardening — unmoved by Qur'an, dry-eyed at reminders, rigid against counsel? The cure for disease is medicine: knowledge for doubt, contentment for envy, fasting for lust, sincerity for show. The cure for hardness is softening: weeping in supplication, visiting graves, serving the poor, remembering death. Whoever treats his heart before Satan's casting finds trial becomes triumph; whoever neglects it finds trial becomes exposure — and the wrongdoers, diseased and hardened, remain in extreme schism, split from mercy by their own fractures.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:54

> **and so that those who have been given knowledge might know that it is the truth from thy Lord, and thus believe in it, and that their hearts be humbled before Him. And truly God guides those who believe unto a straight path.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Knowledge Recognizing Truth**

*Wa li-yaʿlama alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma annahu al-ḥaqqu min rabbik* — "and so that those who have been given knowledge might know that it is the truth from thy Lord." While Satan's casting trials the diseased (v. 53), the same events enlighten the learned: contrast clarifies. *Ūtū al-ʿilm* (those given knowledge) denotes scholars of revelation, recipients of true learning — not mere information but God-granted insight. *Annahu al-ḥaqq* (that it is the truth): they recognize revelation's truth precisely through trials — seeing falsehood's whispering effaced and God's signs firmed (v. 52) confirms divine authorship. Al-Rāzī notes the epistemology: knowledge here is both prerequisite and product — prior learning enables recognition, and recognition deepens learning. The Qur'an elsewhere praises such knowers:

> *"Those who have been given knowledge see that what was sent down unto thee from thy Lord is the truth."* (Qur'an 34:6)

> *"Say: Believe in it or believe not; those given knowledge before it fall upon their faces prostrating when it is recited."* (Qur'an 17:107)

**Belief and Humility of Hearts**

*Fa-yuʾminū bihi fa-tukhbiṭa lahu qulūbuhum* — "and thus believe in it, and that their hearts be humbled before Him." *Fa-yuʾminū* (thus believe): knowledge matures into faith — assent with trust, not mere acknowledgment. *Fa-tukhbiṭa* (*kh-b-t*): hearts become lowly, tranquil, submissive — the same root as verse 34's *mukhbitīn* (humble). Al-Qushayrī notes the sequence: knowledge to faith to humility — learning that does not humble is not *ʿilm* but *maʿlūmāt* (data). True knowers tremble more, not less: the Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"Only those of His servants possessed of knowledge fear God."* (Qur'an 35:28)

Ibn Kathīr cites the Prophet's ﷺ awe as knowledge's fruit: though most knowledgeable, he prayed longest, wept most, feared most — humility proportioned to learning.

**Guidance unto a Straight Path**

*Wa inna Allāha la-hādī alladhīna āmanū ilā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm* — "And truly God guides those who believe unto a straight path." The emphatic *inna... la-hādī* (truly... surely guiding) guarantees believers' direction: through trials, whisperings, and confusions, God leads them straight. *Ṣirāṭ mustaqīm* (straight path) is Islam's essence — the Fātiḥah's daily plea (1:6) answered here. Al-Ṭabarī explains: guidance here means continued direction and firmness — believers are not merely shown the path once but led along it perpetually, past every Satanic casting. The Qur'an elsewhere promises:

> *"God guides those who believe to the truth of that wherein they differed, by His Leave."* (Qur'an 2:213)

**Becoming Among Those Given Knowledge**

The verse maps spiritual pedagogy: seek knowledge (*ūthū* implies receiving through effort and grace), recognize truth through trials, believe with commitment, humble the heart, and walk the straight path under divine guidance. Practically: study revelation with qualified teachers, reflect on confusions until clarity emerges (trials as tutors), convert learning into faith-acts, and monitor humility — if knowledge breeds arrogance, it is disease, not *ʿilm*. The Prophet ﷺ sought knowledge that humbles:

> *"O God, I seek refuge in Thee from knowledge that does not benefit."* (Muslim 2722, from Zayd ibn Arqam)

Modern information-gluttons should heed: terabytes without humility are weight, not wings. Whoever learns, believes, and humbles himself will find, as promised, God's guidance unto a straight path — through every whispering, past every trial, into truth from the Lord.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:55

> **Yet those who disbelieve remain in doubt over it, till the Hour comes upon them suddenly or there comes upon them the punishment of a barren day.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Persistent Doubt Until Suddenness**

*Wa lā yazālu alladhīna kafarū fī miryatin minhu* — "Yet those who disbelieve remain in doubt over it." *Lā yazāl* (cease not) denotes persistence: disbelievers abide in *miryah* (doubt, dispute) over the Qur'an — or over what Satan cast versus God's signs — never resolving, never resting. Al-Ṭabarī explains: their doubt is willful, not intellectual — evidence abounds, but allegiance to desire perpetuates questioning. The Qur'an elsewhere describes such doubt:

> *"And they were brought to doubt concerning that wherein they are in doubt."* (Qur'an 34:54, on persistent skeptics)

*Ḥattā taʾtiyahumu al-sāʿatu baghtatan* — "till the Hour comes upon them suddenly": *baghtah* is surprise — the Hour ambushes the doubting as death ambushes the heedless. Al-Rāzī notes the mercy within warning: doubt's deadline is the Hour — until then, resolution remains possible.

**The Punishment of a Barren Day**

*Aw yaʾtiyahum ʿadhābu yawmin ʿaqīm* — "or there comes upon them the punishment of a barren day." *ʿAqīm* (*ʿ-q-m*), from barrenness (a barren womb bearing nothing), describes a day yielding no good, no escape, no morrow — interpreted as the Day of Judgment (after which no days, no repentance) or as Badr (a day of no blessing for disbelievers, mothers bereaved). Al-Qurṭubī favors Judgment Day, supported by verse 56's *al-mulk yawmaʾidhin li-Allāh* (Sovereignty that Day shall be God's). The Qur'an elsewhere warns of suddenness:

> *"Shall they await aught but the Hour coming upon them suddenly, while they perceive not?"* (Qur'an 43:66; cf. 6:31; 21:40)

Ibn Kathīr notes the pairing: the Hour (universal) or a barren day (particular calamity) — doubt ends either in collective reckoning or personal catastrophe.

**Doubt as a Waystation, Not a Home**

Classical scholars distinguish transient doubt (*shakk* prompting inquiry) from settled doubt (*miryah* resisting answers). The former is a bridge to certainty — Abraham asked "show me" (2:260) and was shown. The latter is a residence — Pharaoh's folk doubted despite nine signs. Al-Rāzī diagnoses persistent doubt's roots: love of the world (certainty would demand sacrifice), pride (submission would humble), and bad company (doubters reinforcing doubters). The Prophet ﷺ counseled the transiently doubtful:

> *"Satan comes... until he says, 'Who created your Lord?' When it reaches that, let him seek refuge in God and stop."* (al-Bukhārī 3276; Muslim 134, from Abū Hurayrah)

Stopping means returning to evidence and worship, not spiraling.

**Resolving Doubt Before the Barren Day**

Practically, verse 55 urges doubters to decide: investigate sincerely — read the Qur'an wholly, ask qualified scholars, pray for guidance — and doubt will dissolve like dawn's mist. But postpone resolution, and doubt hardens into identity until suddenness decides: a diagnosis, a death, the Hour. Believers should also guard against manufactured doubt — algorithms feeding skepticism, forums rewarding cynicism — by anchoring in worship, community, and learning. For doubt unattended becomes *miryah*, *miryah* prolonged meets *baghtah*, and *baghtah* on a barren day yields no second chance. Resolve today what suddenness will resolve tomorrow.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:56

> **Sovereignty that Day shall be God’s; He will judge between them, and those who believe and perform righteous deeds shall be in Gardens of bliss.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sovereignty That Day Belongs to God**

*Al-mulku yawmaʾidhin li-Allāh* — "Sovereignty that Day shall be God's." *Mulk* (sovereignty, dominion) denotes absolute ownership and command. *Yawmaʾidhin* (that Day) is Judgment Day — when all rival claims dissolve. Al-Ṭabarī explains: in this world sovereignty appears shared — kings rule, judges decree, owners possess — but that Day appearances end: crowns fall, thrones empty, and one Voice asks:

> *"Whose is the sovereignty this Day? God's, the One, the Overwhelming."* (Qur'an 40:16)

Al-Rāzī notes the consolation: earthly courts may acquit tyrants and condemn saints, but that Day's Court has one Sovereign — no bribery, no bias, no appeal beyond Him. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"Master of the Day of Judgment."* (Qur'an 1:4)

**He Will Judge Between Them**

*Yaḥkumu baynahum* — "He will judge between them." *Ḥukm* is decisive judgment — settling every dispute: theological (which religion was true — v. 17), interpersonal (who wronged whom), internal (was faith sincere?). Al-Qurṭubī notes the universality of *baynahum* (between them): all six communities of verse 17, all adversaries of verse 19, all doubters and believers — judged together, distinguished precisely. The Qur'an details the judgment's precision:

> *"And We shall set up just balances for the Day of Resurrection, so no soul shall be wronged in aught, though it be the weight of a mustard seed."* (Qur'an 21:47)

Ibn Kathīr adds that judgment includes requital's execution — verdict and sentence in one divine act.

**Believers in Gardens of Bliss**

*Fa-alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti fī jannāti al-naʿīm* — "and those who believe and perform righteous deeds shall be in Gardens of bliss." *Jannāt al-naʿīm* (Gardens of bliss/delight) emphasizes experiential felicity — *naʿīm* is soft, lush comfort. The verse repeats faith-plus-deeds as bliss's key, answering verse 55's doubters: certainty acted upon yields delight. The Qur'an elsewhere describes such Gardens:

> *"Truly those who believe and perform righteous deeds — for them are Gardens of bliss, abiding therein; God's promise in truth."* (Qur'an 31:8–9)

The Prophet ﷺ described their least inhabitant:

> *"The lowest of Paradise's people in rank... will be told, 'Wish!' and he will wish, then be given ten times the world."* (al-Bukhārī 6571; Muslim 186, from Ibn Masʿūd)

**Living Under Coming Sovereignty**

Verse 56 reframes present power: every sovereignty is leased, soon repossessed. The executive signing orders, the judge pronouncing verdicts, the influencer commanding crowds — all will stand where *al-mulku li-Allāh* alone. This relativizes tyranny (its day ends) and dignifies obedience (its reward begins). Practically, believers live as subjects of the coming Sovereign: obeying Him over bosses, judging justly as His deputies, and investing in Gardens of bliss rather than gardens of vanity. Whoever acknowledges God's sole sovereignty that Day will find Him merciful; whoever contested it will find Him just — and His judgment between them will be perfect.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:57

> **And as for those who disbelieve and deny Our signs, theirs shall be a humiliating punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Disbelief Compounded by Denial**

*Wa alladhīna kafarū wa kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* — "And as for those who disbelieve and deny Our signs." *Kafara* (disbelieve) is to cover truth — ingratitude of the heart; *kadhdhaba* (deny, belie) is to call truth falsehood — aggression of the tongue. The pairing describes active rejection: not merely failing to believe but campaigning against belief — the thwarters of verse 51, the disputants of verses 3 and 8, the mockers of punishment's promise. Al-Ṭabarī notes the possessive *bi-āyātinā* (Our signs): they denied not ambiguous hints but God's own revealed verses and cosmic proofs — rejection of the Evident. The Qur'an elsewhere joins the pair:

> *"And those who disbelieve and deny Our signs — they shall be the inhabitants of the Fire."* (Qur'an 2:39; 5:10; 57:19)

Ibn Kathīr observes the symmetry with verse 56: believers-plus-deeds yield Gardens; disbelievers-plus-denial yield punishment — each harvest matches its sowing.

**A Humiliating Punishment**

*Fa-ulāʾika lahum ʿadhābun muhīn* — "theirs shall be a humiliating punishment." *Muhīn* (*h-w-n*), from humiliation, describes punishment that abases — not merely painful but degrading. Al-Rāzī explains the correspondence: disbelievers were arrogant (*mustakbirīn*) — twisting necks (v. 9), boasting might (like ʿĀd), demanding hastening (v. 47) — so their punishment humbles: dragged on faces (25:34), crowned with boiling (44:48–49), addressed with irony ("taste — thou art the mighty!"). The Qur'an elsewhere promises humiliation to the arrogant:

> *"Truly those who wax arrogant against My worship shall enter Hell abased."* (Qur'an 40:60)

Al-Qurṭubī notes the contrast with verse 56's *naʿīm* (bliss): bliss honors, humiliation dishonors — eternity amplifies earthly postures.

**Humility Now or Humiliation Then**

Classical scholars derive the verse's moral physics: honor flows from submission, humiliation from arrogance. Whoever humbles himself before God's signs — believing, obeying, trembling — is honored with Gardens. Whoever exalts himself above them — denying, mocking, thwarting — is humbled with fire. The Prophet ﷺ stated the law:

> *"Whoever humbles himself for God, God raises him."* (Muslim 2588, from Abū Hurayrah)

Its converse is verse 57: whoever exalts himself against God, God abases him. Pharaoh claimed "I am your lord most high" and drowned ignominiously; Abū Jahl swaggered as "the mighty" and fell at Badr's well; every tyrant's Mausoleum becomes a warning.

**Choosing Honor's Path**

Practically, verse 57 warns againstarrogance toward revelation: dismissing verses as outdated, mocking practices as backward, preferring opinion over text. Each is a seed of *kadhdhaba* (denial) risking *muhīn* (humiliation). The remedy is humble reception: "We hear and we obey" (2:285) — even when wisdom is hidden, even when desire objects. Modern believers pressured to apologize for revelation — hiding verses, diluting rulings — should remember: honor lies not in appeasing mockers but in honoring signs. Whoever honors God's signs now will be honored then; whoever humiliates them now will be humiliated then — a humiliating punishment for arrogant denial.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:58

> **And as for those who emigrate in the way of God and are then slain or die, God will surely provide them with a beautiful provision. And truly God is the best of providers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Emigration, Martyrdom, and Death**

*Wa alladhīna hājarū fī sabīli Allāhi thumma qutilū aw mātū* — "And as for those who emigrate in the way of God and are then slain or die." *Hijrah* (*h-j-r*) means to abandon — leaving homeland, kin, and comfort for God's sake. *Fī sabīli Allāh* (in God's way) qualifies: emigration for faith, not for fortune. *Qutilū aw mātū* (slain or die) encompasses martyrs (*shuhadāʾ*) and natural deaths after emigration — both honored. Al-Ṭabarī preserves the occasion: two Emigrants died naturally in Madinah, and some said martyrs outrank them; the verse equalizes — both emigrated, both are provided. The Qur'an elsewhere promises:

> *"Whosoever emigrates in God's way shall find much refuge and abundance on earth. And whosoever leaves his home emigrating unto God and His Messenger, then death overtakes him — his reward falls upon God."* (Qur'an 4:100)

Ibn Kathīr notes the verse's generality: every emigration for religion — from persecution, corruption, or temptation — qualifies, until the Hour.

**A Beautiful Provision**

*La-yarzuqannahumu Allāhu rizqan ḥasanā* — "God will surely provide them with a beautiful provision." The emphatic *la-yarzuqanna* (will surely provide) is oath-like assurance. *Rizq ḥasan* (beautiful provision) denotes Paradise's bounty — pure, abundant, delightful — and, by some commentators, worldly honor and sufficiency preceding it. Al-Rāzī notes the beauty (*ḥusn*): provision befitting sacrifice — they abandoned homes, so God grants mansions; abandoned kin, so God grants angelic company; abandoned livelihoods, so God grants unfailing bounty. The Qur'an elsewhere describes martyrs' provision:

> *"Rather, they are alive with their Lord, provided for."* (Qur'an 3:169)

The Prophet ﷺ detailed emigration's reward:

> *"Whoever emigrates for God and His Messenger, his emigration is unto God and His Messenger; whoever emigrates for worldly gain or a woman to marry, his emigration is unto what he emigrated for."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907, from ʿUmar — intention determining reward)

**God, the Best of Providers**

*Wa inna Allāha la-huwa khayru al-rāziqīn* — "And truly God is the best of providers." *Khayr al-rāziqīn* (best of providers) acknowledges apparent providers — employers, patrons, states — while affirming God's supremacy: He provides without need, without stint, without reminding. The phrase recurs for divine bounty (5:114; 23:72; 34:39; 62:11). Al-Qurṭubī explains the comfort: emigrants fear destitution — abandoning jobs, lands, networks — and God answers: the Best of Providers guarantees your *rizq*. History confirms: destitute Muhājirūn became governors, scholars, and saints — provided beautifully in both worlds.

**Emigration's Enduring Forms**

Though territorial hijrah from Makkah ended at its conquest ("no hijrah after the conquest" — al-Bukhārī 3900; Muslim 1864), scholars affirm its spirit endures: emigrating from sin to obedience, from bad company to righteous, from unlawful lands or livelihoods to lawful. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The emigrant is he who abandons what God forbade."* (al-Bukhārī 10; Muslim 40, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr)

Practically, every believer emigrates daily: leaving the bed for dawn prayer, the gossip-circle for remembrance, the doubtful income for the lawful. Whoever emigrates thus — then lives striving or dies hoping — is promised beautiful provision from the Best of Providers. Abandon for God, and God provides beautifully.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:59

> **He will surely cause them to enter an entrance with which they shall be content. And truly God is Knowing, Clement.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Entrance of Contentment**

*La-yudkhilannahum mudkhalan yarḍawnah* — "He will surely cause them to enter an entrance with which they shall be content." The emphatic *la-yudkhilanna* (will surely admit) continues verse 58's assurance to emigrants. *Mudkhal* (*d-kh-l*) denotes a place/act of entering — Paradise itself, or its gates, mansions, and stations. *Yarḍawnah* (with which they shall be content): *riḍā* (contentment) is the soul's complete satisfaction — no regret, no wish for otherwise. Al-Ṭabarī explains: unlike worldly entries — new cities with hardship, new homes with defects — this entrance pleases perfectly. The Qur'an elsewhere describes such mutual contentment:

> *"Return unto thy Lord, content (rāḍiyah), contenting (marḍiyyah). Enter among My servants. Enter My Garden."* (Qur'an 89:28–30)

Al-Rāzī notes the doubling: emigrants left homes contenting God despite discontenting kin; God admits them to homes contenting them eternally.

**God Is Knowing, Clement**

*Wa inna Allāha la-ʿalīmun ḥalīm* — "And truly God is Knowing, Clement." *ʿAlīm* (Knowing) affirms God's awareness of emigrants' sacrifices — hidden tears, secret fears, unrecorded losses — none escapes Him. *Ḥalīm* (Clement, Forbearing) affirms His gentleness: He does not hasten punishment for their shortcomings, does not diminish reward for their lapses. Al-Qurṭubī notes the pairing's comfort: Knowledge without clemency would terrify (every fault known), clemency without knowledge would ring hollow (forgiveness of the unknown); together they assure — He knows your struggles fully and treats you gently. The Qur'an elsewhere joins them:

> *"And God is Knowing, Clement."* (Qur'an 2:225; 3:155; 4:12 — oft-repeated for the repentant and tried)

**Contentment as Paradise's Essence**

Classical scholars reflect that *riḍā* (contentment) surpasses even Gardens' pleasures: palaces delight bodies, contentment delights souls. The Prophet ﷺ described God's supreme gift:

> *"God will say to Paradise's people: 'Are you content?' They will say, 'How should we not be content, having been given what none was given?' He will say, 'I grant you better: My pleasure (riḍwān) — I shall never be wrathful with you after it.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6549; Muslim 2829, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

Al-Qushayrī therefore called *riḍā* the emigrant's wage in kind: they were content with God's decree in exile (poverty, estrangement, danger), so God contents them with His abode. The Qur'an elsewhere promises:

> *"God is content with them, and they are content with Him."* (Qur'an 5:119; 9:100; 98:8)

**Entering Contentment Before Paradise**

Practically, verse 59 invites believers to practice entrance-contentment now: be content with God's apportioning — dwelling, income, health — as emigrants were content with Madinah's date-huts after Makkah's homes. Contentment (*riḍā*) does not preclude ambition but purifies it: strive for better while loving God's present decree. The Prophet ﷺ taught:

> *"Whoever is content with God as Lord, Islam as religion, and Muhammad as prophet — Paradise is his due."* (Muslim 1884, from Abū Saʿīd; similar in Abū Dāwūd 1529)

And trust the Knowing, the Clement: He knows your sacrifices (unthanked overtime for family, unnoticed kindnesses, secret tears) and will admit you, through clemency, to an entrance of contentment. Emigrate toward Him daily, and He will enter you where discontent never enters.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:60

> **Thus it is. And whosoever retaliates with the like of that which he has suffered, and is then aggressed upon, God will surely help him. Truly God is Pardoning, Forgiving.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Retaliation Bounded by Likeness**

*Dhālika wa man ʿāqaba bi-mithli mā ʿūqiba bihi* — "Thus it is. And whosoever retaliates with the like of that which he has suffered." *ʿIqāb* (*ʿ-q-b*) denotes requital, consequence — here lawful retaliation (*qiṣāṣ*). *Bi-mithli* (with the like) bounds it: equivalence, not excess. Al-Ṭabarī explains the occasion: Muslims attacked by idolaters in a sacred month (Muḥarram) retaliated equivalently, then felt remorse for fighting in sanctity; the verse assures them — bounded retaliation is just. The Qur'an elsewhere legislates equivalence:

> *"And the requital of evil is the like thereof."* (Qur'an 42:40)

> *"So whoever aggresses against you, aggress against him in like manner as he aggressed."* (Qur'an 2:194)

Al-Qurṭubī notes the verse permits what patience might surpass: retaliation is lawful, forgiveness is nobler — but the retaliator within bounds is not sinful.

**Aggressed Upon After Retaliating**

*Thumma bughiya ʿalayh* — "and is then aggressed upon." *Baghy* (*b-gh-y*) is transgressive aggression — exceeding bounds. The scenario: the wronged retaliates equivalently, then the original aggressor escalates — refusing settlement, seeking revenge. *La-yanṣurannahu Allāh* — "God will surely help him": the emphatic promise attaches to the doubly-wronged — first victimized, then persecuted for seeking justice. Al-Rāzī notes the justice: God helps not the initial aggressor nor the excessive retaliator, but the measured respondent facing fresh aggression. The Qur'an elsewhere promises:

> *"And whoever is killed wrongfully, We have given his heir authority — but let him not exceed in killing."* (Qur'an 17:33)

Ibn Kathīr extends the verse to all oppressed who respond lawfully then face renewed oppression — God becomes their Helper.

**God Is Pardoning, Forgiving**

*Inna Allāha la-ʿafuwwun ghafūr* — "Truly God is Pardoning, Forgiving." *ʿAfuww* (*ʿ-f-w*) effaces sin entirely, as wind effaces footprints; *Ghafūr* (*gh-f-r*) covers it protectively. Al-Qurṭubī explains the closing's relevance: the retaliators' remorse over sacred-month fighting is met with pardon — God effaces their anxiety and forgives any excess. More broadly, the Names counsel the wronged: as God pardons, pardon when able. The Qur'an elsewhere elevates pardoning:

> *"But whoever pardons and makes amends — his reward falls upon God."* (Qur'an 42:40)

The Prophet ﷺ embodied this: wronged at Uḥud, he pardoned; wronged at Makkah's conquest, he declared general amnesty: "No blame upon you today" (cf. 12:92).

**Justice Without Excess, Pardon Without Weakness**

Verse 60 legislates a complete ethic of wrong-response. First, equivalence: respond proportionately — lawful claims, truthful testimony, legitimate defense — never escalating to cruelty. Second, trust: if the aggressor persists after your measured response, God Himself helps — through courts, allies, exposure, or Hereafter. Third, pardon: consider forgiving, for the Pardoning loves pardoners. Modern applications span courtrooms (sue justly, not vindictively), workplaces (answer slander truthfully, not with counter-slander), and nations (defend proportionately per humanitarian law). The believer holds both rights: the right to retaliate equivalently and the nobility to pardon — choosing by wisdom, trusting that whether through justice or pardon, the Pardoning, the Forgiving helps the wronged who wrong not.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:61

> **That is because God makes the night pass into the day and makes the day pass into the night, and because God is Hearing, Seeing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The God Who Interleaves Night and Day**

*Dhālika bi-anna Allāha yūliju al-layla fī al-nahāri wa yūliju al-nahāra fī al-layl* — "That is because God makes the night pass into the day and makes the day pass into the night." *Īlāj* (*w-l-j*) means to insert, to interpenetrate — night's darkness merging into day's light at dawn, day's light dissolving into night's dark at dusk. *Dhālika* (that is) links to verse 60's promise of help: God helps the wronged because He is the God who alternates cosmic opposites — the Power that transitions night to day can transition oppression to relief. Al-Rāzī notes the daily miracle: 365 seamless transitions yearly, lengthening and shortening with seasons, never colliding, never failing. The Qur'an elsewhere cites this as proof:

> *"Thou makest the night pass into the day, and Thou makest the day pass into the night."* (Qur'an 3:27)

> *"And a sign for them is the night: We strip the day from it, and behold, they are in darkness."* (Qur'an 36:37)

**Hearing and Seeing as Grounds of Help**

*Wa anna Allāha samīʿun baṣīr* — "and because God is Hearing, Seeing." *Samīʿ* hears every plea — the wronged's midnight supplication, the retaliator's remorse, the aggressor's plotting. *Baṣīr* sees every act — the equivalent response, the excessive revenge, the renewed aggression. Al-Ṭabarī explains the connection: God's help (v. 60) is perfectly informed — He hears claims and sees facts, so His aid never mistakes aggressor for victim. The Qur'an elsewhere pairs these Names for justice:

> *"Truly God hears and sees all things."* (Qur'an 4:134; 31:28)

Ibn Kathīr adds that Hearing and Seeing also console: no wrong is unheard, no tear unseen — the wronged need not despair that heaven is deaf.

**Cosmic Power, Judicial Precision**

The verse joins omnipotence (alternating day-night) with omniscience (Hearing, Seeing) — power to help, knowledge to help rightly. Al-Qurṭubī notes the theology: God's help is never blind force nor passive sympathy but omnipotent wisdom — transitioning situations as He transitions skies, precisely and beautifully. The alternation itself preaches hope: as surely as night yields to dawn, oppression yields to relief; as surely as day yields to rest, triumph yields to accountability. The Prophet ﷺ taught supplication through these rhythms:

> *"O God, by Thy night's entering into day..."* — and the dawn's two units are "better than the world and what is in it" (Muslim 725, from ʿĀʾishah).

**Trusting the Turner of Nights**

Practically, verse 61 counsels the wronged awaiting help: watch the skies. The God who nightly performs the greater transition (cosmic) will perform the lesser (personal). Supplicate to the Hearing — He hears whispered grievances; act righteously before the Seeing — He sees secret integrity. And interpret delays by dawn's logic: night's darkest hour precedes sunrise; oppression's peak precedes relief. Modern despair — "injustice never ends" — forgets every dawn witnessed: thousands of nights ended, as will this one. The Turner of night into day turns wrongs into rights, tears into joys, and prayers into help — for He is Hearing, Seeing.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:62

> **That is because God is the Truth and what they call upon apart from Him is false, and because God is the Exalted, the Great.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Is the Truth, Idols Are Falsehood**

*Dhālika bi-anna Allāha huwa al-ḥaqqu wa anna mā yadʿūna min dūnihi huwa al-bāṭil* — "That is because God is the Truth and what they call upon apart from Him is false." The verse echoes verse 6's *Allāhu huwa al-ḥaqq* (God is the Truth), now contrasted with *al-bāṭil* (the false, the null). *Ḥaqq* denotes firm reality — existent, effective, enduring; *bāṭil* denotes nullity — perishing, useless, void. Al-Ṭabarī explains: God's help (vv. 60–61), cosmic alternation, and judgment all flow from His Truth — real power producing real effects — while idols' promises are *bāṭil* — null words producing nothing. The Qur'an elsewhere states the law of their encounter:

> *"Truth has come, and falsehood has vanished; truly falsehood is ever vanishing."* (Qur'an 17:81)

> *"And say: Truth has come from your Lord; so whoever wills, let him believe."* (Qur'an 18:29)

Ibn Kathīr notes the verse is nearly identical to 31:30 — repetition underscoring tawḥīd's centrality.

**The Exalted, the Great**

*Wa anna Allāha huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-kabīr* — "and because God is the Exalted, the Great." *ʿAlī* (*ʿ-l-w*) denotes elevation above all — in essence, power, and sanctity; *Kabīr* (*k-b-r*) denotes greatness beyond measure. Al-Qurṭubī explains the pairing: Exaltedness negates rivals above Him, Greatness negates equals beside Him — together they establish absolute supremacy. The Qur'an elsewhere joins them:

> *"And He is the Exalted, the Great."* (Qur'an 2:255, in the Throne Verse; 34:23; 40:12; 42:4)

Al-Rāzī notes the context: idols are low (*asfal*) and small (*ṣaghīr*) — stones beneath feet, fictions within minds — while God is High and Great; worshipping the lowly beside the Lofty is cosmic inversion.

**Truth's Triumph as History's Law**

Classical scholars read verse 62 as history's verdict: every contest between *ḥaqq* and *bāṭil* ends one way — delayed, but decided. Pharaoh's magicians' ropes (falsehood) were swallowed by Moses' staff (truth); Quraysh's idols (falsehood) fell before the Prophet's ﷺ staff reciting truth; every ideology opposing revelation — materialism, tyranny, licentiousness — flourishes then vanishes. The Prophet ﷺ enacted this at Makkah's conquest:

> *"He struck the idols with his staff, reciting: 'Truth has come and falsehood has vanished.'"* (al-Bukhārī 4287; Muslim 1781, from Ibn Masʿūd)

Al-Qurṭubī derives confidence: believers need not despair when falsehood dominates headlines — *bāṭil* is by nature *zāhiq* (vanishing); its noise is its death-rattle.

**Anchoring in Truth, Abandoning Falsehood**

Practically, verse 62 is a daily creed: affirm God as *al-Ḥaqq* (reality's anchor), dismiss rivals as *bāṭil* (nullities), and exalt Him as *ʿAlī Kabīr* (above every anxiety, greater than every threat). The believer measures claims by this verse: does this ideology, habit, or alliance partake of *ḥaqq* (truthful, enduring, Godward) or *bāṭil* (deceptive, perishing, selfward)? He clings to the former, releases the latter. And in worship he magnifies: *Allāhu akbar* (God is Greater) — greater than idols feared, greater than tyrants obeyed, greater than desires served. For God is the Truth, all else called upon is false, and He is the Exalted, the Great.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:63

> **Hast thou not considered that God sends down water from Heaven, and then the earth becomes green? Truly God is Subtle, Aware.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Water from Heaven, Green from Earth**

*A-lam tara anna Allāha anzala min al-samāʾi māʾan fa-tuṣbiḥu al-arḍu mukhḍarrah* — "Hast thou not considered that God sends down water from Heaven, and then the earth becomes green?" *Raʾā* (considering) invites reflection on the familiar: rain descending, desert greening. *Tuṣbiḥ* (becomes, literally "enters morning") suggests overnight transformation — evening's dust, morning's green. *Mukhḍarrah* (green) denotes lush vegetation — life's color. Al-Ṭabarī notes the verse recalls verse 5's desiccated earth revived — now as standalone proof of divine mercy and power. The Qur'an elsewhere multiplies the image:

> *"And thou seest the earth barren, but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells."* (Qur'an 41:39; cf. 22:5)

> *"And He sends down water from heaven in measure, and We revive thereby a dead land."* (Qur'an 43:11)

Ibn Kathīr observes the timing: after verses of judgment and help, nature's revival reassures — the God who greens deserts revives hopes.

**God Is Subtle, Aware**

*Inna Allāha laṭīfun khabīr* — "Truly God is Subtle, Aware." *Laṭīf* (*l-ṭ-f*) denotes exquisite subtlety — reaching where coarse means cannot: water penetrating soil's pores, roots threading rock's cracks, mercy reaching despair's depths. *Khabīr* (*kh-b-r*) denotes intimate awareness — knowing soils' needs, seeds' states, servants' secrets. Al-Qurṭubī explains the pairing: Subtlety in action, Awareness in knowledge — God knows exactly where rain must fall and gently delivers it. The Qur'an elsewhere joins them:

> *"Vision perceives Him not, but He perceives all vision; and He is the Subtle, the Aware."* (Qur'an 6:103)

Al-Rāzī notes the agricultural precision: rain in measure (too much drowns, too little starves), timed to seasons, distributed to valleys — subtlety and awareness in every drop.

**Subtlety in Providence**

Classical scholars extend *Laṭīf* beyond meteorology to providence: God provides subtly — through unexpected doors, rejected means, delayed timings. The emigrant fearing destitution (v. 58) is provided through strangers; the wronged awaiting help (v. 60) is aided through enemies' errors; the doubter seeking truth finds a verse, a friend, a dream. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"God is Subtle with His servants; He provides whomsoever He wills."* (Qur'an 42:19)

The Prophet ﷺ taught trust in subtle provision:

> *"If you relied upon God as He should be relied upon, He would provide you as He provides birds: they leave hungry and return full."* (al-Tirmidhī 2344; Ibn Mājah 4164, from ʿUmar; graded fair)

**Greening Dead Hearts**

Sufis like al-Qushayrī read the verse inwardly: hearts are earths — some desiccated by sin, hardened by heedlessness — and revelation is rain: when it descends on receptive hearts, they green with faith, flower with worship, fruit with character. The believer's task is soil-preparation: break hardness through repentance, remove weeds of envy, then await rain through supplication and recitation. Modern deserts — literal (expanding Sahara) and spiritual (expanding despair) — both await water from heaven. Whoever considers rain's greening will trust: the Subtle, the Aware greens lands and hearts alike, in His measure, in His season, without fail.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:64

> **To Him belongs whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth. And God is truly the Self-Sufficient, the Praised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Universal Ownership**

*Lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — "To Him belongs whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth." *Lahu* (to Him) denotes exclusive ownership: every atom above and below — angels, stars, seas, souls, wealth — belongs to God. Al-Ṭabarī explains the context: after describing provision (rain, subservience, sacrifice), God declares the Provider's ownership — He gives from His own, not from others'. The Qur'an repeats this ownership as tawḥīd's foundation:

> *"To God belongs whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth."* (Qur'an 2:284; 3:109; 4:131; and throughout)

Al-Rāzī notes the implication: idols own nothing (they are owned — carved, carried, burned), while God owns all — worship belongs to the Owner, not the owned. The Prophet ﷺ taught this in supplication:

> *"O God, Owner of Sovereignty, Thou givest sovereignty to whom Thou wills and strippest it from whom Thou wills."* (Qur'an 3:26, oft-recited as duʿāʾ)

**The Self-Sufficient**

*Wa inna Allāha la-huwa al-ghaniyyu* — "And God is truly the Self-Sufficient." *Ghanī* (*gh-n-y*) denotes rich independence — needing nothing, possessed of all. The emphatic *inna... la-huwa* (truly... He is) stresses exclusivity: only God is absolutely self-sufficient; all else is needy (*faqīr*) — angels need sustenance, kings need sleep, earth needs rain. Al-Qurṭubī explains the corollary: God's commands benefit servants, not Him — prayer disciplines us, charity purifies us, sacrifice feeds us (v. 37: flesh does not reach Him). The Qur'an elsewhere declares:

> *"O mankind! You are the needy unto God, and God is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised."* (Qur'an 35:15)

> *"And whoever is grateful is grateful for his own soul; and whoever is ungrateful — truly my Lord is Self-Sufficient, Generous."* (Qur'an 27:40)

**The Praised**

*Al-ḥamīd* — "the Praised." *Ḥamīd* denotes worthiness of praise in every state — praised by all tongues willingly or unwillingly, in bounty and in trial. Al-Rāzī notes the pairing with *Ghanī*: self-sufficiency without praiseworthiness could be cold independence; praiseworthiness without self-sufficiency could be needy vanity. Together they describe perfect Divinity: needing no praise yet deserving all. The Qur'an elsewhere commands praising Him:

> *"And say: Praise be to God, who has taken no son."* (Qur'an 17:111)

The Prophet ﷺ taught that praise fills scales:

> *"Purity is half of faith, and 'Praise be to God' fills the scale."* (Muslim 223, from Abū Mālik al-Ashʿarī)

**Ownership as Liberation**

Verse 64 liberates through ownership: if all belongs to God, then loss is His reclaiming (borne patiently), gain is His lending (spent gratefully), and rivals' threats are empty (they own nothing to withhold). The Self-Sufficient frees believers from servitude to the needy — bosses, patrons, crowds — directing need to the Needless. The Praised directs praise from flattery to worship — thanking creatures as instruments, God as Source. Practically: begin acquisitions with *bismillāh* (using the Owner's property by permission), endure losses with *innā li-Allāh* (we belong to the Owner), and end days with *al-ḥamdu li-Allāh* (praise to the Praised). Whoever knows the Owner is Self-Sufficient and Praised will neither grovel before the needy nor boast over fellow borrowers.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:65

> **Hast thou not considered that God has made whatsoever is on the earth subservient unto you—and the ship sails upon the sea—by His Command? And He maintains the sky lest it fall upon the earth, save by His Leave. Truly God is Kind and Merciful unto mankind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Subservience of the Earth**

*A-lam tara anna Allāha sakhkhara lakum mā fī al-arḍ* — "Hast thou not considered that God has made whatsoever is on the earth subservient unto you." *Taskhīr* (subjugation) denotes harnessing: soil yielding crops, animals bearing burdens, minerals serving industry, winds turning mills — all compelled to benefit man. *Lakum* (unto you) marks humanity as beneficiary — not owner (v. 64) but trustee. Al-Ṭabarī notes the verse recalls verse 36's subdued camels, now universalized: all earth serves. The Qur'an elsewhere enumerates:

> *"And He made subservient unto you whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth, all from Him."* (Qur'an 45:13)

> *"It is He who made the earth tractable for you, so walk its slopes and eat of His provision."* (Qur'an 67:15)

Al-Rāzī argues: subservience proves both power (who tamed the untamable?) and mercy (who gifted the service?) — atheism explains neither the harness nor the horse.

**Ships Sailing by His Command**

*Wa al-fulka tajrī fī al-baḥri bi-amrih* — "and the ship sails upon the sea — by His Command." *Fulk* (ship, ark) evokes Noah's salvation and humanity's commerce — wood floating on abyss by buoyancy's law. *Bi-amrih* (by His Command) attributes navigation to decree: winds, currents, and floatation obey God, not sailors. Al-Qurṭubī notes seafaring's Qur'anic prominence as mercy's emblem — perilous abyss made highway:

> *"And thou seest ships plowing therein, that you may seek of His bounty."* (Qur'an 16:14; cf. 14:32; 17:66; 31:31)

The Prophet ﷺ taught sea-travelers' supplication, acknowledging peril and protection. Modern container-ships carrying humanity's sustenance fulfill the verse daily — *tajrī bi-amrih* in steel and satellite.

**Maintaining the Sky**

*Wa yumsiku al-samāʾa an taqaʿa ʿalā al-arḍi illā bi-idhnih* — "And He maintains the sky lest it fall upon the earth, save by His Leave." *Imsāk* (maintaining, holding) denotes sustaining: gravitational order, atmospheric shield, orbital precision — heaven held aloft. *An taqaʿa* (lest it fall) imagines the alternative: cosmic collapse — meteors, radiation, chaos. *Illā bi-idhnih* (save by His Leave) excepts judgment's portents, when leave is granted for shaking. Al-Rāzī marvels: tons of atmosphere press yet sustain, rocks orbit yet never strike cities at will — holding is continuous creation. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"Truly God maintains the heavens and the earth lest they vanish."* (Qur'an 35:41)

**Kind and Merciful unto Mankind**

*Inna Allāha bi-l-nāsi la-raʾūfun raḥīm* — "Truly God is Kind and Merciful unto mankind." *Raʾūf* (*r-a-f*) is tender kindness — pitying weakness; *Raḥīm* is encompassing mercy — bestowing good. The emphatic *inna... la-* (truly... surely) seals subservience, sailing, and sustaining as mercies — not entitlements. Al-Qurṭubī notes *bi-l-nāsi* (unto mankind) universalizes: believer and disbeliever alike eat subdued earth's crops, sail commanded seas, shelter under held skies — common grace preceding special grace. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"And My mercy embraces all things."* (Qur'an 7:156)

Practically, verse 65 prescribes gratitude as physics: every meal (subdued soil), every voyage (commanded sea), every breath (held sky) is *raʾfah* and *raḥmah*. Whoever considers subservience will serve the Subduer — and find the Kind, the Merciful kinder still.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:66

> **And He it is Who gave you life; then He causes you to die; then He gives you life. Truly man is ungrateful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Threefold Cycle**

*Wa huwa alladhī aḥyākum thumma yumītukum thumma yuḥyīkum* — "And He it is Who gave you life; then He causes you to die; then He gives you life." Three verbs chart existence: *aḥyā* (gave life) — creation from nothing, womb to world; *yumīt* (causes to die) — appointed death, world to grave; *yuḥyī* (gives life) — resurrection, grave to judgment. *Huwa alladhī* (He it is who) stresses exclusivity: none shares these acts — neither idols (who neither harm nor benefit — v. 12) nor nature (which serves — v. 65). Al-Ṭabarī notes the verse echoes 2:28's fuller cycle (dead → alive → dead → alive → return), here condensed to life's bookends. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"How can you disbelieve in God, when you were dead and He gave you life, then He causes you to die, then gives you life, then unto Him you return?"* (Qur'an 2:28)

Ibn Kathīr explains: the first life proves the last — Whoever authored beginning authors return.

**Truly Man Is Ungrateful**

*Inna al-insāna la-kafūr* — "Truly man is ungrateful." *Kafūr* (*k-f-r*) is intensely ungrateful — covering blessings, denying the Benefactor. The emphatic *inna... la-* (truly... surely) universalizes with exceptions (believers grateful): man's default is ingratitude — receiving life, death's respite, and resurrection's promise, yet denying. Al-Rāzī notes the placement: after subservience (v. 65) and before rites (v. 67), ingratitude is the hinge — blessings received, thanks withheld, worship neglected. The Qur'an elsewhere laments:

> *"Truly man is ungrateful to his Lord."* (Qur'an 100:6)

> *"And few of My servants are thankful."* (Qur'an 34:13)

Al-Qurṭubī distinguishes *kufr* (disbelief) from *kufrān* (ingratitude): verse 66's *kafūr* encompasses both — denying God's existence and denying His bounty.

**Ingratitude's Anatomy**

Classical scholars dissect *kufrān*: using blessings against the Giver (strength for oppression, wealth for vice), attributing blessings to self ("my skill earned this" — 39:49), and forgetting the Giver in bounty while remembering Him only in need. The Qur'an elsewhere diagnoses:

> *"And when We bestow favor upon man, he turns away and withdraws; but when evil touches him, he is full of supplication."* (Qur'an 41:51)

The Prophet ﷺ prescribed gratitude's discipline:

> *"Look to those below you, not those above, that you may not belittle God's blessings."* (al-Bukhārī 6490; Muslim 2963, from Abū Hurayrah)

Al-Qushayrī added: gratitude (*shukr*) increases blessings (14:7), while ingratitude forfeits them — the ungrateful heart constricts provision, the grateful expands it.

**From Kafūr to Shakūr**

Practically, verse 66 invites conversion from *kafūr* (ungrateful) to *shakūr* (grateful): acknowledge life as gift (praise each morning: "praise God who gave us life after death" — al-Bukhārī 6312), prepare for death as return (repent nightly), and hope for resurrection as reunion (work daily). Count blessings concretely — breaths, meals, shelters, guides — and thank specifically. For the cycle is certain: He gave life, He will cause death, He will give life — and the grateful will hear "enter Gardens," while the persistently *kafūr* will face what ingratitude earns. Life is short; gratitude makes it eternal.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:67

> **For every community We have appointed a rite they are to perform; so let them not argue with thee over the matter. And call to thy Lord. Truly thou art following straight guidance.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Every Community's Rite**

*Li-kulli ummatin jaʿalnā mansakan hum nāsikūh* — "For every community We have appointed a rite they are to perform." The verse echoes verse 34's *li-kulli ummatin jaʿalnā mansakan*, now with *hum nāsikūh* (they are to perform it) — emphasizing obligation, not option. *Mansak* encompasses sacrifices, prayers, pilgrimages, and lawful customs — each nation's divinely suited worship. Al-Ṭabarī explains the repetition: verse 34 established unity of God beneath ritual diversity; verse 67 derives conduct from it — since God appointed each rite, disputing the Prophet's ﷺ rite is disputing God's appointment. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"For every community there is a messenger."* (Qur'an 10:47)

> *"And We sent a messenger unto every community: Worship God and shun false gods."* (Qur'an 16:36)

Ibn Kathīr notes the abrogation's wisdom: prior rites were true for their times; Muhammad's ﷺ rite consummates and supersedes — diversity in history, finality in Islam.

**Let Them Not Argue Over the Matter**

*Fa-lā yunāziʿunnaka fī al-amr* — "so let them not argue with thee over the matter." *Munāzaʿah* (*n-z-ʿ*) is contentious wrangling — pulling truth to and fro. The prohibition (*lā yunāziʿunna*) addresses disputants: desist from quarreling with the Prophet over rites, sacrifices, and religion. Al-Rāzī notes the alternative reading: the Prophet is commanded not to engage their wrangling — refuse futile debate, proceed with calling. Both converge: disputation over appointed rites is forbidden — by them (stop contending) and for him (do not descend). The Qur'an elsewhere counsels:

> *"And dispute with them in the most beautiful manner."* (Qur'an 16:125 — beautiful discourse, not wrangling)

> *"And turn away from the ignorant."* (Qur'an 7:199)

**Call to Thy Lord**

*Wa udʿu ilā rabbik* — "And call to thy Lord." *Daʿwah* (calling) replaces *munāzaʿah* (wrangling): invite to God — His unity, worship, and mercy — rather than sparring over forms. Al-Qurṭubī explains the priority: the Prophet's ﷺ mission is positive proclamation, not reactive polemics. *Ilā rabbik* (to thy Lord) specifies the direction: call to God, not to self, tribe, or victory. *Innaka la-ʿalā hudan mustaqīm* — "Truly thou art following straight guidance": the emphatic assurance steadies the caller — his path is straight (*mustaqīm*), needing no validation from wranglers. The Qur'an elsewhere confirms:

> *"And truly thou guidest unto a straight path."* (Qur'an 42:52)

**Calling Instead of Quarreling**

Practically, verse 67 reforms religious discourse: when confronted with wrangling — sectarian polemics, bad-faith interrogations, ritual mockery — do not wrangle; call. Present God's unity, mercy, and worship positively; answer sincere questions beautifully; ignore provocations gracefully. Modern believers drowning in comment-section *munāzaʿah* need this: every hour wrangling is an hour not calling. The Prophet ﷺ, disputed over sacrifices, prayer-direction, and prophethood, responded with revelation and character — not insults. Whoever calls to his Lord upon straight guidance will find wranglers refuted by truth's beauty — and seekers guided by the caller's focus.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:68

> **And if they dispute with thee, say, “God knows best what you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**When Disputation Persists**

*Wa in jādalūka fa-qul Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā taʿmalūn* — "And if they dispute with thee, say, 'God knows best what you do.'" After prohibiting wrangling (v. 67), the verse provides the response if opponents persist: *Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā taʿmalūn*. *Jidāl* here is adversarial disputation — not sincere inquiry but contentious resistance. Al-Ṭabarī explains the sentence's function: it ends debate by referring it to omniscience — "your arguments do not persuade, your motives are known to God, and He will judge." The Qur'an elsewhere prescribes similar referrals:

> *"And if they turn away, say: God suffices me."* (Qur'an 9:129)

> *"To you your religion, and to me mine."* (Qur'an 109:6)

Ibn Kathīr notes the dignity: the Prophet ﷺ is not commanded to out-argue, insult, or coerce — but to declare God's knowledge and withdraw.

**God Knows Best What You Do**

*Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā taʿmalūn* — "God knows best what you do." *Aʿlam* (knowing best) affirms comprehensive knowledge: outward disputations and inward intentions — envy, pride, tribalism driving objections. *Bi-mā taʿmalūn* (what you do) encompasses deeds, words, and schemes — all recorded. Al-Rāzī notes the sentence's dual edge: warning for disputants (your hostility is known and will be requited) and comfort for the Prophet ﷺ (your sincerity is known and will be rewarded). The Qur'an elsewhere affirms such knowledge:

> *"And God knows best what they hide and what they disclose."* (Qur'an 16:19; 36:76)

Al-Qurṭubī adds that invoking God's knowledge is itself *daʿwah* (calling): it reminds disputants of accountability, planting a seed argument could not.

**The Etiquette of Ending Debates**

Classical scholars derive from this verse the etiquette of disengagement: when debate becomes *jidāl* (contention) rather than *ḥiwār* (dialogue) — when opponents repeat refuted points, mock, or shift goalposts — the believer declares God's knowledge and departs gracefully. The Prophet ﷺ modeled this with Najrān's Christians: after exposition and their persistence, he proposed *mubāhalah* (mutual invocation) then honored their treaty — truth declared, coercion refused, knowledge invoked. He also warned:

> *"I guarantee a house in Paradise's outskirts for whoever abandons disputation though right."* (Abū Dāwūd 4800, from Abū Umāmah; graded fair)

Al-Qurṭubī therefore distinguished obligatory clarification (present truth once, clearly) from forbidden wrangling (perpetual contention) — verse 68 commands the former's conclusion.

**Saying "God Knows" Today**

Practically, verse 68 equips believers for unwinnable arguments: the relative mocking practice, the colleague ridiculing belief, the online mob demanding endless justifications. Present truth kindly; if contention persists, say "God knows best what we do" — meaning: He knows my sincerity and your motives, and He will judge. Then disengage with dignity — no insults, no anxiety, no compulsion. This is not defeat but delegation: transferring the case from flawed human debate to perfect divine court. Whoever delegates thus preserves his peace, his character, and his caller's credibility — and leaves disputants with the one sentence argument cannot answer: God knows.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:69

> **God will judge between you on the Day of Resurrection concerning that wherein you used to differ.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Divine Judgment Over Differences**

*Allāhu yaḥkumu baynakum yawma al-qiyāmati fīmā kuntum fīhi takhtalifūn* — "God will judge between you on the Day of Resurrection concerning that wherein you used to differ." The sentence completes verse 68's referral: God's knowledge issues in God's judgment. *Yaḥkumu baynakum* (will judge between you) encompasses the Prophet ﷺ and disputants, believers and disbelievers, all communities differing over rites, creeds, and prophets. *Fīmā kuntum fīhi takhtalifūn* (concerning that wherein you differed) is comprehensive: God's unity, Muhammad's ﷺ prophethood, resurrection, lawful rites — every religious difference. Al-Ṭabarī notes the verse echoes 2:113 (Jews and Christians disputing, God judging) and 5:48 (laws differing, God judging) — a Qur'anic constant: this world debates, the next decides. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"Then unto your Lord is your return, and He will inform you of what you used to differ in."* (Qur'an 6:164; 16:92)

**Why Judgment Waits for Resurrection**

Al-Rāzī addresses the wisdom of deferral: immediate judgment would compel belief through terror, nullifying faith's voluntariness and testing's meaning. This world is *dār al-ʿamal* (abode of acting), the next *dār al-jazāʾ* (abode of requital) — mixing them would corrupt both. Deferral also manifests mercy: disputants receive lifetimes to repent, seekers receive space to investigate, and believers receive opportunities to exemplify truth through character. The Qur'an elsewhere explains:

> *"And had thy Lord willed, those on earth would have believed, all together. Wouldst thou compel men till they become believers?"* (Qur'an 10:99)

Ibn Kathīr adds that deferral perfects justice: all evidence gathered, all witnesses assembled, all contexts known — no hasty verdicts, no partial hearings.

**Differences That Matter and Those That Don't**

Al-Qurṭubī distinguishes creedal differences (God's unity, prophethood, resurrection) — over which God will judge decisively, vindicating truth and condemning obstinate falsehood — from juristic differences (*ikhtilāf fiqhī*) among believers — over which God is merciful, rewarding both strivers (the correct twice, the mistaken once — al-Bukhārī 7352; Muslim 1716). Verse 69 concerns the former: inter-communal disputes over revelation's truth. Yet its spirit counsels intra-communal civility: since God judges even major differences in the Hereafter, believers should debate minor ones gently now. The Prophet ﷺ warned against weaponizing differences:

> *"Do not differ, lest your hearts differ."* (Muslim 432, from Ibn Masʿūd, on prayer-rows — extended by scholars to unity)

**Awaiting Judgment While Acting Justly**

Practically, verse 69 teaches eschatological patience: when truth is disputed, evidence rejected, and falsehood triumphant — do not despair or coerce; act justly and await judgment. Present truth, live it beautifully, delegate verdicts to God. This patience is not passivity: believers still enjoin right, forbid wrong, and defend the oppressed (vv. 39–41) — but they do not usurp God's gavel, declaring final damnation or forcing conversion. Modern sectarian hatreds — excommunicating, dehumanizing, killing over differences God reserved for Resurrection — violate verse 69's delegation. Whoever believes God will judge need not judge finally now; whoever acts justly now will be vindicated then — concerning all wherein they differed.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:70

> **Knowest thou not that God knows whatsoever is in Heaven and on earth? Truly that is in a Book. Truly that is easy for God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Knows Whatsoever Is in Heaven and Earth**

*A-lam taʿlam anna Allāha yaʿlamu mā fī al-samāʾi wa al-arḍ* — "Knowest thou not that God knows whatsoever is in Heaven and on earth?" The rhetorical question (*a-lam taʿlam* — knowest thou not?) affirms what reason and revelation establish: God's knowledge encompasses all — visible and hidden, past and future, atoms and angels. Al-Ṭabarī notes this is the Qur'an's sole use of singular *al-samāʾ* (Heaven) in such contexts (elsewhere plural heavens) — emphasizing comprehensiveness through unity: one Heaven, one earth, one Knower. The Qur'an elsewhere details:

> *"And with Him are the keys of the Unseen; none knows them but He. And He knows what is on land and sea; and no leaf falls but that He knows it."* (Qur'an 6:59)

> *"If it be but the weight of a mustard seed, be it in a rock, in the heavens, or on the earth, God will bring it forth."* (Qur'an 31:16)

Ibn Kathīr explains the context: after promising judgment (v. 69), God affirms judgment's basis — perfect knowledge, nothing forgotten, nothing mistaken.

**Truly That Is in a Book**

*Inna dhālika fī kitāb* — "Truly that is in a Book." *Kitāb* here is the Preserved Tablet (*al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ*), in which all things are inscribed before creation. Al-Qurṭubī explains: God's knowledge is not reactive but pre-eternal — events are known and written before occurring. The Prophet ﷺ affirmed:

> *"God wrote the decrees of creatures fifty thousand years before creating heavens and earth."* (Muslim 2653, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr)

> *"The first thing God created was the Pen. He said: Write. It said: What shall I write? He said: Write all that shall be until the Hour."* (Abū Dāwūd 4700; al-Tirmidhī 2155, from ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit; graded authentic)

Al-Rāzī notes the comfort: believers' sufferings, disbelievers' schemes, and disputants' words are all in the Book — nothing surprises God, nothing escapes reckoning.

**Truly That Is Easy for God**

*Inna dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīr* — "Truly that is easy for God." *Yasīr* (easy, slight) negates any burden: knowing and writing all things costs God nothing — no fatigue, no volume, no complexity. Al-Ṭabarī contrasts human record-keeping (vast archives, failing memories) with divine: effortless omniscience. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms such ease:

> *"And thy Lord's creation and resurrection of you is as a single soul."* (Qur'an 31:28)

Ibn Taymiyyah held this verse refutes both deniers of decree (*qadariyyah*) and anthropomorphists: God's knowledge and writing are perfect, unlike creatures', and easy, unlike creatures' labors.

**Living Before the Knowing, the Recording**

Practically, verse 70 instills *murāqabah* (vigilance): act as one known and recorded — for you are. The believer speaks truthfully (words in the Book), deals honestly (transactions known), and supplicates confidently (needs already inscribed with answers). Anxiety dissolves: provision written cannot be missed, calamity written cannot be dodged, and disputants' plots written cannot exceed leave. The Prophet ﷺ taught Ibn ʿAbbās:

> *"Know that if the community gathered to benefit thee, they could benefit thee only with what God has written for thee; and if they gathered to harm thee, they could harm thee only with what God has written against thee. The pens are lifted and the pages dried."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516; graded authentic)

Whoever knows God knows all, writes all, and manages all with ease will trust fully — and live transparently before the All-Knowing.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:71

> **And they worship, apart from God, that for which He has sent down no authority, and that whereof they have no knowledge. And the wrongdoers shall have no helpers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Worship Without Authority**

*Wa yaʿbudūna min dūni Allāhi mā lam yunazzil bihi sulṭānā* — "And they worship, apart from God, that for which He has sent down no authority." *Sulṭān* (*s-l-ṭ*) denotes authority, proof, warrant — whether revealed (scripture) or rational (demonstration). *Lam yunazzil* (He has not sent down) denies divine sanction: idols lack any revealed authorization — no scripture commands their worship, no prophet validated them. Al-Ṭabarī notes the verse echoes 7:71, 12:40 ("you worship names you named, for which God sent down no authority") — idolatry is human invention without heavenly license. The Qur'an elsewhere challenges:

> *"Or have they partners who prescribed for them of religion what God did not permit?"* (Qur'an 42:21)

Al-Rāzī extends *sulṭān* to rational proof: idols lack both revelation and reason — no text commands, no intellect justifies.

**Worship Without Knowledge**

*Wa mā laysa lahum bihi ʿilm* — "and that whereof they have no knowledge." The second negation is epistemic: idolaters worship without knowledge — neither revealed authority (*sulṭān*) nor personal certitude (*ʿilm*). Their worship rests on conjecture (*ẓann*), imitation (*taqlīd*), and desire (*hawā*) — "we found our fathers" (43:23), "to bring us near to God" (39:3). Al-Qurṭubī notes the verse's parallel with verses 3 and 8 (disputing without knowledge): ignorant disputation produces ignorant worship — the tongue's disease infects the limbs. The Qur'an elsewhere diagnoses:

> *"They follow naught but conjecture and what souls desire, though guidance has come to them from their Lord."* (Qur'an 53:23)

Ibn Kathīr observes the universality: every innovation (*bidʿah*) and superstition shares this twin lack — no authority, no knowledge — whether stone idols or modern charms.

**The Wrongdoers Shall Have No Helpers**

*Wa mā li-l-ẓālimīna min naṣīr* — "And the wrongdoers shall have no helpers." *Ẓālimīn* (wrongdoers) here denotes idolaters — idolatry being supreme wrong (*ẓulm ʿaẓīm* — 31:13), misplacing worship. *Naṣīr* (helper) is denied absolutely (*min* + negation = none at all): neither idols (powerless — vv. 12–13) nor allies (disowning — 2:166) nor intercessors (unpermitted) will help. Al-Rāzī notes the correspondence: they sought helpers (*awliyāʾ*) beside God; God declares them helperless. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"And they took apart from God gods, that haply they might be helped. They cannot help them, though they are hosts made present for them."* (Qur'an 36:74–75)

**Authority and Knowledge as Worship's Conditions**

Verse 71 legislates worship's validity: every devotion requires *sulṭān* (divine authority — Qur'an, authentic Sunnah) and *ʿilm* (knowledge — understanding what, why, how). Worship without authority is innovation; worship without knowledge is ritualism. Practically, believers should audit devotions: "Where is this commanded? What does it mean?" — praying, fasting, and celebrating from texts, not trends. The Prophet ﷺ warned:

> *"Whoever performs a deed not in accordance with our matter — it is rejected."* (Muslim 1718, from ʿĀʾishah)

And socially, the verse judges ideologies worshipped without authority or knowledge — nationalism, consumerism, scientism as creeds: no revelation sent them, no certain knowledge sustains them, and their adherents — wronging truth — will find no helpers when help is needed most.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:72

> **And when Our signs are recited unto them as clear proofs, thou seest denial upon the faces of those who disbelieve. They well-nigh pounce upon those who recite Our signs unto them. Say, “Shall I inform you of what is worse than that? The Fire God has promised to those who disbelieve. What an evil journey’s end!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Denial Visible on Faces**

*Wa idhā tutlā ʿalayhim āyātunā bayyinātin taʿrifu fī wujūhi alladhīna kafarū al-munkar* — "And when Our signs are recited unto them as clear proofs, thou seest denial upon the faces of those who disbelieve." *Bayyināt* (clear proofs) denotes luminous, evident verses — clarity aggravating rejection. *Taʿrifu* (thou seest/recognizest) addresses the Prophet ﷺ (and every reciter): denial is legible — frowning, sneering, darkening. *Al-munkar* (*n-k-r*) is denial, disapproval, rejection — faces confessing what tongues delay. Al-Ṭabarī notes the physiology: hearts' rejection surfaces facially before verbally — God exposes inward *munkar* outwardly. The Qur'an elsewhere describes such faces:

> *"And when Our signs are recited unto him, he turns away arrogantly, as though he heard them not."* (Qur'an 31:7)

> *"Thou seest them looking at thee, but they see not."* (Qur'an 7:198)

**Well-Nigh Pouncing on Reciters**

*Yakādūna yasṭūna bi-alladhīna yatlūna ʿalayhim āyātinā* — "They well-nigh pounce upon those who recite Our signs unto them." *Saṭw* (*s-ṭ-w*) is violent assault — pouncing, gripping, striking. *Yakādūn* (they well-nigh, almost) captures restrained rage — fury barely leashed, hands twitching toward throats. Al-Qurṭubī explains: clear recitation enrages because it exposes — idols' impotence, morals' corruption, judgment's certainty — and exposure provokes violence from those who cannot answer with argument. History confirms: Bilāl tortured for reciting, Khabbāb branded, the Prophet ﷺ throttled in prayer (al-Bukhārī 3854) — *saṭw* against recitation. The Qur'an elsewhere promises reciters protection:

> *"And God will protect thee from men."* (Qur'an 5:67)

**Worse Than That: The Fire**

*Qul a-fa-unabbiʾukum bi-sharrin min dhālikum* — "Say, 'Shall I inform you of what is worse than that?'" *Sharr* (worse, evil) reframes: their rage at recitation is bad, but what awaits is worse. *Al-nāru waʿadahā Allāhu alladhīna kafarū* — "The Fire God has promised to those who disbelieve": *waʿada* (promised/threatened) makes Hell certain — divine promise, not preacher's hyperbole. *Wa biʾsa al-maṣīr* — "What an evil journey's end!": *maṣīr* (destination, becoming) — Hell as final address. Al-Rāzī notes the rhetoric: the Prophet ﷺ answers threatened violence not with counter-threats but with theological truth — "your anger harms reciters briefly; the Fire harms deniers eternally." The Qur'an elsewhere contrasts:

> *"Shall I inform you of worse than that as requital with God?"* (Qur'an 5:60)

**Reciting Before Hostile Faces Today**

Practically, verse 72 prepares reciters for *munkar*-faces: the sneer at hijab-verses, the frown at interest-prohibitions, the eye-roll at resurrection-warnings — and worse, deplatforming, harassment, assault. The response is prophetic: neither silence (abandoning recitation) nor violence (answering *saṭw* with *saṭw*), but declaration — "shall I inform you of worse?" — warning lovingly of Fire while embodying peace. The Prophet ﷺ, pounced upon in Ṭāʾif with stones, answered with supplication for their progeny (al-Bukhārī 3231). Modern reciters — teachers, preachers, parents — should expect faces' *munkar*, endure near-*saṭw*, and persist: clear signs recited patiently melt more hearts than they harden, and the Fire warned of may save those who now frown.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:73

> **O mankind! A parable is set forth, so hearken unto it! Truly those upon whom you call apart from God will never create a fly, even if they gathered together to do so. And if the fly should rob them of aught, they could not rescue it from it. Feeble are the seeker and the sought!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parable for All Mankind**

*Yā ayyuhā al-nāsu ḍuriba mathalun fa-istamiʿū lah* — "O mankind! A parable is set forth, so hearken unto it!" *Ḍuriba mathal* (a parable is struck/coined) announces illustrative teaching — truth through analogy. *Fa-istamiʿū* (so hearken, listen attentively) commands focused hearing — not passive exposure but active pondering. The universal address (*yā ayyuhā al-nās*) signals universal relevance: the parable judges every false god of every age. Al-Rāzī notes the imperative's gravity: God commands listening as He commands prayer — parables are revelation, not entertainment. The Qur'an elsewhere emphasizes parables' purpose:

> *"And We set forth parables for mankind, but none understand them save the knowledgeable."* (Qur'an 29:43)

**Gods That Cannot Create a Fly**

*Inna alladhīna tadʿūna min dūni Allāhi lan yakhluqū dhubāban wa lawi ijtamaʿū lah* — "Truly those upon whom you call apart from God will never create a fly, even if they gathered together to do so." *Lan* (never) is absolute eternal negation. *Dhubāb* (fly) is deliberately the lowliest creature — weak, short-lived, despised — yet beyond false gods' power. *Wa lawi ijtamaʿū* (even if they gathered) imagines collective effort — all idols, tyrants, scientists, and machines cooperating — still impotent. Al-Ṭabarī explains the argument: creation (*khalq*) is divinity's proof; whoever cannot create the least cannot be divine. The Qur'an elsewhere challenges:

> *"Is He who creates as he who creates not? Will you not remember?"* (Qur'an 16:17)

> *"Do they ascribe as partners those who create naught and are themselves created?"* (Qur'an 7:191)

Modern technology, assembling genomes and rovers, still cannot create a living fly from nothing — *lan yakhluqū* stands.

**Robbed by a Fly, Unable to Rescue**

*Wa in yaslubhumu al-dhubābu shayʾan lā yastanqidhūhu minh* — "And if the fly should rob them of aught, they could not rescue it from it." *Salb* (robbing, snatching) depicts the fly stealing — landing on idol-offerings (perfume, food, honey smeared on statues), ingesting, departing. *Lā yastanqidhūh* (they could not rescue/deliver it) exposes double impotence: cannot create, cannot even retrieve. Al-Qurṭubī notes the humiliation: worshippers guard idols from flies with whisks — gods needing protection from what they cannot create. Abraham's debate prefigured this: idols cannot hear, see, or avail (19:42; 21:66). The image is empirical: visit any neglected shrine — flies feast on offerings while gods stare.

**Feeble Are the Seeker and the Sought**

*Ḍaʿufa al-ṭālibu wa al-maṭlūb* — "Feeble are the seeker and the sought!" *Ḍaʿufa* (feeble, weak) judges both: *al-ṭālib* (seeker — the worshipper seeking help) and *al-maṭlūb* (sought — the idol sought for help). Both are weak — the seeker for seeking from weakness, the sought for being sought despite it. Al-Rāzī calls this the parable's verdict: idolatry is weakness doubled. Some commentators reverse the roles (seeker = idol seeking the fly's loot, sought = fly) — either way, feebleness pervades. The Qur'an elsewhere contrasts with God's strength (v. 74: *qawī ʿazīz*).

Practically, verse 73 tests every reliance: "Can what I trust create a fly? Can it retrieve what a fly steals — health, time, youth?" Only God passes. Whoever seeks from the Feeble shares feebleness; whoever seeks from the Strong shares strength.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:74

> **And they did not measure God with His true measure. Truly God is Strong, Mighty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Not Measuring God Truly**

*Wa mā qadarū Allāha ḥaqqa qadrih* — "And they did not measure God with His true measure." *Qadr* (*q-d-r*) denotes measure, estimation, worth — also power and decree. *Ḥaqqa qadrih* (His true measure) means His due estimation: majesty deserved, power acknowledged, worship rendered. *Mā qadarū* (they did not measure) indicts idolaters who equated the Creator of flies and firmaments with stones needing fly-whisks — a catastrophic mismeasurement. Al-Ṭabarī explains: they did not honor, know, or worship God as He deserves — assigning Him daughters (53:21), partners (6:100), and rivals. The Qur'an repeats this indictment thrice:

> *"And they measured not God with His true measure."* (Qur'an 6:91; 39:67; and here)

Each context exposes a mode: denying revelation (6:91), denying resurrection-power (39:67), worshipping impotence (here). Al-Rāzī notes the comprehensive failure: mismeasuring God's essence (likening to creatures), attributes (denying power), and rights (dividing worship).

**The Measure of God's Might**

Sūrah 39 elaborates what true measure entails:

> *"And they measured not God with His true measure, though the whole earth shall be His handful on the Day of Resurrection, and the heavens folded in His Right Hand."* (Qur'an 39:67)

The Prophet ﷺ illustrated:

> *"God will grasp the earth and fold the heavens in His Right Hand, saying: 'I am the King! Where are earth's kings?'"* (al-Bukhārī 4812; Muslim 2787, from Ibn ʿUmar and Abū Hurayrah)

Al-Qurṭubī explains: true measure acknowledges transcendence (*tanzīh*) without denial (*taʿṭīl*) and affirmation without likening (*tamthīl*) — God as He described Himself, beyond creaturely measure yet truly Powerful.

**Truly God Is Strong, Mighty**

*Inna Allāha la-qawiyyun ʿazīz* — "Truly God is Strong, Mighty." *Qawī* (Strong) denotes perfect power — creating flies and galaxies effortlessly, sustaining judgment's terrors. *ʿAzīz* (Mighty) denotes irresistible honor — never thwarted, never humbled, never needing allies. The emphatic *inna... la-* (truly... surely) contrasts verse 73's *ḍaʿufa* (feeble): idols feeble, God Strong; seekers abased, God Mighty. Al-Rāzī notes the pairing answers idolatry's motives: idolaters sought strength (intercession, protection) from weakness; true strength resides only with God. The Qur'an elsewhere joins them for victory:

> *"And God will surely help those who help Him — truly God is Strong, Mighty."* (Qur'an 22:40)

**Measuring God Truly Today**

Practically, verse 74 audits every mismeasurement: obeying creatures over the Creator (measuring bosses greater), despairing of forgiveness (measuring mercy smaller), doubting provision (measuring power weaker), worshipping habits (measuring idols worthy). True measure (*ḥaqqa qadr*) manifests in awe (*khashyah*), hope (*rajāʾ*), obedience (*ṭāʿah*), and reliance (*tawakkul*) proportioned to His Strength and Might. The believer magnifies God in prayer (*Allāhu akbar*), trusts Him in crisis (the Strong suffices), and honors Him in obedience (the Mighty deserves). Whoever measures God truly finds idols immeasurably small — and worships the Strong, the Mighty alone, with His true measure.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:75

> **God chooses messengers from among the angels and from among mankind. Truly God is Hearing, Seeing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Divine Choice of Messengers**

*Allāhu yaṣṭafī min al-malāʾikati rusulan wa min al-nās* — "God chooses messengers from among the angels and from among mankind." *Iṣṭifāʾ* (*ṣ-f-w*) denotes pure choosing — selecting the finest, as cream is chosen from milk. *Rusul* (messengers) from angels include Gabriel (revelation), Michael (provision/rain), Isrāfīl (Trumpet), Mālik (Hell's keeper), Riḍwān (Paradise's keeper). From mankind: Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, Muhammad (peace upon them) and all prophets. Al-Ṭabarī explains the context: idolaters objected that God would send a human messenger ("why not an angel?" — 17:94) or that Muhammad ﷺ specifically was chosen ("why not a great man?" — 43:31); the verse affirms choosing belongs to God — from whichever order, whomever He wills. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"God knows best where to place His message."* (Qur'an 6:124)

> *"And thy Lord creates what He wills and chooses; they have no choice."* (Qur'an 28:68)

**Why Angels and Men**

Al-Rāzī explores the wisdom: angels as messengers suit celestial missions (destroying Lot's folk, announcing Isaac's birth, conveying revelation) — pure, swift, terrifying. Humans as messengers suit earthly guidance — relatable, imitable, merciful. An angelic prophet to men would be unapproachable ("we cannot emulate light"); a human prophet to angels would be incongruous. Each order receives its like — mercy's symmetry. The Qur'an elsewhere notes:

> *"And had We made him an angel, We would have made him a man, and confused for them what they confuse."* (Qur'an 6:9)

Ibn Kathīr adds that Muhammad ﷺ, chosen from men, received Gabriel, chosen from angels — the finest of each order meeting in revelation's chain.

**Truly God Is Hearing, Seeing**

*Inna Allāha samīʿun baṣīr* — "Truly God is Hearing, Seeing." Al-Qurṭubī explains the closing: God hears objections to His choosing and sees objectors' envy — yet chooses wisely. Hearing and Seeing also qualify choosing: He hears servants' supplications and sees their states, selecting messengers most fit — most truthful, patient, and merciful. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms such discerning choice:

> *"God chose Adam, Noah, the House of Abraham, and the House of ʿImrān above the worlds."* (Qur'an 3:33)

The Prophet ﷺ described his own choosing:

> *"God chose Kinānah from Ishmael's sons, Quraysh from Kinānah, Hāshim from Quraysh, and me from Hāshim."* (Muslim 2276, from Wāthilah ibn al-Asqaʿ)

**Trusting Divine Choosing**

Practically, verse 75 teaches contentment with divine apportioning: God chooses messengers, and likewise chooses livelihoods, spouses, trials, and stations — *iṣṭifāʾ* pervading providence. Envying another's portion disputes God's choosing as Quraysh disputed Muhammad's ﷺ. The believer instead asks: "Why did God choose *me* for *this* mercy/trial, and how shall I fulfill its trust?" He honors chosen messengers — loving them, obeying them, sending blessings upon them — without elevating them to divinity (they are chosen servants, not choosing gods). And he trusts the Hearing, the Seeing: objections are heard, states are seen, and choosing is wise — in prophets and in providence alike.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:76

> **He knows that which is before them and that which is behind them. And unto God are all matters returned.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Knowledge of Before and Behind**

*Yaʿlamu mā bayna aydīhim wa mā khalfahum* — "He knows that which is before them and that which is behind them." The pronoun refers to messengers (angels and men, v. 75) — or, generally, all creatures. *Mā bayna aydīhim* (what is before them) encompasses futures, Hereafter, and manifest acts; *mā khalfahum* (what is behind them) encompasses pasts, this world left behind, and hidden acts. Al-Ṭabarī explains: God's knowledge surrounds messengers' missions entirely — their pasts (sincerity, preparation), presents (conveyance, struggles), futures (outcomes, rewards). The Qur'an repeats this formula for divine omniscience:

> *"He knows what is before them and what is behind them, and they encompass naught of His knowledge save what He wills."* (Qur'an 2:255, the Throne Verse; cf. 20:110; 21:28)

Al-Rāzī notes the comfort: messengers labor under surveillance of mercy — every rejection witnessed, every tear recorded, every effort known.

**Unto God Are All Matters Returned**

*Wa ilā Allāhi turjaʿu al-umūr* — "And unto God are all matters returned." *Turjaʿ* (returned) is passive: all affairs — missions, disputes, schemes, histories — revert to God for judgment and requital. Al-Qurṭubī explains the context: messengers are opposed, messages distorted, outcomes delayed — but return is certain: every matter concludes before God. The Qur'an repeats this return:

> *"And unto God are all matters returned."* (Qur'an 2:210; 3:109; 8:44; 35:4; 57:5)

Ibn Kathīr notes the theology: creation originates from God (*minhu al-badʾ*) and returns to Him (*ilayhi al-maʿād*) — history is a round-trip, not a random walk.

**Return as Consolation and Warning**

Classical scholars draw both edges. Consolation for the righteous: labors seemingly wasted — Noah's 950 years, the Prophet's ﷺ Ṭāʾif wounds, parents' unheeded counsels — return to God, who wastes nothing:

> *"Truly God neglects not the reward of the virtuous."* (Qur'an 7:170; 11:115)

Warning for the wicked: schemes seemingly successful — Pharaoh's drownings, Quraysh's boycotts, modern oppressions — return to God, who requites precisely. The Prophet ﷺ counseled entrusting matters to the Returned-to:

> *"If you ask, ask God; if you seek help, seek it from God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

Al-Qushayrī added: since all matters return to God, the wise return them preemptively — referring decisions to Him in consultation (*istikrārah*), grievances in supplication, and outcomes in contentment.

**Working for the Return**

Practically, verse 76 reorients effort from appearances to returns: do not measure deeds by immediate results (converts, likes, victories) but by their return — "how will this matter arrive before God?" The teacher's quiet lessons, the mother's hidden supplications, the worker's honest hours — all known (before and behind) and all returning. Modern anxiety over outcomes — careers, children, causes — dissolves: outcomes are God's portrayed knowledge and promised return, not man's burden. Labor sincerely in between (before and behind known), entrust matters to the Returned-to, and await the Day when every matter, great and small, arrives exactly where it belongs — with God.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:77

> **O you who believe! Bow, prostrate, and worship your Lord! And do good, that haply you may prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Call to Bow and Prostrate**

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū irkaʿū wa usjudū* — "O you who believe! Bow, prostrate." The vocative (*yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū*) summons believers specifically after mankind generally (vv. 1, 5, 73) — the sūrah's climax addresses its heirs. *Rukūʿ* (bowing) and *sujūd* (prostration) are prayer's pillars — humility's postures. Al-Ṭabarī notes the verse commands prayer's essence: bow bodies as hearts bow. The Qur'an elsewhere joins them:

> *"O Mary, be devout to thy Lord, prostrate, and bow with those who bow."* (Qur'an 3:43)

> *"Thou seest them bowing, prostrating, seeking bounty from God and pleasure."* (Qur'an 48:29)

Ibn Kathīr records that this verse (with verse 18) occasions prostration of recitation (*sajdat al-tilāwah*) by Shāfiʿīs and others — the reader obeys by prostrating, embodying the command. The Prophet ﷺ taught prayer's centrality:

> *"The closest a servant is to his Lord is in prostration, so increase supplication therein."* (Muslim 482, from Abū Hurayrah)

**Worship Your Lord**

*Wa uʿbudū rabbakum* — "and worship your Lord!" *ʿIbādah* (worship) generalizes beyond prayer: all devotion — fasting, charity, pilgrimage, remembrance, obedience. *Rabbakum* (your Lord) personalizes: worship Him who nurtured you (vv. 1, 5) — gratitude's logic. Al-Rāzī notes the sequence: specific postures (*irkaʿū, usjudū*) then universal worship (*uʿbudū*) — prayer as worship's heart, worship as prayer's horizon. The Qur'an elsewhere defines worship's scope:

> *"And I did not create jinn and mankind save to worship Me."* (Qur'an 51:56)

Al-Qurṭubī explains: worship here includes abandoning idols (vv. 30–31), magnifying rituals (v. 32), and sacrificing with *taqwā* (v. 37) — the sūrah's whole program summed in two words.

**Do Good, That Haply You May Prosper**

*Wa ifʿalū al-khayra laʿallakum tufliḥūn* — "And do good, that haply you may prosper." *Khayr* (good) is comprehensive: every benefit — kindness to parents, charity to poor, honesty in trade, reconciliation, knowledge-sharing. *Ifʿalū* (do) demands action, not sentiment. *Laʿalla* (that haply) expresses hopeful means: doing good is prosperity's path. *Tuflīḥūn* (*f-l-ḥ*, to split earth for harvest) evokes farmers' success — felicity as harvest of good. The Qur'an elsewhere promises:

> *"And whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it."* (Qur'an 99:7)

The Prophet ﷺ detailed *khayr*'s breadth:

> *"Every good deed is charity: a smile, guiding the lost, removing harm from the road."* (al-Bukhārī 6021; Muslim 1009, from Abū Dharr and others, in meaning)

**Worship and Goodness as Prosperity's Pair**

Verse 77 pairs vertical (*irkaʿū, usjudū, uʿbudū*) with horizontal (*ifʿalū al-khayr*): prayer without goodness is hollow, goodness without worship is rootless. Classical scholars called this Islam's two wings: *ḥuqūq Allāh* (God's rights) and *ḥuqūq al-ʿibād* (servants' rights). Modern distortions sever them — ritualists neglecting character, humanists neglecting prayer — and verse 77 reunites: bow, prostrate, worship *and* do good. Prosperity (*falāḥ*) — the adhān's promise ("come to prosperity") — crowns both. Whoever prays humbly and does good widely will prosper doubly: in this world through tranquillity and love, in the next through Gardens — for *falāḥ* is harvest, and worship-plus-goodness is its seed.

## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:78

> **And strive for God as He should be striven for. He has chosen [for] you—and has placed no hardship for you in the religion—the creed of your father Abraham. He named you muslims aforetime, and herein, that the Messenger may be a witness for you, and that you may be witnesses for mankind. So perform the prayer and give the alms, and hold fast to God. He is your Master. How excellent a Master, and how excellent a Helper!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Strive for God as He Should Be Striven For**

*Wa jāhidū fī Allāhi ḥaqqa jihādih* — "And strive for God as He should be striven for." *Jihād* (*j-h-d*) denotes exerting utmost effort — against the soul's caprice, Satan's whispering, disbelief's hostility, and injustice's oppression. *Ḥaqqa jihādih* (as He should be striven for / His true striving) demands wholehearted, sincere, complete exertion — fearing no blame (al-Ṭabarī), seeking no display (al-Qurṭubī), sparing no lawful means. Al-Rāzī notes the parallel with verse 74's *ḥaqqa qadrih* (true measure): as God deserves true estimation, He deserves true striving. The Qur'an elsewhere describes such strivers:

> *"And those who strive for Us — We shall guide them in Our ways."* (Qur'an 29:69)

The Prophet ﷺ, asked about the best *jihād*, answered diversely by context: "a just word before a tyrant" (Abū Dāwūd 4344; al-Tirmidhī 2174), the pilgrim's ḥajj (al-Bukhārī 1520), and striving against the soul — comprehensiveness confirming *jihād*'s breadth. The "greater striving" narration (returning from Tabūk to struggle against souls — al-Bayhaqī) captures one dimension scholars affirm in meaning.

**Chosen for the Creed of Abraham, Without Hardship**

*Huwa ijtabākum wa mā jaʿala ʿalaykum fī al-dīni min ḥaraj* — "He has chosen [for] you — and has placed no hardship for you in the religion." *Ijtibāʾ* (choosing, electing) honors the community: God selected them for final revelation, final prophet, and witnessing mission. *Ḥaraj* (hardship, constriction) is negated absolutely (*min* + negation): no unbearable burden in religion — concessions for travelers, sick, and compelled; ease in ablution, prayer, and fasting; expiations for lapses. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"God desires ease for you, and He does not desire hardship for you."* (Qur'an 2:185)

> *"God desires to lighten for you, and man was created weak."* (Qur'an 4:28)

*Milla abīkum Ibrāhīm* — "the creed of your father Abraham": *millah* is the followed way — Abraham's ḥanīf monotheism (2:135; 16:123), inherited by Arabs through Ishmael and by all Muslims through allegiance. Al-Qurṭubī explains: Abraham is "father" by lineage (for Arabs) and by spiritual paternity (for all believers) — his supplication "make us muslims" (2:128) founded the community.

**Named Muslims, Witnesses for Mankind**

*Huwa sammākumu al-muslimīna min qablu wa fī hādhā* — "He named you muslims aforetime, and herein." *Sammā* (named) is attributed to God (or Abraham, by other readings): the name *muslimūn* (submitters) was bestowed in prior scriptures (*min qabl* — aforetime) and in this Qur'an (*fī hādhā* — herein). Al-Ṭabarī favors God as namer, citing Abraham's prayer answered by divine naming. *Li-yakūna al-rasūlu shahīdan ʿalaykum wa takūnū shuhadāʾa ʿalā al-nās* — "that the Messenger may be a witness for you, and that you may be witnesses for mankind." *Shahīd* (witness) testifies: the Prophet ﷺ witnesses his community's reception (conveyance completed), and the community witnesses mankind's (truth transmitted). The Qur'an elsewhere establishes:

> *"Thus did We make you a middle community, that you may be witnesses for mankind and that the Messenger may be a witness for you."* (Qur'an 2:143)

Al-Rāzī notes witnessing requires uprightness (*ʿadālah*): God ennobles the community as His testimony's bearers — a dignity demanding justice, truthfulness, and exemplarity. The Prophet ﷺ described the scene:

> *"Noah will be asked 'Didst thou convey?'... and Muhammad's community will testify."* (al-Bukhārī 3339, 4487, from Abū Saʿīd)

**Prayer, Alms, Holding Fast — and the Excellent Master**

*Fa-aqīmū al-ṣalāta wa ātū al-zakāta wa iʿtaṣimū bi-Allāh* — "So perform the prayer and give the alms, and hold fast to God." The sūrah's final commands recapitulate its program: prayer (vv. 26, 35, 41, 77), alms (vv. 28, 36, 41), and *iʿtiṣām* (holding fast — clinging to God's rope, covenant, and protection). *Huwa mawlākum* — "He is your Master": *mawlā* (Master, Protector, Patron) — the true *Mawlā* versus verse 13's *biʾsa al-mawlā* (evil ally). *Fa-niʿma al-mawlā wa niʿma al-naṣīr* — "How excellent a Master, and how excellent a Helper!": *niʿma* (how excellent!) is praise's superlative — God's mastership and help surpass all. The Qur'an elsewhere affirms:

> *"And hold fast to God; He is your Master — an excellent Master and an excellent Helper."* (Qur'an 8:40)

The sūrah that opened with the Hour's terror closes with the Master's excellence: revere Him (v. 1), dispute not without knowledge (vv. 3, 8), worship not on brinks (v. 11), sacrifice with *taqwā* (v. 37), resist wrong justly (vv. 39–40), heed warners (v. 49), ponder parables (v. 73), strive truly — and hold fast to God. For He chose you, eased you, named you, and witnesses you — how excellent a Master, and how excellent a Helper!
