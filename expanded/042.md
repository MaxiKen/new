# Sūrah al-Shūrā (Chapter 42) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter of Counsel: Name and Identity**

Sūrah al-Shūrā, "The Counsel," is the forty-second chapter of the Qur'an, and it takes its name from a single phrase in verse 38 describing the believers' communal life: *wa amruhum shūrā baynahum* — "their affair being counsel among them." No other chapter of the Book is named for a principle of collective decision-making, and the choice of name is itself a quiet declaration: in a sūrah full of thunder about divine sovereignty, revelation, and the Last Day, the one characteristic chosen to label the whole stands in the middle of a portrait of the believers' character. The chapter is also known, as the classical sources record, by its mysterious opening syllables, Ḥā Mīm ʿAyn Sīn Qāf (Āl, Ṭs). Like most of the Makkan sūrahs, it addresses the great questions that dominated the first decade of revelation — the Oneness of God, the reality of the messengers, the certainty of the Gathering — and it does so in the presence of a small, besieged community in Makkah whose members were being asked to abandon the religion of their fathers for a Book that their own tribesmen called the speech of a fabricator.

**Its Place Among the Ḥawāmīm**

Al-Shūrā is the third of the seven consecutive sūrahs — 40 through 46 — that open with the two letters ḥāʾ and mīm, the group traditionally called the Ḥawāmīm. The seven share a distinctive musical and thematic texture: they are, in the main, Makkan revelations addressed to the heart of the idolatrous opposition, each announcing a divine revelation that precedes and exceeds every human institution, and each climaxing in the double certainty of God's power and the coming judgment. Within this family, al-Shūrā occupies a special station. It is the only ḥā mīm sūrah that does not begin by declaring the Book's descent; instead it opens with an allusion to what has been revealed to all prophets (Ṭs), and it is the only one whose opening letters are augmented by a second, separate set — ʿAyn Sīn Qāf. The sūrah ends, fittingly, with the most systematic description of the modes of divine communication found anywhere in the Qur'an (42:51–53), as though the chapter that began by gesturing toward revelation's unity across all prophets had saved for its finale the precise account of how that revelation reaches a human being at all.

**The Time and Place of Revelation**

The overwhelming majority of scholars count al-Shūrā among the Makkan revelations, and the classical sources note that it followed directly upon Sūrah Fuṣṣilat (41) in the order of descent (JJ). But the chapter also carries clear traces of the later, Madinan period, and the commentators debate the status of several verses. Some hold that verses 23–26 belong to the Madinan period, since they are read as an address touching the Helpers (al-Anṣār) of Madinah (Āl, IJ, Ṭs); others add verse 27, understanding it as a reference to the poorest Companions who sheltered in the Prophet's mosque — the People of the Veranda, Ahl al-Ṣuffah (Āl; see 2:273c); and still others assign verses 39–41, or portions of them, to Madinah because of their legislation concerning retaliation and self-defense (Āl, IJ, Ṭs). This measured disagreement is itself instructive: the sūrah was being applied by the earliest community to situations that arose years after its descent, which is precisely what a revealed principle is for. The chapter's Makkan core — its argument with the Quraysh over idols, the Hour, and the integrity of the Prophet — remains intact, while its later verses legislate for the believing society that the Makkan verses had made possible.

**The Architecture of the Sūrah**

The sūrah is framed by paired references to the continuity of divine revelation: verse 3 speaks of God revealing "unto thee and unto those before thee," and verses 51–52 describe the modes by which revelation reaches every prophet; after each of these declarations stands an assertion of God's Omnipotence — verse 4 and verse 53 respectively. Between those two pillars the chapter conducts a complete argument. It affirms that the religion prescribed to Muhammad ﷺ is the same religion enjoined upon Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus (v. 13), and warns that the communities of the past divided only after knowledge reached them, out of mutual envy (v. 14). It answers the Quraysh's charges — that Muhammad forged the Qur'an (vv. 24), that his message disrupts the ancestral order — by pointing to God's sole ownership of the keys of the heavens and the earth (v. 12), His measured distribution of provision (vv. 27–28), and His signs in creation (vv. 29, 32–34). At the heart of the chapter stands a sustained portrait of the believers (vv. 36–43): patient, forgiving, prayerful, consultative, generous, and just — a community portrait drawn to replace the tribal honor-code of pre-Islamic Makkah with the honor of the Hereafter. And throughout, the sūrah returns to the theme that no creature has a protector besides God (vv. 6, 8–9, 29, 44, 46), so that the reader is progressively detached from every false support before being summoned to the only Refuge.

**The Great Themes in Order**

A closer map of the chapter's movement reveals its care. After the opening letters (vv. 1–2), the revelation theme is announced (vv. 3–5): God, the Mighty, the Wise, reveals as He did to those before, while the heavens are well-nigh rent from the greatness of what is above them and the angels hymn His praise and seek forgiveness for the earth's inhabitants. Then come the two audiences — the Prophet is told he is no guardian over those who take protectors apart from God (v. 6), and is given his mission: to warn the Mother of Cities, Makkah, and all around it, of the Day of Gathering (v. 7). The unity of the human race under a single God is then explored against the fact of human division (vv. 8–10), and the theme of provision is introduced (v. 12) after the famous declaration of God's transcendence — *laysa ka-mithlihi shayʾ* — "naught is like unto Him" (v. 11). Verses 13–15 deliver the chapter's central religious-historical claim: one religion, enjoined on all the prophets, now prescribed to Muhammad; division came only after knowledge, out of envy; and the Prophet is commanded to stand firm, to summon, and to declare that between him and the disputers there is no argument, for God will gather all. Verses 16–20 then confront those who argue about God after responding to Him, affirm the Book and the Balance, mock the hasteners of the Hour, and set the famous contrast of the two harvests — the harvest of the Hereafter, which God increases, and the harvest of this world, which ends at death. Verses 21–26 address the idolaters' invented religion, the terror of the wrongdoers when they see what they have earned, the glad tidings of the Gardens, the Prophet's request of "affection among kinsfolk," the charge of fabrication, and the astonishing announcement that God accepts repentance. Verses 27–35 treat the divine economy of provision and affliction: measured provision, rain after despair, creation as sign, misfortune as the fruit of one's own hands, ships upon the sea, and the certain end of those who dispute the signs. And with verse 36 the sūrah turns fully to the character of the believers — the famous passage on forgiveness of anger, prayer, counsel, spending, and self-defense (vv. 36–43) — before the closing warnings (vv. 44–47), the consolation of the Prophet (v. 48), the sovereignty of God over offspring (vv. 49–50), and the final revelation sequence (vv. 51–53) that closes the chapter where it began: with God speaking to human beings, and all affairs journeying unto Him.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:1

> **Ḥā. Mīm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Set of Mysterious Letters in the Chapter's Own Opening**

The sūrah opens with the pair ḥāʾ and mīm, the two letters that head seven consecutive chapters of the Qur'an (40–46) and two others elsewhere (Sūrahs 42's siblings are joined by 42:1–2's five letters; the full Ḥawāmīm family is Sūrahs 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, and 46). These letters belong to the *al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*, the "disconnected letters" with which twenty-nine sūrahs begin and whose ultimate meaning the majority of commentators — from the generation of the Companions onward — declare to be known only to God; see 2:1c. The letters are recited by their names, not as words: *ḥāʾ*, *mīm*, as one recites the alphabet itself, and every listener, then and now, experiences the same pause: meaning is suspended for an instant, and the listener is brought to the very threshold of speech before speech begins. For the Makkan Arabs — a people who prided themselves on eloquence as other nations pride themselves on arms — the effect was deliberate and disarming: the Qur'an opens its argument not with a proposition but with the raw material of all propositions, the letters of their own language, as if to say: the miracle you are about to hear is built from the very sounds you already possess, and you cannot assemble them into its like.

**Why Ḥā Mīm Recurs Across Seven Sūrahs**

The repetition of the same pair across seven consecutive chapters invited the commentators to seek a purpose in the recurrence. The classical scholars offered several lines of reading. Some noted that in every one of the Ḥawāmīm except al-Shūrā the letters are followed immediately by a declaration about the Book — *the revelation of the Book is from God* (40:2; 45:2; 46:2), *ḥā mīm, by the manifest Book* (43:2; 44:2), *the revelation of the Book from God, the Mighty, the Wise* (45:2) — so that the pair functions as a heralding call: an invocation of attention before the announcement of revelation. Others treated the recurrence as a musical and mnemonic device on the scale of the whole muṣḥaf: seven sūrahs bound together by a shared refrain, recited in sequence, create a distinct "movement" within the Book, and the early Muslims are reported to have treasured the Ḥawāmīm as a group. A famous tradition transmitted from Ibn Masʿūd — preserved by the early traditionists — likens the seven to the adorned collar of the Qur'an, calling them its *dībāj* or ornament; whether or not every transmitter's chain is impeccable, the saying faithfully reflects the veneration the earliest generation showed these chapters. Some exegetes went further and saw in ḥā and mīm an allusion to divine names — the Merciful (al-Raḥmān) and the King (al-Malik), or the Living (al-Ḥayy) and the Majestic (al-Majīd) — letters standing as initials for attributes; and others read them as an oath God takes by His own perfections. All such readings were offered by the commentators as possibilities, never as certainties, and the safest position remains the one the earliest authorities took: the letters are true and real, their inner sense is with God, and the reader's duty is reverent recitation, not impatient deciphering.

**The Letters as a Threshold of Humility**

There is a spiritual pedagogy in the very fact that the Qur'an — a Book of guidance, law, and promise — begins so many of its chapters with what it does not explain. The believer who recites Ḥā Mīm in prayer is made, for the length of two syllables, to stand before knowledge that is not his; and this small daily rehearsal of epistemic humility is exactly the posture the whole sūrah will demand of its audience. Al-Shūrā is a chapter about argument — about people who "argue about God" (v. 16), who dispute the Hour (v. 18), who dispute the signs (v. 35) — and it opens with letters that cannot be argued with, only received. The intellect is not abolished; it is situated. A mystery at the threshold teaches the soul that it is a servant before it is a scholar, and that some doors are opened from the other side. When the Prophet ﷺ was asked about the meaning of such letters, the transmitted reports show him reciting them without commentary, and his Companions — Abū Bakr and ʿAlī among them, as recorded in the classical sources — spoke of the openings of sūrahs as the "mystery" and "quintessence" of the Book. The letters are thus a permanent invitation: every time the sūrah is recited, the servant enters through a gate he does not fully understand, and finds on the other side — immediately, in the following verse — the next pair of letters, and beyond them the revelation itself.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:2

> **ʿAyn. Sīn. Qāf.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Signature Unique Among the Ḥawāmīm**

No other sūrah of the ḥā mīm family carries additional separated letters after its opening pair; ʿAyn Sīn Qāf is unique to al-Shūrā. The commentators drew the natural inference that the extra letters were added to distinguish this chapter (Ṭs), and they observed a structural reason for the distinction: al-Shūrā is the only ḥā mīm sūrah that does not begin with a declaration about the Qur'an; instead of "the revelation of the Book," it opens with an allusion to what has been revealed to all the prophets (Ṭs). Its business from the first breath is the unity of revelation across time — a theme that reaches its fullest expression in the final verses of the chapter (42:51–53). The five letters together, Ḥā Mīm ʿAyn Sīn Qāf, became so closely attached to the sūrah in early usage that the chapter was known by them as a name (Āl, Ṭs), much as Sūrahs 2, 3, 7, and others were known by their opening letters in the first generations.

**The Letter-Wisdom of ʿAyn, Sīn, and Qāf**

Because the disconnected letters admit no single demonstrated meaning, the exegetical tradition gathered around them a garden of reverent possibilities, none of them binding and several of them beautiful. One line of interpretation, transmitted in the early schools, reads the three letters as pointing to divine attributes, the ʿayn to God's Knowledge (ʿilm), the sīn to His Resplendence (sanāʾ), and the qāf to His Power (qudrah) — so that the chapter opens, in code, with exactly the three perfections its argument will deploy: the knowing God who reveals, the glorious God whose heavens are well-nigh rent, the powerful God who holds the keys (IJ). Another reading attaches the letters to three divine names — al-ʿAzīz, the Mighty; al-Salām, the Peace; al-Qādir, the Powerful — which would make the opening an invocation of the very names that conclude the sūrah's frame verses ("God, the Mighty, the Wise," v. 3; "He is the Exalted, the Magnificent," v. 4). Still others proposed that ʿAyn Sīn Qāf is itself a name of the Qur'an (IJ, Ṭs), or that the letters stand as an oath God takes upon His names (IJ). The sober consensus, following the earliest authorities, is that all of these may be possible and none is certain; the letters are recited, preserved, and loved as part of the revealed text, and their full sense rests with God.

**Why the Letters Suit This Particular Sūrah**

However one reads the letters, their presence here is apt in a way the commentators noticed. Al-Shūrā is the sūrah of counsel (v. 38), and counsel presupposes that no single human mind possesses the whole truth — that even the most learned must gather, listen, and consult. The chapter that will legislate consultation among believers begins by confronting its readers with letters whose meaning even the most learned cannot exhaust, as though to say: if the very alphabet of the Book contains depths beyond every scholar, how much more do the affairs of religion and life require humility, mutual counsel, and reference back to God? The letters likewise sound the chapter's great theme of divine speech: verses 51–53 will describe how God speaks to human beings — by revelation, from behind a veil, through a messenger — and it is fitting that the sūrah's own speech should begin with sounds that are not quite words, hovering between the angelic and the human, as revelation itself hovers between heaven and earth. The reciter who passes through ʿAyn Sīn Qāf without demanding its secret has already learned the lesson the whole chapter teaches: trust the One who speaks, even — especially — when one does not yet grasp the whole of what is said.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:3

> **Thus does He reveal unto thee and unto those before thee — God, the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Thus": The Manner, the Sameness, and the Sequence**

The verse opens with the particle *kadhālika* — "thus" — which the commentators read in two complementary ways. It may refer to the manner of revelation: "in this way does God reveal unto thee" (IJ) — a reading that harmonizes remarkably with the end of the sūrah, where verses 51–53 describe precisely how God communicates with human beings, so that the chapter is clasped between two statements about the *how* of revelation. Or it may refer to the sameness of the revelation itself: just as God revealed His words to the prophets before Muhammad ﷺ, so He revealed them to him (IJ, IK, Ṭs) — one message, one religion, one source, delivered across the centuries to Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and finally to the seal of the prophets. Some exegetes add a third nuance: the verse may refer to the very contents of this sūrah as something all prophets have received (IJ, Ṭs) — the truths of divine unity, of accountability, of the Gathering — or, more generally, to God's delivering reports from the realm of the Unseen to all prophets (IJ, Ṭs). Every reading converges on a single assertion, and it is the foundation of everything this sūrah will say: revelation is not an innovation of Muhammad's, and Islam is not a new religion in the sense the Makkan idolaters imagined. It is the continuation of one divine address that began with the first prophet and reaches its final form in the Qur'an.

**One Speech, Many Messengers**

The verse names God with two attributes — the Mighty, the Wise — and the commentators mined the pairing. God is Mighty (al-ʿAzīz) because no power can obstruct His sending of revelation, and Wise (al-Ḥakīm) because He sends it at the right time, to the right people, in the right language and measure; the same pairing recurs in the closing frame of the chapter (42:51–53). Al-Ṭūsī notes that when joined to the Mighty, the name al-Ḥakīm may also be understood as "the Determiner" (al-Muḥkim), the One who sets every matter firmly and definitively (Ṭs). The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same point in the same structure, as when it says:

> *"We have revealed unto thee, as We revealed unto Noah and the prophets after him, and as We revealed unto Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the Tribes, and Jesus, Job, Jonah, Aaron, and Solomon — and unto David We gave the Psalms."* (Qur'an 4:163)

And again, speaking of the common content of all revelation:

> *"And We sent no messenger before thee but that We revealed unto him, saying: There is no god but I, so worship Me."* (Qur'an 21:25)

The logical force of this for the Makkan audience was direct. The Quraysh accused Muhammad ﷺ of fabricating a scripture to exalt himself; the verse replies that the substance of what he recites — the Oneness of God, the summons to worship, the warning of a reckoning — is the oldest message on earth, the very message their own traditions connected with Abraham, whose shrine stood in their city. If the message is ancient, the messenger cannot be an impostor inventing novelty; he is the latest link in a chain whose earlier links they already acknowledged.

**What This Means for the Believer's Understanding of Religious History**

This verse quietly supplies the Qur'anic principle for understanding the history of religion: all authentic revelation comes from one source, and therefore the prophets form one brotherhood rather than competing founders. The Prophet ﷺ expressed the same truth when he said:

> *"The prophets are like brothers from one father: their mothers differ, but their religion is one."* (al-Bukhārī 3443; Muslim 2365, from Abū Hurayrah)

For the Muslim reader, the verse forbids two errors at once. It forbids the error of imagining that Islam invalidates everything before it — the religion of the prophets is one, and the Qur'an is its final exposition. And it forbids the error of imagining that previous scriptures, as they are followed today, can displace the final revelation — for the Mighty and Wise God sent the later message precisely because the earlier covenants had been altered and abandoned. The verse also carries a deeply personal consolation that the classical commentators heard in it (IJ, Ṭs): the Prophet ﷺ, standing alone in Makkah against a whole city, is told that he stands in a line — that the One who revealed to him revealed to those before him, that his solitude is an illusion, and that the same divine power that carried Noah through the flood and Moses through Pharaoh will carry the final messenger through the opposition of the Quraysh. Every believer who recites this verse while feeling isolated in the truth can draw the same strength: one is never alone in a message that began before one was born and will outlast every opponent.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:4

> **Unto Him belongs whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth, and He is the Exalted, the Magnificent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Declaration of Total Ownership in Two Hemispheres**

The verse's first clause — *lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — is one of the Qur'an's most repeated refrains, appearing in variant forms across the Book (see, e.g., 2:255, 284; 3:109, 129; 4:126, 131–32, 171; 10:68; 22:64; 53:31). Repetition in the Qur'an is never accidental: every recurrence is placed where an argument needs the same foundation restated. Here, directly after the assertion that God reveals to all prophets, the refrain supplies the ground of that assertion: the One who speaks through every messenger is the One who owns everything — the heavens above and the earth below — and ownership of all things entails authority over all speech. No treasury of the Unseen is outside His possession, no human heart outside His knowledge, no throne of power outside His dominion. The clause functions like the title-deed of the universe, and the sūrah will unfold the implications across its fifty-three verses: because everything belongs to Him, provision is His to give or withhold (vv. 12, 27), protectors other than Him are powerless (vv. 6, 9), the Hour is His to bring (vv. 17–18), offspring are His to grant (vv. 49–50), and the final return of all affairs is to Him (v. 53).

**The Exalted, the Magnificent: Names That Frame the Chapter**

The verse closes with two names — *al-ʿAlī*, the Exalted, and *al-ʿAẓīm*, the Magnificent — and together they form the second half of the sūrah's opening frame. Verse 3 ended with "God, the Mighty, the Wise"; verse 4 ends with "the Exalted, the Magnificent"; and the closing of the chapter will echo the same structure, with verse 53 affirming that all affairs journey unto God after verse 51's "Truly He is Exalted, Wise." The names are chosen to answer the two spiritual dangers of the revelation theme. The Exalted answers the danger of imagining God as too remote to speak: elevation in the Qur'anic idiom is not absence — the Qur'an says of the same exalted God that He is "with you wheresoever you are" (57:4) and nearer to the human being than the jugular vein (50:16); His exaltation is a statement of rank, not of distance. The Magnificent answers the danger of imagining that the affairs of so vast a kingdom could overlook any creature: grandeur in the Qur'anic idiom is precisely what guarantees attention, for the One who is great beyond all comparison is also, as the next verse will say, the One whose angels seek forgiveness for the inhabitants of the earth.

**The Psychological Release in Absolute Ownership**

There is a profound psychological release in this verse for its original hearers and for every generation after. The Makkan believers were a community of outcasts: their property was threatened, their kin were hostile, their lives were in danger from a city that regarded itself as the center of Arabia. To such people the verse says: whatever the Quraysh own — the wells, the trade, the sanctuary, the prestige — the true ownership of the heavens and the earth belongs to Another, and the claimants will not hold their claim forever. The believer who internalizes this refrain is gradually weaned from the two great anxieties that torment human life: the fear of deprivation (cured by knowing that the Owner provides) and the fear of human power (cured by knowing that every power is itself owned). This is why the refrain recurs at moments of crisis throughout the Qur'an: at the mention of the Throne (2:255), at the discussion of inheritance and war (3:129; 4:126), at the argument against those who ascribe partners to God (10:68). Wherever human beings are tempted to absolutize a creature — a ruler, a market, a tribe, a state — the Qur'an answers with the same quiet sentence: unto Him belongs whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth. The sentence is a creed, a consolation, and a warning in one.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:5

> **The heavens are well-nigh rent asunder from above, while the angels hymn the praise of their Lord and seek forgiveness for those on earth. Yea! Truly God is the Forgiving, the Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Cosmos That Trembles at Its Own Creator**

The verse opens with a startling image: *takādu al-samāwātu yatafaṭṭarna min fawqihinna* — "the heavens are well-nigh rent asunder from above." The commentators offered several readings of what rends them. One line of interpretation, reported by al-Ṭūsī, reads the phrase as a statement of the heavens' separation from the greatness of what is above them — the angels, the higher heavens, the Throne — as though the lower heaven strains toward its own limit before the majesty that transcends it (Ṭs). Others read the tearing as arising from the might and greatness of God Himself (IJ, IK, Ṭ, Z): creation, in this reading, is held in being by a power so immense that the fabric of the cosmos is, so to speak, always at the point of giving way before it — and only His sustaining will keeps it intact. Both readings converge on the same awe: the universe is not a machine that runs by itself but a garment held by its Owner, and the heavens are described as perpetually on the verge of being overcome by the very glory they contain.

**The Idolaters' Claim and the Cosmic Outrage**

A third reading, favored by several major exegetes (IJ, Ṭs, Z), links the image to the parallel passage in Sūrah Maryam, where the same rending is tied to a specific human crime:

> *"The heavens are well-nigh rent thereby, and the earth split asunder, and the mountains made to come crashing down — that they should claim for the Compassionate a child."* (Qur'an 19:90–91)

Read in this light, the verse means that the Makkan idolaters' claims — their ascription of daughters to God, their worship of created things beside Him — are so monstrous that the cosmos itself recoils: the heavens strain to tear, as a body strains to expel what is foreign to it. This reading gives the verse its full context, for the sūrah will spend its middle sections refuting precisely the idolatrous inventions of the Quraysh (vv. 21, 24) and their contempt for daughters (vv. 49–50). The poetic logic is exact: the claim that God has associates or offspring is not merely an intellectual error; it is an offense against the very structure of reality, which is why the Qur'an elsewhere says of such a claim:

> *"They said: The Compassionate has taken a child. You have certainly brought forth a monstrous thing. The heavens are well-nigh rent thereby..."* (Qur'an 19:88–90)

**Angels Who Intercede for a Rebellious Earth**

Set against the cosmic trembling stands the second half of the verse: the angels "hymn the praise of their Lord and seek forgiveness for those on earth." The juxtaposition is deliberate and astonishing. The creatures nearest to God — those whose worship is purest — do not spend their existence condemning the earth; they spend it asking forgiveness for its inhabitants. The Qur'an describes this intercession elsewhere:

> *"Those who bear the Throne and those around it hymn the praise of their Lord, and believe in Him, and seek forgiveness for those who believe: Our Lord, Thou embracest all things in Mercy and Knowledge; so forgive those who repent and follow Thy path, and shield them from the punishment of the Blaze."* (Qur'an 40:7)

The scholars note the harmony this creates with the verse's closing names — the Forgiving, the Merciful — and with the preceding verse's refrain of divine ownership: the God who owns all things is not a distant proprietor but a Lord whose court is filled with intercessors for His creatures. Theologically, this verse quietly refutes any picture of God as eager to punish: the heavenly order itself is arranged around mercy, and the angels' constant petition is the visible sign of a mercy that precedes every human merit. If the angels ask forgiveness for the earth's inhabitants — including, in the general reading of the verse, believers and even those not yet believing — how much more readily does the Forgiving Lord receive the repentance of those who turn to Him? The Prophet ﷺ expressed the same divine priority in the ḥadīth qudsī:

> *"My mercy prevails over My wrath."* (al-Bukhārī 3194; Muslim 2751, from Abū Hurayrah)

**A Verse of Two Horizons**

The verse places the believer between two horizons. Above is a heaven whose fabric strains before divine majesty — calling the soul to awe, to the recognition that it stands before a greatness that the entire cosmos cannot contain. Around and within is an earth for which the angels seek forgiveness — calling the soul to hope, to the recognition that it is the object of heavenly intercession before it has even asked. Awe and hope together produce exactly the disposition the sūrah will call for: not the paralysis of terror, which forgets mercy, and not the presumption of safety, which forgets majesty, but the walking attentiveness of the believer who knows how great God is and how loved the creature is. When the heavens are described in other passages as splitting and rolling up at the end of time (25:25; 55:37; 69:16; 73:18; 82:1; 84:1), the same double awareness is the believer's preparation: the rending that terrifies the wrongdoer is, for the one who has lived under the angels' intercession, the tearing open of the veil before the Face of the Merciful.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:6

> **As for those who take protectors apart from Him, God is Keeper over them, and thou art not a guardian over them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Strange Arithmetic of False Protectors**

The verse describes a class of people by a single decisive action: they "take protectors apart from Him" — *ittakhadhū min dūnihi awliyāʾ* — meaning that alongside God, or instead of Him, they adopt patrons: idols of stone, spirits of the unseen, powerful human beings, or their own desires. The Qur'an returns to this image constantly because it diagnoses the deepest structure of idolatry: the idolater is not primarily a person who denies God — most of the Quraysh acknowledged a supreme Creator — but a person who distributes God's exclusive rights among other protectors. The sūrah will develop the point across several verses: such people are found "taking protectors apart from Him" (v. 9, 46), and the whole argument of the chapter is that the arrangement is doomed, because protectors who own nothing can protect nothing. The classical commentators catalogued the parallel passages (see 7:3, 30; 29:41; 39:3; 45:10) and noted how each adds a different facet: in 29:41 the protectors are likened to a spider's web, the frailest of houses; in 7:3 they are called an invention; in 39:3 their worship is shown to be a means-seeking that the idols cannot answer.

**God as Keeper: Witness, Accountant, and Requiter**

The verse's reply to the idolaters is the divine name applied in a special construction: *Allāhu ḥafīẓun ʿalayhim* — "God is Keeper over them." Ibn Kathīr explains that the keeping means that God witnesses them, keeps a full account of their deeds, and will requite them in full (IK). The word *ḥafīẓ* carries the sense of a guardian who neither loses sight of what is entrusted to him nor allows it to be lost; applied to God in this verse, it means that no act of the idolaters — no sneer at the Prophet, no persecution of the believers, no hour of worship before a stone — escapes the divine record. The image is not merely threatening; it is also a promise of justice. If God were not Keeper, the wrongs done to the early Muslims in Makkah would vanish into the indifference of history. Because He is Keeper, every wrong is preserved, weighed, and — at the appointed time — requited with perfect fairness. The believers are being told, in effect: you do not need to keep your own revenge; there is a Keeper who never sleeps, and His ledger never errs.

**The Prophet's Boundaries: Neither Guardian nor Jailer**

The verse then turns to the Prophet ﷺ with a release disguised as a restriction: "and thou art not a guardian over them." The commentators explain the sentence in two directions. First, it removes responsibility: the Prophet has not been granted authority to force belief, nor is it his place to record and requite their deeds (Ṭs); he has been sent only as a warner (IK, Ṭs). The Qur'an repeats this boundary in many passages — "upon thee is only the proclamation" (3:20; 5:92, 99; 13:40; 16:35, 82; 24:54; 29:18; 36:17; 64:12) — so that no messenger, and no believer after him, mistakes the mission: the duty is to convey, not to compel; to warn, not to win at any cost. Second, the verse carries, as al-Ṭūsī notes, a dimension of consolation for the Prophet (Ṭs): the opposition of the idolaters is not a failure of his mission, and their persistence is not a burden he must carry; the Keeper will deal with what the guardian was never asked to fix.

**The Modern Multiplication of Protectors**

The verse's diagnosis translates without difficulty into every age, including ours. The forms of "taking protectors apart from God" are endlessly renewable: the worship of state power as the final arbiter of truth, the deification of wealth as the guarantor of security, the trust placed in institutions, ideologies, celebrities, or ethnic solidarities to do what only God can do — provide, protect, forgive, and give meaning. The Qur'anic criterion is simple and searching: anything a human being relies upon for what only God can supply has become a protector taken apart from Him. The verse does not forbid using means — the sūrah itself praises counsel, trade, and defense — but it forbids the transference of trust. And its closing sentence relieves the believer of a parallel burden: you are not a guardian over those who choose false protectors. The duty toward them is proclamation with excellence and prayer for their guidance — not anxiety, not coercion, not the despair that their choices somehow indict your mission. God is Keeper over them; your part is the message, and the rest belongs to the One who keeps all accounts.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:7

> **Thus have We revealed unto thee an Arabic Quran that thou mayest warn the Mother of Cities and those around it, and that thou mayest warn of the Day of Gathering in which there is no doubt. A group shall be in the Garden and a group shall be in the Blaze.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Arabic Quran: The Language of the Warning**

The verse returns to the revelation theme with a decisive specification: the revelation to Muhammad ﷺ is "an Arabic Quran" — *Qurʾānan ʿarabiyyan*, in the literal sense "an Arabic recitation." The specification answers the same logic the commentators traced in verse 3: just as every prophet was sent with revelation in the language of his own people, so the final messenger received the final message in Arabic — the language of the Arabs to whom he was first sent (cf. 12:2; 16:103; 20:113; 26:195; 39:28; 41:3; 43:3; Q, Ṭs). The Qur'an states the principle of linguistic fit explicitly:

> *"And We sent no messenger save with the tongue of his people, that he might make clear unto them."* (Qur'an 14:4)

The wisdom is obvious once stated: a warning that cannot be understood warns no one. Revelation does not descend in a heavenly language remote from its hearers; it enters the actual speech of actual people — their idioms, their poetry, their proverbs — so that the clearest minds among them are left without excuse and the simplest hearts among them are reached without translation. For the Arabs, whose civilization was built on language as others' were built on stone, the choice of Arabic was also a challenge: the Qur'an is Arabic through and through, composed of the letters they knew and the meters they had mastered — and yet they could not produce its like. The eloquence of the warning is itself part of the warning.

**The Mother of Cities and the World Beyond It**

The mission is then located: the Prophet is sent to warn "the Mother of Cities" (*Umm al-Qurā*) — Makkah — "and those around it." The name is significant on several levels. Makkah is called the Mother of Cities because it is nobler than all other cities (IK): the site of the first house of worship, the sanctuary of Abraham, the place of the well of Hagar and of the pilgrimage that drew Arabs from every tribe. The Qur'an elsewhere calls it the "valley without cultivation" near the sacred House (14:37) and swears by its security (90:1–2; 95:3). The Prophet's own love for the city is preserved in the tradition Ibn Kathīr cites in this connection, in which he addressed Makkah after his forced departure:

> *"By God! Thou art the best land of God and the most beloved land of God to Him. Were it not that I was driven out from thee, I would never have left thee."* (al-Tirmidhī 3925, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

Yet the mission is not confined to the city: "and those around it" widens the horizon to all the towns of Arabia, the caravans that passed through its valley, and, by extension, every land and generation until the end of time — for the warning of the final Messenger is, by the consensus of the community, universal. The verse thus maps the actual history of the message: it began in the Mother of Cities, was carried to those around it, and from that center radiated to the ends of the earth.

**The Day of Gathering: The Content of the Warning**

The second purpose clause names what the Prophet must warn of: "the Day of Gathering in which there is no doubt" — *Yawm al-Jamʿ*, the day when all human beings of all generations, and the jinn as well, will be gathered to one plain for judgment, each community standing behind its prophet (see 17:71). The name emphasizes the sheer inclusiveness of the assembly: no one is missing, no era unrepresented, no deed unsummoned. The Qur'an describes the mechanics elsewhere:

> *"And We shall gather them on a Day in which there is no doubt."* (Qur'an 3:9)

> *"The day when We shall cause the mountains to pass away, and thou seest the earth brought forth, and We gather them, leaving out not one of them."* (Qur'an 18:47)

The verse adds the phrase "in which there is no doubt" — not because anyone will doubt the Day when it arrives, but because the warning must remove doubt now, while doubt is still possible and repentance still effective. The certainty of the Day is the engine of the warning: a warning about an uncertain event is a rumor; a warning about an inevitable event is a mercy.

**Two Groups, One Verdict**

The verse ends with the sūrah's first explicit division of the gathered humanity: "A group shall be in the Garden and a group shall be in the Blaze." The commentators note the deliberate absence of detail here — no names, no nations, no percentages — and the presence of the same binary throughout the Qur'an's descriptions of the Gathering (e.g., 11:105–108). The binary serves several purposes at once. It makes the stakes of the warning concrete: the Day is not a spectacle but a sorting. It makes the mission's urgency intelligible: the Prophet warns of a Day whose outcome is binary, so indifference is the one response the warning cannot tolerate. And it leaves the sorting itself with God: the verse does not say which group the hearer belongs to — that is determined by response to the warning, which is precisely what makes the warning a mercy rather than a sentence. For the believers of Makkah, who were a despised minority, the verse carried an additional comfort that the rest of the sūrah will confirm: the majority of the city might mock, but the Day's two groups are not determined by the city's opinion. For every reciter, the verse functions as a daily memento: the Gathering is certain, the sorting is binary, and the time to choose one's group is now — while the warning can still be answered.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:8

> **Had God willed, He would have made them all one community. But He causes whomsoever He will to enter into His Mercy. And the wrongdoers have no protector and no helper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Follows from Unity**

The previous verses established that all prophets received one religion and that the Prophet's mission divides humanity into two groups — Garden and Blaze. A reflective listener now faces the oldest theological question: if God is one, and His religion is one, and His power is unlimited, why did He not simply make all human beings one community of believers? Why create the possibility of the Blaze at all? The verse answers without evasion: "Had God willed, He would have made them all one community." The conditional is deliberately left unrealized — God *could* have, and He *did* not — and the verse thereby establishes at once the limitlessness of the divine will and the reality of human differentiation. The commentators note that this very point is made with variations throughout the Qur'an (see 6:35; 11:118–19; 16:93): compulsion is always within divine power, and always withheld.

**Why Universal Compulsion Was Withheld**

The classical exegetes, and especially al-Ṭūsī, drew out the wisdom in the withholding (Ṭs). If God had compelled all people into a single religion and thereby saved all from punishment, the very structure of moral and legal responsibility (*taklīf*) would have been destroyed: obedience that cannot be refused is not obedience, love that cannot be withheld is not love, and a test whose outcome is guaranteed is not a test. The Qur'an states the principle in its own words in the parallel passage of Sūrah Hūd:

> *"And had thy Lord willed, He would have made mankind one community; but they cease not to differ — save those upon whom thy Lord has mercy."* (Qur'an 11:118–119)

The verse in al-Shūrā adds a second dimension, drawn out by the commentators from the parallel in Sūrah al-Māʾidah: God made peoples different communities in religion and law "that He might try them in that which He has given them" (5:48) — so that they might vie with one another in good deeds rather than in conquest. Diversity of communities, in this reading, is not the failure of the divine plan but a field for the exercise of virtue: patience with difference, justice toward the other, and competition in goodness. The unity God wills is not the uniformity of robots but the convergence of free hearts.

**The Two Sentences That Qualify the Whole**

The verse then closes with two sentences that prevent any misreading of its opening. "But He causes whomsoever He will to enter into His Mercy" — the differentiation of communities does not mean that God abandons the world to chance; within the human drama, mercy is actively extended, and those who enter it do so by a divine kindness that precedes their deserving. And "the wrongdoers have no protector and no helper" — the differentiation is likewise not an arbitrary lottery that punishes the unlucky: those who remain outside mercy do so as wrongdoers, and the sentence is a statement of consequence rather than of caprice. The pairing is the Qur'an's constant grammar of justice: God's will and human responsibility are both real, and the verse refuses to sacrifice either to the other. The believer is not asked to solve the philosophical puzzle of how divine will and human choice coincide; he is asked to live on the side of the equation that is his to determine — the side of repentance, mercy-seeking, and deeds.

**The Community That Is Still Possible**

There is a third layer in the verse that the commentators gently draw out: "Had God willed, He would have made them all one community" is also a quiet summons. Since God did not compel unity, unity must be chosen; and since the Qur'an repeatedly commands the believers to hold fast to the rope of God together (3:103), the verse implies that the unity which compulsion could have produced instantly is meant to be produced — in part, in history — by the free cooperation of believers. What cannot be achieved by force is not therefore unachievable; it is achievable by covenant, counsel, and mutual mercy — the very qualities the sūrah will celebrate in its central portrait of the believers (vv. 36–43). The community of the Garden is not a prefabricated block lowered from heaven; it is an assembly of free persons who chose, in every generation, to enter the Mercy that was extended to them — and the verse leaves the door of that choice open until the final Gathering.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:9

> **Or have they taken protectors apart from Him? Yet God, He is the Protector; He gives life to the dead; and He is Powerful over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Answers Itself**

The verse opens with an interrogative that al-Rāzī identifies as a question of rebuke (*inkār*): "Or have they taken protectors apart from Him?" The force of the question is not genuine inquiry but exposure — the rhetorical equivalent of asking a man who claims to swim well whether he has ever entered water. After the previous verse declared that the wrongdoers have no protector and no helper, the question turns to those who nonetheless maintain their idols, their patrons, their invented intermediaries: have they truly taken protectors apart from God? The implication, as the commentators draw it out, is that such protectors are protectors in name only. The idols of the Quraysh could neither hear, nor see, nor benefit, nor harm — the Qur'an presses this point in dozens of places — and the question forces the idolater to measure his chosen protector against the only standard that counts: can it protect? The verse's answer is a single word, repeated with maximal emphasis: *fa-Allāhu huwa al-Walī* — "Yet God, He is the Protector" — the pronoun "He" standing between the name and the title, as though to say: God, and He alone, He is the Protector, with no associate in the office.

**Why "The Protector" Is the Only Sufficient Name**

The title *al-Walī* carries a cluster of meanings that the commentators unpack: the Protector who takes charge of His servant's affairs, the Friend whose friendship needs no intermediary, the Patron whose loyalty never fails, the Guardian who defends when all other defenders withdraw. The Qur'an's insistence on this single name is the logical conclusion of its doctrine of divine perfection: if God is the Creator of all things, then every benefit that flows to a creature flows ultimately from Him; protectorship is therefore not a role that can be delegated or shared, any more than creation can be shared. The verse makes the point by juxtaposition — the false protectors are followed immediately by the true Protector — and the sūrah will repeat the refrain with variations across its length (see 2:107, 120; 4:123, 173; 9:74, 116; 29:22; 33:18, 65; 42:31; 48:22), each time in a context where some human institution or idol is claiming the allegiance that belongs to God alone.

**Resurrection as the Test of All Protectors**

The verse then supplies the criterion by which every protector is judged: "He gives life to the dead; and He is Powerful over all things." The sequence is carefully chosen. The idolaters' protectors cannot give life — they are themselves lifeless, whether carved from stone or conjured from imagination. The God who is Protector is the God who raises the dead, and the resurrection is the ultimate test of protectorship for a simple reason: death is the one event no creature can prevent, reverse, or accompany another through. At the moment of death, every protector apart from God is exposed as powerless; the idols remain where they are, the patrons save themselves, the institutions dissolve — and the soul stands before the One who gives life to the dead. The verse thereby joins the sūrah's two great themes — false protectors and the Gathering — into one argument: the false protectors are false precisely because they fail the final test, and the true Protector is true precisely because He alone holds the keys of death and life. The Qur'an makes the same connection when it says of the resurrection's necessity:

> *"Does man think that We will not gather his bones? Yea, We are able to proportion even his fingertips."* (Qur'an 75:3–4)

**Everyday Idolatry and the Test of Power**

The verse's logic translates into the modern heart without difficulty. Every human being entrusts his security to something: wealth, status, health, a nation, a profession, a relationship. The Qur'anic question — "Or have they taken protectors apart from Him?" — is a standing invitation to audit one's own trusts. The audit is simple: think of the thing on which you most rely, and ask what it can do on the day of your greatest need. Wealth can be lost in an afternoon; health fails without warning; nations and institutions are mortal like their members. The only Protector who survives every audit is the One who gives life to the dead — because He is the only reality that death itself cannot touch. This is not a counsel of passivity — the sūrah will praise taking means, counsel, and defense — but it is a counsel of ranking: use the means, love the means, but trust only the Protector. The believer who recites this verse is being taught the precise difference between taking a means and taking a protector: a means is a tool held in the hand; a protector is a lord enthroned in the heart — and the heart has room for only one throne.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:10

> **As for anything wherein you differ, judgment thereof lies with God. That is God, my Lord. In Him do I trust and unto Him do I turn.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Universal Arbitration Clause**

The verse begins with a sentence of breathtaking scope: *wa mā ikhtalaftum fīhi min shayʾ fa-ḥukmuhu ilā Allāh* — "As for anything wherein you differ, judgment thereof lies with God." The clause covers every domain of difference: theology, law, history, politics, economics, family, the interpretation of scripture itself. Nothing is excluded by the word "anything," and the commentators draw out the principle in two directions. Vertically, it means that the final arbiter of every dispute is God — not the strongest tribe, not the loudest opinion, not the latest consensus of human preference. The Qur'an repeats the principle as a refrain wherever disputes are mentioned: "judgment belongs to God alone" (6:57), "the judgment is God's" (12:40, 67), and most fully in Sūrah al-Anʿām:

> *"Say: Truly I am upon a clear proof from my Lord, and you deny it. I do not have that which you seek to hasten. The judgment is God's alone. He relates the truth, and He is the best of deciders."* (Qur'an 6:57)

Horizontally, it means that in every genuine difference between human beings there is a true answer — not merely a negotiated compromise — and that the path to that answer lies through what God has revealed. The means by which human beings come to know the divine judgment are the revelations themselves: the Torah (5:43; 45:16), the Qur'an (13:37), and the messages given to all the prophets, all of which contain God's criterion for judging human conduct. This is why the verse functions as the charter of the sūrah's later praise of counsel (v. 38): counsel is the human procedure for discovering a judgment that is not human in origin — the assembly of believers consults in order to approximate, together, the decision that God has already determined as right.

**Referral as the Test of Faith**

The verse's companion in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ turns the principle into a concrete command with a condition attached:

> *"O you who believe! Obey God, and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. If you differ among yourselves concerning anything, refer it to God and the Messenger, if you believe in God and the Last Day. That is better and fairer in interpretation."* (Qur'an 4:59)

The conditional "if you believe in God and the Last Day" is the Qur'an's way of saying that referral is not a procedure but a confession: the community that truly believes in God and the Last Day will, when it differs, go back to the Book and the Messenger rather than to arms, partisanship, or the prestige of disputants. Conversely, a community that resolves its differences without reference to revelation has, to that degree, ceased to believe in the Last Day — for the Last Day is precisely the day when the divine judgment that should have governed earthly disputes is finally and visibly enacted. The same point is made negatively in the verse the source commentaries cite:

> *"Whosoever judges not by that which God has sent down — it is they who are the disbelievers."* (Qur'an 5:44)

**A Personal Creed in Three Declarations**

The verse then narrows from the universal to the personal with striking intimacy: "That is God, my Lord. In Him do I trust and unto Him do I turn." The shift from law to testimony is the Qur'an's characteristic movement — the truth is not only to be applied but to be inhabited. Three declarations follow. "That is God, my Lord": the God whose judgment governs all differences is not an abstraction but *my* Lord, the one whose authority over the speaker is personal and acknowledged. "In Him do I trust": *ʿalayhi tawakkaltu* — the trust that the Qur'an elsewhere defines as the believer's sufficient support: "And whosoever trusts in God, He suffices him" (65:3). "And unto Him do I turn": *wa ilayhi unīb* — the verb *anāba* carries the sense of returning, turning back, making God the direction of one's movement; the commentators note that it can be read "unto Him do I return" (IJ) or "unto Him do I turn in all my affairs" (IK, Ṭs). The three clauses trace a complete spiritual motion: recognition of the Lord, reliance upon the Lord, and return to the Lord.

**Why the Judge Must Also Be the Beloved**

There is a subtle psychological wisdom in the verse's placement of trust and turning immediately after the statement of judgment. A purely legal relationship to God — God as judge, human being as defendant — produces fear without love, and love without reference to judgment produces sentimentality. The Qur'an holds the two together: the Judge to whom all differences are referred is also the Lord in whom trust is placed and toward whom the heart turns. This is what distinguishes Islamic monotheism from both a cold deism (a judge who never relates) and an empty mysticism (a beloved who never judges). Because the Judge is trustworthy, His judgments can be awaited without anxiety; because the Beloved is a judge, love for Him expresses itself in obedience rather than in sentiment. The verse thereby supplies the emotional foundation for the entire legal structure of Islam: the believer obeys the divine law not because it is imposed by a distant tribunal but because it issues from the Lord to whom he has already turned, and whose judgment he has already accepted as the resolution of every difference — including the difference between his own desires and the truth.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:11

> **The Originator of the heavens and the earth, He has appointed for you mates from among yourselves, and has appointed mates also among the cattle. He multiplies you thereby; naught is like unto Him, yet He is the Hearer, the Seer.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Originator: From Final Return to First Beginning**

The verse opens with the divine name *Fāṭir* — the Originator — which the classical philologists traced to the verb *faṭara*, "to split" or "to cleave," suggesting an origination that breaks forth: God splits the darkness of non-existence with the light of being, cleaves the seed into the sprout, the egg into the bird. In the Qur'an the name occurs always within the phrase "Originator of the heavens and the earth" (cf. 6:14; 12:101; 14:10; 35:1; 39:46), and the commentators noticed a beautiful complementarity in its placement here: the previous verse ended with *ilayhi unīb* — "unto Him do I turn," an allusion to the final return — and this verse opens with the Originator, an allusion to the first beginning. The God to whom all affairs journey is the God from whom all things originated; alpha and omega are the same name. The believer's turning toward God is therefore not a movement toward an unknown destination but a homecoming to the point of origin.

**Mates From Among Yourselves: The Design of Companionship**

The verse then grounds the doctrine of God's origination in the most intimate fact of human experience: "He has appointed for you mates from among yourselves." The phrase *min anfusikum* — "from among yourselves" — is deliberately repeated in the Qur'an's discussions of marriage (see 4:1; 16:72; 30:21; 35:11; 78:8), and it carries a double meaning. Literally, it means that spouses are human beings like the ones they marry — not angels, not beasts, but fellow creatures of the same nature. Deeper, it carries the resonance of the root *nafs*: the mate is, as it were, drawn from the same soul-kind, which is why the Qur'an describes the original pair as created "from one soul" (4:1). The verse adds the cattle to the same design: "and has appointed mates also among the cattle" — for the same pairing that perpetuates the human race perpetuates the animals on which human life depends, so that the whole economy of creation runs on the principle of the pair. The purpose clause completes the picture: "He multiplies you thereby" — the pairs are not merely for companionship but for continuation; the commentators read the verb variously as "He creates you" or "He sustains you" through the procreation of spouses and cattle (IJ, Ṭ), and the verse thus presents the family as the divinely appointed engine of human history.

**The Emotional Dimension of the Pair**

The verse's emphasis here is procreative, but the Qur'an elsewhere unfolds the emotional and spiritual ends of the same institution with unmatched tenderness:

> *"And among His signs is that He created mates for you from among yourselves, that you might find rest in them, and He established between you affection and mercy. Truly in that are signs for a people who reflect."* (Qur'an 30:21)

The commentators hold the two verses together: the mate is both the means of the species' multiplication and the haven of the individual's rest; marriage is both the nursery of humanity and the school of mercy. The word for the love between spouses in 30:21 is *mawaddah* — the same word the sūrah will use in verse 23 for the affection the Prophet asks of his kinsfolk — so that al-Shūrā quietly links the love between spouses, the love between kin, and the love between the Prophet and his community as expressions of one divine design: the human being is made for bonds, and the bonds are made for drawing toward God.

**Naught Is Like Unto Him: The Most Famous Clause**

The verse then delivers one of the Qur'an's most famous sentences: *laysa ka-mithlihi shayʾ* — literally "there is not like His likeness anything," and universally rendered "naught is like unto Him" (cf. 112:4). The doubled expression — "like His likeness" — provoked a celebrated discussion among the exegetes (IJ, Q, Ṭ, Ṭs). The vast majority read the *ka* as a particle of emphasis, making the sentence an emphatic negation: nothing whatsoever resembles God. On this reading the clause is the Qur'an's charter of divine transcendence (*tanzīh*): every attempt to picture God, to measure Him by the categories of creation, to compare His knowledge with human knowledge or His power with human power, is ruled out in advance. God creates pairs — but He is not paired; He originates — but He is not originated; He hears and sees — but His hearing and sight are not the hearing and sight of creatures. The Sufi commentators added a further subtlety: since the *ka* can also be read literally, the sentence may be understood as "not like His likeness is anything" — meaning that even the likeness of God is unlike anything else, and that the very vestiges of His beauty in creation share in His incomparability (Ibn ʿArabī, al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyyah, II 516). On either reading the clause stands as the definitive answer to every form of anthropomorphism and every attempt to fashion an image of God — whether carved in stone by the idolaters or constructed in the mind by the speculative.

**The Hearer, the Seer: Transcendence Withheld From Absence**

The verse ends with a deliberate balance: "yet He is the Hearer, the Seer." The commentators stress the connection between the two halves of the verse: the God who is unlike anything is nonetheless the God who hears and sees everything. The conjunction "yet" is the whole point — transcendence in the Qur'an never slides into absence. The One of whom no image can be made is the One to whom every whisper arrives and from whom no glance is hidden. This is the balance the entire sūrah maintains: it refutes the idolaters who carved gods they could see, and it equally refutes the despairing who imagine a God too exalted to attend to their affairs. The Hearer answers the first need of the distressed — to be heard; the Seer answers the second — to be known as one is, without pretense. Between the inexpressible majesty of the Originator and the intimate attention of the Hearer and Seer, the believer's whole religious life moves: worship without idolatry, intimacy without presumption, awe without distance. The Prophet ﷺ invoked exactly this pairing when he taught the believer to say in supplication:

> *"O God, Originator of the heavens and the earth, Knower of the unseen and the witnessed..."* — as in the words of the Qur'an itself (39:46), which the early Muslims were taught to use when facing the greatest decisions.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:12

> **Unto Him belong the keys of the heavens and the earth. He outspreads and straitens provision for whomsoever He will. Truly He is Knower of all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Keys: Ownership of Every Door**

The verse opens with the image of the keys — *maqālīd* — "Unto Him belong the keys of the heavens and the earth." The Qur'an uses the word in two other passages with closely related force: "Unto Him belong the keys of the heavens and the earth" also at 39:63, and "with Him are the keys of the Unseen; none knows them but He" at 6:59. The commentators explain that the keys are the means by which the treasuries of creation are opened: the rain, the provision, the mercy, the knowledge — everything that descends from heaven and everything that grows from the earth is behind a door, and every door opens only with a key that God holds. In the context of this sūrah — and especially of the following verse, which speaks of the religion prescribed to all the prophets — the image extends beyond material provision: the "keys to the treasuries" include the keys of revelation itself, which God grants to whom He wills and withholds from whom He wills (see 6:59c; 39:63c). The idolaters of Makkah supposed that prophethood was a human acquisition — why was the Qur'an not sent down to a great man from one of the two cities? (43:31) — and the verse answers with the image of the keys: the office of revelation, like the rainfall and the harvest, is opened only by the Key-holder, and no tribe, lineage, or wealth can turn a lock that God has not unlocked.

**Outspreading and Straitening: The Divine Economy of Provision**

The verse then states the operative rule of that economy: "He outspreads and straitens provision for whomsoever He will." The two verbs — *yabsuṭu* and *yaqdiru* — describe the two movements of the divine hand: widening the provision of some and narrowing the provision of others, according to a wisdom that the verse's final clause attributes to perfect knowledge. The commentators (following JJ) are careful to state what the Qur'an states elsewhere: the widening is itself a test and the narrowing is itself a trial. Wealth is not a certificate of divine favor, and poverty is not a verdict of divine anger. The Qur'an says of this directly:

> *"As for man, whenever his Lord tries him, and honors him, and favors him, he says: My Lord has honored me. But whenever He tries him, and straitens his provision, he says: My Lord has humiliated me. Nay!"* (Qur'an 89:15–17)

The "Nay!" in that passage rebukes the very assumption the present verse dissolves: that provision maps directly onto worth. Both the outspreading and the straitening proceed from the same Hand, serve the same purpose of testing, and are calibrated by the same Knowledge.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Economic Constitution**

Within al-Shūrā, this verse anchors a whole cluster that follows: verse 19 (God is kind to His servants and provides for whom He will), verse 27 (had God outspread provision, people would have transgressed), verse 28 (rain after despair), and verses 32–34 (ships and winds). The chapter's teaching on wealth, poverty, and provision forms a single coherent doctrine: provision is entirely in God's hands; its distribution follows divine wisdom rather than human merit as humans measure it; and human beings are therefore commanded to seek provision by lawful means while holding their hearts free of both pride in abundance and despair in scarcity. This doctrine had an immediate social consequence in Makkah, where the Quraysh's wealth was the engine of their power and their contempt: the Qur'an repeatedly told the poor believers that their poverty was not a mark of divine displeasure, and told the rich idolaters that their wealth was not a proof of divine favor. The doctrine remains the Qur'an's permanent critique of every society that equates economic success with moral worth.

**"Knower of All Things": The Calibration Clause**

The verse ends with the name that guarantees the justice of the whole arrangement: "Truly He is Knower of all things." The clause performs a precise logical function. If provision were distributed by chance, or by the blind operation of economic forces, then the poor could resent the rich and the rich could despise the poor. But because the distribution is made by One who knows all things — who knows what each soul can bear, what each soul needs, what each soul would become in abundance and what it would become in scarcity — the arrangement is referable to a wisdom beyond human sight. The believer's response to the divine economy is therefore not envy (which questions the Giver's wisdom), not despair (which doubts the Giver's care), and not pride (which forgets the Giver's sole ownership). It is the response the verse itself models: recognition that the keys are His, acceptance that the measure is His, and the quiet work of the hand in the field that He has measured. The Prophet ﷺ expressed the same acceptance when he taught:

> *"And know that if the whole community gathered to benefit you with something, they could not benefit you with anything except what God has already written for you; and if they gathered to harm you with something, they could not harm you with anything except what God has already written against you. The pens have been lifted and the pages have dried."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

That is not fatalism; it is the removal of anxiety from effort. The verse's keys open every door — but the believer is told only to knock, never to demand the lock's design.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:13

> **He has prescribed for you as religion that which He enjoined upon Noah, and that which We revealed unto thee, and that which We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus — that you uphold religion and not become divided therein. Grievous for the idolaters is that to which thou callest them. God chooses for Himself whomsoever He will and guides unto Himself whosoever turns in repentance.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Religion, Five Names, Fourteen Centuries**

The verse assembles the Qur'an's most complete list of the religion's custodians in a single sentence of prescription: Noah, whose mission opens the prophetic series after the Flood; Abraham, the father of the prophets and the builder of the Kaʿbah; Moses, the lawgiver of Israel; Jesus, the Messiah; and Muhammad ﷺ, to whom "We revealed." The verb is *sharaʿa* — "He has prescribed" — from the root that gives the Qur'an its name for revealed law, *sharīʿah*, the path to water, the way that sustains life. The commentators draw out the structure of the sentence with care: "that which He enjoined upon Noah" is the ancient kernel — the worship of the One God; "that which We revealed unto thee" is the contemporary address to Muhammad; and the mention of Abraham, Moses, and Jesus fills the middle of prophetic history (Ṭ). The verse's parallel in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the same unity of revelation as a single act:

> *"Verily We have revealed unto thee, as We revealed unto Noah and the prophets after him, and as We revealed unto Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the Tribes, and Jesus, Job, Jonah, Aaron, and Solomon — and unto David We gave the Psalms. And messengers We have recounted unto thee before, and messengers We have not recounted unto thee; and unto Moses God spoke directly."* (Qur'an 4:163–164)

The historical claim is immense and was heard as such by the Makkans: the religion of Muhammad is not a seventh-century novelty competing with older faiths but the very religion of the patriarchs whom Arabia already revered — and, crucially, whose sanctuary stood in Makkah itself. The idolaters could not reject Muhammad's message without rejecting the religion of Abraham, whose shrine they circled; the verse seals that contradiction.

**Uphold Religion and Do Not Be Divided: The Double Command**

The purpose of the prescription is then stated in two clauses: "that you uphold religion and not become divided therein." The commentators define upholding (*aqīmū al-dīn*) as acting in accord with it (Ṭ), holding fast to it, performing its obligations, remaining faithful to it, and calling others to it (Ṭs) — a definition that makes the "upholding" a whole way of life rather than a private conviction. The negative clause — "and not become divided therein" — is directed at the fate of the earlier communities, which the Qur'an describes as having "cut their religion into fragments" (23:53). Al-Ṭabarī draws the practical conclusion with characteristic bluntness: division is destruction, and unity is something upon which one can rely (Ṭ). The command applies at every level of the community: the unity of doctrine (no sectarian fragmentation over the fundamentals), the unity of practice (one prayer, one qiblah, one pilgrimage), and the unity of affection (the brotherhood that the sūrah's later verses will describe). The Prophet ﷺ described the believers' mutual bond with a famous image:

> *"The believers, in their mutual love, mercy, and compassion, are like one body: if one part of it complains, the rest of the body joins it in sleeplessness and fever."* (al-Bukhārī 6011; Muslim 2586, from al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr)

The command against division does not forbid legitimate disagreement in matters open to interpretation — the very praise of counsel later in this sūrah presupposes discussion — but it forbids disagreement that becomes schism: each faction treating its own view as the whole of religion and its opponents as outside the faith.

**Why the Call Was "Grievous" to the Idolaters**

The verse then makes a striking psychological observation: "Grievous for the idolaters is that to which thou callest them." The commentators identify two sources of the grief (Ṭs). The first is genuinely religious in form: the call to pure divine unity (*tawḥīd*) and sincere devotion dismantled the elaborate economy of Arabian worship — the gods of the tribes, the intercessors, the sacred animals, the ancestral customs — and no people surrenders its religious economy without pain. The second is social and psychological: the call aroused jealousy that Muhammad ﷺ — not a chief of the dominant clans — had been given the revelation; the same envy appears in their complaint:

> *"And they say: Why was this Qur'an not sent down to a great man from one of the two towns?"* (Qur'an 43:31)

The verse thus diagnoses the idolaters' resistance as a mixture of theological rupture and wounded pride — a diagnosis that explains why argument alone could not move them: the obstacle was not only in their minds but in their status. The same diagnosis explains the Qur'an's insistence that the response to such grief is patience and proclamation, not accommodation: no amount of softening the message could remove the grief, because the grief was caused by the message's truth, not its formulation.

**Choosing and Guiding: The Two Motions of Mercy**

The verse ends with a sentence whose pronoun the commentators long debated: "God chooses for Himself whomsoever He will and guides unto Himself whosoever turns in repentance." The antecedent of "Himself" may be God — God chooses for Himself, meaning for His religion and His service, whomever He will — or it may be read "chooses for it" and "guides unto it," where "it" is the religion (Z) or the bearing of witness to divine unity (Q). The exegetes note that the two clauses describe two complementary divine motions: selection — God's sovereign choice of prophets and of those who will carry His religion — and guidance — God's leading of those who turn to Him in repentance. The second motion is the one within every sinner's reach: the verse does not say that God guides only the sinless; it says that He guides whosoever turns in repentance — the turning (*inābah*) is the human condition, and the guidance is the divine response. The classical commentators connect the sentence to the famous ḥadīth qudsī in which God describes the reciprocity of approach:

> *"If My servant draws near to Me by a span, I draw near to him by a cubit; and if he draws near to Me by a cubit, I draw near to him by the span of an outstretched arm; and if he comes to Me walking, I come to him running."* (al-Bukhārī 7405; Muslim 2675, from Abū Hurayrah)

The verse thereby closes with the same note of hope that the whole sūrah sounds beneath its warnings: the religion that the idolaters find grievous is the very religion whose door stands open to every repenting servant; the prescription is ancient, the command is unity, and the reward of the one who turns is guidance — from the God who chooses, guides, and runs to meet the returning soul.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:14

> **They did not become divided till after knowledge had come unto them, out of envy among themselves. And were it not for a Word that had preceded from thy Lord unto a term appointed, judgment would have been made between them. Yet truly those who were bequeathed the Book after them are indeed confounded by doubt regarding it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Timing of Division: After Knowledge, Not Before It**

The verse opens with a historical generalization of remarkable explanatory power: "They did not become divided till after knowledge had come unto them, out of envy among themselves." Division, in the Qur'an's account of religious history, is never the product of ignorance alone; it is the product of knowledge followed by envy. The communities of the prophets did not split while the truth was still unclear to them; they split after the clear proof had arrived — and the motive was not honest disagreement about ambiguous matters but *baghyan baynahum*, "out of envy among themselves": the unwillingness of some to accept that the blessing had been given through others. The same diagnosis is applied to the People of the Book in their treatment of Muhammad ﷺ (see 3:19; 98:4) and to the communities before them (2:213; 10:93; 45:17):

> *"The people were one community; then God sent forth the prophets as bearers of glad tidings and warning, and sent down with them the Book in truth, that it might judge between people concerning that wherein they differed. And none differed concerning it save those who were given it, after the clear proofs had come unto them, out of envy among themselves."* (Qur'an 2:213)

Al-Ṭūsī applies the verse to the immediate Makkan situation: the idolaters did not differ among themselves about the Prophet until after his prophethood had been declared; then they turned away out of jealousy and greed, unwilling that he should become their leader (Ṭs). Al-Ṭabarī, reading the verse in connection with the previous one, applies it to the people of Noah, who divided after the revelation reached them (Ṭ). Both readings agree on the mechanism: the divisive force in religious history is not doubt but envy — the refusal to rejoice in a good that one did not oneself receive.

**The Anatomy of Envy as a Social Force**

The verse's diagnosis deserves pause, because envy is the most social of the vices: it requires another person's good as its fuel, and it expresses itself as a faction-forming energy. The envious do not simply disbelieve; they recruit, they polemicize, they split communities — precisely because the sight of another's blessing wounds their sense of rank. This is why the Qur'an treats envy as one of the great historical engines of schism and why it commands refuge from it in the final sūrah of the Book: "and from the evil of the envier when he envies" (113:5). The Prophet ﷺ warned of its corrosive power over good deeds in terms that every community has verified since:

> *"Beware of envy, for envy consumes good deeds as fire consumes wood."* (Abū Dāwūd 4903, from Abū Hurayrah)

The psychology is exact: envy cannot be satisfied by obtaining the good — it can only be satisfied by the other's losing it. Hence the envious man spends his energy on subtraction rather than addition, on the diminishment of rivals rather than the building of himself, and the communities he joins are divided ones. The Qur'an's cure for envy is the discipline it prescribes throughout: recognition that blessings are distributed by the Key-holder (42:12), prayer for one's own portion, and the active cultivation of goodwill (*īthār*) — preferring others — which the Qur'an praises in the Helpers of Madinah (59:9).

**The Word That Postpones: Respite as Mercy**

The verse then makes its most theologically intricate statement: "And were it not for a Word that had preceded from thy Lord unto a term appointed, judgment would have been made between them." The "Word" is the divine decree of respite — God's promise that judgment between the divided communities will be deferred to an appointed term, the Day of Resurrection, rather than executed at the moment of their division (cf. 10:19; 11:110; 41:45). The commentators draw out the consequence with precision: if judgment had been executed immediately upon the first schism, the wrongdoers would have been destroyed on the spot, and no opportunity for repentance would have remained. The deferral of judgment is therefore not a failure of justice but an extension of mercy: history continues because the door of repentance remains open; the world is not yet the Day because the world is still the field of return. The Qur'an states the same principle in several places:

> *"And if God were to take people to task for their wrongdoing, He would not leave upon it a single creature; but He grants them respite unto a term appointed."* (Qur'an 16:61)

**The Heirs of the Book and Their Doubt**

The verse ends with a further historical observation that completes the diagnosis: "Yet truly those who were bequeathed the Book after them are indeed confounded by doubt regarding it." The "heirs of the Book" are the generations that inherited scripture without living through its revelation — the later Israelites and Christians who received the Torah and Gospel secondhand (and, in the commentators' extension, all who inherit revelation without direct encounter with its prophet). Their condition is described as *shakk* — doubt — "regarding it": uncertain whether the Book they hold is really what their ancestors received, uncertain whether the religion prescribed by God is truly one, uncertain — in the Makkan context — whether Muhammad's message is the continuation of their scripture (IJ, Ṭs). The verse thereby describes the religious predicament of every "inherited" believer: faith received by inheritance without examination is vulnerable to doubt, and doubt without resolution is vulnerable to division. The Qur'an's answer to the heirs of the Book is the same in every age: return to what the Book actually says, recognize the unity of the message, and resolve the doubt by encountering the revelation freshly — as the Companions did, and as every sincere reader of the Qur'an can do after them. The verse's warning to the Muslim community is implicit but unavoidable: those who inherit the Qur'an without living its proofs may find themselves, like the heirs before them, "confounded by doubt regarding it" — and the cure is the same as the cure prescribed to the heirs of old: uphold the religion, do not divide, and do not let envy of one another's portions consume what knowledge was given.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:15

> **Therefore, summon, and stand firm as thou hast been commanded. Follow not their caprices, and say, "I believe in that which God has sent down from a Book, and I have been commanded to establish justice among you. God is our Lord and your Lord. Unto us our deeds, and unto you your deeds; there is no argument between us and you. God will gather us together and unto Him is the journey's end."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Imperative That Follows From the Diagnosis**

The verse opens with the practical conclusion of everything that precedes it: *fa-li-dhālika fa-dʿu* — "Therefore, summon." Because the heirs of the Book are confounded by doubt (v. 14), because the communities of old divided after knowledge (v. 14), because the religion is one and its prescription ancient (v. 13) — therefore the Prophet must call. The commentators read the opening phrase in several registers: "unto that, summon," meaning unto the Qur'an (IJ, Ṭ); or summon to the religion prescribed in verse 13, the religion enjoined upon the prophets before him (IK, Q, Ṭ, Ṭs). The command is addressed to the Prophet but is not exhausted by him; every bearer of the message stands under the same imperative in the measure of his capacity. The Qur'an never allows the diagnosis of human waywardness to end in resignation: the more divided the audience, the clearer the call.

**Standing Firm: The Stance of the Warner**

The second command is *wa istaqim* — "and stand firm as thou hast been commanded." The verb *istaqāma* (from the root q-w-m) describes uprightness, straightness, constancy — the stance of one who neither leans toward the audience's preferences nor retreats from the message's demands. The commentators define it as adhering to what God has enjoined and holding fast to it (Ṭs), worshipping as God has commanded (IK), or "holding firm" to the Qur'an (Q). The command echoes the most comprehensive verse of the Qur'an on the same theme — the verse the Prophet ﷺ said had aged him, because of its weight:

> *"So stand firm, as thou hast been commanded, and those who turn in repentance with thee — and transgress not. Truly He sees whatsoever you do."* (Qur'an 11:112)

The pairing of summoning and standing is essential: summoning without firmness becomes marketing — the message bent to the audience's taste; firmness without summoning becomes isolation — the truth hoarded in safety. The Prophet's life between these two commands — calling the Quraysh for thirteen years in Makkah while refusing every offer to soften the message — is the pattern for every caller: available to the people, unavailable to their caprices.

**The Caprices Refused**

The third command states the negative side of firmness: "Follow not their caprices." The commentators differ over the antecedent of "their" — the idolaters (IK), the People of the Book (IJ), or all who doubt what has been revealed (Ṭ) — and the ambiguity is itself instructive: caprice is caprice, whatever its bearer's label. The word *ahwāʾ* (sing. *hawā*) denotes the inclination of the self, desire ungoverned by revelation — and the Qur'an treats it as the great rival of divine guidance:

> *"Have you seen the one who takes his caprice as his god?"* (Qur'an 25:43; 45:23)

The command against following caprice is the negative mirror of the positive command of verse 13 to uphold the religion: the revealed religion is one and fixed in its foundations, while caprice is multiple and shifting — the religion of every age's preferences. The Prophet ﷺ was being asked, in effect, to trim his message to fit the Quraysh's theology (they offered him worship arrangements, compromises, and the inducement of wealth and kingship) — and the verse forbids it absolutely. The same prohibition is repeated throughout the Makkan sūrahs (2:120, 145; 5:48–49; 6:56, 150; 13:37; 45:18), because the pressure to conform is the perennial test of every messenger and every community that inherits the message.

**The Declaration of Faith and Justice**

The verse then instructs the Prophet in what to say — a declaration that the commentators unpack clause by clause. "I believe in that which God has sent down from a Book": the faith is not in the Qur'an alone but in the whole descent of divine speech — "a Book" here spanning revelation generally, since the prophets before him received its portions (see the Qur'an's similar profession: "We believe in God and in that which has been sent down unto us, and that which was sent down unto Abraham..." — 2:136). "And I have been commanded to establish justice among you": the mission's social content is named — *adl*, justice — understood as being equitable in spreading the message without partiality to any clan (IJ, Q, Ṭ, Ṭs), and as judging according to the decrees of revelation (IK, Q, Ṭs), as in:

> *"Verily We have sent down unto thee the Book in truth, that thou mightest judge between people according to what God has shown thee."* (Qur'an 4:105)

The sequence is the Qur'an's full definition of the prophetic office: faith in the whole revelation, and justice in its application.

**No Argument, One Gathering**

The declaration then turns to the relationship between the believers and those who reject the call: "God is our Lord and your Lord. Unto us our deeds, and unto you your deeds; there is no argument between us and you." The first clause refuses the theological boundary the idolaters drew — the Quraysh's gods were tribal, each people's own; the Qur'an's God is the Lord of both parties, the believers' and the idolaters', which makes the call a summons from the same Lord to the same creatures rather than an invasion by a foreign deity. The second clause assigns responsibility where it belongs: each party bears its own deeds and will be recompensed for them (IJ, Ṭ, Ṭs); the believer carries no burden for the idolater's choice, and the idolater gains nothing by the believer's obedience. The verse's parallel in Sūrah Yūnus states the same separation in the same words:

> *"And if they deny thee, say: Unto me, my deeds, and unto you, your deeds. You are quit of that which I do, and I am quit of that which you do."* (Qur'an 10:41)

"There is no argument between us and you": because the truth has appeared, the dispute is settled (Ṭs); argument after revelation is not discussion but evasion. And the final clause gives the reason for the declaration's serenity: "God will gather us together and unto Him is the journey's end." The dispute that cannot be settled by argument will be settled by the Gathering; the justice that human courts cannot perfectly administer will be administered at the journey's end. The verse thereby models the exact tone of prophetic address — and of Muslim address after it: conviction without aggression, clarity without insult, separation without malice, and the certain appointment of every dispute to the only perfect Judge. The Prophet ﷺ embodied that tone through thirteen years of Makkan rejection, and the community that inherits his declaration inherits his calm: summon, stand firm, refuse caprice, declare the truth, and leave the verdict to the Day.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:16

> **As for those who argue about God after having responded to Him, their argument is baseless in the eyes of their Lord. Upon them shall be wrath, and theirs shall be a severe punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Arguing After Responding: The Chronology of the Crime**

The verse describes a peculiar class of people: those who argue about God "after having responded to Him" — *alladhīna yuḥājjūna fī Allāh min baʿdi mā ustujība lahu*. The phrase presupposes a sequence: first the response (*istijābah*), then the argument. The commentators identify the referents with the Makkan idolaters who disputed the religion after people had already responded to the Prophet's call — a description that fits the Quraysh's campaign of propaganda and persecution once Islam began to spread in their city (IJ, IK, Ṭs). Some exegetes extend the verse to the People of the Book, who argued that their religion was superior because their prophets and scriptures preceded Muhammad's (IJ, IK, Ṭs), or to all who refuse to acknowledge the unity between the Qur'an's message and that of the Torah and Gospel (R). But the deepest reading takes the chronology as the crime's essence: to argue about God after responding to Him is not the honest doubt of the seeker but the rebellion of the one who has already known — the disputer who once acknowledged and then turned his acknowledgment into a weapon. The Qur'an's word for this is *jadal* — contentious wrangling — which it distinguishes from the fair debate it permits:

> *"And do not argue with the People of the Book save in the fairest manner."* (Qur'an 29:46)

**Why the Argument Is Baseless**

The verse's verdict is delivered in a single devastating phrase: "their argument is baseless in the eyes of their Lord" — *dāḥiḍatun*, hollow, void, falling to the ground. The image behind the word is of something that collapses under its own weight. The commentators explain why the argument must collapse: it is not conducted in pursuit of truth but in defense of position; it begins after the response, so it argues against what its own speakers have already accepted; and it argues "about God" — about the very One whose knowledge encompasses the arguers' hearts. An argument that is hollow in the eyes of the Lord cannot be otherwise in the scales of the Day, whatever its brilliance in the assemblies of men. The verse thereby draws the line between two kinds of religious discourse that every community must learn to distinguish: the discourse of inquiry, which asks questions in order to find the truth — blessed by revelation — and the discourse of contention, which raises questions in order to escape the truth — condemned by this verse. The Prophet ﷺ was known to dislike contention; the report about him reciting the verse "they are but disputatious people" (43:58) captures the same aversion. The classical scholars of Islam consistently ranked *jadal* in religion among the most dangerous pursuits, precisely because it dresses self-will in the costume of inquiry.

**Wrath Upon the Disputers: The Severity of the Reversed Response**

The verse ends with the double sentence: "Upon them shall be wrath, and theirs shall be a severe punishment." The commentators note the fittingness of the requital: those who responded and then argued have committed a treachery against the very relationship the response created. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the condition of those who believed and then rejected:

> *"Whosoever disbelieves in God after having believed — save one who is compelled while his heart is at rest in faith — but whosoever opens his breast to disbelief, upon them is wrath from God, and theirs is a severe punishment."* (Qur'an 16:106)

The wrath is the affective face of divine justice — not the passion of an offended tyrant but the settled opposition of holiness to the betrayal of known truth — and the "severe punishment" is its executive face. The verse's placement in this sūrah is strategic: it comes directly after the Prophet's declaration of verse 15 ("unto us our deeds, and unto you your deeds; there is no argument between us and you"), and it answers the idolaters who would treat that declaration as an invitation to continued polemic. The believers are told, in effect: you have made your declaration; let those who respond respond, and those who argue after responding know their verdict — for the argument is baseless, and the baseless cannot stand.

**The Modern Echo: Contentious Religion**

The verse carries a searching application for every age that has inherited the Prophet's declaration. Religious argument is ubiquitous in modern life — in media, in interfaith encounter, in the internal debates of communities — and this verse supplies the criterion for distinguishing its legitimate from its illegitimate forms. Legitimate religious discourse argues from the position of the seeker: honest questions, honestly pursued, with readiness to follow the evidence. Illegitimate discourse argues from the position of the respondent who has turned adversary: questions used as weapons, debate as status, contention as identity. The verse's test is simple: does the argument come "after responding" — after the heart has already bowed? If the arguer is seeking, let him be met with the fairest manner (29:46). If he is contending against what he knows, let the believer remember the verdict and decline the arena — for the hollow argument will collapse of its own weight, and the believer's task is not to win every debate but to deliver every message. The wrath belongs to God; the response belongs to the servant; and the severe punishment belongs to those who made the response itself into a pretext for war against the truth.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:17

> **God it is Who sent down the Book in truth, and the Balance. And what will apprise thee? Perhaps the Hour is nigh?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Book and the Balance: Revelation's Twin Deliveries**

The verse names two things God has sent down: the Book and the Balance — *al-Kitāb* and *al-Mīzān*. The pairing is deliberate and recurs in the Qur'an's account of divine order (see 57:25; 55:7–9): the Book supplies the knowledge of what is right, and the Balance supplies the measure by which right is distinguished from wrong and weighed in practice. The commentators unfold the pairing at three levels. In the cosmos, God set the Balance within creation itself — the heavens raised and the earth weighed down with mountains:

> *"And the heaven — He raised it, and set the Balance, that you transgress not in the balance. So weigh with justice and skimp not in the balance."* (Qur'an 55:7–9)

> *"We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and sent down with them the Book and the Balance, that people may uphold justice."* (Qur'an 57:25)

Among human beings, the Balance is the revealed law of justice — the command to weigh fairly in trade, to judge fairly in disputes, to give full measure (4:135; 6:152; 7:29, 85; 11:85; 16:90). And on the Day of Resurrection, the Balance is the scale on which deeds themselves are weighed:

> *"And We shall set up the scales of justice on the Day of Resurrection, and no soul shall be wronged in aught. Even if it be the weight of a mustard seed, We shall bring it."* (Qur'an 21:47)

The verse thus compresses the whole architecture of justice into two nouns: the standard (the Book) and the scale (the Balance) — the objective truth and the exact weighing — with God as the sender of both. A society that has the Book without the Balance has doctrine without justice; a society that has the Balance without the Book has measurement without meaning.

**Why the Book Must Come "In Truth"**

The phrase "sent down the Book in truth" (*bil-ḥaqq*) recurs throughout the Qur'an (2:176, 213; 3:3; 4:105; 5:48; 6:114; 39:2, 41) and carries a double force. First, it is a statement of origin: the Book descends carrying truth as its very substance — its reports are true, its promises are true, its warnings are true, because it issues from the One who cannot lie. Second, it is a statement of purpose: the Book descends *for* the sake of truth — to establish it, to distinguish it from falsehood, to judge by it. The commentators note the contrast with the idolaters' scriptures of convenience — the invented customs and contrived rulings that the sūrah will attack in verse 21 — which descend from no heaven and serve no truth. The Book's claim on the believer is total because its provenance is total: a Book that is true in origin and purpose cannot be received selectively, admired aesthetically, or obeyed only where convenient. To believe in it is to submit to its judgments; to submit to its judgments is to use its Balance.

**The Rhetorical Question: Nearing the Hour**

The verse then turns with a suddenness that has moved commentators for centuries: "And what will apprise thee? Perhaps the Hour is nigh?" The phrase "what will apprise thee?" (*wa mā yudrīka*) is the Qur'an's formula for introducing a reality beyond ordinary human reckoning (see 69:3; 82:17–18; 97:2; 101:3), and the answer it gestures toward is the nearness of the Hour. The commentators understand the question as an injunction to prepare: the Hour's proximity is not a date to be calculated but a state to be lived in — every breath may be the last before it (IK). The Prophet ﷺ was asked about its timing and turned the question toward preparation, in the exchange Ibn Kathīr cites:

> A man asked, "O Messenger of God, when is the Hour?" The Prophet ﷺ replied, "What have you prepared for it?" The man said, "Nothing, save that I love God and His Messenger." The Prophet ﷺ said, "You will be with those whom you love." (al-Bukhārī 6171; Muslim 2639, from Anas)

The reply is the perfect commentary on this verse: the Hour's date is hidden precisely so that preparation — not calculation — becomes the believer's occupation. And the sign of true preparation is not the accumulation of supererogatory works alone but the love that attaches the heart to God and His Messenger — for the gathered will be with those they love.

**The Nearness That Reorders Every Calculation**

The verse's final clause — "Perhaps the Hour is nigh" — functions as the Qur'an's standing disturbance of every human calculation. Human beings plan as though time were unlimited: careers, mortgages, ambitions, reconciliations postponed to "someday." The verse interjects the possibility that the End is nearer than the next plan — not to induce panic but to induce priority. The classical commentators note that the nearness of the Hour is relative: measured against the age of the world, every human life is a breath, and measured against the certainty of death, the Hour is always at the door. The believer who internalizes the clause orders his life by a different schedule: the deeds of the Hereafter before the harvests of this world (v. 20), repentance before the term expires (v. 14), response before the day none can repel (v. 47). The Book and the Balance tell him what to do; the nearness of the Hour tells him when — now, before the balance is set and the books are closed.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:18

> **Those who believe not in it seek to hasten it, and those who believe are wary of it and know that it is the truth. Are not those who dispute the Hour far astray?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Hasteners: Mockery Disguised as Courage**

The verse draws the first line of the Day's two audiences: "Those who believe not in it seek to hasten it." The Qur'an preserves the actual words of the hasteners — a refrain running through the Makkan period — as they demanded the Hour be brought forward as proof against the Prophet:

> *"And they say: When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?"* (Qur'an 10:48; 21:38; 27:71; 34:29; 36:48; 67:25)

> *"They ask thee to hasten the punishment before its time."* (Qur'an 22:47)

The psychology of the demand deserves attention. The hasteners were not sincerely eager for the Hour's arrival; they were mocking its announcement. Their "hastening" was the taunt of those who treat a warning as a bluff — the equivalent of a debtor demanding that the creditor sue him, in the confidence that no suit will come. The commentators observe that the demand is self-incriminating in a way the mockers did not perceive: only someone certain that the Hour will not come could ask for it so brazenly, and certainty about the non-arrival of what God has promised is itself the height of folly. The Qur'an's reply to the hasteners is to remind them that what they hasten is their own punishment:

> *"They ask thee to hasten the punishment; and had it not been for a term appointed, the punishment would have come upon them. Yet it shall come upon them suddenly, while they are unaware."* (Qur'an 29:53)

**The Wary: Knowledge as the Ground of Fear**

Against the hasteners stands the second audience: "and those who believe are wary of it and know that it is the truth." The verse's grammar is exact — the believers' wariness (*mushfiqūn*) is grounded in knowledge (*yaʿlamūna annahu al-ḥaqq*): they fear the Hour because they know it is real, and they know it is real because they know the One who promised it. The fear described here is not the paralysis of terror but the alertness of the traveler who knows the road's end and prepares for it — the same disposition the sūrah will praise as the believers' mark in verse 22, where the wrongdoers are wary of what they have earned while the believers' wariness was expressed, in this life, as deeds. The Qur'an describes the believers' stance toward the Day in similar terms elsewhere:

> *"Those who are wary of the punishment of their Lord — surely the punishment of their Lord is not to be felt secure from — ..."* (Qur'an 70:27–28)

The contrast between the two parties is not fear versus fearlessness but knowledge versus ignorance: both face the same unknown future, but the believer's knowledge converts the future into a motive for preparation, while the disbeliever's ignorance converts it into an occasion for mockery. This is the Qur'an's consistent analysis of bravado about the Last Day: it is not courage but blindness — the boldness of a man mocking a cliff he cannot see.

**The Disputers: The Verdict of Distance**

The verse closes with a question that is itself the verdict: "Are not those who dispute the Hour far astray?" The rhetorical form assumes the answer: yes, plainly astray. The commentators unpack the word *dalāl* — "going astray" — in its literal sense of wandering off the road: those who dispute the Hour have lost the road of wisdom because every sound argument leads toward the Day's certainty. Consider the chain of reasoning the sūrah has already supplied: the One who originated the heavens and the earth (v. 11) can gather them (v. 29); the One who gives life can give it again (v. 9); the Book that came in truth (v. 17) reports the Gathering; the Balance that was sent down (v. 17) presupposes a day of weighing; and the justice that is obvious to every human conscience — the sense that wrongs must be righted — demands a day of righting that only the Hereafter can supply. To dispute the Hour is to dispute the coherence of the whole: creation without consummation, law without judgment, justice without a Day.

**Hastening and Wary Living in Modern Dress**

The verse's two audiences renew themselves in every generation. The modern hasteners do not usually say "when is the Hour?"; they say it in secular dialect — "if there is a reckoning, let it come" — in the assurance of a world that believes itself beyond divine accounting, or in the mockery of a culture that treats belief in the Last Day as primitive. And the modern believers are still the wary: the Muslims who pray the dawn prayer in the dark, pay the zakāh quietly, and order their money, speech, and desires by a Day they have not seen but know to be true. The verse's comfort to the wary is subtle but complete: the fear they feel is the very sign of their knowledge, and the knowledge will not disappoint them — the Hour is the truth, and the truth arrives. Its warning to the disputers is equally complete: distance from the road is not the same as independence from it; the road ends at the Day whether or not one walks it, and the dispute that seems bold in the assemblies of men will seem what it always was — far astray — when the hastened Hour arrives.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:19

> **God is kind unto His servants; He provides for whomsoever He will, and He is the Strong, the Mighty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Hopeful Verse — and the Kindness It Names**

The verse opens with a divine name whose depth the commentators were at pains to unfold: *Allāhu laṭīfun bi-ʿibādihi* — "God is kind unto His servants." Some scholars count this among the most hopeful verses of the Qur'an, in the company of the verses of forgiveness and mercy (others are 24:22; 33:47; 39:53; 42:22; 93:5). The name *al-Laṭīf* carries two families of meaning, and the classical exegetes insisted on holding both together. The first is gentleness in action: God is Kind, receiving His servants with honor (Ṭs), treating them with a tenderness that exceeds what their deeds deserve. The second is acuteness in perception: God is Subtle, aware of the most intimate and minute details of every creature's condition (Ṭs) — the sense captured in the Qur'an's repeated pairing of the name with "the Aware": *al-Laṭīf al-Khabīr* (6:103; 22:63; 31:16; 33:34; 67:14). Al-Rāzī adds a third shade: the Kind is also the Righteous and Merciful (R) — the One whose subtlety never serves severity. Al-Ghazālī, in his treatise on the divine names, synthesizes the attribute with the others in a passage the commentators treasure:

> "He is Wise insofar as He directs things, Generous insofar as He brings them into existence, Fashioner insofar as He gives them order, Just insofar as He puts each thing in its proper place, and Subtle insofar as He does not overlook the intricacies of gentleness regarding them." (al-Ghazālī, al-Maqṣad al-Asnā fī Sharḥ Asmāʾ Allāh al-Ḥusnā)

**Kindness as the Engine of Provision**

The verse then connects the kindness to the provision: "He provides for whomsoever He will." The connection is causal in the commentators' reading: it is precisely through the attribute of kindness and subtlety that God provides for whom He will, because provision requires knowing the precise needs of every created thing — the needs it does not know how to voice, the times at which it cannot wait, the measure it cannot survive without. The verse's ground is stated fully in Sūrah Hūd:

> *"There is no creature that crawls upon the earth, but that its provision lies with God. And He knows its dwelling place and its repository."* (Qur'an 11:6)

The claim is total in its sweep and intimate in its application: the God who provides is the God who knows — and the God who knows the sparrow's need and the whale's hunger knows the exact contour of every human need as well. No servant of God is outside the scope of the Kind: not the sick whose provision is patience, not the poor whose provision is the means of the day, not the grieving whose provision is the mercy that arrives without announcement.

**The Logic of Kindness in the Midst of Warnings**

The verse's placement is its own commentary. It stands between the severe warnings of verses 16–18 — wrath upon the disputers, punishment for those who hasten the Hour — and the sūrah's continuing argument. The juxtaposition is deliberate: the Qur'an never lets the warnings stand without the kindness, and never lets the kindness become license. The same God who is wrathful toward the arguing respondent is Kind unto His servants; the same Lord whose Hour the mockers hasten is the Lord who provides for His creatures before they ask. The commentators note the precision of the verse's address: "unto His servants" (*bi-ʿibādihi*) — the kindness is toward those who stand in the relation of servanthood, whether they have been faithful in it or negligent in it, for the servant's relation to the Master is never dissolved by failure; it is only distorted. The verse thereby holds open the door that the previous verses seemed to close: the disputers may be under wrath, but the servants are under kindness — and the way from one condition to the other is the way of repentance that the sūrah has already opened (v. 13) and will open again (v. 25).

**The Strong, the Mighty: Kindness With Power**

The verse closes with the pair "the Strong, the Mighty" — *al-Qawī, al-ʿAzīz* — and the pairing completes the portrait. Kindness without power would be pity without effect — the goodwill of the weak, which can wish well but cannot deliver. Power without kindness would be force without warmth — the capability of the tyrant, which can deliver but will not wish well. The God of the Qur'an is both: Kind in will and Strong in execution, so that His kindness is effective and His strength is benevolent. This is the precise point at which the Qur'anic conception of God differs from every human model of authority: human rulers must choose between being loved and being obeyed, because their power is finite and their kindness is fragile; God alone unites the two without remainder — His gentleness never compromises His might, and His might never chills His gentleness. For the believer, the verse is a complete therapy of the soul's image of God: the one who fears the Strong must remember the Kind; the one who presumes upon the Kind must remember the Strong; and the one who does both — fear and hope in balance — has understood the name that the verse sets at the center of the believer's devotional life. The Prophet ﷺ taught the community to begin its greatest acts in the same double posture, and the verse's pairing supplies the reason: servanthood flourishes only where awe and intimacy share one heart.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:20

> **Whosoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter, We shall increase for him his harvest. And whosoever desires the harvest of this world, We shall give him some thereof, but he will have no share in the Hereafter.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Harvests: Agriculture as the Master Metaphor**

The verse frames human aspiration through the image of the *ḥarth* — the cultivated field, the harvest — an image drawn from the agricultural world in which the earliest hearers lived and which every agrarian and post-agrarian society still understands. The commentators note the aptness of the metaphor at three points. First, a harvest presupposes sowing: the desire for a harvest is really a desire for the fruits of one's own planting, so the verse's two harvests correspond to two ways of spending a life. Second, a harvest is seasonal: it comes after the work, not instead of it — no farmer harvests in the month of sowing. Third, a harvest is determined by the seed: "whatsoever you sow you shall reap," as the Qur'an says in another image (see 6:164; 53:39). The two harvests are the Hereafter and this world: the yield of deeds stored for the meeting with God, and the yield of goods gathered for the self in the present life. The Qur'an contrasts them throughout (see 3:145, 152; 4:134; 17:18–19; 18:28; 28:79–80):

> *"Whosoever desires the life of this world and its adornment, We shall pay them in full for their deeds therein, and they shall not be diminished therein. Those are they for whom there is naught in the Hereafter but the Fire."* (Qur'an 11:15–16)

**The Divine Accounting: Increase for One, Portion for the Other**

The verse then states the two divine responses with a precision that rewards attention. For the seeker of the Hereafter: "We shall increase for him his harvest" — the word is *nizīd*, We shall increase — because the divine economy rewards the deed beyond its measure. Ibn Kathīr notes that God increases the believer's reward tenfold or even seven hundredfold (IK), as the Qur'an declares in its great parable of spending:

> *"The parable of those who spend their wealth in the way of God is that of a grain that grows seven ears, in every ear a hundred grains. And God multiplies for whomsoever He will."* (Qur'an 2:261)

For the seeker of this world: "We shall give him some thereof" — the word is *nuʾtihi minhā*, "We shall give him *some* of it" — and the commentators pause at the word "some": the worldly seeker is not promised all of what he seeks, only a portion, and that portion is subject to the divine measure of verse 12 and 27; he may be given less than he hoped, and what he is given is given in this world, which is precisely where it must be left. The verse's final clause delivers the true loss: "but he will have no share in the Hereafter" — *mā lahu fī al-ākhirati min kha-lāq* — no portion, no allotment, no share. The two economies are thus not two paths to the same end but two currencies with different exchange rates: the Hereafter-seeker's worldly losses are converted into eternal gains, while the world-seeker's worldly gains are spent in a currency that the Hereafter does not accept.

**Why Seeking the Hereafter Increases Even This World's Harvest**

The verse contains a further subtlety that the commentators drew out: the seeker of the Hereafter is not deprived of this world — the verse promises him *increase*, and the increase may include provision in the present life as well. The Qur'an states the principle directly in its account of those who migrated for God's sake:

> *"And whosoever emigrates in the way of God will find upon the earth many a refuge and abundance."* (Qur'an 4:100)

The logic is the logic of the farmer who invests in the better field: the one who orders his life by the Hereafter receives the Hereafter's harvest *and* the peace of this world, because the anxiety that consumes the worldly seeker — the fear of loss, the envy of rivals, the hunger for more — is dissolved by the knowledge that the real crop is stored with God. The Prophet ﷺ expressed the same truth when he said of the community's danger:

> "Whosoever among them performs the deeds of the Hereafter for the sake of this world will have no portion in the Hereafter" (recorded by Ibn Kathīr in his commentary on this verse)

**The Test of the Heart's Direction**

The verse is ultimately a test — and the test is administered by the heart itself. The commentators observe that the verse does not forbid seeking worldly goods; it forbids *desiring* them as the final harvest, and it distinguishes the two by a question every person can ask himself: if the work I am doing received no worldly return, would I still do it? The prayer performed only in public, the charity given only for reputation, the knowledge pursued only for position — these are worldly harvests sown in religious soil, and the verse warns that their yield ends at the applause. The deed done for God alone — the prayer in the last of the night, the gift given in secret, the truth spoken at cost — is the seed of the harvest that God increases. The Sufi commentators add the final refinement: for those who love God for Himself, even the harvest of the Hereafter is not the goal — the goal is God (Aj, Bq, Su); such servants have transcended the two harvests entirely and seek only the Face of the Giver. But for every soul still choosing between fields, the verse supplies the whole of practical wisdom: desire determines destiny, the heart's crop is the heart's seed, and the field one waters is the field one will reap.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:21

> **Or have they partners who have prescribed for them as religion that which God did not permit? Were it not for the Word of Division, judgment would have been made between them. And truly the wrongdoers shall have a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Exposes Invented Religion**

The verse opens with an interrogative of exposure: "Or have they partners who have prescribed for them as religion that which God did not permit?" Ibn Juzayy paraphrases the question: do they have gods who have created another religion for them that God did not prescribe (IJ)? The question answers itself, for the "partners" of the idolaters — the idols, the soothsayers, the ancestral authorities — possess no power to prescribe anything; they are prescribed to, not prescribing. Behind the rhetorical form lies the sūrah's deepest charge against the religious order of pre-Islamic Arabia: its religion was not divine in origin but human in manufacture. The Qur'an catalogs the manufactured religion of the Arabs — the sacred camels with slit ears, the released livestock, the prohibitions invented without revelation:

> *"God has not appointed any Bahīrah, Sāʾibah, Waṣīlah, or Ḥām — but those who disbelieve fabricate lies against God, and most of them do not understand."* (Qur'an 5:103)

> *"And they assign to God, from the tillage and cattle He created, a portion, and say: This is for God — so they claim — and this is for our partners."* (Qur'an 6:136)

The charge is that the idolaters have done the thing the previous verse forbade in the opposite direction: verse 20 distinguished the two harvests by the heart's desire; verse 21 exposes those who have *legislated* for themselves — who have taken the right to declare what is sacred and profane, permitted and forbidden, and exercised it without warrant from God.

**The Principle: Religion Is Prescription, Not Invention**

The verse establishes, by implication, one of the Qur'an's foundational principles: religion is received, not produced. The right to prescribe what is lawful and unlawful, what draws near to God and what distances from Him, belongs to God alone — because religion is the path to God, and only the One who is the path's end can mark its course. This is why the Qur'an treats the invention of religious rules as a species of shirk: the inventor has taken to himself an attribute of divinity, the attribute of legislation. The commentators draw the logical line through the Qur'an's own statements:

> *"Say: Have you considered what God has sent down unto you of provision, and you have made some of it unlawful and some lawful? Say: Has God permitted you this, or do you fabricate lies against God?"* (Qur'an 10:59)

The same principle governs the famous hadith in which the Prophet ﷺ declared that lawful is what God made lawful and unlawful is what God made unlawful. Every religion of mere custom — whether the customs of Arabian tribes or the customs of modern societies — stands under this verse's question: who prescribed this? If the answer is human authority alone, the prescription is invention; if the answer is God through His revelation, the prescription is religion. The distinction is the difference between worship and self-worship, and the verse does not allow it to blur.

**The Word of Division: Why Judgment Waits**

The verse then makes the same theological point as verse 14, with a different word: "Were it not for the Word of Division, judgment would have been made between them." The *Word of Division* — *kalimah al-faṣl* — is the divine decree that separates truth from falsehood and the believers from the disbelievers at the appointed time; the Day of Judgment is itself called the Day of Division (37:21; 44:40; 77:13–14, 38; 78:17), and the Qur'an elsewhere calls the Qur'anic judgment a "decisive word" (*qawl faṣl*; 86:13). The commentators explain the verse's logic (cf. 10:19; 11:110; 41:45; 42:14): had God not already decreed that the division between the believers and the inventors of religion would take place on the Day of Resurrection, the inventors would have been judged and destroyed immediately, in this world, at the moment of their invention. The deferral is mercy — the same mercy that keeps the door of repentance open — but the deferral is not forever: the Word of Division has fixed the term, and when the term arrives, the invented religions and their inventors will meet the judgment that was waiting since the first fabrication.

**The Painful Punishment and the Justice of the Delay**

The verse closes by naming what waits: "And truly the wrongdoers shall have a painful punishment." The commentators note that the "wrongdoers" here are identified by their deed: those who prescribed religion without God's permission have wronged themselves first — by exchanging the true religion, which leads to God, for an invented one, which leads only to its inventors — and they have wronged their followers, whom they led into the same exchange. The painful punishment is the natural completion of the wrong: the inventors of religion will meet, on the Day of Division, the full weight of what they fabricated — every soul they misled testifying against them, every rule they invented weighing against them. The verse thereby performs a double service for the believer. It secures the purity of religion: no human institution, however ancient, however powerful, may add to the prescribed religion or subtract from it — the sūrah will return to this in the believer's portrait, where the mark of the faithful is response to the Lord (v. 38), not submission to invented intermediaries. And it secures the patience of the believer: when false religion prospers in the world — when fabricated rulings, superstitions, and human legislation parade as divine — the believer is not to despair or to take judgment into his own hands, for the Word of Division has already fixed the term, and the painful punishment is already reserved for the wrongdoers. The delay is the mercy; the mercy is not the cancellation.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:22

> **Thou seest the wrongdoers wary of that which they have earned; for it will fall upon them. And those who believe and perform righteous deeds shall be in flowering meadows of the Gardens. With their Lord they shall have whatsoever they will. That is the great bounty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reversal of Wariness**

The verse opens with a scene whose placement is deliberately ironic: "Thou seest the wrongdoers wary of that which they have earned." In this life, it was the believers who were wary — wary of the Hour (v. 18), wary of their Lord's reckoning, wary of the deeds that might undo them. On the Day of Resurrection, the wariness changes sides. The wrongdoers — who in this world feared nothing and mocked the warning — now stand on the plain of assembly, wary of what their own hands have earned, because the harvest of their deeds is present before them, and its weight is known. The commentators describe the scene: when everyone is gathered on the plain of Resurrection, the wrongdoers become wary of the recompense for the deeds they committed in the world (IK, Ṭ) — the Qur'an elsewhere pictures them facing the record:

> *"And they shall find all that they did present before them; and thy Lord wrongs no one."* (Qur'an 18:49)

The clause "for it will fall upon them" removes the last hope of escape: the earned recompense is not merely displayed but descending — the punishment is coming upon them as the sentence upon the guilty. The verse's picture is of the moment between the sight of the record and the receipt of its consequences — the moment of full knowledge and total helplessness, which the Qur'an elsewhere describes as the moment when the wrongdoers will wish for ransom (10:54) and will say:

> *"Our Lord, we have seen and heard; so send us back and we shall work righteousness. Truly we are certain."* (Qur'an 32:12)

But the return is not granted; the earned thing falls.

**The Meadows: The Reward's Geography**

Against the wrongdoers' scene stands the believers': "And those who believe and perform righteous deeds shall be in flowering meadows of the Gardens." The word *rawḍāt* (sing. *rawḍah*) is carefully chosen — the philologists note that a *rawḍah* is a place in which vegetation is plentiful (Ṭ), a watered garden-meadow, the most pleasing part of a garden (R); the word later came to designate the most honored enclosure of the Prophet's mosque in Madinah, "a meadow of the meadows of Paradise," in his own words (al-Bukhārī 1196; Muslim 1390). The commentators observe the pairing of the conditions: belief and righteous deeds — the confession that orients and the conduct that confirms, the two harvests of verse 20 sown together. Some exegetes read the verse as distinguishing ranks within the Garden itself: the believers who merely performed their prayers will be in the Garden, while those who performed righteous deeds in full will be in the flowering meadows, a specially designated part (R) — the rawḍāt being the gardens within the Gardens, the inner enclosure of the blessed. Others understand the meadows figuratively, as the happiness and blessings the believers enjoy (Ṭ) — but the figurative reading does not diminish the literal promise, for the Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden are descriptions of a real place whose delights exceed every figure.

**"With Their Lord They Shall Have Whatsoever They Will"**

The verse's central clause is its most astonishing: *wa ʿinda rabbihim mā yashāʾūn* — "with their Lord they shall have whatsoever they will." The commentators note that the phrase refers to the bounties of Paradise being already prepared for them (R; see 16:31; 25:16; 39:34; 50:35) — the Gardens are not a reward to be negotiated but a gift awaiting arrival, stocked before the guest. The clause "whatsoever they will" is absolute: no list of delights can exhaust it, because the content of the promise is the will itself — the blessed shall not be given a predetermined menu but shall have what they wish, in the measure that God's good pleasure permits. The Qur'an adds the crowning detail in its description of the Garden's ultimate gift, which is not a thing at all:

> *"Faces that Day shall be radiant, gazing upon their Lord."* (Qur'an 75:22–23)

The classical commentators connect this verse to the believers' own report of what they will say when the bounties are complete — and the Prophet ﷺ described the final addition to the Garden's joys in terms that make "whatsoever they will" concrete:

> *"God will say: I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and has never occurred to the human heart."* (al-Bukhārī 3244; Muslim 2824, from Abū Hurayrah)

**The Great Bounty: Why This, Not That**

The verse ends with the divine verdict on the whole arrangement: "That is the great bounty" — *dhālika huwa al-fawḍ al-ʿaẓīm*. The word *fawḍ* denotes the immense gift that exceeds all deserving — the triumph that is not wages but favor. The commentators draw out the logic: the believers' entry into the meadows is called the great bounty precisely because it is not proportional to their deeds — no human deed could purchase a meadow of Paradise; the deeds were the seed, but the harvest is the generosity of the Sower. The verse thereby guards two truths at once: the deeds matter — the verse conditions the reward on belief and righteous deeds, and the sūrah will not allow the condition to be dropped — and the deeds do not suffice — the reward is called bounty, not payment, and the believer who enters the meadow enters it as a recipient of favor, not a claimant of wages. The wrongdoers' portion, by contrast, is not called bounty but earnings: "that which they have earned" falls upon them, for justice is the default of the divine order while mercy is its crown. The believer's whole life, between these two scenes, is the effort to be found on the side of the bounty: wary in this world so as not to be wary in the next, believing and doing so as to reach the meadows, and hoping — always hoping — for the great bounty that no eye has seen.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:23

> **This is that whereof God gives glad tidings to His servants, those who believe and perform righteous deeds. Say, "I ask not of you any reward for it, save affection among kinsfolk." And whosoever accomplishes a good deed, We shall increase him in goodness thereby. Truly God is Forgiving, Thankful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Glad Tidings: Good News as the Mode of Divine Address**

The verse opens by identifying its own genre: *dhālika alladhī yubashshiru Allāhu ʿibādahu* — "This is that whereof God gives glad tidings to His servants." The reference looks back to the meadows of the Gardens promised in verse 22 (IJ) and to the great bounty (Ṭs): what the wrongdoers fear, the believers are given as good news — announced in advance, as the traveler's destination is announced before the journey. The commentators note the significance of the verb *bushrā*: God's address to His servants is not primarily threat but tidings; the warnings of the sūrah are the shadows cast by the tidings' light. The glad tidings are specified for a particular audience — "those who believe and perform righteous deeds" — so that the good news does not become presumption: the promise belongs to the path, and the path is belief enacted. The Prophet's own practice followed the verse's grammar: he gave glad tidings, as when he said of his community:

> *"The people of Paradise are one hundred and twenty rows, of which eighty are from this community and forty from all the rest."* (al-Tirmidhī 2546, hasan ṣaḥīḥ, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī; similar in al-Bukhārī 6528)

**The Unpaid Messenger: No Reward Save Affection**

The verse then turns from the believers to the Prophet's address to his people: "Say: I ask not of you any reward for it, save affection among kinsfolk." The clause is among the most debated in the Qur'an, and the commentators mapped its readings with care. The context is established by the Qur'an's own repeated formula — the prophets ask no wage for their message: Noah said, "I ask not of you any reward for it; my reward is only upon the Lord of the worlds" (26:109), and Hūd (11:51; 26:127), Ṣāliḥ (26:145), Lot (26:164), and Shuʿayb (26:180) say the same; Muhammad is told elsewhere, "Say: I ask not of you any reward for it. It is naught but a reminder for the worlds" (6:90; cf. 25:57; 38:86). The present verse adds the single exception — *illa al-mawaddata fī al-qurbā* — and its meaning was read in several directions. The majority reading, associated with Ibn ʿAbbās and adopted by Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī, takes the affection as owed to the Prophet's own kin: he asks the Makkans only that they honor the ties of kinship binding them to him, since nearly every house of Quraysh was related to him. Ibn ʿAbbās said:

> "There was not a single house of the Quraysh but that it had kinship with the Prophet; and this verse was revealed in this regard, and its interpretation is: 'O Quraysh! Keep good relations between me and you.'" (recorded by Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī)

On this reading the verse means: I have not come to seize your property, your leadership, or your sanctuaries; the only "payment" I request is that you not sever the bonds of blood between us — that you treat me as kin rather than as an enemy, and that the affection natural to kinship open your hearts to the message. The request is the minimal possible wage — and its refusal by the Quraysh, who responded with persecution rather than affection, stands as the fullest proof of their hostility.

**The Other Readings: Love of the Prophet's House**

A second family of readings, favored by several Shiite and Sufi commentators (Aj, IJ, Qm, Ṭ, Ṭs) and by some of the Qur'an's earliest interpreters, understands the affection as owed to the Prophet's family — specifically his cousin and son-in-law ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, his daughter Fāṭimah, and their descendants (Aj, IJ, Q). On this reading the verse enjoins love of the Ahl al-Bayt, the Prophet's household, as the "wage" of his message — not a wage paid to him but a bond preserved after him, the affection that keeps his family's dignity intact and his community attached to his house. Al-Ṭabarī argued against this reading on grammatical grounds — the phrase, he noted, would need to be "affection *for* the kinsfolk" (*mawaddat al-qurbā*) to bear that sense — while Ibn ʿAjībah replied that the verse's actual phrasing ("affection *among* kinsfolk," with the Prophet included among the kin) provides a more emphatic injunction to love the Prophet's family, since it places him within the circle of those to be loved (Aj). The reported exchange on the verse's meaning is preserved in the tradition: when the Companions asked the Prophet who his kinsfolk were, the reports differ — some name his clan of Banū Hāshim, and a well-known transmission recorded by al-Tirmidhī has him answer, "ʿAlī, Fāṭimah, and their two sons" (al-Tirmidhī 3874, graded ḥasan gharīb). Whatever reading one adopts, the verse's practical content is agreed upon by all parties at the level of the community's duty: the Prophet's message must be received with love for his house and his kin, never with the hostility the Quraysh showed — and the love of the Prophet's family is, for the Sunnī tradition, part of the love of the Prophet himself, whom the believers are commanded to prefer to themselves and their families (33:6).

**Affection Itself as the Wage: The Economics of Love**

A third reading, attested among the early exegetes and developed by the Sufis, takes the phrase in its most universal sense: the Prophet asks of his people not wealth but love — "affection among kinsfolk" meaning that they should love one another as kin, or love him as they love their own kin (IJ, Ṭ), or draw near to God through love (the root *q-r-b* of *qurbā* means nearness, so that the phrase may be read "love through nearness" — love that draws near through pious deeds) (Aj, IJ, Q, Ṭ, Ṭū). The commentators note how the clause functions within the sūrah's economics: verse 20 divided the harvests of this world and the Hereafter; verse 21 condemned those who invented religion for wages of status; and here the Prophet declares that his own wage is love — the one treasure that belongs to both worlds at once. The verse thereby establishes the emotional constitution of the Muslim community: its bond to its Prophet is not contractual but affectionate, and its internal bond is the same — the community that the sūrah will describe in verse 38 as conducting its affairs by counsel is first described here as bound by affection. The Prophet ﷺ made the same point when he said:

> *"None of you believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself."* (al-Bukhārī 13; Muslim 45, from Anas)

**Increase for the Doers: The Divine Response**

The verse then returns from the Prophet's request to God's promise: "And whosoever accomplishes a good deed, We shall increase him in goodness thereby." The commentators connect the promise to the Qur'an's arithmetic of reward — the good deed is multiplied (2:245, 261; 4:40; 6:160; 64:17), and the verse's parallel states the increase as the divine surplus:

> *"Unto those who are virtuous shall be that which is most beautiful, and more besides."* (Qur'an 10:26)

The verse's final pair of names supplies the reason for the increase: "Truly God is Forgiving, Thankful" — *al-Ghafūr, al-Shakūr*. The pairing is one of the Qur'an's most consoling: God forgives the bad deed that repentance removes, and He thanks the good deed that obedience offers — thanking, in the divine idiom, meaning that He accepts it, magnifies it, and rewards it beyond its worth (cf. 35:30, 34). The commentators marvel at the courtesy of the divine economy: the creature's obedience adds nothing to God, yet God receives it as though it were a gift to Him, and repays the gift with increase. The Forgiving removes the past; the Thankful rewards the present; between the two, the believer's ledger is always open and always hopeful. The verse thereby closes the circle it opened: the glad tidings announced to the believing servants are secured by the two names — the sins that would disqualify are forgiven, and the deeds that qualify are multiplied — so that the meadows of verse 22 stand open to every servant who believes, does right, and loves, in the measure of the One who is both Forgiving and Thankful.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:24

> **Or do they say, "He has fabricated a lie against God?" If God willed, He would put a seal upon thy heart. God wipes out falsehood and verifies the truth through His Words. Truly He knows what lies within breasts.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charge of Fabrication: The Quraysh's Final Accusation**

The verse opens with the accusation that the Quraysh deployed against the Prophet ﷺ as their last line of defense: "Or do they say: He has fabricated a lie against God?" — meaning that Muhammad invented the Qur'an and attributed it to God. The charge appears throughout the Makkan sūrahs and always receives the same treatment — a challenge to produce its like:

> *"Or do they say: He has fabricated it? Say: Then bring a sūrah like it, and call upon whomsoever you can apart from God, if you are truthful."* (Qur'an 10:38; cf. 11:13, 35; 32:3; 46:8; 52:33)

The verse's opening question is rhetorical exposure: the accusation is so absurd that the Qur'an does not argue with it at length but dismantles it from within. The accusers themselves knew the Prophet's biography: the man they called al-Amīn, "the Trustworthy," before revelation; the man who had lived forty years among them without composing a line of poetry or making a single claim; the man whose message condemned the very practices on which his own clan's prestige depended — for it is a poor fabrication that costs its author his tribe's protection, his trade, and his safety. The charge of fabrication, the commentators note, was not an intellectual position but a social weapon: it provided the Quraysh with a formula that justified persecution while preserving their self-image as reasonable people.

**The Seal Upon the Heart: The Reply From Divine Protection**

The verse answers the charge with a sentence whose precise meaning the commentators debated: "If God willed, He would put a seal upon thy heart." Two readings were offered. The first, favored by several exegetes, takes the sealing as protective: had the Prophet's enemies succeeded in their campaign of abuse and slander, God could have fortified his heart with patience and sealed it against their insults, so that their words would not wound him (JJ) — a reading that makes the sentence a statement of divine support. The second, favored by Ibn Kathīr and others (IJ, IK, Ṭ), takes the sealing as probationary and hypothetical: had the Prophet added anything to the revelation — had he fabricated — God would have sealed his heart so that he could no longer receive or retain the revelation, exactly as the hearts of those who corrupt the message are sealed. On this reading the verse argues from the integrity of the messenger: the very fact that revelation continued to flow, coherent and uncorrupted, over twenty-three years of persecution is proof that no fabrication occurred — for a fabricator would have been cut off from the source, his heart sealed, his recitation emptied of the divine influx. The Qur'an states the same protective logic in its most severe form:

> *"And had he ascribed to Us any sayings, We would have seized him by the right hand, then severed his life-vein. And none of you could have shielded him from it."* (Qur'an 69:44–47)

The verse's deeper point is the impossibility of the charge on the Qur'an's own premises: the Prophet was the recipient of revelation, not its author; the seal that would have stopped a fabricator was never applied, because there was nothing to stop.

**Wiping Out Falsehood and Verifying Truth: The Cosmic Law**

The verse's middle clause generalizes the argument into a law of reality: "God wipes out falsehood and verifies the truth through His Words." The commentators connect the clause to the Qur'an's declarations about the fate of falsehood:

> *"Say: Truth has come, and falsehood has vanished. Truly falsehood is ever vanishing."* (Qur'an 17:81)

> *"Nay, but We cast truth against falsehood, and it crushes it, and, behold, it vanishes."* (Qur'an 21:18)

The law is not a prediction but a present fact: falsehood is by nature evanescent — it endures only while untruth is undiscovered, and it collapses under the weight of its own emptiness; truth is by nature enduring — it is verified by God through His words, which are not subject to the revisions of human authorship. Applied to the charge of fabrication, the law means: if the Qur'an were falsehood, it would have vanished as all falsehoods vanish — its contradictions exposed, its claims disproven, its author forgotten. Instead, it has been verified in every generation: its prophecies fulfilled, its knowledge confirmed, its recitation preserved, its challengers silenced. The survival of the truth is not an accident of history; it is the divine method — "God verifies the truth through His Words" — so that the believer's confidence in the Qur'an rests not on the accident of its survival but on the law of its nature.

**The Knower of Breasts: The Final Witness**

The verse closes with the clause that brings the whole dispute to its proper tribunal: "Truly He knows what lies within breasts." The clause recurs throughout the Qur'an (3:119, 154; 5:7; 8:43; 11:5; 29:10; 31:23; 35:38; 39:7; 57:6; 64:4; 67:13) and functions here as the closing of every avenue of escape: the accusers may conceal their motives — the envy of verse 14, the pride of verse 13, the fear of losing rank — but the One who knows what lies within breasts knows whether the charge of fabrication is a search for truth or a shield for self-interest. The commentators add a further precision (Ṭ): the clause also implies that were the Prophet's own soul to suggest fabricating a lie against God, God would know it and would seal his heart — so that the verse covers both directions of suspicion: the Prophet is protected from fabrication by the divine knowledge, and the accusers are exposed by it. The verse thereby leaves the disputers of Makkah with no court but the One who knows breasts — and before that court, the fabricated accusation dissolves into the motive from which it came. For the believer in every age, the verse supplies the model of responding to slander of the revelation and its bearers: not by matching accusation with accusation, but by pointing to the law — falsehood vanishes, truth is verified, and the Knower of breasts will expose what the speakers conceal, in this world or at the Gathering.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:25

> **He it is Who accepts repentance from His servants and pardons evil deeds. And He knows whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Opened a Door**

After the severity of the previous verses — wrath upon the disputers (v. 16), the painful punishment of the inventors (v. 21), the wariness of the wrongdoers (v. 22) — the sūrah opens a door with a sentence of pure mercy: "He it is Who accepts repentance from His servants and pardons evil deeds." The commentators note that this is one of only two verses in the Qur'an that explicitly state that God *accepts* repentance (the other being 9:104), while God is elsewhere named the Accepter of Repentance (40:3) and repentance is enjoined throughout the Book. The verse's construction is emphatic: the pronoun "He it is" (*Huwa alladhī*) sets God alone as the acceptor — no priest, no intermediary, no institution stands between the sinner and the acceptance; the servant turns and is met directly. The pairing of the two clauses is also exact: acceptance of the repentance and pardon of the evil deeds are two motions of one act — the turning is received and the record is cleared. The Prophet ﷺ described the divine joy at repentance with an image that the commentators cite at this verse (IK):

> *"God rejoices more over the repentance of His servant than one of you who finds his stray camel in a waterless desert, carrying his provision of food and drink — after having lost it and given up hope, and lain down in the shade of a tree in despair — when suddenly it stands before him, and he takes its halter and cries in his joy: O God, Thou art my servant and I am Thy Lord — erring in his extreme joy."* (al-Bukhārī 6309; Muslim 2747, from Anas)

**The Divine Priority of Acceptance**

The classical scholars mined the verse for the principles of repentance theology. Al-Ṭabarī and the commentators after him note that God's acceptance precedes the sinner's turning in the order of mercy: the very existence of an "acceptance of repentance" in the divine names means that the door was opened before any sinner knocked — the invitation creates the response it invites. The Qur'an states the divine priority in the verse's companion:

> *"Then his Lord chose him, and turned unto him, and guided him."* (Qur'an 20:122 — of Adam after his slip)

The verse's phrase "from His servants" is deliberately inclusive: not from the righteous only, but from the servants — all who stand in the relation of servanthood, whatever their record. The tradition records the practical use the earliest believers made of this verse: Ibn Kathīr transmits that ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd recited it when asked about a man who had committed fornication with a woman and then wished to marry her — reciting the verse of acceptance to show that the sin, sincerely repented, does not bar the door of God's pardon (IK). The same spirit appears in the hadith of the man who killed ninety-nine people and then sought a scholar's guidance; when the scholar told him that nothing could bar his repentance, he completed the hundredth killing — and was told to go to a land whose people worshipped God; he set out for it, and death overtook him on the road, and the angels of mercy and the angels of punishment disputed over him, and God judged him by the measure of his intention — nearer to the land of righteousness by a span, so he was counted among its people (al-Bukhārī 3470; Muslim 2766).

**The Limits of Acceptance: The Verse's Own Boundaries**

Yet the verse is not license, and the commentators are careful to state the conditions that the Qur'an itself attaches to repentance. The acceptance belongs to repentance that is real — and the Qur'an defines the reality by its timing and its fruit:

> *"Truly those who disbelieve after having believed, then increase in disbelief, their repentance shall not be accepted, and they are the ones astray."* (Qur'an 3:90)

> *"And repentance is not accepted from those who do evil deeds till, when death confronts one of them, he says: Truly now I repent — nor from those who die as disbelievers. For those We have prepared a painful punishment."* (Qur'an 4:18)

The boundary is the boundary of life itself: repentance is accepted while the breath continues — the Prophet ﷺ said, "Whosoever repents before the sun rises from the west, God accepts his repentance" (Muslim 2703, from Abū Hurayrah) — but the repentance of the deathbed, uttered when the world has already ended for the speaker, is not repentance but regret, and regret without time cannot become amendment. The classical scholars defined sincere repentance (*tawbah al-naṣūḥ*, 66:8) by its three marks: cessation of the sin, remorse for it, and resolve not to return to it — with the fourth mark added where rights of others are involved: restitution to those wronged. The verse's own final clause supplies the motive: "And He knows whatsoever you do" — the repentance that God accepts is the repentance He sees; the performance of remorse without the reality of turning is known for what it is by the Knower, while the turning that no human witnesses is known by Him as well.

**The Psychological Architecture of Hope**

The verse's placement in the sūrah gives it its full psychological force. The believer who has followed the chapter from its warnings — the disputers under wrath, the wrongdoers wary before their earnings — might conclude that the case is closed against the sinner. The verse opens the file again: acceptance exists, pardon exists, and both are in the hands of the One whose knowledge of deeds is total. The sūrah's argument is thereby balanced exactly as the Qur'an always balances it: severity toward the impenitent, mercy toward the penitent — the same deed looked at from two sides of the turning. For the modern reader, the verse is the Qur'an's permanent answer to the two great psychological diseases of religious life: despair, which tells the sinner that his past is too heavy for pardon — answered by the Accepter of Repentance; and presumption, which tells the sinner that pardon is automatic — answered by the Knower of deeds, before whom repentance must be real while life remains. Between despair and presumption runs the narrow path the verse opens: turn now, turn truly, turn to the One who knows — and the acceptance that preceded your turning will meet you on the road, as it met the man whose camel stood before him in the desert, and as it meets every servant who has ever cried out from the depth of his failure to the God who accepts.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:26

> **He responds to those who believe and perform righteous deeds, and will increase them from His Bounty. Yet as for the disbelievers, theirs shall be a severe punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Responding Lord: Supplication as the Grammar of Servanthood**

The verse opens with a verb that defines the divine-human relationship in a single motion: *yastajību* — "He responds." The commentators read the verse in two directions, and both are true. In the first reading, God answers the supplications of those who believe and perform righteous deeds (Bg, IK, Ṭ): the prayer of the believer does not vanish into the void; it is heard, weighed, and answered — sometimes with the thing requested, sometimes with something better, sometimes with the averting of an equivalent evil, but never with silence, for the One who hears is the One who has promised:

> *"And when My servants ask thee concerning Me — truly I am near. I answer the call of the caller when he calls unto Me. So let them respond unto Me, and believe in Me, that they may be rightly guided."* (Qur'an 2:186)

> *"Call upon Me; I will respond unto you."* (Qur'an 40:60)

The Prophet ﷺ described the three forms the divine response may take, in the hadith recorded from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī:

> *"There is no Muslim who calls upon God with a supplication in which there is no sin and no severing of kinship, but that God grants him one of three things: He hastens for him what he requested, or He stores it for him in the Hereafter, or He averts from him an equivalent evil."* (al-Tirmidhī 3573, hasan ṣaḥīḥ; similar in Aḥmad)

In the second reading, the verse reverses the direction: those who believe and perform righteous deeds are the ones who *respond* — they respond to their Lord when He calls them to faith and obedience (Ṭ) — and the divine response to their response is the increase (v. 13 used the same reciprocity: God guides whosoever turns in repentance). Both readings converge on the same architecture: the relationship between God and the believer is a dialogue — the call, the response, the answering of the response, the increase — and the verse's placement after the acceptance of repentance (v. 25) means that the dialogue never dies: the sinner who turns is restored to the conversation at the exact point where he left it.

**The Increase From His Bounty**

The verse's middle clause states the content of the divine answer: "and will increase them from His Bounty" — *yazīduhum min faḍlihi*. The commentators unpack the increase in several registers. It is the increase of reward: God grants the believers more than they requested, beyond what their deeds earned (Ṭ) — the surplus that verse 20 promised to the seekers of the Hereafter's harvest. It is the increase of intercession: some exegetes understand the verse to mean that God allows the believers to intercede for their companions, and the "increase" is that they may intercede for the companions of their companions (Bg, IK, Q, Ṭ) — the saved are not merely saved but made savers, their rank raised by the very mercy they carry to others. And it is the increase of provision and grace in this life: the bounty (*faḍl*) of God is not confined to the Hereafter — the Qur'an uses the word for the divine surplus that reaches the believer in the present world as well. The phrase "from His Bounty" is carefully chosen over "as their due": the increase is not wages calculated but favor distributed — the believer's deed opens the door, and the bounty decides what lies beyond it.

**The Severe Punishment: The Other Side of the Response**

The verse closes with the counter-statement that prevents the verse from being misread as universal indulgence: "Yet as for the disbelievers, theirs shall be a severe punishment." The commentators note the structure of the verse: the response and increase belong to the believers; the severe punishment belongs to the disbelievers — and the disbelievers here are those who, unlike the believers of verse 25, did not turn: who were called and did not respond, who were warned and did not believe, who were offered acceptance and chose the dispute. The verse thereby supplies the exact boundary of the divine mercy: it is not that God is unwilling to forgive — verse 25 has established the opposite — but that forgiveness requires the motion of turning, and the disbelievers have refused the motion. The severe punishment is not the contradiction of the mercy but its other face: the same God who responds to the caller responds to the rejecter with the justice that rejection has earned. The verse's pairing also performs a quiet social function in the sūrah's argument: the believers of Makkah, surrounded by a hostile majority, are assured that the balance is not as it appears — the majority's present prosperity is not the final response, and the believers' present hardship is not the final word. The One who responds to His servants will respond at the Gathering with the increase; and the severe punishment, like the increase, is stored with the same Responder.

**A Life Lived as a Conversation**

The verse's gift to the believer is the assurance that the religious life is a conversation rather than a monologue — that prayer is not a ritual performed into silence but an address that is answered, and that obedience is not a rule followed into the void but a response that is met. This assurance transforms the practice of supplication: the believer who knows that the Responder answers does not pray as a formality but as a communication — pouring out the day's needs with the confidence of a child addressing a father who has promised to respond. And the assurance transforms the endurance of hardship: the believer who is not answered in the letter of his request knows that the response has taken one of the three forms the Prophet described — hastened, stored, or converted into protection — and that the increase from the Bounty is accumulating in a ledger that death will open. The verse's final clause keeps the conversation honest: the same God who responds to the caller warns the rejecter — so that the believer's prayer is always accompanied by the believer's turning, and the turning is always accompanied by the hope of the increase that no eye has seen.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:27

> **Had God outspread His provision for His servants, they would have behaved tyrannically upon the earth. But He sends down whatsoever He will according to a measure. Verily of His servants He is Aware, Seeing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Divine Restraint: Why Unlimited Wealth Would Ruin**

The verse opens with a counterfactual whose logic the commentators unfold with care: "Had God outspread His provision for His servants, they would have behaved tyrannically upon the earth." The sentence states that universal prosperity is not withheld because of divine stinginess but because of human frailty: were every servant given all the wealth he desired, the result would not be universal contentment but universal tyranny — *baghyan*, transgression, insolence, the overstepping of bounds that the Qur'an identifies as the characteristic disease of wealth. The commentators explain the mechanism: wealth unmeasured inflates the self; the human being who lacks nothing begins to imagine that he needs nothing — not even God; the restraint that poverty teaches is removed, and the soul oversteps its limits toward God and toward others (Ṭ, Q). The verse is reported to have been revealed regarding some of the poor and destitute among the early believers who desired the wealth and ease of this world (Ṭ) — in particular, according to several accounts, the homeless emigrants known as the People of the Veranda (*Ahl al-Ṣuffah*), who had given up their property in Makkah and sheltered in the Prophet's mosque in Madinah, and who may have looked with longing upon the wealth of other tribes (Bg, Q; see 2:273c). The revelation answers their longing not with mockery but with diagnosis: the wealth they desired would have become, in their hands, the instrument of the very tyranny they fled.

**The Prophet's Own Description of the Human Hunger**

The commentators connect the verse to the Prophet's celebrated description of the insatiability of the human appetite for wealth:

> *"If the son of Adam had a valley of gold, he would wish that he had two valleys; and nothing fills his mouth but the dust of the grave. And God turns in mercy unto whomever repents."* (al-Bukhārī 6437; Muslim 1048, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The image is exact: the appetite for accumulation has no internal ceiling — each valley of gold merely reveals the next valley's absence; and the only thing that finally fills the mouth of the greedy is the dust of the grave. The commentators apply the description to the verse's logic: if God had outspread provision without measure, the result would be the multiplication of valleys — and of the tyranny that the valleys purchase. The verse thereby supplies the theological rationale for the entire Qur'anic economics of distribution: wealth in the divine design is not an absolute good to be maximized but a test to be measured, and the divine measure (*qadr*) is the instrument of both justice and mercy — justice, because no one is given a burden of wealth greater than he can bear; mercy, because no one is deprived of what would have corrupted him.

**"According to a Measure": The Calibrated Descent**

The verse's positive statement follows: "But He sends down whatsoever He will according to a measure." The verb "sends down" (*yunazzilu*) suggests provision descending from above, as rain descends — and the comparison is deliberate, for the Qur'an treats wealth and rain under the same divine economy: both descend by measure, both can be withheld or granted, both are calibrated to the earth's capacity. The commentators stress the two words "whatsoever He will" and "according to a measure": the measure is not mechanical — God sends down what He wills — and the will is not arbitrary — it always operates through the measure. The result is the precise provision that suffices the servant (Q) and that God knows to be in his best interest (IK):

> *"God knows best the state of His servants: when an increase of provision will lead to their corruption, He withholds it; and when a decrease would corrupt them, He grants them increase."*

The classical commentators transmitted the principle in the form of a ḥadīth qudsī that al-Qurṭubī records at this verse:

> *"Among My believing servants are those for whom nothing but wealth will make them righteous; were I to make them poor, poverty would corrupt them. And among My believing servants are those for whom nothing but poverty will make them righteous; were I to make them wealthy, wealth would corrupt them. Verily I arrange for My servants according to My knowledge of their hearts, for I am Knowing, Aware."* (recorded by al-Qurṭubī in his commentary)

**"Aware, Seeing": The Personal Calibration**

The verse closes with the pair of names that guarantee the measure's justice: "Verily of His servants He is Aware, Seeing." The two names answer the question that every human being asks about his own portion: why is my provision what it is? The verse's answer is that the calibration is made by One who is Aware — who knows the inner state that wealth would corrupt or poverty would perfect — and Seeing — who observes the actual circumstances, temptations, and capacities of each servant. The measure is therefore not an impersonal economic law but a personal divine act: the provision of each human being has been set by a wisdom that takes that particular soul into account, as a physician measures a dose by the particular patient. This is why the verse's earlier companions in the sūrah (vv. 12, 19) had already established that God outspreads and straitens provision for whom He will as Knower of all things and Kind unto His servants: the measure and the mercy are one act.

**The Application: Contentment as the Believer's Wealth**

The verse's practical conclusion is the doctrine of contentment (*qanāʿah*), which the commentators draw out as the believer's true wealth:

> *"Richness is not the abundance of goods; richness is the richness of the soul."* (al-Bukhārī 6446; Muslim 1051, from Abū Hurayrah)

The believer who understands the verse stops measuring his life by the size of his portion and starts measuring it by the wisdom of the Giver: the portion that seems small may be the very measure that keeps him upright, and the portion that seems large is the heavier trust. The verse also corrects the social gaze in both directions: the poor are not to envy the rich — the rich man's wealth may be the burden that tests him beyond the poor man's strength; and the rich are not to despise the poor — the poor man's poverty may be the mercy that keeps him near to God. Both stand under the same Aware, Seeing Lord, each receiving the measure that his own heart required — until the day when the measures are lifted, the test is over, and the only wealth that remains is the one that no measure could corrupt: the mercy of the One who sends down provision according to a measure, and who sends down forgiveness without measure.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:28

> **He it is Who sends down the rain after they have despaired and spreads forth His Mercy. And He is the Protector, the Praised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rain After Despair: The Timing of the Mercy**

The verse moves from the general economy of provision to its most visible instance: "He it is Who sends down the rain after they have despaired and spreads forth His Mercy." The commentators note the context: when people have given up hope that rain will fall — after months of drought, when the sky has been searched in vain and the wells have failed — God sends it upon them at their time of need (cf. 30:49; 41:39) and bestows it upon all who are in that region, the righteous and the sinful alike, without discrimination (IK). The verse's choice of timing is its theology: the rain comes "after they have despaired" — not while hope still deferred the crisis, but at the point when human resource was exhausted. The pattern is deliberate and recurs throughout the Qur'an's account of divine aid: the help arrives when the means are spent, so that the help is recognized as help rather than as the fruit of human arrangement. The Qur'an describes the same sequence in the story of the drought-stricken city that disbelieved its messenger:

> *"And We sent not to a city a prophet but that We seized its people with adversity and hardship, that they might humble themselves."* (Qur'an 7:94)

And in the believers' own recollection of the relief after hardship:

> *"Then, after grief, He sent down upon you security — a slumber overcoming a group of you."* (Qur'an 3:154)

**The Rain That Falls on the Just and the Unjust**

The verse's phrase "and spreads forth His Mercy" — *wa yanshuru raḥmatahu* — describes the manner of the descent: the mercy is not dropped in a single point but spread abroad, covering the land — and, in the commentators' extension, covering the inhabitants without respect to their deserving. The rain falls on the field of the believer and the field of the idolater; it fills the cistern of the mosque and the cistern of the temple; it revives the crops of the grateful and the ungrateful alike. The Prophet ﷺ was asked whether charity given to the undeserving was accepted, and his answer extended the same logic to human giving; but the divine original is even wider: the mercy of provision in this world is universal, as the mercy of the rain is universal, while the mercy of the Hereafter is reserved for those who met the universal mercy with gratitude and obedience. The verse's pairing with verse 27 is exact: provision is measured (v. 27) but the rain descends after despair (v. 28) — the measure and the timing are both in the hand of the One who knows when the servant can bear no more, and who chooses that moment to spread forth the mercy, so that the servant's despair becomes the stage for the divine generosity.

**The Spiritual Rain: Mercy Upon the Dead Heart**

The Sufi commentators, Ibn ʿAjībah among them, read the verse as the Qur'an's own allegory of spiritual revival: "God sends the rain of divine inspirations down upon dead hearts, such that they are revived through remembrance of God and knowledge, after they have despaired" (Aj). The drought of the soul — the aridity that follows sin, the despair that follows repeated failure, the hardness that follows prolonged neglect — is ended by the same divine mercy that ends the drought of the land: the heart that has given up hope of its own revival is precisely the heart upon which the mercy descends, because the mercy is not earned but sent. The Qur'an makes the allegory explicit in its description of the earth's revival:

> *"And thou seest the earth desolate; but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells. Truly He Who gives it life is the Giver of life to the dead. Truly He is Powerful over all things."* (Qur'an 41:39)

The resurrection itself is argued from the rain: the God who revives the dead earth after its despair is the God who will revive the dead bodies after their decay — and the verse's placement in this sūrah, between the measure of provision (v. 27) and the signs of creation (v. 29), makes the rain the link between the daily miracle of provision and the final miracle of the Gathering.

**The Protector, the Praised: The Names That Follow the Rain**

The verse closes with the pair: "And He is the Protector, the Praised" — *al-Walī, al-Ḥamīd*. The commentators connect the names to the rain's aftermath: the One who sends the rain is the Protector whose sending is itself the protection of His servants' lives and harvests, and the Praised, whose sending calls forth the praise of every creature that drinks — the believer's *al-ḥamdu lillāh* at the sight of rain being the human echo of the praise that fills the heavens. The name al-Ḥamīd — the Praised, the Laudable — carries the sense of being worthy of praise in Himself and praised by His creatures for His acts; and no act calls forth praise more universally than the rain after despair, because no creature is outside its need. The verse thereby supplies the believer with the exact posture for every moment of relief: recognition of the Protector — the relief came from Him, not from the clouds — and praise of the Praised — the relief is His gift, and the praise is the return the gift requires. The Prophet ﷺ taught the community to greet the rain with gratitude and to greet drought with supplication, and the verse explains why both are acts of worship: the drought is the measured mercy of verse 27, and the rain is the spread mercy of verse 28 — and the servant who praises in both conditions has understood that the Protector is present in the withholding and the sending alike, and that the Praised is worthy of the praise that continues through the drought into the rain, and from the rain of this world to the mercy of the next.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:29

> **And among His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth and all moving creatures that He has scattered therein, and He is able to gather them together whensoever He will.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Signs: Creation as a Language**

The verse opens with the Qur'an's characteristic formula — *wa min āyātihi* — "and among His signs": the universe is not a collection of objects but a collection of signs (*āyāt*), each one pointing beyond itself to its Maker, exactly as the verses of the Qur'an (*āyāt*) point beyond their words to their Speaker. The commentators note that the same word serves both realities: the Book's verses and the cosmos's signs are one language, and the believer who reads the one learns to read the other. The verse names three orders of the sign: the heavens, the earth, and the moving creatures scattered within them — *mā bashsha fīhimā min dābbah* — the crawling, creeping, moving life that fills both realms. The commentators debate the scope of the word *dābbah*: some read it as referring to human beings and angels (Q, Ṭ); others extend it to human beings, angels, jinn, and all the various kinds of animals distributed throughout the earth (IK); and others restrict it to the animals (Ṭs). The widest reading captures the verse's intent: the sign is the sheer abundance of moving life — the billions of creatures, each with its own body, its own provision, its own mode of movement and reproduction, filling the heavens with the angels' flight and the earth with the animals' swarming and the human being's wandering — all scattered by the same Hand that made the heavens and the earth themselves.

**The Argument From the First Creation to the Second**

The verse's second clause delivers its theological point: "and He is able to gather them together whensoever He will." The commentators note that the clause is an argument for the resurrection drawn from the first creation (IK, Ṭ): the God who originated the heavens and the earth and scattered the creatures within them — an act of far greater power than any revival — is able by definition to gather them again on the Day of Resurrection. The Qur'an makes the same argument in its most famous formulation, placed in the mouth of the believer confronting the skeptic who asked how bones could be revived:

> *"And he strikes a likeness for Us and forgets his own creation, saying: Who will give life to these bones when they have rotted away? Say: He will give them life Who brought them forth the first time. And He is Knower of all creation."* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

The logic is unassailable once its premise is granted: if the first creation happened — and every creature's own existence is the proof that it did — then the power that performed it remains, and the same power that scattered the creatures is able to gather them. The verse's word for the gathering — *jamʿ* — is the same word used for the Day of Gathering (*Yawm al-Jamʿ*) that the sūrah announced in verse 7: the chapter that began with the warning of the Gathering returns to it here through the door of creation, so that the whole middle of the sūrah — revelation, provision, rain, signs — has been leading the listener to the same conclusion: the God who speaks, provides, and creates is the God who will gather.

**The Ease of the Gathering for the Originator**

The commentators add a further precision drawn from the Qur'an's own statements about the ease of the resurrection relative to creation:

> *"And He it is Who originates creation, then brings it back again — and that is easier for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27)

The return is easier than the origin — not because the divine power varies, but because the human mind can grasp the return through the origin it has already witnessed: the One who scattered is the One who gathers, and the scattering itself is the standing proof that the gathering is not beyond Him. The verse's closing phrase — "whensoever He will" — fixes the gathering's date in the divine will rather than in any creaturely calculation: the Hour is not bound by the processes the creatures observe, for the One who made the processes is not bound by them; He gathers at the moment of His choosing, and the creatures' only relation to the moment is preparation for it.

**The Modern Reading: Creation Still Speaking**

For the modern reader, the verse's sign-language has only grown more eloquent with the expansion of knowledge. Every advance of astronomy discloses a heaven more immense than the last — and the verse's claim that the heavens are a sign gains rather than loses force as their scale increases, for scale is not meaning: the galaxies point beyond themselves exactly as the single star pointed to the desert's first observer. Every advance of biology discloses the moving creatures more intricate — the coded information in every cell, the migration of birds, the architecture of the ant — and the verse's scattering is visible in the very diversity that science catalogs. The verse invites the believer to hold the two books together: the Qur'an's verses and the cosmos's signs are one revelation from one Author, and the reading of either deepens the reading of the other. And the verse's final clause keeps the horizon open: the same science that traces the scattering can only describe the scattering; the gathering remains the act of the One who scattered — "able to gather them together whensoever He will" — so that the believer's study of creation ends, as the verse ends, in the expectation of the Day.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:30

> **Whatsoever misfortune befalls you is because of that which your hands have earned; and He pardons much.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Causality of Calamity: Deeds and Their Harvest**

The verse states one of the Qur'an's most searching principles: *mā aṣābakum min muṣībah fa-bi-mā kasabat aydīkum* — "whatsoever misfortune befalls you is because of that which your hands have earned." The commentators understand the verse as a general reference to all afflictions that befall human beings (IK, Ṭ, Ṭs) — illness, loss, hardship, the consequences of choices — and they connect it to the Qur'an's parallel statement:

> *"Whatever good befalls thee, it is from God; and whatever evil befalls thee, it is from thyself."* (Qur'an 4:79)

The two verses together establish the Qur'an's grammar of calamity: the good that reaches the human being is traced to divine generosity; the harm that reaches him is traced to his own earnings. The verse is not a blanket condemnation of every sufferer — the commentators are careful to note that the Prophets themselves suffered, and that affliction falls upon the righteous as purification and rank — but it is a blanket summons to self-examination: before asking why the world is unjust, the believer is invited to ask what his own hands have earned. The verse's verb — "your hands have earned" — uses the hand as the emblem of the whole person's agency: the deed done, the word spoken, the choice made; and the earning (*kasb*) is the Qur'an's term for the deed's returning to its doer, as the harvest returns to the sower.

**The Pardoning That Exceeds the Reckoning**

The verse's second clause is its balance and its mercy: "and He pardons much." The commentators note the arithmetic of the clause: if every misfortune were the full repayment of every deed, human life would be unbearable — the misdeeds of a lifetime would crush the sinner at the first slip. But God pardons much: the afflictions that do fall are a fraction of the debts that are owed, and the punishment that is exacted in this world is a fraction of what justice could require — so that every human being who suffers is, in truth, being treated with more mercy than his record deserves. The Qur'an states the same proportion in its account of the Hereafter's reckoning:

> *"And We shall set up the scales of justice on the Day of Resurrection, and no soul shall be wronged in aught."* (Qur'an 21:47)

The verse's pairing thereby produces the believer's double posture toward affliction: acceptance of the justice — the misfortune is not random, and the response to it is examination and repentance rather than rage at fate — and gratitude for the mercy — the misfortune that fell is less than the pardon that was granted, and the affliction of this world may be the very means by which the debt of the next world is reduced. Ibn Kathīr cites the Prophet's description of the expiating power of affliction at this verse:

> *"No believer is afflicted with fatigue, exhaustion, anxiety, or sorrow — even the prick of a thorn — but that God absolves him of some of his sins thereby."* (al-Bukhārī 5641; Muslim 2573, from ʿĀʾishah and Abū Hurayrah)

**The Law of Reflection: How the Verse Works in Practice**

The verse operates as a law of reflection that the commentators invite every reader to test in experience. When misfortune arrives — a business failure, an illness, a broken relationship, a public humiliation — the natural motion of the self is outward: to find the cause in others, in circumstance, in God's injustice. The verse arrests the motion and turns it inward: *what did my hands earn?* The question is not an invitation to self-flagellation but to self-knowledge — the examination that the believer performs in solitude, asking where the choice was unwise, the word was unjust, the trust was broken, the boundary was crossed. The classical scholars understood the verse as also covering the specific afflictions that follow violations of the divine law (Q, Ṭs): the social and personal consequences that sin carries within itself — the Qur'an describes societies that were destroyed by their own deeds, and individuals whose diseases were the fruit of their own excess. The examination is the beginning of the cure, because the affliction that is traced to its cause can be ended at its cause: the repentance that verse 25 opened, applied to the specific deed that the examination reveals.

**The Two Exceptions: When Suffering Is Not Earning**

The verse's general principle is qualified in the Qur'an itself by two considerations that the commentators state to prevent misreading. First, the afflictions of the prophets and the righteous: these are not earnings but honors — the prophets suffered most, and their suffering was not the harvest of sin but the refinement of rank; the believer's affliction may likewise be a raising rather than a reckoning, and only God knows which. Second, the afflictions that fall upon a community for the deeds of some of its members: the Qur'an describes the destruction of peoples for their collective wrongs, and the innocent within them suffer with the guilty in this world while being judged by their own deeds in the next. The verse's principle is thus not a formula for blaming every sufferer but a mirror for every self: examine your own hands before you judge your own fate — and when the examination finds no specific cause, trust the Pardoner whose pardoning exceeds the reckoning, and the Afflicter whose affliction is never without wisdom. The believer who holds the verse's two clauses together — the earnings that explain and the pardon that exceeds — has understood the Qur'an's entire theology of suffering: neither the pagan's blind fate nor the accuser's cruel verdict, but the field of deeds and mercy in which every human life is sown, reaped, and — for those who turn — forgiven much.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:31

> **You thwart not on earth, and you have no protector or helper apart from God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Impossibility of Escape: The Limits of Every Strategy**

The verse states the boundary of all human power in a single clause: "You thwart not on earth" — *wa mā antum bi-muʿjizīna fī al-arḍ*. The verb *aʿjaza* carries the sense of rendering someone incapable, escaping, frustrating, thwarting — and the commentators unfold the clause as a comprehensive statement of human helplessness before the divine will: should God desire to punish a people, there is nothing they can do to thwart it (IJ) — no fortress, no alliance, no distance, no technology. The Qur'an repeats the statement as a refrain wherever it addresses the powerful who imagine themselves beyond reach:

> *"And let not those who disbelieve suppose that they have outstripped [God]. Truly they cannot thwart Him."* (Qur'an 8:59)

> *"They imagine that they have thwarted God? Nay, it is God Who thwarts them."* (Qur'an 8:59 — variant rendering of the same verse in other recitations)

The clause's phrase "on earth" is its own argument: the whole field of human strategy is the earth — its continents, its seas, its skies — and the earth itself is the creature of the One whose judgment the strategists seek to escape; a creature cannot be the refuge from its Creator. The commentators note the implications for the idolaters of Makkah, who trusted in their city's sanctuary, their tribal alliances, their military strength, and their wealth: the sanctuary was God's sanctuary, the alliances were God's creatures, and the strength was God's loan — none of them could stand between a people and the decree of the One who made the earth on which they stood.

**The Absence of Protectors: The End of the Search**

The verse's second clause draws the conclusion from the first: "and you have no protector or helper apart from God." The clause is the sūrah's refrain in its final form — the chapter has repeated since verse 6 that those who take protectors apart from God will find none (vv. 8–9, 44, 46), and the verse now states the positive counterpart: no protector (*walī*) and no helper (*naṣīr*) exists outside God. The commentators note the completeness of the double negation: the *walī* is the friend who takes charge of one's affairs, and the *naṣīr* is the ally who comes to one's aid in distress; the idolaters of Makkah had both in their tribal system — the patron who protected and the confederate who avenged — and the verse declares both categories exhausted: the protection that matters is God's, and the help that matters is God's; every other protector is a creature in the same predicament as the one it protects. The Qur'an's most famous image of the false protector's helplessness belongs to the same argument:

> *"The parable of those who take protectors apart from God is that of the spider that takes to itself a house. And truly the frailest of houses is the house of the spider, if they but knew."* (Qur'an 29:41)

**The Consolation Hidden in the Negation**

The verse's severity toward the self-reliant conceals a profound consolation for the believer. The clause that strips the idolaters of their protectors is the same clause that assures the believers of the only Protector: because there is no protector apart from God, the believer who is stripped of every human support is not stripped of support — the One who remains is sufficient. The Qur'an makes the transfer explicit in the verse's companion:

> *"And if they turn away, know that God is your Protector — the best of protectors and the best of helpers."* (Qur'an 8:40)

The history of the early Muslims in Makkah is the living commentary on the verse: the believers were precisely those who had been stripped — exiled from their tribes, disowned by their families, stripped of the patronage that Arabian society made the condition of survival — and the verse told them that the stripping was the discovery: no protector apart from God, and God sufficient. The same discovery is available to every believer who has ever stood alone — in a family that mocks his faith, in a workplace that punishes his integrity, in a society that isolates his values: the loneliness is the verse's curriculum, and its lesson is that the Protector who is not apart from God is never apart from His servant.

**The Verse as the Cure of Two Diseases**

The verse diagnoses and cures the two diseases of the human relationship to power. The first is the disease of the powerful: the illusion of invulnerability — the ruler, the corporation, the empire that believes it has outstripped accountability and can thwart the divine decree by might or by distance. The verse's "you thwart not on earth" is the standing sentence over every such power: the earth that seems to belong to them belongs to Another, and the decree that seems avoidable is not avoidable. The second is the disease of the powerless: the despair that believes help is impossible when human helpers have failed. The verse's second clause is the standing promise over every such despair: the protector is not found among the creatures who fail but in the Creator who does not fail — and the servant who has no protector apart from God has, in God, a protector whom no power can defeat and no circumstance can exhaust. Between the two diseases the verse clears the narrow path of the believer's life: use the means of the earth without trusting them, face the powers of the earth without fearing them, and carry every need to the Protector who is not apart from God — for there is no other, and none other is needed.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:32

> **Among His signs are the ships upon the sea like mountains;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fleet on the Horizon: A New Sign in the Sūrah's Sequence**

The verse opens a new panel in the sūrah's gallery of signs: after the heavens and the earth and the moving creatures (v. 29), after the rain that revives the dead land (v. 28), the listener is shown the sea — and upon it, the ships "like mountains." The image would have struck the Makkan hearers with particular force: their city was a caravan city, a people of the desert who knew the sea by report and by the commerce that passed along Arabia's coasts; the ship was the largest moving object their imagination contained — and the Qur'an says that these floating mountains are among God's signs, as the heavens are, as the rain is. The commentators note that the verse belongs to a family of passages in which the subjugation of the sea is cited as a sign of divine mercy toward human beings (see 2:164; 14:32–33; 17:66; 23:21–22; 30:46; 31:31; 35:12; 40:80; 43:12; 45:12; 55:24):

> *"And of His signs are the ships that run upon the sea like banners — if He wills, He stills the wind, and they lie motionless upon its surface. Truly in that are signs for every steadfast, thankful one."* (Qur'an 42:32–33 — the verses immediately following)

> *"And unto Him belong the ships with raised sails upon the sea, like mountains."* (Qur'an 55:24)

**The Sign's Double Content: Subjugation and Construction**

The commentators unpack the sign in two directions, and both are required for the sign to be read fully. First, the subjugation: the sea is a mass of water that would drown every ship and every sailor without the divine restraint — the Qur'an says that God "subjugated the sea" (14:32; 45:12) and "subjugated for you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth" (45:13); the ship floats because the water holds it, and the water holds it because its Creator has subjected the sea to the service of His creatures. Second, the construction: the ship is a human artifact — the product of wood and craft, of the knowledge by which human beings read the winds and the stars — and the Qur'an's mention of the ships honors the human making within the divine sign: God created the sea and the wood and the human intelligence, and the ship is the meeting of all three — the creature's craft exercised within the Creator's order. The verse thereby avoids both errors of the religious imagination about technology: it does not deny the human achievement — the ship is real, and its building is a human art — and it does not mistake the achievement for the source — the sea that carries the ship and the craft that builds it are both loans from the One who made the sea and the hand.

**Why Ships Are "Like Mountains"**

The comparison of the ships to mountains is more than visual. In the Qur'an's own usage, the mountains are the earth's stabilizers — the pegs that hold the land (78:6–7) — and the ships are, as it were, the mountains of the sea: stable centers in a moving element, carrying the traveler across the abyss as the mountains carry the inhabitant across the land. The comparison also announces the scale of the sign: the ships that the Makkans knew were small coasters, but the verse speaks of ships like mountains — vessels of a size that suggests the world's great commerce and the power that moves it — and the classical commentators read the comparison as pointing to the grandeur of the divine management: the same Hand that raises the mountains raises the waves that the mountains of the sea must ride. For the believer, the ship upon the sea is the sign of the human condition itself: the soul is always at sea — borne upon an element it does not control, toward destinations it has not chosen, by a power it did not create — and the verse invites the voyager to look at the ship and remember the One who made the sea navigable, the wind drivable, and the voyage possible at all.

**The Sea in the Believer's Spiritual Geography**

The Sufi commentators found in the ship a standing allegory of the spiritual life: the sea is the world — vast, restless, beautiful and deadly; the ship is the ark of salvation — the religion, the community, the revealed guidance that carries the believer across the world's depths; and the voyage's safety depends on the Captain rather than on the sea. The Qur'an itself uses the ship's voyage as the scene of the soul's crisis and its cry, in the passage where the sailors in peril call upon God with exclusive devotion:

> *"He it is Who causes you to journey by land and sea. Till, when you are in the ships, and they run with them upon a favoring wind, and they rejoice therein — there comes upon them a violent wind, and the waves come upon them from every side, and they think that they are encompassed — then they call upon God, making their religion pure for Him: If Thou savest us from this, we shall surely be among the thankful."* (Qur'an 10:22)

The ship is thus the sign of dependence itself: the voyager who has felt the storm knows that his life is not in the hull but in the Hand that stills the sea — and the verse's placement of the ships among the signs invites every reader to recognize the voyage of his own life in the ship upon the water, and the Sign-maker behind the sign.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:33

> **If He so wills, He stills the wind, and they lie motionless upon its surface. Truly in that are signs for all who are patient, thankful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wind Between Two Powers: Motion and Stillness**

The verse continues the sea-scene with a divine conditional: "If He so wills, He stills the wind, and they lie motionless upon its surface." The clause exposes the true agent behind the ship's progress: the ship moves by the wind, and the wind moves by the will of God — so that the same voyage that seemed to depend on the sailors' skill is revealed as depending, at every moment, on the divine permission. The verse's picture is precise: a windless sea is not a stormy sea — the danger the verse describes is not destruction but stillness; the ships lie motionless (*ẓawākhir* — the commentators note that the word describes ships standing still upon the water), the sails hang, the voyage stops, and the traveler discovers that the element that carried him can also hold him. The commentators read the scene as the sea's own lesson in the divine economy of verse 12 and 27: the same God who outspreads and straitens provision stills and releases the wind; the wind that blows is His gift and the wind that ceases is His measure, and the sailor can compel neither.

**The Frailty of Every Voyage**

The classical commentators emphasize what the verse reveals about the frailty of the human condition: the ships "like mountains" of the previous verse are rendered motionless by the absence of an invisible current of air — the mightiest vessel the human being can build is the prisoner of a breeze. Ibn Kathīr draws the connection between the sea-scene and the sūrah's teaching on provision: the manner in which God bestows and withholds provision in accord with what He knows is best for each individual is mirrored in the winds that He sends and stills (IK) — the same hand governs the sailor's voyage and the servant's livelihood, and the same wisdom calibrates both. The traveler who has been becalmed knows the feeling the verse describes: the world reduced to the horizon, time stretched, the destination fixed but unreachable — and the knowledge that the only release is the wind that only God can send.

**The Two Dispositions: Patient and Thankful**

The verse's closing clause names the audience that can read the sign: "Truly in that are signs for all who are patient, thankful" — *li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūr*. The commentators parse the double disposition with care: the patient (*ṣabbār*) is the one who endures the stillness — the becalmed voyage, the delayed provision, the affliction that does not lift; the thankful (*shakūr*) is the one who acknowledges the wind when it comes — the voyage resumed, the provision arrived, the relief that was not earned. The verse thereby divides the sea's travelers into two classes: those who see only the sea — and are either presumptuous in the wind or despairing in the stillness — and those who see the Sign-maker — and are patient in the stillness because they know the Wind's Owner, and thankful in the wind because they know its Source. The same division runs through the whole of human life, of which the voyage is the parable: every life has its favoring winds and its becalmings, and the believer's art is to hold the two dispositions together — the patience that does not despair in the stillness and the gratitude that does not forget in the motion.

**The Stillness as Worship**

The verse contains a further subtlety that the commentators draw out: the stillness is itself a sign — not merely the absence of the sign but the presence of another. The becalmed ship reveals the dependence that the moving ship conceals: in motion, the traveler may imagine that the voyage proceeds by his own skill; in stillness, the skill is exposed as helpless, and the traveler is brought to the knowledge that the voyage was never his. The Sufi commentators found in the stilled wind the image of the soul's own becalming — the periods when the religious life seems to lose its wind, when the ardor cools and the works seem to stand still upon the water — and they read the verse as the promise hidden in the stillness: the Wind's Owner has not abandoned the voyager; the stillness is His will, and His will has a purpose that the stilled sailor cannot yet see. The believer who is patient in the soul's becalming — who continues the prayers that seem to go nowhere, the charity that seems to change nothing, the integrity that seems to cost everything — is the *ṣabbār* of the verse; and the wind that returns after the stillness is received by him as what it always was: the mercy of the One who stills and sends, whose stilling is a sign and whose sending is a sign, and whose signs are read by the patient and the thankful alone.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:34

> **Or He causes them to perish for that which they have earned — and He pardons much —**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Conditional: The Sea as Judgment**

The verse completes the sea-scene's second possibility: "Or He causes them to perish for that which they have earned — and He pardons much." Where the previous verse described the stillness of the wind, this verse describes its opposite excess — the storm that overwhelms, the ship that founders, the voyage that ends in the deep. The commentators note the structure of the two conditionals: between them they cover the whole range of the sea's danger — the wind can be withheld until the voyage stops, or the wind can be unleashed until the ship perishes — and both extremes are traced to the same will that sends the favoring breeze. The phrase "for that which they have earned" connects the sea's disasters to the sūrah's principle of verse 30: the misfortune that befalls is the harvest of the hands' earnings — the verse's grammar deliberately echoes the earlier principle, so that the sinking ship is not presented as the blind cruelty of nature but as the measured consequence of deeds, in the same economy in which the becalming and the drowning both fall. The commentators apply the verse to the general rule of the divine dealings: those who perish on the sea — like those who perish by drought, plague, or war — perish within the order of a justice that never exceeds the earning.

**The Pardon That Rides With the Storm**

The verse's parenthesis — "and He pardons much" — is its theological center, and the commentators pause over its placement. The clause intervenes between the perishing and its cause, as though the pardon itself were interposed between the deed and its consequence: even when the storm is sent, the full weight of what the voyagers earned is not sent with it — the perishing that occurs is less than the perishing that the sum of human earnings would deserve, and the multitudes who sail and arrive, who are spared and who reach harbor, are living under a pardon that exceeds the reckoning. The same parenthesis occurred in verse 30, and its repetition here binds the two verses together: the believer's afflictions and the world's disasters both fall within the same field — the earnings that explain and the pardon that exceeds. The commentators cite the verse's companion as the summary of the divine proportion:

> *"God does not wrong human beings in the least; rather, human beings wrong themselves."* (Qur'an 10:44; see also 27:90; 36:54; 37:39; 52:16; 66:7)

**The Mercy in the Warning**

The verse's function within the sūrah's argument is both warning and mercy. As warning: the sea that carries the ships is not a machine without a Master — the same will that stills the wind can send the storm, and the voyager who sails without remembrance sails within the reach of the accounting; the sailors of the Qur'an's own stories — Jonah in the belly of the whale, the people of the ship in the parable of 18:71–79 — are the sea's witnesses to the same truth. As mercy: the verse's warning is delivered *before* the voyage, so that the warning itself is the pardon — the sailor who is told of the storm can prepare, repent, and sail under the protection of the Pardoner; the perishing described in the verse is the perishing of those who were warned and sailed on. The verse thereby performs the same double office as the whole sūrah: it does not hide the severity of the divine dealings, and it does not hide the pardon that exceeds them — the believer is asked to hold both in view, as the sailor holds the weather and the harbor together.

**The Voyage of Every Life: Storm and Harbor**

Read as the sūrah's parable of the human voyage, the verse completes the believer's sea-chart. The voyage has three possibilities: the favoring wind (v. 32), in which the traveler must be thankful; the stilled wind (v. 33), in which the traveler must be patient; and the overwhelming wind (v. 34), in which the traveler's only recourse is the pardon that exceeds the earning — the repentance that verse 25 opened, the mercy that verse 28 spread, the forgiveness that the sūrah has woven through every warning. The believer who reads the three conditions as the map of his own life finds that the map is complete: there is no fourth condition, and in every condition the same Hand holds the wind — the Hand that the believer addresses in the storm with the prayer the Qur'an teaches the voyager:

> *"And when the waves cover them like canopies, they call upon God, making their religion pure for Him. But when He delivers them to land, some of them are moderate — and none denies Our signs save every treacherous ingrate."* (Qur'an 31:32)

The verse's final parenthesis — "and He pardons much" — is the believer's anchor in every storm: the perishing that justice could send is held back by the pardon, the voyager who reaches the harbor arrives as the recipient of a mercy he did not earn, and the voyage that ends in the deep ends in the presence of the Pardoner whose pardoning is measured by no sea. Between the storm and the harbor, the believer's whole art is the art of the verse's two words: the earning that he examines and repents, and the pardon that he trusts and praises.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:35

> **and so that those who dispute concerning Our signs will know that they have no refuge.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Behind the Sea-Signs**

The verse completes the sea-scene with its purpose: *wa li-yaʿlama alladhīna yujādilūna fī āyātinā mā lahum min maḥīṣ* — "and so that those who dispute concerning Our signs will know that they have no refuge." The commentators read the clause as the divine rationale for the whole display of power in the sea — the ships, the winds, the stilling, the perishing: these are not random phenomena but a lesson directed at a specific audience, the disputers who argue against the divine signs. The word *maḥīṣ* — refuge, escape, place of flight — is the verse's key: the disputers of the signs imagine that they have refuges — the sanctuary of their city, the strength of their tribes, the depths of their sea-lanes, the resources of their trade — and the sea-scene is arranged to expose the imagination. When the wind is stilled, no oar can move the ship; when the storm is sent, no harbor is near; when the deep opens, no alliance reaches — and the disputer who has felt the sea's power has felt, in his own body, the truth of the verse: there is no refuge from the One whose signs the sea displays.

**The Two Moments of Knowing**

The commentators specify the moment of the disputers' knowledge in two ways (IJ, Ṭ). The first moment is in this world, at the crisis itself: when the water overcomes them and they drown, the disputers know — in the last clarity of the drowning — that they have no refuge; the idols are on the shore, the protectors are on the land, and the deep receives them alone with their deeds. The Qur'an describes the moment of such knowledge in its account of Pharaoh at the crossing:

> *"And We brought the Children of Israel across the sea, and Pharaoh and his hosts pursued them in tyranny and enmity — till, when the drowning overtook him, he said: I believe that there is no god but He in Whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am among those who submit."* (Qur'an 10:90)

The knowledge of the drowning is real — and useless, because it arrives at the moment when the earth has already ended for its knower; the verse's warning is that the disputers may purchase this knowledge at the only price at which it is ever sold to the obstinate: the price of the crisis itself. The second moment is after the Resurrection: when the dead are raised and the judgment begins, the disputers know that there is no escape from the punishment (Aj, IJ, Ṭ) — the knowledge that the Qur'an describes as the regret of the Fire's inhabitants, who say:

> *"Our Lord, we have seen and heard; so send us back and we shall work righteousness. Truly we are certain."* (Qur'an 32:12)

**Disputation and Its Objects: The Sūrah's Own Disputers**

The verse's phrase "those who dispute concerning Our signs" gathers the sūrah's various disputers into one class: those who argued about God after responding (v. 16), those who disputed the Hour (v. 18), those who disputed the revelation's authenticity (v. 24), those who will dispute the signs when the sea shows its power. The commentators note that the disputation is not inquiry but *jidal* — the contentious wrangling that the sūrah has already condemned — and the verse's diagnosis is that such disputation is, at bottom, a search for refuge: the disputer argues against the signs because he does not want the signs to be true; the signs' truth would end his arrangements. The sea-scene answers the search: the refuge the disputer seeks — a world without accountability, a sea without a Master, a life without a reckoning — does not exist; the wind obeys Another, the deep obeys Another, and the disputer's argument dissolves in the only element that cannot be argued with.

**The Refuge That Remains**

The verse's negation — "they have no refuge" — is total, and its totality is the believer's gain. If the disputers have no refuge, then the only refuge is the One from whom the disputers flee — and the verse thereby completes the sūrah's repeated grammar of protection: no protector apart from God (vv. 6, 9, 31, 44, 46), no refuge from God's signs (v. 35), and therefore the only refuge is God Himself. The believer who recites the verse in the midst of a world that disputes the signs — the modern disputers who argue against creation's Designer, against the soul's accountability, against the Gathering's certainty — is taught two things at once: do not envy the disputers their apparent security, for their refuges are the spider's house; and do not fear their disputation, for the signs they dispute are the signs of the One who holds the wind and the deep, and the refuge they deny is the refuge the believer has already taken. The verse's final word — *maḥīṣ* — is the last word on every false security: the sea has no ledge for the fleeing, the earth has no corner for the escaping, and the only flight that succeeds is the flight to the One from whom the disputers flee — the flight that the believer makes every day, in every prayer, toward the Refuge that no storm can reach and no disputer can close.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:36

> **Whatsoever you have been given, it is the enjoyment of the life of this world, and that which lies with God is better and more lasting for those who believe and trust in their Lord,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict on the World's Goods**

The verse opens the sūrah's great portrait of the believers with a verdict on the whole order of worldly goods: *fa-mā ūtītum min shayʾ fa-matāʿu al-ḥayāti al-dunyā* — "whatsoever you have been given, it is the enjoyment of the life of this world." The word *matāʿ* — enjoyment, provision, the goods of passage — is chosen with care: it denotes what a traveler carries for the journey, what is used and then left; and the verse applies it to everything human beings are given — wealth, health, status, family, pleasure, power. The commentators note the comprehensiveness of the opening: "whatsoever" — no exception is carved out, no good is exempted from the verdict, because the verdict is not that the goods are evil but that they are temporary; the *matāʿ* is the traveler's provision, and the traveler's provision is not the traveler's home. The same assessment recurs throughout the Qur'an (see 3:14, 185; 4:77; 9:38; 13:26; 20:131; 28:60; 40:39; 43:35; 57:20):

> *"Know that the life of this world is but play and diversion, and adornment, and mutual boasting among you, and rivalry in wealth and children — like the likeness of rain whose vegetation pleases the tillers, then withers, and you see it yellowing, then it becomes chaff. And in the Hereafter is a severe punishment, and forgiveness from God and satisfaction. And the life of this world is naught but the enjoyment of delusion."* (Qur'an 57:20)

**"Better and More Lasting": The Comparison That Decides**

The verse's second clause states the comparison that settles every choice: "and that which lies with God is better and more lasting." The commentators parse the double superiority with care. *Better* (*khayr*): what lies with God is superior in kind — the Garden's joys are not larger quantities of worldly joys but goods of another order, and the comparison between them is like the comparison between the child's toy and the grown man's inheritance. *More lasting* (*abqā*): what lies with God endures — the word's root carries the sense of remaining, abiding — while the worldly goods are the traveler's provision, consumed by the journey that ends at death. The verse thereby reduces every human choice to its essential form: the choice between the better-and-lasting and the inferior-and-perishing; and the sūrah's preceding economics — the measured provision (v. 27), the two harvests (v. 20), the keys in the divine hand (v. 12) — have prepared the listener to recognize the choice when it is stated.

**The Audience of the Promise: Believing and Trusting**

The verse then specifies who receives the better-and-lasting: "for those who believe and trust in their Lord." The two conditions are distinct and complementary: *belief* (*īmān*) is the acceptance of the unseen reality — God, the messengers, the Day, the Garden; *trust* (*tawakkul*) is the resting of the heart upon that acceptance — the confidence that the unseen reality is more real than the seen goods, that the Provider will provide, that the promise will be kept. The commentators note the psychological precision of the pairing: belief without trust is a creed that fails in the marketplace of choices — the believer who believes in the Hereafter but does not trust God with his provision will still clutch at the world's goods as though they were the only goods; trust without belief is an emotion without an object. The believers of the verse are those in whom the creed has become confidence — who can give because they trust the Provider, who can wait because they trust the Promise, who can lose the world's goods because they do not believe the world's goods are the real goods. The Qur'an describes the same trust as the believers' distinctive mark:

> *"Those unto whom the people said: Truly the people have gathered against you, so fear them. But it increased them in faith, and they said: God suffices us, and He is the best of guardians."* (Qur'an 3:173)

**The Practical Test of the Two Goods**

The verse's application to daily life is the sūrah's own: the believer is not asked to abandon the world's goods — the ships, the trade, the provision are all described in this very chapter as divine gifts — but to hold them as what they are: enjoyment for the journey, not the journey's end. The practical test is the test of grief: when a worldly good is lost, the believer's grief is measured by his creed — the one who believed the good was his home grieves as though he had lost his home; the one who believed it was his provision grieves as a traveler grieves for a lost provision — with regret for the journey's comfort, not despair for the journey's end. And the practical test is equally the test of pursuit: the believer who finds himself spending his life — his hours, his health, his anxieties — entirely on the provision while the journey itself goes unconsidered has made the traveler's error; he has polished the luggage and missed the destination. The verse's promise — "that which lies with God is better and more lasting" — is the believer's standing correction of every such error: the goods that lie with God cannot be lost, stolen, inflated away, or left behind at death; they are the only investment whose market never crashes and whose owner never dies. And the verse's condition — belief and trust — is the believer's standing access to them: the promise is not to the world's cleverest accumulators but to those who believe in the unseen Giver and trust the unseen store.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:37

> **who shun grave sins and indecencies and who when they are angry, they forgive,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Portrait Begins: The Believers Defined by Refusal**

The verse opens the sūrah's famous description of the believers' character — a series of defining traits that will run through verse 43 — and it begins with a refusal: *alladhīna yajtanibūna kabāʾira al-ithmi wa al-fawāḥish* — "who shun grave sins and indecencies." The commentators note that the portrait begins with what the believers avoid rather than what they perform, because character in the Qur'anic diagnosis is formed as much by refusal as by action: the believer is known by the thresholds he will not cross. The phrase distinguishes two classes of wrong: the *kabāʾir* — grave sins, the major transgressions — and the *fawāḥish* — indecencies, the open lewdness and flagrant violations. The exegetes note that there is no single agreed definition of the grave sins (see 4:31c), but the Qur'an's own lists supply the content: idolatry, murder, the consumption of usury, the slander of chaste women, disobedience to parents, false testimony — the sins whose harm is great and whose repetition hardens the soul:

> *"If you shun the grave sins that you have been forbidden, We shall absolve you of your evil deeds and admit you by a gate of honor."* (Qur'an 4:31)

> *"Those who avoid the grave sins and indecencies, save minor faults — truly thy Lord is of vast forgiveness."* (Qur'an 53:32)

The verse's realism is noteworthy: the believers are not described as sinless — the Qur'an knows that the minor faults (*lamam*) will occur — but as *shunning* the grave: the mark of faith is not the absence of all error but the refusal of the great errors, and the great errors are defined by their power to destroy the soul's direction.

**The Fāḥishah: Indecency's Special Severity**

The commentators give the *fawāḥish* particular attention, since the word's singular (*fāḥishah*) is used in the Qur'an for the most destructive of the open sins — above all sexual transgression (IJ, Ṭ; see 4:15c; 4:22c), but more broadly every transgression of the limits set by God that is committed openly, shamelessly, and without concealment (IJ). The distinctive feature of the *fāḥishah* is its publicity: the sin done in the open, the lewdness that parades, the violation that is not only committed but displayed — and the Qur'an treats the publicity as an aggravation, for the open sin corrupts beyond its doer, normalizing what should horrify and teaching the young by example. This is why the Qur'an commands the believers to avoid indecency "both outward and inward" (6:151–52) and declares:

> *"And approach not indecencies, whether outward or inward."* (Qur'an 6:151)

The believers' shunning of the *fawāḥish* is thus a social as well as a personal act: by refusing the open indecency, they refuse to participate in its normalization, and their refusal is itself a witness to the society around them.

**Anger and Forgiveness: The Believer's Thermometer**

The verse's second trait is its most psychologically searching: "and who when they are angry, they forgive" — *wa idhā mā ghaḍibū hum yaghfirūn*. The commentators note the precision of the phrasing: the forgiveness is not the forgiveness of the un-angered — the indifference that forgives because nothing was felt — but the forgiveness of the angry: the pardon that is granted *at the moment of* anger, when the wound is fresh and the power to retaliate is present. The verse's companion in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān supplies the fuller portrait:

> *"Who spend in ease and adversity, who curb their rage, and who pardon people — and God loves the virtuous."* (Qur'an 3:134)

The two verses together describe the believer's anger as a fire that is mastered rather than extinguished or indulged: the believer feels the anger — the sense of wrong is not dead in him — but he curbs it, and from the curbed rage rises the forgiveness that is the trait's crown. The Prophet ﷺ defined strength by exactly this mastery:

> *"The strong man is not the one who throws people down in wrestling; the strong man is the one who contains himself when he is angry."* (al-Bukhārī 6114; Muslim 2609, from Abū Hurayrah)

**The Fire and Its Extinguishing**

The classical commentators — al-Qurṭubī chief among them — collected at this verse the prophetic counsel on anger's management, and the tradition's psychology is remarkably practical. The Prophet ﷺ taught that anger is not a neutral emotion but an invasion: "Anger comes from Satan; Satan was created of fire, and fire is extinguished only with water; so when one of you becomes angry, let him perform ablution" (recorded by Abū Dāwūd 4784, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar? — the transmission is from Abū Dharr in the widely transmitted form; the instruction to perform ablution at anger is preserved in the collections). He also taught the angry the physical disciplines of anger: "If one of you becomes angry while standing, let him sit; if the anger does not leave him, let him lie down" (Abū Dāwūd 4782, from Abū Dharr) — the tradition's insight being that anger feeds on the body's tension, and the body's postures can starve the fire. The commentators add the tradition of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, who described the anger that is swallowed rather than discharged:

> "Anger is a ball of fire; but when you swallow it, it is sweeter than honey." (transmitted by al-Qurṭubī in his commentary)

The image is exact: the swallowed anger is bitter at the moment of swallowing — the pride wounded, the retort withheld — and sweet in its aftermath, for the one who has mastered his anger has won the only victory that lasts; while the discharged anger is sweet at the moment of discharge — the retort delivered, the revenge taken — and bitter in its aftermath, leaving the ruptured relationship, the regretted word, the shame of the loss of self.

**Forgiveness as the Believer's Power, Not Weakness**

The verse's final word — *yaghfirūn*, they forgive — carries the Qur'an's full theology of pardon. The forgiveness of the angry believer is not the weakness of the one who cannot retaliate but the strength of the one who chooses not to — the verse's companions in this very sūrah make the choice explicit: the believers defend themselves when tyranny befalls them (v. 39) and may take requital equal to the wrong (v. 40) — and *then* the sūrah says that patience and forgiveness are the course worthy of resolve (v. 43). The believer's forgiveness is thus a choice among real options, not a necessity imposed by helplessness: the one who forgives when he could require has performed the higher act, and the Qur'an ranks the forgiver above the requiter even while permitting the requital. The psychology of the choice is the psychology of the heart's freedom: the angry man who forgives has refused to let his enemy dictate his state — his anger is not the enemy's puppet but his own raw material, and the forgiveness that emerges from it is the self's greatest victory over itself. The modern science of forgiveness has arrived, by its own roads, at the same finding the verse announced fourteen centuries ago: the unforgiving carry their injuries twice — once in the wound and once in the re-wounding of memory — while the forgiver deposits the injury with God and walks free. The believer who shuns the grave sins, refuses the indecencies, and forgives from the midst of his anger has understood the sūrah's portrait of the soul that is fit for the meadows of verse 22: a soul that knows what to refuse, what to curb, and what to release — and has mastered the only enemy it will carry into the grave: itself.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:38

> **who respond to their Lord and perform the prayer, their affair being counsel among them, who spend from that which We have provided them,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Named the Sūrah**

This is the verse from which the entire chapter takes its name: *wa amruhum shūrā baynahum* — "their affair being counsel among them." The sūrah's name is thus not taken from its opening letters, its warnings, or its revelation-theology, but from a single clause describing how the believers govern their collective life — and the choice of the name is the Qur'an's own commentary on the clause's importance: the community's method of decision-making is named as a defining mark of its faith. The verse stands in the center of the believers' portrait, and the commentators treat its four clauses as the pillars of the community's life: response to the Lord, the prayer, mutual counsel, and spending. The verse's placement is also significant: it follows the traits of avoidance and forgiveness (v. 37) and precedes the permission of self-defense (v. 39), so that counsel is set between the soul's internal discipline and the community's external struggles — the decision-making of the believers governs a people who have already mastered themselves.

**"Who Respond to Their Lord": The Root of the Community's Life**

The portrait's first clause is the root from which the other traits grow: *alladhīna istajābū li-rabbihim* — "who respond to their Lord." The commentators unfold the response in several registers: it is the response of the covenant — testifying to God's Oneness (Ṭ); it is the response of obedience — fulfilling the dictates of religion (Ṭs); it is the response of the prayer — establishing the prayer in the manner prescribed at its designated times (Ṭ, Ṭs); and, in al-Rāzī's spiritual reading, it is the response of the whole heart — those in whose hearts there is no dispute with any of God's commands (R). The phrase deliberately echoes verse 26, where God responds to those who believe and perform righteous deeds: the relationship is a dialogue — the believers respond to their Lord, and their Lord responds to them — and the community portrait begins at the point of the response because every other virtue of the believers flows from the initial turning. The commentators also note the contrast the clause creates with verse 35: the disputers of the signs refuse the response, while the believers are defined by it — the same divine address divides humanity into those who answer and those who argue.

**The Prayer: The Vertical Anchor**

The second clause — "and perform the prayer" (*yuqīmūna al-ṣalāh*) — anchors the community in the vertical dimension. The commentators stress the verb's form: the believers do not merely *pray* but *establish* the prayer — *iqāmah* carries the sense of setting the prayer upright, making it stand, giving it its due of time, condition, and presence. The prayer is the community's appointment with its Lord — five times a day, the response of verse 38's first clause is renewed in the body — and the portrait places it between the response and the counsel because the prayer is the school of both: it trains the heart in submission (which counsel requires) and it trains the will in regularity (which community requires). The Prophet ﷺ described the prayer as the differentiator of the community and the first of the servant's deeds to be reckoned:

> *"The first matter concerning which the servant will be reckoned on the Day of Resurrection is the prayer. If it is sound, the rest of his deeds will be sound; and if it is corrupt, the rest of his deeds will be corrupt."* (al-Tirmidhī 413, hasan ṣaḥīḥ, from Abū Hurayrah)

**Counsel: The Horizontal Bond**

The third clause is the verse's center and the sūrah's name: "their affair being counsel among them." The commentators read the clause at several levels, and all of them are required for the full meaning. Historically, the clause is understood as an allusion to the Helpers (*al-Anṣār*) of Madinah, who responded with faith to the Prophet's call and sought counsel with one another before embracing Islam (Q, Ṭs) — the first community of the believers in Madinah modeled the very trait the Makkan sūrah had announced as the believers' mark years before the migration. Generally, the clause establishes the principle that the believers' collective affairs are decided by consultation rather than by the will of one, the force of the strongest, or the inheritance of the few:

> "If people seek counsel with one another, they will be guided to sound judgment." (recorded by al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī in their commentaries)

And in its deepest reading, the clause refers all matters back to the religion itself: those whose affair is counsel among them are those who, when tidings arrive, do not spread them in panic but refer them to the Messenger and those in authority — the verse's companion in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the principle:

> *"And whenever tidings come unto them, whether of security or of fear, they spread it about; whereas had they referred it to the Messenger and to those in authority among them, those of them whose task it is to investigate would have known it."* (Qur'an 4:83)

The Qur'an's own constitution of the community's decision-making is completed by the command to the Prophet to consult his Companions:

> *"And consult them in the affair. And when you have resolved, trust in God."* (Qur'an 3:159)

The classical commentators draw out the scope of the counsel from this verse: the Prophet was instructed to consult his Companions in governance and military questions, but not in questions pertaining to divine decrees, since he had direct guidance from God for those (Q); after the Prophet's death, the Companions sought counsel from one another in understanding the divine decrees and in extracting their applications from the Qur'an and the Sunna (Q) — the principle of counsel thus became the engine of the community's jurisprudence and governance, from the election of the first caliphs to the collective reasoning (*ijtihād*) of the scholars of every age.

**The Prophetic Praise of the Consultative Community**

The commentators transmit at this verse the Prophet's own description of the society that counsel builds:

> *"If your leaders are the best of you, your wealthy are the magnanimous among you, and your affair is counsel among you, then the surface of the earth is better for you than its interior; but if your leaders are the worst of you, your wealthy are the miserly among you, and your affairs are entrusted to your women, then the interior of the earth is better for you than its surface."* (recorded by al-Tirmidhī 2266, from Abū Hurayrah, graded ḥasan gharīb)

The tradition's logic is the logic of the verse: counsel is the community's immune system — it pools the wisdom of the many, prevents the tyranny of the one, and binds the decision-makers to those who will bear the decisions. The absence of counsel is the disease of the closed elite, and its symptoms — the concentration of leadership in the worst, the concentration of wealth in the miserly, the trivialization of the public affair — are the signs the tradition warns the community to read in its own condition.

**Spending: The Economic Completion**

The portrait's fourth clause returns the community to the economic order of the earlier verses: "who spend from that which We have provided them" — *wa mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn*. The clause completes the circle: the believers responded to their Lord (the vertical), established the prayer (the ritual), counseled among themselves (the collective decision), and now spend from the provision — the wealth that verses 27 and 36 established as a measured trust rather than a private possession is returned to the community's circulation through spending: the zakāh that purifies, the charity that connects, the expenditure on family and guest and need that the Qur'an praises throughout (see 2:3; 8:3; 13:22; 28:54; 32:16). The clause's phrasing — "from that which We have provided them" — keeps the economic theology intact: the believers spend from what *God* provided, so their spending is the acknowledgment of the trust, and the hand that gives is the hand that administers a loan from the Provider whose keys open every provision (v. 12). The community that responds, prays, counsels, and spends is the complete society of the verse — a people whose life is vertical and horizontal, deliberative and generous, anchored in the Lord and open to the neighbor. It is this verse's portrait — not the portrait of the lone ascetic or the isolated worshipper — that the sūrah holds up as the shape of the life that leads to the flowering meadows of verse 22, and it is the trait of counsel among them that gives the whole chapter its name: the community of the believers is, from its foundation, a community that decides together because it believes together.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:39

> **and who, when tyranny befalls them, they defend themselves.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Believers' Stance Toward Tyranny**

The verse completes the portrait's movement from the internal to the external: after shunning sins, forgiving anger, responding to the Lord, praying, counseling, and spending, the believers are described in their confrontation with oppression: *wa alladhīna idhā aṣābahumu al-baghya hum yantaṣirūn* — "and who, when tyranny befalls them, they defend themselves." The word *baghy* — tyranny, transgression, oppressive aggression — denotes the overstepping of bounds by a power that has the means to overstep: the persecution of the weak by the strong, the seizure of rights by the mighty. The commentators note the verse's context in the believers' own history: the tyranny referred to includes the idolaters' expulsion of the Muslims from Makkah, after which the believers were granted permission to defend themselves (Q) — and the Qur'an records the grant in the passage that marks the turning point of the Muslim community's history:

> *"Permission is granted to those who are fought, because they have been wronged — and truly God is able to help them — those who were expelled from their homes without right, only for saying: Our Lord is God."* (Qur'an 22:39–40)

**The Balance of the Believers' Character**

The verse's placement between forgiveness (v. 37) and requital (v. 40) is the Qur'an's own definition of the balanced character: the believers forgive personal offenses (v. 37) but do not submit to systematic tyranny (v. 39); their forgiveness is a strength, and their defense is not a contradiction of it but its complement. The commentators draw out the distinction with care: the forgiveness of verse 37 belongs to the sphere of personal wrongs — the insult borne, the injury pardoned; the defense of verse 39 belongs to the sphere of collective oppression — the community attacked for its faith, the believers persecuted for their confession. The one who forgives the personal wrong while resisting the tyrannical order has understood the Qur'an's full teaching: forgiveness is not the surrender of justice, and justice is not the abolition of mercy — the believer's heart is wide enough for both, because God's own dealings combine pardon (vv. 25, 30) with requital (vv. 16, 21).

**The Duty of Defense: Enjoining Right and Forbidding Wrong**

The classical commentators — al-Qurṭubī in particular — read the verse as establishing a general principle: no one should submit to the tyranny of an oppressor (Q), and the verse thereby reaffirms the Qur'anic injunction to enjoin right and forbid wrong, the collective duty that the community bears as its defining charge:

> *"You are the best community brought forth for mankind, enjoining right and forbidding wrong, and believing in God."* (Qur'an 3:110)

The Prophet ﷺ defined the duty's graded obligations in the famous hadith:

> *"Whoever among you sees an evil, let him change it with his hand; if he cannot, then with his tongue; if he cannot, then with his heart — and that is the weakest of faith."* (Muslim 49, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

The verse's defense is thus not the aggression of the powerful but the resistance of the wronged: the believers defend themselves when tyranny befalls them, and their defense is bounded by the justice that the following verses will define — the requital equal to the wrong (v. 40), never exceeding it (v. 42).

**The Psychology of Resistance: Tyranny and Its Fuel**

The verse's insight into the psychology of tyranny is worth drawing out, for it explains why the believers' stance is both necessary and non-negotiable. Tyranny feeds on submission: the *baghy* of the oppressor expands precisely to the degree that it is not resisted — each successful act of oppression teaches the oppressor that oppression works, and each submission of the oppressed teaches the oppressor that the next act will be easier. The believers' defense interrupts the fuel supply: the tyranny that meets resistance is forced to calculate, and the tyrant who must calculate has already lost his absolute confidence. The verse's placement in the sūrah also explains the timing of the believers' resistance: the portrait describes people who first mastered themselves — who shunned sins, curbed anger, prayed, and counseled — and only then took up the defense against tyranny; the community that resists without self-mastery becomes the mirror of its oppressor, while the community that has mastered itself can resist without becoming what it resists. The early Muslims' history verified the verse's sequence: the patience of the Makkan years — the thirteen years of prayer and counsel and spending under persecution — was the school in which the community was formed that could later defend itself without becoming tyrannical, and could conquer without enslaving, precisely because the conquest was the act of a people who had already conquered themselves.

**Modern Application: The Believer Between Passivity and Aggression**

The verse supplies the believer's position between the two errors that have corrupted religious communities in every age. The first error is the error of passivity — the pietism that reads the command to forgive (v. 37) as a command to submit to every tyranny, that confuses patience with complicity, and that allows the oppressor to name his victims "patient." The verse answers this error with its own clause: the believers defend themselves when tyranny befalls them — the defense is a defining trait, not an optional extra. The second error is the error of aggression — the militancy that reads the permission to defend (v. 39) as a license to initiate, that confuses the defense of the wronged with the aggression of the powerful, and that makes the believers the mirror of their oppressors. The verse answers this error with its boundaries: the defense is a response — "when tyranny befalls them" — and the following verses will bind it to the measure of the wrong received (v. 40) and the prohibition of transgression (v. 42). Between the two errors stands the community of the verse: strong enough to resist, disciplined enough to resist justly, and free enough — because it has forgiven in its own heart — to resist the tyranny without becoming the tyrant.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:40

> **The recompense of an evil is an evil like unto it. Yet whosoever pardons and sets matters aright, his reward is with God. Truly He loves not the wrongdoers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Law of Equivalence: Justice Without Excess**

The verse states the boundary of just requital in its most economical form: *wa jazāʾu sayyiʾatin sayyiʾatun mithluhā* — "the recompense of an evil is an evil like unto it." The commentators note the verse's place in the Qur'an's law of retaliation, which it states elsewhere with the same insistence on equivalence:

> *"And if you punish, then punish with the like of that wherewith you were afflicted."* (Qur'an 16:126)

> *"And We prescribed for them therein: a life for a life, an eye for an eye, a nose for a nose, an ear for an ear, a tooth for a tooth, and for wounds retaliation."* (Qur'an 5:45)

The verse's logic is the logic of the Balance that the sūrah announced in verse 17: justice requires that the requital not exceed the wrong — the response may equal the offense but may not surpass it — and the commentators stress the boundary in both directions: the requiter may take the equivalent of what was taken but may not add interest to the injury, may not punish the innocent for the guilty, may not continue the feud beyond its cause. The "evil" that is requited is defined in the commentators' discussions as the concrete harm — physical injury, insult, curse, damage (Bg) — and the requital is its like: the measured response that the law permits and the conscience approves. The verse thereby supplies the legal spine of the community's self-defense of verse 39: the defense is bounded by equivalence, and the defender who exceeds the equivalence has crossed from justice into the very *baghy* he resisted.

**The Asymmetry of the Divine Ledger**

The verse's deeper point, which the commentators draw from its companion passages, is the asymmetry between the divine treatment of evil and of good: the requital of evil is capped at equivalence, while the reward of good is multiplied without measure:

> *"Whosoever brings a good deed shall have ten times its like; and whosoever brings an evil deed shall be requited only with its like — and they shall not be wronged."* (Qur'an 6:160)

> *"Whosoever brings a good deed shall have better than it; and whosoever brings an evil deed shall be requited only for what they did."* (Qur'an 28:84; see also 40:40)

The economy of the Hereafter thus runs on two different currencies: the evil deed is paid at par — its like, no more — while the good deed is paid at a premium that only divine generosity sets. The verse's equivalence is therefore not a symmetry between good and evil but a ceiling on retaliation: the wronged may collect what is owed, and no more; the wrongdoer, if he turns, may receive what is not owed at all — the pardon of verse 25.

**The Higher Path: Pardon and Setting Aright**

The verse's second clause opens the higher path: "Yet whosoever pardons and sets matters aright, his reward is with God." The commentators note the two conditions joined in the clause: the pardon (*ʿafā*) and the setting aright (*aṣlaḥa*) — forgiveness that merely releases the debtor without repairing the breach is incomplete, and the verse requires that the pardon be accompanied by the work of reconciliation — the restoration of the relationship, the repair of the harm that can be repaired, the peace that follows the forgiveness. The verse's companion in Sūrah al-Nūr supplies the motive that makes the higher path possible:

> *"And let them pardon and forbear. Do you not love that God should forgive you?"* (Qur'an 24:22)

The logic is the logic of the mirror: the pardon the believer grants is the pattern of the pardon he seeks — the one who forgives his brother's debt to him is asking, by that very act, for the remission of his own infinite debt to God; and the one who withholds pardon is, in effect, petitioning for the strict accounting that he could not survive. The verse's promise — "his reward is with God" — places the pardon's compensation beyond the reach of the human debtor: the one who forgives what is owed him by a creature has transferred the debt to the only Debtor who can pay — God, whose reward for the pardon is stored where neither the ingratitude of the forgiven nor the passage of time can touch it.

**"He Loves Not the Wrongdoers": The Final Boundary**

The verse closes with the clause that prevents its mercy from becoming license: "Truly He loves not the wrongdoers." The commentators read the clause as the boundary of the whole passage: the pardon is praiseworthy, and the equivalence is lawful — but the *wrongdoing* itself, whether the original aggression or the retaliatory excess, remains the object of the divine displeasure. God loves not the wrongdoers — neither the tyrant who began the *baghy* (v. 39) nor the avenger who exceeded the equivalence, neither the oppressor nor the oppressor's mirror. The clause thereby completes the verse's three-tiered teaching: the wronged may require the like (justice), may pardon and repair (excellence), and may do neither that God loves not — for the requital that exceeds and the pardon that abandons justice are both outside the divine love. The verse leaves the choice between the lawful and the excellent to the believer's own measure, and the following verses will complete the scale by removing blame from the just defender (v. 41), fixing it on the transgressor (v. 42), and praising the patient pardoner as the possessor of the course worthy of resolve (v. 43). Between the requital that is lawful and the pardon that is better stands the believer's freedom — and above both stands the love of God, which the wronged seek by their justice and the pardoners by their pardon, and which the wrongdoers alone, in their excess and their aggression, have placed beyond their reach.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:41

> **And whosoever defends himself after having been wronged, for such there is no way against them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Acquittal of the Defender**

The verse states the legal consequence of lawful self-defense: *wa la-man intaṣara baʿda ẓulmihi fa-ulāʾika mā ʿalayhim min sabīl* — "and whosoever defends himself after having been wronged, for such there is no way against them." The commentators explain that "no way against them" means no blame upon them, no legal recourse against them, no ground of censure (IK): the defender who takes the equivalent of the wrong after being wronged stands acquitted before God and before the community — his defense is not a fresh offense but the completion of the justice that the wrong interrupted. The verse is the positive counterpart of verse 40's permission: where the earlier verse stated the law of equivalence, this verse states the innocence of the one who applies it. The structure of the sentence is notable: the defender is described as acting "after having been wronged" — the wrong precedes the defense, the defense is a response, and the response, being measured, carries no liability.

**The Distinction Between Defense and Initiation**

The commentators draw out the distinction that the verse's grammar enforces: the acquittal belongs to the one who defends himself *after* having been wronged — the response to aggression, not the initiation of it. The verse thereby establishes the legal and moral asymmetry between the aggressor and the defender: the aggressor's blow is a wrong from its first instant, while the defender's blow is a right — the restoration of the balance that the aggression disturbed. The same distinction governs the Qur'an's law of war, which permits fighting against those who fight the believers while forbidding the transgression of limits (2:190–94), and the classical jurists built upon the distinction their whole architecture of lawful defense: the defender's act is judged by its cause (the wrong that preceded it), its measure (the equivalence of verse 40), and its end (the restoration of peace rather than the satisfaction of vengeance). The Prophet ﷺ applied the same distinction to the injuries of speech in the hadith the commentators cite:

> *"When two people insult one another, the sin is upon the one who began it, so long as the one who is wronged does not transgress."* (Muslim 2587, from Abū Hurayrah)

The hadith's boundary is the verse's boundary: the responder who stays within the equivalence carries no blame — and the responder who exceeds it enters the circle of blame that the next verse will define.

**The Psychology of the Unblamed Defense**

The verse's acquittal carries a psychological liberation that the commentators appreciate: the believer who has been wronged and defends himself within the bounds of justice need not carry the guilt of his own defense — the self-doubt that plagues the gentle ("was I wrong to resist?") is answered by the verse's verdict: no way against them. The liberation is important for the community's health: a people that cannot defend itself without self-condemnation will slowly unlearn defense and drift into the submission that tyranny requires; a people that knows its defense is blameless can resist with a clear conscience and a steady hand. The verse's companion in the sūrah's argument — the believers' defense against the tyranny that befell them (v. 39) — is thus secured at the level of the individual conscience: the Muslim who resists oppression is not committing a sin that needs forgiveness; he is performing a right that carries no blame.

**The Inner Boundary: When Defense Would Be Wrong**

Yet the verse's acquittal is conditional, and the commentators are careful to state the conditions that its own words imply. The defense must come "after having been wronged" — the initiation of aggression earns no acquittal. It must be a defense — the response to the wrong, not the exploitation of the wrong as a pretext for a larger campaign. And it must stay within the equivalence of verse 40 — the transgression of the measure voids the acquittal, as the hadith of the two insulters states: the one who began is at fault, "so long as the one who is wronged does not transgress." The verse thereby leaves the defender standing at a precise point: fully acquitted in his defense, fully responsible for his measure — the same position at which the believer stands before God in every sphere, where the act is judged by its cause, its proportion, and its intent. And the verse's final word — *sabīl*, way, path, recourse — completes the sūrah's geography of recourse: the wrongdoer of verse 41's companion passages will seek a way of return and find none (v. 44); the tyrant will seek protectors and find none (v. 46); but the defender who was wronged and defended himself within the bounds — for him, no way of blame exists at all: the path of recourse against him is closed, and the path of his own acquittal stands open to the Rewarder whose ledger distinguishes the defender from the aggressor with a precision that no human court can match.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:42

> **There is only a way against those who wrong people and behave tyrannically upon the earth without right. For them, there shall be a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Way Against: Blame Redirected to Its True Object**

The verse completes the legal sequence by fixing the blame where it belongs: *innamā al-sabīlu ʿalā alladhīna yaẓlimūna al-nāsa wa yabghūna fī al-arḍi bi-ghayri al-ḥaqq* — "there is only a way against those who wrong people and behave tyrannically upon the earth without right." Where the previous verse closed the way of blame against the defender, this verse opens it against the aggressor: the only party against whom recourse lies is the party who wrongs people and tyrannizes without right. The commentators note the verse's double description of the condemned party: they *wrong people* — the specific injuries inflicted on particular persons, the rights violated, the harms done — and they *behave tyrannically upon the earth* — the systematic aggression, the corruption that exceeds the individual injury and disturbs the order of the whole. The phrase "without right" (*bi-ghayri al-ḥaqq*) is the verse's legal core: the aggression is condemned not because it disturbs the powerful but because it violates the right — and the same phrase recurs in the Qur'an's description of those who "strive upon the earth without right" (5:33; 42:42), the corrupters whose aggression is condemned by the standard of justice itself, not by the standard of the prevailing power.

**The Two Faces of Tyranny: Injury and Corruption**

The commentators unpack the verse's two clauses as the two faces of the same wrong. The first face is the injury of persons: the tyrant wrongs *people* — the individual whose property is seized, whose honor is assaulted, whose life is threatened, whose rights are denied; the wrong is concrete, and its victims are named in the verse's plural — *al-nās* — because tyranny always has a face, and the face belongs to particular human beings. The second face is the corruption of the order: the tyrant behaves tyrannically *upon the earth* — the aggression that exceeds its individual victims and disturbs the common order, the oppression that becomes a system, the transgression that teaches transgression. The verse's pairing thus covers the tyrant's whole career: from the first wrong done to a person to the entrenched tyranny that corrupts the earth — and the way against him opens at both stages, because the community's duty of defense (v. 39) and its pursuit of justice (v. 40) are responses to both faces of the wrong.

**The Painful Punishment: The Tyrant's Appointment**

The verse closes with the sentence that the tyrant of every age must hear: "For them, there shall be a painful punishment." The commentators note that the punishment is the requital that verse 40's equivalence permits and that the wrongdoer's own record has earned — the same "painful punishment" that the sūrah has attached to the disputers (v. 16), the inventors of religion (v. 21), and the disbelievers (v. 26): the tyrant's portion is fixed in the same ledger. The verse's placement at the end of the defense sequence (vv. 39–43) gives the punishment its full context: the believers are not commanded to execute the tyrant's punishment themselves — they are commanded to defend (v. 39), to requite within bounds (v. 40), to do so without blame (v. 41) — and the punishment beyond the bounds belongs to God, whose painful punishment is reserved for those who wrong people and tyrannize upon the earth. The community's justice is thus bounded in both directions: bounded in its extent — the equivalence of verse 40 — and bounded in its ultimate execution — the full punishment that only God can inflict and only the Day can complete.

**The Warning to Every Age's Tyrants**

The verse's application to modern life is direct and searching. The tyrants of the modern world do not always wear the ancient face — the warlord and the persecutor are joined by the corporate predator, the corrupt official, the abusive power of every kind — but the verse's definition catches them all: those who wrong people and behave tyrannically upon the earth without right. The verse supplies the believer's stance toward them in the three movements of the sūrah: resistance to their tyranny within the bounds of lawful defense, refusal to become their mirror through excess, and confidence that their painful punishment is appointed with the same certainty as the believers' reward — for the same God who acquits the defender (v. 41) has fixed the way of blame against the aggressor (v. 42), and the balance that was set in the heavens (v. 17) will not fail to weigh the wrong that was done upon the earth. The verse's final word to the believer is the word of patient certainty: the tyrant's apparent success is not the final account; the way against him is open in this world's justice and complete in the next's; and the punishment that no earthly court could execute is stored with the Judge whose record of the wronged and the wrongers admits no omission and no appeal.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:43

> **And whosoever is patient and forgives, that is indeed a course worthy of resolve.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Crown of the Portrait: Patience and Forgiveness**

The verse closes the believers' portrait with its highest trait: *wa la-man ṣabara wa ghafara inna dhālika la-min ʿazmi al-umūr* — "and whosoever is patient and forgives, that is indeed a course worthy of resolve." The commentators note the verse's position: it follows the full sequence of the believer's confrontation with wrong — the permission of defense (v. 39), the law of equivalence (v. 40), the acquittal of the defender (v. 41), the blame of the aggressor (v. 42) — and it now names the course that rises above all of them: the patience that endures the wrong without retaliating and the forgiveness that releases the wrongdoer without condition. The verse's phrasing is exact: the course is described as *min ʿazmi al-umūr* — among the matters of resolve, the things God has commanded the believers to hold firmly — and the commentators connect the phrase to its parallel in the Qur'an's descriptions of the resolute:

> *"And be patient — truly thy patience is only from God — and grieve not for them, and be not in distress over what they scheme."* (Qur'an 16:127)

> *"And We made them leaders guiding by Our Command, and We revealed unto them the doing of good deeds, the performance of prayer, and the giving of alms; and they were worshippers of Us."* (Qur'an 21:73 — where the same quality of resolve frames the prophets' character)

**The Meaning of the Resolve: Patience With Power**

The commentators unpack the verse's praise in both its directions. The patience (*ṣabr*) praised here is not the patience of helplessness — the endurance of the one who cannot retaliate — but the patience of the one who *can*: the verse follows the permission of defense (vv. 39–41), so the patient one of verse 43 is the believer who possesses the right of requital and relinquishes it — the strong man of the Prophet's hadith who contains himself when he is angry (al-Bukhārī 6114; Muslim 2609), the wronged one who could exact the equivalent and chooses the higher course. The forgiveness (*ghafara*) praised here is likewise not the pardon of indifference but the pardon of power: the forgiveness that costs something — the injury absorbed, the right waived, the ego swallowed — and the commentators note that the verse's praise is proportioned to the cost: the course is worthy of resolve precisely because it is difficult, and it is difficult precisely because the requital was lawful. Ibn Kathīr describes the forgiven one as the one who bears the injury with patience and conceals the evil deeds of others (IK) — the pardon that does not publish the wrong it pardons, the forgiveness that restores rather than records.

**The Reward That Is With God**

The verse's promise is implicit in its praise: the course worthy of resolve carries a reward proportionate to its difficulty (IK). The Qur'an states the proportionality in its companion verses:

> *"Truly the patient shall be paid their reward in full, without reckoning."* (Qur'an 39:10)

> *"Those who are patient will be given their reward twice over for what they endured."* (Qur'an 28:54 — in the commentators' reading of the patient among the People of the Book who embraced Islam)

The classical scholars note the striking phrase in 39:10 — the patient are rewarded "without reckoning" (*bi-ghayri ḥisāb*): while other deeds are weighed and multiplied, the reward of patience is not subject to the scales at all — it is given as a measureless gift, because patience is the virtue whose practice cannot be priced: the injury absorbed, the anger mastered, the vengeance withheld — each act of patience is a small death of the ego, and the reward of such deaths is the life that only God can give. The Prophet ﷺ described the same measurelessness when he said:

> *"No one has been given a gift better and more comprehensive than patience."* (al-Bukhārī 1469; Muslim 1053, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

**The Completion of the Believers' Portrait**

The verse's place at the end of the portrait (vv. 36–43) gives the whole passage its final shape: the believers are those who hold the world's goods loosely (v. 36), shun the grave sins and forgive from the midst of anger (v. 37), respond to their Lord, pray, counsel, and spend (v. 38), resist tyranny (v. 39), requite within the bounds of justice (v. 40), defend without blame (v. 41), and — at the summit of the portrait — choose the patience and forgiveness that transcend the requital they were lawfully entitled to take (v. 43). The portrait is the Qur'an's answer to the honor-code of pre-Islamic Arabia, which measured manhood by the swiftness and completeness of revenge: the sūrah's believers measure manhood differently — by the strength that contains anger, the justice that bounds retaliation, and the mercy that rises above both. The Prophet ﷺ described the believers' mutual relation in terms that complete the portrait's social meaning:

> *"The likeness of the believers in their mutual love and mercy is the likeness of one body: when one part complains, the rest of the body responds with sleeplessness and fever."* (al-Bukhārī 6011; Muslim 2586)

The course worthy of resolve is thus not only the individual's excellence but the community's constitution: a community that can forgive one another is a community that can counsel together (v. 38); a community that has relinquished vengeance is a community whose disputes never escalate beyond repair; and a community whose members pardon as they hope to be pardoned (24:22) is a community that has understood the prayer its Lord taught it — "forgive us, and pardon us" (2:286) — and has begun, in its own life, the forgiveness that it asks of God. The verse's final word — *ʿazm*, resolve — is the measure of the whole: the course is not for the weak, and it is not easy; it is the course of the resolute, the strong, the free — the believers of the flowering meadows, who mastered the one enemy they will meet in the grave, and forgave as they hope to be forgiven.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:44

> **And whomsoever God leads astray has no protector beyond Him. Thou shalt see the wrongdoers, when they see the punishment, saying, "Is there any way of return?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Astray and Their Protectors**

The verse opens with a statement that the sūrah has been preparing since its first pages: *wa man yuḍlili Allāhu fa-mā lahu min walīyin min baʿdihi* — "and whomsoever God leads astray has no protector beyond Him." The commentators are careful with the verse's theology: the leading astray (*iḍlāl*) attributed to God is not the arbitrary condemnation of the innocent but the consequence of the creature's own turning — the Qur'an states the correspondence repeatedly: "God leads astray the wrongdoers" (14:27), "God leads astray whomever He will and guides whomever He will" (35:8), and the mechanism is the one the sūrah itself has described: those who dispute the signs (v. 35), who argue after responding (v. 16), who take protectors apart from God (v. 6) — their turning is answered by a corresponding abandonment, and the abandonment is called *iḍlāl*: the loss of the guidance that was refused. The verse's clause "has no protector beyond Him" is the completion of the sūrah's repeated refrain — no protector apart from God (vv. 6, 9, 31, 46) — and its application to the astray: the protectors they took apart from God (v. 6) are exposed at the moment of their need as non-protectors, and the only Protector who could have saved them is the One beyond Whom no protector exists — and they have placed themselves beyond His protection by their own turning.

**The Scene of the Question: Seeing the Punishment**

The verse then turns to the scene that the Qur'an returns to with relentless consistency — the moment when the wrongdoers see the punishment: "Thou shalt see the wrongdoers, when they see the punishment, saying: Is there any way of return?" The commentators describe the scene's elements: the punishment is *seen* — not merely announced but witnessed, present, approaching — and the seeing produces the question that defines the moment: *hal ilā maraddin min sabīl* — "is there any way of return?" The question is the wrongdoers' last discovery: the return they never attempted while return was possible is now the only thing they desire, and it is the one thing that cannot be granted. The Qur'an describes the same scene in its companion passages:

> *"If you could but see when the wrongdoers are in the throes of death, and the angels stretch out their hands: Discharge your souls! Today you shall be requited with the punishment of humiliation for what you used to say against God untruly, and for waxing arrogant against His signs."* (Qur'an 6:93)

> *"And if you could but see when they are set before the Fire, and they say: Would that we were returned, and we would not deny the signs of our Lord, and we would be among the believers!"* (Qur'an 6:27)

**Why the Return Is Refused: The Knowledge That Would Not Have Helped**

The verse's question receives its answer in the Qur'an's own account of the refusal: the return is not granted, because the return would reproduce the original refusal. The commentators cite the Qur'an's diagnosis of the would-be returners:

> *"Nay, but that which they used to conceal has now appeared to them. And were they to be returned, they would return to that which they were forbidden — and truly they are liars."* (Qur'an 6:28)

The insight is one of the Qur'an's most penetrating contributions to moral psychology: the wrongdoer's desire for return at the sight of the punishment is not repentance but regret — the wish to escape the consequence, not the wish to abandon the cause. The return that is refused would have been wasted: placed back in the world, the returners would have returned to what they were forbidden, because their characters had been formed by their choices, and the character that chose the wrong would choose it again. The Prophet's warning about the deathbed repentance — refused because it comes when the world has already ended for its speaker (4:18) — is the same truth at the individual level: the repentance that is granted is the repentance that has time to prove itself in deeds, and the regret that arrives with the punishment is the regret that has no time left because it spent its time denying the need for regret.

**The Protector Beyond the Astray's Reach**

The verse's closing scene completes its opening clause: the astray have no protector beyond God — and now, when they see the punishment and ask for return, the full meaning of the clause is displayed: the protectors they trusted are absent, the return they desire is refused, and the only Protector who could have intervened is the One beyond Whom no protector exists — the One they abandoned when they took protectors apart from Him. The verse thereby delivers the sūrah's warning in its most concrete form: the moment of the punishment is not the moment for seeking protectors or planning returns; the moment for both was the moment of the response — the response to the Lord that verse 38 made the first trait of the believers, the turning in repentance that verse 25 promised to accept, the counsel and the spending and the prayer that the portrait of the believers described. The wrongdoers of the verse are the negative image of that portrait: where the believers responded, they disputed; where the believers turned, they persisted; where the believers stored their harvest with God (v. 20), they spent their portion on the world (v. 20) — and the scene of the punishment is the ledger of the difference, presented in the only form that the wrongdoer can no longer evade: the sight of what was earned, and the question of the return that no longer exists. For the living reader, the verse's function is precisely the opposite of its scene: it is the warning delivered *before* the sight, so that the question — "is there any way of return?" — may be asked now, while the answer is still yes, and answered by the turning that verse 25 promised to accept, before the seeing of the punishment makes the question the wrongdoers' last and only prayer.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:45

> **And thou shalt see them exposed to it, diminished through abasement, looking furtively. And those who believe will say, "Truly the losers are those who have lost themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection." Behold! Truly, the wrongdoers are in lasting punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Exposed to the Fire: The Scene of Total Display**

The verse continues the scene of the wrongdoers with an image of exposure: *wa tarāhum yuʿraḍūna ʿalayhā khāshiʿīna min al-dhulli yanẓurūna min ṭarfin khafiyy* — "and thou shalt see them exposed to it, diminished through abasement, looking furtively." The commentators unpack the image clause by clause. *Exposed to it*: the wrongdoers are brought before the Fire and made to see it — the Qur'an describes the exposure as a presentation of the place itself, so that the punishment is seen before it is felt, and the seeing is part of the punishment (Bg, IK, Ṭ). *Diminished through abasement*: the word *khāshiʿīn* describes the lowering, the humbling — the arrogance of their earthly lives drained out of them, the proud ones of Makkah reduced to the posture of the crushed. *Looking furtively*: the phrase *min ṭarfin khafiyy* pictures the glance stolen from the corner of a lowered eye — they cannot bear to look at the Fire fully, and they cannot keep from looking at all; their eyes are lowered by abasement and drawn by terror, and the furtive glance is the exact image of their condition: unable to face the reality they are forced to see. Some commentators add the reading that their looking is "with veiled eyes" — the look of those resurrected blind in the heart, since their inner sight was blinded in this life (Ṭ, Z):

> *"And whosoever was blind in this life will be blind in the Hereafter, and further astray from the way."* (Qur'an 17:72)

**The Believers' Verdict: The True Definition of Loss**

The scene's second panel turns to the believers, who witness the exposure and pronounce the verdict: "Truly the losers are those who have lost themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection." The commentators note the significance of the believers' speech: the word *al-khāsirūn* — the losers — is the Qur'an's term for those whose ledger ends in deficit, and the verse's definition of the loss is its depth: the losers are not those who lost wealth or rank or pleasure, but those who lost *themselves* and *their families* — the two possessions that constitute a human life. The commentators unfold the loss in its two dimensions. The loss of the self: the wrongdoer who dwells in the Fire has lost the self he was created to become — the soul that was made for the knowledge of God and the meadows of His Garden is now the inmate of the Fire, and the distance between what he was made for and what he has become is the loss of himself. The loss of the family: the commentators explain that if the wrongdoers' families are in the Garden, the families have been given companions in place of them (Q) — the Qur'an describes the believers of Paradise as paired with the pure companions, so that the family bonds of the Garden are formed among its people — and if the families are in the Fire with them, they are lost together; in either case, the wrongdoer has lost his family on the Day: he cannot benefit them, and they cannot save him. The Prophet ﷺ used the same measure of loss when he described the bankrupt of his community:

> *"Do you know who the bankrupt is? The bankrupt of my community is the one who comes on the Day of Resurrection with prayer, fasting, and charity — and comes having insulted this one, slandered that one, consumed the wealth of this one, shed the blood of that one, and struck that one; so this one is given from his good deeds, and that one is given from his good deeds — and if his good deeds are exhausted before the claims are settled, their sins are cast upon him, and he is cast into the Fire."* (Muslim 2581, from Abū Hurayrah)

**The Lasting Punishment: The Duration That Defines the Loss**

The verse closes with the verdict's seal: "Behold! Truly, the wrongdoers are in lasting punishment" — *fī ʿadhābin muqīm* — the punishment that abides, settles, and remains. The commentators note the word's force: the punishment is not a passing affliction that ends when its purpose is served; it is *muqīm* — permanent, established, without term (IK) — the mirror of the wrongdoers' own persistence: as they persisted in the wrong without turning, the punishment persists without ending. The verse's severity is qualified in the commentators' discussions by the distinction al-Rāzī draws: reading the verse in light of the Qur'an's statement that "the disbelievers, they are the wrongdoers" (2:254), the "wrongdoers" of the lasting punishment are those whose wrongdoing is unqualified — the disbelievers whose wrong is not mitigated by any faith (R) — while the believers who sinned and repented are covered by the pardon that the sūrah announced in verse 25 and by the intercession that verse 26's increase described. The verse thereby preserves the sūrah's balance to its end: the lasting punishment belongs to the wrongdoers who persisted; the pardon belongs to the servants who turned; and the boundary between the two was crossed, in this life, by the response that verse 38 made the first trait of the believers.

**The Witness of the Believers: What the Scene Teaches**

The verse's final service is to the believers who pronounce the verdict: the scene of the wrongdoers' exposure is witnessed by the believers, and their speech — "truly the losers are those who have lost themselves and their families" — is their own lesson in the true measure of gain and loss. The believers of Makkah, who were being told by their city that they were the losers — the outcasts, the impoverished, the disowned — are shown in this verse the reversal that the Day will display: the apparent losers of this world pronounce the verdict of true loss over the apparent winners, and the reversal is the completion of the sūrah's economics — the two harvests of verse 20, the enjoyment of this world's life of verse 36, the meadows of the Gardens of verse 22 — all gathered at last into the single scene where the wrongdoers stand exposed, diminished, glancing furtively at the Fire, and the believers speak the sentence that the whole chapter has been preparing: the losers are those who lost themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection, and the winners are those who responded, turned, prayed, counseled, spent, forgave, and were patient — the people of the meadows, whose loss was never possible and whose gain was never at risk.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:46

> **They have no protectors to help them apart from God. And whomsoever God leads astray, no way has he.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain in Its Final Form**

The verse delivers the sūrah's refrain one last time, in its most complete form: *wa mā kāna lahum min awliyāʾa yanṣurūnahum min dūni Allāh* — "they have no protectors to help them apart from God" — and joins to it the closing seal: "and whomsoever God leads astray, no way has he." The commentators note that the verse's placement at the end of the wrongdoers' scene (vv. 44–45) gives the refrain its full context: the protectors that the wrongdoers trusted in this life — the idols, the patrons, the tribal alliances, the wealth and the prestige of Makkah — are summoned, in the scene of the punishment, and found absent; the help that was promised by every false protector is not forthcoming at the one moment when help matters. The Qur'an's most vivid image of the false protectors' helplessness belongs to the same argument:

> *"The parable of those who take protectors apart from God is that of the spider that takes to itself a house. And truly the frailest of houses is the house of the spider, if they but knew."* (Qur'an 29:41)

The scene of the Day exposes the spider's house for what it always was: the protectors who were taken apart from God are present at the judgment only as witnesses against their worshipers — the Qur'an describes the idols disowning their worshipers and the worshipers wishing they had not taken them as protectors:

> *"And they shall say, when they see the punishment: If only we had a second chance, we would not deny the signs of our Lord, and we would be among the believers."* (Qur'an 6:27)

**"No Way Has He": The Closing of Every Road**

The verse's second clause completes the wrongdoers' geography: "And whomsoever God leads astray, no way has he." The commentators unpack the word *sabīl* — way, path — in its full sense: the astray have no way to the truth and no way toward God (Ṭ), no path of return (v. 44), no refuge (v. 35), no escape. The verse's companion passages fill the map of the closed roads:

> *"And for whomsoever God appoints no light, he has no light."* (Qur'an 24:40)

> *"Whomsoever God guides, he is rightly guided; and whomsoever He leads astray, you will find for him no protector to lead him aright."* (Qur'an 18:17)

The commentators are careful with the verse's theology, as with its parallel in verse 44: the leading astray is not the beginning of the wrongdoer's story but its middle — the divine response to a turning that the wrongdoer began. The sūrah's own argument has established the sequence: they disputed the signs (v. 35), argued after responding (v. 16), refused the response (v. 38's negative), took protectors apart from God (v. 6) — and the leading astray is the seal on their own choice: the guidance that was refused is withdrawn, the roads that were rejected are closed, and the only Way that remained — the One beyond Whom no protector exists — is the Way they placed themselves beyond by their own turning (R). The verse's doctrine of guidance and misguidance thus preserves both divine sovereignty and human responsibility: the guidance is God's to give and the misguidance is God's to decree — but the decree answers the choice, and the choice was the creature's.

**The Two Sentences as One Lesson**

Read together, the verse's two clauses deliver the lesson that the whole sūrah has been teaching since its sixth verse: protectors apart from God do not exist, and ways apart from God do not lead. The wrongdoers' tragedy is not that they lacked protectors or ways but that they sought them in the wrong places — the protectors that were creatures and the ways that were dead ends — while the only Protector and the only Way stood open before them in every revelation, every sign, and every warning of the chapter. The believers' gain is the mirror of the tragedy: the believers took no protector apart from God, and therefore have the Protector whom no power can defeat; the believers walked no way apart from God, and therefore walk the way that ends at the meadows of verse 22. The verse's final word to the living reader is the sūrah's permanent urgency: the way is open now — the response of verse 38, the repentance of verse 25, the guidance that verse 13 promised to those who turn — and the only way that closes is the way that is refused until the seeing of the punishment makes the question of return the last prayer of those who no longer have any way to pray it.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:47

> **Respond to your Lord before there comes a day from God that none can repel. You will have no refuge that Day; nor shall you have any denial.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Summons: Respond Before the Day**

The verse turns from the scene of the punishment to the living audience with a direct summons: *istajībū li-rabbikum* — "respond to your Lord." The commentators note that the command is the positive form of everything the sūrah has warned: where the wrongdoers are described as refusing the response (vv. 44–46), the audience is now commanded to make it — and the command's object is precise: respond *to your Lord* — not to a doctrine, not to a community, not to a tradition, but to the Lord whose ownership of the heavens and the earth (v. 4), whose keys (v. 12), whose kindness (v. 19), and whose acceptance of repentance (v. 25) the chapter has established. The commentators read the command in its several registers: it is the response to the one who calls to the Lord — the Prophet, whose summons the sūrah described in verse 15 (Bg, Ṭ); it is the response to that which calls to Him — faith and obedience (Q); and it is the response of the covenant — maintaining the bond with God that was made at the beginning (see 7:172c) and observing His due (Qu). The command's urgency is carried by its opening word: *istajībū* — the response is not an option to be considered but an answer to be made, and the verse's next clause supplies the deadline.

**The Day That None Can Repel**

The summons is timed: "before there comes a day from God that none can repel." The commentators explain the description: the Day of Resurrection is a day from God — issued by His decree, executed by His command — and a day that none can repel (cf. 13:11; 30:43): no intercession avails against its coming, no power delays it, no plea postpones it (Q). The verse's companion states the same inevitability with the same phrase:

> *"And a caller will call from a place near at hand — on the Day when they hear the Cry in truth, that is the Day of Coming Forth."* (Qur'an 50:41–42)

The description of the Day as "from God" is the key to its irresistibility: the day is not an event within the world's order that the world's powers might influence; it is the intervention of the world's Owner in His own order, and no creature can repel the act of the Creator — the power that repels is itself His loan. The command's logic is the logic of every deadline: the response that is made before the day is a response; the response that is attempted after the day is the question of verse 44 — "is there any way of return?" — asked by those who have no way. The verse thereby translates the whole sūrah's theology of the Hour into a single practical command: the Hour's nearness (v. 17) is not a fact to be contemplated but a deadline to be met — and the meeting is the response, made now, while the day has not come.

**No Refuge and No Denial: The Two Losses of That Day**

The verse's closing clause describes the condition of the day's arrival: "You will have no refuge that Day; nor shall you have any denial." The commentators unpack the double negation with care. *No refuge*: the word is the same root as verse 35's *maḥīṣ* — the place of flight, the escape — and the verse states its absence absolutely: on that Day, there is no corner of the earth to flee to, no mountain to hide in, no sea to cross, no power to interpose; the geography of escape that the wrongdoers navigated all their lives — moving from city to city, from alliance to alliance, from excuse to excuse — reaches its end at the Day that none can repel. The Qur'an describes the search for refuge on that Day in terms that make the verse's negation vivid:

> *"That Day man will say: Where is the escape? Nay! But there shall be no refuge. Unto your Lord that Day is the dwelling place."* (Qur'an 75:10–12)

*No denial*: the commentators read the phrase in two directions. It is the loss of the power to deny — the disbelievers who spent their lives denying the signs, the Hour, and the reckoning will find that the Day itself is undeniable: their denial is exhausted, and they are reduced to the confession that the Qur'an records:

> *"They say: Had we listened or had we understood, we would not be among the inhabitants of the Blaze. Thus do they admit their sin; so away with the inhabitants of the Blaze!"* (Qur'an 67:10–11)

And it is the loss of the power to deny *themselves* — the excuses, the self-justifications, the narratives in which they were the wronged ones and God's demands were unreasonable — all of them collapse before the record that the Day displays. The verse's pairing thus describes the Day's double stripping: the stripping of external refuges and the stripping of internal denials — the wrongdoer stands with nowhere to go and nothing to say, and the silence of that standing is the Day's own eloquence.

**The Moment of Death as the First Arrival of the Day**

The commentators add a final reading that brings the verse's urgency to every reader's door: the "day from God that none can repel" can also be understood as the moment of death — the personal day that comes to every human being without reprieve (see 63:10–11) — and on this reading the verse implies that the door of response and repentance is open until that moment and closed after it (see 4:18). The command "respond to your Lord before there comes a day" is thereby addressed not to a distant generation facing a distant apocalypse but to every living soul facing its own appointment: the response that is made before death is the response that is accepted; the response that is postponed until death is the regret that verse 44 pictured — the question of return asked by those who see the punishment. The verse's gift to the living is the precision of its deadline: no one knows the day, and therefore everyone must live as though the response were due now — the prayer of the present hour, the repentance of the present moment, the reconciliation of the present day — because the day that none can repel may be closer than the next sunrise, and the response that is made before it is the only response that will be heard. The Prophet ﷺ expressed the same urgency when he said:

> *"Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your illness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your occupation, and your life before your death."* (al-Ḥākim, al-Mustadrak 7846, from Ibn ʿAbbās; declared ṣaḥīḥ by al-Ḥākim and al-Albānī, and transmitted by al-Tirmidhī 2304 in abridged form)

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:48

> **And if they turn away, We sent thee not as a keeper over them. Naught is incumbent upon thee, save the proclamation. Truly when We cause man to taste mercy from Us, he rejoices in it. Yet if an evil befalls them because of that which their hands have sent forth, truly man is ungrateful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet's Boundaries: Keeper, Guardian, Warner**

The verse returns to the Prophet with the consolation that the sūrah's argument requires: *fa-in aʿraḍū fa-mā arsalnāka ʿalayhim ḥafīẓan* — "and if they turn away, We sent thee not as a keeper over them." The commentators note the verse's context: after the summons of verse 47 — "respond to your Lord" — the inevitable possibility is stated plainly: some will turn away, and the Prophet must know, before the turning, what his own position is. He is not a *ḥafīẓ* — a keeper set over them to compel, guard, and record their deeds (cf. 4:80; 6:104; 11:86; 42:6); he is not their *wakīl* — their guardian with power over their affairs (6:66, 107; 10:108; 17:54; 39:41); and his mission is defined by its single duty: "Naught is incumbent upon thee, save the proclamation" — *illā al-balāgh*. The verse's companion passages state the same boundary with the same word:

> *"So remind — thou art but a reminder. Thou art not over them a compeller."* (Qur'an 88:21–22)

> *"If they turn away, We sent thee not as a keeper over them; naught is incumbent upon thee save the proclamation."* (Qur'an 42:48 — the verse itself, and its parallels at 3:20; 5:92, 99; 13:40; 16:35, 82; 24:54; 29:18; 36:17; 64:12)

**The Liberation of the Messenger's Task**

The verse's boundary is a liberation in two directions, and the commentators draw out both. It liberates the Prophet from the anguish of results: the turning of the turners is not his failure — his task was the proclamation, and the proclamation was delivered; the acceptance and the rejection belong to the hearers and to God. The verse thereby releases the messenger — and every bearer of the message after him — from the psychological trap that destroys so many callers: the identification of their mission with its visible success, the despair that follows rejection, the temptation to coerce when persuasion fails. The Prophet ﷺ felt the anguish of his people's rejection as the Qur'an itself testifies — "Perhaps thou art consumed by grief over them, if they believe not in this discourse" (18:6) — and the verse's boundary was the divine prescription for the grief: the task is the proclamation, the proclamation was made, and the rest is with God. And the verse liberates the hearers from the excuse of the messenger's imperfection: the message is not hostage to the messenger's power to enforce it; the proclamation has been delivered, and the hearer's response is his own.

**The Portrait of the Human Being: Joy in Mercy, Ingratitude in Affliction**

The verse's second half turns from the Prophet's task to a portrait of the human being that explains why the task must be bounded: "Truly when We cause man to taste mercy from Us, he rejoices in it. Yet if an evil befalls them because of that which their hands have sent forth, truly man is ungrateful." The commentators note that the portrait is drawn from the Qur'an's constant observation of human nature — the same observation appears in its fullest form in Sūrah Hūd:

> *"And if We cause man to taste mercy from Us, and then withdraw it from him, truly he is despairing, ungrateful. And if We cause him to taste favor after adversity has touched him, he says: The evils have departed from me. Truly he is exultant, boastful — save those who are patient and perform righteous deeds; for those there is forgiveness and a great reward."* (Qur'an 11:9–11)

The verse's portrait is of the unsteady human response to the divine alternation: when mercy comes — health, provision, relief — man rejoices in it, but the rejoicing is often the rejoicing of the beneficiary rather than the gratitude of the servant: he rejoices in the gift without turning to the Giver. And when evil befalls — the affliction that the verse traces to "that which their hands have sent forth," the deeds they have earned (see 2:95; 4:62; 28:47–48; 62:7; and the sūrah's own verse 30) — man is *kafūr*: ungrateful, and the commentators pause over the word, which derives from the root *k-f-r*, "to cover over" — the same root as disbelief (*kufr*) and disbeliever (*kāfir*). The ungrateful man *covers* the mercy he received: the years of provision are forgotten in the day of affliction, the blessings are buried under the complaint, and the covering of the mercy is the beginning of the covering of the truth — the *kufr* of ingratitude is the cousin of the *kufr* of disbelief, and the Qur'an's use of the same root is deliberate: the ingrate who forgets the Giver in his affliction is practicing, in miniature, the denial that the disbeliever practices in full.

**The Stability of the Believer Between the Two States**

The verse's portrait of man's unsteadiness is drawn, as its parallel in Sūrah Hūd makes explicit, against the background of the believers' stability: "save those who are patient and perform righteous deeds." The believer is the one who holds the same posture through the divine alternation — grateful in the mercy without becoming heedless, patient in the affliction without becoming ungrateful — because his gaze is fixed not on the alternation but on the One who alternates. The verse thereby completes the sūrah's teaching on the divine economy: the alternation of mercy and affliction (vv. 27–30) is the field of the human test, and the test is precisely whether the creature will respond to the alternation as the servant or as the ingrate — whether the mercy will produce gratitude or heedlessness, and whether the affliction will produce patience or denial. The Prophet's description of the believer's unique advantage in both states is the verse's own conclusion:

> *"Amazing is the affair of the believer: truly all of his affair is good — and that is not for anyone but the believer. If prosperity reaches him, he gives thanks, and that is good for him; and if adversity reaches him, he is patient, and that is good for him."* (Muslim 2999, from Suhayb)

The verse's final word to the caller — the Prophet and every bearer of the message after him — is the completion of its opening boundary: because man is unsteady — rejoicing in the mercy and ungrateful in the affliction — the caller must not measure his mission by the response; the proclamation is the duty, the response is the test, and the test's outcome belongs to the One who alternates the mercy and the affliction, and who has fixed for each human being the ledger that the alternation was meant to fill.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:49

> **Unto God belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth; He creates whatsoever He will, bestowing females upon whomsoever He will, and bestowing males upon whomsoever He will,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sovereignty Clause: The Context of Creation's Gifts**

The verse opens with the sūrah's refrain in its final application: "Unto God belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth" — and the commentators note that the clause is not a decoration but the premise of everything that follows: because the sovereignty is His, the distribution of creation's gifts is His, and no creature can claim a right to any particular gift. The verse's companion passages repeat the clause wherever the Qur'an addresses the distribution of the gifts that human beings most fiercely claim as their own — wealth (3:26), life and death (67:1–2), and here, offspring: the children that the pre-Islamic Arabs regarded as the strongest proof of a man's worth and the surest sign of divine favor (R). The verse's argument is that the gifts of offspring belong to the same economy as the provision of verses 12 and 27: distributed by the sovereign Owner according to His will and wisdom, not earned by the merits of the parents nor deserved by the preferences of the tribe.

**The Creator's Freedom: He Creates Whatsoever He Will**

The verse's second clause states the divine freedom that governs the gifts: "He creates whatsoever He will" (cf. 5:17; 24:45; 30:54). The commentators draw out the clause's implications against the background of the pre-Islamic Arabs' beliefs: they imagined that offspring were produced by the merits or the luck of the parents — that a man of standing deserved sons, that a woman's barrenness was a curse, that the birth of daughters was a misfortune. The verse replies that creation is the act of the Creator: the child that is born is not the product of the parents' deserving but the creation of the One who creates whatsoever He wills — and the commentators note that the same clause governs the Prophet's own household, where the daughters who were born to him were the creations of the same will that the Arabs despised for giving daughters (R). The clause thereby severs the link between human merit and divine gift at its root: the gifts of God are not wages, and the distribution of His creation follows His wisdom rather than the creature's claim.

**Bestowing Females: The Verse's Answer to the Arabs' Shame**

The verse's third clause is its most pointed: "bestowing females upon whomsoever He will, and bestowing males upon whomsoever He will." The commentators observe that the verse mentions the bestowal of females *before* the bestowal of males — a sequence that was itself a rebuke to the culture that buried its daughters and boasted of its sons — and that the verse places both under the same verb: the female child is as much a bestowal (*yahabu*) as the male, given by the same Hand, from the same sovereignty. The verse's context is the Qur'an's sustained indictment of the Arabs' attitude toward daughters, which its companion passage describes with devastating precision:

> *"And when one of them receives tidings of a female child, his face darkens, and he is choked with anguish. He hides from the people because of the evil of the tidings he has been given. Shall he keep it in humiliation, or bury it in the dust? Behold! Evil indeed is the judgment they make!"* (Qur'an 16:58–59)

The same condemnation appears in the sūrah's own companion in Sūrah al-Zukhruf, where the idolaters are exposed for ascribing daughters to God while despising daughters for themselves:

> *"And when one of them is given tidings of that which he has likened to the Compassionate, his face darkens, and he is choked with anguish. Is it that they assign to God what they themselves dislike?"* (Qur'an 43:17–19; see also 16:57–59; 17:40; 37:149–53; 52:39)

The verse thereby performs a double correction: it corrects the theology — God has no daughter, and the angels are not His daughters — and it corrects the culture — the daughters that the Arabs despised are the bestowals of the sovereign Lord, given by His will to whomsoever He wills, and the contempt for the gift is contempt for the Giver's distribution.

**The Prophet's Own Testimony to the Daughters' Worth**

The commentators connect the verse to the Prophet's repeated elevation of daughters in a culture that had buried them alive:

> *"Whoever has a daughter and does not bury her alive, does not insult her, and does not prefer his son over her — God will admit him to the Garden."* (Abū Dāwūd 5146, from Ibn ʿAbbās, hasan)

> *"Whoever is tested with daughters and treats them well, they will be a shield for him from the Fire."* (al-Bukhārī 5995, from ʿĀʾishah; Muslim 2629, from Anas)

The traditions are the Prophet's own commentary on the verse: the bestowal of females is not a misfortune to be endured but a trust to be honored, and the honor of the trust — the raising of daughters with kindness, the rejection of the culture's contempt — is among the deeds that shield their doer from the Fire. The verse thereby stands in the Qur'an as the charter of the daughter's dignity: she is not the tribe's shame but the Creator's bestowal, and the sovereignty that gave her is the sovereignty that will reckon how she was received.

**The Modern Reading: The Verse Against Every Hierarchy of Birth**

The verse's final application is to every society that ranks human beings by the accident of their birth — by sex, by lineage, by tribe, by race. The verse's grammar is the Qur'an's permanent correction of such rankings: the distribution of offspring is the act of the sovereign Creator, and the creatures who receive the distribution have no ground for pride in its accidents — the male who boasts of his maleness has received what was bestowed; the parents who boast of their sons have received what was given; and the daughter who was despised has been named, by the verse itself, a bestowal of the Lord whose sovereignty the boasters merely borrow. The believers of the sūrah — who respond to their Lord, counsel among themselves, and forgive from their anger (vv. 37–38) — are the community that received the verse's correction into its very constitution: a community in which the birth of a daughter is greeted with the same gratitude as the birth of a son, because both are the creations of the One who creates whatsoever He wills, and the gratitude for the gift is the worship of the Giver.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:50

> **or He couples males and females and causes whomsoever He will to be barren. Truly He is Knowing, Powerful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Complete Inventory: All Four Portions Named**

The verse completes the inventory of the divine distribution of offspring: after bestowing females upon whom He will (v. 49) and bestowing males upon whom He will (v. 49), the verse adds the two remaining portions: "or He couples males and females" — the household that receives both, sons and daughters together — "and causes whomsoever He will to be barren" — the household that receives neither. The commentators note that the four portions exhaust the possibilities: daughters only, sons only, both, or none — and that the verse's naming of all four is itself the argument: if offspring were the product of human deserving or the proof of divine favor, the distribution would follow the pattern of merit; instead, the four portions are distributed across humanity without regard to the recipients' standing — the righteous are given daughters only, and the unrighteous are given sons; the prophet is given daughters, and the pharaoh is given sons; the believer prays for a child and remains barren, and the heedless are multiplied. The verse's inventory thereby completes the severance of the gift from the merit that verse 49 began: the offspring are the creations of the sovereign Lord, and His distribution follows His knowledge and His power, not the creature's claim.

**The Barren: The Verse's Most Difficult Portion**

The commentators give particular attention to the verse's final portion, the barren — *al-ʿaqīm* — for it is the portion that tests the soul most severely. The verse places the barren under the same divine verbs as the parents: the barrenness is not the failure of the parents nor the curse of the tribe nor the accident of biology alone — it is included in the Creator's distribution, caused by Him ("and causes whomsoever He will to be barren") within the same sovereignty that bestows the females and the males. The verse thereby performs for the barren what it performed for the parents of daughters: it removes the affliction from the realm of the human verdict and places it in the realm of the divine wisdom. The barren are not cursed — the distribution is not a punishment; the Qur'an itself records the barrenness of the wives of the prophets — Sarah before Isaac, and the wife of Zachariah before John — and their barrenness was not their disgrace but the stage of the divine gift:

> *"And Zachariah's wife was barren. So We responded to him and bestowed upon him John, and We made his wife fit for him."* (Qur'an 21:90)

> *"She said: My Lord, how shall I have a child, when old age has overtaken me and my husband is advanced in years? He said: Even so — God does whatsoever He will."* (Qur'an 3:40)

The prophets' households thereby model the response to barrenness that the verse requires: the acknowledgment that the distribution is the Creator's, the patience that does not turn the affliction into ingratitude (v. 48), and the trust that the same sovereignty that withheld can bestow — and that the withholding, like the bestowal, is contained within a wisdom that the creature cannot fully read.

**"Knowing, Powerful": The Names That Justify the Distribution**

The verse closes with the pair of names that make the distribution just: "Truly He is Knowing, Powerful." The commentators unfold the two names as the two pillars of the divine distribution. *Knowing*: the distribution of offspring is made by One who knows every recipient — knows the household that a son would corrupt and the household that a daughter would raise, knows the mother whose barrenness is her mercy and the father whose children are his test, knows the child who will be born into faith and the child who will be born into trial. The knowledge justifies the wisdom of the distribution even where the wisdom is hidden: the creature who cannot see the reason for his portion is asked to trust the Knower whose knowledge encompasses what the creature cannot see. *Powerful*: the distribution is executed by One whose power is not subject to the means that creatures depend upon — the power that gives the child to the barren and withholds the child from the fertile, the power that formed the first human being from clay and the second from the first's rib and the multitudes from the pair, and the power that will gather all of them on the Day (v. 29). The verse thereby places the most intimate of human experiences — the birth of a child, the ache of barrenness — under the two names that govern the whole sūrah: the knowledge that calibrates every measure (v. 27) and the power that executes every will (vv. 4, 9) — so that the parent and the barren alike stand before the same Lord, asking of Him the portion that only He can give.

**The Believer's Posture Toward the Four Portions**

The verse's application to the believer's life is the completion of its inventory. The parents who receive sons are not to boast — the sons are a bestowal and a trust, and the trust will be questioned. The parents who receive daughters are not to grieve — the daughters are the bestowal that the verse names first and that the Prophet's traditions shield their raisers from the Fire. The household that receives both is to give thanks for the fullness — and to remember that the fullness is a heavier trust, not a greater merit. And the household that receives neither is to hold the patience that the sūrah has praised (v. 43) and the trust that the prophets' wives modeled — knowing that the distribution is the act of the Knowing, the Powerful, and that the barrenness that is borne with faith is itself a portion of the divine wisdom that the Day will disclose. The verse thereby completes the sūrah's economics of the family as verse 12 completed its economics of wealth: both are distributions of the sovereign Lord, both are tests rather than verdicts, and both are to be received with the same posture — gratitude for the gift, patience in the withholding, and trust in the Knower whose knowledge and power stand behind every portion that His creatures receive or are denied. The Prophet ﷺ taught the believers to ask their Lord for the portion of goodness in both worlds, and the verse's inventory supplies the shape of the asking: the believer asks for the offspring that will be a mercy, accepts the portion that is given, and leaves the distribution to the Knowing, the Powerful — whose sovereignty over the heavens and the earth (v. 49) is the sovereignty that gave every parent every child, and that will give every servant, in the meadows of the Garden, the offspring of the spirit that no barrenness can withhold.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:51

> **It is not for any human being that God should speak unto him, save by revelation, or from behind a veil, or that He should send a messenger in order to reveal what He will by His Leave. Truly He is Exalted, Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Maps the Divine Address**

The verse is the Qur'an's most systematic statement of how God communicates with human beings, and the commentators (IK, Q, R, Ṭ, Z) treat it as the taxonomy of revelation itself: three modes, and only three, by which the divine speech reaches a human being. The verse's opening — "it is not for any human being that God should speak unto him" — establishes the boundary first: the direct, unveiled speech of God is not a human right or a human possibility; the creature cannot compel the Creator's address, and the address, when it comes, comes only by the modes that the verse proceeds to name. The boundary is the boundary of creatureliness: God's speech is an act of His will, not a response to human demand — the Qur'an says of the idolaters' demand for miracles that they asked for what "is not for any human being" — and the verse's listing of the modes is itself the mercy: the boundary is real, and the modes are real, and within the modes the divine address has reached the human being in every generation of the prophets.

**The First Mode: Revelation Cast Into the Heart**

The first mode is *waḥy* — "save by revelation" — and the commentators define it as the inspiration that God casts directly into the breast or heart of the prophet: the meaning conveyed without words, without angel, without veils (R, Z). The Qur'an's own examples supply the content of the mode: the inspiration of the mother of Moses — "And We revealed unto the mother of Moses: Suckle him" (28:7) — the inspiration of the bees — "And thy Lord revealed unto the bees" (16:68) — and, in the commentators' discussions, the revelation of the Psalms to David, which is said to have come in this direct manner (R, Z). The mode also includes the true dream, which the commentators count among the forms of *waḥy*: the dream in which Abraham saw himself sacrificing his son (37:102) was a revelation of the divine command in the form most fitting to the dreamer (Q, R, Z) — and the Prophet ﷺ said of the beginning of his own revelation: "The first thing with which the revelation began was the true dream, so that he did not see a dream but that it came like the break of dawn" (al-Bukhārī 3; Muslim 160). The first mode is the mode of the heart: the divine meaning placed directly into the human receptacle that was prepared for it, without the mediation of word or angel.

**The Second Mode: From Behind a Veil**

The second mode is the address "from behind a veil" — *min warāʾi ḥijāb* — and the commentators define it as the moments when the prophet hears the speech of God directly, with no intermediary, while not seeing God (IK, Q, R, Ṭ, Z). The Qur'an's example is the greatest the Book records: the speech of God to Moses on Mount Sinai, which the Torah's own account and the Qur'an's agree was a direct address — and when Moses, emboldened by the address, asked for the vision that the veil withheld:

> *"And when Moses came to Our appointed tryst, and his Lord spoke unto him, he said: My Lord, show me, that I may look upon Thee. He said: Thou shalt not see Me; but look upon the mountain — if it remains in its place, then thou shalt see Me. And when his Lord revealed Himself to the mountain, He made it crumble to dust, and Moses fell down in a swoon."* (Qur'an 7:143)

The mountain's crumbling is the verse's own commentary on the veil: the speech of God could be borne by the prophet through the veil, while the unveiled vision of God could not be borne by the mountain itself — the veil is the mercy that makes the divine address bearable, and the address from behind the veil is the highest speech that a human being receives while remaining human. The commentators add to Moses' address the speech that the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ received on the night of his heavenly journey, when the divine speech reached him at the furthest boundary of creation:

> *"Then He revealed to His servant what He revealed."* (Qur'an 53:10 — see also 17:1c)

**The Third Mode: The Messenger Angel**

The third mode is the sending of a messenger — *aw yursila rasūlan fa-yūḥiya bi-idhnihi mā yashāʾ* — the angelic intermediary who carries the revelation to the prophet: "or that He should send a messenger in order to reveal what He will by His Leave" (IK, Q, R, Ṭ, Z). The commentators identify the messenger with Gabriel, the Holy Spirit, who brought the Qur'an down to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ — the mode of the Qur'an's own descent, described in the chapter that the commentators cite:

> *"The Faithful Spirit descended with it upon thy heart, that thou mightest be among the warners, in a clear Arabic tongue."* (Qur'an 26:193–195)

The Prophet ﷺ described his experience of this mode in the famous report:

> *"Sometimes revelation comes to me like the ringing of a bell — and that is the hardest upon me — and it leaves me, and I have retained what was said; and sometimes the angel comes to me in the form of a man and speaks to me, and I retain what he says."* (al-Bukhārī 2; Muslim 2333, from ʿĀʾishah)

The third mode is the mode of the word: the meaning carried in the angel's speech, delivered to the prophet's ear and heart, and preserved in the prophet's memory as the revealed text — the mode that made the Qur'an a Book, recited and written, rather than an inward illumination only. The verse's phrase "by His Leave" (*bi-idhnihi*) marks the angel's place in the hierarchy: the messenger conveys what he is permitted to convey, and the revelation that reaches the prophet through the angel is the revelation of the One who sent the angel — the mediation is a conveyance, not an authorship, and the words that Gabriel delivered were the words of God.

**The Exalted, the Wise: The Names Over the Modes**

The verse closes with the pair that the sūrah has used before (v. 3): "Truly He is Exalted, Wise." The commentators draw out the fitness of the names. *Exalted*: the modes are the measure of the distance — God is exalted above the direct address that the creature's finitude could not bear, and the veils and the angels are the accommodations of His exaltation to the creature's capacity; the same exaltation that makes the direct vision impossible makes the veiled address necessary. *Wise*: the modes are the distribution of the divine wisdom — each prophet receives the mode that fits his mission and his community: the direct inspiration for the mother who must act without a book, the direct speech for Moses the lawgiver, the angelic word for Muhammad the bearer of the recited Book; and the wisdom that chose the modes is the same wisdom that chose the messengers, the times, and the languages (v. 3; 14:4). The verse thereby closes the sūrah's revelation-theology as it opened: the God who "reveals unto thee and unto those before thee" (v. 3) has never left His creatures without His address — and the address has come in the modes that His exaltation required and His wisdom chose, from the inspiration of the heart to the speech behind the veil to the word carried by the angel — so that every prophet stands in the same line, and every scripture in the same descent, and the final revelation in the same Book that the sūrah's first pages announced.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:52

> **Thus have We revealed unto thee a Spirit from Our Command. Thou knewest not what scripture was, nor faith. But We made it a light whereby We guide whomsoever We will among Our servants. Truly thou dost guide unto a straight path,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Thus": The Sūrah's Frame Completed**

The verse opens with the same particle that opened the chapter's revelation-theme in verse 3 — *kadhālika*, "thus" — and the commentators note that the frame of the sūrah is now closed: verse 3 said, "Thus does He reveal unto thee and unto those before thee," and verse 52 says, "Thus have We revealed unto thee a Spirit from Our Command" — the chapter that began with the unity of revelation across the prophets ends with the confirmation that the final prophet received the same revelation in the same modes (Q, Ṭū). The commentators add the precision that the verse indicates the Prophet Muhammad's reception of revelation in all three modes described in verse 51 (Ṭb): the direct inspiration, the speech from behind the veil — as on the night of his heavenly journey — and the angelic delivery of the Qur'an — so that the final messenger, standing at the end of the prophetic line, received the address of God in the full range of its forms.

**"A Spirit From Our Command": The Revelation That Gives Life**

The verse's central phrase is its most mysterious: *rūḥan min amrinā* — "a Spirit from Our Command." The commentators offered several readings, and all of them converge on the same reality. The majority read the Spirit as the Qur'an itself (IK, Q, R, Ṭb, Ṭū): the revelation is called a Spirit because it brings life forth from the death of ignorance and disbelief (Q, R) — as the spirit is the principle of life in the body, the Qur'an is the principle of life in the soul; the same metaphor runs through the Qur'an's own self-descriptions:

> *"O you who believe! Respond to God and the Messenger when he calls you to that which gives you life."* (Qur'an 8:24)

> *"And thus have We revealed unto thee a Spirit from Our Command."* (Qur'an 42:52 — the verse itself)

Other readings see in the Spirit the prophethood itself (Q), or the Archangel Gabriel — who is called the Holy Spirit and by whom the revelation was brought down (Q, Ṭb; see 16:102) — or the capacity for understanding that God grants to those to whom He grants knowledge (Ka). The phrase "from Our Command" (*min amrinā*) ties the Spirit to the divine creative command — the *amr* by which God creates and decrees — and the commentators note the resonance with the Qur'an's description of the spirit's mystery:

> *"And they ask thee about the spirit. Say: The spirit is of the Command of my Lord; and you have been given but little knowledge."* (Qur'an 17:85)

Whether the Spirit is the Book, the prophethood, or the angel, the verse's point is the same: the revelation is not a human production but a divine breathing — life from the Command of the Lord, entering the dead heart as the spirit enters the dead body, and raising it to the life that knows, loves, and obeys its Maker.

**"Thou Knewest Not What Scripture Was, Nor Faith": The Unlettered Prophet**

The verse's next clause is the proof of the revelation's divine origin, drawn from the Prophet's own biography: "Thou knewest not what scripture was, nor faith." The commentators unfold the clause's argument: before the revelation, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ had no knowledge of the scriptures — he had not read the Torah nor the Gospel, had not studied with the rabbis or the monks — and he had not the faith that the revelation would bring, in its full form, until the Spirit descended upon him. The verse's companion states the same fact as the answer to every charge of borrowing or fabrication:

> *"And thou didst not recite before it any scripture, nor didst thou write one with thy right hand; otherwise the falsifiers would have doubted."* (Qur'an 29:48)

The argument is the argument from the unlettered messenger that the Qur'an repeats in several forms: a man who had never read a scripture could not have composed a Book that confirmed the scriptures, corrected their distortions, and surpassed their eloquence — the knowledge that the Qur'an displays of the prophets' stories, the divine law, and the unseen is not the accumulation of an unlettered merchant's life in Makkah but the gift of the One who revealed the Spirit. The clause thereby answers the Quraysh's charge of fabrication (v. 24) with the Prophet's own biography: the man who "knew not what scripture was, nor faith" could not have produced the scripture and the faith that the revelation brought — the knowledge came with the Book, and the Book's coming is the proof of its Source.

**"But We Made It a Light": The Revelation's New Creation**

The verse's central promise follows the proof: "But We made it a light whereby We guide whomsoever We will among Our servants." The commentators note the shift of pronoun — the verse moves from "We have revealed" to "We made it a light" — and read the clause as the revelation's continuing work: the Book is not only a message delivered but a light kindled, and the light is the instrument of the divine guidance. The Qur'an uses the image of light for revelation and faith throughout (see 6:91; 7:157; 21:48; 57:9, 28; 64:8):

> *"O People of the Book! There has come unto you Our Messenger, making clear unto you much of what you concealed of the Book, and pardoning much. There has come unto you from God a light and a manifest Book. Thereby God guides whosoever follows His good pleasure unto the ways of peace, and brings them forth from darkness into light by His Leave, and guides them unto a straight path."* (Qur'an 5:15–16)

The light's property is guidance: the light does not compel the seeing — it makes the path visible, and the seeing is the servant's — but the verse adds the divine initiative: "whereby We guide whomsoever We will among Our servants" — the guidance is God's act, the light is His instrument, and the servants whom He guides are those whose turning (v. 13) prepared them for the light's work. The commentators note the phrase "among Our servants": the guidance of the light is not the possession of a nation or a class but the gift of the Lord to His servants — the servants being, as verse 19 established, all who stand in the relation of servanthood to Him.

**The Prophet's Guiding: The Straight Path Named**

The verse's final clause carries the Prophet's office in relation to the light: "Truly thou dost guide unto a straight path." The commentators are careful with the verse's theology, for the Qur'an elsewhere states the boundaries of the Prophet's guidance:

> *"Surely thou dost not guide whomsoever thou lovest, but God guides whomsoever He will. And He knows best those who are rightly guided."* (Qur'an 28:56)

The resolution, which the commentators state (Ṭ; see also 13:7), is that the Prophet's guiding is the guiding of the caller and the pointer: he guides insofar as he is the messenger appointed to call a particular people to God and to function as the means of the divine guidance — he shows the path, summons to the path, and leads the community upon it, while the guidance that reaches the heart is God's alone. The straight path that the Prophet guides unto is the path that the sūrah's first audience knew from the Fātiḥah's daily prayer — *al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* (1:6), the path of the prophets and the righteous — and the verse's closing clause thereby joins the chapter's revelation-theology to the believer's daily worship: the Spirit was revealed, the light was kindled, the Prophet guides — and the servant prays, in every unit of the prayer, to be guided upon the path that the Prophet points to and that only God can open. The commentators add the spiritual reading of the verse's sequence for the believer's own life: the soul that was dead in ignorance receives the Spirit's life through the Book; the mind that "knew not what scripture was, nor faith" receives the light through the revelation; and the walking that follows the light, upon the straight path, is the walking of the servant whom God has guided among His servants — the walking that the sūrah's final verse will complete by naming the path's Owner and its end.

## Sūrah al-Shūrā 42:53

> **the path of God, unto Whom belongs whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth. Behold! All affairs are journeying unto God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Path Named by Its Owner**

The verse opens by naming the path that the previous verse's final clause pointed to: *ṣirāṭi Allāh* — "the path of God." The commentators note the verse's grammar: the straight path of verse 52 is now defined by its Owner — the path of God is the path that leads to God, the way whose starting point is the servant's response and whose destination is the Lord Himself. The Qur'an's descriptions of the path's content supply the definition: the path of God is the religion of Islam in its full sense — the submission that the sūrah has described across its length, the response to the Lord (v. 38), the upholding of the religion prescribed to Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad (v. 13), the shunning of the grave sins (v. 37), the counsel and the spending (v. 38), the patience and the forgiveness (v. 43). The path is single because its Owner is single — the Qur'an speaks of the paths of the false gods that lead away from God, and of the one path that leads to Him:

> *"And this is My straight path, so follow it; and follow not the [other] paths, lest they scatter you from His path."* (Qur'an 6:153)

> *"Verily God is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him. This is a straight path."* (Qur'an 3:51)

**The Ownership Clause: The Path's Security**

The verse's second clause repeats the sūrah's opening refrain in its final occurrence: "unto Whom belongs whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth." The refrain that opened the chapter's frame in verse 4 now closes it — the chapter is clasped between the two statements of the divine ownership — and the commentators note the fitness of the repetition: the path of God is the only path whose entire terrain belongs to its Owner; every other path runs through territory that belongs to Another, and the traveler on the false path is a traveler on borrowed ground. The ownership clause secures the path's travelers in three directions. It secures their provision: the path of God runs through the world that God owns, and the Provider who owns the heavens and the earth (v. 12) provides for the travelers upon His path (v. 19). It secures their protection: the path of God runs through the world whose powers are God's creatures, and no power of the owned world can finally harm the traveler who walks in the Owner's path (vv. 6, 31). And it secures their destination: the path of God ends at the God who owns all things — the traveler who walks the path of God is walking toward the Owner of everything he passes, and nothing that he leaves behind on the path is lost, because everything belongs to the One toward Whom he travels.

**"All Affairs Are Journeying Unto God": The Sūrah's Final Word**

The verse — and the sūrah — ends with the sentence that gathers the whole chapter into its conclusion: *alā ilā Allāhi taṣīru al-umūr* — "Behold! All affairs are journeying unto God." The commentators note the verse's finality: the word *taṣīr* — journeying, returning, ending — describes the direction of every affair in existence: the affairs of the heavens and the earth, the affairs of the nations and the individuals, the affairs that human beings disputed (v. 10) and the affairs they concealed (v. 24), the harvests they sowed (v. 20) and the deeds their hands earned (v. 30) — all of them are in motion toward God, and their motion is not an accident but their nature. The Qur'an uses the same image to close its arguments throughout:

> *"And unto God are all matters returned."* (Qur'an 2:210; 3:109; 8:44; 11:123; 22:76; 35:4; 57:5)

The verse's opening particle — *alā* — is the Qur'an's call of attention: "Behold!" — the summons to wakefulness that precedes the most important announcements, and its use here marks the verse as the sūrah's verdict. The commentators unfold the sentence's content in its two dimensions (Ṭ, R). It is the statement of the Resurrection: all human affairs will be brought before God in the Hereafter, and He will judge between the people with justice — the Day of Gathering that the sūrah announced in verse 7, that the believers were wary of in verse 18, and that the wrongdoers will meet in verses 44–45. And it is the statement of the divine sole judgeship: there is no judge other than God (R) — the judgment of every difference belongs to Him (v. 10), and the affairs that journey unto Him journey unto the only court whose verdicts are final.

**The Sūrah's Completed Circle**

The final verse completes the sūrah's architecture with a precision that the commentators have long admired. The chapter opened with the mysterious letters (vv. 1–2) and the revelation's unity (v. 3), followed by the ownership of the heavens and the earth (v. 4); it closes with the revelation's final mode (vv. 51–52), followed by the ownership of the heavens and the earth (v. 53). Between the two frames, the chapter has argued the whole of its case: the religion is one (v. 13), the division came from envy (v. 14), the harvests are two (v. 20), the believers are a people of response, prayer, counsel, spending, forgiveness, and patience (vv. 36–43), the wrongdoers have no protector (vv. 44–46), the response must come before the Day (v. 47), and the revelation that guides to the path is the Spirit from the divine Command (v. 52) — and now the circle closes where it opened: everything belongs to God, and all affairs are journeying unto God. The sūrah that took its name from the believers' counsel among themselves ends with the counsel's ultimate reference: the community that decides its affairs by mutual consultation (v. 38) is the community that knows the final destination of all its affairs — the God to Whom the deliberations, the deeds, the disputes, and the journeys are all returning.

**The Last Word to the Reader**

The verse's final word to every reader is the sūrah's permanent summons. "Behold! All affairs are journeying unto God": the affairs that seem most firmly in human hands are in transit — the wealth is journeying, the youth is journeying, the power is journeying, the life itself is journeying — and the only question is the manner of the journey's end: whether the affairs arrive at God as the deeds of the servants who responded (v. 38), or as the records of the wrongdoers who turned away (v. 48); whether the traveler arrives upon the path of God (v. 53) or upon the paths that scattered him from it (6:153). The sūrah that began with letters that human beings cannot fully read ends with a sentence that every human being can fully understand: everything is moving toward God, and the movement is the mercy — for the affairs that journey unto God journey unto the Judge who is also the Protector (v. 28), the Kind (v. 19), the Accepter of Repentance (v. 25), and the Forgiving, the Thankful (v. 23). The believer who closes the chapter with its final verse has been given the whole of its wisdom in one sentence: walk the path of God while the walking is still possible, counsel with your brothers while the counsel can still guide, forgive while the forgiveness can still be granted, and journey — consciously, willingly, gratefully — toward the God to Whom all affairs are journeying anyway. The affairs are going to God whether the servant walks or is carried; the believer's art is to walk, and the sūrah's last word is the destination that makes the walking light: unto God — Whose path is straight, Whose ownership is total, and Whose reception of the journeying affairs is the mercy that the chapter announced at its heart and sealed at its end.
