# Sūrah al-Aḥqāf (Chapter 46) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last of the Ḥāʾ Mīm Sūrahs**

Sūrah al-Aḥqāf takes its name from the sand dunes — *al-aḥqāf* — mentioned in verse 21, where the Arabian prophet Hūd stood to warn his people, the tribe of ʿĀd. It is the forty-sixth chapter of the Qur'an, thirty-five verses in length, and it closes a solemn family of seven sūrahs that open with the paired letters *Ḥāʾ Mīm*: al-Ghāfir (40), Fuṣṣilat (41), al-Shūrā (42), al-Zukhruf (43), al-Dukhān (44), al-Jāthiyah (45), and al-Aḥqāf (46). The early generations of Qur'anic readers revered this group so deeply that they spoke of them by a single affectionate name — *al-Ḥawāmīm* — and a saying reported from the righteous forebears captures their esteem:

> *"Everything has an ornament, and the ornament of the Qur'an is the Ḥawāmīm."* (reported in the classical tafsīr literature, attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās; its chain is debated, but the saying circulated widely among the early readers)

Whatever one makes of that attribution, the placement of al-Aḥqāf at the end of the series is itself instructive: after six chapters that alternately unveil God's majesty, expose the emptiness of idolatry, and rehearse the fate of nations, the seventh concludes with a single, quiet command — *be patient, as the resolute among the messengers were patient* (46:35). The Ḥawāmīm end not in thunder but in endurance.

**A Late Makkan Revelation in the Shadow of Rejection**

Most of the sūrah belongs to the final phase of the Makkan period — by the majority reckoning, revealed around two years before the Hijrah, close in time to Sūrah al-Jinn (72), whose story it anticipates. This dating matters enormously for understanding its mood. The Prophet ﷺ had now preached for more than a decade. The Quraysh had heard the recitation countless times; they had witnessed its effect on slaves, youth, merchants, and nobles; and their leadership had settled, with eyes open, into organized rejection. They called the revelation sorcery, fabrication, and "fables of the ancients." The early believers had endured boycott, mockery, and loss. This is the atmosphere the sūrah breathes: it is a chapter addressed to a community whose patience is nearly spent, and its answer to that exhaustion is not a promise of quick victory but a longer memory — Hūd before them, and the Day of Recompense after them.

Some commentators held the whole sūrah to be Makkan, while others identified individual verses — verse 10 by broad agreement, and some added verses 15 or 35 — as belonging to the Madinan period; Ibn ʿAbbās himself, as al-Qurṭubī reports, is among those whose views were canvassed on this question. The classical method was comfortable with a Makkan sūrah containing a Madinan seam, for revelation follows God's wisdom, not a compiler's folder system.

**The Architecture of the Chapter**

The sūrah unfolds in deliberate movements. It opens with the mystery of *Ḥāʾ Mīm* and the declaration that this Book is sent down from the Mighty, the Wise (vv. 1–2), then grounds that claim in the purposefulness of creation itself (v. 3). From there it stages a courtroom drama: the idols are summoned and found to have created nothing, owned nothing, and testified to nothing (vv. 4–6); the Quraysh's counter-charges — sorcery, fabrication — are dismantled one by one (vv. 7–9); and a witness from the Children of Israel steps forward to confirm the case (v. 10). The social sneer "the lowly outstripped us to it" (v. 11) is answered by the continuity of revelation (v. 12), and the portrait of the believer — one who says "our Lord is God" and stands firm — is set beside the portrait of the arrogant son who scolds the very parents who beg him to believe (vv. 13–17). Judgment scenes follow (vv. 18–20), and then the sūrah turns to its centerpiece: Hūd and ʿĀd among the sand dunes, the mocking challenge, the deceptive cloud, and the annihilating wind (vv. 21–25). The ruins of the nations around Makkah are cited as visible evidence (vv. 26–28), the jinn who heard the Qur'an at Nakhlah model the response the Quraysh refused to give (vv. 29–32), the argument for resurrection is sealed (vv. 33–34), and the chapter closes by commanding the Prophet — and every believer after him — to wear the patience of the resolute messengers (v. 35).

**Why This Sūrah Speaks to Every Age**

Stripped to its skeleton, al-Aḥqāf is a study in how people resist truth and what that resistance costs. It dissects, with almost clinical patience, the whole repertoire of denial: the demand for proof one never intends to accept, the appeal to social class as a test of truth, the accusation of borrowing from other teachers, the mockery that mistakes God's patience for God's absence. And against that repertoire it sets a different set of witnesses — a wind that answered a prayer for rain, a band of jinn who needed only one recitation to say "we have heard a Book," and a prophet who confessed "knowledge lies with God alone." For the modern reader, the sūrah functions as a mirror: nearly every argument hurled at revelation in seventh-century Makkah is still hurled today, wearing newer clothes; and nearly every answer the Qur'an gives is still complete, still patient, and still waiting to be understood.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:1

> **Ḥāʾ. Mīm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Letters Standing at the Door of the Final Ḥawāmīm**

The last of the seven *Ḥawāmīm* opens with the same two letters that opened the six before it: *Ḥāʾ Mīm*, recited by their names, standing alone as a complete utterance before any sentence begins. These are among the *al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*, the "disconnected letters," which appear at the head of twenty-nine sūrahs of the Qur'an. From the first generation, the community treated them with a specific reverence: they recited them exactly as they received them, without inventing expansions, without speculating aloud into doctrine, and without smoothing them away. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib is reported to have said that "every book has a quintessence (*ṣafwah*), and the quintessence of this Book is its spelled-out letters" — a saying already cited in the commentary on al-Baqarah's opening (2:1c), and worth recalling here because al-Aḥqāf is the very last place in the Qur'an where this particular pair, Ḥāʾ Mīm, will ever greet the reciter.

**The Majority Position: Silence Before Divine Knowledge**

Al-Ṭabarī, after surveying the many proposed interpretations of the paired letters, delivers the verdict that became the mainstream position of Sunni exegesis: the safest and most truthful statement about the disconnected letters is that their full meaning is known to God — or to God and those firmly rooted in knowledge — and that reciting them with faith while entrusting their secret to God is itself an act of devotion. Ibn Kathīr echoes this restraint. Ibn Taymiyyah, in his treatises on the foundations of tafsīr, adds a subtle point: the fact that the Prophet ﷺ recited these letters in prayer, and the Companions received them without demanding explanation, demonstrates that the Qur'an contains an accepted dimension of divine speech whose function operates even where human comprehension does not fully reach — sound and letter consecrated before meaning is parsed. This is not anti-intellectualism; it is a boundary marker. A revelation that begins by exceeding our analysis humbles the analyst at the threshold.

**The Challenge Reading: The Alphabet as Evidence**

The better-known interpretive stream, reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and developed across the centuries, holds that the letters are a deliberate provocation aimed at the Arab masters of eloquence. The reasoning is elegantly simple: *Ḥāʾ* and *Mīm* are not foreign glyphs; they are two of the twenty-eight letters with which every Arabic poet, orator, and soothsayer of the Quraysh composed everything they had ever said. When a Book opens by isolating the raw material of their own art and then proceeds to produce discourse that none of them can approach, the letters become Exhibit A in a courtroom argument: *here is your own alphabet — now produce even one sūrah like what follows, if you are truthful*. The falsification test is stated elsewhere in the Qur'an in precisely these terms:

> *"Say: If mankind and the jinn gathered together to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like of it, even if they were to one another helpers."* (al-Isrāʾ 17:88)

On this reading, Ḥāʾ Mīm at the head of al-Aḥqāf is not decoration; it is the drawn curtain before the evidence, a silence in which the challenge is felt before it is voiced.

**A Structural Key to the Series**

Some classical readers — and many modern students of the Qur'an's architecture after them — noticed that the seven Ḥāʾ Mīm sūrahs function as a connected suite, with recurring refrains: God's sending down of the Book, the vanity of idols, the scenes of the Day of Judgment, and the fate of the ancient nations. Al-Aḥqāf, as the finale, gathers all of these threads and cuts them: the idolatry theme concludes with the idols forsaking their worshippers (46:28), the revelation theme with the jinn's testimony (46:29–30), and the judgment theme with the Fire's terrible question, "Is this not true?" (46:34). Read this way, Ḥāʾ Mīm here is less a fresh opening than the final chord of a seven-movement symphony, resolving the tension the previous six sūrahs had sustained. Whether one holds to the esoteric silence of al-Ṭabarī or the rhetorical challenge of Ibn ʿAbbās, both readings converge on one practical effect: the letters stop the reciter before the argument begins, ensuring that what follows is heard as speech from outside the human repertoire.

**What the Letters Do to the Listener**

There is a quietly psychological wisdom in beginning this way. A Makkan polytheist, hearing *Ḥāʾ Mīm*, was confronted with sound before claim — no god was named, no law was issued, no ancestor was insulted. The letters are, functionally, a neutral space into which any listener may enter without defenses raised. Only afterward does the sūrah unfold its argument. It resembles the practice of a wise teacher who begins a difficult lesson with a question or a strange sound, not to confuse, but to disarm attention from its habitual channels and gather it fresh. In an age of infinite distraction, the reciter of al-Aḥqāf still experiences this: the two letters are a breath's pause, a clearing of the throat of the world, before the eternal address resumes.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:2

> **The revelation of the Book from God, the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tanzīl: A Word That Answers an Unspoken Accusation**

The second verse is a single declarative sentence: *tanzīlu l-kitābi mina llāhi*, "the revelation of the Book is from God." The noun *tanzīl* is a *maṣdar* — a verbal noun — that can be read appositionally ("this is a revelation of the Book") or as a predicate of emphasis ("the coming down of this Book is from God"). Classical grammarians note the difference between *tanzīl*, the intensive form suggesting repeated, staged sending down, and *inzāl*, which can suggest a single descent. The Qur'an uses both: the Book was sent down in truth, and it was sent down piecemeal across roughly twenty-three years — a fact the sūrah is about to defend against the charge of fabrication. Ibn Kathīr reads the verse exactly this way: it is a preemptive announcement, placed before the objections of the Quraysh are even voiced, that whatever they are about to say about this Book, its origin is not in doubt to the One who sent it.

**Why the Verse Opens with Might**

God names Himself here as *al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty, the Invincible — and *al-Ḥakīm*, the Wise. The pairing is deliberate and load-bearing. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both observe that revelation needs two credentials: power, to guarantee that what is revealed cannot be overpowered, corrupted, or intercepted; and wisdom, to guarantee that what is revealed is worth receiving. A message from a weak source would be unreliable; a message from a powerful but unwise source would be terrifying or useless. The Qur'an claims both in one breath. Al-Maybudī, in his Persian tafsīr preserving the preaching of the Sufis, adds a tender reading cited in the source commentary: *al-ʿAzīz* may also be understood as an active participle, "the One who makes mighty," so that the name proclaims that God has already honored the believers:

> *"The name means that He exalted the believers by making them the object of His address and worthy of His book and message."* (al-Maybudī, commenting on this verse, as cited in the source)

What the world saw as a despised handful following an orphan of ʿAbd Allāh was, in the ledger of heaven, the most honored assembly on earth — singled out as the recipients of the speech of the Mighty.

**A Refrain That Binds the Ḥawāmīm**

Nearly identical openings occur at the head of al-Zumar (39:1), Ghāfir (40:2), and al-Jāthiyah (45:2): *the revelation of the Book is from God*. This repetition across the Ḥawāmīm family is not redundancy; it is the refrain of a long legal brief. Each sūrah in the series faces a different species of denial — denial of resurrection, denial of God's exclusivity, denial of the Prophet's truthfulness — and each returns to the same foundation stone: before we argue about *content*, settle the question of *origin*. Modern readers may recognize the structure: in any debate, if the provenance of the evidence is contested, no downstream conclusion can be reached. The Qur'an, across seven sūrahs, refuses to let provenance be the hijacked term.

**Logic Sealed by Character**

There is also a subtle apologetic in naming the Sender's attributes rather than merely the Sender. Anyone can claim "this is from God"; the claim is cheap. What the verse does instead is identify God by two qualities that the audience's own experience could test. The Quraysh knew themselves to be powerless before the shifts of the desert — drought, storm, death — so an ʿAzīz God was not a hypothesis but the ground of their survival. And they knew that their own sages, when they spoke at all wisely, spoke in scattered aphorisms, not in a coherent law of life and Hereafter. The Book in their hands displayed a wisdom of a different order: legislation, history, theology, and liturgy in one seamless voice. The verse thus invites a simple comparison — measure the might you already acknowledge, and the wisdom you can already detect, against this Book, and ask whether it speaks like them or like us. The question answers itself for any who hear it without armor.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:3

> **We did not create the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them, save in truth and for a term appointed. Yet those who disbelieve turn away from that of which they were warned.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation with a Purpose, Not a Pastime**

The argument of the sūrah now widens from the Book to the cosmos: *We did not create the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them save in truth* — *mā khalaqnā al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā illā bil-ḥaqq*. The phrase *bil-ḥaqq* ("in truth / with truth / for a true purpose") is the Qur'an's great corrective to the idea that the universe is an accident, a game, or a purposeless churn. The same assertion is repeated almost verbatim:

> *"We created not the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them save in truth, and for a term appointed."* (al-Ḥijr 15:85)

And in the third person:

> *"Did they not contemplate the dominion of the heavens and the earth and whatsoever God has created?"* (al-Aʿrāf 7:185, summarizing the same summons)

Al-Rāzī draws out the double sense of *in truth* recorded in the source: the world is created with precision and perfection — every atom fitted to its task — and its constitution is *just*, ordered by a moral lawgiver rather than a blind indifference. Ibn al-Jawbān (IJ) and the other commentators cited in the source note that the added phrase *and for a term appointed* (*wa-ajalin musammā*) points to the fixed duration of the world: creation, being other than God, necessarily has a beginning, and whatever has a beginning on a schedule has an ending on a schedule. The Qur'an states this transience elsewhere in unforgettable terms:

> *"All things perish, save His Face."* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28:88)

**The Appointed Term: A Doctrine with Consequences**

The *ajal musammā* — the named, decreed term — is a pillar of Islamic ʿaqīdah with immediate psychological effects. It means that history is not open-ended chaos: individuals have their spans, nations have their spans, and the world itself has its span, each known precisely to its Creator. Al-Qurṭubī connects the phrase both to the end of the heavens and the earth and, as the source notes, to the Final Judgment beyond it; the two are one arc. The believer who internalizes this lives, in the famous words of the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"Be in this world as if you were a stranger or a passer-by."* (al-Bukhārī 6416, from Ibn ʿUmar)

Ibn ʿUmar, from whom this counsel is transmitted, used to complete the thought: if you survive to the evening, do not await the morning, and if you survive to the morning, do not await the evening; take from your health for your sickness and from your life for your death. This is not morbidness; it is scheduling. A traveler who knows the departure time packs differently from one who imagines the inn is his home.

**The Strange Turn of Those Who Should See Most**

The verse ends with a jolt: *yet those who disbelieve turn away from that of which they were warned* — *aʿriḍū*, they swerved aside, the verb of deliberately rotating one's face from a thing. Al-Rāzī notes the reading recorded in the source: they turned away from *understanding the true nature of creation* — they declined to reflect on the signs and on the world's ephemerality. The logic of the rebuke deserves attention. The verse does not say the disbelievers lacked evidence; it says they turned away *from being warned*, from a message already delivered. Reflection on the heavens and the earth was available to every tent-dweller who watched the stars wheel nightly; what was missing was not data but willingness. In this the verse anticipates a permanent feature of human psychology: denial is rarely an intellectual verdict reached after inquiry; more often it is a posture adopted before inquiry, and the arguments are recruited afterward as counsel for the defense.

**Why Purposefulness Matters Morally**

There is a deeper coherence between this verse and everything the sūrah will later say about judgment. If the world had no truth-structure and no term, then Hūd's warning, the wind over the dunes, the Fire of 46:34, and the patience commanded in 46:35 would all be theater. Purpose and deadline are precisely what give moral urgency to life: a universe with a term is a universe with an accounting. Conversely, a person who truly believes both — that the cosmos is meaningful and that his own term is written — finds that recklessness loses its charm. The verse is thus the quiet foundation stone of the entire chapter: before the sūrah argues about messengers, idols, and resurrection, it establishes the courtroom in which those arguments matter. Modern life, which often treats time as an infinite scroll, is being gently corrected here: the scroll has an end, and the scrolling is being read.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:4

> **Say, "Have you considered that which you call upon apart from God? Show me that which they have created of the earth. Or do they have a share in the heavens? Bring me a book before this or some vestige of knowledge, if you are truthful."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God's Own Cross-Examination of Idolatry**

Verse 4 is one of the Qur'an's courtroom summations, and its rhetorical form is extraordinary: God dictates the questions His Prophet must put to the accusers. *Say* — the command makes the Prophet, and every believer after him, an attorney in a case God Himself has already argued. Three demands are stacked in sequence, each more devastating than the last. First: *show me what they have created of the earth* — even one clod, one valley, one spring? The challenge is deliberately minimal. The Qur'an does not ask the idolaters to demonstrate the idols' management of galaxies; it asks for a single artifact of earthly dust bearing their maker's mark. Second: *or do they have a share in the heavens?* — the word is *shirk*, the very noun the Qur'an uses for polytheism itself: do they hold partnership, co-ownership, a percentage in the skies? The choice of word is surgical, for it forces the hearer to notice that his religion consists of assigning shares in God's property without a deed of title. Third: *bring me a book before this, or some vestige of knowledge* — the demand for documentary evidence.

**The Meaning of "a Vestige of Knowledge"**

The phrase *athāratin min al-ʿilm* provoked rich discussion among the exegetes, as the source records. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī offer the main lines: *a book revealed before this* — any scripture authorizing the worship of these beings; or *some remnant of the teachings of those of old* — an inherited doctrine with a traceable chain; or, more broadly, *any authentic transmitted report* establishing that these idols are genuine partners of God. The root of *atharah* carries the sense of a footprint, a track, a residue left behind by something that passed — which gives the phrase a haunting quality: the idolaters cannot produce even a footprint of knowledge for their theology. Ibn ʿAbbās, in reports cited by al-Ṭabarī, links the demand to the total absence of any revealed warrant for shirk. And the Qur'an presses the same point rhetorically elsewhere:

> *"Or did We give them a Book before this, such that they stand upon a clear proof from it?"* (Fāṭir 35:40)

**A Falsifiable Challenge — from God**

What makes this verse remarkable in the history of religious argument is that it is framed as a falsification test. The Qur'an repeatedly invites its opponents to satisfy a condition that would vindicate them: produce a book, produce knowledge, produce one created thing. Al-Baqarah 2:23, Yūnus 10:38, al-Anbiyāʾ 21:24, al-Qamar 54:43–44, and al-Qalam 68:37 all deploy the same structure. This is not the language of a system that fears scrutiny; it is the language of a system that has staked itself on evidence and challenges the opposition to bring theirs. In modern terms, the verse identifies the burden of proof and places it where it belongs: on whoever asserts that these beings deserve worship. The Muslim who memorizes verse 4 possesses, compressed into a few clauses, an entire method for evaluating religious claims — including, honestly applied, any claim made in the name of Islam itself: What did this source create? What share does it hold? What revealed or transmitted evidence backs it?

**The Psychology of Gods Made Convenient**

Beneath the legal form of the verse lies a psychological insight. The idols of the Quraysh — Hubal, al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt, and the host of others — were not conclusions the Arabs had reasoned toward; they were inheritances that flattered social custom and licensed festivals. They asked nothing, corrected nothing, and threatened nothing; worship that costs nothing and demands no moral transformation is religiously cheap and psychologically comfortable. The Qur'an's three questions strip the comfort away: a being that created nothing you can point to, owns no share of the sky, and cannot produce one line of revealed authorization is not a god; it is a habit with a shrine. Ibn Taymiyyah, analyzing the logic of worship across his writings, pressed the same point: worship is only owed to what possesses perfect benefit and harm, and that possession belongs to the Creator alone. The verse therefore performs, in miniature, what the whole sūrah will do with the story of ʿĀd: it shows that idolatry is not an alternative theology so much as an unexamined relationship — and that examination, once honestly begun, dissolves it.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:5

> **Who is more astray than one who calls, apart from God, upon such as do not answer him, [even] unto the Day of Resurrection, and who are heedless of their calling.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Qur'an's Superlative of Misguidance**

The verse opens with a rhetorical structure the Qur'an reserves for the gravest verdicts: *wa-man aḍallu* — "and who is more astray?" — a superlative question whose expected answer is "no one." The same construction is used elsewhere to crown the greatest wrongdoers:

> *"And who does greater wrong than one who fabricates a lie against God?"* (al-Anʿām 6:21)

Here the superlative is pinned to a precise act: *calling upon* (*yadʿū*) something besides God. Duʿāʾ — invocation, supplication, calling upon — is in Islamic teaching the marrow of worship itself; the Prophet ﷺ said, as al-Tirmidhī records, that *duʿāʾ is worship*. To direct the heart's most essential act toward an address that cannot receive it is, in the Qur'an's estimation, not one error among many but the very summit of going astray, because it misplaces the one gesture that defines servanthood.

**Three Failures in One Sentence**

The verse assigns the false objects of worship three disabilities, each more humiliating. They *do not answer* — *lā yastajību lahu* — not sometimes, not slowly, but *unto the Day of Resurrection*: the guarantee of eternal silence is built into the sentence. They are *heedless of their calling* — *ghāfilūn* — the word the Qur'an most often uses for humanity's own negligence, turned around here upon the idols: the worshipper is attentive to a god who is not attentive to him. And, as the next verse will add, when the great gathering comes, these same gods will turn *hostile*. Al-Qurṭubī records the two readings the source notes: the heedless ones may be the idols (oblivious even of being invoked), or the idolaters themselves (oblivious that their invocations rise toward nothing). Both readings wound; together they describe a relationship in which neither side is actually present.

**A Simple Thought Experiment the Qur'an Demands**

The argument of the verse is accessible to anyone who has ever waited for a reply. Call a friend who loves you and your phone rings back within minutes. Call a bureaucrat and you may wait a month. Call an idol — a statue, a star, a dead saint imagined as an independent power, a talisman — and the line never connects, not in a lifetime, not until the Resurrection. The Qur'an makes the silence of the idols a measurable fact of experience:

> *"Indeed those you call upon apart from God will never create a fly, even if they gathered together for it..."* (al-Ḥajj 22:73)

> *"Say: Have you considered your partners whom you invoke apart from God? Show me what they created of the earth — or have they a share in the heavens?"* (al-Aḥqāf 46:4, the preceding verse)

Modern psychology would describe such one-directional devotion as a parasitic relationship: all output, no input. The Qur'an had already diagnosed it fourteen centuries ago, and its diagnosis extends beyond stone idols to anything modern hearts set up as ultimate — wealth, status, ideologies, even one's own desires, which hear every prayer and grant nothing that lasts.

**Heedlessness as the Mirror Image of Faith**

There is a final symmetry worth savoring. The Qur'an's portrait of the believer is of one who *remembers* God constantly, whose heart is present when it calls; the portrait of the idolater in this verse is of one who calls upon the *ghāfil* — the absent, the unaware — and who may himself be ghāfil about the futility of his own act. Faith is a relationship of mutual presence: the Servant knows his Lord hears, and the Lord answers in His wisdom and timing — sometimes by granting, sometimes by deflecting harm, sometimes by storing the reward. Ibn Kathīr remarks that God's answers to the believer's duʿāʾ are guaranteed in one of three ways, as the Prophet ﷺ stated in the hadith of the one whose supplication is answered, or its harm averted, or its equivalent stored for the Hereafter — a report al-Tirmidhī transmits and declares ḥasan. Against that tripartite generosity stands the idol's triple silence: no answer, no deflection, no store. The believer's phone call is always received; the idolater's is a letter mailed to an address that does not exist, and the Day of Resurrection will reveal the entire post office of shirk to have been empty all along.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:6

> **When mankind is gathered, such will be enemies unto them and deniers of their worship.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reversal Every Idolater Dreads**

Verse 5 described the idols' silence in this world; verse 6 describes their speech in the next — and the first thing they will do with their newly granted voice is disown their worshippers. *When mankind is gathered* — *idhā ḥushira al-nāsu* — refers, as the commentators unanimously hold, to the Day of Judgment, when the generations are assembled for reckoning; the Qur'an uses the same gathering language at 42:7. The scene is a radical inversion of everything the idolater counted on. In this life, the relationship ran one way: he fed the shrine, sang to the idol, named his children after it, and trusted it to intercede for him before God. On that Day, the direction of loyalty flips: *they will be enemies to them* — *aʿdāʾan* — the plural of *ʿadūw*, enemy, the word for open, avowed hostility. And beyond hostility, *deniers of their worship*: *kāfirīn bi-ʿibādatihim* — literally "disbelievers in their worship," the strongest verb of repudiation the Arabic language affords.

**The Testimony Already Scripted**

The Qur'an is remarkably consistent about what the false objects of worship will say at the reckoning, and the source commentary cites the key passage from Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ:

> *"Our Lord! These are those whom we perverted. We perverted them as we ourselves were perverse. We disavow them before Thee; it was not us whom they worshipped."* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28:63)

Whether the speakers are the idols themselves or, as many exegetes hold, the satans and leaders who whispered the worship into being, the plea is identical: *we never asked for this; release us from their credit*. Sūrah Maryam renders the same betrayal in terms of misplaced hope:

> *"And they have taken gods apart from God, that they might be for them a source of strength. Nay! They will deny their worship and become opponents against them."* (Maryam 19:81–82)

And Sūrah Yā Sīn completes the picture of their impotence:

> *"They cannot help them, nor can they help themselves."* (Yā Sīn 36:43)

Al-Qurṭubī adds, as the source records, the deeper causality: the idols are their enemies *because calling upon them was the very cause of their destruction* — the "friendship" was the wound. This is the shattering realization of the Day: the thing you leaned on was the knife.

**Loyalty Built on Need Collapses at the Reckoning**

The verse exposes a law of false attachment that operates already in this world, of which the Hereafter is only the unmasked version. Alliances founded on shared convenience — not truth — survive only while convenience lasts. The accomplice abandons the accomplice at the courtroom door; the flatterer evaporates when the patron's power does; the crowd that thronged the powerful man's gate disperses when the sentence is read. The Qur'an informs us that the oldest false alliance of all — humanity's compact with its invented gods — will follow the same pattern on the grandest scale. And this is not caprice on the idols' part; it is the logic of what they are. A being that had no power to help also has no loyalty to keep, and no bond of truth binds it to its worshippers when truth itself is the standard of that Day.

**A Mercy Disguised as a Warning**

For the believer, this verse is one of the most tender in the sūrah precisely because it is frightening. God is showing His servants, in advance, the receipt of a transaction He longs for them to cancel while cancellation is still possible. Every person who today feels the pull of some substitute for God — an attachment he half-knows is false — is being handed the ending of that story early, the way a father shows his child the burn scar before the child touches the stove. Ibn Taymiyyah reflected in many places that God's warnings are themselves gifts: the doctor who describes the disease in detail is not cruel; he is the one who takes the illness seriously. And for those who spent their lives calling on God alone, the verse works in reverse as reassurance: their Lord, who answered them in this world with wisdom, will not disown them in the next. The one addressed in every prayer of the believer — *rabbanā* — will be the first to acknowledge His servants on the Day mankind is gathered.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:7

> **Yet when Our signs are recited unto them as clear proofs, those who disbelieve say to the truth when it comes to them, "This is manifest sorcery."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Phenomenon of Truth Arriving and Being Reclassified**

The verse describes a moment of contact: *Our signs are recited unto them as clear proofs* — *bayyināt*, self-evident clarifications, recited aloud so that the audience actually hears them. The tragedy is in what happens at the moment of contact: *those who disbelieve say to the truth when it comes to them: this is manifest sorcery*. Note the sequence — the truth *comes to them*; it does not remain distant or hypothetical. The Qur'an is testifying that its audience was not rejecting a rumor about revelation; they were rejecting the recitation itself, syllable by syllable, in their own tongue. And because they could not answer its content, they reclassified its category: not speech, but spell; not argument, but *siḥr*, sorcery — *mubīn*, manifest, flagrant. The accusation itself conceded defeat, for one does not need to call a thing sorcery unless it has already demonstrated an unaccountable power over hearts.

**An Old Charge with a Long Paper Trail**

The sorcery accusation was the reflexive defense of every entrenched establishment confronted with a messenger. The Qur'an notes that the same word was flung at Moses before Pharaoh's court — *then when the truth came to them from Us, they said: this is manifest sorcery* (10:76) — and at the messengers in general: *thus did there not come to them a messenger but they mocked him* (36:30). The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ received the identical verdict from the Quraysh leadership, and the Qur'an records their most revealing slip:

> *"And they say: this is nothing but sorcery imparted [from others] — nothing but the speech of a mortal."* (al-Muddaththir 74:24–25)

The phrase *sorcery imparted* — *siḥrun yuʿallam*, "magic that is taught" — was the confession of a cornered opponent, al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, who listened to the Prophet's recitation, felt its effect, and then searched his entire vocabulary for a negative label that would fit. The Qur'an answered the borrowing insinuation directly:

> *"And We know indeed that they say: it is only a mortal who teaches him. The speech of him they wickedly point to is foreign, while this is clear Arabic speech."* (al-Naḥl 16:103)

**Why "Sorcery" Is the Last Refuge of the Cornered Mind**

Psychologically, the verse documents what later eras would call motivated reasoning. When evidence cannot be refuted on its merits, the mind protecting a prior commitment has two exits: attack the source, or deny that it is evidence at all. Calling the Qur'an sorcery accomplished both — it admitted the Book's uncanny effect while filing that effect under "illegitimate manipulation," thereby excusing the listener from engaging it. It is the ancient ancestor of every modern label that ends a conversation without resolving it: "propaganda," "brainwashing," "demagoguery." The pattern is remarkably stable across time: first the elite listens in secret (as the Quraysh did by night at the Prophet's wall), then the effect becomes undeniable, then the category-attack is issued publicly. The Qur'an's diagnosis is that this is not an intellectual position but a *state* — *those who disbelieve* is a participle describing a settled condition that precedes and interprets the hearing, rather than resulting from it.

**The Believer's Consolation in the Same Pattern**

For the Prophet ﷺ and his followers, this verse was armor. It told them that the charge of sorcery was not evidence of their failure but of their message's power — the same badge Moses carried, the same badge the messengers before carried. Anyone today who presents the Qur'an sincerely will meet refined descendants of the same reflex: the recitation that moved a listener becomes "beautiful poetry," the transformation of a convert becomes "emotional vulnerability," the coherence of the Book becomes "clever compilation." In every case the underlying grammar is identical: acknowledge the effect, deny the source. The believer's task, learned from this verse, is patience with the reflex and persistence with the recitation — for the Qur'an also records that some who began by calling it sorcery ended by calling it light, and the hearts that mocked by day sometimes wept by night.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:8

> **Or do they say, "He has fabricated it"? Say, "Had I fabricated it, you would not be able to avail me aught against God. He knows best that about which you carry on. He suffices as a Witness between me and you, and He is the Forgiving, the Merciful."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From "Sorcery" to "Fabrication": The Shrinking Accusation**

Verse 7 recorded one counter-charge; verse 8 begins with another: *or do they say: he has fabricated it?* — *iftarāhu*, from the root f-r-y, to forge or invent, the technical verb for falsely attributing speech to God. The Quraysh cycled through accusations the way a losing attorney cycles through defenses: he is a poet, a soothsayer, a madman, a sorcerer — and when each collapsed, the final fallback was "he invented it himself." The Qur'an's confidence shines through the order of the list: fabrication is the *last* accusation, reached only after the supernatural quality of the Book had made all naturalistic charges embarrassing. Elsewhere God turns this last accusation into a challenge:

> *"Or do they say: he has fabricated it? Say: then produce ten forged sūrahs like it, and call upon whom you can apart from God, if you are truthful."* (Hūd 11:13)

**The Prophet's Courtroom Answer, Dictated by God**

The reply is commanded: *Say: had I fabricated it, you would not be able to avail me aught against God* — *falā tamlikūna lī mina llāhi shayʾan*. The logic deserves careful statement. The Prophet is made to say: if I have forged God's speech, then the offense is against God, and God needs neither your verdict nor your punishment; no power on earth could shield me from the One I had wronged, and none of your hostility adds anything to a case that the Almighty Himself would already be prosecuting. Ibn Kathīr reads the verse precisely this way, citing the parallel:

> *"For whomsoever God desires that he be tried, thou hast no power to avail him aught against God."* (al-Māʾidah 5:41)

The argument is airtight from within the Prophet's own preaching — and even from within the audience's own theology, since the Quraysh never denied that God existed or that He was the supreme Creator. Their own premises guaranteed that a forger of God's name could not flourish untouched for a single decade, let alone prevail, inherit Arabia, and die with his mission established. The Qur'an elsewhere points at the same protective logic: God shielded His Messenger even from the subtler temptation to soften the revelation under pressure:

> *"And they had nearly seduced you away from what We revealed to you... and then We would have made you taste double [punishment] in life and double in death, and you would have found no helper against Us."* (al-Isrāʾ 17:73–75)

**He Knows Best What You Utter**

The middle clause — *He knows best that about which you carry on* — uses a verb (*tufīḍūna* in the canonical reading; *tafīḍūna* in another) whose root connotes pouring out, gushing, running over: God knows best what your tongues pour forth in your councils about me. It is a quiet reminder that the entire trial is being transcribed. Then comes the acquittal that ends all appeals: *He suffices as a Witness between me and you* — *kafā bihi shahīdan*, the same formula the Qur'an applies at 13:43 and 29:52. In human courts, witnesses are multiplied precisely because any single witness may be deceived; God's witness needs no corroboration, and His testimony, though deferred to the Day of Judgment, is already entered into the record.

**Why the Verse Closes with Mercy**

The final pair of names is startling in context: *He is the Forgiving, the Merciful* — *al-Ghafūr, al-Raḥīm*. After an exchange of accusations, one expects a name of severity; instead the verse ends with an open door. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both draw out the implication: the door of repentance remains open even to those who called the Prophet a forger. The mention of forgiveness at exactly this juncture is God's own commentary on the quarrel: the dispute is not beyond repair; the accusers are being invited, mid-argument, to step back from the cliff. This is a signature of Qur'anic rhetoric — wrath announced with mercy appended, so that no one can claim the door was shut before they tried it. For the modern reader, the verse models both halves of prophetic conduct under attack: total confidence in God's vindication, and total willingness to forgive the attacker who turns back.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:9

> **Say, "I am no innovation among the messengers, and I know not what will be done with me or with you. I only follow that which has been revealed unto me, and I am naught but a clear warner."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"No Innovation Among the Messengers"**

The Prophet is commanded to introduce himself with a negation: *Say: I am no innovation among the messengers* — *mā kuntu bidʿan mina al-rusul*. The word *bidʿah*, from the root b-d-ʿ (to begin, to originate), means something without precedent, a novelty. The claim of Islam's Prophet is precisely the opposite of novelty: he is the last installment of the oldest message on earth. This single sentence does enormous theological work. It binds the Qur'an to the chain of previous revelations, it stakes the Prophet's credibility on the verdict of history rather than on personal charisma, and it deprives the Quraysh of the comfortable fiction that they were facing something unprecedented. The Prophet ﷺ stated the doctrine in the famous terms transmitted by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"The prophets are brothers of one father and different mothers; their mothers differ, and their religion is one."* (al-Bukhārī 3443; Muslim 2369, from Abū Hurayrah)

"Brothers of one father, different mothers" — one God, one core faith, differing laws and scriptures. Al-Tustarī, as the source records, drew from the verse the essence of that shared religion:

> *"There were messengers before me who commanded what I command and forbade what I forbid. I am not a marvel among the messengers. I do not call you to anything except testifying to God's Oneness (tawḥīd), and I do not guide you to anything other than the most noble character traits. It was with this [message] that the prophets before me were sent."* (al-Tustarī, as cited in the source)

**The Prophet Who Confesses the Limits of His Knowledge**

The next clause is one of the most strikingly humble sentences in the Qur'an: *I know not what will be done with me or with you* — *wa-mā adrī mā yufʿalu bī wa-lā bikum*. The messenger of God stands before his people and disclaims knowledge of the unseen, even concerning his own fate. This was a direct strike at the popular expectation of what a prophet should be — in the Arabian imagination, a *kāhin* (soothsayer) who could not tell you where your lost camel was, was no seer at all. The Qur'an makes the Prophet's honesty about his ignorance a credential rather than a liability, and elsewhere commands the same candor about the greatest unknown of all:

> *"They ask you about the Hour: when is its arrival? Say: its knowledge is only with my Lord; none can disclose it at its time but He."* (al-Aʿrāf 7:187)

Some early readers, as al-Qurṭubī notes, considered this clause abrogated by the later verse of victory — *that God may forgive thee thy sins that went before and that are to come* (48:2). But al-Qurṭubī himself, as the source records, resolves the tension without abrogation: this verse concerns the unfolding events of this world, whose outcomes God withheld even from His Prophet, while 48:2 concerns forgiveness and station with God — different questions, both true. The Prophet's later words confirm the harmony: he taught that no one's deeds alone will carry him to Paradise:

> *"None of you will be saved by his deeds alone... not even I, unless God covers me with His mercy."* (al-Bukhārī 6463; Muslim 2816, from Abū Hurayrah)

**A Messenger Whose Authority Is Revelation, Nothing Else**

*I only follow that which has been revealed unto me* — *in ittabiʿu illā mā yūḥā ilayya*. The phrase is the job description of prophethood: to follow, not to originate. The Prophet's personal preferences, moods, and judgments in worldly affairs were known to be his own — he famously corrected himself in matters like date-pollination, saying they knew their worldly affairs better — while in revelation he had no discretion to add or subtract. And the job title is given in the final clause: *naught but a clear warner* — *nadhīr mubīn*. The Qur'an assigns the Prophet the roles of *bashīr* and *nadhīr*, bearer of glad tidings and warner; here, in a sūrah steeped in the smoke of ʿĀd's destruction, the warner function is to the fore. In this the Prophet stands in Hūd's exact position, as verses 21–25 will show: a man from among the people, carrying a warning he cannot soften and cannot hasten.

**The Integrity of a Message That Claims Nothing for Itself**

There is a quiet apologetic in the verse's cumulative humility. Fabricators of religious movements, as history endlessly demonstrates, claim exemption — secret knowledge, guaranteed salvation, immunity from question. Here is a prophet who says: I am not new, I do not know my tomorrow, I follow what I am given, and I am only here to warn you. Each clause removes a prop that an impostor would have kept. What remains standing is the message itself — which is exactly where the Qur'an wants the listener's scrutiny to rest.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:10

> **Say, "Have you considered if it is from God and you disbelieve in it, though a witness from the Children of Israel bore witness to the like thereof, then believed in it, while you waxed arrogant? Surely God guides not wrongdoing people."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Structure of an Unanswerable Dilemma**

The verse is framed as a thought experiment with a fork: *have you considered — if it is from God, and you disbelieve in it, while a witness from the Children of Israel has already testified to its like and then believed...?* The sentence is left deliberately incomplete, the way a lawyer lets the jury finish the obvious conclusion. The Quraysh are asked to inhabit, for one moment, the hypothesis they refuse: that this Book really is from God. If it is, then their position is not neutral — it is the position of people contradicting a second, independent, older witness who examined the same class of evidence and reached the opposite verdict. The verse then names their real condition: *you waxed arrogant* — *istakbartum* — arrogance, not analysis, is the stated cause of their rejection. And the verse closes with the divine rule that governs such a case: *God guides not wrongdoing people*.

**The Identity of the Witness: ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām**

Most exegetes, as the source records, identify the witness as ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām — one of the leading Jewish scholars of Madinah, whose birth name was al-Ḥusayn and whom the Prophet ﷺ renamed ʿAbd Allāh upon his conversion. He was a man whose entire scholarly formation was the Torah; he knew revelation when he saw it, because he had spent his life inside one. Upon hearing the Prophet's description and recognizing him from his scriptural knowledge, he believed. Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ testified to the honor God gave him:

> *"I never heard the Prophet ﷺ say of anyone walking upon the earth that he is of the people of the Garden, except of ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām. And the following verse was revealed concerning him: 'a witness from the Children of Israel bore witness to the like thereof.'"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ)

His people's reaction is preserved in a report al-Bukhārī transmits: Ibn Salām advised the Prophet to ask the Jews about his standing *before* they learned of his conversion. When asked, they declared him their best and the son of their best; when he emerged and declared his faith, they immediately pronounced him the worst among them — a controlled experiment on testimony, with the variable changed mid-trial, and the result: partisanship, not truth, was driving their assessments. If, however, as other commentators hold, the sūrah's core is Makkan, the verse may instead allude to Moses himself or to a broader Israelite tradition of witnesses who testified to revelation's like — the grammar *ʿalā mithlihi* ("to the like of it") allows the witness to attest to the earlier scripture whose like the Qur'an is.

**A Book Inscribed Beforehand**

A further reading of *bore witness to the like thereof* — "bore witness to one like him" — understands the verse as an affirmation that the coming of an unlettered Arabian prophet was inscribed in previous scripture, in line with:

> *"...the unlettered prophet whom they find inscribed in the Torah and the Gospel that is with them."* (al-Aʿrāf 7:157)

On this reading, the witness testifies not merely that revelation of this kind exists, but that this very messenger was foretold. Classical Muslim scholarship, as the source notes, has long identified Biblical passages — such as Deuteronomy 18:18, "I will raise up for them a prophet like you from among their brothers" — as traces of that inscription. Muslims therefore read the verse as the Qur'an appealing to a documentary chain that pre-dates its own advent by centuries: the final Book arrives with references already filed in the archives of the earlier Books.

**Arrogance as the Decisive Variable**

The verse's final diagnosis — *you waxed arrogant* — explains why evidence that persuaded a learned Israelite bounced off the Quraysh nobility. The difference between the two was never intelligence; it was posture. Ibn Salām had standing to lose among his own people, and he lost it; the Quraysh leaders had standing to lose among theirs, and they protected it. Arrogance, in Qur'anic psychology, is not primarily an emotion of self-admiration but a social reflex: the refusal to be seen following what one's inferiors have already followed — which is precisely the objection voiced in the next verse, *had it been good, they would not have outstripped us to it*. God's rule *He guides not wrongdoing people* must be read carefully: it is not that God coercively withholds guidance as a punishment, but that a heart committed to arrogance has locked the very door guidance opens. As the Sufis said, water cannot enter a vessel sealed by pride. The verse thus leaves its hearers at a fork that is still open today: the same recitation that humbled a rabbi into faith will harden a nobleman into denial — not because it differs in their ears, but because they differ at the door.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:11

> **And those who disbelieve say of those who believe, "Had it been good, they would not have outstripped us in [attaining] it." Since they will not be guided by it, they will say, "This is an ancient perversion."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument from Social Class, Exposed in One Line**

The Quraysh now deploy what they considered their strongest argument: *had it been good, they would not have outstripped us in it.* The logic, stated plainly, is: truth announces itself first to the elite; whatever the elite spurn must be worthless. The first Muslims — Bilāl the freed Abyssinian, ʿAmmār and his parents the clients of Makkah, Ṣuhayb the Roman, Salmān the Persian, the poor of Makkah such as those mocked in another sūrah — were, by aristocratic accounting, evidence against the message rather than for it. The Qur'an records the sneer verbatim elsewhere:

> *"And thus We tried some of them by others, that they may say: Are these the ones whom God has graced among us? Is not God best aware of the thankful?"* (al-Anʿām 6:53)

The same gatekeeping was rehearsed at Noah's court — *we see none who follow you save the lowliest among us* (11:27) — and at the court of the messenger sent to Thamūd — *shall we believe you, when the lowliest follow you?* (26:111). Across millennia the argument never changes, because it was never an argument: it is caste wearing the costume of epistemology.

**Why God Sends Truth to the Lowly First**

The Qur'an's implicit answer to the sneer is devastating when unpacked. The first believers possessed exactly two qualifications that matter in revelation's court: unencumbered hearts and freedom from reputational investment. Wealth and lineage build no bridge to truth; if anything, they construct the very fortifications — status, clientele, inherited prestige — that truth must first breach. The Prophet ﷺ described the heavenly calculus in a report that inverts every aristocratic scale:

> *"Many a disheveled, dusty man, turned away from doors — if he were to swear by God, God would fulfill it."* (Muslim 2622, from Abū Hurayrah)

That is the divine economy in one sentence: the man the world would not admit through its gates is the man whose oath God honors. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have wept when reciting such verses, saying that this ummah's poor would enter Paradise centuries ahead of its rich — the last first and the first last. The social composition of early Islam was, in this light, not a weakness of the movement but its signature of authenticity: a message that recruited the unencumbered first was behaving precisely like truth, and precisely unlike a courtier's ideology, which always recruits the powerful first.

**The Final Fallback: "An Ancient Perversion"**

The verse then records the disbelievers' terminal position: *and since they will not be guided by it, they will say: this is an ancient perversion* — *ifkun qadīm*, an old lie, *asāṭīr al-awwalīn*, the fables of the ancients, in the phrase of the parallel verses (6:25; 8:31; 25:5). Al-Qurṭubī, as the source notes, reads the sequence as causal: *because they will not be guided by it*, they must categorize it away. The accusation is logically preposterous when examined: the Quraysh simultaneously claimed the Prophet fabricated the Book *and* that it was recycled legend — two charges that cancel each other unless one simply needs any label whatsoever. What the label reveals is exhaustion. When a man has rejected truth by class, and then cannot refute its content, the only remaining move is to file it under "discredited by time" without ever producing the discrediting. Modern parallels are abundant: ideas dismissed as "outdated" by people who have never read them; doctrines called "medieval" as though a date were an argument. The verse trains the believer to hear such phrases correctly — not as conclusions, but as the sound of a door closing from the inside.

**The Believer's Dignity Under the Sneer**

For the early community, this verse was a shield against humiliation. It told Bilāl and ʿAmmār that their poverty was not an accident of the mission's demographics but a badge of its truth; that they had "outstripped" the mighty precisely because nothing held them back from the race. Every believer since who has been mocked as unsophisticated for faith — the first in a family, the convert among colleagues, the young person in a skeptical classroom — stands in the line this verse honors: the vanguard, not the residue. And every scholar should note the warning embedded on the other side: attachment to status is the exact mechanism by which the Quraysh nobles damned themselves. The verse is therefore not merely history; it is a mirror asking each reader, in what social mirror does he check the truth before accepting it?

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:12

> **Yet before it there was the Book of Moses, a guide and a mercy. This is a Book that confirms in an Arabic tongue to warn wrongdoers, and a glad tiding for the virtuous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Archive, Many Volumes**

The verse answers the "ancient perversion" slur with a genealogy: *before it there was the Book of Moses, a guide and a mercy* — *imāman wa-raḥmatan*, a leader and a mercy. The Torah is honored here in terms Muslims should never forget: the Qur'an calls a prior scripture a *guide* and a *mercy*, words it uses for itself. This is the doctrine of the unity of revelation: God has maintained one moral archive across human history, with volumes in different languages, each *muṣaddiq* — confirming — what came before it. The Qur'an states its own relationship to that archive explicitly:

> *"And We have sent down unto thee the Book in truth, confirming the Book that came before it, and as a protector over it."* (al-Māʾidah 5:48)

From the Qur'anic perspective, the falsehood lies not in the older Books but in the allegation that God's final Book is a deviation from them; the deviation would be a religion *without* the chain. The source commentary preserves a remark of al-Dārimī that settles the question of language with beautiful simplicity:

> *"God knows all languages and speaks in whichever language He wishes: if He wishes, He speaks in Arabic; and if He wishes, He speaks in Hebrew; and if He wishes, in Syriac. So He has made the Qur'an His Word in Arabic."* (al-Dārimī, al-Radd, as cited in the source)

**Why an Arabic Tongue**

*This is a Book that confirms, in an Arabic tongue* — *lisānan ʿarabiyyan*. The phrase is not ethnic pride; it is pedagogy. Revelation must land inside a community's comprehension to be an argument addressed to it, as the Qur'an states as a universal principle:

> *"And We have sent no messenger save in the language of his people, that he might make clear to them."* (Ibrāhīm 14:4)

The same doctrine appears at 12:2 — *an Arabic Qur'an, that you might understand* — and 41:3 — *a Book whose signs have been detailed as an Arabic recitation for a people who know*. The claim embedded in *lisānan ʿarabiyyan* is quietly polemical: the Quraysh were the world's acknowledged masters of Arabic; the Book arrived in their own instrument at a pitch of mastery they could not match. Had it come in Hebrew, they would have had the excuse of foreignness. It did not, and they did not.

**The Dual Function: Warning and Glad Tidings**

The Book's mission statement closes the verse: *to warn wrongdoers, and a glad tiding for the virtuous* — *nudhur* for *alladhīna ẓalamū*, *bushrā* for *al-muḥsinīn*. Note the asymmetry of terminology: those who do evil are warned; those who do *beauty* — the root ḥ-s-n means to make beautiful — are given good news. Islamic anthropology in this verse is neither cynical nor sentimental. Human beings are capable of ẓulm — the darkening of things from their right places, the misuse of trust — and therefore need warning; and they are capable of iḥsān, the Beautifying, and therefore deserve glad tidings. A message addressed only with warnings would drive away the wounded; a message of only glad tidings would anesthetize the oppressor. The Qur'an is calibrated to the full human range, and every balanced preacher since has learned the calibration from verses like this one.

**The Mercy of a Verifiable Chain**

There is a final apologetic subtlety. By naming the Book of Moses as its predecessor, the Qur'an placed its own claims inside a checkable framework: its stories of Moses, its moral law, its monotheism could be compared, by any Jew or Christian resident in Arabia, with what those communities preserved. A fabricator inventing a synthetic religion avoids cross-examination; the Qur'an courts it. The verse thus completes the argument of verses 10–11: a learned Israelite witness recognized the family resemblance and believed; the Quraysh elite dismissed the first believers by class; and now the Book itself displays its pedigree — guide after guide, mercy after mercy, one God speaking Arabic this time to a people who had never before been addressed in revelation's own words. The "ancient perversion" label collapses: an old lie does not confirm the oldest truth; a new volume does.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:13

> **Truly those who say, "Our Lord is God," then stand firm, no fear shall come upon them; nor shall they grieve.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Sentence of Faith, Then a Lifetime of Standing**

The portrait of the believer is compressed into two movements: *those who say: our Lord is God, then stand firm* — *thumma staqāmū*. The order is the entire religion in sequence. First comes the word of allegiance — *rabbanā Allāh*, "our Lord is God" — a confession that is simultaneously a declaration of surrender, of adoption, and of destination. Then comes *istiqāmah*, standing firm, from the root q-w-m, to rise and stand upright: the spine of the moral life. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī, as the source records, explain that those who stand firm do not confound truth with falsehood, and do not set themselves against God's commands and prohibitions. Islam here rejects both of the shortcuts humanity habitually takes: the shortcut of confession without conduct (faith as a password) and the shortcut of conduct without surrender (ethics as self-salvation). The Prophet ﷺ compressed the verse into his famous reply to Sufyān ibn ʿAbdillāh al-Thaqafī, who asked for a comprehensive word on Islam:

> *"I said: O Messenger of God, tell me something about Islam that I can ask of no one but you. He said: Say, 'I believe in God' — then stand firm (thumma staqim)."* (Muslim 38)

When Sufyān asked for more, the Prophet repeated it. That was all — because that was everything.

**What Standing Firm Costs**

Istiqāmah is easy to recite and expensive to perform, and the early community knew its price. In the Makkan years, "standing firm" meant Bilāl on the burning sand under Umayyah's weight, saying *aḥadun, aḥadun* — "One, One." It meant ʿAmmār's mother Sumayyah, the first martyr of Islam, refusing to trade her confession for Abu Jahl's spear. The Qur'an links standing firm with its inevitable companions — opposition, mockery, loss — and locates the reward in the Hereafter rather than in quarterly results. Abū Bakr, in the report cited by the exegetes on the sister verse 41:30, was asked about istiqāmah and gave its minimal definition:

> *"That you do not associate anything with God."* (reported in the tafsīr literature on 41:30; al-Ḥākim transmits it and grades it sound)

Ibn ʿAbbās, in the same literature, gave its maximal definition: that a man keep all the commandments and prohibitions — "they did not waver like the wavering of the reed." Both definitions are true, as the least and the most of one thing. And the Prophet ﷺ tempered overreach with realism:

> *"The most beloved of deeds to God are the most constant, even if they be few."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from ʿĀʾishah)

Standing firm is not standing spectacularly; it is standing tomorrow, and next year, and on the morning you do not feel like it.

**The Twin Negations: No Fear, No Grief**

The reward is given as two absences: *no fear shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve* — *falā khawfun ʿalayhim wa-lā hum yaḥzanūn*. The formula recurs throughout the Qur'an as the signature blessing of the righteous (2:38; 2:62; 41:30), and its psychology is exact. Fear (*khawf*) faces forward — the dread of what may come; grief (*ḥuzn*) faces backward — the ache of what has gone. The promise covers both directions of the soul's anxiety. Al-Rāzī, as the source notes, adds that many believers are spared even the terrors of the Resurrection itself: the Day that dismantles every other security will not dismantle theirs. The promise is repeated almost verbatim at 41:30, with one difference — there the angels deliver it at the moment of death:

> *"Truly those who say: our Lord is God, then stand firm — the angels descend upon them: fear not, nor grieve; but rejoice in the Garden you were promised."* (Fuṣṣilat 41:30)

Here the promise is narrated by God directly; there it is whispered by heaven's servants at the bedside. One doctrine, two deliveries.

**A Promise Wide Enough for All Who Meant It**

The verse carries an ecumenical weight the source commentary notes through its cross-reference to 2:62: whoever — of any revealed community — genuinely said "our Lord is God" with the light available to him, and stood firm on what he knew, has his reward with his Lord. Ibn Taymiyyah observed that the verse ties salvation not to the abundance of knowledge but to the truthfulness of the stand: the sentence is short because the door is wide. For the modern believer, the verse is a diagnostic instrument of rare precision: it separates the religiously decorative life (words without the stand) from the religiously real one (the stand after the words), and it promises the one who stands that the two great tremors of the human heart — the future's threat and the past's grief — will be lifted from him by the One to whom he first said *rabbanā*.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:14

> **They are the inhabitants of the Garden, abiding therein, as a recompense for that which they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inheritance Deed of the Standing Ones**

Verse 13 named the believers and their promise; verse 14 executes it: *they are the inhabitants of the Garden* — *aṣḥābu al-jannah*, the companions of the Garden. The Arabic term for "companions of" is deliberate: in the Qur'an's idiom, one is not merely *located* in Paradise but *keeps company* with it — the Garden is a home one inhabits, a friend one dwells beside. The verse then adds the duration: *abiding therein* — *khālidīna fīhā* — the great Qur'anic antithesis to the term appointed (*ajal musammā*) of verse 3. The world that perishes save God's Face has its fixed term; the Garden has none. The believer's transit is from the leasehold of this world to the freehold of the next. And the verse closes with the accounting principle of the entire sūrah: *as a recompense for that which they used to do* — *jazāʾan bimā kānū yaʿmalūn*.

**Reward for Deeds, Financed by Grace**

The phrase *a recompense for what they used to do* must be held together with a doctrine the Qur'an states elsewhere with equal clarity: no one enters the Garden by his deeds alone — the Prophet ﷺ taught that even he would enter only by God's mercy (al-Bukhārī 6463; Muslim 2816). The reconciliation, which Ibn Kathīr states with his customary balance, is that deeds are the *cause* God has appointed, while His grace is the *currency*: the believer works, and God pays; but the payment always exceeds the wage. God multiplies a good deed tenfold to seven hundredfold and more, as the famous hadith of the two scribes states:

> *"Whoever intends a good deed and does not do it, God writes it with Him as a full good deed; and if he does it, He writes it as ten good deeds, up to seven hundredfold, up to many times over..."* (al-Bukhārī 6491; Muslim 131, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The sister passage to this verse makes the gracious arithmetic explicit, as the angels hand over the keys:

> *"And those who say: our Lord is God, then stand firm — the angels descend upon them... enter the Garden in peace... This is the Garden you are made to inherit for what you used to do."* (Fuṣṣilat 41:30–32, summarized)

Inheritance language (*ūrithtumūhā*) is itself a mercy-term: heirs receive by the generosity of the testator, not by invoice.

**The Tense of Salvation: "They Used to Do"**

The Arabic *kānū yaʿmalūn* — "they were doing" — describes continuous, habitual action, not isolated heroics. This grammatical observation, stressed by al-Rāzī and Ibn al-Qayyim in different registers, aligns with the Prophet's counsel that the most beloved deeds are the most constant. Salvation in this verse is indexed to a *practice*, a pattern of life — which both warns and comforts. It warns the one whose religion is a season: the pilgrim who is devout for a month and predatory for a decade. It comforts the one whose religion is a quiet routine: the daily prayers of an unremarkable believer, the steady honesty of an unknown merchant, the mother who stood firm at home for forty years. Paradise, in this verse, is the harvest of habits. The garden of eternity is planted in the repetitions of ordinary time.

**The Content of the Recompense**

The Qur'an consistently declines to itemize the Garden's goods in consumer terms, except through imagery — rivers, shade, spouses, and the like — because its essential commodity is stated elsewhere:

> *"No soul knows what is hidden for them of comfort — a recompense for what they used to do."* (al-Sajdah 32:17)

Upon which the Prophet ﷺ commented, in the hadith qudsī transmitted by al-Bukhārī:

> *"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and has not occurred to the heart of man."* (al-Bukhārī 7498, from Abū Hurayrah)

That is the scale of the *jazāʾ* this verse assigns to those who stood firm. The logic is worth resting in: God, who is not wearied by creating the heavens and the earth (46:33), is not stingy in furnishing the Garden. The same overplus of power that makes the resurrection certain makes the reward unrestrained. For the believer, verse 14 is therefore less information than assurance — the deed of a home already titled, held in trust against the Day when the term of this world expires.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:15

> **And We have enjoined man to be virtuous unto his parents. His mother carried him in travail and bore him in travail, and his gestation and weaning is thirty months, such that when he reaches maturity and reaches forty years he says, "My Lord inspire me to give thanks for Thy blessing with which Thou hast blessed me and hast blessed my parents, and that I may work righteousness such that it pleases Thee; and make righteous for me my progeny. Truly I turn in repentance unto Thee, and truly I am among those who submit."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command That Stands Second Only to God's**

The sūrah now descends from the Garden to the most concrete ground of moral life: *We have enjoined upon man iḥsān toward his parents.* The verb *waṣṣaynā* — We enjoined, counseled, made a binding testament — is the verb of a solemn will. Iḥsān, as the source explains, is from the root ḥ-s-n, "beautiful": it means to *make beautiful* one's treatment of another, to do right beyond the minimum, with excellence as the standard. The Qur'an repeatedly welds this duty to the worship of God alone, making the pair inseparable:

> *"Worship God, and associate nothing with Him, and be beautiful toward parents..."* (al-Nisāʾ 4:36)

> *"Thy Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him, and be beautiful toward parents..."* (al-Isrāʾ 17:23)

In the architecture of Islamic ethics, the vertical covenant with God issues immediately in the horizontal debt to those through whom He gave us life. And within that debt, this verse tilts the scales toward the mother: *his mother carried him in travail and bore him in travail* — *kurhan* twice, the noun of burden under compulsion, from the root k-r-h, to bear what one would never choose. The Qur'an elsewhere stacks her hardships in that haunting phrase:

> *"His mother bore him in weakness upon weakness."* (Luqmān 31:14)

Al-Rāzī draws the juristic conclusion the source records: the mother's right exceeds the father's, and the Prophet ﷺ canonized the hierarchy in the most quoted dialogue in all of adab:

> *"A man asked: O Messenger of God, who among the people is most deserving of my good companionship? He said: Your mother. He said: Then who? He said: Your mother. He said: Then who? He said: Your mother. He said: Then who? He said: Your father."* (al-Bukhārī 5971; Muslim 2548, from Abū Hurayrah)

Three times for the woman who carried, bore, and nursed; once, after all that, for the father who shares everything else.

**Thirty Months: A Verse That Legislates for the Unborn**

The verse then fixes a number: *his gestation and weaning is thirty months* — *ḥamluhu wa-fiṣāluhu thalāthūna shahran*. Coupled with the Qur'an's ruling that complete weaning takes two full years for one who wishes to complete the term (*...that he should be weaned in two years* — al-Baqarah 2:233), the jurists derived a minimum gestation of six months — a piece of legislative arithmetic the classical courts actually used. The tafsīr and fiqh literature report the celebrated case: a woman bore a child at six months, and ʿUmar, as judge, was inclined to the pre-Islamic assumption that such a child could not be viable and treated the matter as adultery; ʿAlī cited 46:15 and 2:233 — thirty months total, twenty-four of weaning, therefore six of gestation — and the ruling was reversed. The Qur'an, revealed for hearts, turned out to contain, enfolded in a verse of tenderness, a ruling precise enough for a courtroom. It is a standing reminder that the Book's mercy-language and its law-language are one fabric.

**Forty: The Age of Accounting**

*When he reaches maturity and reaches forty years* — the commentators, as the source records, understand full physical maturity (*ashudd*) at thirty-three and the arrival of completed judgment and spiritual ripeness at forty. Forty is the Qur'an's age of completion: the Prophet ﷺ received revelation at forty; the Israelites' desert term was forty years; and at forty a person has, in the classical reckoning, seen enough of the world to stop being deceived by it. The verse places at this watershed a prayer that is nothing less than the ideal curriculum of the second half of life. Its petitions move in four directions at once: backward to God — *inspire me to give thanks for Thy blessing with which Thou hast blessed me and my parents*; upward in conduct — *that I may work righteousness such that it pleases Thee*; forward to the future — *make righteous for me my progeny*; and inward in return — *truly I turn in repentance unto Thee, and truly I am among those who submit.* The man at forty prays his life's ledger into balance: gratitude for what was, righteousness for what is, guidance for what will come after him, and tawbah for everything missed. Al-Qurṭubī notes the linguistic nicety recorded in the source — *awziʿnī an ashkura*, literally "cast into me thankfulness": the pray-er asks God to perform even his gratitude through him, knowing that the ability to thank is itself the first gift to be thanked. Al-Ṭabarī glosses *make righteous my progeny* as a prayer for their guidance, and al-Rāzī observes that righteous children rank among the greatest blessings a parent receives — the only treasure one can take, by God's mercy, into the grave's far side.

**For Whom the Verse Was Sent Down**

The exegetes report two readings of the verse's occasion, and the source preserves both. Some reports, through al-Ḥasan and others, anchor it in Abū Bakr: when his parents embraced Islam late, he was seen weeping with joy and praying this very prayer — and the verse came down concerning him. Ibn ʿAbbās is also reported to have identified its subject as Abū Bakr, making it, remarkably, a Makkan verse honoring the Prophet's closest friend. But al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, with his customary breadth, declared the verse general — *it applies to all people*, as the source notes — and the majority of later scholars followed him, taking the prayer as the sunnah of every believer who reaches maturity. The two readings do not compete: a general command can arrive at a particular door, the way light fills a whole room by entering at one window. Either way, the verse teaches the community how to grow old: not in nostalgia, not in accumulation, but in a forty-year-old's prayer that gathers parents, self, children, and God into one sentence of gratitude and return. The Prophet ﷺ sealed the ethic of the generations in one line:

> *"He is not one of us who does not show tenderness to our young and honor to our elders."* (al-Tirmidhī, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

A community that recites verse 15 and keeps that hadith has its family architecture built to last.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:16

> **They are those from whom We accept the best of that which they have done and over whose evil deeds We pass. [They] will be among the inhabitants of the Garden—the true promise that they were promised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God's Accounting of the Standing Ones**

Verse 15 ended with the believer's prayer; verse 16 opens with heaven's answer: *they are those from whom We accept the best of that which they have done* — *naqbalu ʿanhum aḥsana mā ʿamilū* — and *over whose evil deeds We pass* — *wa-natajāwazu ʿan sayyiʾātihim*. The verbs are divine plurals of majesty, and both are active graces: acceptance (*qabūl*) and pardon (*tajāwuz*, literally "passing over," from the root j-w-z, to cross). Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī explain the referent exactly as the source records: *they* are the people of verse 15 — the thankful servants who prayed at maturity and stood firm — and God now publishes what He will do with their file. Their good is accepted at its best valuation; their evil is not merely forgiven but passed over, as a traveler crosses a valley without stopping. The Qur'an uses this exact promise of double grace repeatedly:

> *"Those who believe and work righteousness — We shall not waste the wage of any whose deed is beautiful."* (al-Kahf 18:30)

> *"God will absolve them of the worst of what they did, and repay them their due for the best of what they used to do."* (al-Zumar 39:35)

**The Asymmetry of Divine Bookkeeping**

The verse quietly discloses a stunning asymmetry at the heart of Islamic theology: with God, the good is credited beyond its weight, and the evil is dropped below its weight — when the servant turns to Him in repentance and faith. The hadith of the scribes, already cited at verse 14, reveals the mechanism: an intended good is written as done; a done good is multiplied tenfold to seven hundredfold and beyond; an intended evil is not written; a committed evil is written as a single evil — or erased. The distribution of mercy is not an occasional amnesty; it is the system's default setting for those who stand firm. Al-Qurṭubī remarks that this verse is among the most hope-giving in the Book for the ordinary sinner who keeps his covenant: his standing (istiqāmah) does not require sinlessness — the standing ones *do* have evil deeds to pass over — it requires direction. The believer is not the one with a blank ledger; he is the one whose ledger is open, whose pen is moving, and whose Lord has already announced, from eternity, what He does with ledgers held in the right direction.

**The Grammar of Divine Generosity**

Note carefully what God does *not* say. He does not say "We accept their deeds because their good outweighed their evil," which would make salvation an arithmetic in which the servant is the auditor. He says *We accept* — acceptance is God's initiative and His choice, not a balance automatically triggered. This preserves both truths the Qur'an holds in tension: deeds matter (they are the *recompense* of v. 14), and grace reigns (the acceptance and the passing-over are acts of God's will). Ibn Taymiyyah formulated the balance in a maxim the tradition has never improved: neither the murjiʾah's false comfort (faith without works) nor the khārijī's false severity (works as the sole measure) — but the Qur'an's own order: faith that works, purified by a Lord who gives more than is owed. The psychological yield of this doctrine is enormous and measurable: a religion whose God publishes, in advance, that He takes the best of His servants' record and pardons the worst produces servants who confess rather than conceal, who return rather than despair. Compare the spiritual climates of concealment and confession, and the verse's wisdom becomes plain: a God who passes over first raises children who come forward.

**The Promise That Cannot Default**

The verse closes: *among the inhabitants of the Garden — the true promise (waʿd al-ḥaqq) that they were promised.* Al-Qurṭubī, as the source notes, allows the reading either "the promise of truth" or "the true promise" — the genitive of quality. The emphasis falls on promise-keeping as a divine attribute. In human affairs, promising is cheap and default is routine; the Qur'an therefore repeatedly underwrites its promises with God's own honor:

> *"God does not fail in His promise."* (Āl ʿImrān 3:9, among many)

The Garden of verse 14 and the acceptance of verse 16 are not aspirations or possibilities; they are contracts drafted by the Almighty, notarized by His name, and due for execution on the Day the term appointed arrives. For the believer, this changes the grammar of hope from *perhaps* to *indeed* — the only religion on earth whose paradise is not a wager but a written promise from the One who has never broken His word.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:17

> **As for one who says to his parents, "Fie upon you both! Do you promise me that I shall be brought forth when generations have passed away before me?" while they call upon God for succor, "Woe unto you! Believe! Surely God's Promise is true." Whereat he says, "This is naught but fables of those of old."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Portrait Placed Opposite the Prayer**

Verse 15 painted the grateful son at forty; verse 17 hangs beside it the counter-portrait, and the contrast is the sūrah's sharpest brushwork: the son who *says to his parents: uff* — *uffin lakumā*, fie upon you both. The particle *uff* is the smallest possible sound of contempt in Arabic — a snort, a breath through closed teeth — and that is precisely the point the Qur'an makes by legislating against it elsewhere:

> *"Say not to them 'uff,' and do not repel them, but speak to them a noble word."* (al-Isrāʾ 17:23)

God did not begin by forbidding striking or cursing; He began by forbidding the sigh. A law that prohibits the minimal exhalation of irritation has, by implication, prohibited everything above it. And here, in 46:17, the son does not merely sigh — he deploys the word as a weapon against his parents' faith itself: *do you promise me that I shall be brought forth when generations have passed away before me?* Theologically, this is the resurrection mocked at the family dinner table; ethically, it is the parents' lifelong sacrifice repaid with scorn at the very moment their hearts plead for their child's salvation. The Qur'an says elsewhere of such ingratitude:

> *"And We have enjoined upon man concerning his parents... that he should be grateful to Me and to his parents; unto Me is the journey."* (Luqmān 31:14)

**Two Parents Begging for a Soul**

The middle of the verse is almost too painful to read slowly: *while they call upon God for succor* — the verb *yastaghīthāni*, to cry out for help against drowning — the parents are depicted as rescuers calling to God *while* they wrestle their drowning son: *woe unto you! Believe! Surely God's Promise is true.* The particle *wayl* — woe, ruin — is a curse-word in ordinary speech; here it is love's last severity, the language of people who would rather wound their child with a word than watch the Fire claim him. And his answer seals the portrait: *this is naught but fables of those of old* — *asāṭīr al-awwalīn*. The disbelievers of Makkah used the identical formula against the Prophet's recitation (6:25; 8:31; 25:5), and the verse thereby makes its subtle point: the son's contempt for his parents is the same species as the Quraysh's contempt for the Prophet — arrogance inheriting itself down the generations, the child reciting the elders' script. Most commentators, as the source records, read the verse as covering every form of parental disobedience; others, like al-Rāzī, specify the rejection of the parents' call to religion — the worst case, because it adds disbelief to ingratitude, breaking both tablets of the covenant at once.

**Why the Hereafter Is Mocked at the Deathbed Generation**

The son's stated reason deserves attention: *generations have passed away before me* — and none returned. The argument is the ancient one from silence: no one has come back, therefore no one will. The Qur'an's rejoinder is not an argument first, but an event: the dead generation is not the end of the story, and the sūrah will shortly prove the point twice — with ʿĀd, whom no one believed could be destroyed until the wind came, and with the resurrection itself, whose possibility verse 33 grounds in the ease of the original creation. Mockery of the unseen is always an argument from a sample of one moment. The parents, in the verse, hold the longer data set: they know God's promise-keeping across their own lifetimes, and they stake their son's eternity on it. And the Prophet ﷺ attached to this family drama the most fearsome of his sayings on filial ingratitude:

> *"May he be humbled! May he be humbled! May he be humiliated! — he who finds his parents in old age, one or both, and does not enter Paradise."* (Muslim 2551, from Abū Hurayrah)

The one moral failure that can forfeit Paradise while one's parents yet live in one's home is the failure this verse enacts. And of the reverse, the Prophet ﷺ said: *"The parent is the middle gate of Paradise"* (al-Tirmidhī, from Abū al-Dardāʾ) — the most direct door in the wall.

**The Verse as a Mirror for Every Generation**

The verse is not only about antiquity; it is a mirror with adjustable focus. At full zoom, it is the young believer whose faith is mocked by aging, faithful parents inverted — today, often, the case is reversed, and it is the young Muslim who pleads with a distant parent. The structure holds whoever holds the truth: when someone who loves you begs you toward God, the arrogance that answers "fables" is not intellectual sophistication; it is the particle *uff* grown to full size. Islam's family ethics turn on this verse and its companions: the child's default setting toward parents must be *iḥsān* (v. 15), the parent's default setting toward the child must be the tearful summons to believe — and God stands over both, accepting the prayer of the grateful (v. 16) and recording the snort of the contemptuous. Between verses 15 and 17 lies the entire moral space of the family; every household in every century is somewhere on that line.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:18

> **They are those for whom the Word among the communities of jinn and men that had passed before them came due. Truly they are lost.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**When the Sentence Is Already Pronounced**

The fate of the scoffing son — and of all who share his posture — is given here in juridical language: *the Word came due against them* — *ḥaqqa ʿalayhimu al-kalimah*, the divine sentence proved true and binding. *Kalimah*, the Word, is the Qur'an's term for God's prior decree of destruction upon those who persist in disbelief after truth has been made plain; it is not an impulsive punishment but the maturing of a standing verdict. The Qur'an invokes the same decree elsewhere with the same gravity:

> *"Indeed the Word has proved true against most of them, yet they do not believe."* (Yā Sīn 36:7)

> *"But the Word of punishment has come due for the disbelievers."* (al-Zumar 39:71)

> *"Those against whom the Word of your Lord has proved true will not believe, though every sign come to them — until they see the painful punishment."* (Yūnus 10:96–97)

Al-Qurṭubī explains the grammar of *ḥaqqa* — literally "it became binding, it became owed" — as the language of inevitability: their persistence filled the measure, and the decree that was contingent became, by their own choices, certain. This is the Qur'an's most sobering doctrine of free will: God does not compel the verdict; the verdict is the arithmetic of a lifetime of refused light. Human responsibility writes the sentence; divine justice signs it.

**Communities of Jinn and Men: The Widening Court**

The verse specifies the scope of the precedent: *among the communities of jinn and men that had passed before them*. Two expansions occur at once. First, the destruction pattern is communal, not merely individual: entire peoples — ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, Pharaoh's Egypt — stood under this Word and fell under it. Second, and startlingly, the court's jurisdiction includes jinn: mankind is not the only morally accountable species in the Qur'an's universe. The jinn are addressed by messengers (6:130: *O assembly of jinn and mankind, did there not come to you messengers from among you...?*), they respond to revelation (as verses 29–32 of this very sūrah will narrate), and they are punished or rewarded in communities like men. For the Quraysh listener, the mention was personal: the Arabs invoked jinn as partners and protectors — the very beings they called upon besides God (v. 5's *mā tadʿūna min dūni Allāh*) — and God now announces that some of those jinn nations had already been sentenced and destroyed. The protectors needed protection; the invoked were themselves arraigned.

**The Verdict of Loss**

The verse's last word is the Qur'an's most economical definition of ruin: *inna hum khāsirūn* — truly they are the losers. Loss (*khusrān*) in Qur'anic accounting is not failure in one venture but net loss on the only portfolio that matters: a life's allocation of time, strength, and opportunity, traded for pride and the mockery it purchased. The Qur'an opens with the oath-sārah of loss:

> *"By time. Truly mankind is in loss — save those who believe, and do righteous deeds, and counsel one another to truth, and counsel one another to patience."* (al-ʿAṣr 103:1–3)

Against that universal baseline, the believer escapes loss narrowly — through a four-part strategy of faith, works, mutual truth-telling, and mutual patience. The people of verse 18, by contrast, invested everything in the four-part strategy of the mockers: denial, arrogance, mutual ridicule, and mutual abandonment. The trade completed, the account closed: *they are the losers* — present tense, a standing fact awaiting only its public reading on the Day the Word is executed.

**The Mercy of Being Told the Verdict in Advance**

Yet even this verse is mercy wearing judicial robes. God did not need to inform mankind that a verdict-system exists; He chose to disclose the doctrine of the *kalimah* precisely so that no one would walk into it unwarned — *warners had passed away before him and after him*, as verse 21 will say of Hūd. The verse functions like a sign at a cliff's edge: the warning is severe, but the severity is the love. And it closes the family drama of verses 15–17 with a quiet observation every parent of a wayward child should hold: the ungrateful son is not beyond the reach of hope in this life — the Word "came due" upon the communities *that had passed before*, whose days of choice were spent. While a sinner draws breath, his file is open; the sentence of this verse is the terminus of a lifetime, not the verdict on a season. The parents who cry *believe! Surely God's Promise is true* have not yet heard the gavel; that is why they keep calling.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:19

> **For each there are degrees according to that which they have done. He will pay them for their deeds in full, and they will not be wronged.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Universe Graded, Not Flattened**

Verse 18 announced the verdict; verse 19 publishes the sentencing structure: *for each there are degrees* — *darajāt*, ranks, degrees, steps of a staircase. The principle applies, as the source notes from al-Jaṣṣāṣ, to believers and disbelievers alike: there are degrees in the Garden and degrees in the Fire. This doctrine demolishes the two great flattening errors of religious imagination. The first flattens heaven into a featureless sameness, as though a lifetime of secret charity and a lifetime of grudging minimums received one identical reward; the second flattens hell into one undifferentiated furnace. The Qur'an insists on proportion everywhere:

> *"For all there are degrees of what they did."* (al-Anʿām 6:132)

> *"...those who obey God and the Messenger shall be with those whom God has blessed — prophets, the truthful, the witnesses, the righteous; and beautiful are they as companions."* (al-Nisāʾ 4:69, the ranks of the blessed)

> *"God will raise those who believe and those given knowledge by degrees."* (al-Mujādalah 58:11)

The Prophet ﷺ made the hierarchy of Paradise visible in his famous description of the chambers of the Garden:

> *"Indeed the people of Paradise will see the chambers above them as you see the far-shining star in the horizon... and that is because of the differing of their degrees."* (al-Bukhārī 3256; Muslim 2831, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

Believers, in other words, will *see* the architecture of their own choices: proximity to God is earned territory, and the view from every level will testify to what was done below.

**Degrees Are Earned, Not Conferred**

The verse pins the ranking to its cause with precision: *according to that which they have done* — *mimmā ʿamilū*, from what they worked. Not from what they intended only, not from their lineage, not from their tribe's reputation, not from their following — from what issued from them in act. In Makkan society, where a man's rank was his clan's rank, this was a revolution in one line: God's ledger ignores the family tree and reads the file. The Qur'an had already told the Prophet to declare this meritocracy of the Hereafter: *and that man shall have nothing but what he strove for* (al-Najm 53:39). And the Prophet ﷺ attached the mechanism of social ranking to the same cause in this world: *whoever humbles himself for God's sake, God raises him* — recorded by Muslim. Even within Islam's internal honors — the imam's place, the scholar's chair — the Qur'an ranks by knowledge and faith (58:11), not by wealth or voice. A community that internalizes verse 19 stops competing for titles and starts competing for deeds, precisely because the real ladder is vertical and God-built.

**Paid in Full: The Perfection of Divine Justice**

The middle clause states the audit's completeness: *He will pay them for their deeds in full* — *wa-li-yuwaffiyahum aʿmālahum*, from w-f-y, to pay in full, to complete to the last unit. Nothing done will be unaccounted; nothing owed will be withheld. The verse's final clause seals it with the negation of the one fear every litigant has ever held: *and they will not be wronged* — *wa-hum lā yuẓlamūn*. The verb ẓ-l-m, to place a thing out of its right place, is the Qur'an's word for injustice; the promise is that on that Day nothing will be misplaced — no testimony suppressed, no influence peddled, no sentence skewed by the defendant's kin. The Qur'an returns to this guarantee again and again:

> *"And we set the scales of justice for the Day of Resurrection, so that no soul is wronged in anything; and if there be the weight of a mustard seed, We bring it — and sufficient are We as reckoners."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47)

> *"God wrongs not mankind anything, but mankind wrong themselves."* (Yūnus 10:44)

**Why Perfect Justice Is Both Terror and Comfort**

The doctrine cuts both ways, and the verse intends both edges. For the deniers who mocked the resurrection, perfect accounting is terror: every snort of *uff*, every sneer of "fables of the ancients," is on the itemized bill. For the wronged — and this is the verse's deepest social function — it is the only final comfort: the slave wronged by his master, the parent wounded by his child, the believer mocked by his people, all hold cases that earthly courts never heard. Ibn Taymiyyah noted that belief in the scales is the single greatest civilizer of both rulers and ruled: the ruler who knows his ledger is open rules differently; the victim who knows the court is certain can forgo vengeance without forgoing justice. The scales of 46:19 are, in this sense, the foundation of every earthly courtroom's aspiration and the answer to every earthly courtroom's failure. Degrees for what was done; payment in full; and no one — noble or slave, prophet's neighbor or orphan's oppressor — wronged by a single atom's weight.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:20

> **And the day when those who disbelieve are exposed to the Fire: "You squandered your good things in your life in the world and sought enjoyment therein; so today you are recompensed with the punishment of disgrace for having waxed arrogant upon the earth without right and for having been iniquitous."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Audit of Consumed Blessings**

The scene is set at the Fire itself: *the day when those who disbelieve are exposed to the Fire* — the verb *yuʿraḍū*, to be presented, paraded before — which al-Rāzī, as the source records, understands as their being shown the Fire's terrors before entering it, and al-Maybudī sharpens with the Arabic idiom *ʿuriḍa ʿalā al-sayf*, "he was presented to the sword," meaning slain by it: destroyed by the Fire. Then comes the charge, read aloud: *you squandered your good things in your life in the world* — *adhiʿtum ḥasanātikum*, literally "you let slip away, dissipated your good things." The verb d-y-ʿ connotes property lost through negligence — the heir who burns through an inheritance. The Qur'an's shocking claim is that comfort itself was an advance payment, and the disbeliever's tragedy is not that he was punished arbitrarily but that he *consumed his capital*: the health, wealth, ease, and pleasure of his earthly term were the whole of his allotment, spent without a single coin of gratitude, and the account is now empty exactly when eternity presents its bill. The Prophet ﷺ made the doctrine explicit in his exchange with ʿUmar, recorded in the tafsīr of this verse:

> *ʿUmar implored the Prophet to pray for prosperity like that of the Persians and Byzantians, whereupon the Prophet said: "Ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, do you doubt that they are a nation for whom good things have been advanced to them in the life of this world?"* (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī, commenting on this verse, as cited in the source)

**Enjoyment Is Not the Crime**

Precision matters here, and the source commentary rightly insists on it: this verse does not reprimand the disbelievers for enjoying the world. The Qur'an expressly permits and dignifies lawful enjoyment:

> *"Say: Who has forbidden the adornment of God, which He has brought forth for His servants, and the good things of provision? Say: they are for those who believe in the life of this world, purely on the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Aʿrāf 7:32)

The charge is rather twofold, as al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī frame it: enjoyment *without gratitude* — consuming the gift while denying the Giver; and enjoyment *of the unlawful* — the second half of the verse names it: *for having waxed arrogant upon the earth without right and for having been iniquitous*. The same doctrine of spent blessings is drawn in full narrative detail in the story of Qārūn, who said of his wealth, *"this has been given to me only because of knowledge I have"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28:78), and was swallowed by the earth. And the Prophet ﷺ stated the whole ledger in one line:

> *"The world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever."* (Muslim 2956, from Abū Hurayrah)

The disbeliever's paradise is the one he already had; the believer's prison is the one he is leaving. The Qur'an elsewhere weeps at the shortness of the purchased pleasure: *and they rejoice in the life of this world, when the life of this world is but brief comfort compared to the Hereafter* (al-Raʿd 13:26).

**The Punishment of Disgrace Matches the Crime of Arrogance**

The sentence is *ʿadhāb al-hūn* — the punishment of disgrace, abasement. The correspondence is exact and is the Qur'an's signature justice: those who *waxed arrogant upon the earth without right* — *tastakbirūna fī al-arḍi ghayra al-ḥaqq* — are repaid with humiliation, because arrogance was their chosen delusion and disgrace is its antidote. Arrogance, in Qur'anic psychology, is the claim of worthiness without warrant; hell's first curriculum is the enforced discovery of one's actual size. The pairing of charges — arrogance *and* iniquity (*fisq*, the root f-s-q, of a fruit bursting from its peel: deviation from one's created design) — covers the two axes of sin: the vertical (defiance of God) and the horizontal (harm to people). The sūrah will shortly describe ʿĀd in the identical terms — *they were arrogant in the land without right* (41:15) — binding the Quraysh listener to the doomed tribe by shared grammar before the wind story even begins.

**The Verse as Economics Education**

The modern believer should sit with this verse longer than with most, because the modern world is the most sophisticated engine ever built for *adīʿtum ḥasanātikum* — the dissipation of good things. Comfort is now frictionless, gratification instantaneous, and the entire advertising industry is dedicated to converting blessing into entitlement and gratitude into appetite. The verse does not ask asceticism; it asks bookkeeping. The Qur'anic believer enjoys his good things *as a believer* — halal, thankful, proportionate, sharing — and thereby keeps them as capital that follows him forward:

> *"Whatever you are given is the comfort of the life of this world; and what is with God is better and more enduring — for those who believe and upon their Lord rely."* (al-Shūrā 42:36)

The question verse 20 puts to every reader is not "how much do you have?" but "in which column is it being posted?" — the column that dies with the term appointed, or the column that survives the audit and is repaid, in full, with increase.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:21

> **And mention the brother of ʿĀd, when he warned his people by the sand dunes; warners had passed away before him and after him, [saying], "Worship none but God. Truly I fear for you the punishment of a tremendous day."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hūd the Brother: A Prophet Who Belonged to His People**

The sūrah now turns from eschatology to history — or rather, it turns to history *as* eschatology: the story of ʿĀd is the Day of Judgment played out early, in one theater, for the instruction of every later audience. *Mention the brother of ʿĀd* — Hūd, called "brother" because, like all the Arabian prophets, he was of the very tribe he was sent to warn; the Qur'an uses the same kinship idiom for Noah, Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb, and Lot ("the brother of..." in each address: 7:65, 7:73, 7:85, 11:50). The title is theology in a word: God sends to a people one of their own, who has shared their food, known their dialect, carried their hopes — a messenger who is a kinsman, not a foreign prosecutor. *When he warned his people among the aḥqāf* — the sand dunes that gave the sūrah its name: *al-aḥqāf* is the plural of *ḥaqf*, the technical term for a sand hill curved and hollowed by the wind, and the classical geographers and commentators place it in the desert wastes toward Ḥaḍramawt in southern Arabia, the traditional homeland of ʿĀd; some, as the source notes from al-Jaṣṣāṣ, take al-Aḥqāf as the proper name of a specific valley there.

**Who ʿĀd Was: The Superpower of the Sands**

The Qur'an memorializes ʿĀd as a people of terrifying capability — the passage every commentator cites is the oath-sworn description in Sūrah al-Fajr:

> *"Did you not see what thy Lord did unto ʿĀd — Iram of the Pillars, the like of whom was never created in all the land?"* (al-Fajr 89:6–8)

They succeeded Noah's generation, inherited the earth after the flood, and built a civilization of physical prowess and monumental architecture: *as for ʿĀd, they were arrogant in the land without right, and said: who is mightier than us in strength?* (Fuṣṣilat 41:15). The Qur'an credits their boasts with real substance — *and He increased you broadly in creation* (al-Aʿrāf 7:69) — and then asks the question that dismantles every empire: *did they not see that God, who created them, is mightier than them in strength?* (41:15). The lesson is not that power is evil, but that power is borrowed, and borrowers forget. In the memory of Arabia, ʿĀd's ruins were a byword; Ibn Kathīr, as the source records, cites the tradition that travelers' reports spoke of the remains of their dwellings in the southern wastes — ruins without an heir.

**The Message: One Sentence Across All History**

Hūd's message is given in four Arabic words that constitute the entire prophetic curriculum: *allā taʿbudū illā Allāh* — "worship none but God." No nuance, no negotiation, no opening bid. Every messenger from Noah to Muhammad ﷺ carried the same creed — the Prophet ﷺ affirmed it in the hadith of the paternal half-brothers: their mothers differ (their laws differ) and their religion is one. And the motive Hūd voices is not the reward of paradise first, but the fear of a day: *truly I fear for you the punishment of a tremendous day* — *yawm ʿaẓīm*. Al-Maybudī, as the source notes, supplies the unstated condition: "if you disobey me." The prophet's fear is *for* his people, not for himself — the fear of a physician, not of a judge. Ibn Kathīr observes that Hūd's address deliberately bypassed the tribal economy of honor: he did not threaten their trade or their idols' reputations; he warned them of a day when neither gold nor gods would interpose.

**Warners Before and After: The End of the Excuse**

The verse adds a clause of enormous doctrinal weight: *warners had passed away before him and after him* — *wa-qad khalati al-nudhuru min bayni yadayhi wa-min khalfihi*. ʿĀd could not claim novelty, confusion, or ignorance: the procession of warners reached back to Noah ahead of them and would continue to Muḥammad ﷺ after them. The Qur'an states the principle as a law of divine justice:

> *"And We never destroyed a township but it had warners — as a reminder; and We were never unjust."* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:208–209)

> *"And thy Lord would never destroy the towns unjustly, while their people were unaware."* (al-Anʿām 6:131, paraphrased in 28:59's form: "...until We raise in their mother-city a messenger reciting to them Our signs; nor would We destroy the towns unless their people were wrongdoers.")

God's punishments in history are never ambushes; they are terminations of a long, documented mercy. This doctrine — the universality of warning — is also the Qur'an's invitation to humility for every reader: each of us stands somewhere in that unbroken chain of nudhur, and the question is never "was I warned?" but only "what did I do with the warning?"

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:22

> **They said, "Have you come to us to pervert us from our gods? Then bring upon us that wherewith you have threatened us, if you are among the truthful."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply of a Civilization That Stopped Listening**

ʿĀd's answer to their kinsman-prophet reveals, in two sentences, the entire anatomy of establishment disbelief. First, the reframing: *have you come to us to pervert us from our gods?* — the verb *li-tanaṣṣaʾanā*, from the root n-ṣ-ʾ, to turn someone away, distract, divert. What Hūd announced as rescue, they re-labeled as sabotage. It is the same rhetorical operation the Quraysh performed on the Prophet ﷺ — *do you come to us that we may worship God alone?* (al-Aʿrāf 7:70, in Noah's people's identical words) — and that every reformer in history has met: the dentist is called a wrecker of teeth, the surgeon a mutilator of flesh, because the first step in resisting a cure is to re-describe it as an injury. Second, the challenge: *bring upon us that wherewith you threaten us, if you are truthful.* The demand for immediate, on-demand punishment is the mockers' oldest card, and the Qur'an records it from every century of prophecy:

> *"They say: when will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?"* (Yūnus 10:48; see also 21:38; 27:71; 36:48; 67:25)

**Mistaking Patience for Absence**

The psychology beneath the challenge deserves dissection, because the sūrah intends it dissected. ʿĀd had enjoyed their power for generations without a single season of defeat; they stood, in their own minds, outside history. The absence of immediate consequences had been metabolized into a cosmology: nothing above us, nothing after us, nothing to fear. The Qur'an names this reasoning error explicitly elsewhere:

> *"And they swore by God their most binding oath: God will not raise the dead. Nay — it is a binding promise upon Him... that they may recompense those who believed and worked righteousness... and that they who disbelieved may know they were liars... And when We decreed destruction for a town, We command its affluent ones; they commit iniquity therein; the Word proves true against it; and We destroy it utterly."* (al-Isrāʾ 17:97's companion passage; and, stated with perfect brevity) *"And they seek to hasten thee with evil before good, though there have passed away before them exemplary punishments."* (al-Raʿd 13:6)

They demanded the punishment *early* precisely because they were certain it would never come *at all* — bravado as theology. The Prophet ﷺ is elsewhere instructed to answer exactly as Hūd answers in the next verse: knowledge of timing belongs to God alone (67:25–26). The Qur'an also discloses why the timing is withheld — mercy's margin:

> *"And they seek to hasten it, though God will never fail in His promise... and were it not for a term appointed, the punishment would have come upon them; but it will come upon them suddenly, while they perceive not."* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29:53)

**The Gods Named, the Stakes Raised**

Notice, too, what the challenge concedes. By demanding the punishment, ʿĀd transformed the dispute from an argument into a wager with a date. If Hūd were a fraud, the wager was safe; if he were a messenger, the wager was suicidal. Their own arrogance closed the option of a middle path — quiet reflection, a month of watching, a private look at the horizon. This is the deeper warning of the verse for all who toy with sin's deadlines: mockers do not merely disbelieve in punishment; they eventually *request* it, because pride cannot retract publicly, and each public escalation burns another exit behind it. The Qur'an describes the end of that escalation with terrible symmetry: the disbelievers demanded the punishment on their schedule, and received it on the one day it could not be answered. Verse 24 will show them looking at the answerable horizon — a cloud — and cheering.

**A Mirror for the Modern Reader**

The verse is not safely historical. Every human being keeps a private schedule of consequences he is sure will never arrive — the health deferred, the reconciliation postponed, the reckoning with God penciled indefinitely. Hūd's people made their wager aloud; most people make it silently, but the wager is the same. The Qur'an's counsel to its Prophet, and to every believer who watches the wagering, is in the sūrah's own final verse: patience, and no hastening — *seek not to hasten for them* (46:35). The servant's task is not to referee the deadline; it is to keep warning clearly, as Hūd did, and to leave the calendar to the One who wrote it.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:23

> **He said, "Knowledge lies with God alone, and I only convey to you that wherewith I have been sent. But I see that you are an ignorant people."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messenger's Honest Confession**

Hūd's reply to the demand for a date is a masterclass in prophetic integrity: *knowledge lies with God alone* — *innamā al-ʿilmu ʿinda Allāh*. One word of restriction — *innamā*, "only" — does all the work: the knowledge of the Hour, of lifespans, of when destruction descends, is God's deposit alone. The Prophet ﷺ was commanded to give the same answer to the same demand:

> *"They say: when will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful? Say: the knowledge is with God alone, and I am only a clear warner."* (al-Mulk 67:25–26)

And of the Hour itself:

> *"They ask you as though you were solicitous of it. Say: its knowledge is with God alone — but most people know not."* (al-Aʿrāf 7:187)

There is a subtle credential of truthfulness here that the classical scholars never tired of noting: frauds specialize in confident timetables. Every false prophet in history has sold dates — the end on such a year, the catastrophe by such a month — because dates are the cheapest commodity of charisma. The true messenger's refusal to improvise a date, even under public mockery, is the signature of a man transmitting rather than inventing. It also, as the commentators note, matched the Arabian expectation of a soothsayer (*kāhin*) who claims to know the unseen: Hūd and Muḥammad ﷺ both conspicuously declined the job description their audiences tried to force on them.

**The Job Description of a Messenger: Conveyance**

The second clause states the entire mandate: *and I only convey to you that wherewith I have been sent* — *u-ballighukum mā ursiltu bihi*. The root b-l-gh carries the sense of delivering a message whole, reaching its destination intact. Tablīgh — conveyance — is the prophetic office reduced to its essence, and the Qur'an issues it to the final Prophet in uncompromising terms:

> *"O Messenger! Convey what has been sent down to thee from thy Lord; and if thou do not, thou hast not conveyed His message at all."* (al-Māʾidah 5:67)

And it marks the limit of the office with equal clarity: *the Messenger's duty is only conveyance* (5:99; 24:54). A messenger is a courier of the divine, not a co-author; he is accountable for delivery, not for outcomes. This distinction, which Hūd here embodies, is the theological root of the Islamic prohibition on compelling faith: *there is no compulsion in religion* (al-Baqarah 2:256), and *thou art not over them a controller* (al-Ghāshiyah 88:22). God alone owns both the calendar and the hearts.

**"A People Who Do Not Know"**

Hūd's final observation is the verse's sharpest edge: *but I see that you are an ignorant people* — *qawman tajhalūn*. In the Qur'an's lexicon, *jahl* is not lack of information but active misrelation to knowledge: the antonym of ʿilm as a *lived* knowledge of God. ʿĀd were master builders, engineers of irrigation, experts of the caravan route — and *jāhilūn* nonetheless, because they had plumbed the earth and never read its Author. Al-Ṭabarī notes the pointed contrast: they demanded *knowledge* of the hour of punishment from the one man telling them truth, while standing in their own ocean of unknowing. The Qur'an turns the same mirror on the Arabs of the revelation and on every learned age since:

> *"They know an outward aspect of the life of this world, but of the Hereafter they are heedless."* (al-Rūm 30:7)

Our own century deserves the mirror as much as any: humanity that reads the genome and splits the atom, that times eclipses to the second, remains — where its origin, purpose, and destination are concerned — precisely as Hūd's audience: technically brilliant, existentially *jāhil*, mistaking its instruments for wisdom.

**The Peace of a Defined Role**

There is a pastoral depth in Hūd's sentence for everyone who carries truth to others. The messenger who accepts that outcomes, timing, and hearts belong to God is released from the two corrosions that destroy well-meaning people: despair when the audience refuses, and manipulation when results are demanded. Hūd delivered, disclosed his ignorance of the schedule, named the ignorance of his people — and then, as the next verse shows, went on living among them, still a brother of ʿĀd, until God's own calendar turned its page. That serenity in the gap between effort and outcome is not resignation; it is the professional calm of one who has read his job description and trusts the Manager who wrote it.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:24

> **And when they beheld it as a cloud bound for their valleys, they said, "This is a cloud bringing us rain." Nay! It is what you sought to hasten—a wind carrying a painful punishment,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cloud on the Horizon**

The scene is cinematic in its economy. *When they beheld it* — the demonstrative pronoun holds the suspense: they saw *it*, the thing whose timing they had demanded. What they saw was an *ʿāriḍ* — the technical Arabic term for a bank of cloud that *presents itself* on the horizon's edge (from ʿaraḍa, to present oneself, to appear sideways), *mustaqbila awdiyatihim* — advancing, head-on, toward their valleys. The commentators sketch the background the source preserves: ʿĀd had suffered years of drought; they had prayed for rain; and when the mass finally gathered on the skyline and drove toward their wadis, their hearts leapt: *this is a cloud bringing us rain* — *hādhā ʿāriḍun muṭirunā*, literally "a cloud giving us rain." The Arabic phrase is a participle of giving: they did not merely hope; they counted the gift delivered. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Maybudī register the full irony their tradition transmits — they received the wind as an answer to their prayer for water, a mercy's outline concealing the sentence they had themselves subpoenaed: *nay! It is what you sought to hasten* — *bal huwa mā staʿjaltum bihi*.

**When the Answer to a Mockery Arrives Shaped Like a Blessing**

The Qur'an loves this figure — punishment that enters dressed as the very thing desired. Pharaoh pursued the sea that was parted *for* Moses; the people of Lot were visited at dawn, the hour of expected guests; and ʿĀd's executioner arrived looking like the rain cloud for which a parched nation prays. The theological point is exact: God's signs are morally double-exposed — the same horizon that means hope to the repentant means doom to the defiant, because the meaning of a blessing is decided by the heart that receives it. The believers among ʿĀd — for the Qur'an notes Hūd was saved *with those who believed with him* (Hūd 11:58) — presumably saw the same sky; what fell on the valleys was filtered, for them, through God's mercy into rescue. The rest of the sūrah's readers should note the pattern's persistence: nations that demand signs on their own terms receive them on God's, and the last sign a mocker receives is frequently the one he mistook for good news.

**The Wind Itself: Scripture's Chilling Details**

The verse names the instrument: *a wind* — *rīḥ* — *bearing a painful punishment*. Other verses complete the forensic description. Sūrah al-Qamar:

> *"Indeed We sent upon them a roaring wind on a day of continuing misfortune, plucking people away as though they were uprooted palm-trunks."* (al-Qamar 54:19–20)

Sūrah Ḥāqqah, with its frozen aftermath:

> *"And as for ʿĀd — they were destroyed by a roaring, icy wind, which God imposed upon them for seven nights and eight days consecutively; and you would see the people therein felled as if they were hollow trunks of palm-trees. Do you see any remnant of them?"* (al-Ḥāqqah 69:6–8)

And Sūrah Fuṣṣilat frames the theology of the exchange:

> *"So We sent upon them a raging wind in days of ill fortune, to make them taste the punishment of humiliation in the life of this world — and the punishment of the Hereafter is more humiliating still, and they will not be helped."* (Fuṣṣilat 41:16)

Seven nights and eight days: the destruction was not instantaneous annihilation but a sustained, inescapable siege — long enough for a civilization of unmatched strength to understand, fully, what it had wagered. The "hollow palm trunks" detail — trunks emptied of pith, lying where they fell — is the Qur'an's own photographic negative of ʿĀd's boast of might: the mightiest bodies in Arabia, emptied in a week by moving air.

**The Prophet's ﷺ Own Fear of That Sky**

The Qur'an itself trains the believer's memory of ʿĀd into a living fear of the same instrument. ʿĀʾishah, the Prophet's wife, reports the conduct Muslims transmit verbatim:

> *"Whenever the wind blew stormily, the Messenger of God's face would change, and he would say: 'O God, I ask Thee for what is good in it, and the good that it contains, and the good for which it was sent; and I seek refuge in Thee from what is evil in it, and the evil it contains, and the evil for which it was sent.' And when the sky darkened, his color would change; he would go out and in, backward and forward; and when it rained, he was relieved — and I noticed that upon his face. I asked him about it, and he said: 'It may be what the people of ʿĀd said — when they saw it advancing on their valleys, they said: this is a cloud bringing us rain.'"* (reported by Ibn Kathīr citing this account from ʿĀʾishah; the wind supplication is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 899, Kitāb Ṣalāt al-Istisqāʾ)

The Prophet ﷺ, sinless and secure, *feared* the wind — not irrationally, but because he read the sky through the Qur'an's own commentary: the same mercy that waters the earth once unmade a nation, and the sight of an approaching front is, for a remembering heart, a rehearsal of that Day. His fear was the spiritual heir of Hūd's warning: no believer who has absorbed verse 24 watches a storm front quite casually again.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:25

> **destroying everything by the Command of its Lord. They became such that naught was seen but their dwellings. Thus do We recompense the guilty people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"By the Command of Its Lord": The Wind as Servant**

The verse opens by completing the description begun in verse 24: the wind *destroys everything* — *tuhliku kulla shayʾin*, obliterates every thing — *by the command of its Lord* — *bi-amri rabbihā*. Two theological anchors are dropped here. First, the wind *has* a Lord, and knows it: creation is not an inventory of autonomous forces but a staff of servants, each dispatched on assignment. The Qur'an elsewhere shows the same chain of command in other instruments of judgment — the flood, the cry, the stone-storm — and in instruments of mercy, as with the winds that heralded rain to another nation: *and We sent the winds fertilizing* (al-Ḥijr 15:22). Second, the destruction is *bi-amr*, by command: nothing in the storm was wild. For seven nights and eight days (69:7) the most violent force on earth operated within a mandate issued from above it. Ibn Kathīr draws the comparison the verse invites: ʿĀd, the strongest of peoples, destroyed by the frailest of elements — because the strength of an instrument is irrelevant when the Commander of all things has scheduled the outcome. Air, which cannot even hold a candle steady, unmade the nation that said *who is mightier than us in strength?*.

**A Landscape Emptied to Its Architecture**

The result is given in an image of eerie stillness: *they became such that naught was seen but their dwellings* — *fa-aṣbaḥū lā yurā illā masākinuhum*. The verb *aṣbaḥū* — "they came to be, by morning" — carries the Qura'nic resonance of the morning state, the new condition after a night's transformation; and what the morning revealed was subtraction: a skyline of houses whose inhabitants had been *plucked away as though they were uprooted palm-trunks* (54:20). The dwellings — *masākin*, from the root s-k-n, denoting stillness, habitation — remained as testimony precisely because they were the part of ʿĀd that had no soul to account for: the buildings stood, the builders were gone. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note the tradition that the site remained, and remains, among the wastes of southern Arabia, ruins that later travelers described — a silent museum whose entire collection is one lesson. The Qur'an pauses on such aftermaths deliberately:

> *"So many a town that wronged itself have We destroyed, and raised after them another people."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11)

> *"And We did not wrong them, but they wronged themselves; and their gods, whom they called upon apart from God, availed them naught when the command of thy Lord came — they increased them not except in ruin."* (Hūd 11:101)

**The Formula of Just Recompense**

The verse closes with the Qur'an's recurring verdict-formula: *thus do We recompense the guilty people* — *kadhālika najzī al-qawma al-mujrimīn*. The word *mujrimīn*, from j-r-m (to cut off, as a criminal *cuts* the fruit from the branch), denotes those who have severed themselves from the covenant — criminals in the theological sense: not merely lawbreakers, but ones who treated their own making as an ownership rather than a trust. The formula appears after each destruction narrative (e.g., 18:59; 28:78's context) like the stamp after a judicial sentence, and its recurrence is itself doctrine: these events are not tales of the strange but rulings of a court whose jurisdiction never lapses. Verse 26 will draw the moral for the Quraysh explicitly — those ruins are geographically near you — but already the verse demands the reader's verdict: the wind was obedient, the command was just, and the guilt was self-authored.

**What the Empty Houses Say to the Modern Reader**

There is a modern habit of reading such verses as the mythology of a vanished world; the Qur'an asks rather for a contemporary application, and the application is not distant. Every civilization since has had its ʿĀd-moment of overconfidence — the "unsinkable" technologies, the "invincible" systems, the pride-papers of every fallen empire are in the historical record, from the drowned coasts of lost cities to the twentieth century's own ruins. The believer reads 46:24–25 as a standing law of moral physics: strength is a loan, loans have terms, and the term is known to One. And the verse carries a private, quieter application: every individual has his own *masākin* — the things guaranteed to outlive him: his house, his accounts, his works. What will remain "seen" of us when our term arrives? For the people of Hūd, nothing living remained but the warning they had refused; the believer inverts the tragedy by attaching himself, before the wind ever gathers, to the One who commands it.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:26

> **Indeed We established them in a manner in which We did not establish you, and We endowed them with hearing, sight, and hearts. But their hearing, sight, and hearts availed them naught, since they rejected God's signs, and that which they used to mock beset them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Direct Challenge to Quraysh: Your Predecessors Outclassed You**

The sūrah now turns from history to its audience and makes the comparison explicit: *We established them in a manner in which We did not establish you* — *makkannāhum*, from the root m-k-n, to give *tamkīn*: firm establishment, entrenched power, settled dominion. Ibn Kathīr, as the source records, spells out the ledger: ʿĀd were among the most powerful peoples in the Arab memory — *the like of whom was never created in all the land* (al-Fajr 89:8) — towering in stature, unrivaled in building, while the Quraysh of the revelation were a merchant tribe in a town without agriculture or empire. The argument runs like a prosecutor's summation: if God destroyed a civilization stronger than yours, your strength is no shield; the variable that decides is not capability but response. The Qur'an frames the same logic as a question to all deniers:

> *"Have they not considered how many generations We destroyed before them, whom We had established in the land more firmly than We have established you, and We poured on them rain from the sky in abundance, and made the rivers flow beneath them — yet We destroyed them for their sins, and raised after them another generation?"* (al-Anʿām 6:6)

**The Three Faculties: Given, and Then Wasted**

The verse then lists the equipment: *hearing, sight, and hearts* — *al-samʿ, al-abṣār, al-afʾidah* — the triad of perception in Qur'anic anthropology. Hearing gathers revelation; sight reads the signs in creation; the *fuʾād* — the heart as the seat of understanding — synthesizes both into knowledge of God. The tragedy is stated with surgical repetition: *their hearing, sight, and hearts availed them naught* — *mā aghnā ʿanhum* — because *they rejected God's signs* — the verb *yajḥadūn*, to deny knowing, the deliberate refusal of what the faculties delivered. The Qur'an devotes one of its most haunting passages to this exact malfunction:

> *"And We have created for Hell many of the jinn and mankind: they have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, ears with which they do not hear; they are like cattle — nay, more astray; they are the heedless."* (al-Aʿrāf 7:179)

The faculties were never defective; the will was. As the source records from the classical commentators — and it is the point Ibn Kathīr makes — they did not listen to the revelation, did not see the signs of God's oneness in creation, and did not reflect: perception without reception. It is the difference between an ear and a listener, an eye and a witness; God gave ʿĀd the instruments, and they used them for everything except their purpose.

**Mockery Come Home to Roost**

The verse's final clause has the compression of law: *and that which they used to mock beset them* — *ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūn*, from the root ḥ-w-q, to encircle, to come around upon, as a debt falls due. The thing mocked is the thing that arrives; the punchline of the joke turns out to be the sentence. The Qur'an repeats this lex talionis of ridicule across many sūrahs:

> *"Messengers indeed were mocked before thee, but that which they used to mock beset those who mocked."* (al-Anʿām 6:10)

> *"And they swore their most binding oath by God: if a sign comes to them, they will believe in it... but when it came they did not believe... and that which they used to mock beset them."* (al-Anʿām 6:109–110's close, and the same refrain at 11:8; 16:34; 21:41; 39:48; 40:83; 45:33)

Mockery, in Qur'anic psychology, is not harmless banter but the final form of pride — contempt discharged at what one refuses to answer. And because it produces nothing, builds nothing, and refutes nothing, it leaves its author with exactly one future: to meet the reality he laughed at, unarmed. The scholar might note how precisely the divine justice is tailored: no new punishment was invented for ʿĀd; they were simply handed over to the consequences of their own theater.

**God Wrongs No One — the Door the Verse Leaves Open**

Yet the verse, read in full context, is not fatalism but warning, and two guardrails in the Qur'an keep it so. First, the destruction was earned, not decreed at birth: *God wrongs not mankind anything, but mankind wrong themselves* (Yūnus 10:44). Second, the remnant was spared: *and when Our command came, We saved Hūd and those who believed with him by a mercy from Us, and We saved them from a harsh punishment* (Hūd 11:58) — the same verse that names the rescue also seals the doom. Every faculty listed in 46:26 — hearing, sight, heart — remains, for every reader, a live account; the Quraysh still had theirs, and so do we. The verse asks a question that no technology will ever answer on our behalf: not "how sharp are your instruments?" — ours are sharper than any nation's before us — but "to what are they pointed?" The most sophisticated ear that never listens for God is, on the last day, ʿĀd's ear in miniature; and the ear that turns even once, in humility, has already begun to hear.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:27

> **And indeed We destroyed the towns around you, and We vary the signs that haply they might return.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ruins Within Visiting Distance**

The argument now becomes geographic: *We destroyed the towns around you* — *mā ḥawlakum*, what surrounds you. Ibn Kathīr, as the source records, names the ring of ruins around the Quraysh's world: the people of ʿĀd in the south; Thamūd at al-Ḥijr on the trade route between Madinah and Syria; Sabaʾ in Yemen, whose dam burst; the Midianites on the road to Palestine; and the people of Lot by the Dead Sea on the northern road. The significance is that the Quraysh were not being asked to believe reports from a mythical elsewhere — their own caravan schedules ran through the wreckage of punished nations. Every summer journey to Syria (the *riḥlat al-shitāʾ wa-l-ṣayf* of Sūrah Quraysh) threaded past the empty tombs of Thamūd; every southern route skirted ʿĀd's territory. The ruins were, as it were, billboards on the highway of Arab commerce, each one announcing the same advertiser. And the Qur'an adds the second half of the verse: *and We vary the signs* — *ṣarrafnā al-āyāt* — the divine pedagogy of rotating presentation, spelled out at 6:46 and 17:41: signs delivered in every genre — narrative, oath, argument, scene of the Garden, scene of the Fire — *that haply they might return*.

**A Prophet's Conduct Among the Ruins**

That the ruins were not abstraction to the first Muslims is proven by the Prophet's ﷺ own conduct when his army, in the year of Tabūk, camped at al-Ḥijr, the very heartland of Thamūd. The report is among the most cited of all sīrah:

> *"When the army of the Messenger of God ﷺ passed by al-Ḥijr, the dwellings of Thamūd, he said: 'Do not enter the dwellings of those who wronged themselves, save weeping, lest what befell them befall you.' And he hastened his mount until they left the valley."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Ibn ʿUmar, in the account of the Tabūk expedition)

Note the precision of the instruction: not superstition, not tourism, not gawking — but a passing lesson in moral physics, delivered at a gallop. The Prophet ﷺ taught his Companions to read a ruin the way one reads a coroner's report: with attention, and with a shudder, and with movement away from the cause of death. Muslims who today visit the archaeological remains of vanished nations — or scroll through documentaries of fallen empires — hold in this hadith their sunnah of interpretation: history's ruins are scripture's footnotes.

**The Sunnah of Varying the Signs**

The clause *We vary the signs* deserves a separate reflection, for it discloses God's teaching method. Human attention habituates: a warning repeated in one form fades into wallpaper; an argument heard in one register stops being heard at all. The Qur'an therefore *rotates its modalities* — the same doctrine of accountability arrives as history (ʿĀd), as parable (the gardeners, 18:32), as direct oath (91:1–15), as courtroom scene (36:52–65), as question (52:30–43). Al-Qurṭubī observes that variation is mercy tailored to variety of temperament: the historian is reached by narrative, the logicians by argument, the anxious by promise, the proud by warning. Teachers and preachers since have made this the sunnah of their pulpits: the message is one, but its clothing is tailored anew — *wa-ḍaribnā li-nās* mathalan? — because hearts, like eyes, see best what is presented from an angle they had not fixed. Any student of the Qur'an who has found that "the same verse" speaks differently in different years of his life has experienced precisely what this clause promises.

**"That They Might Return": Judgement's Secret Purpose**

The verse's final words are its tenderest: *laʿallahum yarjiʿūn* — that haply they might return. The stated goal of destruction's display is not terror but *return* — the verb r-j-ʿ, to turn back, the root of *tawbah* itself in its concrete sense of coming home. Everything God does in this sūrah — the ruins, the varied signs, the wind story, the jinn's testimony, the promised Day — bends toward one outcome: that the human being turn. The Qur'an thereby reveals a stunning asymmetry: God does not delight in the destruction; He *recycles* it — even the punishment of past peoples is salvaged as signage for future ones. Ibn Taymiyyah notes that the Qur'an calls God's punishment "a mercy for the worlds" in this derivative sense: it restrains evil and awakens sleepers, and even its memory works as medicine. For the believer, the verse settles the heart's question about the justice of displaying the damned: the exhibit exists because the Curator loves the visitors more than they know, and the exit sign reads, in every language of every age, *return*.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:28

> **Why, then, did they—whom they had taken as gods apart from God and as a means of drawing nigh [unto God]—not help them? Nay, they forsook them. That was their perversion and that which they used to fabricate.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Intermediary Theory of Worship on Trial**

Verse 28 completes the case against shirk by asking the question the ruins had already answered: *why, then, did they not help them?* The "they" is double — the destroyed nations, and the gods they worshiped. The verse names the gods by their theological job description: *qurbān* — a means of drawing near (from q-r-b, nearness). This is the classical self-understanding of sophisticated polytheism, which the Qur'an quotes verbatim from the mouths of its practitioners:

> *"And those who take protectors apart from God say: we do not worship them save that they may bring us near to God."* (al-Zumar 39:3)

The doctrine is the "intermediary clause": we do not deny the High God; we merely retain middlemen. The Qur'an's answer in this verse is empirical, not merely theoretical: the middlemen were subpoenaed by history — at al-Ḥijr, at the dunes of ʿĀd, at the dead banks of Sabaʾ's dam — and not one appeared in court. The gods did not answer because they *could* not, as verse 5 of this sūrah had already established; but here the point is pressed into the historical record for the Quraysh's own eyes: *nay, they forsook them* — *ḍallū ʿanhum*, literally "they went astray from them," became lost to them, disappeared from their side. Some commentators, as the grammar permits, read the converse: the worshippers strayed from the gods — either way, the partnership dissolves exactly when it is needed. The Qur'an had already pronounced the verdict generally:

> *"And they have taken gods apart from God, that they might be for them a source of strength. Nay! They will deny their worship and become opponents against them."* (Maryam 19:81–82)

> *"Or have they gods that defend them besides Us? They cannot help themselves, nor are they protected from Us."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21:43)

**No Intercession Without a License**

The theological principle underneath the verse is one of the Qur'an's most carefully fenced doctrines: mediation before God is not a commodity in private hands. Intercession exists, but it exists by permission, not by brokerage:

> *"Who is it that intercedes with Him save by His permission?"* (al-Baqarah 2:255)

> *"They have no intercession, save such as has gained permission from his Lord."* (Yā Sīn 36:23's theme; the permission-doctrine at 20:109 and 34:23: *"Intercession avails nothing with Him, save for one to whom He has given leave...")

The Quraysh's error was not the concept of nearness-seeking — the pilgrim seeks nearness — but the illegal franchise of it: appointing unauthorized agents, then worshipping the agents. Ibn Taymiyyah, analyzing the shirk of the Arabs, concluded that all of it reduced to this one move — a true instinct (drawing near to God) executed through a false mechanism (created intermediaries invested with independent power). And the falsification test of the mechanism is precisely this verse's question: *when the crisis came, did the intermediary deliver?* The entire archaeological ring around the Quraysh answered in silence.

**"Their Perversion and What They Fabricated"**

The verse closes with two verdict-nouns: *that was their ifk* — their perversion, the Qur'an's strongest word for a lie inverted into a worldview (the same term used of the slander of ʿĀʾishah and of the pagans' creed generally) — *and that which they used to fabricate* — *yaftarūn*, the forging verb of verse 8. The connection between verse 8 and verse 28 closes a loop: the Quraysh accused the Prophet of *iftirāʾ* — fabricating God's speech — while their entire religious system was, in God's accounting, the actual fabrication: gods invented, powers delegated, mediations licensed — all on human authority, all without one revealed document (v. 4's demand), all disproven at the first historical audit. The charge rebounded to its authors. This is a recurring Qur'anic irony worth meditating on: the accusation the deniers level is very often the autobiography they cannot read — the forger calls the honest man a forger, the fabricators condemn fabrication.

**The Modern Franchises of the Middleman**

The verse is not safely archived in pre-Islamic Arabia. The intermediation instinct — outsourcing one's relationship with the Ultimate to a broker who takes a commission — reappears wherever a created being is invested with the power to benefit or harm independently of God: the saint's tomb consulted as a licensing office, the amulet as insurance, the guru as indispensable conduit, and in secular dress, the entire industry of access-selling — connections, back doors, fixers. Islam's tawḥīd is the radical flattening of every such franchise: *the nearest of them does not seek a way to God except through obedience* — as the Qur'an says of even the righteous servants, *those whom they call upon seek a means to their Lord, striving as to which of them is nearest* (al-Isrāʾ 17:57) — they seek nearness by obedience, not by being sought. The believer's dignity, purchased in this verse, is direct access: no god needs to be fed before the prayer ascends; the Lord of the worlds is nearer than the middleman ever was — *and when My servants ask thee of Me, I am near* (al-Baqarah 2:186).

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:29

> **And [remember] when We made a group of jinn incline unto thee, listening to the Quran, when in its presence they said, "Hearken!" Then when it came to an end, they went back to their people as warners.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Divine Redirect on the Road from Ṭāʾif**

The sūrah now records an event of prophetic history from the hardest season of the Makkan mission. The classical context, as the source summarizes, is this: a group of jinn — seven or nine, in the reports most cited — were made to incline toward the Prophet ﷺ while he recited the dawn prayer in the valley of Nakhlah, on his return journey from Ṭāʾif, where the city's folk had set their children upon him. It is one of history's tender ironies: the city of men rejected him, and God comforted His Messenger with an audience from a world men cannot see. The account that anchors the episode is the long hadith of Ibn ʿAbbās:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ set out with a group of his companions intending the market of ʿUkkāẓ... and the jinn had been barred from hearing [the heavens] and pursued by shooting stars. So a group [of them] alighted at Nakhlah, while the Prophet ﷺ was leading his companions in the dawn prayer. When they heard the Qur'an, they listened attentively and said: 'This is what has come between you and the hearing of the heavens!' — and they returned to their people as warners. Then God revealed to His Prophet: 'Say: it has been revealed to me that a group of the jinn listened...' (72:1)."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 449, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

Sūrah al-Jinn (72) gives the event in the jinn's own first-person report; Sūrah al-Aḥqāf, revealed in the same period, recalls it here as the sūrah's hinge: after the Quraysh's refusal, a preview of humanity's future — unseen hearers receiving the Qur'an and becoming its missionaries.

**"We Made Them Incline": The Grammar of Grace**

The verb the verse chooses for the encounter is exact: *ṣarafnā* — We diverted, redirected, turned *toward* you — a group of jinn. The jinn did not stumble upon the recitation by chance; they were steered into earshot. The same verb in another form describes God's turning hearts; here it describes the traffic-control of the unseen worlds: audiences are assembled by heaven. For the discouraged preacher, the doctrine is oxygen: the daʿwah's outcomes are not commensurate with visible responses — God is delivering the message, this hour, to hearers the messenger cannot number or see: the future reader, the distant listener, the seeker in another dimension of creation entirely. The Qur'an's own horizon of mission was always wider than its messenger's sight: *O assembly of jinn and mankind* (55:33); *did there not come to you messengers from among you?* (6:130).

**"Hearken!": Liturgical Etiquette from the Unseen Congregation**

What the jinn said on arrival is preserved in one imperative: *anṣitū* — "Be silent! Listen!" — the command of full-body listening, from the root n-ṣ-t. It is a word of the mosque, not the marketplace, and the Qur'an legislates it for the visible congregation as well:

> *"And when the Qur'an is recited, listen to it and be silent, that you may receive mercy."* (al-Aʿrāf 7:204)

The jinn thus model, before the angels' own audience, the adab of recitation: hush first, understanding second, mission third. Compare their conduct with the Quraysh's — the same city, the same recitation, the same weeks: the Quraysh conversed over it, mocked it, labeled it sorcery and fabrication; the jinn fell silent, and then organized a daʿwah. Ibn Kathīr notes the pointed contrast: the unseen beings recognized in one dawn what the seen elite refused across a decade. The believer who worries that the world's noise has drowned the Qur'an should remember that its most attentive congregation once arrived invisibly, in the dark, at a roadside prayer.

**Warners, Not Spectators**

The verse's final clause completes the pattern: *when it was concluded, they returned to their people as warners* — *mundhirīn*. The jinn did not return as scholars, mystics, or tourists with a story; they returned as *warners* — the very title the Qur'an gives its Prophet (v. 9). One recitation, rightly received, produced missionaries. The sequence is the sūrah's whole theology of the Word in miniature: hear → hush → believe → return → warn. And it maps exactly onto what the Muslims of Makkah were being trained to do with the Qur'an themselves — and what every generation of believers since has been commissioned to do: take the recitation, and carry it home. The Prophet ﷺ guaranteed the economy of that carrying:

> *"Whoever calls to guidance has reward like the rewards of those who follow him, without diminishing their rewards at all."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2674, from Jarīr ibn ʿAbdillāh)

The jinn of Nakhlah earned, in one dawn, the standing of callers; and the sūrah will next record their sermon — the model khutbah of the unseen world, 46:30–32.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:30

> **They said, "O our people! Truly we have heard a Book sent down after Moses, confirming that which came before it, guiding to the truth and to a straight road."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sermon of the Jinn: A Model Certificate of the Qur'an**

What the jinn preached to their own people is recorded as direct speech — the Qur'an preserving the sermon of the unseen congregation for the instruction of the seen one. Their address opens with the warmest word of Arabian oratory: *yā qawmanā* — "O our people!" — the same vocative every prophet from Noah to Ṣāliḥ used; the jinn had learned, in a single recitation, the etiquette of prophetic address: identify with your people, then tell them the truth. Then follows their testimony in three clauses, which together form one of the Qur'an's most compact self-descriptions: *a Book sent down after Moses, confirming that which came before it, guiding to the truth and to a straight road.* Every clause answers a question a seeker should ask of any scripture. Origin: *sent down* — *unzila* — from above, not composed below. Chronology and continuity: *after Moses* — a link in the chain, not a rupture. Relation to prior revelation: *confirming* — *muṣaddiqan* — validating the truth still traceable in what came before. Function: *guiding* — *yahdī* — to *al-ḥaqq*, the Reality/God, and to *a straight road* — *ṭarīq mustaqīm*.

**Why "After Moses"? The Chain of Custody**

The jinn's choice of Moses as the reference point — rather than Abraham, David, or Jesus — is discussed by the commentators, as the source records. Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī note one reading: these jinn had been Jews, or lived among the People of the Book, and judged new revelation by the Torah's standard. But the broader reading fits the wider context: Moses is the revelation-paradigm — the messenger with a Book and a law, confronting a tyrant, whose story every community of the Book knew. To say *after Moses* is to file the Qur'an in the world's known archive of heavenly Books, precisely as verse 12 had done: *before it was the Book of Moses, a guide and a mercy; and this is a Book confirming...* The jinn, in other words, heard one recitation and correctly located the Book in its lineage — an act of religious literacy the Quraysh, with far more exposure, never performed. It is a standing rebuke: proximity to revelation is not comprehension of it; the ear is in the heart.

**The Straight Road: A Qur'an's-Worth of Meaning in One Word**

This verse is unique in the Qur'an for saying *ṭarīq mustaqīm* — a straight *road* — where the Book's standard term, repeated daily in every prayer, is *ṣirāṭ mustaqīm*, the straight *path* (al-Fātiḥah 1:6). Scholars of the Qur'an's diction savor the distinction: a *ṣirāṭ* is the single, defined way; a *ṭarīq* is a trodden road, wide enough for travel, beaten by the feet of travelers. Al-Tustarī hears in the pairing a mystic reading the source preserves:

> *"It indicates that the road of truth is to leave worldly transactions (muʿāmalāt) and formalities (rusūmāt), and to realize the truth, which is the straight path."* (al-Tustarī, as cited in the source)

Whether one follows Tustarī inward or the philologists outward, the phrase's import is the same: the Qur'an does not merely describe the road; it *is* the road under the traveler's feet — guidance in the practical, walkable sense, from the first step (a clean intention) to the last (a good ending). The jinn's sermon thus ends where every sermon should: not with wonder at the Book, but with direction along the road — *guiding to the truth* (al-Rāzī: to God, *al-Ḥaqq* being His name) *and to a way home*.

**The Qur'an as Its Own Best Argument**

There is a final, quiet apologetic in the passage as a whole. The jinn were experts in neither Arabic rhetoric nor the religion of Abraham — they were, in fact, from a species the Quraysh themselves worshipped and consulted as soothsayers. If the Qur'an were "sorcery imparted" or "fables of the ancients," these hearers — themselves dwellers in the realm of the occult — were the ideal auditors to detect the forgery, since jinn, of all created beings, know what originates in their own world. Instead they issued the certificate: *sent down, confirming, guiding*. The Qur'an relished the irony and preserved their sermon verbatim. And it left a standing test for every reader in every century: the Book's credentials have never depended on the approval of its critics' establishment — only on its power to make honest hearers, of whatever world, into warners. The jinn passed the test in one dawn; the invitation of the next verse — *answer God's caller* — extends the same examination to us.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:31

> **O our people! Answer God's caller and believe in him, that He may forgive you some of your sins and protect you from a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Caller Has a Name: Daʿwah as God's Own Project**

The jinn's sermon reaches its summons: *answer God's caller* — *ajībū dāʿiya Allāh*. The genitive is decisive: the caller is *God's* — the daʿwah to the Qur'an is not the Prophet's private enterprise, still less the jinn's initiative; it is God's own project staffed by God's appointee. The Qur'an uses the same frame in the believers' famous prayer:

> *"Our Lord, we have heard a caller calling to faith: 'Believe in your Lord' — and we have believed."* (Āl ʿImrān 3:193)

Al-Ṭabarī records the options the source notes: to answer the caller is to respond to the Prophet ﷺ; *believe in him* may mean believe in the Prophet, or in God, or in all that the Prophet commands. The options converge, for in Qur'anic grammar the Prophet is believed *in* precisely as God's authorized voice — answering him is answering God, and ignoring him is ignoring God: *whoso obeys the Messenger has obeyed God* (al-Nisāʾ 4:80). This doctrine dignifies the office of every caller since: the Sunday-school teacher, the scholar, the parent reciting to a child — all are honored precisely as *dāʿīs of God*, and the duty of response owed to them is owed, in truth, to the One who deputized the message. The Prophet ﷺ attached heaven's reward-structure to the office:

> *"Whoever calls to guidance has a reward like the rewards of those who follow him, without diminishing their rewards at all; and whoever calls to misguidance bears its burden like the burdens of those who follow it."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2674, from Jarīr ibn ʿAbdillāh — the full form of the report cited at verse 29)

**"Some of Your Sins": Faith's Ledger and Deeds' Completion**

The promised reward is stated with a precision that provoked the commentators, as the source records: *that He may forgive you* — the Arabic reads *min dhunūbikum* — *some of your sins*. Some read the *min* strictly: faith alone wipes some sins, and the rest await repentance, restitution, and deeds done in obedience — a doctrine that makes belief the beginning of accounting, not the cancellation of it. Others read *min* as explanatory: "your sins, namely those you committed," with forgiveness total. The first reading, which al-Maybudī and al-Rāzī report, carries the sūrah's internal logic: verse 16 promised the Garden to those whose *best deeds* were accepted and evil *passed over* — an acceptance contingent on the standing firm of verse 13; the forgiveness of verse 31 opens the account, and the lifetime of verse 15 funds it. Islam's grammar of salvation is thus neither a single-transaction absolution nor an anxious earning-scam: it is an adopted relationship whose forgiveness is real, daily, and proportioned to a life actually being lived with God.

**Protection from the Painful Punishment**

The second half of the promise: *and protect you from a painful punishment* — *wa-yujirkum*, from the root w-j-r, to shield, grant refuge — a word of sanctuary, of being taken *inside* a protection. Forgiveness deals with the past; protection deals with what would come. The pairing is complete salvation: absolution behind, shelter ahead. And the shelter's logic recalls the sūrah's own courtroom: the jinn themselves were among those whom the idolaters called upon for protection (vv. 4–5) — and they knew, better than any audience alive, that *they* could protect no one; hence the urgency of redirecting their people to the One who can. Verse 32 will state the negative corollary: whoever does not answer God's caller has *no protectors* besides Him. The jinn's sermon thus closes with the only insurance policy they had ever verified: God's.

**The Caller Within: Tustarī's Interior Reading**

Al-Tustarī, whose tafsīr is a treasury of the early Sufis' inward hearing, draws from this verse a reading the source preserves:

> *"In the heart of every believer there is a caller calling him to his sound judgment (rushd). Happy is the one who listens to the call of the caller, and follows it."* (al-Tustarī, as cited in the source)

The outward daʿwah and the inward caller are one providence: God recruits external messengers to echo the address He has already placed in the fitrah, the primordial constitution (30:30). Every human being knows the sensation: the pull at the conscience at a threshold moment — the unreturned trust, the overdue prayer, the word of truth swallowed in a meeting. Tustarī names that pull *the caller*, and pronounces the same blessing on its hearers that the jinn pronounced on theirs. The verse therefore meets every reader where he sits: somewhere, at this hour, a caller — a recitation, an invitation, a conscience — is sounding. The jinn of Nakhlah answered within one dawn. *Ajībū dāʿiya Allāh*: answer God's caller. That is the whole of religion in three words.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:32

> **And whosoever does not answer God's caller thwarts not on earth and has no protectors apart from Him—they are in manifest error."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Futility Clause: Nowhere to Outrun God**

The jinn's sermon ends by stating the negative side of the summons, in two annihilations of hope. First: *whoever does not answer God's caller cannot thwart on earth* — *falaysa bi-muʿjizin fī al-arḍ*. The word *muʿjiz* is from the root ʿ-j-z, whose core meaning is the point at which strength *fails* — the inability to keep pace, to catch up, to escape the stronger party. To "thwart" God is to outsprint Him, to reach a place His hand does not close upon. The Qur'an's verdict on that project is promulgated in more than a dozen verses, as the source catalogs:

> *"You cannot escape on earth or in heaven; and you have no protector nor helper apart from God."* (al-ʿAnkūbūt 29:22)

> *"...and they were not, in the earth, ones to thwart — nor had they protectors apart from God."* (Sabaʾ 34:22's theme; the refrain recurs at 6:134; 8:59; 10:53; 11:20; 16:46; 22:51; 24:57; 34:5, 38; 35:44; 42:31)

The image is of a runner discovering that the terrain itself is the pursuer's property: every road is His road, every border His border. Al-Qurṭubī remarks that the verse's geography is deliberate — *in the earth* — because the earth is precisely where the rebellious imagine their scope: its caves, its distant coasts, its wealth, its refuges. The heavens were never in question; the surprise is that the earth, configured as a habitat for man's freedom, remains God's jurisdiction absolutely.

**The Protector Clause: Every False Guardian Dissolves**

Second: *and has no protectors apart from Him* — *awliyāʾ min dūnihi*. The term *walī* is the Qur'an's word for the patron-guardian — the champion, advocate, and sheltering power — and the Book returns relentlessly to the audit of false wilāyah:

> *"Or have they taken protectors apart from Him? But God — He is the Protector; He gives life to the dead; and He is powerful over all things."* (al-Shūrā 42:9)

> *"...those who take protectors apart from Him: God is the Guardian of them; and thou art not a guardian over them."* (al-Anʿām 6:66's theme; and the decisive inventory at 7:196)

The jinn knew this clause experientially: their own kind were invoked as protectors by the idolaters (v. 29's context) — and no jinn had ever averted a decree, not for their worshippers and not for themselves. Their sermon, ending on *no protectors apart from Him*, is thus the testimony of the very class of beings falsely appointed to the job. It is as if the false gods' own colleagues, breaking ranks, published the disclaimer their worshippers never read.

**"Manifest Error": The Diagnosis, Not the Insult**

The verse — and the jinn's entire sermon — closes with the clinical label: *they are in manifest error* — *fī ḍalālin mubīn*, error made clear, self-evident once stated. The label is not invective; it is geometry. Remove the Caller, and there is no direction; remove the Protector, and there is no shelter; keep the freedom, and it becomes the freedom of a swimmer beyond the horizon — absolute, and irrelevant. The Qur'an's logic of dalāl is always this twofold coordinates: the misdirection is *manifest* precisely because the alternative is *mustaqīm*, straight, walkable, marked by a Book and a caller. One does not call a road lost except from the vantage of one that is found.

**The Sermon's Mirror for the Modern Reader**

Set beside its moment of delivery, the jinn's closing line is almost unbearably apt. They spoke it of their own people — and the Qur'an preserved it because Makkah, and every city since, was in the same condition. The modern refusals of the Caller arrive dressed differently: busyness, deferral, intellectual fashion, the comfortable assumption that consequences, being distant, are doubtful. The verse answers all of them with two facts that no technology will amend: the earth remains jurisdiction without exit, and every protector man fabricates — wealth, state, wellness routine, ideology — remains a creature of the term appointed. The sermon's final word belongs, fittingly, to the only two real securities named in it: the Caller, who must be answered, and God, beside whom there is no protection. Between those two poles — answer and shelter — lies the entire straight road the jinn announced; outside them, *manifest error* is not a curse but a map's honest legend.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:33

> **Have they not considered that God, Who created the heavens and the earth and did not weary in their creation, is able to give life to the dead? Yea! He is Powerful over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument from Greater to Lesser**

The sūrah now returns from the jinn's sermon to the lingering root of the Makkan denial — the resurrection, which verse 17 had already mocked through the ungrateful son: *generations have passed away before me* — and it does so with the Qur'an's favorite proof-structure: the a fortiori argument, qiyās al-awlā, "the judgment of the stronger case." *Have they not considered* — *a-wa-lam yaraw*, did they not look with the looking of reflection — *that God, who created the heavens and the earth and did not weary in their creation, is able to give life to the dead?* The logic: restoration is a lesser feat than origination. One who builds the furnace need not prove he can relight it. Ibn Kathīr, as the source notes, presents the verse exactly so: if the creation of the heavens and the earth — immeasurable, unrepeatable by man in even the smallest degree — presented no difficulty, the reassembling of one small body is not a second category of power. The Qur'an states the comparison as a ranking:

> *"Creation of the heavens and the earth is greater than the creation of mankind; but most people know not."* (Ghāfir 40:57)

And it hurls the argument back at the denier with a question of its own:

> *"Does man not see that We created him from a drop of fluid? Yet lo, he is an open adversary! And he coins for Us a likeness, and forgets his own creation. He says: who will give life to bones when they are decayed? Say: He will give them life who produced them the first time; and He knows every creature."* (Yā Sīn 36:77–79)

**The Theology of a God Who Does Not Tire**

The verse's middle clause is a doctrine in four words: *did not weary in their creation* — *wa-lam yaʾsa bikhalqihinna*, from the root ʿ-y-y, to be fatigued. Fatigue belongs to the economy of creatures: we tire because our energy is finite and our outputs draw it down. God's act draws on nothing and depletes nothing — the doctrine the Qur'an had already enshrined in the Pedestal Verse:

> *"Neither slumber overtakes Him, nor sleep."* (al-Baqarah 2:255)

Al-Qurṭubī, commenting on 2:255, explains that the negation of fatigue entails the negation of any need for rest, and of any limit on the scope of sustained action: creation is not an event in God's past that He recovered from; it is a continuously sustained state — *He holds the heavens and the earth lest they vanish* (35:41). The connection to resurrection is then airtight: resurrection would be *additional work* only for a being whose workload matters. For a God unwearied by galaxies, the reanimation of the dead costs no more than the first animation did, and the first animation is already on record. The Qur'an presses the point through the vocabulary of ease:

> *"It is He who begins creation, then repeats it — and it is easier for Him."* (al-Rūm 30:27)

**Yea! He Is Powerful over All Things**

The verse's own answer to its question is an exclamation of certainty: *balā* — "Yes indeed! Verily!" — *He is Powerful over all things.* The Qur'an lets no silence follow its question; the truth is too plain for suspense. It is the same affirmative thunder used at 75:3–4, where God answers the skeptics of bodily resurrection with the detail of details:

> *"Does man reckon he will be left aimless? Was he not a drop of spilled fluid? ... And that He is able to return him, on the day when hidden things are tried — that day he has no strength nor helper."* (al-Qiyāmah 75:36–37, paraphrased; and) *"We are able to restore his very fingertips."* (al-Qiyāmah 75:4)

The fingerprints — identity's irreducible signature — are God's chosen emblem of the completeness of re-creation: not a generic revivification, but *you*, restored to the last line of the last finger.

**Everyday Resurrections Already Delivered**

The Qur'an never leaves resurrection in the realm of the abstract; it posts daily collateral in every man's experience. Sleep is a small death with a nightly raising — *God takes souls at the time of their death, and those that die not during their sleep; He keeps those on whom He has decreed death, and sends the others back for an appointed term* (al-Zumar 39:42). The parched earth resurrects with every rain:

> *"And among His signs is that thou seest the earth humble; then, when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells — He who gives it life will surely give life to the dead; and He is powerful over all things."* (Fuṣṣilat 41:39)

The argument of verse 33 is thus not merely logical but empirical: the audience had watched a thousand mini-resurrections before breakfast. Al-Ṭabarī closes his comment with the practical upshot: the one who doubts the raising has, in truth, stopped looking at the world — for the heavens above him and the seedbed beneath him are two witnesses he passes daily without cross-examining either. The believer, by contrast, lives inside the evidence: every morning he wakes on time, in a body he did not reassemble, on an earth greening again after winter — a standing foretaste of *balā, He is powerful over all things*.

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:34

> **On the day when those who disbelieve are exposed to the Fire: "Is this not true?" They will say, "Yea, by our Lord!" He will reply, "Taste the punishment for having disbelieved."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Asked When Answering No Longer Avails**

The scene is the terrible mirror of verse 33's certainty. On the Day the disbelievers are *exposed to the Fire* — presented to it, as verse 20 has already staged — God asks one question: *a-hādhā haqqun* — "Is this not true?" The interrogative is almost gentle; the situation is not. Their reply is an oath: *naʿam wa-rabbinā* — "Yes, by our Lord!" — the confession wrung from sight itself. And the verdict follows immediately: *taste the punishment for that which you disbelieved.* The structure of the exchange deserves close attention, because it discloses the Qur'an's deepest law of faith: acknowledgment obtained by sight is not faith, and therefore saves no one. During the term appointed — the very lifetime the deniers spent demanding signs — assent was free and therefore meritorious; in the Hereafter, assent is compelled by evidence and therefore merely factual. The verse's sister passage makes the principle unmistakable:

> *"If you could but see when they are set before their Lord! He will say: is this not real? They will say: yes, by our Lord! He will say: then taste the punishment for that which you disbelieved."* (al-Anʿām 6:30)

The same scene replays for Pharaoh, who believed at the moment the sea closed over him: *now? When before you rebelled...* (Yūnus 10:91); and the Qur'an states the rule that governs all such late conversions:

> *"So when they saw Our might, they said: we believe in God alone... But their faith availed them not once they saw Our might — the sunnah of God which has passed among His servants; and there the disbelievers lost."* (Ghāfir 40:84–85)

**The Cancellation of the Alibi**

Beneath the dialogue lies a quiet judicial marvel: the question *is this not true?* is asked not to inform God, nor even to inform them — it is asked to close every file without exception. On that Day, the last ideological alibi — "we would have believed if we had seen" — is examined and found to have been a lie, for when they see, they do believe, and it avails nothing. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the confession *by our Lord* adds the final irony: the deniers in life refused to swear by Him truly, and swear by Him they now do, in the first honest sentence of their eternity — a sentence that doubles as their sentence. The Qur'an elsewhere shows the righteous in the same courtroom across the aisle, affirming the same reality with opposite result:

> *"And the companions of the Garden will call to the companions of the Fire: we have found what our Lord promised us true — have you found what your Lord promised true? They will say: yes."* (al-Aʿrāf 7:44)

Two verdicts from one shared "yes": the difference lay entirely in the direction each faced before the veil lifted. And the verses that follow 7:44 strip away any residual hope in class, wealth, or numbers on that day — as this sūrah's verses 19–20 have already decreed.

**The Closed Door of Belated Repentance**

Islamic theology reads in this verse the sealing of the door of tawbah at death. The Qur'an states the deadline explicitly:

> *"Repentance is not for those who do evil deeds until, when death comes to one of them, he says: I repent now — nor for those who die disbelieving."* (al-Nisāʾ 4:18)

The mercy of God, vaster than the sins of the world in this life, is not arbitrary in the next: it honors the structure of moral probation, which requires that the choice be made without compulsion of sight. A merchant who demands to see the harvest before planting has not traded; a bride who demands proof of the marriage's happiness before vowing has not married; faith is the vow made in trust — *al-ghayb* — and the Qur'an defines the reverent precisely as those who *believe in the Unseen* (al-Baqarah 2:3). Verse 34 is thus, paradoxically, one of the Book's most powerful invitations: it is the ultimatum's photocopy, delivered early, while the ink of choice is still wet — *taste* is still avoidable, the *yes* still savable, the Door still, in this hour, open.

**"Taste": The Verb of Experience Chosen for the Reckoning**

Finally, note God's chosen verb: *fa-dhūqū* — "taste." Not "endure," not "receive," but taste — the verb of the tongue, the most intimate and undeniable of the senses. The Qur'an uses it of punishment dozens of times (3:181; 4:56; 6:30; 32:14), and its aptness is exact: the deniers' whole crime was the refusal of experience — they declined to *taste* faith, to sample surrender, to risk the experiment of prayer; they demanded instead proof at a distance, data without devotion. The verdict answers the refusal in kind: an experience, at last, that cannot be held at a distance. For the believer, the verse lands as the reverse resolution: taste now — the sweetness of faith has never once been described by those who practised it as inferior to the theory of it; and the sweetest of what God prepared for those who do is, by His own report, beyond the record of any eye or ear (al-Bukhārī 7498).

## Sūrah al-Aḥqāf 46:35

> **So be patient, as the resolute among the messengers were patient. And seek not to hasten for them. It shall be for them, on the day when they see that which they are promised, as though they had tarried naught but an hour of a day. A proclamation! Will any but the iniquitous people be destroyed?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's Last Word to the Weary Messenger**

Everything in al-Aḥqāf has pointed to this closing command: *fa-iṣbir* — so be patient. The Prophet ﷺ, in the sūrah's own setting, was returning from Ṭāʾif bleeding and rebuffed, having preached a decade to a city that answered with "sorcery," "fabrication," and "fables of the ancients"; and the sūrah that recounted Hūd's rebuffs, the jinn's dawn conversion, and the Fire's final "yes" now resolves into a single prescription: patience of a specific grade — *kamā ṣabara ūlū al-ʿazmi mina al-rusul* — "as the resolute (ūlū al-ʿazm) among the messengers were patient." The instruction is not merely to endure, but to endure *in the company of an elite*: the ūlū al-ʿazm are, in the interpretation most cited by Ibn Kathīr and al-Kalbī as the source records, the messengers who bore new Books and new laws — Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muḥammad ﷺ, the five named together in the covenant verse:

> *"And when We took from the prophets their covenant — and from thee, and from Noah, and Abraham, and Moses, and Jesus son of Mary..."* (al-Aḥzāb 33:7)

Others, as al-Qurṭubī catalogues, proposed narrower or wider lists, and al-Tustarī, characteristically, read the term through the lens of trials endured:

> *"The resolute among the messengers are Abraham, God's friend; he was tried with fire and with the sacrifice of a son, but was content and submitted. Job was tried by trials... Noah was tried with denial but was patient... Jonah was in the belly of the fish... Joseph was tried with prison and the well... Jacob was tested by the loss of his sight and the loss of a son... There are twelve prophets who were patient with what befell them, and they are the resolute among the messengers."* (al-Tustarī, as cited in the source)

Whatever list one holds, the definition of ʿazm is constant: unbending resolve under a load that would bend ordinary piety. The Prophet ﷺ is being told, in effect: your weariness is real, but you are not the first to carry it — take the heavier precedents as your fellowship.

**"Seek Not to Hasten": The Prohibition on Praying for the Fire**

The second command has a precise prophetic context: *and seek not to hasten for them* — *wa-lā tastaʿjil lahum*. The exegetes, as the source notes from Ibn Kathīr's citations, understand it as a restraint upon invoking destruction against the mockers — as some prophets before had done, and as the Prophet ﷺ was sorely tempted to do after Ṭāʾif. When the angel of the mountains offered to crush Makkah between the two peaks, the Prophet's answer has become the emblem of the mission's whole spirit:

> *"Rather, I hope that God will bring forth from their descendants those who will worship God alone, associating nothing with Him."* (al-Bukhārī 3231; Muslim 1795, from ʿĀʾishah)

And on the day of Uḥud, bleeding from his own people's blows, he prayed the prayer of his brother-prophets:

> *"O God, forgive my people, for they know not."* (al-Bukhārī 3477; Muslim 1792, from Ibn Masʿūd)

The prohibition thus encodes a theology of time: punishment deferred is not justice denied; the term appointed (v. 3) has margins of mercy, and the caller's office is testimony, not execution. Al-Qurṭubī adds God's two possible reasons, as the source records: postponement as mercy — the door of tawbah held open — or as accumulation of a greater requital for the obdurate. Either way, the servant is freed from the one burden he was never assigned: the calendar.

**An Hour of a Day: The Collapse of Worldly Time**

The verse then gives the Prophet the consolation of perspective — a preview of the Day of Resurrection from the viewpoint of those standing inside it: *it shall be for them, on the day they see what they are promised, as though they had tarried naught but an hour of a day.* The Qur'an is remarkably consistent in this arithmetic of the end of time:

> *"And on the day the Hour arrives, the criminals will swear they tarried but an hour; thus were they used to deluding themselves. But those given knowledge and faith will say: you tarried, by the decree of God, until the Day of Resurrection — this is the Day of Resurrection, but you knew not."* (al-Rūm 30:55–56, and the same at 10:45; 17:52; 23:112–114; 79:46)

A lifetime of mockery, and a wind's seven nights, and two thousand years of grave-silence — all of it, viewed from eternity's rim, one afternoon's hour. The consolation cuts both ways, and is meant to: for the weary believer, it shrinks his trial; for the heedless, it shrinks his excuse. What is patience for a few hours' wait, when the wait ends in the Garden of verse 14? And what is pleasure's squandering (v. 20) for a single hour's comfort? The entire Qur'anic doctrine of the world's smallness is compressed into this clause, and the Prophet ﷺ taught his community to read the smallness both ways: *the world, compared to the Hereafter, is like one of you dipping his finger in the sea — let him see what it brings back* (Muslim, reported in the famous imagery of the hadith of Sahl).

**"A Proclamation! Will Any but the Iniquitous Be Destroyed?"**

The sūrah's final words: *balāgh* — a proclamation, a message delivered, a term of completion: the Prophet's mission is a *balāgh* — a fully communicated notice; and the closing question, *fa-hal yuhlaku illā al-qawmu al-fāsiqūn* — "will any be destroyed except the defiantly sinful people?" — is answered by the Qur'an's own law of justice, stated elsewhere as guarantee:

> *"And We never destroyed a township but it had warners — as a reminder; and We were never unjust."* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:208–209)

> *"And thy Lord would never destroy the towns unjustly, while their people were setting things right."* (Hūd 11:117)

Destruction is not an act of divine caprice but the terminus of a documented pattern: warning delivered, warning mocked, term expired. The question expects the answer *none* — no nation was ever destroyed for ignorance, only for defiance after knowledge; and no individual will be wronged by a single atom (v. 19). The sūrah thus ends by handing the verdict's pen back to its hearers: Hūd's people wrote their own ending; the Quraysh held the pen now; and every reader since — you — holds it still, while the ink of choice remains wet and the Caller still calls. From *Ḥāʾ Mīm* to *balāgh*, al-Aḥqāf has delivered its message complete: the Book is from the Mighty, the Wise; the ruins around you are not silent; the Fire's question is already drafted; the wind obeys its Lord; and the only unpardonable strategy, on this side of the veil, is to answer the Caller with the patience of the mockers instead of the patience of the messengers. *Fa-iṣbir* — so be patient: the last word of the Ḥawāmīm, and the first duty of everyone who reads them.
