# Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn (Chapter 83) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Named After Its Least Romantic Word**

Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn, "The Defrauders," takes its name from the very first word of its indictment: *waylun lil-muṭaffifīn* — "Woe unto the defrauders." Of all the titles the Qur'an could have chosen for a chapter that will end in the musk-sealed wine of Paradise and the unveiled vision of God, it chose a word for crooked merchants. That choice is itself the first lesson of the chapter. The Qur'an never presents religion as a private mood detached from the scales in a shop; it binds the destiny of souls to the weight of grain. Thirty-six verses — the seventy-fourth chapter in the order of the muṣḥaf, sitting inside the thirtieth part (Juzʾ ʿAmma) between Sūrah al-Infiṭār and Sūrah al-Burūj — carry a single moral argument from a merchant's thumb on the balance pan to the musk-sealed cup of the companions of the right, and the argument never changes direction: the measure you give is the measure you will receive.

The chapter belongs to the family of late sūrahs whose gaze is fixed on the Day of Judgment — al-Takwīr, al-Infiṭār, al-Ḥāqqah, al-Qāriʿah, al-Inshiqāq, al-Burūj — and it shares with its immediate twin, al-Infiṭār, an unmistakable symmetry. Al-Infiṭār compressed the whole moral universe into two lines:

> *Truly the pious shall be in bliss, and truly the profligate shall be in the Blaze.* (82:13–14)

Al-Muṭaffifīn unpacks that couplet into a full chapter: *the profligate in the Blaze* becomes the sealed book in Sijjīn, the rusted heart, the veil, and the Fire (83:7–17); *the pious in bliss* becomes the inscribed book in ʿIlliyyūn, the radiant faces, the couches, the pure wine, and Tasnīm (83:18–28). The two sūrahs were read by the tradition as a diptych — the same two destinies, first announced, then dramatized with names, places, tastes, and textures.

**When Was It Revealed: A Chapter Between Two Cities**

The chronology of al-Muṭaffifīn is unusually layered, and the classical sources preserve the debate. Al-Baghawī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ṭabarī transmit that the opening verses were revealed just after the Prophet's ﷺ arrival in Madinah, when its markets were notorious for cheating — the first four verses are therefore counted by many as among the earliest Madinan legislation, an economics text revealed before the community had finished unpacking. Verses 5–28 are then generally assigned to Madinah as well. But the final eight verses (83:29–36), which describe the wealthy mockers of the believers, carry the unmistakable climate of Makkah, where the followers of the new faith were the poor and the powerless and their persecutors were the merchant princes of Quraysh; many authorities consequently held that these verses were among the very last portions of the Qur'an revealed in Makkah before the Hijrah — while others maintain that everything after the first four verses was Makkan. The chapter thus spans, in the traditional accounts, the whole arc of the prophetic career: a Makkan court where believers are laughed at, and a Madinan market where measures are skimmed. Between those two cities, the sūrah insists, runs one unbroken moral law.

The sūrah also lived in the Prophet's ﷺ own liturgy. Ḥudhayfah ibn al-Yamān — the man the Prophet trusted with the secrets of the hypocrites — said of him:

> *"We used to trace out the places of his recitation and the places of his prostration: he recited al-Muṭaffifīn in the Maghrib prayer."* (al-Bukhārī 764)

The Companions knew not only that the Prophet recited the Qur'an, but *where in the prayer* he recited each sūrah, and they recorded it. The chapter of the crooked scales was sung over the evening meal of the first community — a quiet instruction that the honest weight is an act of worship to be renewed at every sunset.

Ibn Kathīr, in his commentary on the opening, also transmits a report of virtue — "whoever recites this sūrah, God will cast light into his two eyes on the day when mankind stands before the Lord of the worlds" — which the ḥadīth critics have graded weak, but whose very circulation among the early generations shows how closely the chapter's final scene (83:6, *a day when mankind shall stand before the Lord of the worlds*) was treasured: the reader of the warning was promised a share in the light of that standing.

**Why a Chapter on Market Scales Belongs in Revelation**

A modern reader might expect a scripture to speak of prayer and leave prices to the magistrates. The Qur'an does the opposite: it treats the marketplace as the opening theater of the Last Day. The reason is theologically precise. Fraud is the sin of the unwatched moment — committed in the back of the shop, beneath the counter, in the decimal of a spreadsheet, where no policeman stands and no neighbor sees. The only restraint that reaches that moment is belief in an audience that sees everything. When belief in the Resurrection decays, the hidden scale tips; when the hidden scale tips, the visible world fills with hidden theft. This is the logical spine of the whole chapter: verses 1–3 describe the fraud, verses 4–6 expose its root (*"Do they not think that they will be resurrected?"*), and the rest of the sūrah shows what the fraudsters have really written against themselves in the ledgers of heaven.

For the same reason, commerce runs through revelation as a standing moral test. The Prophet ﷺ had been a merchant for twenty-five years before prophethood, and tradition remembered him as *al-Amīn*, "the Trustworthy" — a title earned in trade before it was honored in prophecy. He said of the honest trader:

> *"The truthful, trustworthy merchant will be with the prophets, the truthful, and the martyrs."* (al-Tirmidhī 1209)

And he drew the negative border with equal clarity:

> *"Whoever cheats us is not one of us."* (Muslim 102)

The longest verse in the Qur'an (2:282) is not about prayer or pilgrimage but about the written documentation of a debt. The Qur'an legislates measures, prohibits usury, demands written contracts, protects the weak seller and the weak buyer, and repeatedly commands: *"Give full measure and be not among those who cause loss"* (26:181). Al-Muṭaffifīn is the chapter where all of that economic law finds its eschatological anchor — the place where the Qur'an explains *why* the scales matter: because every gram weighs on an eternal balance.

**The Architecture of the Chapter: Two Ledgers and One Reversal**

The chapter is built with a classic Qur'anic mirror. It opens (83:1–6) with the defrauders: they take in full and give short, because they do not truly believe in the Day of standing. It then divides humanity into two companies, each with its book and its place. The book of *the profligate* (al-fujjār) lies in **Sijjīn** — "Nay! Truly the book of the profligate is in Sijjīn... A book inscribed" (83:7–17) — followed by the rust that covers their hearts, the veil from their Lord, and the Fire, closing with the taunt: *"This is that which you used to deny."* Then the hinge-word *kallā* ("Nay!") turns the page: the book of *the pious* (al-abrār) lies in **ʿIlliyyūn** — "Nay, truly the book of the pious is in ʿIlliyyūn... A book inscribed, witnessed by those brought nigh" (83:18–28) — followed by the couches, the radiance of bliss on their faces, the sealed wine whose seal is musk, and its blend of Tasnīm, the spring of those brought nigh. The same verb pair, the same adjective (*marqūm*, "inscribed"), the same interrogative (*"And what will apprise thee of..."*), and the same opening particle are used for both companies: the Book deliberately weighs the two destinies on identical grammar, as though to say the difference lies not in the scales of language but in the scales of deeds.

The closing movement (83:29–36) is the chapter's dramatic reversal — the scene the tradition holds to be among the last words revealed in Makkah. In Makkah, the sinners laughed at the believers, winked at one another as they passed, returned home exulting, and pronounced them astray. The chapter answers with a single temporal inversion: *so that Day* — the believers laugh, upon couches, gazing, while the question is put to the mockers: *"Have the disbelievers been requited for that which they used to do?"* Every mockery in time is met by its counterpart in eternity; the laughter of the last days of Makkah is answered by the laughter of the first morning of the Resurrection.

**A Chapter for the Age of Shortcuts**

The reason this ancient chapter reads so freshly in the modern world is that fraud has not aged — it has modernized. The defrauder of Makkah pressed his thumb on the pan of grain; his heirs inflate the label on the bottle, quietly shrink the package, hide the fee in the fine print, pollute the sample, pad the invoice, copy the assignment, manipulate the metric, and sell the counterfeit with a genuine wrapper. Institutions rise and fall on the integrity of measurements no citizen will ever personally verify — which is precisely the situation the Qur'an addressed when it warned that the whole moral order rests on scales only God fully watches. Al-Muṭaffifīn teaches the believer a habit of soul: to give the same weight in secret as in public, because the One who weighs is present in both. And it teaches the deepest comfort of the wronged: no skimmed measure, no stolen hour, no cheated wage ever disappears from the record, for *"We shall set up the scales of justice on the Day of Resurrection, and no soul shall be wronged in anything — even if it be the weight of a mustard seed"* (21:47).

The commentary that follows treats each of the thirty-six verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order, exactly as mandated: every verse is quoted individually, then expanded through the Arabic language, the classical commentators, the Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the logic of its teaching, and its bearing upon modern life. Where the source groups verses, they are here separated and given full individual treatment. Nothing is compressed because the chapter is short; the measure the Qur'an demands in the marketplace is the measure this reading attempts to honor on the page — full weight, to the very edge.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:1

> **Woe unto the defrauders,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Heaviest Word in the Qur'an Placed on the Lightest of Sins**

The chapter does not open with a narrative, a command, or a description of the Hereafter. It opens with a verdict — a single word of ruin pronounced before any crime has even been named: *waylun*. Of all the imprecations in the Qur'an, *wayl* (وَيْلٌ) is among the most severe. Linguistically it is a noun of calamity and destruction, a word whose very sound falls like a stone; the Arabic lexicographers define it as destruction joined to humiliation. The Prophet ﷺ himself, when asked what *wayl* was, answered:

> *"It is a valley in Hell — were it not that you speak much of it, none of you would have the strength to hear of it."* (al-Tirmidhī 2576)

Whatever one makes of the details of that report, its point stands at the center of the verse: *wayl* is not a scolding. It is the Qur'an's word for a final, settled catastrophe. And here, astonishingly, that catastrophe is hung upon what many moralists in every age have classified as a petty sin — short measures in the marketplace. No adulterer, no murderer, no tyrant is addressed in the opening of this chapter; a shopkeeper with a thumb on the scale is. The placement teaches before a single argument is made: the severity of a sin in God's sight is not measured by its drama but by its reach — and the reach of a false measure extends silently into every meal of every household it touches.

The grammarians noticed something further. The definite article in *al-muṭaffifīn* — "the defrauders" — is what Arabic rhetoric calls a definite article of established occurrence (*alif-lām* of ʿahd, denoting that the thing has actually taken place). The Qur'an does not say "woe to whoever might defraud"; it says woe has *already* been pronounced upon a specific class of people whose crime is real and recorded. Commentators such as al-Zamakhsharī drew from this the lesson that the warning functions as settled law: the sentence is passed in heaven before the merchant finishes his final short measure on earth. A verdict announced in the past tense about a sin of the present is the Qur'an's way of collapsing the distance between the marketplace and the courtroom of the Last Day.

**The Anatomy of the Word: Ṭaff, Ṭafīf, and the Man Who Skims the Edge**

Every syllable of *al-muṭaffifīn* (الْمُطَفِّفِينَ) is doing moral work. The root is **ṭ-f-f**, and its primary concrete meaning is *the edge, the rim, the selvage of a thing* — the *ṭaff* of a garment is its hem; the *ṭaff* of a valley is where its slopes fall away. From "edge" the word came to mean *a tiny amount*, because the edge is the thinnest part of anything: a *ṭafīf* is a small quantity, a trifle, something barely worth counting. From there the verb *ṭaffafa* means to take or give slightly less than is due — to shave the rim off the measure. The intensive form *muṭaffif* therefore names a person defined by this practice, one for whom skimming the edge is not an incident but a method.

The precision of the imagery deserves a moment's reflection. The defrauder is not depicted as a robber who seizes a sack by force, nor as a liar who invents a false price by mutual haggling. He takes what everyone agrees is his to take — he *sells*, he *measures*, he *weighs* — and then, at the last inch, where the grain meets the rim of the measure, he keeps back a fistful. The crime lives in the gap between a lawful transaction and a full one. It is the sin of the near-honest. And that is why the word chosen for it is so exact: *ṭaff* — the edge — is the place where most people never look.

Al-Qurṭubī and the source commentary both note that the term broadened in the Qur'anic usage far beyond dry goods. *Al-muṭaffifīn* came to denote anyone who demands his own rights to the full while shortchanging the rights of others, and anyone who sees the faults of others with a microscope and his own faults with a telescope. A man who never touches a grain measure may still be a muṭaffif — in his office, his courtroom, his marriage, his classroom, his online reviews — wherever he applies to himself a generous measure and to others a stingy one. The Qur'an names the market practice, and the market practice names a universal human temptation.

**Why the Book of the Hereafter Begins with the Book of the Market**

The order of the chapter is not accidental, and the classical commentators paused over it. Before Sijjīn and ʿIlliyyūn are named, before the rust on hearts and the seal of musk, the Qur'an conducts its reader through a shop. Al-Rāzī and others drew the lesson: faith that does not govern scales is not faith but ornament. A community may pray in rows and still starve its customers by grams; God therefore begins not with the creed's summit but with the creed's proof. The marketplace is where belief is *audited* — the one arena a man enters daily, where his religion is tested not by what he says about God but by what he hands his neighbor across the counter. Ibn Masʿūd, the great Companion and early commentator, would later extend the very word of this sūrah to prayer itself — *"Prayer is a measure; whosoever completes its measure, God will complete his reward"* — showing how the first generation understood that the muṭaffif is any servant who shortchanges any duty. The chapter's opening verse thus installs a principle that will govern everything that follows: **the measure is moral, and the measure is everywhere.**

There is also a social logic in the choice. Theft by force provokes outrage and retaliation; theft by measure provokes almost nothing, because each victim loses too little to protest. The defrauder's method is to injure in quantities too small for anger. That is precisely why the Qur'an raises the volume: it supplies the outrage the victims cannot individually supply. One cheated customer is a nuisance; a million cheated customers are a civilization rotting from its granaries outward — and the Qur'an speaks on behalf of all of them at once, in the first word of the chapter.

**The Market of Madinah and the Moment of Revelation**

The tradition preserved the occasion. Ibn ʿAbbās — the Torah-of-the-nation, as the scholars called him — related:

> *"When the Prophet ﷺ first moved to Madinah, its inhabitants were the worst kind of defrauders, and so God revealed: Woe unto the defrauders, who, when they take measure from people, demand it in full. After the revelation of this verse, they became honest in their weighing."* (recorded by al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Wāḥidī)

The report is remarkable on two counts. First, it tells us that the verse was revealed as *social surgery*: Madinah — the city that would become the model polity of Islam — was admitted to have had the worst commercial corruption, and the Qur'an's response was not a gradual awareness campaign but a direct, thunderous imprecation. Second, the report ends with a fact as plainly stated as a market ledger: *"they became honest in their weighing."* The earliest Muslims understood revelation as something to be *complied with* before it was understood as something to be admired. No committee, no phase-in period, no grace of years is mentioned; the community heard "woe," looked at their pans and their hands, and corrected them. It is one of the clearest cases in the sīrah of revelation functioning immediately as economic law — and one of the most telling contrasts with our own age, in which the ethics of measurement is endlessly debated and endlessly deferred.

Alongside Ibn ʿAbbās's report, the tradition transmitted a vivid instance of the type. According to Abū Hurayrah, these verses referred directly to a particular kind of man:

> *"A man who had two scales: one that he used when buying, and another when selling."* (recorded by al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarṣī)

The image has never lost its usefulness. One heavy scale for receiving, one light scale for giving out — the entire pathology of the muṭaffif compressed into a picture a child can understand. Every reader will recognize its modern descendants: the customer who demands the world from a shop and the shop that shortchanges the same customer an hour later; the employer who wants loyalty beyond contract and pays below it; the reviewer who punishes one late delivery and ships defective goods without apology. Two scales, one heart — and one word of ruin over both.

**"Five for Five": What a Skimmed Measure Does to a Civilization**

The commentators also preserved from the Prophet ﷺ a teaching that lifts the verse from individual fraud to national consequences. It is transmitted that when these verses were revealed, the Prophet ﷺ recited them to the people of Madinah and said: *"Five for five."* Asked what he meant, he replied:

> *"No people break their covenant, but that God sets their enemies over them. None judge by other than what God sent down, but that poverty spreads among them. None commit fornication widely, but that death spreads among them. None defraud, but that their crops fail and they are plagued by famine. No community refuses the alms, but that God withholds rain from them."* (recorded by al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī)

The wording places defrauding — *taghnīṭ* — in a catalogue beside betrayed covenants, corrupted judgment, and abandoned charity, and assigns it a harvest of its own: failing crops and famine. Whatever the full mechanism of such traditions, their social logic is transparent to any student of history or economics. A market in which measures cannot be trusted is a market in which trade shrinks to the circle of kin and acquaintance; surplus cannot find buyers, scarcity cannot find relief, and the first famine turns a crippled economy into a dying one. Modern development economics has reached the same conclusion from the opposite direction: societies with pervasive measurement fraud and contract cheating remain poor not for lack of resources but for lack of trust, because trust is the true infrastructure of every exchange. The Qur'anic warning — fraud brings famine — is thus not a superstition appended to ethics; it is ethics revealed as economics.

**"Whoever Cheats Us Is Not One of Us": The Prophetic Border of the Marketplace**

No verse of the Qur'an on fraud stands closer to a saying of the Prophet ﷺ than this one stands to the ḥadīth of the wet grain. Abū Hurayrah narrated:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ passed by a heap of food. He put his hand into it and his fingers felt wetness. He said: 'What is this, O owner of the food?' The man said: 'Rain fell upon it, O Messenger of God.' He said: 'Would you not have placed it on top so that the people could see it? Whoever cheats us is not one of us.'"* (Muslim 102)

Three elements of this ḥadīth mirror the sūrah point for point. The crime is at the *edge* — wet grain at the bottom of the heap, invisible to the buyer, exactly where the defrauder operates. The excuse is half-true — rain really did fall; the defrauder is rarely a total liar, he is a selective one. And the sentence is social excommunication: *laysa minnā*, "he is not one of us." The Prophet ﷺ did not say the cheater is a weak Muslim or a sinful one merely; he drew a boundary of identity around the community and placed the deceiver outside its wall. Faith, in the Prophet's grammar, has a border, and the border runs through the marketplace. A community could survive without poets and without generals; it could not survive, he taught, if its members stopped being able to believe one another's measures.

The pairing of verse and ḥadīth also answers a modern misconception — that Islamic law's concern with *ḥalāl* and *ḥarām* is a matter of foods and rituals. Here is the Prophet ﷺ defining membership in his community by the humidity of grain. The implication for every Muslim professional is direct: the engineer's tolerance, the pharmacist's dilution, the journalist's quotation, the contractor's concrete, the programmer's test coverage — each is a "heap of food" into which the modern buyer cannot thrust his hand, and each will be asked about.

**The Verdict as Invitation: What the Opening Verse Offers the Honest**

Finally, it should be noticed that the verse, for all its severity, is a mercy to the honest. The overwhelming majority of buyers in any market are *not* experts in weights; they cannot verify the pan, the calibration, the tare. Left to purely human supervision, commerce would belong to the ruthless. By pronouncing *wayl* upon the defrauders at the head of a chapter about the Day of Judgment, the Qur'an tells every ordinary buyer in every age: your blindness is not your ruin; the scale you could not watch is watched. This is the function of the verse within the sūrah's architecture — the first stone in a bridge from the shop counter to the divine balance: *"Do they not think that they will be resurrected?"* (83:4). The defrauder's error, the chapter will argue, is not first a defect of honesty but a defect of vision. He cheats because he has stopped believing in the audience that sees the bottom of the heap. Restore that audience, and the two scales of Abū Hurayrah's story collapse into one — the only scale that was ever true: the one in which a man weighs for others exactly what he would take for himself.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:2

> **who, when they take measure from people, demand [it] in full,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Half of a Crooked Ledger: Taking Without a Wrinkle**

The verse begins the anatomy of the defrauder not with his cheating but with his demanding. *Alladhīna idhā ktālū ʿalā al-nāsi yastawfūn* — "who, when they take measure from people, demand it in full." The verb *istawfū* is Form X of the root **w-f-y**, the root of completion, fulfillment, and paying a debt in its entirety (*wafāʾ* is the full discharge of an obligation; *al-Wāfī* is one of God's own attributes as the fulfiller of promises). With the Form X prefix *ist-*, the verb acquires the shade of *claiming* or *demanding* that completion from another: not merely receiving full measure, but insisting upon it, auditing it, running a finger along the rim of the pan to be certain not one grain sits below the line. The defrauder, in other words, is first portrayed as a man of standards. He believes in accuracy — in *accuracy for himself*. His belief in the measure is sincere; what is missing is any intention that it apply in both directions. The Qur'an opens his portrait with his virtue, and only the next verse will reveal the fraud, because that is precisely how the fraud presents itself in life: as exactness.

The commentators observed that this order of description — demanding full, giving short — is a psychological key to the whole type. Al-Qurṭubī and the later tradition treat the two verses as one mirror with two faces: the same man appears at the counter twice, once as buyer and once as seller, and the Qur'an shows both visits. To receive, he is the strictest of accountants; to give, the loosest. It is worth noticing what the Qur'an does *not* say. It does not say the defrauder steals. It does not say he refuses to pay. It says he takes *fully* — lawfully, by the letter of the transaction — and then gives *less than fully* under circumstances engineered so the shortfall will not be detected. The whole crime lives in the asymmetry. If he took fully and gave fully, he would be a merchant. If he took little and gave little, he would be a miser. Because he takes fully and gives short, he is a *muṭaffif* — and the word of ruin is his.

**The Double Standard as the Oldest Disease of the Heart**

What the verse isolates is a moral pathology so common that most people commit it daily without a tremor of conscience: the doctrine of two measures. Every human society has a word for it — the double standard — and every human being has felt its pull, because it flatters the self at no visible cost. The manager who demands overtime beyond the contract and grudges the stipulated break; the tenant who wants the landlord's repairs yesterday and his own rent remitted quietly; the motorist who wants mercy from every other driver and obedience from every light he approaches; the student who wants the grader's generosity and the grader's infallibility from his own markers. None of these people believes he is wicked; each simply operates, as Abū Hurayrah's report put it, with two scales — one heavy for receiving, one light for giving.

Modern psychology has mapped this terrain carefully under names like *self-serving bias* and the *actor–observer asymmetry*: human beings judge their own conduct by their intentions and pressures, and others' conduct by its visible outcomes. The Qur'an located the same truth fourteen centuries earlier in a single grammatical contrast — *yastawfūn* for taking, *yukhṣirūn* (next verse) for giving — and it located the cure not in sentiment but in a rule: **one measure for all parties, including oneself.** The revelation's insistence on this point is one of its most practical gifts. A man can be trained to honesty in the abstract and remain a muṭaffif in the concrete, because the concrete is where the self sits on the scale. The Qur'an therefore does not exhort fairness in general; it legislates the *symmetry* — full in both directions, or short in neither.

**"Love for Your Brother": The Prophetic Rule That Ends the Two Scales**

The moral engine that makes symmetry possible was stated by the Prophet ﷺ in one of the most cited definitions of faith in the entire tradition:

> *"None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself."* (al-Bukhārī 13; Muslim 45)

The connection to the defrauder's psychology is exact. The muṭaffif's defect is not ignorance of the measure — he *demands* full measure, so he knows precisely what full measure is. His defect is that he has reserved the knowledge for one side of the counter. The Prophet's rule removes the reservation: whatever you would demand as a buyer, you must supply as a seller; whatever tolerance you ask for your weakness, you extend to your brother's. Islam thus grounds commercial integrity not in an external code enforcement but in an internal transfer of self-love — the only motive strong enough to operate when no inspector is present. It is, if one may say so, the ancient cure for the modern rule that "everyone does it": everyone does it because each person exempts himself; the believer is forbidden the exemption.

The social consequence is worth pausing over, because it is the difference between an economy and a den of traps. In a marketplace where every party tries to out-skim the other, transaction costs devour the trade itself: buyers must weigh everything twice, hire testers, distrust labels, and pay a fear-tax on every purchase. In a marketplace governed by the single measure, that entire tax vanishes, and the saving is distributed as prosperity. The two scales, in other words, are not merely a private vice; they are a public budget of friction. Nations that discover this late pay for the discovery in famines, as the Prophet's "five for five" teaching warned and as the history of collapsed trade cities confirms.

**Even Worship Is Measured: Ibn Masʿūd's Extension of the Verse**

The first generation did not confine this verse to the grain market, and the most beautiful evidence is the application made by ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd — a Companion who carried the Qur'an from the Prophet's own mouth and became one of the earliest commentators. He said:

> *"Prayer is a measure. Whosoever completes its measure, God will complete his reward for him. And whosoever lightens (ṭaffafa) its measure — you have heard what God has said regarding al-muṭaffifīn."* (recorded by al-Ṭabarṣī)

Notice what Ibn Masʿūd did with the grammar of his own soul: he imported the very root of the sūrah — *ṭaffafa*, to skim, to lighten — into the ritual life. Prayer, he taught, has a rim and an edge like any measure: the stillness in the bowing, the tranquility in the prostration, the presence of the heart, the recitation not hurried past understanding. A man may "take" from God's promise — salvation, provision, forgiveness — in full, while "giving" God the skimmed remainder of his attention: the prayer caught at the last minute before sunset, the fast emptied of restraint by daylight slander, the zakāh computed to the last permissible deduction. Ibn Masʿūd's warning quietly extends the chapter's first verse across the whole of religion: *you have heard what God said about those who lighten.* The believer who would never shortchange a customer is hereby invited to check the other counter — the one at which he stands five times a day — and ask whether his own payment there is up to the rim.

This extension also explains why the sūrah's opening indictment can address believers. A man may be scrupulous in the mosque and a muṭaffif in the market, or scrupulous in the market and a muṭaffif in his prayers; the category tracks the *skimming*, not the uniforms. The Qur'an's purpose is not to sort people into villains and saints but to hold the mirror at every counter where the reader actually stands.

**Fullness as Worship: Why Islam Glorifies Completing the Measure**

It is significant that the Qur'an's first description of the defrauder is a description of *demanding fullness* — because fullness itself is not the vice. On the contrary, the Qur'an repeatedly commands fullness as an act of religion:

> *"Give full measure when you measure, and weigh with the straight balance. That is better and fairer in the end."* (17:35)

> *"And fulfill the measure and the balance, and defraud not people of their things."* (7:85)

> *"And the heaven He raised up, and He set the balance, that you may not transgress in the balance."* (55:7–8)

The cosmos itself, in that last passage, is hung from a balance. The believer's full pan is thus not conformity to a market norm but participation in a cosmic order: God has filled every measure He has poured — the rain, the days, the lungs, the mercies — *"and if you were to count God's bounty, you could not reckon it"* (16:18). The servanthood of the believer answers that generosity with a corresponding fullness in whatever he pours for others. This is the deep logic of why the defrauder's sin, however small in grams, is so offensive in kind: he receives from a God who gives "without measure" (from the same root, *bighayri ḥisāb*) and hands out by the skimmed edge. The two verses of this sūrah set the two quantities side by side — full taken, short given — and the disproportion itself is the indictment.

For the modern reader, the verse lands with particular force in an economy of asymmetric information. The buyer almost never sees the factory, the ledger, the algorithm; he takes the label's word. Every domain in which the *provider* knows vastly more than the *receiver* — medicine, finance, insurance, food, software, education — is a domain of hidden measures, and therefore a domain where this verse speaks. The demand it makes is stunningly simple to state and demanding to live: *write the same number on both sides of your ledger.* Give to the invisible party the exact weight you would claim if you were the one holding out your hand.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:3

> **and when they measure for them or weigh for them, they stint.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of the Transaction: Giving Out by the Skimmed Edge**

The first verse caught the defrauder receiving; this verse catches him giving. *Wa-idhā kālūhum aw wazanūhum yukhṣirūn* — "and when they measure for them or weigh for them, they stint." The verbs now run in the opposite direction: *kālūhum*, "they measure *for them*," *wazanūhum*, "they weigh *for them*" — the customer stands on the far side of the counter with an open bag, and the defrauder fills it. The verb of the crime is *yukhṣirūn*. Al-Ṭabarī, the earliest of the great commentators, records both derivations the Arabic language offers. The first takes the word from the root **kh-s-r**, the root of *khasārah*, "loss and diminution": they cause the given measure to *fall short*, dealing out loss in grams. The second takes it from *khaṣl*, "a small tuft, a little clump" — as one says *khaṣl min al-ṣūf*, "a small lock of wool" — so that *ikhtiṣāl* pictures the merchant withholding from the measure a mere pinch: too little for the buyer to litigate, more than enough for the seller to profit, multiplied over a thousand transactions a year. Either derivation lands on the same portrait: loss administered at doses below the threshold of protest.

It is worth noticing the two instruments the verse names — *measuring* (volume, for grain and liquids) and *weighing* (weight, for precious goods) — because together they cover the entire commercial technology of the pre-modern world. The Qur'an does not say "when they cheat," which would allow the honest man to imagine himself excluded; it names the two everyday acts by which every seller hands over goods, and pronounces the stinting upon them. By naming the instruments rather than the abstraction, the verse effectively legislates at the point of the hand: the millimeter where the measure meets the rim is brought directly under the law of God. The modern reader holds far more instruments than a balance and a bushel — the receipt, the label, the invoice, the timestamp, the certificate, the code — and the verse's logic extends with them: whatever the instrument by which value passes from your hand to another's, the passing is supervised from above.

**The Variant Reading: "When They Measure, or They Weigh, They Stint"**

The science of Quranic recitation preserves for this verse a subtle variant that sharpens the indictment. In the standard reading, the pronoun *hum* ("them") in *kālūhum* and *wazanūhum* is taken as the direct object — "when they measure *them* (the people), or weigh *them*." But the pronoun can also be read as an affirmation of the subject, yielding: "when they measure — or they weigh — they stint." On this reading the pronouns do not point at the customers at all; they simply affirm the defrauder as the agent of every measuring and weighing, and the crime becomes unconditional. Not "when he deals with strangers he skims and with friends he is honest," but *whenever he measures, whenever he weighs — stinting.* The variant thereby removes the last refuge of the occasional cheat: the man who is honest on audit day. The standard reading shows the two-sided fraud (full in taking, short in giving); the variant shows the one-sided heart (short in all giving). Both are preserved in the tradition, and the believer may take from both the same lesson: the measure reveals the man, and the man is measured by his measures.

**Shuʿayb's Sermon: The Oldest Prophetic Case File on Short Weights**

Long before Madinah's markets were reformed by this sūrah, the Qur'an had already devoted an entire prophetic biography to the crime of scales. Shuʿayb, sent to the people of Midian, preached with an insistence that borders on repetition:

> *"And to Midian their brother Shuʿayb. He said: O my people, worship God; you have no god other than He. There has come to you a clear proof from your Lord. So give full measure and full weight, and defraud not people of their goods, and do not work corruption in the land after it has been set in order. That is better for you, if you are believers."* (7:85)

> *"And O my people, give full measure and weight with justice, and defraud not people of their goods, and do not go about the land working corruption."* (11:85)

> *"And give full measure and weight with justice, and stint not men of their goods, and work not corruption in the land after it has been set in good order."* (26:183)

Notice how in all three passages the command to honest measures is fused with the command to worship God alone — as though monotheism and the straight balance were one subject taught in one breath. Shuʿayb's people did not deny God's existence; they denied that God's existence had any jurisdiction over their pans. Their theology was intact and their commerce was criminal, and the Qur'an treats the second as proof of the first. That is precisely the logic of the present sūrah: the defrauders' real creed is exposed not by what they say in the mosque but by what they do at the rim of the measure. The fate of Midian — "the punishment of the day of gloom" (11:67) — stands in the Qur'an as the first historical certificate of what this chapter proclaims eschatologically: skimmed measures do not merely rot economies; they summon heaven.

There is also a quiet historical resonance in Shuʿayb's very name and setting. Midian lay on the caravan routes of the northern Arabian trade; its wealth was measured in weights at every way-station. The Qur'an's choice of that nation as the standing parable of false measures is thus documentary, not decorative: wherever value travels by scale, the temptation of *ikhtiṣāl* travels with it.

**"Their Transaction Will Be Blessed": The Economics of Honest Hands**

Against the defrauder's arithmetic — that the skimmed pinch is free money — the Prophet ﷺ set a counter-economics of *barakah*, blessing, which the defrauder forfeits without ever seeing it leave. Ḥakīm ibn Ḥazm narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The seller and the buyer have the option (to revoke) so long as they have not separated. If they speak the truth and make things clear, their transaction will be blessed; and if they conceal and lie, the blessing of their transaction will be wiped out."* (al-Bukhārī 2079; Muslim 1532)

The wording is precise: concealment and lying do not merely *diminish* the transaction's blessing; they *annihilate* it — *muḥaqat*, wiped out entirely. The buyer may walk away with the goods and the seller with the coin, but the barakah — the hidden multiplier by which wealth nourishes rather than corrodes — has left the deal like heat from an open door. And the Prophet ﷺ defined the summit of the honest trade in the famous report:

> *"The truthful, trustworthy merchant will be with the prophets, the truthful, and the martyrs."* (al-Tirmidhī 1209)

The company promised — prophets, veracious ones, martyrs — is the company of those who lived for others at cost to themselves. The honest merchant is ranked among them because he does daily, in small negotiable quantities, what the martyr does once: he prefers another's right to his own gain. This is the positive vision beneath the sūrah's thunder. The Qur'an does not merely criminalize the short pan; it canonizes the full one.

The warning side is equally documented. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Beware of the supplication of the oppressed, for there is no barrier between it and God."* (al-Bukhārī 2448; Muslim 19)

The defrauded buyer is the classic *maẓlūm* — the wronged one whose loss is too small for any court. Each victim feels the pinch, mutters, and moves on; the aggregate of their mutterings, the Prophet taught, travels without obstruction to the Throne. The short measure is thus not a private arrangement between a sharp seller and a soft buyer; it is a queue of unanswered lawsuits whose judge has never once been absent.

**Why the Skimmed Measure Corrodes the Man Who Skims It**

Beyond its social chemistry, the verse diagnoses a psychological wound that the defrauder inflicts upon himself, and it is worth spelling out because the defrauder never sees it on any balance. The man who maintains two measures must maintain two selves: the self presented to buyers and the self that knows the truth; the public ledger and the private one. Psychologists who study habitual deception describe its cumulative cost — the working memory consumed by tracking lies, the flattening of conscience through repetition, the quiet relocation of other people from persons to be honored into marks to be taken. Each individual skim is forgettable by design; the *practice* of skimming is unforgettable in its effects. The Qur'anic portrait anticipates this exactly, for the muṭaffif of this sūrah will be shown, twelve verses later, with rust covering his heart (83:14) — the sinner's conscience, plated over layer by layer like metal in damp air. The short measure is, so to speak, the first coat of that rust: applied invisibly, drying fast, easy to repaint.

The honest seller, by contrast, enjoys a wholeness that no margin can purchase. He stands behind one ledger. His word is his bond in every direction; he need not remember what he told whom; he sleeps, as the maxim has it, with a single account. The Qur'an's phrase for the opposite condition — *"and woe to those whose hearts are hardened against the remembrance of God; those are in manifest error"* (39:22) — describes a man divided at the root. The straight balance, it turns out, is a treatment for the heart before it is a service to the customer.

**The Measure in Modern Clothing**

To bring the verse fully into the present is to realize how many of daily life's transactions are now "measures and weighings" conducted without any physical pan at all. The manufacturer who quietly reduces the contents while keeping the package the same size is performing *ikhtiṣāl* to the milliliter. The service that advertises one price and bills another through buried fees is the wet grain at the bottom of the heap. The contractor who waters the concrete, the dealer who turns back the odometer, the fund that reports returns net of nothing, the restaurant that serves the photographed portion to one table and the shrunken portion to another, the platform that "measures" a creator's watch-time with a hidden thumb on the counter — each is the defrauder of this verse wearing contemporary dress, and each stands precisely where the Qur'an's *wayl* was pronounced: at the edge, where the buyer cannot see and the seller imagines no one does.

The verse's final gift to the modern reader is its realism about the limits of regulation. Every civilization has tried to police weights, and the police are always, by construction, somewhere else. Inspectors visit the market by day; the thumb goes on the pan at dawn. What the Qur'an adds to the inspectorate is an inspector who does not sleep — the standing before *"the Lord of the worlds"* that the chapter is even now preparing to describe (83:5–6). The skimmed pinch is invisible to the muḥtasib and indelible to God. That asymmetry, the sūrah will argue, is the only reliable foundation ever discovered for the straight measure — and the defrauder's tragedy, announced in the very next verse, is that he has talked himself out of believing in it.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:4

> **Do they not think that they will be resurrected**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Explains the Crime**

After two verses of observed behavior, the Qur'an performs an act of diagnosis. It does not ask, "Do they not know cheating is wrong?" — the defrauders know that perfectly well; it is the first rule of every market ever held. It does not ask, "Do they not fear detection?" — they have engineered their crime precisely to escape detection. Instead it asks a question no magistrate would ever think to ask: *alā yaẓunnu ulāʾika annahum mabʿūthūn* — "Do they not think that they will be resurrected?" With that question the sūrah locates the true organ of the disease. The short measure, it teaches, is not at bottom a failure of honesty; it is a failure of belief — specifically, the quiet, unexamined conviction that this life is the whole ledger and that nothing follows. A man who truly expects to stand before his Lord does not skim the pan when he is alone with the grain. He may still struggle against temptation, but the *system* of his conduct changes, because his model of the future changes. Fraud is bad bookkeeping done by bad eschatology.

The particle *alā* ("do they not...!") is an Arabic particle of alert and wonder — it behaves like a hand laid on the shoulder of a distracted man. The verb *yaẓunnu* is from **ẓ-n-n**, the root of *ẓann*, "to think, to suppose, to hold as opinion" — a degree below *certainty*. The Qur'an thus describes the resurrection-deniers as people who have not even reached the level of firmly *thinking* it false; they have simply declined to think about it at all. Their unbelief in the Last Day is not a reasoned position but an incurious habit, the moral equivalent of a shopkeeper who never checks his own scale because checking might cost him money. The question of the verse is therefore double-edged: it exposes what they have *failed to conclude* (the resurrection) and *how* they failed to conclude it (by never letting the thought finish).

**A Word for Thinking: Why the Qur'an Attacks the Defrauder's Mind, Not Only His Hands**

The choice of *ẓann* rather than a verb of pure knowledge or pure belief is deliberate, and the commentators noticed its breadth. In the Qur'an, *ẓann* can be used for faulty estimation (*"they think every crier is against them"* 63:4), for groundless conjecture (*"they follow nothing but conjecture"* 6:116), and — as here — for the assumptions by which men actually live. The verse says: they do not *think* they will be raised; somewhere inside, the whole subject has been closed without a hearing. Al-Rāzī points out that this is the precise psychological mechanism by which great sins become easy: not by argument but by *aversion of attention*. The defrauder does not sit down and construct a proof that there is no Day of Judgment; he simply declines to finish the thought every time it begins, because a finished thought would collapse his profit margin. Sin in the modern idiom would call this motivated reasoning; the Qur'an calls it the heart's rust (83:14) applied in advance to the intellect.

The Book returns to this same psychological key wherever resurrection is denied:

> *"Does man think that We shall not gather his bones? Yes indeed! We are able to restore his very fingers."* (75:3–4)

> *"Those who disbelieve claim (yaẓunnu) that they will never be raised. Say: Yea, by my Lord, you shall surely be raised."* (64:7)

> *"And they say: There is nothing but our life of this world; we die and we live, and nothing destroys us but time."* (45:24)

In every passage the pattern is identical: the denial of the resurrection is shown to be not an intellectual discovery but an act of *preferred thinking* — thought bent by desire. And the Qur'an's reply is never merely philosophical; it is forensic. Against the man who thinks the bones cannot be gathered, God points at his own fingertips: the being who assembled you the first time, from a drop, is questioned about repeating what He has already done (*"And he strikes out a parable for Us, forgetting his own creation"* 36:78). The argument from creation to resurrection runs through the whole Book like a spine: the first origin is the harder miracle; the second is a repetition.

**Why Belief Weighs More Than Law: The Logic of the Unwatched Moment**

There is a deep principle in this verse that deserves the fullest statement, because it is one of the Qur'an's most original contributions to social thought. Human law can regulate only what it can see. It can post inspectors at the market gate, certify scales, publish standards; it cannot stand behind every counter at every hour — and the entire profit of fraud lies in the hours when no one stands there. Al-Muṭaffifīn's insight is that the *only* surveillance that reaches the unwatched moment is belief in an Unseen Witness. The verse therefore performs an act of moral engineering: it fastens the lowest behaviors of the market to the highest object of theology. The man who skims the measure and the man who denies the resurrection are revealed to be the same man, at two levels of the same act.

This is why, in the Qur'an's social teaching, faith functions as the load-bearing wall of civilization rather than its decoration. Where belief in accountability thrives, cheap honesty becomes possible — transactions completed on trust, contracts honored in the dark, quality maintained where the buyer will never look. Where that belief dies, every transaction must be armored: tested, certified, insured, litigated — and even then, the armor only covers the visible surface. The Prophet ﷺ stated the divine version of this supervision in words every Muslim carries:

> *"He is with you wherever you are."* (Qur'an 57:4)

and again, in the tradition:

> *"Fear God wherever you are."* (al-Tirmidhī 1987)

The defrauder's error, in the light of this verse, is an error of *population*. He counted his audience: a buyer, maybe a neighbor, no inspectors — and concluded no one relevant was watching. The Qur'an corrects the census: the audience includes the Lord of the worlds, the recording angels (*"two receivers, seated on the right and the left"* 50:17), and the assembled humanity of the Last Day. Fraud is, at bottom, a mistake in demography.

**The Prophetic Nearness of the Hour**

The Prophet ﷺ joined this verse's subject — the resurrection — to the lived urgency of his community by holding up two fingers. Anas ibn Mālik narrated:

> *"The Hour and I have been sent like these two,"* and he joined his index finger and his middle finger. (al-Bukhārī 650; Muslim 2951)

The image teaches proportion: between the beginning of the final revelation and the end of all things there is, in the scale of history, the width between two adjacent fingers. The community of the Qur'an therefore lives in the terminal hour of the human story, whatever its remaining length — and the resurrection is not a remote abstraction but the next great event on the calendar. Abū Dharr transmitted the Prophet's ﷺ own emotional commentary on this nearness:

> *"If you knew what I know, you would laugh little and weep much, and you would go out to the hilltops supplicating God."* (Muslim 2353)

The verse's question — *do they not think?* — thus gains its full urgency. The defrauder prices his crime against a horizon of a few comfortable decades; the Qur'an re-prices it against an event closer than his own heartbeat. No ethical system in history has offered a shorter distance between the marketplace and the final audit.

**From the Shop to the Skeleton: How the Question Works Upon the Mind**

It is worth asking, with the commentators, why the Qur'an so often argues for the resurrection through *images of the body* — bones gathered, fingers restored, the earth confessing. The reason is psychological. Men can hold abstract denials comfortably; the body will not let them. Every shopkeeper who shortchanges his neighbor carries with him, at every moment, the evidence the Qur'an cites: a body that was assembled from nothing and disassembles daily, cells replaced, hair grayed, strength borrowed and returned. The resurrection-denier must avert his eyes from his own hands. The Qur'an's method is to turn his attention back upon the one exhibit he cannot dismiss — himself. *"And man says: What! When I am dead shall I be raised alive? Does not man remember that We created him before, when he was nothing?"* (19:66–67).

For the modern reader, the same method answers a modern version of the denial: the assumption, rarely argued and constantly practiced, that death is simply the end of the record. The verse's question cuts across all its versions. *Do you not think it through?* The defrauder's comfort rests on never finishing the thought; the Qur'an's mercy is to insist that he finish it — and having finished it, to discover that the honest measure is not a tax on his life but the only way of living that survives its own audit.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:5

> **unto a tremendous day—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tremendous: The Grammar of Awe**

The question of the previous verse — *do they not think they will be resurrected?* — now receives its object, and the object is weighted like nothing else in the chapter: *li-yawmin ʿaẓīm* — "unto a tremendous day." The adjective *ʿaẓīm* comes from the root **ʿ-ẓ-m**, which carries the sense of *greatness, magnitude, and might* — the same root that gives *al-ʿAẓīm* among the names of God's magnificence and *taʿẓīm*, the reverent awe a servant owes his Lord. The Arabic lexicon defines the *ʿaẓīm* as that whose greatness is such as to restrain and overawe the beholder. The day, in other words, is not merely long or eventful; it is a day of a different *order of being* from all other days — a day whose size presses upon the soul.

It is worth noticing how sparingly the Qur'an spends this particular adjective on things of time. It calls this day *yawm ʿaẓīm* here and in Sūrah al-Qamar ("turn away on the day the caller calls to a **tremendous** thing," 54:6); it calls the event of revelation itself an affair *ʿaẓīm* (38:67? — *"and it is a tremendous Qur'an"* at 15:87? — the *qurʾān ʿaẓīm* of 15:87); and it names the greatest of terrors *al-fazʿ al-akbar* (21:103), "the Great Terror," of the same day. The Qur'an's rhetoric elsewhere piles superlatives on the worldly — markets, palaces, harvests — but for this day it reserves a vocabulary of measured enormity: *ʿaẓīm*, tremendous; *faẓʿ akbar*, the great terror; *al-wāqiʿah*, the Event; *al-ḥāqqah*, the Reality that strikes. The measured tone is itself a device: the Book treats this day the way one treats an object too heavy to lift casually — with bent knees and both hands.

**Why the Day Is Called Great: Not Its Length but Its Lord**

In what does the day's tremendousness consist? The Qur'an answers in scene after scene. It is tremendous in its *chemistry* — the dissolution of the created order that men mistook for permanent:

> *"The Day the earth is changed into other than the earth, and the heavens; and they shall come forth before God, the One, the Vanquisher."* (14:48)

> *"When the sun is folded up, and when the stars are scattered, and when the mountains are set moving..."* (81:1–3)

> *"When the earth is crushed, crushed, crushed, and thy Lord comes, and the angels rank upon rank."* (89:21–22)

It is tremendous in its *duration* — the Qur'an itself supplies the scale in Sūrah al-Maʿārij: *"The angels and the Spirit ascend unto Him in a day the measure of which is fifty thousand years"* (70:4). It is tremendous above all in its *presiding Person*: the day is tremendous because of Who holds it. The classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī — agree that the adjective serves a rhetorical purpose within the sūrah's logic: the defrauders are being shown the *size* of what they have declined to think about. A man may shrug at a court date; no one shrugs at an appointment with the dissolution of the universe. The verse deliberately supplies the one fact that makes previous avoidance impossible: this is not a day among days. It is *the* day — tremendous beyond every category by which men have ever measured an appointment.

**The Sun Drawn Near: A Narration of the Day's Stages**

The tradition preserved the Prophet's ﷺ own sketches of that day's staggering conditions. Al-Miqdād ibn al-Aswad narrated that the Messenger of God ﷺ said:

> *"The sun will be drawn near the creatures on the Day of Resurrection until it is a mile from them — and the mile is a great distance. The people will then be in their perspiration according to their deeds: among them one whose perspiration reaches his ankles, one whose reaches his knees, and one whose reaches his waist..."* (Muslim 2930)

In another narration of the same event, the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"People will stand in their perspiration up to the middle of their ears."* (recorded by al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī)

and in a further version preserved by the commentators, he said that among the people will be one with *"a bridle of perspiration"* — *lijām al-ʿaraq* — and the Prophet ﷺ pointed to his own mouth as he said it (recorded by Muslim 2864 and the commentators). The details repay attention. Perspiration is the body's confession: under the sun drawn near, each human frame will visibly *display* the weight of what its owner did. The tradition does not present these degrees as arbitrary torments but as *proportional readouts* — sweat to the ankles for the light of deed, to the ears for the heavy. The Day's tremendousness, in this narration, is personalized: every creature will carry, on his own skin, the meteorology of his own account. The defrauder who imagined his skimmed pinch would never be seen will stand in a court where his deeds are not merely known but *worn*.

**Tremendous for Whom: The Two Readings of the Same Morning**

The Qur'an is careful, throughout its descriptions of that day, to keep two registers sounding at once — terror for the unjust and relief for the just — and the adjective *ʿaẓīm* belongs to both. For the wrongdoers it is the day of the Great Terror: *"The Great Terror shall not grieve them"* is said of the God-fearing (21:103) precisely because to others it *will*. The Prophet ﷺ, describing the same morning to his Companions, painted the believer's side with a different brush. Abū Hurayrah transmitted:

> *"There are seven whom God will shade in His shade on the day when there is no shade but His shade..."* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031)

— and the first of the seven, significantly for this sūrah, is *"a just ruler,"* with the honest giver of charity and the one whose heart is attached to the mosques among the rest. The same morning that drowns the defrauders in a bridle of their own sweat provides shade for the just — because the Day's tremendousness is not a fixed quantity of horror but a function of the ledger it opens. One day; two weathers. This is the pattern the whole chapter will elaborate: Sijjīn and ʿIlliyyūn share a single grammar and opposite temperatures.

**Pricing Today by That Day: The Tremendous Day as a Rule of Decision**

Beyond its imagery, the verse performs a quiet act of moral accounting that the believer carries into every decision. To call the day *tremendous* is to fix its exchange rate: whatever is weighed against it becomes light. The defrauder's arithmetic — the extra percentage point of profit — is denominated in a currency that this verse declares about to be recalled. The Prophet ﷺ repeatedly taught his community to conduct life's calculations with the Last Day in the ledger; his supplications returned to it morning and evening, and he instructed that a man remember it abundantly, *"for it is the meeting place of all outcomes."* The modern mind, trained to discount distant events toward zero, finds here a deliberate reversal: the Qur'an discounts *everything else* toward zero and inflates the final day beyond every horizon. That reversal is the practical meaning of *li-yawmin ʿaẓīm* — and it is the exact inversion of the defrauder's mind, who had inflated the present pinch of profit and discounted the tremendous day to nothing. The verse restores the true prices. A measure is honest not because inspectors enforce honesty but because the day it will be audited on is *tremendous* — and between that day and every skew of every scale, the believer now knows the relative weight.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:6

> **a day when mankind shall stand before the Lord of the worlds?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Standing, Not Sitting: The Posture of the Judgment**

The sentence that began as a question about resurrection and swelled through "a tremendous day" now completes itself with its most concrete image: *yawma yaqūmu al-nāsu li-rabbi al-ʿālamīn* — "a day when mankind shall stand before the Lord of the worlds." The single word *yaqūmu*, "shall stand," carries the whole scene. Standing is the posture of judgment in every human court — when the judge enters, the room rises — because standing is what a creature does before authority: uncovered, still, without any device of comfort or retreat. There will be no chairs on that plain, and the Qur'an's deliberately plain verb refuses to ornament the fact. Every human being who ever lived — the merchant and his customer, the caliph and the slave, the prophet and his accusers — will be upright on two feet, in one field, before one Lord.

The tradition filled in the physical details of that standing with matter-of-fact precision. ʿĀʾishah, hearing the descriptions of the gathering, asked how men and women could stand looking at one another in such a state, and the Prophet ﷺ answered:

> *"The matter will be too grave for them to look at one another."* — and he described mankind as gathered *"barefoot, naked, uncircumcised."* (al-Bukhārī 6527; Muslim 2860)

The bareness is the point: every worldly instrument of status — clothing, title, retinue, currency — is the wardrobe of *this* world; the day of standing admits none of it. The richest defrauder of the market and the poorest buyer he cheated will stand in the same undress, and their accounts will be the only garments the court recognizes. That leveling is itself a quiet reply to the sūrah's opening: the man who gained his advantages by the skimmed measure will discover that measures of cloth and coin do not survive the changing room of the Resurrection.

**Before the Lord of the Worlds: An Audience, Not a Mob Scene**

The preposition is *li-* — "before," "for," "unto" — *li-rabbi al-ʿālamīn*: mankind will stand *before the Lord of the worlds*, not merely "on" a day or "in" a place. The phrasing makes the standing an *audience*: an appearance in the presence-chamber of the King. And the tradition describes that chamber's opening with awesome concision. Abū Hurayrah transmitted that the Messenger of God ﷺ said:

> *God will grasp the earth on the Day of Resurrection and fold the heaven in His right hand, then say: "I am the King. Where are the lords of the earth?"* (al-Bukhārī 4812; Muslim 2787)

Every empire that ever certified a false measure — every inspector bribed, every law bent, every market rigged — is in that handful of folded heaven and earth. The title the verse chooses, *Lord of the worlds* (*rabb al-ʿālamīn*), is the same title that opens the Fātiḥah and closes the Qur'an's vocabulary of sovereignty; its presence here is pointed. The defrauder skimmed the goods of *the worlds* — the grain of the fields, the wool of the flocks, the labor of the creatures who populate those worlds — and he will render his account not to any magistrate of any single market but to the Lord of *all* the worlds whose goods he touched. The verse thus joins the smallest economic act to the largest possible jurisdiction. There is no small-claims court in this system; there is one bench, and it is cosmic.

**How Long the Standing: The Narrations of a Day Without Clocks**

The scholars also transmitted discussions of the standing's sheer duration, and the source commentary preserves the range. Some held with the verse of Sūrah al-Maʿārij that the day spans what men would count as *"fifty thousand years"* (70:4); others computed a thousand years — for God says *"a day with thy Lord is as a thousand years of what you count"* (22:47); others read still other figures — three hundred, a hundred, forty — reconciling the numbers as measuring different segments of that day's long liturgy of judgment (al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī record the debate). What no school denied was the experience the narrations describe: a waiting without shade, described by the Prophet ﷺ in the ḥadīth of the perspiration that rises *"according to their deeds"* (Muslim 2864; 2930), and a sun drawn near until, in his words, *"the distance between it and them is a mile."* A standing of that length, in that heat, under that sun, is itself a first installment of judgment — the sentence beginning before the sentence is spoken. Al-Qurṭubī notes the pedagogical point: the Qur'an teaches the torment of that waiting to a generation that could grow careless, so that no believer should imagine the Day as a formality briefly dispensed. It is an age of standing, and the body will feel every hour of it.

**Shade on a Day Without Shade: The Other Side of the Plain**

Yet the same tradition that describes the sun-drawn-plain describes, with equal concreteness, its exceptions. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"There are seven whom God will shade in His shade on the day when there is no shade but His shade: a just ruler; a youth who grew up in the worship of God; a man whose heart is attached to the mosques; two men who love one another for God, meeting for His sake and parting for His sake; a man who is invited by a woman of rank and beauty and says: I fear God; a man who gives charity and conceals it so that his left hand does not know what his right hand spends; and a man who remembers God in private and his eyes overflow."* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031)

Set beside the defrauders' bridle of perspiration, this ḥadīth is the sūrah's other ledger in miniature. The shading deed that leads the list is *justice* — the quality whose market-form is the straight measure. The tradition thereby answers, in advance, the sūrah's opening lament: if the world is full of short weights, the cure is not merely inspectors but a population trained in the seven shaded habits, at whose head stands the just man. The believer reads verse 6 and the ḥadīth together and understands the Day's deepest secret: its tremendousness is not a wall but a doorway. It is tremendous *in every direction* — tremendous terror for the accounts that fail, and tremendous honor for the accounts that pass, up to the promise the Prophet ﷺ transmitted from his Lord that the righteous will be given security and nearness while the verdicts are still being read. The same sun; two destinies of light.

**Why "the Lord of the Worlds" and Not Simply "God": The Verse's Closing Logic**

One last observation belongs to this verse, and it gathers the chapter's opening movement to a point. The Qur'an had a shorter way to say this: "a day when mankind shall stand before God." Instead it says *before the Lord of the worlds* — the title of providence and nurture, the Rabb who feeds every creature in every world. The defrauder is thus reminded *whose* supply he tampered with. The grain he skimmed was grown by the Lord of the worlds; the buyer he cheated was fed by the Lord of the worlds; the scales themselves hang, as Sūrah al-Raḥmān taught, beneath the heaven *He* raised (55:7). To defraud the creatures is to mishandle the property of the Lord of the worlds — and that Lord has now set a standing-room in which every creature He nurtured will be present to watch the accounting. The chapter's first movement ends where all its argument will continue: with a God who sees the rim of the measure because He raised the sky it is weighed under, and with a standing so long, so bare, and so public that no thumb on any pan will ever again go unnoticed.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7

> **Nay! Truly the book of the profligate is in Sijjīn.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Nay!": The Hinge-Word of the Whole Chapter**

The verse opens with a particle barely a syllable long that does more structural work than any other word in the sūrah: *kallā* — "Nay! No indeed!" In Arabic it is a particle of forceful repudiation, the verbal equivalent of striking a table. Here it rejects, once and for all, the comfortable assumption embedded in the defrauders' behavior — the assumption that nothing is being written down. And it pivots the chapter from the marketplace of Madinah to the archives of eternity: whatever the defrauders *think* about their unrecorded pinch of grain, the truth is the opposite. There is a book, a place, a registration, and a custody. *Kallā* will return twice more in this chapter — at v.14 ("Nay! But that which they used to earn has covered their hearts with rust") and at v.18 ("Nay, truly the book of the pious is in ʿIlliyyūn") — so that the particle becomes the sūrah's hinge-system: each *kallā* slams shut one human illusion and opens one divine reality. Here, the first of the three shatters the illusion of the unrecorded deed.

The verse also widens the chapter's cast. The opening indictment named a profession — the defrauders of the grain market. This verse names a *class*: *al-fujjār*, "the profligate," all of them, in the intensive broken plural that Arabic uses for a type embodied by multitudes. The linkage is deliberate. The defrauder is not a separate species from the great sinners; he is their market representative. His skimmed measure is his entry in the same ledger that holds the tyrant's massacres and the liar's fabrications. Al-Qurṭubī and others note the warning contained in the widening: a man may think his sins too small to be filed with the great criminals of history; the Qur'an files them together, because the filing is not by size but by direction — and the direction of a short measure is the direction of the Fire.

**The Profligate: A Word That Bursts Its Banks**

The word *al-fujjār* is itself a piece of moral cartography. Its root, **f-j-r**, is the verb for what water does when it breaks through the ground: *fajjara* — to gush, to burst forth, to split a way where there was none; the *fajj* of a mountain is its split. From this root the Arabs named immorality, because sin, in the Qur'anic picture of the soul, is precisely the bursting of banks: desire that pours through the dam of conscience and floods where it was never authorized to flow. The *fājir* is the man whose inner geography has lost its channels — not the man who stumbles once, but the one whose restraint has been structurally breached. And *al-fujjār* is that breach embodied in a crowd.

The contrast with the sūrah's other company is audible in the roots themselves. The pious are called *al-abrār*, from **b-r-r**, the root of *birr* — goodness, reverence, dutifulness — a root whose concrete image is that of a horse that is *barara*, broken and gentle, wide of chest and easy of rein: power that has accepted governance. The two words thus give the chapter its two hydrologies: the fujjār, waters that have burst their banks and cut their own destructive channels; the abrār, a strength brought under the rein and therefore free to run where it gives life. The Qur'an had already set the pair side by side in the previous sūrah — *"Truly the pious shall be in bliss, and truly the profligate shall be in the Blaze"* (82:13–14) — and here it gives each company its address.

**Sijjīn: Reading the Name of the Place**

Then comes the name: *Sijjīn*. The commentators offered its anatomy with care, and the source preserves their findings. The majority derive it from **sijn**, the ordinary Arabic word for *prison* (al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī record this derivation): Sijjīn is the intensive form, the *prison of prisons*, the incarceration beneath all incarcerations. A minority derive it from **sajā**, "to be still, motionless" — suggesting a place of absolute stillness and confinement, where nothing moves except in torment. Both derivations converge on the same terror: a place defined by the removal of liberty and movement — the exact opposite of the bustling, hand-over-hand commerce with which the chapter opened. The defrauder moved freely among his scales; Sijjīn is where movement ends.

Where is it? The tradition answers with a hadīth the commentators uniformly relate:

> *"Sijjīn is the lowest of the seven earths."* (recorded by Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī)

The Qur'an elsewhere speaks of seven earths (*"It is He who created for you all that is in the earth"* — and in 65:12, *"It is God who created seven heavens and of the earth the like thereof"*), and the hadīth places Sijjīn at the bottom of that descending stack — the lowest ground below all ground, beneath the habitable world and its six unseen siblings, the deepest point in the direction *away* from God. Ibn Kathīr and others connect the geography to two further Qur'anic images: the *"lowest of the low"* (95:5) into which the ungrateful are cast, and the *"narrow place"* into which the condemned will be driven — *"and when they are cast into it, a narrow place, chained together, they will call there for destruction"* (25:13). The vertical symbolism is unmistakable and is meant to be felt: the book of the wicked lies at the greatest possible distance beneath the highest things — its counterpart, as the sūrah will show at v.18, lies in the highest place above all heavens. Deeds, in the Qur'an's cosmology, do not vanish; they *ascend or descend*, and each company's record rests at the pole its deeds chose.

**The Book That Waits: What Kind of Record Sijjīn Holds**

Alongside the reading of Sijjīn as a place, the commentators — al-Qushayrī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarṣī among them — transmit a second reading: that Sijjīn here characterizes *the book itself*, the register in which evil deeds are indelibly recorded, named after the place of its custody the way one says "the court record of the dungeon." On either reading, the operative fact is the same and is the verse's entire point: *the record exists.* The sūrah's opening showed deeds performed in the half-dark of the marketplace; this verse shows where those deeds went. The recording apparatus of the Qur'an has been running since the first page of revelation: *"Meticulous writers"* (82:10–11) attend every soul; *"two receivers, seated on the right hand and the left"* catch every uttered word, *"not a word does he utter but that with him is a watcher ready"* (50:17–18). The defrauder's crime had two witnesses from the moment of the skimmed pinch; this verse discloses the filing cabinet.

Here the modern reader may feel an unexpected shiver of familiarity, for the contemporary world has rediscovered, through technology, the truth this verse taught by revelation: that no act is really lost. Cameras, logs, metadata, archives — our civilization now generates, against its will, the very totality of record that the Qur'an asserted fourteen centuries ago. Yet all of that is parable and shadow. The Book of Sijjīn differs from every human database in two decisive respects: it was kept when there were no sensors, and it records what no sensor reaches — the intention behind the wet grain at the bottom of the heap. The verse invites the reader to conduct a private audit: if a transcript existed of every measure I have given and taken, every price I have quoted, every corner I have cut when no one watched — where would its custodian keep it, and with what care?

**Why the Defrauders' Story Continues Here: The Chapter's Widening Lens**

Finally, the verse should be read as the answer to the question of vv. 4–6 rather than as a new subject. There the Qur'an asked: *do they not think they will be resurrected?* Here it supplies the evidence that the resurrection is no idle threat: their file already exists, already has an address, already is sealed (as the next verse will add). The logic is that of a prosecutor laying the dossier on the bench before the defendants have finished insisting that no dossier exists. And the widening from defrauders to fujjār tells every reader how to apply the verse: find the sin whose smallness protects it — the skim, the quiet lie, the unwatched shortcut — and understand that its entry has been carried, with the same care as the greatest crimes, to the lowest of the seven earths. *Kallā* — the universe is not careless. The book is in Sijjīn; the only open question is whose hand wrote it there.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8

> **And what will apprise thee of Sijjīn?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Confesses Its Own Limits**

The Qur'an now does something it does in only a handful of places, always at the moments of deepest mystery: it asks a question and, in the asking, admits that no answer in this world's language can suffice. *Wa-mā adrāka mā Sijjīn* — "And what will apprise thee of Sijjīn?" The formula comes from the root **d-r-y**, whose verb *adrayta* means to bring someone to *real knowledge*, knowledge attained and possessed. The question is therefore not "do you know what Sijjīn is?" but something stronger: *what could ever bring you to truly know it?* And the rhetorical expectation built into the Arabic is: nothing here can. The verse is addressed to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ — the most knowledgeable of all creatures concerning the unseen — and by addressing him it addresses every reader through him: if the Messenger cannot be *brought to know* Sijjīn by anything short of revelation's pointer, then no description, no sermon, no imagination of ours exhausts the thing.

The Qur'an uses this exact interrogative template at the great obscurities of destiny: *"And what will apprise thee of the Night of Decree?"* (97:2–3); *"And what will apprise thee of the Staşter?... And what will apprise thee of the Blazing Fire?"* (104:5–6); *"And what will apprise thee of the crushing blow?"* (101:9–11); *"And what will apprise thee of the Day of Judgment? Again, what will apprise thee of the Day of Judgment?"* (82:17–18). In every case the device performs the same triple function: it exalts the matter (only things of ultimate weight get this question), it humbles the knower (the Prophet ﷺ himself is made to stand at the edge of his knowledge), and it concentrates the reader's attention (a confessed mystery is attended to more, not less, than an explained one). Al-Rāzī notes that the formula teaches a complete epistemology in two words: there are realities whose existence is certain and whose *nature* is veiled; belief attaches to the first, humility to the second.

**Why God Names What He Does Not Describe**

A thoughtful reader may ask: if Sijjīn cannot be conveyed, why name it at all? The answer lies in how the Qur'an teaches the unseen. It gives the unknown a *proper name* the way a physician names a disease before its course is understood — the name anchors the mind, gathers the fear and the warning into one addressable point, and makes the thing a subject of attention rather than a fog. The Arab would speak of Sijjīn by name as he spoke of the Kaʿbah or the seasons; the word entered the community's vocabulary as a place-name of dread, the downward counterpart of the upward names — ʿIlliyyūn, Tasnīm, al-Kawthar — that the same chapter will soon bestow. The Qur'an thus gives the two destinies a matched set of proper nouns: a name for the lowest of the seven earths and a name for the exalted height, so that no believer can plead that the two roads were left unmarked. Naming, in revelation, is a form of signage.

The theological restraint of the verse deserves equal notice. Islam forbids speculation that outstrips the texts — the Prophet ﷺ warned against speaking of God and the unseen without knowledge — and this verse is the standing charter of that restraint. Where the Qur'an gives a name and a pointer, the believer may say what the sources say and must stop where they stop. The verse converts an epistemological limit into a spiritual posture: before the realities of the Hereafter the correct state is * certainty of existence with silence about the details* — the posture the Prophet ﷺ embodied when he said, in the long narration of the night journey and other places, that he was shown things he was permitted to convey only in part.

**The Pedagogy of Awe: Teaching by Admitted Incompleteness**

There is also a deliberate psychology in the device. Human attention sags before what it thinks it has mastered. If the Qur'an had offered a complete floor plan of Sijjīn — dimensions, temperatures, durations — the reader would soon feel he had *understood* it, and understanding breeds the fatal comfort of the defrauder: the sense that the account is manageable. By confessing that the thing exceeds conveyance, the verse keeps the account permanently open, permanently larger than the reader's mental model of it. It is the rhetorical opposite of the fraudster's own bookkeeping, which always balances by leaving things out. Al-Zamakhsharī and the school of rhetorical analysis (balāghah) count these questions among the Qur'an's master-strokes of *taʿjīb* — the provocation of wonder — and wonder, in the Qur'an's curriculum, is not decoration; it is the beginning of vigilance. A man walks carefully past a pit he cannot see the bottom of.

Modern communication science stumbled onto the same principle from below: warnings that feel fully processed lose their force, while acknowledged unknowns keep behavior guarded. The Qur'an, in this single question, both warns and heals the modern overconfidence that believes every mystery is one investigation away from closure. Some doors, the verse teaches, are known by their lock rather than their room: Sijjīn is known as *the place of the wicked's book* — that much is given — and what it is beyond that will be known only by arrival. The believer's task is to make sure his book is not there when the knowledge becomes unavoidable.

**Two Questions, Two Poles: The Symmetry Already Rising**

One final feature prepares the chapter's architecture. This question — *what will apprise thee of Sijjīn?* — will be matched, eleven verses later, by its twin: *"And what will apprise thee of ʿIlliyyūn?"* (83:19). The same interrogative, aimed once downward and once upward, tells the reader that the two poles are symmetric mysteries: the terror below is as far beyond conveyance as the bliss above. This symmetry disciplines both errors the soul is prone to. It corrects the flippancy that treats Hell as a vague metaphor — it is named, located, and filed. And it corrects the presumptuousness that claims to map Paradise to the last chamber — even its highest register is introduced by a question that concedes it exceeds telling. Between the two questions the whole middle of the sūrah will unfold; and the reader who has absorbed this first question already holds the method for reading all of it: *believe the names, heed the pointers, and let the unknowable do exactly what the verse intends — make the weight of eternity press upon the decisions of this afternoon.*

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9

> **A book inscribed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Inscribed and Sealed: The Vocabulary of a Finished Record**

The two-word verse is a drumbeat of finality. *Kitābun marqūm* — "a book inscribed." The participle *marqūm* comes from the root **r-q-m**, whose core meaning is *writing*, and specifically — as the lexicographers and the source commentary note — *large, bold, decisive writing*: the inscription cut into stone or painted boldly onto a surface, not the loose jotting of a memorandum. The classical commentators add a second layer to the word. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī report that *marqūm* here means *sealed* (makhtūm) — the writing closed like a document whose contents are fixed; and Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī draw the consequence in plain terms: what is written there *cannot be decreased, cannot be increased, and cannot be erased*. The book of the profligate is not an open ledger awaiting further entries or editorial mercy; it is a completed, bold, sealed record — the archival opposite of everything the defrauder's mind imagined. He dealt in small, deniable, invisible quantities; his file has been kept in the largest possible letters, in permanent ink, under seal.

There is an exquisite irony in the placement. The defrauder's entire method depended upon the *unwritten* — the pinch of grain that never entered any record, the wetness buried at the bottom of the heap where no document could testify. The Qur'an's reply is that heaven out-documented him. Every skimmed measure that he kept off the earthly books was written — boldly, irreversibly — into the one archive no clerk can be bribed to amend. The verse thus completes the logic begun at v.7: *kallā* — the deed is not lost; *fī Sijjīn* — it has an address; *kitābun marqūm* — it is in permanent, sealed, bold hand. Three verses, three nails in the coffin of the illusion of the unrecorded deed.

**A Civilization Built on the Written Record: The Qur'an and the Pen**

It is fitting that the Book of God describes the Last Day through a metaphor of writing, for no scripture ever labored more consciously to build a civilization of the written record. The very first verses revealed to the Prophet ﷺ exalted the instrument: *"Read, in the name of thy Lord who created... who taught by the pen, taught man that which he knew not"* (96:1–5). The Qur'an calls itself *"in a Preserved Tablet"* (85:21–22) — the archetype of every safe record — and its longest single verse (2:282) is not about prayer or doctrine but about the *written documentation of debt*: reduce your loans to writing, dictate justly, call witnesses, let neither scribe nor debtor be wronged. Islamic law went on to require written contracts, to train professional scribes and witnesses, and to build the chancery culture that carried the classical civilization's administration. The moral logic running through all of it is the logic of this verse: *because God keeps a perfect record, His servants are commanded to keep honest ones.* The sealed book in Sijjīn is, in a sense, the source code of every honest ledger below.

The verse also dignifies, by contrast, the believer's own habit of self-accounting. The early Muslims taught each other to keep the inner ledger before the outer one arrives. It is related that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb said:

> *"Reckon yourselves before you are reckoned, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed; for the accounting will be lighter tomorrow upon those who took themselves to account today."*

Whether one takes the saying as his or as the counsel of the early generation generally, it became the operating principle of Islamic spirituality under the name *muḥāsabah* — self-audit. The believer who reviews his day's measures, prices, promises, and shortcuts is doing in small what this verse says has been done in total: he is reading his own file while amendment is still possible. The sealed book is fixed; the *draft* of it, so to speak, is still in the believer's hands. That asymmetry — eternal fixity of the record, present availability of the repentance — is where the verse's urgency and its mercy live at once.

**Why a Sealed Record Is Both Terror and Mercy**

The finality of *marqūm* cuts in two directions, and the Qur'an wants both felt. Toward the defiant, it is the closure of every loophole: no appeal to forgetfulness that was really policy, no re-negotiation of the entry, no erasure by the powerful. Human tyrants burn archives; God's archive does not burn. The Qur'an has the record itself speak on that Day: *"Read thy book! Today thy soul suffices as thy own reckoner"* (17:14), and *"This is Our book that speaks against you in truth; indeed We were recording what you used to do"* (45:28–29). A witness that cannot be cross-examined, threatened, or lost — that is the terror of *marqūm* for the fujjār.

Toward the wronged and the honest, the same seal is pure mercy. Every defrauded buyer whose loss was too small to litigate, every laborer whose stolen wage left no paper trail, every merchant whose competitor prospered by fraud — the verse tells them all that the record exists in the boldest possible writing and cannot be tampered with. Injustice in this world often wins by *destroying the documentation*; the verse announces that in the only court that finally counts, documentation destruction is impossible. The believer who suffers quietly under an unprovable wrong is invited by this verse to a specific comfort: the wrong is not unprovable — it is inscribed, sealed, and stored at the depth where God keeps such things. What remains for the wronged is not despair but the patience of those who know where the true archive lies.

**The Ledger Beneath Every Ledger: A Closing Reflection**

Every age keeps records, and every age discovers that its records can be gamed: the honest-looking document conceals the dishonest deal; the audited account conceals the unaudited assumption. *Kitābun marqūm* tells the reader that beneath all human bookkeeping runs a bookkeeping that cannot be gamed — not because its keeper is cleverer than the defrauders, but because He is present at the deed itself, requiring no entry to be copied from anywhere. The practical yield for daily life is a rule as simple as it is demanding: *live as if written down, because you are.* Write your contracts honestly (2:282), keep your own accounts nightly, and let the visible record be a shadow of the invisible one — never its contradiction. The man who does this has already reconciled with the book in Sijjīn in the only way it can be reconciled with: by making sure it holds nothing of his, or by seeking the erasure God alone can grant — for the seal on the book of deeds is absolute for the unrepentant, while the repentant have been promised a rewriting: *"God changes not what is in a people until they change what is in themselves"* (13:11), and the Prophet ﷺ recorded from his Lord the promise that the one who draws near to God by a hand's breadth in repentance is met by a drawing near of arms' lengths. The boldest inscription in the universe is the record of deeds; the boldest eraser in the universe is the turning heart.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:10

> **Woe that Day to the deniers,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain of Ruin: Why the Qur'an Repeats Its Harshest Word**

With this verse the sūrah enters a rhythm the reader of the Qur'an will recognize from its closing pages: *waylun yawmaʾidhin lil-mukadhdhibīn* — "Woe that Day to the deniers." The formula is a refrain. It tolls through Sūrah al-Mursalāt ten times ("Woe that Day to the deniers" at 77:15, 19, 24, 28, 34, 37, 45, 47, 49), rings twice in Sūrah al-Ṭūr (52:11, 13), and returns here in al-Muṭaffifīn. The repetition is not redundancy; it is the Qur'an's drum. In oral revelation, delivered verse by verse to listeners who memorized as they heard, a recurring cadence fixed the message in the memory the way a bell fixes the hour — and each toll gathered new meaning from its surroundings. Here, sounding after the sealed book of the profligate has been exhibited, the refrain tells the listener why that book was opened: because its owners were, at bottom, *deniers*. The fraud was their practice; the denial was their creed; and the woe is pronounced on the creed that licensed the practice.

It is worth feeling the rhetorical physics of a refrain. A single warning can be discounted as an outburst; a warning that returns, identically, again and again, becomes a law of the text's universe — something the reader learns to expect, and therefore something that cannot be dismissed as rhetorical weather. The Qur'an's refrains (this one, "Woe that Day to the deniers," and its merciful mirror, the refrain of reward in Sūrah al-Raḥmān — *"so which of your Lord's bounties will you deny?"* thirty-one times) are among the most powerful devices in all religious literature precisely because repetition, handled thus, does not dull the edge but *rings* it. Tradition also remembers that the Arabs, for all their eloquence, stood before this word. The Prophet ﷺ himself, explaining the weight of *wayl*, said it is *"a valley in Hell — were it not that you speak much of it, none of you would have the strength to hear of it"* (al-Tirmidhī 2576). When the Qur'an chooses to ring that valley like a bell ten times over one class of people, the reader should adjust his posture accordingly.

**"That Day": The Date Stamp of the Verdict**

The refrain adds something the sūrah's first word of woe did not carry: a date. At 83:1 the woe was pronounced without qualification — *waylun lil-muṭaffifīn*, woe to the defrauders, wherever and whenever they are. Here the woe is pinned to a specific appointment: *yawmaʾidhin* — "that Day," the tremendous day of standing described in vv. 5–6. The shift carries a warning about time's two courts. The defrauder who prospers for forty years is tempted to conclude that no verdict was ever delivered; the refrain answers that the verdict was merely *scheduled*. In God's order, acquittal in time proves nothing — the trial is adjourned, not dismissed — and *yawmaʾidhin* is the adjournment's end. Every reader who has watched a wrongdoer die comfortable has felt the objection this verse pre-empts: the refrain, repeated across sūrahs like a court order re-issued, insists that the date on the file has not changed. Woe was real when it was first pronounced; it will be *executed* that Day.

There is also comfort folded into the same phrase, for the wronged. The cheated measure will be avenged *on that Day* — not arbitrarily late, but exactly on the day when all accounts are opened together, when the fraudster stands in the same crowd as his victims and his crime is read from a sealed book. The Qur'an teaches the believer to carry his grievances not as grudges but as filed claims — and the refrain is the receipt: the hearing is set.

**"The Deniers": A Word for a Profession of Refusal**

The verse's target is *al-mukadhdhibīn*, from the root **k-dh-b**, "to lie." But the form is the intensive *faʿʿāl* pattern — *mukadhdhib*, not merely *kādhib* — and the Arabic grammar carries a theology inside it. A *kādhib* is a man who tells a lie; a *mukadhdhib* is a man for whom lying-back-truth has become a settled profession, one who habitually, deliberately, structurally rejects what is true when it confronts him. The commentators underline the distinction: the denial meant here is not ignorance of the truth, nor mere doubt, but *rejection after exposure* — truth delivered, truth understood, truth refused. The next verses will make the exposure explicit: *"When Our signs are recited unto him, he says: Fables of those of old!"* (83:13). The mukadhdhib is the man who answers revelation with a sneer.

This is why the refrain's severity is just rather than arbitrary. Every moral system can condemn the man who never heard the truth; the Qur'an's heaviest word is reserved for the man who heard it and *worked to suppress it* — because his refusal corrupts others. The professional denier is the intellectual engineer of every society's corruption: the one who assures the market that the wet grain is a rumor, that the measure is honest, that the reckoning is myth. He does not merely sin; he *manufactures permission* for sin at scale. The refrain that follows him across ten verses of Sūrah al-Mursalāt is, in effect, the sound of the file being marked at every one of his appearances.

**The Chain of the Chapter: From Thumb to Creed, from Creed to Woe**

Placed within al-Muṭaffifīn, this verse completes the chapter's second diagnosis and sets up its third. The movement of the sūrah's logic is now fully visible. Verses 1–3 observed the *hands*: demanding in full, giving short. Verses 4–6 exposed the *mind*: they do not think they will be resurrected. Verses 7–9 exhibited the *file*: the book of the profligate, in Sijjīn, inscribed and sealed. And now verses 10–12 name the *creed* that explains the mind that licensed the hands: denial — first of the Day (v.11), then of every sign that predicted it (v.13). The chain from a wet grain heap to a cosmic refusal is complete, and the Qur'an wants the reader to see that it *is* a chain: no one begins by denying the Lord of the worlds; one begins by wanting a profit that accountability would forbid, and then works backward, creed by creed, until the whole eschatology has been demoted to "fables of those of old." The refrain stands over that chain like a magistrate over the dock: whatever stage of it a man occupies — thumb on the pan, thought refused, file sealed, or sneer recited — *waylun yawmaʾidhin lil-mukadhdhibīn* is the sentence already printed at its end.

For the modern reader, the verse's warning has acquired new relevance, because ours is the age of professional denial systems — institutions and habits built to make truth-dodging scalable. The mukadhdhib no longer stands in one marketplace; he manages narratives. The Qur'an's refrain, tolling unchanged across fourteen centuries, informs him that narrative management is itself a measured transaction: every suppressed truth is a quantity taken in full and given short, entered in bold hand, sealed in a book, and dated to a Day.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:11

> **who deny the Day of Judgment,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day of Judgment: The Fātiḥah's Creed Returned as an Indictment**

Every Muslim recites, in every unit of every prayer, the declaration *māliki yawmi al-dīn* — "Master of the Day of Judgment" (1:4). This verse now names the ones who recite it with their tongues while denying it with their lives: *alladhīna yukadhdhibūna bi-yawmi al-dīn* — "who deny the Day of Judgment." The connection to the opening of the Qur'an was not lost on the commentators. The Fātiḥah mapped humanity onto a straight path and two deviant roads; the closing sūrahs of the muṣḥaf populate those roads. Al-Muṭaffifīn's deniers are the Fātiḥah's "those who have earned wrath and gone astray" seen from the inside — their creed stated in a single clause. The Qur'an is a single fabric: the prayer's daily confession and the market's hidden fraud meet on the same page, and this verse is the seam.

The word *dīn* itself rewards unpacking, for it carries in Arabic a family of meanings that this sūrah deliberately plays upon. *Dīn* is judgment and reckoning; *dīn* is religion, the way of life owed to God; and from the same root, *dayn* (plural *duyūn*) is *debt* — an obligation outstanding between two parties. The "Day of al-Dīn" is therefore, in a single word: the day of final reckoning, the day of religion's vindication, and — in a sense the defrauder feels in his bones — *the day of debt collection*. The Qur'an makes the connection explicit elsewhere: *"The Judgment (al-dīn) shall surely come to pass"* (51:6); *"This is the Day of Judgment, which you used to deny"* (37:21); and, in the immediately preceding sūrah, *"Nay, but you deny the Judgment"* (82:9). Every skimmed measure is an outstanding debt; every defrauder is a debtor who believes the ledger closed. The verse tells him the debtor's day is set — the day of *al-dīn* is precisely the day when *duyūn* are paid.

**Denial of the Day as the Root Corruption: The Lesson of Sūrah al-Māʿūn**

Why does this one denial merit a refrain of woes across the Qur'an's final pages? Because, as the Book teaches with unmatched clarity in another short sūrah, the denial of the Judgment does not remain a private opinion; it *trickles down* into small daily cruelties. Sūrah al-Māʿūn opens with the identical verb: *"Hast thou seen him who denies the Judgment (al-dīn)?"* (107:1) — and then answers its own question with a portrait:

> *"Such is he who repulses the orphan, and urges not the feeding of the needy. So woe unto those who pray, yet are neglectful of their prayers, those who make a show, and withhold small kindnesses."* (107:2–7)

The order of the portrait is the whole argument. The man who denies the Judgment is discovered not at a philosophical conference but at the door where an orphan is turned away — and the chain descends from false creed to neglected prayer to *withholding the small kindnesses* (māʿūn: the little loanable things of daily life — the bucket, the tools, the pinch of salt). Al-Muṭaffifīn is al-Māʿūn's chapter-length twin: both sūrahs locate the great denial in small refusals. The logic is exact. A man who truly stands before the Lord of the worlds next Thursday — so to speak — cannot relax his grip on the neighbor's bucket or the buyer's grain. Remove the standing, and every small withholding becomes "reasonable." The denial of the Day is thus not one sin among others; it is the *deregulating* sin — the one that removes the governor from every other engine at once.

**The Defrauder's Creed: Practical Atheism About the Audit**

The commentators observed that this verse, following vv. 4–6, exposes a subtler truth about unbelief than the modern debate usually allows. The defrauders of Madinah were not necessarily atheists in the metaphysical sense; most of them performed the rites their society knew. Their denial was *functional*: they denied the Day in the operative sense that nothing in their conduct presupposed it. Belief, in the Qur'an's accounting, is not what a man says about the unseen but what his conduct *takes for granted*. The verse's grammar — the deniers defined by *what they deny*, placed directly after the sealed book has been shown — instructs the reader to audit creeds by consequences: show me the measures a man gives in the dark, and I will show you his eschatology. This is why the Qur'an can call the defrauder a denier without asking him for a statement of faith; the wet grain at the bottom of the heap was his statement of faith. It said, more sincerely than any creed: *no one is keeping the books.*

The verse also dignifies, by contrast, what true belief in the Day does for a community. A population that genuinely expects the audit develops what we would now call low-cost trust: sellers who correct errors against their own interest, borrowers who repay without chase, judges who cannot be bought because they will be audited by the One who cannot be deceived. Economists call these "institutions"; the Qur'an calls them the fruit of *īmān bi-yawmi al-dīn* — and this verse catalogs what their absence looks like: *wayl*.

**The Rejecters Then and Now: Why the Clause Speaks to Every Age**

Finally, the verse's present-participle form — *yukadhdhibūna*, "those who deny," ongoing, active — tells every generation where to look for its own fulfillment. The Day is denied today less often by argument than by *calendar*: the assumption, built into careers and ledgers and national plans, that the only deadlines are worldly deadlines. A civilization can run entire industries on the practical premise that no reckoning follows death — and can then be astonished at the short measures that premise quietly produces. Against that astonishment, the Qur'an's short clause stands as the permanent explanation: *they deny the Day of Judgment.* Where the measures run crooked, the verse teaches the believer to name the root without hatred and without despair — and to repair his own corner of the audit first: to become, in his shop, his office, and his home, one man for whom the Day of *dīn* is so real that no debt of his remains outstanding when the sun of that morning rises.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:12

> **which none deny save every sinful transgressor.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Not a Mistake but a Transgression: The Grammar of the Verdict**

The verse now delivers its conclusion about the psychology of denial, and it does so in a sentence whose every word is loaded: *wa-mā yukadhdhibu bihi illā kullu muʿtin ʿadhdhāthim* — "which none deny save every sinful transgressor." The key term is the rare *ʿadhdhāthim* — a *ḥapax legomenon*, a word that occurs nowhere else in the Qur'an, and the commentators worked to fix its sense. It derives from **ʿ-d-th**, a root whose core meaning in Arabic is *filth, impurity, noisome thing*; the Arabs used *ʿadth* of offensive matter that had become putrid. Combined into the intensive form and paired with the verbal noun, the phrase *kullu muʿtin ʿadhdhāthim* designates *every habitual sinner steeped in his own impurity* — the sinner not as one who fell, but as one who has *marinated*. Ibn ʿAbbās glosses it simply as "the great sinner" (kullu athim kabīr). Al-Rāzī draws out the syntax's logic: the two words are not two separate descriptions but one composite portrait — a transgressor (*muʿtin*, one who oversteps the bounds set for him) whose transgression has accumulated into a settled condition of impurity. The verse thus issues a stunning diagnosis: *no one denies the Day except the man whose sins have made him require its nonexistence.* Denial is not an intellectual position held from neutrality; it is a position held from *contamination*.

The sentence's structure — "none deny save..." (*illā*) — is a logical claim of exclusivity, and its converse deserves to be heard: whoever is *not* steeped in sinful self-interest does not, in fact, deny. The Qur'an states this epistemology of the heart in general terms:

> *"Have you seen him who takes his desire for his god?"* (45:23; cf. 47:14)

The Qur'an's position, distilled by this verse, is that creed follows appetite. Men do not usually reason their way to rejecting the reckoning; they *need* to reject it, and the need is manufactured by sin. The first theft makes the second audit unwelcome; the second makes the auditor hated; by the tenth, the office of the auditor itself must be abolished — and the abolishing is dressed up as philosophy. This is the verse's cold-eyed account of the genealogy of unbelief, and every honest reader can test it against his own smaller denials: the duties we stop believing in are so often the ones that had begun to cost us.

**Denial as Crime: The Inversion of the Modern Categories**

The modern world classifies belief and disbelief as neutral epistemic states — private conclusions, outside the reach of moral law. The Qur'an's categories are different, and this verse is one of the places where the difference is sharpest. Denial of a truth clearly delivered is treated as a *deed* — a chosen act with victims: the truth itself is wronged, the messengers who carried it are slandered, and the social fabric that the truth protects (honest measures, kept covenants, unviolated trusts) is stripped of its load-bearing beam. Islam's theology of responsibility rests on freedom: *"Whosoever wills, let him believe; and whosoever wills, let him disbelieve"* (18:29) — the invitation is free precisely so that the refusal can be guilty. No one is punished for an inability to know; the *ʿadhdhāthim* is punished for a *refusal to acknowledge* — the difference between stumbling in the dark and putting out the lamp.

That difference matters pastorally, and the commentators have always guarded it. The doubter who says "I cannot see my way to this" is not the verse's target; the sneer that says "I decline to let this be true, because its truth inconveniences me" is. Islam distinguishes *shakk* (honest doubt) from *juḥūd* (knowing refusal) as medicine distinguishes illness from malingering — and the prognosis differs accordingly: doubt can be healed by light; refusal must first be cured of its *reason*, which is the sin. This is why the tradition's treatment of the doubting seeker is patience and argument, while its treatment of the defiant denier is this verse's cold verdict: such denial has an etiology, and the etiology is moral, not logical.

**The Sound Heart and the Sealed One: The Prophetic Anatomy**

The verse's portrait of denial-as-disease prepares the sūrah's next great image — the heart covered with rust (v.14) — and stands beside the most famous statement the Prophet ﷺ ever made about the organ that does our believing. It is narrated from al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr, who said:

> *"Beware! In the body there is a morsel of flesh: if it is sound, the whole body is sound; and if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt. Beware! It is the heart."* (al-Bukhārī 52; Muslim 1599)

The ḥadīth and the verse are two halves of one anatomy. The heart is the body's king — when it is *salīm* (sound, wholesome), perception itself is healthy: truth arrives, is recognized, is obeyed. When the heart is corrupted by settled sin, the corruption spreads upward into cognition: the man does not merely do wrong; he *sees* wrongly — the Day of accounts becomes incredible, the prophets become fabulists, the conscience becomes an embarrassment to be managed. Modern psychology has documented the machinery under the names of rationalization and moral disengagement: people who need to keep misbehaving progressively adjust their *ideas* to license it. The Qur'an had already named the mechanism, diagnosed its seat, and — in the coming verse — named its visible symptom: rust.

**"None Deny Save...": The Comfort Hidden in the Verdict**

It remains to notice that the verse's exclusivity is good news for the vast majority of human beings, including the sincere doubters and the confused. If *none deny save the steeped transgressor*, then denial is not the default condition of mankind, nor the fate of the intelligent, nor the honest conclusion of open inquiry. The ordinary human heart — the farmer, the nurse, the child, the seeker — when it is not clogged with the sediment of willful wrong, *recognizes* the truth of the reckoning as it recognizes fire's heat. This is the Qur'an's anthropology: *fiṭrah*, the original disposition that knows its Maker and expects its audit. The verse thereby relativizes the noise of denial: it is the pathology of a class — those with a motive — not the verdict of the species. And it hands every man the most practical of self-tests: not "how clever is my argument?" but "what would my argument be worth if the sin it protects were removed?" The tradition's counsel follows at once: clean the motive, and watch how quickly the creed heals. The sūrah is even now prescribing the solvent — repentance that polishes, remembrance that keeps the polish — for rust that this verse has explained.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:13

> **When Our signs are recited unto him, he says, "Fables of those of old!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scene of Recitation: Truth Delivered Audibly**

The verse moves from the general portrait of the denier to a single concrete scene, rendered in the singular — *idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā*, "When Our signs are recited *unto him*" — as though the whole class of deniers were gathered into one man sitting across from the reciter. The verb of the scene is *tulā*, from **t-l-w**, whose root meaning is *to follow closely*: to follow one letter after another, one word after another, in order. Recitation in the Qur'anic sense is thus not the casual casting of ideas into a room; it is the deliberate, sequential delivery of revelation, word by word, into a hearer's ear. The phrase "Our signs" (*āyātunā*) spans the full range of God's communications — the verses of the Book and the signs in creation the verses interpret. Both, the scene says, have been *personally delivered* to this man: he has heard the recitation with his own ears. The verse thereby fixes the ground of the coming verdict: the denial to be judged is not absence of message but response to message. The signs came; he answered them with a phrase.

The Qur'an records this identical scene at every station of its final decades. In Sūrah al-Qalam, the exact exchange appears: *"And when Our signs are recited unto him, he says: Fables of the ancients"* (68:15). In al-Aḥqāf, the son argues with his believing father in the same words (46:17). In al-Anʿām: *"And among them are those who listen to thee; and We have placed upon their hearts coverings, lest they understand it, and in their ears a heaviness. And if they see every sign they will not believe in it, until when they come to thee, they dispute with thee — those who disbelieve say: This is nothing but fables of the ancients"* (6:25). The repetition of the sneer in the Qur'an's record is itself documentation: the phrase was *the* standard answer of the Quraysh to the recitation, and the Book preserved their slogan so that every later generation would recognize it when they heard it again.

**The Evasion and Its Content: What "Fables of the Ancients" Meant**

The denier's answer, *asāṭīr al-awwalīn* — "fables (or legends) of the ancients" — was a precise accusation, not a vague wave of the hand. *Asāṭīr* (singular *usṭūrah*) denotes old tales, written narratives handed down from former peoples; the plural combines with "the ancients" to allege: *what you recite is recycled literature* — the stories of the Persians, the Arabs' night tales, the scriptures of the Jews, gathered and retold. The Quraysh pressed the charge with dates and mechanics. They said the Prophet ﷺ was *"taught"* by a foreign youth; and they said, as Sūrah al-Furqān records:

> *"And they say: Fables of the ancients that he has had written down, and they are dictated to him morning and evening."* (25:5)

and in Sūrah al-Anfāl, put into the mouths of the listeners at the recitations themselves:

> *"And when Our signs are recited unto them, they say: We have heard; if we wished we could speak the like of this; these are nothing but fables of the ancients."* (8:31)

The allegation, in other words, was plagiarism plus mediocrity: old tales, newly copied, sayable by anyone. It is worth stating plainly what the Qur'an did *not* do in reply. It did not answer with a defense of sources — no chain of transmission is offered, no library cited. It answered with the text itself, because the allegation was about the text: is this the like of what men can say? The standing challenge frames the whole controversy: *"And if you are in doubt concerning what We have sent down upon Our servant, then bring a sūrah like it, and call your witnesses apart from God, if you are truthful"* (2:23); *"Say: If mankind and the jinn gathered together to bring the like of this Qur'an, they could not bring the like of it, even if they backed one another"* (17:88). And the historical circumstance itself was an argument no scribal conspiracy could explain: *"Thou didst not recite any book before it, nor didst thou write it with thy right hand; then the dealers in falsehood would have doubted"* (29:48). The man accused of dictating from hidden manuscripts was the man who could neither read nor write, in a city that watched him daily. The generation that made the accusation could not answer the challenge; their heirs have had fourteen centuries of further attempts, and the Book still stands recited in the same syllables. The response to "fables of the ancients" was, finally, not an argument but a presence: a text unlike any other text, producing a community unlike any other community.

**Why the Label Works: The Psychology of the Dismissive Shortcut**

The verse's lasting value, however, lies in its exposure of a *method*, for the method has outlived the Quraysh. The denier does not engage the signs; he *files* them — and filing requires only a label. "Fables of the ancients" performs three feats in four words: it disposes of the content without examination (old tales), it disposes of the messenger without inquiry (a reteller), and it flatters the denier without labor (he, unlike his ancestors, is not taken in). Cognitive science would later name the machinery — categorization shortcuts, the appeal to the unfashionable, the genetic fallacy of judging a claim by its alleged origins rather than its truth. The Qur'an's point is moral rather than merely logical: the label is chosen *because* examination is dangerous. Al-Rāzī notes that the recitation threatened precisely what the previous verse described — the sinner's settled impurity; a serious hearing of the signs might have cost him his sins, and his sins had become his identity. The sneer was cheaper than repentance.

Every age issues its own version of the phrase, and the believer should learn to hear it as the Qur'an heard it: not as an argument but as a *symptom*. "Medieval superstition," "ancient mythology," "outgrown fairy tales" — the vocabulary changes; the function is identical, and the function, as this sūrah has insisted from its opening, is protection of the skimmed measure. A man walking past a mosque recitation with that phrase ready on his tongue is the Quraysh merchant walking past the Prophet's ﷺ pulpit: the signs are being recited, the answer has been prepared, and the answer's brevity is its confession.

**The Reader's Mirror: What the Scene Asks of the Believer**

Because the scene is rendered in the second person's radius — "recited *unto him*" while the reader stands just outside the frame — the verse also turns gently on the believer. Those who accept the Qur'an can still possess the denier's ears. There are verses a Muslim has heard a hundred times in prayer and never allowed to finish their thought; there are commands recited annually in Ramadan whose compliance has been filed under "later." The difference between the sneering denier and the sleepy believer is smaller than either would like — both have answered recitation with a phrase. The cure for both is the root meaning of *tilāwah* itself: to *follow*, word after word, into practice — to let the recitation cost something before one's answer is recorded. The deniers of this verse refused that following, and their file in Sijjīn, bold and sealed, is the record of four words said over a lifetime of signs. The believer's file is still being written; the scene stands ready to be re-entered at the next recitation, and the next answer is, as always, a choice.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14

> **Nay! But that which they used to earn has covered their hearts with rust.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Nay! But Rust": The Second Hinge and the True Explanation of Denial**

The second *kallā* of the sūrah falls like a gavel over the denier's self-portrait. He has just explained himself: the signs are "fables of the ancients" — a reasoned position, he imagines, of a critical mind. The verse overturns the explanation: *kallā bal rāna ʿalā qulūbihim mā kānū yaksibūn* — "Nay! But that which they used to earn has rusted over their hearts." Their refusal of revelation is not the verdict of reason; it is the symptom of a condition. Between their ears and the truth stands not an argument but a *deposit* — and the deposit was manufactured by their own earning. The Arabic is precise: *mā kānū yaksibūn*, "what they *used to earn*" — the continuous past of habit, the compound interest of repeated deeds. Rust does not appear in a day; it is the slow chemistry of exposure. So here: no single sin sealed the heart; the *accumulation* did — sin upon sin, each one small enough to deny itself, until the surface that once recognized its Lord could no longer.

The word *rān* (رَان) is one of the Qur'an's most famous rarities — it occurs in this form only here, and the philologists traced its root, **r-y-n**, to *ruwaynah*, the rust that forms on metal — above all on the blade of a sword left sheathed. ʿIkrimah, the great student of Ibn ʿAbbās, read the word in exactly that register: *rust, as rust upon a sword.* The image is exact in every particular. A sword rusts not by catastrophe but by neglect — sheathed, unused, damp with its own acids; and the rust that forms is itself the metal, transformed into its own corruption. So the heart: it is not invaded from outside; its own repeated acts oxidize its surface. And a rusted blade still *looks* like a blade — which is why the denier can sit through the recitation of the signs sincerely believing himself a critical thinker. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and the others read the verse as the diagnosis of the *inviolately sinful*: those for whom sin upon sin has sealed the heart from perceiving truth — enclosed, as another verse says, by their own deeds (*"they are surrounded by what they used to earn,"* 2:81? — rather *"until, when they are in the agonies of death..."* — the "surrounded by sins" image is of 2:81: *"No indeed! Those who earn evil and are surrounded by their sin — those are the inhabitants of the Fire"*) — while the honest heart still feels the verse of warning as fire feels a hand.

**The Hadith of the Black Spot: The Prophet's Own Exegesis**

The tradition supplies, in the Prophet's ﷺ own words, the verse's running commentary — a ḥadīth the source notes was related concerning this very verse:

> *"Verily, when the servant commits a sin, a black spot appears in his heart. If he turns in repentance, his heart is polished clean; but if he persists, the spot grows, until it covers his heart. That is the statement of God: Nay! But that which they used to earn has covered their hearts with rust."* (recorded by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī; transmitted in the Musnad of Aḥmad and the Mustadrak of al-Ḥākim, who graded it sound)

Every clause of the ḥadīth is doctrine. The spot is *quantitative but also qualitative*: one sin makes one spot; persistence multiplies and merges them; repentance does not merely stop the spread but *reverses* it — the heart is polished back. The ḥadīth thereby abolishes two modern errors at once. Against the fatalist idea that character is fixed, it teaches that the heart's surface is renewable at every turning. Against the permissive idea that sins are inert events with no residue, it teaches that every act *deposits* something — the black spot is sin's receipt, kept in the organ that did the choosing. The verse's word "earn" (*yaksibūn*) fits the ḥadīth's ledger exactly: *kasb* is acquisition, the gathering of gain; sin is an economy, and the rust is its inventory.

**The Polish of Remembrance: The Prophetic Prescription**

What then is the solvent? The tradition gives the diagnosis and the prescription in one breath. In another wording of the black-spot report the Prophet ﷺ warned: *"Beware of sins, for they leave rust upon the heart"*, and then he was asked for the cure, and his answer is one of the most quoted prescriptions in the entire tradition:

> *"Indeed, everything has a polish, and the polish of the heart is the remembrance of God. And nothing is more efficacious in saving a man from the punishment of God than the remembrance of God."* It was said: "Not even striving in the path of God?" He said: *"Not even if one were to strike with his sword until it breaks."* (recorded by al-Bayhaqī in Shuʿab al-Īmān and in the Musnad of Aḥmad; graded sound by the tradition's scholars)

The dialogue form of the report carries its weight: the Companions instinctively reached for the most strenuous deed they knew — jihād, with all its honor and hazard — and the Prophet ﷺ set above it the quiet, unrewarded-by-the-world act of *remembering*. The theology beneath the ranking is this verse's own: the disease is on the surface of the heart, and the cure must be an abrasive of constant, gentle contact — dhikr, the rehearsal of God's name, the Qur'an recited, the tongue returning to its Lord until the deposit wears thin. The early Muslims expressed the same prescription in the same metallurgy: it is reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and the early authorities that *"the hearts rust as iron rusts,"* and were asked what would polish them, answered: *"the recitation of the Qur'an and the remembrance of death."* The Qur'an itself confirms the chemistry from the opposite side, describing the gathering of the God-fearing around His speech: *"God has sent down the most beautiful of utterances, a Book consimilar, reiterated, whereat shudder the skins of those who fear their Lord; then their skins and their hearts soften unto the remembrance of God"* (39:23) — rust's exact opposite: skin and heart *softening*, the surface made permeable again to speech.

And the Qur'an warns of the end-state with the same concreteness it warned the earlier communities: *"Has not the time come for those who believe that their hearts should soften to the remembrance of God and to the truth that has come down, and that they be not as those who were given the Book before, for whom the term grew long, so their hearts hardened, and many of them are transgressors?"* (57:16). Time + neglect = hardness; the verse's rust is that equation drawn as a line.

**The Heart as a Mirror: The Psychology of the Numb Conscience**

The classical image behind *rān* — and the Sufis after the commentators made it explicit — is the heart as a mirror: created clear, clouded by breath and touch, restored by polishing. Modern moral psychology, arriving by another road, has verified the mechanism's core. Conscience operates as a signal — the twinge, the dread, the sleeplessness — and signals habituate: the tenth lie costs less anguish than the first, not because the tenth is better but because the instrument has recalibrated. Studies of repeated deception show the brain's responses to self-serving dishonesty *declining* with repetition — dishonesty, in the laboratory's phrase, escalates as its emotional signal wears. The Qur'an's rust is that wearing, announced fourteen centuries in advance, together with the one behavioral rule that the modern findings also confirm: *do not let the small sins normalize* — because the seal is made not of one great crime but of "what they used to earn," the habitual, the daily, the deniable. The defrauder of this sūrah is the textbook case: his crime is engineered to be below the threshold of feeling, and this verse announces the true price of that engineering — the death of the faculty that could have warned him.

**Rust Is Not the Final State: The Doors That Are Still Open**

Because the verse says the rust *covered* their hearts — not that the hearts were destroyed — the classical commentators stop short of despair, and so must the reader. The black-spot ḥadīth names the reversal explicitly: repentance polishes. The Prophet's ﷺ own supplication taught his community to ask daily for the mirror-state: *"O God,turner of hearts, make my heart firm upon Thy religion"* (al-Tirmidhī 2140? — "يا مقلب القلوب ثبت قلبي على دينك" — al-Tirmidhī 2140), and the tradition's saints built whole disciplines — the nightly audit, the blanket of istighfār, the company of the rememberers — around keeping the surface bright. The practical rule the verse yields for every believer is as urgent as it is simple: *sin quickly repented is a spot; sin repeated is a season of rust; sin re-labeled as virtue is the seal.* Between those three states the whole moral life is lived — and the verse that diagnosed the sealed heart closes, by implication, with its invitation: the polish is cheap, the water is at hand, and the mirror was made to show a Face, as the next verse will announce — the Face from which the rusted are about to be told they will be veiled.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15

> **Nay! Surely on that Day they will be veiled from their Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third "Nay!": The Veil as the Heart of the Sentence**

The third and final *kallā* of the sūrah arrives at the wicked cycle's darkest summit — and states it, remarkably, not as fire and not as torment but as *distance*: *kallā innahum ʿan rabbihim yawmaʾidhin lamaḥjūbūn* — "Nay! Surely on that Day they will be veiled from their Lord." The intensive *lamaḥjūbūn* — from **ḥ-j-b**, the root of veil, curtain, and partition (the word *ḥijāb* itself) — is doubled in force by the emphatic *lām*: they will *assuredly* be veiled. After the rust of the previous verse, the image completes itself: the heart oxidized in the world becomes, in the Hereafter, a person behind a curtain. The commentators across the schools — al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī, al-Ṭabarṣī, al-Zamakhsharī — all register the shock of the formulation: of all the punishments the Qur'an could have placed here, at the climax of the profligates' file, it places *separation*.

**Veiled from What? Mercy, or Vision — the Two Classical Readings**

What exactly are they veiled from? The tradition preserved two readings, and the source commentary reports both. The first: they are veiled from *God's mercy and magnanimity* (*karāmah*) — the curtain is over the treasury; no kindness of that Day will reach them (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and others record this). The second, pressed by al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī, is more literal and more terrible: in Arabic usage, one who is barred from *beholding* the king is said to be "veiled from him" — and thus the verse announces that the deniers are forbidden the *sight of their Lord*, while others are granted it. The verse's phrase, "veiled from their Lord" — using the intimate possessive *rabbihim*, "their" Lord — deepens the wound: the One they will be barred from is the very One who nurtured them, fed them, and gave them every lungful of their lives. The veil is not between them and a stranger.

The second reading attaches this verse to one of the great doctrines of Islam: *ruʾyat Allāh*, the vision of God in the Hereafter, which Ahl al-Sunnah affirm on the weight of the Qur'an and the mass-transmitted ḥadīth. The Qur'an says of the radiant on that Day: *"Some faces, that Day, shall be radiant, gazing upon their Lord"* (75:22–23) — and distinguishes them from others whose faces are *covered* by darkness (10:26–27), a pairing that mirrors this sūrah exactly. The Prophet ﷺ asked his Companions, on a cloudless night: *"Do you see the moon, and can any of you screen it?" They said yes. He said: "You will see your Lord as you see this moon; you suffer nothing in seeing it."* (recorded in the two Ṣaḥīḥs). And he described the finale of the feast of Paradise — after the inhabitants have exhausted every delight — as the moment God says: *"Do you wish Me to give you something more?"... and He will remove the veil, and they will not have been given anything more beloved to them than gazing upon their Lord.* (Muslim 181; al-Bukhārī 554). Against that horizon, the deniers' veil is not one punishment among others; it is the *definition* of their ruin. Everything else — the Fire, the taunt, the sweat — is circumstance. The sentence is the distance.

**The Vision as the Summit of Delight: Why the Veil Is the Sum of Losses**

To feel the verse's weight, one must understand what the tradition says the believers will be given. The vision of God is called, in the language of the scholars, *naʿīm al-aqṣā* — the furthest, highest bliss — greater than the gardens, the rivers, the companionship, the youth that never fades. The Prophet ﷺ recited the verse *"For those who do good is the best (ḥusnā) and more"* (10:26) and explained: *"the best is Paradise; and more is the vision of your Lord."* He wept at night, and when asked why, replied: *"I weep in longing for what I see of the intimacy (of the Hereafter)"* — and the Companions understood that the highest station of the prophet and the martyr, the abstinent and the patient, is all funneled toward that unveiling. Now the inverse logic completes itself: if the summit of reward is to *see*, the summit of punishment is to be *screened*. Al-Rāzī draws the conclusion the verse demands: the reprobate are not merely placed somewhere unpleasant; they are placed in the one condition their whole lives had prepared — for the rusted heart was a veil already, worn in the world, and the Hereafter merely makes its weave permanent. *"Whoever is blind in this world shall be blind in the Hereafter, and further astray from the path"* (17:72).

**The Veil Woven in the World: Sin as Self-Exile**

The commentators' phrase for this is exact: no one is veiled *against* his will by an arbitrary decree; the veil is *woven by the life*. Every skipped obligation, every jeered recitation, every skimmed measure added a thread; the Hereafter simply raises the finished curtain. Ibn Taymiyyah and the tradition after him loved to say: the punishment of the sinner is of the *same species* as his sin — the one who ignored God's signs is ignored by them; the one who veiled his heart from the Reminder is veiled from the Veiled-One's face. The verse thus converts a doctrine of the Hereafter into a manual for the present: the question "am I being veiled?" is answerable today, by inspection. Does the heart still *move* at the recitation? Does the mention of God still land like coolness on a burn? Is prayer still an audience or a transaction? These are the threads' colors, and the weaving is happening now. The Prophet ﷺ taught his community to dread exactly this distance above all distances, and to plead against it in the words he recited at every trial: *"O God, I seek refuge in Thee from Thy displeasure and from being veiled from Thee"* — for the Companions understood that a man can lose wealth and recover, lose health and be restored, but the Curtain, once drawn in that world, has no door in this one.

**The King's Curtain: Deprivation as the Deepest Sentence**

It remains to say why the Qur'an chooses *distance* as the climax of punishment, for the choice is a window into the nature of the human soul. A creature made for God — *"and I created jinn and mankind only that they worship Me"* (51:56) — carries in its core a hunger that no created thing stills. The damned may be given fire, and the fire is real; but the Qur'an teaches that beneath the fire's pain runs a deeper ache, the ache of exile — the condemned know *Whom* they are barred from, and the knowledge itself is coals. The commentators note that on that Day the deniers will call for destruction twice over — *"they will call therein for destruction: our Lord, remove us; we will do righteous deeds"* (25:12–15, 35:37) — not for comfort, but for escape from the knowledge of what their insolence cost. The Persian master Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī, whom the source commentary cites at this verse, put the metaphysics in a single couplet:

> *"Everyone sees the Unseen in proportion to the clarity of his heart. That depends upon how much he has polished it. Whoever has polished it more sees more — more Unseen forms become manifest to him."* (Mathnawī 4:2909–10)

Polish and vision, rust and veil: the sūrah has now stated the whole law. And the law reads, for the living, as both warning and map. The defrauders of the opening verses thought they were skimming *measures*; this verse discloses what they were actually skimming — their own capacity to see God. Every honest measure given, every truth heard without a sneer, every night of remembrance is thread removed from a curtain; and the end of that unweaving is the verse the believers live for: faces, radiant, gazing upon their Lord.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:16

> **Then they will burn in Hellfire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sequence of the Sentence: Veiled First, Then Consumed**

The verse opens with the ordering particle *thumma* — "then" — and the ordering is the doctrine. The sūrah has not said "they will burn in the Fire and therefore be veiled"; it says: first the veil, *then* the burning. Punishment begins as *distance*, and the fire follows as its completion. The sequence teaches the reader where the center of damnation lies: the primary catastrophe is separation from God; the flames are its instrument, not its essence. A reader who imagines Hell as merely a place of physical suffering has not yet heard this sūrah's teaching — the Fire is given to those who have already lost the one thing the soul cannot survive losing, and the burn is the external form of an inward exile already in progress. Classical commentators pause on this order to draw exactly that lesson: deprivation precedes consumption, because for a creature made *for* God, nothing consumed can compare to something withheld.

The verb of the burning is one of the Qur'an's great rarities: *la-ṣālū* — emphatically marked by the lām of assertion, from the root **ṣ-l-y**. The classical philologists gloss the root by its sound: it is the word for what water does when it is cast upon fire — the hissing, spitting *ṣalāl* of violent contact. The choice of verb is itself a description: the condemned enter the Fire the way water enters flame — with a noise, a struggle, and a total transformation of state. The noun for the Fire is *al-jaḥīm*, from **j-h-m**, whose core sense is *fierce, blazing heat* — the fire at its most ravenous. The emphatic lām plus the intensive verb plus the intensive noun gives the short verse the force of a door slamming: *thumma innahum la-ṣālū al-jaḥīm* — "Then — assuredly — they shall roast in the Blaze."

**"One Part of Seventy": The Fire Beyond the World's Fire**

Because the verse names a fire, the reader's imagination naturally reaches for the fire he knows — the campfire, the hearth, the burning building. The Prophet ﷺ anticipated that reach and corrected its scale. Abū Hurayrah narrated that the Messenger of God ﷺ said:

> *"This fire of yours, which the son of Adam kindles, is one part of seventy parts of the Fire of Hell."* They said: "By God, even that is sufficient, O Messenger of God!" He said: *"It is sixty-nine parts more, all of them like these — in heat."* (al-Bukhārī 3265; Muslim 2843)

The ḥadīth performs the same service the interrogative of v.8 performed: it declares the reality *unknowable in kind*, beyond every earthly scale, while still being real enough to warn against. The Companions' gasp — "even that is sufficient!" — and the Prophet's ﷺ calm multiplication — "sixty-nine parts more" — frame the doctrine with terrible sobriety: the fire of this world is a *sample*, the least of seventy, drawn from the same chemistry as the whole. The believer who has grasped the sample grasps the argument for avoiding the whole. And the ḥadīth's mathematics has a pastoral edge the Qur'an's verse shares: the threat is calibrated not to terrorize the weak into despair but to price the sins of the defiant accurately. The skimmed measure, the jeered verse, the refused sign — each was small; the Fire is not.

**Fire as Finale: The Architecture of the Two Escalations**

Set against the pious half of the sūrah, this verse's position reveals the chapter's deliberate architecture. The wicked cycle (vv. 7–17) escalates downward through three *thumma*-steps: the sealed book (7–9), the woe and denial (10–13), the rust (14), the veil (15), *then* the burning (16), *then* the taunt (17). Each step is worse than the last, and the worst — the confrontation with their own denial — is reserved for last, as the finishing blow. The blessed cycle (vv. 22–28) escalates *upward* through the same technique: bliss (22), couches and gazing (23), radiant faces (24), the sealed wine (25), the seal of musk and the call to strive (26), the blend of Tasnīm (27), the spring of the brought-nigh (28). The Qur'an thus writes the two destinies as two sequences — one climbing from record to loss, one climbing from record to abundance — and the reader stands between them, holding both escalators' rail. The choice of which *thumma* to ride is being made in the present tense, in the marketplace of the sūrah's first verses, and the escalation of either track begins with a measure given honestly or otherwise.

There is also a psychological reading of the fire's late arrival. The Qur'an does not lead with horror because horror *numbs*; it leads with truth — the file, the rust, the veil — truths that can be verified by self-inspection today. Only when the soul has understood *why* it is being lost does the verse spend its fire. The modern preacher of fear inverts this order and exhausts his audience; the Qur'an's order is diagnosis before sentence, evidence before execution — and the fire, arriving after the veil, arrives as the legal consequence of a case already fully proved.

**The Fire and the Veil in One Breath: What the Believer Should Carry**

For the believer, the verse's instruction is not to memorize a temperature but to understand a hierarchy. If the Qur'an places distance-from-God *before* the Fire, then the believer's daily prayer — his five appointments in the presence the deniers are barred from — is worth guarding above every comfort; and his fear of sin should be scaled accordingly: less fear of loss of things, more fear of the thickening veil. The Prophet ﷺ taught this priority in a prayer he bequeathed to his community: *"O God, I ask Thee for Thy love, and the love of those who love Thee, and the love of every deed that brings me near to Thy love"* (al-Tirmidhī 3490) — a prayer whose negative image is this verse: he who has no love is given distance, and after distance, the fire. *Thumma* — then. The verse is short; the pause it demands of the reader is not.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:17

> **Then it is said unto them, "This is that which you used to deny."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Taunt That Closes the Case: The Sentence Reads Its Own Indictment**

The wicked cycle of the sūrah ends not with a new torment but with a *sentence spoken aloud*: *thumma yuqālu hādhā alladhī kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūn* — "Then it is said to them: This is that which you used to deny." The formula is one of the Qur'an's recurring judicial closings. In Sūrah al-Ṭūr it appears over the Fire itself: *"This is the Fire which you used to deny"* (52:14). In al-Jāthiyah, over the transgressors: *"Has not My revelation been recited unto you? And you used to call them liars?"* (45:31). In al-Anʿām, over the assembled deniers before their Lord: *"Is this not the truth?" They say: "Yea, by our Lord!" He says: "Taste then the punishment, for that you disbelieved"* (6:30). In al-Ṣāffāt, over the companions of the Blaze: *"This is the Blaze which you used to deny"* (37:20–21). The pattern is consistent and deliberate: on that Day the condemned are not merely punished; they are *read to*. The file is opened, the denial is quoted in their own register, and the reality they rejected is set before their eyes with a pointing word — *hādhā*, "this" — the demonstrative of the thing present, visible, undeniable.

The unnamed announcer — the passive *yuqālu*, "it is said" — has drawn comment from the interpreters. Some read the speaker as the angels; some as the Command of God conveyed directly; al-Zamakhsharī notes that the passive leaves the sentence with the weight of the court itself rather than any particular officer. The effect is architectural: the taunt belongs to the Day the way the verdict belongs to the bench — voiceless in origin, absolute in authority. And its function is not sadism but *justice in form*: a sentence that did not cite its crime would be arbitrary; this sentence *quotes the crime verbatim*. The deniers spent their lives saying of the Hereafter "fables of the ancients" (v.13); the Day's first service is to return the accusation, with the Fire standing as exhibit A.

**Why the Reminder? The Theology of Confrontation**

Why does God insist that the condemned *acknowledge* what they denied? The Qur'an's answer, developed across its final pages, is that acknowledgment completes the justice and closes every appeal. The condemned are never punished in ignorance or on hearsay; they are punished *in the light of a confession extracted by reality itself*. Sūrah al-Sajdah preserves the scene in its fullest form:

> *"If thou couldst see when the guilty hang their heads before their Lord: Our Lord, now we have seen and heard; so return us, and we will do righteousness; we are now certain."* (32:12)

The certainty (*mūqinūn*) they could not reach through a lifetime of recitation arrives in a moment — and arrives *too late to be faith*, because faith, by definition, is the unseen trusted before the unveiling. Sūrah al-Anʿām adds the bitter footnote to their plea "return us":

> *"Hadst thou seen when they are set before their Fire and say: Would that we be returned, and we deny not the signs of our Lord, and be among the believers! — No! There appeared to them what they had been concealing before."* (6:27–28)

"What they had been concealing" — the verse locates the entire mechanism: the denial was never honest uncertainty; it was concealment wearing the costume of critique. The Day's announcement — "this is that which you used to deny" — is thus the unmasking, not the mocking. The tradition's scholars draw the sobering inference: the worst moment of that Day for the defiant is not the first touch of flame but the first syllable of that sentence, because in it they must hear their own life's thesis refuted by the world itself.

**The Full Circle of This Sūrah: From Skimmed Measures to a Quoted Denial**

Within al-Muṭaffifīn, the verse also ties the chapter's two halves together with terrible economy. The chapter opened with men who honored measures only when *receiving* and stinted when *giving*. Now the pattern returns at the cosmic scale: all their lives they *received* the signs — recited to them, v.13 tells us, with God's own pronoun of tenderness, "Our signs" — and they *stinted* on the only return the signs asked, acknowledgment. They were muṭaffifīn of revelation itself: taking the full delivery and giving back four words of scorn. The Day's quoted sentence is the receipt for that transaction. And the reversal that the coming verses promise — the believers who were denied now vindicated — is prepared by this closing of the wicked file: the court has established, on the record and by the defendants' own confession, who was truly astray.

**The Believer's Use of the Verse: Hearing the Sentence Today**

The practical yield of this verse is a discipline of anticipation. Every argument a man wins against his own conscience is, in reality, a sentence being drafted — and this verse lets the draftsman hear it read aloud in advance: *this is that which you used to deny.* The believer who meditates on the closing verses of the wicked cycle learns to ask, at the moment of each rationalization, a simple question: when the file is quoted on that Day, which of my words do I wish it to contain? The Companions are described as learning the Qur'an and acting on it *immediately* — believing before seeing — precisely because they understood that belief's whole value lies in the period before the unveiling, and that the sentence "this is that which you used to deny," spoken on the Day, is the one human sentence that can never be answered. Better to answer it now, in the dark, with the unseen still unseen: *I deny it no longer; I believe.* That answer, given in time, is the one thing the Fire cannot quote.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:18

> **Nay, truly the book of the pious is in ʿIlliyyūn.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third "Nay!" Turns the Page: From the Depths to the Heights**

The sūrah's last *kallā* is its most merciful. Twice the particle has slammed doors — against the illusion of the unrecorded deed (v.7), against the deniers' self-portrait as critics (v.14). Now it swings the whole architecture around: *kallā innā kitāba al-abrāri la-fī ʿilliyyīn* — "Nay, truly the book of the pious is in ʿIlliyyūn." The grammar is a deliberate mirror of v.7 — the same emphatic *kallā*, the same assertive *inna*, the same structure "truly the book of X is in Y" — set in the opposite direction. The Book weighs the two companies on identical grammar so that the reader will weigh them on identical honesty: the difference between the two destinies is not rhetoric, not pedigree, not luck — it is the content of two books and the direction two kinds of living travel. After nine verses in the depths, the Qur'an teaches by ascent. The commentators note that the particle here also rebukes the reader's despair: whatever the permanence of the wicked's file, *kallā* — the ledger of the righteous exists, is kept, and is kept *higher*.

**Al-Abrār: The Word for Wholesome Goodness**

The company named here is *al-abrār*, singular *barr*, from the root **b-r-r**, whose native range is *wholeness, soundness, dutiful devotion* — and, in the world of horses, the *barīd*: the mount that is wide-chested, smooth-gaited, and gentle of rein. The word thus carries a distinctive moral image: goodness as *integrity* rather than mere compliance — the whole animal moving as one, power accepting guidance. The Qur'an fills the word with content in its great definition verse, which the commentaries on this sūrah summon precisely here:

> *"It is not righteousness (birr) that you turn your faces to the East and the West; but righteousness is he who believes in God and the Last Day and the angels and the Book and the prophets; and gives wealth, for love of Him, to kin and orphans and the needy and the wayfarer and those who ask, and for the freeing of slaves; and performs the prayer, and gives the alms; and those who fulfill their covenant when they covenant, and the patient in hardship and affliction and in time of violence: those are the sincere, and those are the God-fearing."* (2:177)

Notice what the definition contains: creed (belief in God, the Last Day, angels, Book, prophets) and *the entire social ethics of money* — giving to kin, orphans, the needy, the wayfarer; fulfilling covenants; patience under loss. The abrār of this sūrah are, by that definition, the very opposite of the muṭaffifīn who opened it: those gave short to others; these give *full* to others, from love of God. And the Prophet ﷺ compressed the whole word into the practical: *"Righteousness is that with which the soul is at rest and the heart is at rest, and unrighteousness is that which wavers in the soul and moves to and fro in the breast"* (Muslim 2553) — and, in another narration of the same sitting: *"Righteousness is good character."* The abrār, in sum, are those whose inward and outward have been joined into one wholesome whole — whose measures, words, and covenants run to the rim.

**ʿIlliyyūn: The Name of the Height**

Then the name: *ʿIlliyyūn* — the downward pole of Sijjīn answered by an upward pole. The word derives from **ʿ-l-w**, the root of height, elevation, and dominance (*ʿulūw*, to be high; *muʿtali*, lofty; the *ʿāliyāt* of the Qur'an are the ships that "sail aloft," 55:24). Grammatically it is an unusual plural, and the philologists debated its singular — the source preserves the range: it indicates *high places*, or *persons seated in high places*, or *dwellers in the heights of mountains*; here it denotes a register exalted by many degrees. The commentators then locate it with the caution the subject demands. Al-Qurṭubī gathers the opinions: ʿIlliyyūn is *beneath the Throne in the seventh heaven*; or *beyond the seven heavens, to the right of the Throne*; or it signifies *height without limit*, beyond location; and some connect it to the *Lote Tree of the Boundary* at which the Prophet's ﷺ night journey ended — *"near the Lote Tree of the Boundary"* (53:14). The Shiite tradition preserves a matching symmetry from the fifth Imam, Muḥammad al-Bāqir:

> *"Sijjīn is the seventh earth, and ʿIlliyyūn is the seventh heaven."*

The disagreement of the schools is itself an instruction: the exact floor plan is not vouchsafed, and the Qur'an's own restraint (as v.19 will make explicit) forbids forcing the map. What the texts *do* establish is the axis: the book of the pious is kept at the highest point of the created order, as far *toward* God as creation reaches — the precise vertical opposite of the lowest of the seven earths. Deeds, the Qur'an teaches, have geography. The tradition even describes the shipping: *"Deeds are presented (to God) on Monday and Thursday, and God forgives every believer..."* (recorded in the Musnad of Aḥmad and graded sound) — the weekly ascent of the ledgers, in the Prophet's ﷺ telling, is part of the order of the world.

**The Book in the Height: Honor in the Custody**

Why does the location matter? Because in the Qur'an's world, custody is honor. The profligate's book lies *below* all things — filed in the dark, with the sealed finality of a prison registry. The pious book lies *above* all things — and the next verses will add that it is *witnessed by those brought nigh* (v.21), meaning the highest angels and the foremost of the righteous themselves see it, honor it, rejoice in it. A man's work, the sūrah teaches, acquires the address of its quality: the deed done in secret for God is not buried in secret; it is *promoted* in secret. This is the stunning inversion of worldly bookkeeping, where hidden good deeds are lost capital. In the divine economy they are the only capital that appreciates — moved upward at every audit, until they rest where the eyes of the highest company can see them.

The sūrah's own cross-reference clinches the point. Of the man who receives his book in his right hand, the Qur'an says: *"So he will be in a pleasing life, in a lofty Garden (jannatin ʿāliyah)"* (69:19–22) — and *ʿāliyah* is from the very same root as *ʿIlliyyūn*: the man whose book was kept in the Height is housed in a garden named Highness. Book and home are one direction of travel. The believer who grasps this stops asking whether his unseen good deeds "count" and starts asking where they are being *stored* — for the verse answers: above the seventh heaven, in bold and beautiful script, at the address from which the rewards of the coming verses will be drawn.

**The Symmetry That Preaches: Standing Between Two Books**

Finally, the verse completes the chapter's most powerful teaching device: the double ledger laid open before the reader. Two books (7/18); two names (Sijjīn/ʿIlliyyūn); two questions (8/19); two inscribed records (9/20); two destinies unwinding in matched steps toward the sūrah's end. The structure itself is the sermon: *both files are being written about you.* Every reader is, this hour, an entry in one of the two registers — and the grammar's cold symmetry announces that heaven will not blur the distinction at the audit. The mercy is that the writing is still in progress. The rusted heart can be polished; the short measure can be repaid; the sneer can become a salām. The book in ʿIlliyyūn has room, and its keeper is *"the best of writers."* The sūrah's third *kallā* is thus, for the honest reader, less a verdict than an invitation: the heights have been named; the way up has been priced — in honest measures, kept covenants, and a heart kept soft — and the ascent, unlike every worldly ascent, is open in the very hour the sūrah is being recited.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19

> **And what will apprise thee of ʿIlliyyūn?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Twin of the Question: Awe at the Other Pole**

Eleven verses after the downward question — *"And what will apprise thee of Sijjīn?"* (83:8) — the Qur'an asks its twin: *wa-mā adrāka mā ʿilliyyūn* — "And what will apprise thee of ʿIlliyyūn?" The same interrogative formula from the same root (**d-r-y**, to bring to real knowledge), the same confession built into the grammar: *nothing in your world can bring you to truly know this.* But the direction of the awe has reversed. The first question asked, in effect, how terrible the depths are; this one asks, in the same breath, how magnificent the heights are. The symmetry is the doctrine: the unseen has two poles, and the believer's required knowledge of both is of the same kind — *certain in existence, humble about the details*. He must be as sure that ʿIlliyyūn is as he is that Sijjīn is; and he must be as unable to picture either as the other. Faith, in the Qur'an's epistemology, is not the replacement of wonder with information; it is the discipline of *acting correctly under confessed mystery* — and the mystery now standing before the reader is bliss, not horror.

Notice, with the commentators, what this pairing does to the reader's heart. Fear and longing are the two wings of worship in the Qur'anic curriculum, and the paired questions assign each wing its own altar. A sermon that described only Sijjīn would breed a grim, flight-ridden religiosity; the Qur'an therefore asks the ʿIlliyyūn question with identical force, telling the believer: your *desire* for the heights is meant to be as educated, as vivid, and as operative as your dread of the depths. The Prophet ﷺ embodied the balance; his supplications interlaced the two — *"I seek refuge in Thee from the evil of what I have done and from the evil of what I have not done"* beside *"I ask Thee for the delight of gazing upon Thy face and the longing to meet Thee"* (al-Tirmidhī 3235) — until the community learned that the man who wants nothing from the heights has misread the first question as thoroughly as the man who fears nothing from the depths.

**Why the Highest Things Are Hardest to Tell**

There is a deeper reason — articulated by the tradition and felt by every honest reader — why the summit of bliss is introduced by an admitted inability to describe. Language is built from worldly furniture: every word the reader owns was minted to handle grain, faces, rivers, and cups. The Hereafter's currencies are of another mintage, and revelation does the best that speech can do: it *names* (ʿIlliyyūn, Tasnīm, al-Kawthar) and *points* (couches, radiance, sealed wine), while confessing at each summit that the pointers are pointers. The Qur'an itself draws the veil over its own descriptions: *"No soul knows what comfort of the eyes is kept hidden for them — a recompense for what they used to do"* (32:17). And the Prophet ﷺ transmitted the divine voice over the whole subject:

> *God said: "I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no heart of man has conceived."* (al-Bukhārī 4779; Muslim 2824)

The ḥadīth is the charter of the verse's method: the Qur'an's gardens and rivers and cups are true — but they are *translations* into our vocabulary of realities for which our vocabulary was never designed. The translators of bliss chose the most delectable words the Arabs knew and then, at the edge of translation, asked this question: *and what will appraise thee of it?* — that is: do not mistake the translation for the territory.

**The Rhetoric of Desire: How a Question Inflames Longing**

The rhetorical mechanics deserve notice, because they show revelation *working on the soul* rather than merely informing it. A description can be browsed and shelved; a question must be carried. "What will apprise thee of ʿIlliyyūn?" lodges in the mind as an open account — the reader walks away trying to answer it, enlarging it, furnishing it with every beautiful thing he knows and then confessing each furnishing inadequate. The Qur'an deploys this device at both poles — the terrors (the Crusher, the Ākhirah, the Scorcher) and the delights (the Night of Decree, the Day of Judgment, ʿIlliyyūn) — and the paired questions of this very sūrah show the device calibrated like a scale: equal weights of awe, hung at equal distances, with the reader's heart as the beam. Al-Rāzī, commenting on such questions, observes that God teaches His servants humility about the intellect's reach *at the very moments the intellect most wants to rule* — and that this humility is not anti-intellectual but protective: it keeps the worshipper a worshipper, a creature at the foot of wonders rather than a tourist with a completed guidebook.

**A Name Given to Longing: The Practical Yield**

For the believer's daily life, the verse yields a discipline that Islam's spiritual masters made central: *cultivated aspiration* (irādah, longing for the highest). The Prophet ﷺ taught that God says: *"I am as My servant expects of Me"* (al-Bukhārī 7405; Muslim 2675) — expectation, ḥusn al-ẓann, is itself an act of worship, and the highest expectation a creature may lawfully hold is the expectation of the heights this verse names. The companions of the Prophet ﷺ dramatized the lesson: ʿUmar, seeing a man absorbed in longing, asked what he wanted, and the man answered that he wished for the garden and its springs; ʿUmar did not rebuke him but prayed that God grant it — for the early community understood that longing for ʿIlliyyūn, kept honest by effort, is the fuel of every difficult virtue in this sūrah: the full measure given when cheating would be easier, the truth spoken when the sneer is cheaper, the remembrance kept when the heart grows damp and dull. *And what will apprise thee of ʿIlliyyūn?* — the question stands unanswered by design, so that the believer will spend his life paying toward an answer no speech can give and no death can withhold from the sincere.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20

> **A book inscribed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Seal, the Other Direction: An Exact Phrase, an Opposite Fate**

The verse repeats, word for word, the phrase that sealed the profligates' file: *kitābun marqūm* — "a book inscribed." The Qur'an rarely repeats a phrase with such exactness, and when it does, the repetition is the lesson. The book of the pious enjoys every formal property the book of the profligate enjoyed: it is a *kitāb* — a definitive register, not a heap of memories; and it is *marqūm* — written in bold, decisive script, sealed against decrease, increase, and erasure (as Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī read the word at v.9). The two ledgers are identical in *form* and opposite in *fortune*. The permanence that terrifies in Sijjīn dignifies in ʿIlliyyūn. What was above described as a prison registry is here a patent of nobility: the same archival rigor, the same seal, the same bold hand — and the whole difference lies in whose deeds were copied into it. The Qur'an's implicit counsel is exact: the *permanence of the record is not your enemy.* Deeds are not lost in either direction; the question this verse poses is merely which direction the reader's entries are being carried — and permanence, which damns the dishonest, is the honest man's wealth. Every forgotten kindness, every unthanked hour, every secret charity survives in the one archive that cannot corrupt.

The commentators add a subtle disagreement that enriches the picture. Most hold — al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarṣī, al-Zamakhsharī among them — that the verse describes the *book of the righteous* (as v.9 described the book of the profligate), the adjective of v.9 being applied to its opposite number. Others read it as describing *ʿIlliyyūn itself*: on this reading *kitāb* would mean *kitābah*, "the writing" — the writing inscribed, i.e., the record of the pious *is* ʿIlliyyūn's inscription; their deeds are not merely stored at the height; they constitute part of the height's very text (al-Rāzī records this). Either way the practical import is the same, and the second reading states it more boldly still: the righteous do not simply *send* their deeds up; their deeds become *architecture* in the upper world — the height is written with them.

**Permanence as Promise: What the Sealed Record Gives the Believer**

The verse's theology of documentation repays slow reading. In the world below, the honest man's greatest fear is that his goodness is *unrecorded* — that the kindness no one saw, the wage never claimed, the wrong never answered have simply evaporated. *Kitābun marqūm* is the standing refutation of that fear. The Qur'an insists at every audit-scene that nothing is lost: *"And they find what they did present before them"* (18:49); *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it"* (99:7); *"We were recording what you used to do"* (45:29). The sūrah's own chronology now rings with promise: the defrauded of its opening verses, whose losses were too small for any court, have their losses *and their patience* inscribed at the height of heaven. The seal that closed the wicked file against appeal closes the righteous file against *loss*. In both cases the message is the mercy: the universe is documented; nobody's truth falls through the floor.

**Bold Writing, Honored Custody: The Dignity of the Righteous Archive**

One more nuance completes the verse. Recall that *marqūm*, by the lexicographers' account, denotes *large, bold* writing — the script of proclamation, not the cramped hand of a back-room ledger. The pious record, in other words, is written the way kings write proclamations. There is no anonymity in the divine archive: the deed done for God in a hidden room is transcribed *upward* in letters meant to be seen — and the next verse will say by whom they are seen: the brought-nigh themselves, the highest company of heaven. The world's pattern is inversion: deeds done for display are hidden by death, and deeds hidden for God are displayed by eternity. The believer is thereby taught where to spend his display: the audience that matters is assembling above the seventh heaven, and it is already reading.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:21

> **witnessed by those brought nigh.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Book with an Audience: The Verb of Being Seen**

The pious book's final attribute is relational: *yashhaduhu al-muqarrabūn* — "witnessed by those brought nigh." The verb *yashhadu*, from **sh-h-d**, the root of presence and testimony, means to *be present at* and therefore to *attest*: the book does not merely exist at the height; it is *attended*. Heaven's archive is not a sealed vault into which deeds vanish; it is a chamber of ongoing audience, where the most exalted of creation read the records of the faithful the way a court reads its honored precedents. The contrast with Sijjīn completes itself in a single participle: the wicked file is sealed in the dark of the lowest earth, with no visitor named; the righteous file is *public* in the best sense — displayed before the highest spectators in existence. Worldly honor is a crowd's attention for a season; the Qur'an here describes an attention that never tires, from an audience that cannot die.

**Al-Muqarrabūn: Who Are the Brought-Nigh?**

The commentators preserve a fruitful disagreement about *al-muqarrabūn*, "those brought nigh" (from **q-r-b**, the root of nearness — *qurba*, *qurbah*, the drawing close that worship seeks). The first reading: they are *the archangels* — the term appears in just this sense in the Qur'an: *"They (the angels) say: We each have a known station... Not one of us but has a known place; we are they arrayed in ranks... those brought nigh"* (37:164–166; cf. 4:172). The angels nearest the Throne — Gabriel and Michael among them — witness the righteous records as their standing duty. The second reading: they are *the elect among the pious themselves* — the *sābiqūn*, "the foremost," of whom the Qur'an says: *"And the foremost, the foremost! Those are they brought nigh"* (56:10–11). On this reading, the greatest of the righteous read the books of the righteous: the company of prophets and truthful ones and martyrs beholds, celebrates, and attests the incoming registers of the faithful. And a third view, which the source records, joins the two: *both* the archangels and the foremost are "brought nigh," and both witness. The disagreement is a generous one — every candidate for the verse's audience is an audience of honor — and its very existence teaches the believer the sociology of the Hereafter: nearness to God creates a community of mutual beholding, in which the highest are the most attentive to the least.

**Why Witnessing Is Honor: The Registry Read Aloud**

Why does the Qur'an report *who sees* the pious book? Because custody by the mighty is dignity. A deed entered in a register that archangels attend is not a dead entry; it is a document in active circulation among the finest of creatures — and the tradition elsewhere hints at the living interest the higher world takes in the lower: the Qur'an itself describes the angels praying for the believers in assembly (*"those who bear the Throne, and those around it, celebrate the praise of their Lord and believe in Him, and ask forgiveness for those who believe"* 40:7). The witnessing of v.21 is that care, formalized: the record of the pious is read in the highest chambers not as paperwork but as *good news*. And there is comfort in the mechanics for every unsung servant of God: the nurse's unseen night, the merchant's quiet honesty, the convert's lonely prayer — each is read aloud, as it were, in the one court whose applause endures. The verse quietly relocates the believer's sense of audience: he works no longer for the room he can see, but for the gallery he cannot.

**The Foremost Watching the Faithful: Paradise Without Envy**

On the second reading, a further beauty opens. The foremost of the righteous — those who raced ahead in every generation — *witness* the incoming books of ordinary believers, and their witnessing is an act of rejoicing, not rivalry. In the world, excellence breeds envy; excellence is hoarded as advantage. In the height, the sūrah teaches, excellence is a *vantage*: the nearest are the most eager to see others honored. This is the social physics of Paradise that later verses will confirm — the companions of the Garden calling across to the companions of the Fire in sincere testimony (7:44), the dwellers blessed *"no vain speech and no lying"* (78:35) — a community whose members' success is each other's joy. The believer who cultivates that physics now — learning to love another's excellence rather than audit it — is already practicing the grammar of ʿIlliyyūn, where the books are witnessed by those brought nigh, and the brought-nigh are made glad.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:22

> **Truly the pious shall be in bliss,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Ledger to the Life: The Book Becomes a Dwelling**

With a single particle of emphasis the sūrah crosses from archives to apartments: *inna al-abrāra la-fī naʿīm* — "Truly the pious shall be in bliss." The emphatic *inna... la-* construction — "truly, assuredly" — stamps the sentence the way a registrar stamps a deed: this is not poetry's hope but record's certainty. And the transition is the teaching: the book of the pious (vv. 18–21) was kept in ʿIlliyyūn *because* its owners are *in* bliss (v.22); the record and the reward are one continuum. The verse thereby answers, in advance, the skeptic's sneer that the Hereafter is a myth of compensation: the Qur'an presents reward not as a consolation prize distributed after the fact but as the *destination of a journey already documented* — the file in the heights and the seat in the gardens being the same thing seen from two sides.

The word for bliss, *naʿīm*, is chosen with care. Its root, **n-ʿ-m**, denotes *softness, ease, repose* — the *naʿamāʾ* are camels at pasture, sleek and at rest on soft ground; *naʿīm* is the condition of a creature wholly at ease, every need met, nothing chafing. The Qur'an uses the word and its garden-compound *jannāt al-naʿīm* — "Gardens of bliss" — as the standing address of the saved: *"those who believe and do righteous deeds — they are the inhabitants of the Garden; they shall abide therein"* (18:107); *"on couches facing one another"* they pass a cup that brings *"no headiness nor are they intoxicated"* (37:43–47); the faithful are brought *"in companies unto the Garden of bliss"* (22:56). The commentators enumerate the occurrences across the Book (5:65; 10:9; 22:56; 37:43; 52:17; 56:12, and the companion-forms at 9:21; 26:85; 31:8; 56:89; 68:34) and draw the obvious: *naʿīm* is the Qur'an's most repeated single word for the saved state — bliss not as an event but as a *climate*, the total restoration of ease to a being that spent its worldly life chafing.

**The Twin Sūrah's Promise Fulfilled**

The verse is also the second half of a contract signed in the immediately preceding sūrah. Al-Infiṭār had compressed the two destinies into one couplet — *"Truly the pious shall be in bliss, and truly the profligate shall be in the Blaze"* (82:13–14) — and al-Muṭaffifīn now fulfills the couplet clause by clause: *fī naʿīm* here, *fī jaḥīm* at v.16, with fourteen verses of detail hung on each. The recurrence of *al-abrār* in both sūrahs marks the same company: the wholesome, the whole, the dutiful — defined, as we saw at v.18, by 2:177's fusion of creed and social mercy. The structural echo teaches a reading habit for the whole Qur'an: its short sūrahs are panels of one triptych, and the promises they trade back and forth are meant to be *collected*. The believer who recites these chapters in prayer holds, in the space of ten minutes, the entire moral map of the final audit.

**Prison Now, Bliss Then: The Reversal the Verse Guarantees**

Why does the Qur'an stake so much emphasis on the *certainty* of this bliss? Because the abrār's worldly resume rarely advertises it. The verse follows fourteen verses of the wicked prospering — merchants who skimmed and thrived, deniers who mocked and feasted — and its emphatic grammar is the answer to the honest man's darkest hour: the moment when piety looks like a losing trade. The Prophet ﷺ located the reversal in one of his most quoted sayings: *"The world is the believer's prison and the disbeliever's paradise"* (Muslim 2956) — the believer feels the world's constraints precisely because he is traveling; the dissolute feel its freedoms precisely because they are unguarded. Bilāl the tortured caller to prayer, ʿAmmār with his parents slain, Khabbāb with his branded back — the abrār of the first generation knew the prison from inside. This verse is their written parole: *inna al-abrāra la-fī naʿīm* — the tense future, the double emphasis, the state permanent. The commentators' note on the verse's timing applies to every age of persecution: the bliss is not *despite* the prison years; in the Qur'an's accounting the patience of the prison years is itself the currency the bliss purchases.

**Wholeness Now, Bliss Then: The Logic of the Reward**

One last thread ties the verse to the sūrah's opening. The abrār are the *whole* — those who did not maintain two measures, two faces, two ledgers; who gave the full weight and kept the soft heart in the same hour. And *naʿīm* is the state of the creature with *nothing chafing* — no gap between what he is and what he was made for, no rust, no veil, no unresolved account. The reward is thus of the same species as the deed, as the Qur'an's justice requires: the defrauder's punishment is distance, because he defrauded the ties that bind; the wholesome's reward is ease, because he lived at ease with every right-holder around him. The verse's practical imperative follows without a sermon: wholeness is not a temperament some are born with; it is a *policy* — one measure for self and other, one face for mosque and market, one ledger kept open for audit. Policy becomes climate; climate, on that Day, becomes address: *in bliss.*

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:23

> **upon couches, gazing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Posture of the Finished: Couches in the Qur'an's Paradise**

The verse opens the sūrah's gallery of delights with a posture: *ʿalā al-arāʾiki yanzurūn* — "upon couches, gazing." The *arāʾik* (singular *arīkah*) are the padded, canopied couches of honor on which the Arabs received their most cherished guests — the furniture of the feast, not the chair of the office. The Qur'an seats its righteous upon them repeatedly: *"reclining upon couches ranged in ranks"* (56:15–16); *"upon inlaid couches, reclining upon them, facing one another"* (56:15–16); *"therein couches raised high and cups set"* (88:13–14); *"upon golden couches they will be reclined"* (37:44). The commentators read the posture in two registers, and both are intended. Physically, the couch is rest absolute — the end of every labor, journey, and vigil that the abrār's faithfulness cost them; the body that prayed on hard floors is finally *seated* by its Lord. Spiritually — as the source commentary notes — the couch figures the state of the soul *at peace*, the *nafs muṭmaʾinnah* of Sūrah al-Fajr: *"O thou soul at peace, return unto thy Lord, well-pleased and well-pleasing"* (89:27–28). One cannot recline in the presence of an unsettled account; the posture itself is the receipt. The gazing — *yanẓurūn* — is then the only activity left: no agenda, no anxiety, nothing to fix. The verse paints blessedness as *attentive ease*: eyes open, body at rest, heart unguarded for the first time since birth.

**Gazing at What? The Two Classical Readings**

At what do they gaze? Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarṣī preserve the discussion. The first reading: at *the delights around them* — the gardens, the rivers, the service, the faces of their companions: paradise enjoyed with the unhurried attention of those who have forever. The second reading, which the great commentators favor and the ḥadīth supports: at *God Himself* — the verse as a sister of Sūrah al-Qiyāmah's: *"Some faces that Day shall be radiant, gazing upon their Lord"* (75:22–23). We have already heard (at v.15) the doctrine and its proof-texts: the Prophet's ﷺ moon-ḥadīth — *"You will see your Lord as you see this moon — you suffer nothing in the seeing"* (al-Bukhārī 554; Muslim 181) — and the finale of the Paradisal feast, when, every delight exhausted, God unveils: *"and they will not have been given anything more beloved to them than their gazing upon Him"* (Muslim 181's fuller narration). On this reading, the verse's two words hold the entire summit of Islam's promise: seated — because justified; gazing — because *unveiled*. The abrār's eternal occupation, beyond the gardens and the rivers, is the Face of the One who kept their file on high.

A third reading is recorded in the commentaries — that the pious gaze upon *the punishment of their enemies* (alluding to the mockery-vv. 29–36) — but the source commentary rightly notes, with al-Qurṭubī, that this sits ill with the Prophetic descriptions, in which the blessed are given *removal of rancor* rather than ringside seats of vengeance: *"And We shall remove whatever rancor is in their breasts; as brothers, upon couches facing one another"* (15:47). The blessed gaze, in the Qur'an's own economy, is an eye *fed* by beauty and by the Face of God — not by the agony of others. The rejected reading is itself a lesson: even our imaginations of reward must submit to revelation's ethics.

**Gazing as the End of All Striving: The Sūrah's Arc Completed Here**

Set within al-Muṭaffifīn, the verse performs a quiet miracle of proportion. The chapter opened with men bent over measures — thumbs on pans, eyes on margins, every glance a calculation. It now seats its righteous in the one posture beyond calculation: *gazing* — seeing without taking, possessing nothing because possessing everything, the eye at last free of the market's squint. The commentators loved this symmetry: the skimmer's eye contracted by greed; the abrār's eye dilated by glory. And between the two states run all the verses of this chapter — the honest measure, the polished heart, the inscribed book — each one, the sūrah implies, a length of road from the squint to the gaze. The practical instruction is almost liturgical: train the eye now. The Prophet ﷺ tied the sight of God to the discipline of sight in the world, instructing ʿAlī: *"Do not follow one glance with another; the first is for you, the second is against you"* (al-Tirmidhī 2701) — for the eye that has learned obedience in the marketplace and the street is being trained, this verse promises, for the only view that never ends.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:24

> **Thou dost recognize in their faces the splendor of bliss.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Face as Readable Text: The Grammar of Radiance**

The verse does something intimate: it hands the reader a skill. *Taʿrifu fī wujūhihim naḍrata al-naʿīm* — "Thou dost recognize in their faces the splendor of bliss." The verb *taʿrifu* — "you recognize" — assumes that blessedness, far from being invisible, is *legible*; and the word it chooses for what is read, *naḍrah* (from **n-ḍ-r**, the root of freshness, verdure, and flourishing), is the Arabs' word for the moist brightness that comes over a face at the height of joy — the sheen of new leaves after rain, of a face at a homecoming. Bliss, in this verse, has a *complexion*. And the Qur'an's grammar of address — "thou" — can be read, as the source commentary notes, in two ways: as an address to any reader or listener whatsoever (whoever beholds them will see it), or as a specific address to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, the eyewitness of that Day; a third school recites the verse passively — "the splendor of bliss can be recognized in their faces" — making the legibility absolute (al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī record the readings). All three converge on the same fact: in the Hereafter, the interior will be worn on the surface, and the abrār's surface will be wearably, visibly *green with joy*.

**Radiance in the Narrations: The Faces Like the Full Moon**

The tradition supplies the verse's portraiture. In the long ḥadīth of the gathering, the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"You will be gathered barefoot, naked, uncircumcised... Then the people of Paradise will be brought, their faces bright as the moon on the night it is full."* (al-Bukhārī 3330)

—and he explained the *seal* verse of the Qur'an's other promise by the same image: *"For those who do good is the best and more" (10:26): the best is Paradise, and more is that they are given the vision of their Lord, and that is the radiance (al-naḍrah) upon their faces"* — so that the very word of this verse, *naḍrah*, was defined by the Prophet ﷺ as the visible residue of the Vision. The commentators joined the two verses into one doctrine: the face shines in the Hereafter because the eye it sits behind has seen God. The believers' faces will be lamps lit from within.

The first generation needed no eschatology to test the principle; they had watched it operate in miniature. The Companions describing the Prophet ﷺ could not exhaust his description — *"his face, as if the sun had burst forth in it"* — and the early Muslims noted that worship writes itself on the face: the Qur'an says of the Prophet's community, *"their mark is upon their faces from the trace of prostration"* (48:29), and the scholars of the inner life counted the *nūr* of wudūʾ, the softness of the frequent faster, the collectedness of the night-pray-er, among the observable phenomena of the path. The verse merely completes the scale: if a night's sincerity shows by morning, what will eternity's show?

**Inner States Are Legible: The Psychology of the Face**

There is also a truth of ordinary human experience beneath the verse, and the commentators were not too reverent to note it. The face is the body's public bulletin: sleeplessness, guilt, peace, and joy all *print* there, whether we consent or not. Modern students of the face describe the same transparency — micro-expressions betraying what the mouth conceals; the "authentic smile" involving muscles that cannot be hired. The Qur'an's verse stands at the end of that continuum: in the world, faces print the weather of the soul through clouds; in the Hereafter, the clouds are removed and the printing is total. The wicked of this sūrah wore their lives on their faces too — the coming verses will show the mockers' circuit of winks and smirks — and the abrār wear naʿīm the way the world wears dust. The practical inference is the Sufis' oldest observation: *polish the heart, and the face needs no cosmetics.* A man cannot finally hide from his own surface; he can only choose, day by day, what is being printed there.

**Read Today: The Verse as an Invitation to Become Legible**

For the believer, the verse converts eschatology into a daily aesthetic. It asks: *what would a face lit by naʿīm be doing this afternoon?* — and then supplies the activities that print that particular light: the prayer said with presence (48:29's trace), the charity given in secret, the measure given full when no one is checking, the tongue that remembered rather than mocked. The sūrah's two worlds thus meet in a single mirror: the defrauder's art was to keep his surface honest while his depths rotted; the abrār's reward is a surface that can no longer conceal a depths of joy. Between the two stands every reader, at his mirror, every morning — already, in miniature, readable; and being invited, by this verse, to become legible in the only script that lasts: the splendor of bliss.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25

> **They are given to drink of pure wine sealed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb of Being Served: Honor in a Cup**

The verse moves from the face to the table, and its first word is a passive of tenderness: *yusqawna* — "they are *given to drink*" — not "they drink." In the desert culture into which the Qur'an spoke, nothing honored a guest like the act of the host *making him drink*: the cup carried to his hand was the certificate of welcome, and to be *made* to drink was to be *made much of*. The Qur'an keeps that grammar throughout its Paradise: *"their Lord shall give them to drink a drink most pure"* (76:21); *"cups from a flowing spring"* (37:45); *"a cup from a spring"* (76:5–6). The abrār are not guests who fetch; they are guests *served* — and the Host is God. The entire verse is thus an act of divine hospitality: the sūrah that opened with men defrauding each other's measures closes its blessed half with God Himself pouring what cannot be defrauded.

**Raḥīq Makhtūm: The Vocabulary of a Perfect Vintage**

The drink is named with two adjectives of precision. *Raḥīq* denotes the purest extract of wine — the lexicographers define it as wine that is *āthar*, aged, fully matured, its impurities long settled: in the vocabulary of the vine, the vintage of vintages. *Makhtūm* — "sealed" — is the wine-trade's word of guarantee: in the ancient world, fine wine traveled in amphorae closed with a stopper and stamped with a seal attesting its origin, its vintage, and its untouched contents; a *sealed* jar was a *certified* jar, unopened since the Maker's own hand closed it. The Qur'an had already shown the drink's properties in the two sūrahs that flank this one's family: *"a cup from a spring, white, delicious to those who drink — no headiness in it, nor are they intoxicated by it"* (37:45–47); *"cups... wherefrom they suffer no headache nor stupefaction"* (56:18–19). And al-Rāzī records the opinion that this *raḥīq makhtūm* is superior even to the *rivers* of wine that flow in the Garden — the standing rivers of 47:15 — because rivers, however sweet, are common currency, while the sealed draught is the *reserve*, brought out for the honored. The commentators' scale of Paradisal drinks — springs below, rivers above, the sealed extract above all — tells the reader that Paradise has an interior aristocracy of taste, every level true, the heights being heights.

**Why Wine Is Forbidden Below and Served Above: The Logic of the License**

No pairing of verses in the Qur'an invites the question this one invites: the substance *khamr* is among the gravest of prohibitions on earth — *"O believers, wine and games of chance, idols and divining arrows are an abomination of Satan's handiwork; so avoid it, that you may prosper"* (5:90–91) — and yet the saved are *served wine in Paradise*, and served the best. The resolution lies in what the prohibition actually targets. What God forbade below was never the juice of the grape as such; it was the *chemistry of the fall*: intoxication that dissolves the reason God honored, prayer missed with slurred tongue, quarrels brewed in cups, wealth squandered and families emptied (5:91 lists exactly these: enmity and hatred, and turning from God's remembrance and from prayer). Paradise's wine is the same creature with the fall *removed*: *"no headiness in it"* (37:47) — no *ghawl*, no intoxicating froth; *"no headache nor stupefaction"* (56:19) — no hangover of the body or the mind. The drink of the Garden is thus the wine as God *meant* it, tasted by servants whose reason is now permanent, whose prayer is now unending, whose enmity is now removed. The lesson beneath the license is one of the Qur'an's deepest: **things are not unholy; conditions make them so.** The same hand that forbids a thing in its corrupting context serves it in its perfected one — and the believer learns to love the Giver above every gift, prohibited or promised.

The tradition confirms the wine's licit home. The Prophet ﷺ said of the great rivers: *"Sayḥān, Jayḥān, the Euphrates, and the Nile — all are of the rivers of Paradise"* (al-Bukhārī 3250) — the geography of the Garden leaking, as it were, into the maps of this world; and Sūrah Muḥammad describes its wine-rivers *"delicious to the drinkers"* (47:15) as parts of the same estate of which the *raḥīq makhtūm* is the crown.

**Sealed: The Drink No One Has Skimmed**

And here the verse quietly closes the circle the sūrah opened. The chapter's villains were men who *adulterated* — who left the buyer's measure short at the rim, who buried the wet grain at the bottom of the heap. The abrār's cup is the perfect *anti-adulteration*: sealed by its Maker, opened for its owners, guaranteed unskimmed from vine to hand. Nothing has been borrowed from it, watered in it, or withheld from it; the seal itself is the signature that says *full measure*. The commentators who note that the seal is a mark of quality and untouchedness were feeling the sūrah's own logic: in a world of short weights, God honors the honest with the one drink whose fullness is certified by the seal of the King. The believer reading v.25 after v.1 hears the sentence complete itself: *woe to those who skim the measures of men — and for those who gave in full: a cup that no hand has ever skimmed, poured by the Hand that never will.*

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26

> **whose seal is musk—so for that let the strivers strive—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**What the Bottom of the Cup Reveals: The Seal of Musk**

The verse descends, in the vocabulary of the wine-cellar, to the last thing tasted: *khitāmuhū misk* — "its seal is musk." The commentators transmit two readings of the detail. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Ṭabarṣī hold that the vessel is *literally closed with a seal of musk* — the stopper itself carved of the perfume, so that the luxury begins before the first sip. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī also record the second reading: that the *khitām* is the drink's *end* — and where the wines of this world end in bitterness, dregs, and headache, the wine of the Garden *ends in musk*: the final taste upon the lips is perfume. The choice between the readings is a choice of metaphor rather than doctrine, and both carry the same payload: in God's hospitality, the best is kept for the last. This is the inversion of every worldly trajectory, which begins with sweetness and ends in consequence. The Prophet ﷺ sketched the two trajectories in the famous parable: *"The example of the good companion and the evil companion is that of the seller of musk and the bellows-smith: the musk-seller will give you some, or you will buy from him, or at least you will come away with a good scent from him; and the bellows-smith will burn your clothes, or you will come away with his stench"* (al-Bukhārī 2101; Muslim 2628). Musk is thus, in the Prophetic vocabulary, the smell of *company kept with what is pure* — and the sealed wine of the abrār is, at its deepest level, the fragrance of a life spent in the company of God.

**"So for That Let the Strivers Strive": The Only Competitive Command in the Verse**

Then, in a clause unique in the Qur'an's phrasing, the verse turns from description to provocation: *wa-fī dhālika fal-yatanāfisi al-mutanāfisūn* — "so for that, let the strivers strive." The root is **n-f-s** — *nafs*, breath, soul — and the verb *tanāfasa* pictures competitors breathing down one another's necks, pressing forward at the same pace, each drawing breath to overtake. The Qur'an's other verses of holy rivalry use the same racetrack imagery: *"So vie with one another in good works"* (2:148); *"And hasten toward forgiveness from your Lord, and a Garden the breadth of the heavens and the earth"* (3:133); *"they would hasten in good deeds and call upon Us in longing and dread"* (21:90). But this verse's doubled form — *fal-yatanāfisi al-mutanāfisūn*, an intensive idiom found nowhere else — gives the rivalry its superlative: *let the strivers of striving compete* — the race for the sealed cup is the race above all races. Al-Qurṭubī notes the audacity of the rhetoric: God, having described musk and wine, does not say "rest"; He says *compete*. Revelation here blesses ambition itself — purifies it of the world's shabby prizes and re-aims it at the heights. The desire to outdo, which ruins markets and poisons brotherhoods below, is not abolished by the verse; it is *converted*, its engine tuned to a new track where every competitor's gain is truly gain.

**The Real Contest: Self-Mastery as the Gateway Sport**

The Prophet ﷺ, the community's coach in this rivalry, took care to define the event correctly, lest the believers train for the wrong race. He said:

> *"The strong man is not the one who throws others in wrestling; the strong man is the one who controls himself when angry."* (al-Bukhārī 6114; Muslim 2609)

The redefinition is the key to the verse's practical meaning. The contest that ends at the musk-sealed cup is *not* primarily won against other people — it is won against the self: against the temper that wants to betray the measure, the appetite that wants the shortcut, the tongue that wants the smirk of the coming verses. Every fast is a heat of this race; every anger mastered, every glance lowered, every withheld retaliation is a stride. The tradition even promises the race its spectators and its spoils: *"Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your sickness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your occupation, and your life before your death"* (recorded by Aḥmad and al-Ḥākim, graded sound) — the running lanes of mortality, each closing in turn, each usable only now. The strivers the verse addresses are therefore not an elite category; they are whoever has a remaining lane.

**Why God Advertises: The Economics of Sanctified Longing**

It remains to notice the verse's implicit theology of motivation. God does not say: seek the heights *without* desire, desire being beneath the dignity of the spirit. He *advertises* — musk, seal, vintage, cup — and then commands the competition. Longing (*shawq*) is, in the Qur'anic curriculum, one half of the soul's twin engines, fear and hope, and the Prophet ﷺ steered his community by both: *"If you knew what I know, you would weep much"* — and, in the same breath of his mission, promises of gardens, rivers, and faces. The commercial imagery is deliberate and safe: God markets His Paradise to His servants with the most persuasive vocabulary available to their hearts, precisely because *longing rightly aimed does the disciplining that fear alone cannot.* A man who has truly tasted the verse's provocation does not need to be flogged away from the skimmed measure; the musk has ruined the smell of every shortcut. The classical commentators, in weighing the clause, observed that it is placed *inside* the description of reward — as though the verse cannot finish describing the wine without turning to the reader and shaking him: this is real, it is reserved, and the race is open. The striver who hears it has already left the starting line — or the verse has, at least, told him where it is.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27

> **and whose mixture is of Tasnīm,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mizāj: The Art of the Blend**

One more attribute is added to the sealed wine: *wa-mizājuhū min tasnīm* — "and its mixture is of Tasnīm." The word *mizāj* (from **m-z-j**, to mix, blend, temper) belongs to the connoisseur's vocabulary: the *mizāj* of a wine is its blend — in the classical world, fine wines were drunk *tempered* with water from a prized spring, the water no less decisive than the vintage in the character of the cup. The choice of word tells the reader that Paradise's hospitality operates at the level of *composition*, not merely of substance: even the water that tempers the sealed wine is itself a named, noble thing. God's garden, the verse teaches in passing, has no generic anything — no house wine, no unmarked spring. Every component of the abrār's joy carries a name, a provenance, and a rank.

**Tasnīm: The Name and Its Readings**

The name *Tasnīm* is itself part of the verse's mystery. The commentators diverge from the first generation onward. Al-Ṭabarī records the view that it is a *proper name with no derivative meaning* — like a person's name or a place's, given, not derived. Others derive it — al-Rāzī records this — from the verb **sannama**, "to raise, to elevate, to ennoble": Tasnīm is *the water that elevates*, the drink that raises those who drink it — an etymology the spiritual masters of Islam savored, since they read every Paradise-text also as a map of the ascending soul. And the geography of the spring was debated with the same reverent precision the schools gave ʿIlliyyūn: some said it *flows from beneath the Divine Throne* into the Garden — the highest possible provenance for any drink; the source commentary records the majority's consensus on the essential point: *"Most commentators agree that Tasnīm is the purest and noblest drink of Heaven"* (al-Ṭabarī). Whatever its precise location, its rank is fixed at the summit of the Garden's cellar.

**The Two Companies at One Spring: Mixtures and Pure Draughts**

The next verse will complete the picture — Tasnīm is *"a spring whence drink those brought nigh"* — and the classical commentators, reading the two verses together, preserved a beautiful architecture of the feast. Some held: the sealed wine *of the abrār* is blended with Tasnīm, while the *muqarrabūn* — those brought nigh — drink Tasnīm *itself*, unmixed, in its purest form. Others: the muqarrabūn drink it pure, and *all other inhabitants* of the Garden receive it blended. The picture that emerges is a single spring serving a single feast at different concentrations of nearness: the companions of the right, honored beyond reckoning, drink the royal water *tempered*; the brought-nigh drink it *face to face*, untempered, as those who sit at the fountainhead. And no one is envied: the blended cup is wholly its drinker's; the pure draught, wholly its drinker's. Paradise, the sūrah teaches in its very cellar-plan, is a hierarchy without humiliation — every station a fullness, every fullness secure.

**Ibn ʿAbbās's Reading: Tasnīm as the Hidden Delight**

Then comes the reading that lifts the verse from the table to the throne of meaning. Ibn ʿAbbās — the tribunal of early exegesis — is reported to identify what Tasnīm *is*, by pointing to the verse we have already heard at v.19:

> *"No soul knows what comfort of the eyes is kept hidden for them — a recompense for what they used to do."* (32:17)

Tasnīm, in his reading, is *that hidden comfort* — the unnamed, unimagined joy prepared for the servants, the "more" beyond the gardens. Al-Rāzī, carrying the interpretation to its summit, wrote what the source commentary quotes:

> *"Tasnīm is the knowledge of God and the delight of gazing upon the Face of God."*

On this reading the verse's cellar-language becomes epistemology: the abrār's wine is tempered with the *vision*; the muqarrabūn gaze upon none but God, and so their drink is none but Him — while the companions of the right, whose eyes sometimes wander the delights of the paradisal world and sometimes lift to the Face, drink the mixture of both. The Sufi commentators, as the source notes, found in Tasnīm the highest station of *maḥabbah* — divine love — the drink of those whose thirst has outgrown creation. However one weighs these spiritual readings against the literal ones, their shared instinct is the Qur'an's own: *the waters of Paradise are finally one water, and its name is nearness.* The cup, the couch, the radiance — all are vessels of a single Gift, and the verses of this sūrah rank the vessels by the nearness of the Gift within.

**The Blend and the Believer: What Tasnīm Teaches the Thirsty Now**

For the living, the verse leaves a practical residue. Thirst, in the spiritual literature, is the posture of the soul before God — *"O men, you are the poor toward God, and God is the Rich, the Praised"* (35:15) — and the named waters of Paradise teach the believer to *specificy* his longing: not bliss in general, but *His* nearness in particular; not reward in the abstract, but the Face behind every face of reward. The Prophet ﷺ prayed for exactly this specification — *"I ask Thee for the delight of gazing upon Thy face and the longing to meet Thee"* (al-Tirmidhī 3235) — and the tradition's saints drank, in this world, what the next world's verses describe: the water of dhikr, the vintage of the Qur'an, the spring of night-prayer — mixtures, they would say, of Tasnīm, tasted by anticipation. The sealed wine of v.25 was the abrār's reward; its blend of Tasnīm is their reward's *secret heart*: that beneath every delight God gives His friends runs one current, and those who learn its taste in time will recognize it, uncorked and unmixed, when the seals of the Garden are finally broken.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28

> **a spring whence drink those brought nigh.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Geography Completed: The Spring Under the Throne**

The verse sets the last stone in the blessed cycle's architecture: *ʿaynan yashrabu bihā al-muqarrabūn* — "a spring whence drink those brought nigh." The word *ʿayn* means a spring that issues from the earth *itself* — not a basin, not a cistern, but a living source — and the verse now answers the question v.27 opened: where does the blend of Tasnīm come from? From a spring — and the ones who drink *from the spring itself*, at the source, untempered and unmediated, are *al-muqarrabūn*, "those brought nigh": the company the Qur'an crowns at the head of the Hereafter's ranks — *"And the foremost, the foremost! Those are they brought nigh, in Gardens of bliss"* (56:10–12). The commentators, as we saw, debate whether the spring's water is identical with the sealed wine's blend and whether the muqarrabūn drink it pure while the companions of the right drink it mixed; the sūrah's own emphasis falls elsewhere — on the *proximity*: some guests are served; the nearest are seated at the wellhead. Nearness, in the Qur'an's final sociology, is the reward of rewards, and every drink in the Garden is a meter marking the distance it has closed.

**The Waters of Paradise in the Prophetic Narrations: Al-Kawthar**

The tradition gives the spring-imagery its most tender instance. Anas ibn Mālik transmitted that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"While I was walking in Paradise I came upon a river whose two banks were domes of hollow pearl, and I said: What is this, O Gabriel? He said: This is al-Kawthar, which your Lord has given you. Its earth is musk."* (Muslim 400)

Al-Kawthar — "the abundance" — the river God swore to His grieving Prophet in Makkah's darkest hour: *"We have given thee al-Kawthar"* (108:1). The tradition fills the name: a river of light upon pearl, its beds of musk, its cups as countless as the stars — given *to him*, and through his intercession to his people. The believer should notice the family resemblance between al-Kawthar and Tasnīm: musk, pearl, abundance, a gift addressed to a weeping man — the waters of Paradise are, in the Qur'an's whole economy, God's answers to the grief of the faithful. The spring of v.28 is that tenderness, named and located.

**The Prophet's Own Sip: The Vessels of the Night Journey**

The narrations of the Night Journey preserve a moment of the Prophet ﷺ *himself* at the waters of the unseen world — and its symbolism is the sūrah's own. On the night of the Isrāʾ, at Jerusalem, he was brought two vessels:

> *"...one of wine and one of milk. He took the milk and looked at it; and it was said: You have chosen the natural disposition (al-fiṭrah) — you and your nation will be upon the natural disposition."* (al-Bukhārī 3887; Muslim 164)

The Prophet chose milk — the drink of guidance and uncorrupted nature — over wine, *in a world where the wine was lawful*; his nation's temperament was thereby sealed: a people whose instinct is sobriety, whose path is the fitrah. Only at the end of the road — in the Garden of v.25 — does the sealed wine return, now bearing no intoxication because bearing no fall. The two vessels thus bracket the whole human story: below, the choice of milk; above, the musk-sealed cup. The believer lives between the two drinks, and the sūrah's moral is precisely which choices keep the second one waiting.

**Zamzam and the Shadows of Heavenly Waters**

The tradition also teaches the living to treat *this* world's waters as their shadows. The Prophet ﷺ said of the well of Zamzam in Makkah: *"Zamzam is for whatever it is drunk for"* (Ibn Mājah 3062) — water drank for healing heals, for learning illuminates — and the early Muslims drank it with intentions, the shadow of an economy in which *drink itself is reward*. The Qur'an's waters below — the rain called down *"a pure drink"*? — rather: *"And We sent down from the heaven blessed water"* (50:9) — are, in the commentators' reading, the Garden's waters in disguise. The practical devotion that grew from these texts is easily underestimated: the Muslim's sip, said over with God's name, is practice for a feast in which drinking is an honor and the Host is present. The table-manners of the Hereafter are learned at the kitchen tap.

**Who Drinks Pure: The Rank That Is Open**

Finally, the verse's enumeration of the drinkers — *al-muqarrabūn* — must be heard with its biblical — with its Qur'anic openness: the foremost are a *rank*, not a closed family; the Qur'an names the ranks so that none may be treated as a birthright. Nearness to God is earned by the means this sūrah has catalogued all along: the measure given full, the covenant kept, the heart polished, the eye disciplined, the night prayed. The seven whom God shades on the day without shade (al-Bukhārī 660) are its portrait in miniature — the just ruler, the worshipful youth, the secret charity — ordinary people whose ordinary fidelity is, in heaven's census, *nearness*. The spring of v.28 is therefore not the private well of a spiritual aristocracy; it is the head of the water-supply of the entire blessed city, and the verse names its purest drinkers to make the believer thirsty in a particular direction. The feast is laid; the couches are set; the musk is on the seal. What remains — and the sūrah will now say it with a turn toward the mockers — is to keep believing *now*, when belief still costs.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:29

> **Truly those who sinned did laugh at those who believed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scene Shifts: The Sūrah Remembers Makkah**

With a single verb of memory the chapter returns to the world of its final verses' revelation: *inna alladhīna ajramū kānū min alladhīna āmanū yaḍḥakūn* — "Truly those who sinned *used to* laugh at those who believed." The tense is the past continuous of habit — *kānū yaḍḥakūn* — laughter not as an incident but as a *routine*, the recurring weather of two communities crossing in the same streets. The commentators identify the scene (al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī among them): the leaders of Quraysh, the wealthy and the established, laughing at the believers — and the believers who bore the brunt were, precisely, the poor. Islām's first congregation was drawn disproportionately from the powerless — the slaves, the clients, the young, the laborers — and their social weakness made them the elite's entertainment. The verse's phrase *alladhīna ajramū* — "those who sinned," the criminal-class of the moral universe — placed beside *alladhīna āmanū* — "those who believed" — fixes the scene's irony: the world saw strength laughing at weakness; the verse records crime laughing at faith. The labels have been switched in advance of the verdict, and the switch is the sūrah's whole argument in miniature.

The Qur'an preserved the mockers' own retrospective of this routine, dictated on the Day of Judgment, in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn:

> *And they say: "Our Lord, we believed, so forgive us and have mercy upon us, for Thou art the best of the merciful." But you took them in mockery until they made you forget My remembrance, and you used to laugh at them.* (23:109–110)

The sentence *"until they made you forget My remembrance"* exposes mockery's deep function: not entertainment but *erasure* — laughter as a technology for making the reminder ridiculous, so that the reminded could forget. The mockers of Makkah were not idly cruel; they were, by their own account, fighting a war of distraction, and laughter was their artillery.

**The Laughter of the Strong at the Weak: Khabbāb's Testimony**

What did the laughter feel like from inside? The tradition preserves the answer in the voice of one who received it. Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt — a blacksmith of the early Muslims, tortured on heated coals until the marks of his torment sat permanently on his back — came to the Prophet ﷺ with the community's plea:

> *"We complain to you, O Messenger of God, while you are leaning on your garment in the shade of the Kaʿbah: will you not call upon God for us, will you not pray to God for us? He said: Among those before you, a man would be seized, a pit dug for him, and he would be sawn in two; and he would be combed with iron combs, tearing flesh from bone and sinew — and he would not turn from his religion. By God, this matter will be completed until a rider travels from Ṣanʿāʾ to Ḥaḍramawt fearing none but God and the wolf for his sheep — but you are hasty."* (al-Bukhārī 3612)

The ḥadīth is the verse's companion-document: the "laughter" of the strong was accompanied by saws and combs and branding irons, and the Prophet's ﷺ answer to the hasty was not a promise of immediate rescue but a *horizon*: the matter (al-amr) will be completed, the faith will run the caravan roads, and the sufferers are merely earlier on the calendar than the triumph. The verse of the sūrah, recording the laughter, is writing that horizon in advance: the laughter has a scheduled counter-scene (v.34), and the ones laughed at have a file in ʿIlliyyūn. Every believing reader who has been laughed at — for the headscarf, the prayer in a stairwell, the abstention, the honesty that costs — is invited to read v.29 as documentation: the scene is ancient, the archive is accurate, and the reversal is dated.

**The Counter-Laughter of God: The Qur'an's Answer in Principle**

The Qur'an does not treat mockery as a small vice; it treats it as an *attack on the structure of honor itself* — and it answers in kind, revealing that the mockery runs upward. Of those who scorn the believers' small charities, Sūrah al-Tawbah says:

> *"Those who find fault with the believers who give alms willingly, and with those who find nothing to give but their labor, and they scoff at them — God will scoff at them, and theirs shall be a painful torment."* (9:79)

And of those who believed with their tongues while mocking in their hearts, Sūrah al-Baqarah reveals heaven's counter-intrigue in a single chilling clause:

> *"...God will mock them, and He will extend them in their tyranny, wandering blindly."* (2:15)

The two verses establish the doctrine the mockers never priced: contempt for the believers is received by God as contempt for His own choice of them — and heaven's *istihzāʾ* (mocking-response) is not petty revenge but the restoration of true proportions: the laughers will learn who was actually the joke. The Prophet ﷺ was forbidden to answer mockery in kind, but he was never forbidden to *report* the counter-laughter; the sūrah's coming verses (34–36) are that report, delivered as certainty rather than wish.

**Why Laughter Wounds: The Social Physics of Scorn**

It is worth pausing on why the Qur'an files laughter among the injuries that merit eternal reversal. Laughter, as the social scientists have documented and the playground has always known, is an *enforcement technology*: it punishes deviation from the group's norms while allowing the punishers to plead innocence — "we were only joking." It does its work precisely by being deniable: no court tries a smirk. The mockers of Makkah understood this perfectly; their laughter was the only weapon the elite could use against the faithful that left no visible wound, and it was deployed accordingly — daily, publicly, at the poor. The Qur'an's response is to make the deniable weapon *undeniable*: by recording it in revelation, God gave the smirk a documentary afterlife; by scheduling its reversal, He gave it a price. The believer who grasps this stops waiting for worldly vindication and starts trusting the file — for the record, as this whole sūrah has insisted, is bold, sealed, and utterly accurate.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30

> **When they passed them, they would wink at one another.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wink: Contempt in the Smallest Muscle**

The verse zooms the camera to a single gesture: *wa-idhā marrū bihim yataghāmazūn* — "and when they passed them, they would wink at one another." The verb *yataghāmazūn*, from **gh-m-ẓ**, is the Arabic word for the *taghmīzah* — the wink of one eye, the coordinated signal exchanged over the head of a third party. The commentators explain the scene exactly: as the believers passed — on their way to prayer, in the market lanes, by the Kaʿbah — the Quraysh elite would catch one another's eye and wink: *look at them*, *there goes another one*. The gesture's genius, and its poison, is that it communicates everything while saying nothing. No law forbids a wink; no court convicts a raised eyebrow. The wink is cruelty with deniability built into its anatomy — a message aimed at the victim but technically addressed to someone else, so that the victim cannot even protest without appearing paranoid. The Qur'an, which noticed the wet grain at the bottom of the heap, notices this: the smallest muscle of the face, moving for a quarter of a second, is entered in the same bold register as the sawn bodies and the skimmed measures. No gesture is too small for the archive.

**The Anatomy of the Winker: The Qur'an's Character Portrait**

The Book had already drawn the full anatomy of this personality in the portrait-gallery of Sūrah al-Qalam, which names the type the Quraysh revered:

> *"And obey not every hollow swearer, despicable, going about with slander (hammāz, mallāmāz), forbidding good, transgressing, sinful, cruel, besides that ignoble."* (68:10–13)

Among the titles of this gallery stands *mallāmāz* — the intensive form of the one who *blames and mocks*, the going-about defamer whose instrument is the gesture and the sneer. The scholars of ethics built on these portraits a precise taxonomy of the sins of the eye and the tongue — the backbite (*ghībah*), the tale (*namīmah*), the mocking (*istihzāʾ*), the *taghmīzah* — and the Prophet ﷺ fenced the whole territory with one of the most sweeping of his rulings on the sanctity of persons:

> *"All of the Muslim is inviolable to the Muslim: his blood, his property, and his honor."* (Muslim 2564)

and, in the same narration, the clause that targets the wink exactly:

> *"It is enough evil for a man to despise his Muslim brother."* (Muslim 2564)

The wink is *iḥtiqār* — despising — made muscular. The ḥadīth names it as an evil *sufficient in itself*, needing no accomplice and no consequence to be sinful: the contempt, discharged through a muscle, is already the crime.

**The Law of Laughter: Sūrah al-Ḥujurāt's Legislation**

The Qur'an then legislated the matter directly, in the verse that stands over every mocking circle in every age:

> *"O you who believe, let not a people mock another people: it may be that they are better than they; nor let women mock women: it may be that they are better than they. And do not defame yourselves, nor call one another by nicknames. Wretched is the name of iniquity after faith! And whoever repents not — those are the wrongdoers."* (49:11)

Three features of the legislation deserve notice. First, its *epistemic humility*: the ground of the prohibition is that the mocker does not know the rank of the mocked *"with God"* — the winking aristocrats of Makkah had no idea that Bilāl the freed slave stood higher in the heaven's census than the lords who winked at him. Second, its *symmetry*: even the mocked are forbidden to answer with mockery — the believer's dignity is not defended by adopting the enemy's weapon. Third, its *end-term*: *"whoever repents not"* — the sin of scorn, unlike some others, is curable, and the cure is named: repentance, which here means learning to see people as God sees them. The verse's placement in the sūrah's arc is exact: the mockers' winks are being filmed now so that the repentance of v.36's horizon may still be possible — and so that, if it is refused, the file is complete.

**The Digital Wink: Contempt at Scale**

No translation of the Qur'an ages faster than its illustrations, yet this verse's illustrates itself anew every decade. The *taghmīzah* of Makkah — the signal exchanged over a victim's head, deniable, cheap, entertaining — has become the standard traffic of the age: the eye-roll emoji under a believer's post; the screenshot forwarded to the group chat with no comment, because none is needed; the subtweet, the quote-post, the algorithmic pile-on in which thousands of strangers wink at one another across a victim who cannot even see them all. The technology has changed the *range* of the wink, not its anatomy — contempt, signaled to companions, discharged at a passerby, deniable by design. And the verse's ancient answer still governs: the gesture is *seen*; the signal's recipients are not the only audience; and the archive bold enough for a wet grain of wheat is more than adequate for an emoji. The believer online stands where Bilāl stood in the lane: passed, perhaps; winked at, certainly; and — if he holds to the path — documented, on high, with the moon-faced company.

**What the Believers Did: The Protocol of ʿAmmār**

The tradition even preserves the first community's protocol for enduring the lane of winks. It is related concerning ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir — mocked, taunted, and slandered by the factions after the Prophet's death — that the Prophet ﷺ had told him:

> *"O ʿAmmār, your people will hurt you: upon you is half-silence. As for the first half, it is the greeting and the peace; and as for the second, it is restraining yourself from them."* (recorded in the Musnad of Aḥmad; graded sound)

The instruction is a complete behavioral science in two clauses: *give the salām* — meet the scorner's contempt with the prayer of peace, the exact inverse of the wink; and *withhold* — do not debate, do not retaliate, do not audition for their laughter. The believer passing through a mocking world is neither to hunger for its approval nor to fight for its respect; he greets, he passes, he entrusts the file to the One who winked at no one and recorded everyone. The protocol of ʿAmmār is v.30 lived in real time — and its reward is already drafted in the sūrah's architecture: those who were passed with winks will be passed, in v.35, with *gazing* — the same lane, the same faces, and the reversal complete.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:31

> **And when they returned to their people, they would return blithely.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return Home: Contempt Recharges Its Audience**

The verse follows the mockers home: *wa-idhā inqalabū ilā ahlihimu inqalabū fākihīn* — "and when they returned to their people, they would return blithely." The doubled verb — *inqalabū... inqalabū*, "they turned back... they turned back" — paints the round trip of an afternoon's cruelty: out into the lanes where the believers passed, winks exchanged (v.30), and then the *turn back* — to the majlis, the courtyard, the circle of friends — where the day's sneering was converted into applause. For mockery is a performance, and a performance needs an audience; the lane supplied the victim, but the *return* supplied the theater. There the winks were re-enacted, expanded, laughed at again, and the mocker went to sleep fortified in his contempt. The verse's insight into the machinery is exact: contempt is not discharged in a single act; it is *cycled* — recharged nightly among one's own — and the cycle is why it never fades of itself. The tradition of the commentators identifies the circle precisely: *ahl al-majlis*, the councils of Quraysh where al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah and his peers convened to price the Prophet's ﷺ revelation and plot its ridicule.

**Two Readings of One Word: Blithely Amused or Riding High**

The verse carries a famous variance of recitation that the source notes and the schools of qirāʾah preserve. Most readings have *fākihīn* — from **f-k-h**, *delight and amusement*: they returned *entertained*, the day's cruelty having served as comedy. The school of Ḥafṣ — the reading that has become the widest-spread in the Muslim world — recites *fakihīn* — from **f-kh-r**, *pride and exultation*: they returned *puffed up*, swaggering in self-satisfaction. The difference of one vowel encompasses the two halves of the same vice: amusement and arrogance, the sneering heart and the swelling one — and the tradition, preserving both, hands the reader a matched pair of warnings. The amused mocker feeds on others' pain as comedy; the proud mocker feeds on others' smallness as status. Both go home *satisfied*, and satisfaction is the verse's indictment: no twinge of conscience interrupted the entertainment.

Against the *fakihīn*, the Prophet ﷺ drew the boundary of Paradise itself:

> *"Whoever has in his heart an atom's weight of pride shall not enter it."* A man asked: "But a man loves fine clothes and fine sandals." He said: *"God is beautiful and loves beauty; pride is rejecting the truth and despising people (baṭar al-ḥaqq wa-ghamṭ al-nās)."* (Muslim 91)

The definition locates pride in its two operations: *baṭar al-ḥaqq*, the truth shoved aside — which is what the councils did with every verse they heard; and *ghamṭ al-nās*, people held in contempt — which is what the winks did to every believer they passed. The verse's returning mockers exhibited both in one stroll, and the atom's-weight warning assigns the swagger its exact eschatological weight.

**Lying for Laughter: The Prophet's Fence Around the Majlis**

Because the verse indicts the *return* — the after-party of contempt — the tradition's legislation about what may be said to make one's own people laugh stands directly over it. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Woe to him who tells tales and lies in order to make people laugh: woe to him! Woe to him!"* (al-Tirmidhī 2319, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The tripled woe echoes the sūrah's opening word, and the parallel is not accidental: the *majlis*-entertainer who fabricates for applause is the house-guest of the muṭaffif — both trade in false quantities, one in measures, one in stories, both paid in the currency of other people's ease. And the Prophet ﷺ, the tradition's own witness to permissible humor, modeled the alternative: when the Companions marveled that he joked with them, he answered: *"I only speak the truth"* (al-Tirmidhī 1990). Islam did not abolish the laughter of the return home; it abolished its *counterfeit currency*. The believer's majlis is to be a place where the amusement is true — true stories, just words, kind subjects — for the audience of one's own people is the first audience one's character ever faces.

**The Council That Rode Too High: Al-Walīd's Ledger**

The Qur'an preserved, in Sūrah al-Muddaththir, the fullest file of the council this verse describes — the return-home of al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, Quraysh's arbiter of eloquence, who heard the revelation and was shaken by it:

> *"Leave Me with him whom I created alone, and granted him vast wealth, and sons present with him, and spread out for him all things evenly. Yet he desires that I give more. By no means! He has been hostile to Our signs. I shall burden him with an ascending climb. He pondered and determined — may he perish how he determined! — then he looked, then he frowned and scowled; then he turned his back and grew proud, and said: This is nothing but magic, handed down — this is nothing but the speech of a mortal."* (74:11–25)

The passage is v.31 unrolled in time: the council chamber, the private pondering, the frown, the proud turn, the verdict — "magic, handed down" — delivered to the applause of the assembled. And the passage also contains the price, pronounced before the applause finished: *"I shall burden him with an ascending climb"* — a burden the tradition glossed with the image of a man under a mountain, the very wealth and sons he exulted in made the weight of his ruin. The mockers' *return home blithely* was, in the Qur'an's cartography, the ascent of a road whose far end is named. The believer reading v.31 learns to hear the sound of every triumphant majlis differently: the laughter echoing in a courtyard may be the happiest sound in the city — or the sound of a burden being fitted.

**Where Do the Believers Return? The Mirrored Homecoming**

One final symmetry completes the verse. The mockers returned to their councils to recharge contempt; the believers returned to *their* circles — poor, few, hunted — to recharge faith: the secret gatherings of Ṣafā and the house of al-Arqam, where the hunted recited what had been recited to them and went out again into the winking lanes. Two homecomings, two recharges, two tomorrows. The sociologists of our own century, describing echo chambers and the applause economies of online tribes, have rediscovered the verse's mechanism — every community rehearses itself nightly, and what it rehearses it becomes. The believer's practical question is therefore asked by the verse itself: *when you turn back to your people — your family table, your group chat, your friends' table — what does the circle recharge you for?* The mockers went home to grow prouder; the abrār went home to grow softer; and the two files, as the sūrah has already shown, were filed accordingly.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:32

> **And when they saw them, they said, "Truly these are astray!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inversion of the Labels: The Lost Calling the Guided Lost**

The mockers now supply a verdict to accompany their winks: *wa-idhā raʾawhum qālū innā hāulāʾi la-ḍāllūn* — "and when they saw them, they said: Truly these are astray!" The sentence completes a reversal that only eternity will finish. *Ḍāllūn* — "astray" — is the exact word the Qur'an reserves for those who wander from the path; it is the word of the Fātiḥah's last petition, *"not of those who have incurred wrath, nor of the astray"* (1:7), and the word of the Qur'an's verdict on its rejecters. For the aristocrats of Makkah to spend that word on Bilāl and ʿAmmār and the praying poor was, in their own eyes, simple observation: the believers had abandoned the gods, the customs, and the prosperity of their fathers for a persecuted faith and a promise of gardens — *obviously lost*. The Qur'an records the accusation in order to exhibit its anatomy, for this is one of the Book's standing patterns: in every generation, the worldly look at the otherworldly and diagnose *them*. Pharaoh's court called Moses a sorcerer; the towns called every warner mad. The pattern's records read:

> *"And thus no messenger came to those before them but they said: A sorcerer, or a madman!"* (51:52)

> *"Thou art not, by the favor of thy Lord, a madman."* (68:2)

> *"And when they see thee, they take thee only in mockery: Is this the one God has sent as a messenger?"* (25:41)

The mockers' physics is inverted but consistent: they measure by *outcomes in the market of this world* — wealth, honor, numbers — and find the believer insolvent; the believer measures by *the file in ʿIlliyyūn* and finds the market itself insolvent. Each calls the other lost, but only one of them has receipts in the height.

**The Majority Argument: The Crowd as False Compass**

Implicit in the mockers' verdict is the argument of the crowd: *everyone* can see these people are astray. The Qur'an confronts that argument at its root:

> *"And if thou obeyest most of those upon the earth, they will lead thee astray from God's path; they follow nothing but conjecture, and they do nothing but surmise."* (6:116)

Truth, in the Qur'an's constitution, is not tallied by census. The verse does not say the many are always wrong; it says the many cannot be *trusted as evidence* — because the many follow *ẓann*, conjecture, the same faculty of unexamined assumption this sūrah diagnosed at v.4 ("do they not *think*..."). The mockers' verdict "these are astray" was precisely such a *ẓann*: no investigation, no argument — just the felt certainty of the prevailing view. The believer who feels the weight of numbers against him — in a workplace, a family, a culture — is hereby given the Qur'anic permission to distrust the weight: the ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm has never once been wide, and the Fātiḥah taught the faithful to *ask* for it precisely because the crowd's road is the default, not the goal.

**Who Judges Guidance? The Prophetic Criterion**

Against the mockers' market-standard, the Prophet ﷺ supplied the real instrument for identifying the guided. He said:

> *"Whoever God desires good for, He grants him understanding of the religion (fiqh al-dīn)."* (al-Bukhārī 71; Muslim 1037)

Guidance, in the Prophetic calculus, is *juridical intimacy with the truth* — the ability to see the path from inside it — and its marks are not wealth or numbers but the quiet inventory the sūrah itself has catalogued: honest measures, polished hearts, kept covenants, prayers said with presence. By this criterion the verdicts of Makkah read as farce: the "astray" were the very people whose scales were full, whose faces were clean, whose book was witnessed by the brought-nigh; and the "guided" were thumbing their measures and rehearsing their sneers. The sūrah's architecture has, in effect, published both ledgers side by side and let the reader conduct the audit the mockers never troubled themselves with. The criterion remains the working tool of every age: when a community wants to know who is truly lost, it is not to the census it must go, but to the scales.

**The Word That Will Return: 7:44's Great Reversal**

The Qur'an preserved, for the mockers' verdict, an answering scene — the one moment in eternity when the two companies will compare verdicts across the divide:

> *"And the inhabitants of the Garden shall call to the inhabitants of the Fire: We have found what our Lord promised us to be true; have you found what your Lord promised to be true? They shall say: Yes. And a herald shall proclaim among them: The curse of God shall be upon the wrongdoers, who bar from God's path, desiring it to be crooked, and who disbelieve in the Hereafter."* (7:44–45)

Notice what the herald's proclamation names as the wrongdoers' capital crime: *desiring the path to be crooked* — the exact vice of the mockers, who could not tolerate the believers' straightness because it convicted their own skew. The scene is v.32's answer scheduled: those who said "truly these are astray" will hear, from the mouths of the very people they diagnosed, the public confirmation that the *findings* were true — God's promise kept — and the verdict reversed. The believer mocked today reads v.32 with v.7:44 in the other hand: the label is temporary, the hearing is set, and the One who will pronounce the final *ḍāllūn* has already told us where He will pin it.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:33

> **Though they were not sent as guardians over them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Not Your Warrant: The Unappointed Police of Other Souls**

The verse strips the mockers of the office they had assumed: *wa-mā ursilū ʿalayhim ḥāfiẓīn* — "though they were not sent over them as guardians." The verb is the Qur'an's verb of *mission* — *ursilū*, "sent" — the same verb that authorizes prophets and witnesses of God; the mockers, the verse notes, were sent by no one. *Ḥāfiẓīn* — from **ḥ-f-ẓ**, the root of guarding, keeping, and watching over — names an office of surveillance and custody, and the commentators, as the source records, add a sharpening gloss from al-Ṭabarṣī's school: some read *ḥāfiẓīn* as *shāhidīn* — "witnesses" — meaning the mockers had arrogated to themselves the power to *testify against* the believers, to certify their misguidance as official record. The verdict of v.32 ("truly these are astray") was not offered as private opinion; it was delivered as *testimony* — the mockers acting as the state's Notaries of Misguidance, stamping the believers' file with their seal. The verse's response is surgical: *you were not sent.* The surveillance was self-appointed; the testimony, unauthorized; the whole bureau of judgment over other souls, a bureaucracy of one's own invention. And its files, heaven announces, will be returned unopened — while the believers' counter-testimony, as the source notes, will be *received*: *"in the Hereafter it is the believers who will bear witness against them."*

**The Audacity of Self-Appointment: Suspicion as the Falsest of Speech**

What licenses a man to appoint himself keeper of other souls? The Qur'an answers at the root: nothing but suspicion dressed as perception. The verse of the same moral territory, Sūrah al-Ḥujurāt, legislates the entire attitude:

> *"O you who believe, avoid much suspicion; indeed some suspicion is a sin. And spy not, neither backbite one another."* (49:12)

and the Prophet ﷺ gave the suspicion-verse its famous commentary:

> *"Beware of suspicion, for suspicion is the falsest of speech (akdhab al-ḥadīth). Do not seek out one another's faults, do not spy, do not contend with one another... " and, in the same tradition-cluster, *"whoever conceals the fault of a Muslim, God will conceal his fault in the world and the Hereafter."* (al-Bukhārī 6064; Muslim 2563; Muslim 2699)

The sequence of the legislation maps the mockers' descent exactly: suspicion (the assumption that the believers were astray) → seeking out faults (watching the lanes for their passes) → publicity (the winks and verdicts) → self-appointment (the guard-post of v.33). Each step, the Qur'an teaches, is sin; the last is also *theft* — the theft of an office that belongs to God alone, the Judge, and to those He actually deputizes. The guardian of souls is God (*"He watches over every soul as to what it earns,"* 13:33); the watchers He does send are angels with ledgers, not aristocrats with eyebrows. For a man to occupy the post by winks is, in the sūrah's framing, a kind of *fraud* — the muṭaffif of offices: claiming a commission he was never issued.

**The Reversal of Witnessing: When the Witnessed Testify**

The gloss preserved by al-Ṭabarṣī — that the believers will *witness against* the mockers in the Hereafter — connects this verse to one of the Qur'an's grand judicial scenes, the calling of witnesses at the final audit:

> *"How will it be when We bring a witness from every community, and bring thee as a witness over these?"* (4:41)

> *"And We shall draw forth from every community a witness, and say: Bring your proof."* (28:75)

The mockers of Makkah wanted the future in their control; they appointed themselves the witnesses of their age so that the record would carry their verdicts. Heaven's answer is procedural and devastating: witnesses will be drawn — but from *God's* appointment, and among them the believers themselves, whose patient endurance under mockery becomes, at the audit, *testimony*. The wink that was never documented in any earthly court will be documented in the only one that matters, by witnesses who saw it daily and did not retaliate — for the believer's restraint, the sūrah implies, is itself the collection of evidence. The verse thus teaches a doctrine of *inverted documentation*: the mocked who keep their dignity are, all unknowing, preparing depositions; the mockers who keep their audience are, all unknowing, preparing their own cross-examination.

**Watching Others, Unwatched by Themselves: The Psychology of the Moral Voyeur**

The verse's psychology deserves the final word, for the self-appointed guardian of other souls has outlived Makkah by millennia. There is in every human society a type — the moral voyeur — whose eye is a searchlight pointed permanently outward: he audits his neighbor's faith, his colleague's piety, his brother's failings, with a diligence he has never once applied to his own ledger. The energy is not virtue but *evasion*: attention directed outward is attention not directed at the self, and the self, precisely, is where his file is rotting. The classical scholars named the cure in the maxim attributed to the early generation: *"Be the guardian of your own soul, and leave the souls of the creatures to their Guardian"* — *kun ʿalā nafsika ḥafīẓan wa-daʿ ḥifẓ al-ʿibād li-ḥafīẓihā*. The believer's single legitimate surveillance beat is himself; his nightly audit, his own scales; his testimony, reserved for the court that asks him. The mockers of this verse failed at the one watching they owed and excelled at the one watching they owed nothing — and the verse's cold verb, "they were not sent," is the final notation on every such career: the post was never yours, the warrant was never issued, and the Keeper of souls will now attend to yours.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:34

> **So that Day those who believe shall laugh at those who disbelieve,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Great Reversal: The Verse the Mockers Never Priced**

After five verses inside the mockers' routine — the laughter, the winks, the triumphant returns, the verdict of "astray," the self-appointed guardianship — the sūrah delivers the counter-scene in a single temporal hinge: *fa-l-yawma alladhīna āmanū mina al-kuffāri yaḍḥakūn* — "So *that Day*, those who believe shall laugh at those who disbelieve." Every element of the mockers' own script returns with the roles exchanged. The laughter they gave is laughter they receive. The believers they passed in the lanes now *regard* them. The day whose appointments they denied becomes the day of their denouement. The Qur'an had already announced the principle — *"they scoff at them, but God will scoff at them"* (9:79); *"God will mock them"* (2:15) — and had recorded the mockers' own future confession: *"you took them in mockery until they made you forget My remembrance, and you used to laugh at them"* (23:110). Here the principle is staged: the reversal is not metaphor but *scene*, complete with couches, gaze, and a closing question. The mockers thought themselves the audience of the believers' story; the verse reveals that they were only its middle pages.

The grammar carries the theology. The verse does not say the believers *will mock* (yastahziʾūn) — the Qur'an keeps that word for the wicked's sin and God's reply — it says *yaḍḥakūn*: they will *laugh*. The distinction matters. Mockery (istihzāʾ) is contempt weaponized to degrade; laughter (ḍaḥik) here is the *relief of vindication* — the involuntary exhalation of a long-held breath. The tradition supplies the model for this laughter in the story of the messengers' guest: when the angels told the aged Sarah that she would bear a son, *"his wife was standing, and she laughed"* (11:71) — laughter at the impossible come true, laughter as the sound of promise landing. The believers' laughter on that Day is of this species: the laughter of those who trusted an unseen file through years of winks, and who now stand watching the file open exactly as written. It is justice experienced as *joy* — and joy, not gloating, is the emotion the Qur'an assigns the saved.

**Is the Reversal Vengeful? The Ethics of the Blessed Laughter**

A sensitive reader asks, with the commentators: how can the inhabitants of bliss laugh at the burning? Does not the sūrah itself teach — at v.23 — that the blessed gaze is fed by beauty, not by agony? The reconciliation lies in what the laughter is *at*. The blessed do not rejoice in suffering as such; they rejoice in the *vindication of the truth and the restoration of proportion* — the same joy the Qur'an attributes to them in the scene of 7:44, where the companions of the Garden call across to the companions of the Fire not in scorn but in witness: *"We have found what our Lord promised us to be true; have you found what your Lord promised to be true?"* And the Qur'an explicitly strips all rancor from the blessed state: *"And We shall remove whatever rancor is in their breasts; as brothers, upon couches facing one another"* (15:47). Spite cannot survive in the Garden; but *recognition* can, and the laughter of v.34 is recognition's sound — the moment the believer sees, with his own eyes, that the mockery he endured was never about him and always about the God who chose him. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī, treating the parallel scene, frame it exactly so: the laughter is at the *overturning of the mockers' certainties* — the men who certified the believers as "astray" now stand certified themselves; the comedy of that inversion, the cosmic joke played entirely on the sneerers, is what heaven's company finds itself unable to suppress. The wicked laughed *at* persons; the blessed laugh *at the reversal of the lie*. The distinction is the verse's moral license and its limit.

**The Two Cameras of Time: How to Watch a Wink Now**

For the believer under mockery, the verse supplies a way of *watching* — a double exposure. The eye of the present sees only the lane: the smirk, the passed-over believer, the evening's applause in the mocker's courtyard. The eye of faith sees the second exposure already printed: the same lane on another morning, the same faces, the roles exchanged, the believers seated and the scoffers standing at the question of v.36. To live with that double image is the practical discipline the sūrah has been building from its first word: *endure the present camera; trust the scheduled one.* The Prophet ﷺ, who absorbed more mockery than any man — called sorcerer, madman, poet, soothsayer in his own city — was himself the first to live by this verse, and his sunnah of response is the believer's working manual: never sneer back, never stop the work, answer the slander with the prayer of peace (ʿAmmār's half-silence), and leave the laughter of the reversal to its appointed day. He who laughed *little* below — his laughter, the narrations say, was mostly a smile — will be given, above, a laughter whose subject is the vindication of every believer who ever walked a mocking lane. The verse promises it with the sūrah's own emphasis: *that Day* — the day already named, already booked, and already, in the file of ʿIlliyyūn, written.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:35

> **upon couches, gazing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Words, the Second Scene: Why the Qur'an Repeats Itself Here**

For the second time in the sūrah the reader hears the identical phrase: *ʿalā al-arāʾiki yanẓurūn* — "upon couches, gazing" — word for word as at v.23. The repetition is not an economy of composition; it is the architecture's final statement. In the blessed cycle (vv. 22–28), the phrase painted the abrār's reward: the rest of the finished, the gaze of the unveiled. Now, in the reversal cycle (vv. 29–36), the same phrase seats *the same believers* in the same posture — but in the scene that answers their mockers. The Qur'an thereby closes its chiasm with matched corners: the mockers' cycle has lanes, winks, councils, and verdicts; the believers' answer has couches. The furniture of contempt finds no place on the other side of the ledger — the blessed do not descend into the lane of exchange; they simply *remain seated*, exactly where the first half of the sūrah left them, while the mockers' story unwinds below. The repetition is thus the sūrah's quietest sermon: *the reward did not change; the world did.* The believers were promised couches when they were hunted in Makkah; the couches were there the whole time, being prepared; the mockers' timeline merely caught up to them.

**Gazing at What? The Two Harmonized Readings**

The commentators, as the source records, differ on what the repeated *yanẓurūn* contains in this second scene. One reading — that of Ālūsī among others — takes the gaze as directed at the *mockers' requital*: the believers behold the disbelievers receiving what their mockery earned, and the sight completes the vindication of v.34's laughter. Another reading takes the verse as a *reassertion of the same paradisal state* as v.23 — the believers are simply described again, seated and beholding, the repetition emphasizing that their bliss is uninterrupted by the drama below. And a third, which the great commentators favored at v.23, stands behind both: the gaze at *God Himself*, per the radiant-faces verse — *"Some faces that Day shall be radiant, gazing upon their Lord"* (75:22–23). The readings do not compete so much as *zoom*: the wide shot shows the Garden's calm; the middle shot shows the requital completing every promise; the close shot shows the Face that makes every other delight the prelude. The believer may hold all three, for the Qur'an's own scenes of that Day pass fluidly between them — and the harmonization carries a pastoral point: *no glance of the blessed is ever wasted on malice.* Whether at the Face, at the fulfillment, or at the reversal, the gaze of the couches is a gaze of *recognition*, not gloating — the eye confirming what the heart trusted.

**The Peace of the Finished: The Scene's Temperature**

Everything about the verse's diction is cool. The mockers' cycle spoke in verbs of motion and excitement — laughing, winking, returning exultant, pronouncing verdicts. The believers' answer-cycle runs through a single posture: *seated, looking*. The commentators note the contrast as deliberate: fury is effort; contempt is effort; but *vindication is a state*, and the blessed simply inhabit it. There is a profound anthropology here. In this world, the believer's patience feels like passivity — the mocked believer, the cheated customer, the wronged worker often wonder whether their restraint is weakness. The verse re-prices patience at the currency of eternity: restraint, endured for God, *becomes* the throne. The abrār do not win the argument by shouting louder in the lane; they win by *outlasting the arena* — and their outlasting is seated. Every reader who has swallowed an insult for God's sake has, in the sūrah's map, a couch with his name on it, and the verse's repetition tells him it has not moved.

**The Believer's Use of the Verse: Practicing the Final Posture**

The practical yield of this repeated verse is a posture of the heart to be rehearsed *now*. The scholars of the inner life observed that the Hereafter's states begin in embryo in this world: the one who masters his rage at mockery, who learns to watch a sneer pass without needing to answer it, is already practicing the couch — the interior stillness that lets God conduct the proceedings. The Prophet ﷺ modeled the practice through years of taunts, answering poetry with poetry that never descended to insult, answering the plot with patience that outlasted the plotters; on the day of the Conquest of Makkah — the historical mirror of this eschatological one — he entered the city of his mockers *with his head bowed so far in humility that it nearly touched his saddle*, and pronounced general amnesty. That is v.35 lived before v.35: the believer so secure in the scheduled reversal that he can afford, in time, to be gracious to everyone who will lose the argument. The couches of the final verse await those who learned, in the lanes of Makkah and every Makkah since, that the deepest triumph is the one that needs no wink, no smirk, no audience — only the gaze, and what it rests upon.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:36

> **Have the disbelievers been requited for that which they used to do?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict Dressed as a Question: The Chapter's Closing Formula**

The sūrah ends not with a declaration but with an interrogative: *hal thuwiba al-kuffāru mā kānū yafʿalūn* — "Have the disbelievers been requited for that which they used to do?" The Qur'an loves to close its scenes of requital with questions — *"Is the requital of evil aught but the like of it?"* (10:27); *"Is this requited?"* — because the question drags the reader into the verdict's chair: the answer is obvious, and being *made to say it* seals it in the soul. The rhetorical form also carries a note of *astonishment*, as though the plane itself murmurs the question over the spectacle: look — is this not requital complete? And the source commentary delivers the double answer that unlocks the verse's depth: **yes and no.** Yes — the disbelievers have been requited, fully, for the evil they did; the punishment is exact, the file quoted, the mockery returned. No — they have *not* been requited for what only *appeared* to be good deeds: the charities, the hospitalities, the honors of their worldly résumé — because those deeds, severed from faith, *"have come to naught in this world and the Hereafter"* (3:22), *"and We shall proceed to what they have done of deeds and make it scattered dust"* (25:23). The question thus carries the sūrah's final, hardest doctrine: requital is exact — *and it is exact only for what was truly done*; the counterfeit entries are not discounted but voided.

**The Perfection of the Requital: The Scales of the Mustard Seed**

The affirmative half of the answer rests on the Qur'an's most repeated promise of precision. The scales are God's own:

> *"And We shall set up the scales of justice on the Day of Resurrection, and no soul shall be wronged in anything; and if there be the weight of a mustard seed, We shall bring it; and sufficient are We as reckoners."* (21:47)

> *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it."* (99:7–8)

> *"Whoever does an evil deed shall be requited only with the like of it; and whoever does right, whether male or female, being a believer — those shall enter the Garden, provided for without reckoning."* (40:40)

And the multiplication on the side of good exceeds all accounting: *"Whoever brings a good deed shall have ten the like of it; and whoever brings an evil deed shall be requited only with the like of it; they shall not be wronged"* (6:160). Evil is repaid *one for one* — exactly, never inflated; good is repaid *tenfold to seven-hundredfold* — and for the sincere, *"without reckoning"* (bighayri ḥisāb): a phrase the scholars loved to dwell upon, since the same Qur'an that opens with a chapter of scales ends its arithmetic with an uncountable sum. The wrongdoer's requital is documented in the Prophet's ﷺ warning about the smallest thefts of right:

> *"Whoever wrongs his brother in anything of his honor or his property, let him seek his release from it today, before there come a day when there is neither dinar nor dirham: if he has righteous deeds, they will be taken from him according to the measure of his wrong; and if he has no righteous deeds, the evil deeds of his brother will be taken and laid upon him, and he will be driven into the Fire."* (al-Bukhārī 2449; Muslim 2581)

The ledger closes with total rigor: no wrong smaller than a hand-span of ground escapes — the Prophet ﷺ warned that *"whoever wrongs by a hand-span of land, God will make him wear a collar of it from the seven earths"* (al-Bukhārī 2321; Muslim 1610). The mockers of this sūrah, who thought their winks weightless, stand in the verse's exact beam: *hal thuwiba al-kuffāru mā kānū yafʿalūn* — yes; to the mustard seed; in bold script.

**The Voided Entries: When "Good Deeds" Are Not Deeds**

The negative half of the answer is the verse's more disturbing teaching. The disbelievers *did* things the world called good — the generosity of Quraysh, the feeding of pilgrims, the courage of their codes — and at the audit these are not weighed as deeds at all. The Qur'an's criterion is stated at its clearest:

> *"And those who disbelieve — their deeds are as a mirage in a plain: the thirsty supposes it water, until, when he comes to it, he finds it nothing, and finds God there; so He pays him his account in full, and God is swift at reckoning. Or like darknesses in a deep sea..."* (24:39–40)

and again: *"And We shall proceed to what they have done of deeds, and make them scattered dust"* (25:23); *"those are they whose efforts have been lost in the life of the world, and they think they are doing good"* (18:104). The word used for such deeds, *ḥabaʾan manthūrā* — dust scattered by wind — is not a metaphor of punishment but of *nonexistence*: the deeds never entered the ledger, because deeds, in the Qur'an's definition, are acts *directed at God*; an act with no addressee but applause is a private performance that ends with the audience. The source commentary notes the grammatical pattern that enforces the point: the Qur'an's standing phrase for the saved life — *"those who believe and do righteous deeds"* — always couples the deed to faith; it occurs over fifty times, and never once does the deed travel alone. The lesson for the believer is not that his works are worthless without perfection — the Prophet ﷺ reassured the anxious: *"God does not grow weary until you grow weary"* (al-Bukhārī 6465) — but that the *direction* of a deed is part of the deed. Work done for display has found its reward in the display (as the Prophet ﷺ said of the show-off: that is his entire payment); work done for God is filed in ʿIlliyyūn, and its file is what this whole sūrah has been reading.

**The Mercy That Outruns the Ledger: The Last Word Is Not Fear**

Yet the sūrah — and the Qur'an — will not let the closing question ring without its counter-tone, and the tradition supplies it from the Prophet's ﷺ own mouth. The ledger of requital is exact for the defiant, but it *melts* before repentance: the Prophet ﷺ transmitted from his Lord the protocol of the deeds-audit — *"Whoever intends a good deed and does not do it, God writes it for him as a full good deed; if he does it, it is written as ten the like of it, up to seven hundred times and many times over; and whoever intends an evil deed and does not do it, God writes it for him as a full good deed..."* (al-Bukhārī 6491; Muslim 128–129) — an accounting in which even the *abandoned sin* is converted to credit, and God *"laughs"* — rejoices — at the return of His servant as a man rejoices at the finding of his lost camel (Muslim 2747). The same Prophet ﷺ who reported the collar of the seven earths also reported: *"God is more merciful to His servants than this woman to her child"* (al-Bukhārī 5999; Muslim 2754) and the decree that mercy *"prevails over wrath."* The verse's question — *have they been requited?* — is thus answered in the full Qur'anic key: requited *exactly* who refused every off-ramp; forgiven *entirely* who took any one of them. The mockers of Makkah had fourteen chances a day to answer the recitation differently, and the door they refused is, at this chapter's close, still the door the reader is standing before.

**Full Measure Given: The Chapter's Last Scales**

So the chapter ends where it began: at a measure. The opening verse pronounced woe on those who *take in full and give short*; the closing verse shows the universe's own transaction — requital given *in full, to the rim, without stint and without inflation* — and asks the reader to certify it. The defrauders' thumb is nowhere in God's scales; neither is mercy absent from His prices. Between the two verses the sūrah has laid the whole map: the file in Sijjīn and the file in ʿIlliyyūn, the rust and the polish, the veil and the gaze, the wink and the couch, the mockery of time and the laughter of the Day. Its final interrogative is, for the believer, the gentlest of all its sentences — for the one who believes and does righteous deeds is the person for whom the answer to *hal thuwiba...* is the Qur'an's greatest promise, stated in the verse of 55:60 that stands behind every scale in this chapter: *"Is the reward of goodness aught but goodness?"* — *hal jazāʾu al-iḥsāni illā al-iḥsān.* The measure is full. The Host is generous. The strivers are summoned. And the last word of Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn belongs, as the first did, to the scales — now revealed as the scales of a King whose mercy outruns His judgment and whose books, bold and sealed, never lose a mustard seed of what His servants spent.
