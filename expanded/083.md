# Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn (Chapter 83) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Named After a Sin of Small Fractions**

Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn, "The Defrauders," is the eighty-third chapter of the Qur'an in the order of the muṣḥaf and consists of thirty-six verses. It takes its name from its very first word after the exclamation of woe: *al-muṭaffifīn*. The word is built on the root *ṭ-f-f*, from which comes *ṭaff*, "the very edge, the brim, the rim" of a vessel, and *ṭafīf*, "something slight, insufficient, not reaching the brim." A *muṭaffif* is therefore not a highway robber or an armed thief; he is the merchant who fills the measure almost to the top and stops a fraction short, or who leans a thumb against the scale so that the weight reads a little in his favor. The Qur'an's choice to name an entire chapter after this sin of *small fractions* is itself the first lesson of the sūrah: God does not measure the gravity of injustice by the size of the sum stolen, but by the quality of the heart that steals it and by the corrosion it works on the whole fabric of human trust.

The classical commentators broadened the term from the first generation onward. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Qushayrī both note that the *muṭaffif*, in the widest sense, is anyone who demands his own rights in full but is slack in giving others theirs; who sees the faults of people clearly but is blind to his own; who wants to be paid every penny of his wage but arrives late and leaves early. The Companion ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd extended it even to worship: "Prayer is a measure. Whoever fills it, God fills [his reward]; whoever skims from it (*ṭaffafa*), you have heard what God said concerning the *muṭaffifīn*." The sūrah thus opens on the marketplace but its subject is the human being as such — the creature who is forever tempted to receive by one standard and to give by another.

**Where and When It Was Revealed**

The scholars of the sciences of the Qur'an differ over the chronology of this sūrah, and the difference is instructive. A number of early authorities, including reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatādah, hold that the opening verses came down shortly after the Prophet's ﷺ arrival in Madinah, when the markets of that city were notorious for dishonest measuring. Al-Nasāʾī and Ibn Mājah transmit from Ibn ʿAbbās:

> *"When the Prophet ﷺ came to Madinah, its people were among the worst in measuring. So God revealed, 'Woe unto the defrauders...' and after that they measured honestly."* (Sunan Ibn Mājah 2223; Sunan al-Nasāʾī al-Kubrā; graded ḥasan)

On this basis some hold that vv. 1–6 (or 1–4) are Madinan while the remainder is Makkan; others hold the whole sūrah Madinan; others still, including many who look to its style — the short rhymed verses, the sharp eschatological contrasts, the mockery of believers by wealthy sinners which so closely matches the Makkan situation — regard it as Makkan in origin, perhaps the very last sūrah revealed in Makkah before the Hijrah, with the Madinan report indicating not a first revelation but the Prophet ﷺ *reciting* these verses publicly upon his arrival to correct a practice he found there. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī record both positions, and Ibn Kathīr inclines toward the Madinan report for the opening verses.

What matters for the reader is what all positions share: the sūrah was addressed to a concrete situation — a community in which cheating in commerce was normalized — and the revealed words changed behavior. A society's habits of trade were reformed by a passage of scripture. This is a precedent for what the Qur'an expects of itself: not to be admired as literature but to alter how a person fills a measure.

**The Architecture of the Sūrah**

Al-Muṭaffifīn has a clear four-part architecture, and recognizing it helps the reader see why a chapter that begins with grocers' scales ends with the laughter of Paradise.

The first section (vv. 1–6) pronounces woe upon the defrauders, defines their double standard precisely, and then asks the question that reveals the root of their sin: *Do they not think that they will be resurrected unto a tremendous Day, a Day when mankind shall stand before the Lord of the worlds?* Cheating, the Qur'an says, is at bottom a failure of belief in accountability. No one shortchanges a customer while truly picturing himself standing before the Lord of all worlds to answer for it.

The second section (vv. 7–17) turns to the *fujjār*, the wicked or profligate — the broader category to which the defrauders belong. Their record is in Sijjīn, a written and sealed register in the lowest depths; they deny the Day of Judgment; they call the revelation "fables of the ancients"; and the sūrah diagnoses the cause of that denial with one of the most penetrating verses in the Qur'an: *Nay! But that which they used to earn has covered their hearts with rust* (v. 14). The consequence is a triple deprivation: they are veiled from their Lord, they burn in Hell, and they are told, *This is what you used to deny.*

The third section (vv. 18–28) mirrors the second, point for point, for the *abrār*, the pious. Their record is in ʿIlliyyūn, a written register in the heights, witnessed by the angels brought near. They recline on couches, gazing; the radiance of bliss shines from their faces; they are given sealed pure wine whose seal is musk, mixed with Tasnīm, a spring from which those brought nearest to God drink in its purity. In the middle of this description God inserts the sūrah's single explicit exhortation: *So for that let the strivers strive* (v. 26).

The fourth section (vv. 29–36) returns to the earth and to history. The wealthy sinners used to laugh at the believers, wink at one another as they passed, return home in gloating amusement, and pronounce the believers "astray" — *though they were not sent as guardians over them*. The sūrah closes by inverting the picture: on that Day it is the believers who laugh, reclining on couches, gazing, as the disbelievers are requited for what they used to do.

**The Single Thread: Measuring Justly Because One Will Be Measured**

The unifying idea of the whole chapter is *measure* — *kayl* and *wazn*. The defrauders manipulate the measure; God keeps a measure that cannot be manipulated, "a book inscribed." The defrauders demand full measure for themselves and give short measure to others; God will give every soul its full measure and wrong no one. The sinners "measured" the believers with mocking eyes and declared them astray; God re-measures both parties on the Day of Judgment. The sūrah is a sustained meditation on the truth stated elsewhere in the Qur'an:

> *"And We shall set up the just scales for the Day of Resurrection, so no soul will be wronged in anything. And if there be the weight of a mustard seed, We shall bring it forth. And We suffice as Reckoner."* (Qur'an 21:47)

The person who truly believes that he will be weighed does not tamper with weights. That is the logic of the entire chapter, and it is why the sūrah moves so naturally from commerce to eschatology: the two are not separate subjects but a single subject viewed from two sides.

**Why This Sūrah Speaks to the Modern Reader with Special Force**

Few sūrahs are as immediately applicable to the conditions of contemporary life. The modern economy runs almost entirely on measures the customer cannot check: the digital scale at the counter, the fuel meter at the pump, the fine print in a contract, the algorithm that sets a price, the invoice from a professional whose hours cannot be verified, the quality of work done behind closed doors. The opportunities for *taṭfīf* — skimming a little where no one will notice — have multiplied a thousandfold beyond the grain merchant's stall of seventh-century Madinah. At the same time, the sūrah's portrait of the confident, amused, wealthy skeptic who regards believers as naive and "astray" is a portrait many believers today recognize from their offices and universities. And its diagnosis of the heart rusted over by accumulated small wrongs until it can no longer perceive the truth is one that any honest person can confirm from within his own experience.

The sūrah's promise, however, is equal to its warning. It says that no honest gram given, and no mocking glance endured, is ever lost. It is written, in bold and permanent script, in ʿIlliyyūn, witnessed by the nearest angels; and its reward is a joy so complete that it shows in the face. That is the sūrah of *al-Muṭaffifīn*: a warning to those who skim, and a consolation to those who are skimmed from, delivered in thirty-six verses of extraordinary compression and force.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:1

> **Woe unto the defrauders,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Weight of a Single Word: *Wayl***

The sūrah opens with one word that is at once a warning, a sentence, and a lament: *wayl*. In Arabic, *wayl* is a word of threat and grief — the sound a person makes at the arrival of disaster. It is used in the Qur'an over forty times, almost always aimed at a specific category of wrongdoer: *"Woe unto those who pray, yet are heedless of their prayer"* (107:4–5); *"Woe unto every slanderer and backbiter"* (104:1); *"Woe that Day unto the deniers"* (77:15, and repeated ten times in that sūrah). The grammarians note that *wayl* here stands as a nominal sentence in the sense of a fixed decree: "Woe *is* for the defrauders" — not merely "may woe befall them," but a statement of what already awaits them.

The commentators transmit from Companions and Successors a further meaning. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr record from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, from the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"Wayl is a valley in Hell down which the disbeliever will slide for forty autumns before reaching its bottom."* (Musnad Aḥmad; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3164; the chain is discussed by the scholars, and al-Tirmidhī graded it gharīb)

Whether or not one takes this report as establishing a literal place, the point of citing it is the same: *wayl* is not a mild disapproval. When God opens a chapter with this word, the reader is meant to feel the gravity before he even learns the crime. The sequence — first the sentence, then the accused — is deliberate. It is like a judge who reads out "Guilty, and sentenced" before naming the defendant; the effect is to make everyone in the courtroom check whether the name to follow might be his own.

**Who Exactly Is a *Muṭaffif*?**

The word *al-muṭaffifīn* is the active participle of the verb *ṭaffafa*, in the second (intensive) form of the root *ṭ-f-f*. The base meaning of the root is *ṭaff*: the brim, the edge, the rim of a vessel — the last point before it is full. From this comes *ṭafīf*, "slight, small, paltry." The verb *ṭaffafa*, then, means "to fall short of the brim by a trifle," "to give a little less than the full measure." A *muṭaffif* is one who habitually does this.

Two features of this word deserve attention. First, the intensive verbal form implies not a single slip but a practice, a settled habit, a way of doing business. Second, and more remarkably, the very word chosen for the criminal describes the *smallness* of his theft. God did not say "Woe unto the thieves" or "Woe unto the robbers." He said "Woe unto those who take a fraction." This is the first great teaching of the verse: the moral seriousness of a wrong is not proportional to its monetary size. The merchant who skims five grams from every kilo is, in God's sight, worthy of *wayl*, precisely because his crime is calculated to remain beneath the threshold of notice. It is a sin that relies on going undetected — and the entire sūrah will go on to say that nothing goes undetected.

Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī both observe that the *muṭaffif* is by definition one who cheats *by stealth*. A person who openly refuses to pay is a usurper (*ghāṣib*); a person who takes by force is a robber; but a person who fills the measure to just below the brim while assuring the customer that it is full is a *muṭaffif*. His crime therefore combines injustice with deception — and deception, the scholars note, adds to a wrong the element of betrayed trust, which is why the Qur'an treats it with such severity.

**The Occasion of Revelation: A City of Two Scales**

The most widely cited report on the occasion of these verses is from Ibn ʿAbbās, transmitted by al-Nasāʾī, Ibn Mājah, Ibn Ḥibbān, and al-Ḥākim, who graded it authentic:

> *"When the Prophet ﷺ came to Madinah, its people were among the worst in measuring. So God revealed, 'Woe unto the defrauders.' After that, they measured honestly."* (Sunan Ibn Mājah 2223; Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn Ḥibbān 4919)

Al-Qurṭubī records a further detail transmitted from Abū Hurayrah and others: there was a man in Madinah — some name him Abū Juhaynah — who kept two sets of measures, one for buying and one for selling. When he bought, he used the larger measure; when he sold, the smaller. This individual is a perfect concrete image of what the verses that follow describe abstractly: a person who has, quite literally, one standard for receiving and another for giving.

The historical setting explains why the verse landed so forcefully. Madinah was an agricultural town whose economy depended on the exchange of dates, barley, and wheat by measure (*kayl*). A community that tolerated dishonest measures was a community in which every transaction was quietly poisoned by suspicion. The report that "after that, they measured honestly" is one of the clearest documented cases of a Qur'anic revelation directly reforming a social practice. It also shows the method of the Qur'an in reform: it did not first legislate market inspectors (though Islamic law would later institute the *muḥtasib* for exactly this purpose); it first addressed the conscience and tied the scale in the shop to the standing before God on the Day of Judgment.

**Why the Qur'an Treats a Small Theft So Gravely**

The reader may reasonably ask: why does the Qur'an open a chapter with a threat of Hell over a few grams of grain? The answer lies in what the small theft reveals and what it destroys.

What it *reveals* is the state of the heart. A person who cheats in something large may be driven by desperation, greed, or a single moment of weakness. A person who cheats in something small, repeatedly, has made a settled decision that his gain matters more than another person's right, and that God's watching does not matter at all. The smallness of the theft is not a mitigating factor; it is an aggravating one, because it shows that the person is willing to sell his integrity for very little. The Prophet ﷺ warned in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"Whoever usurps unjustly a hand-span of land, it will be hung around his neck from the seven earths on the Day of Resurrection."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2452; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1610)

A hand-span is nothing; yet it becomes a collar of seven earths. The principle is the same.

What the small theft *destroys* is trust, which is the invisible foundation on which every economy and every society rests. Commerce is possible only because the buyer trusts that the seller's measure is honest. When that trust is gone, every transaction requires verification, suspicion, and defensive cost, and the whole community becomes poorer, more anxious, and more hostile. Modern economists have a name for this: transaction costs, which rise steeply in low-trust societies. The Qur'an knew it fourteen centuries ago. This is why the Prophet ﷺ, when he recited these verses in Madinah, is reported to have added a warning about the collective consequences of dishonesty, recorded by Ibn Mājah and al-Bayhaqī from Ibn ʿUmar:

> *"No people ever cheat in measure and weight but that they are seized by famine, hardship of provision, and the tyranny of their rulers over them."* (Sunan Ibn Mājah 4019; part of a longer ḥadīth on the "five for five")

**The Defrauder Beyond the Marketplace**

Although the immediate reference is to merchants, the commentators were unanimous that the principle extends to every sphere in which a person is entrusted with giving another his due. Al-Qurṭubī writes that *taṭfīf* is found "in measure, weight, ablution, prayer, and speech." Mālik ibn Anas, the imam of Madinah, is reported to have said: "Everything has its *taṭfīf*; even prayer." Ibn Masʿūd said, as noted in the source commentary:

> *"Prayer is a measure. Whoever gives full measure, God will give him full measure. And whoever skims (ṭaffafa), you know what God said about the muṭaffifīn."*

The employee who is paid for eight hours and works six is a *muṭaffif*. The contractor who uses cheaper materials than the ones invoiced is a *muṭaffif*. The student who copies a portion of an essay is a *muṭaffif*. The scholar who quotes a text selectively to make it say what it does not say is a *muṭaffif* of knowledge. The person who demands full patience and forgiveness from his spouse but gives a fraction of it back is a *muṭaffif* of the heart. Al-Qushayrī, from the spiritual side, defined the *muṭaffif* as "one who wants his own rights honored in full but does not honor the rights of others" and, more subtly, "one who sees the faults of others and is blind to his own." Once this is understood, no reader can hear *Woe unto the defrauders* as a verse about other people.

**The Logic of Beginning with Woe Rather Than with Law**

Islamic law contains detailed rules about honest weights and measures, the duty of the market inspector, the invalidity of fraudulent sales, and the obligation to return ill-gotten gains. But the Qur'an does not begin this sūrah with any of that. It begins with *wayl* — with the emotional and spiritual truth of the matter — because law without conscience is a net with holes in it. A dishonest merchant can always find a way around the inspector; he cannot find a way around God. By opening the chapter with the destination rather than the regulation, the Qur'an addresses the only mechanism that actually works when no one is watching: the belief that Someone is.

This is a general principle of Qur'anic pedagogy. It does not primarily produce compliance by fear of the state; it produces integrity by awareness of God, *taqwā*. The person of *taqwā* handles a scale the way a careful driver handles a mountain road at night — not because a police car is behind him, but because he knows what lies beyond the edge. The word *ṭaff* means "edge." The *muṭaffif* lives at the edge, always a fraction short. The believer is asked to fill the measure to the brim and beyond, and the sūrah's later verses will describe the overflowing cup — sealed wine mixed with Tasnīm — that God pours for those who did.

**A First Reflection for the Reader**

Before moving to the detailed description of the defrauder in the next two verses, the opening word invites every reader to a moment of self-examination. Where in my life do I receive by one measure and give by another? In my work, do I take the full wage and give the full effort? In my relationships, do I demand exactly what I fail to offer? In my worship, do I fill the measure of prayer or skim from its bowing and its stillness? The Qur'an opens with *wayl* not to crush the reader but to wake him — because the one who wakes now, while the scale is still in his hand, can set it right before the Day when the scales belong to God alone.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:2

> **who, when they take measure from people, demand [it] in full,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Half of a Double Standard**

Having pronounced woe upon the defrauders, the sūrah now defines them with surgical precision in two verses that form a single balanced sentence. This verse gives the first half: *alladhīna idhā iktālū ʿalā al-nāsi yastawfūn* — "who, when they take measure from people, demand it in full." The next verse will give the second half: when they measure out for others, they give less. Between the two halves lies the whole moral anatomy of the defrauder. He is not a man who does not know what justice is. He knows it perfectly — when it concerns himself.

The verb *iktālū* is the eighth form of the root *k-y-l* (to measure by volume). The eighth form here carries the sense of "to take by measure for oneself," "to receive one's due in measure." The prepositional phrase *ʿalā al-nās*, "from people" (literally "against people"), is significant: many commentators, including al-Zamakhsharī, note that the Qur'an chose *ʿalā* rather than *min* to convey that the defrauder takes from people as one who has a claim *over* them, pressing his right upon them with insistence. Al-Farrāʾ held that *ʿalā* and *min* are interchangeable here; but the majority sensed a nuance — the defrauder receives not humbly but demandingly.

The verb *yastawfūn* is from the root *w-f-y*, which gives *wafāʾ* (fulfillment), *wāfī* (complete), and *tawaffā* (to take in full — the very verb the Qur'an uses for death, when God "takes the soul in full"). The tenth form *istawfā* means "to seek and obtain the full amount," "to insist on complete payment." The defrauder, in other words, is meticulous. When he is on the receiving end of a transaction, he watches the measure like a hawk, insists that it be filled to the brim, and will not leave a single grain behind.

**The Psychology of Knowing Justice Only for Oneself**

This verse is a profound psychological observation compressed into a few words. The defrauder is not ignorant of justice; his behavior when receiving proves that he understands exactly what a full measure is, exactly where the brim of the vessel lies, and exactly how much the difference between "full" and "almost full" is worth. His sin is therefore not a failure of knowledge but a failure of *application*: he applies the standard he knows to be right in one direction only.

Al-Rāzī draws out the implication. The defrauder, he notes, cannot plead ignorance or difficulty on the Day of Judgment, because his own conduct as a buyer is the witness against him as a seller. If he claims that exact measuring is too hard, God can reply: you managed it perfectly when it was your grain being measured. This is why the Qur'an placed the description of him as *receiver* first. It establishes his competence before exposing his crime.

The human tendency described here is universal and is not restricted to markets. Every person has a keen sense of his own rights and a dim sense of his obligations. We notice instantly when we are underpaid, kept waiting, spoken to rudely, or given the smaller portion; we notice far less when we underpay, keep others waiting, speak harshly, or take the larger share. The Prophet ﷺ described the same asymmetry in the domain of faults, in a saying recorded by al-Bukhārī in *al-Adab al-Mufrad*:

> *"One of you sees the speck in his brother's eye and forgets the log in his own."* (al-Adab al-Mufrad 592; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī)

The *muṭaffif* is the economic form of this spiritual blindness. He has a microscope for what is owed to him and a fog for what he owes.

**The Qur'anic Standard: One Measure, Both Directions**

The Qur'an repeatedly demands that human beings apply a single standard in both directions. The most concise statement of this is in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ, in the context of testimony:

> *"O you who believe! Be steadfast maintainers of justice, witnesses for God, even if it be against yourselves, or your parents and kinsfolk, whether rich or poor..."* (Qur'an 4:135)

"Even if it be against yourselves" — that is the precise antidote to *taṭfīf*. The defrauder is a witness for justice when it profits him and against justice when it costs him. The believer is asked to be a witness for justice in both cases, and the Qur'an says that this is what it means to be a witness *for God* rather than for one's own pocket.

The Prophet ﷺ gave the practical rule of this symmetry in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 13; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 45)

Applied to this verse, the rule means: whatever fullness of measure you love to receive, love to give. The defrauder loves to receive in full and hates to give in full; he has failed the very definition of faith that the Prophet ﷺ laid down.

**A Legitimate Right Turned into a Symptom**

It should be noted that the verse does not condemn the defrauder for demanding full measure when he buys. Demanding one's right is lawful; the Qur'an elsewhere commands that measures be given in full *to* people, and so a buyer who insists on receiving his due is doing nothing wrong. Al-Qurṭubī makes this point explicitly: *istīfāʾ* (taking in full) is not itself a sin. What is condemned is the *combination* — the demanding of fullness for oneself joined to the withholding of fullness from others. The Qur'an mentions the lawful behavior here because in the defrauder's case it functions as evidence: it is the proof that his short-measuring is knowing, deliberate, and self-serving.

This is a subtle and important distinction for real life. A person is not a hypocrite for wanting fairness for himself. He becomes a hypocrite when his desire for fairness stops at the boundary of his own interest. The test is not "Do I want justice?" — everyone wants justice for himself — but "Do I want justice when I am the one who must pay for it?"

**The Social Damage of the One-Way Standard**

Consider what happens to a community in which the one-way standard becomes common. Each party to a transaction insists on full measure when receiving and gives short measure when giving. The result is not equilibrium but mutual predation: everyone is cheating everyone, and everyone knows it, so the honest are punished and the dishonest rewarded. Over time, the honest either leave the market or become dishonest to survive. This is the "race to the bottom" that the Qur'an's warning is designed to prevent. The Prophet ﷺ tied this dynamic to divine consequences in the report already cited from Ibn ʿUmar in Sunan Ibn Mājah: communities that cheat in measure are afflicted with scarcity and oppressive rule — because, one may reason, a society of cheats produces exactly the conditions of scarcity (through lost trust and reduced trade) and tyranny (since only force can then govern relations among people who do not trust one another).

By contrast, the Prophet ﷺ described the blessing that follows the opposite behavior in the famous ḥadīth of the two trading parties, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Ḥakīm ibn Ḥizām:

> *"The two parties to a sale have the choice [to annul it] so long as they have not parted. If they are truthful and make things clear, their sale will be blessed for them; but if they conceal and lie, the blessing of their sale will be wiped out."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2079; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1532)

The word *barakah*, blessing, is the key. A defrauder gains a few grams and loses the blessing of the whole transaction. The honest seller loses a few grams and gains a blessing that cannot be weighed.

**The Modern Face of "Demanding in Full"**

The behavior of this verse is instantly recognizable in contemporary life. There is the customer who checks his change to the last coin but pads his expense report. There is the employee who files a grievance the moment his overtime is short by half an hour but arrives fifteen minutes late every day without a thought. There is the landlord who deducts from the deposit for every scratch but leaves repairs undone for months. There is the person who is outraged at being interrupted and interrupts constantly. In each case the person's outrage at receiving short measure is itself the evidence that he knows precisely what full measure is.

There is also the collective form: a nation or class that demands full respect for its own rights, its own borders, its own dignity, while treating the rights of others as negotiable. The Qur'anic diagnosis applies at every scale.

**Reading the Verse as a Mirror**

The practical value of this verse lies in a simple exercise it invites: to notice the moments when one demands fullness, and to ask whether one gives it. The next time you feel the sting of being shortchanged — in money, in time, in attention, in credit for work done — let that sting become a measuring instrument. It is telling you exactly how the other person feels when you shortchange him. The defrauder wastes this instrument; he feels the sting only as grievance. The believer uses it as a mirror. The verse says *yastawfūn* — they insist on fullness. The believer's task is to insist on the same fullness in the direction that costs him: to give the full hour, the full effort, the full portion, the full truth, the full apology, the full prayer.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:3

> **and when they measure for them or weigh for them, they stint.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half: The Sentence Completed**

This verse completes the definition begun in the previous one. *Wa idhā kālūhum aw wazanūhum yukhsirūn* — "and when they measure for them or weigh for them, they cause loss." The two verses together form a perfectly balanced antithesis: *when they receive*, they demand fullness; *when they give*, they diminish. The rhetorical structure mirrors the content. Just as the defrauder's two scales are unequal, so the two halves of his description stand in stark contrast, and the reader is meant to feel the imbalance as a moral ugliness.

Two verbs are used for the act of giving: *kālū* (they measured by volume, from *k-y-l*) and *wazanū* (they weighed, from *w-z-n*). Volume-measuring was used for grain, dates, and liquids; weighing for gold, silver, spices, and denser goods. By naming both, the Qur'an closes every loophole: whether the commodity is measured by the cup or by the balance, the defrauder finds a way to give less. The commentators note that the mention of both methods is a form of comprehensiveness, indicating that *every* means of quantifying exchange is covered — and by extension, in the modern world, every meter, gauge, timesheet, invoice, and algorithm.

**A Grammatical Question with a Moral Answer**

The source commentary notes an interesting grammatical point. In the phrase *kālūhum aw wazanūhum*, the pronoun *hum* ("them") is attached directly to the verbs. In classical Arabic one would ordinarily expect *kālū lahum* ("measured *for* them"). The attached form *kālūhum* is a recognized idiom of the Ḥijāz — one says *kiltuka* meaning *kiltu laka* ("I measured for you") — and al-Farrāʾ, al-Zamakhsharī, and al-Ṭabarī all confirm it. Some early grammarians proposed instead that *hum* is an emphatic pronoun affirming the subject: "when *they themselves* measure or weigh, they diminish." Both readings converge on the same sense, but the emphatic reading adds a sting: it is *they*, the very people so vigilant about their own rights, who diminish when the measure is in their hands.

The verb *yukhsirūn* is from the root *kh-s-r*, which gives *khusrān*, "loss," one of the most important words in the Qur'an's moral vocabulary: *"By time! Truly mankind is in loss (khusr)"* (103:1–2); *"the losers (al-khāsirūn)"* is the standard Qur'anic designation for those who lose the Hereafter. Here *yukhsirūn* in the fourth form means "they cause loss," "they make deficient." But the Qur'an's choice of this root carries an undertone: the one who causes loss to others is, in the ultimate sense, the one who is *in loss*. The defrauder thinks he is causing loss to his customer; in reality he is inflicting *khusrān* upon himself.

**The Qur'an's Insistence on the Full Measure Elsewhere**

This is not the only place where the Qur'an addresses honesty in measuring. It is one of the very few commercial matters the Qur'an mentions repeatedly, which indicates its importance. In Sūrah al-Anʿām, among the ten commandments of that chapter:

> *"And give full measure and weight with justice. We task no soul beyond its capacity."* (Qur'an 6:152)

In Sūrah al-Isrāʾ, among the commandments listed there:

> *"And give full measure when you measure, and weigh with an even balance. That is better and fairest in the end."* (Qur'an 17:35)

In Sūrah al-Raḥmān, honest weighing is placed in cosmic context:

> *"And the heaven He raised, and He set the Balance, that you not transgress in the balance. So establish weight with justice, and fall not short in the balance."* (Qur'an 55:7–9)

And, most extensively, in the story of the Prophet Shuʿayb, sent to the people of Madyan, whose entire prophetic mission is described as a struggle against this very sin:

> *"And to Madyan, their brother Shuʿayb. He said, 'O my people! Worship God; you have no god other than Him. And do not diminish the measure and the balance. I see you in prosperity, and I fear for you the punishment of an encompassing Day. O my people! Give full measure and weight with justice, and do not deprive people of their things, and do not commit abuse on the earth, spreading corruption.'"* (Qur'an 11:84–85)

The parallel between Shuʿayb's people and the defrauders of this sūrah is close. In both cases the sin is short-measuring; in both cases it is tied to denial of the Day of Judgment; in both cases the phrase used for the harm is that people are "deprived of their things" (*bakhs*). The fate of Madyan — destroyed by a cry and an earthquake — is the historical illustration of the *wayl* pronounced in this sūrah's first verse. The Qur'an is telling the defrauders of Madinah and Makkah: you are repeating the sin of a people who were destroyed for it.

**The Phrase from Sūrah al-Anʿām: "We Task No Soul Beyond Its Capacity"**

One detail in the verse from al-Anʿām deserves special attention because it answers a natural objection. After commanding full measure "with justice," God adds: *"We task no soul beyond its capacity."* The classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, explain that this addresses the fear of the scrupulous person who worries that he can never achieve perfect, atom-exact measurement. God's answer is that the obligation is to intend justice and to do one's honest best; unintentional, unavoidable differences of a grain here or there are forgiven. What is condemned is the *deliberate* diminishing — the *taṭfīf* of this sūrah. This distinction matters for practical life: the believer is not asked to be paralyzed by anxiety over microscopic exactness, but to be honest in intention and careful in practice.

**Why "Stinting" Is Worse Than It Looks**

The defrauder reasons: "It is only a little. The customer will never notice. It harms no one significantly." The Qur'an's reply is fourfold.

First, it is theft, however small; and theft of what belongs to another is forbidden absolutely, without a threshold. The Prophet ﷺ said in his Farewell Sermon, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"Truly your blood, your property, and your honor are sacred to one another, as sacred as this day of yours, in this month of yours, in this city of yours."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 67; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1679)

Property is sacred; there is no clause exempting small amounts.

Second, the smallness is multiplied by repetition. A merchant who skims a small amount from every sale, every day, for years, has stolen a great deal — from a great many people. The individual theft is tiny; the aggregate is enormous. And on the Day of Judgment each victim will come with his claim. The Prophet ﷺ described this in the ḥadīth of the bankrupt, recorded by Muslim:

> *"The bankrupt of my nation is he who comes on the Day of Resurrection with prayer, fasting, and zakāh, but he had insulted this one, slandered that one, consumed the wealth of this one, shed the blood of that one, and struck that one. So this one is given from his good deeds, and that one from his good deeds; and if his good deeds run out before what he owes is paid off, their sins are taken and cast upon him, and then he is thrown into the Fire."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2581)

A lifetime of skimmed grams becomes a queue of creditors on the Day when the only currency is good deeds.

Third, the small theft is committed precisely *because* it is small — that is, precisely because the defrauder believes it will escape detection. His sin is therefore a small theft plus a large denial: the denial that God sees. This is why the very next verses of the sūrah turn immediately to the Resurrection. The Qur'an sees the short measure not primarily as an economic offense but as a theological one — a practical declaration that God is not watching.

Fourth, the small theft corrupts the thief more than the large one. A large theft shocks the conscience; the thief knows he has done something terrible. A small, habitual theft anesthetizes the conscience. Each repetition makes the next easier. The sūrah will later describe the end of this process: *that which they used to earn has covered their hearts with rust* (v. 14). The verb "earn" (*kasaba*) there is a commercial word. The rust is made of exactly these small, repeated, "harmless" grams.

**The Weighing of the Weighers**

There is an irony in this verse that the Qur'an's later verses make explicit. The defrauder spends his life adjusting scales. But he himself will be placed on a scale:

> *"And the weighing on that Day is the truth. So those whose scales are heavy — it is they who are the successful. And those whose scales are light — it is they who have lost their souls, because they wronged Our signs."* (Qur'an 7:8–9)

*Those whose scales are light* — the very word for the defrauder's crime, lightness of measure, becomes the description of his fate. He made others' measures light; his own scale will be light. God's justice here has the quality of perfect symmetry: the punishment is the sin turned upon its author. And the reverse is also true: the honest measurer, who filled others' vessels to the brim, will find his own scale heavy with what he gave.

**Stinting in the Non-Material Measures of Life**

As with the previous verses, the classical scholars insisted that the principle applies beyond commerce. Every relationship involves a giving and receiving, and every giving can be stinted. The husband who gives a fraction of the attention he demands; the parent who demands total obedience and gives partial care; the teacher who demands full attendance and gives half-prepared lessons; the friend who takes support freely and gives it grudgingly; the citizen who demands full public services and evades his fair contribution; the worshipper who wants full answers to his prayers and offers hurried, distracted prayer — each *yukhsir*, each stints. The Prophet ﷺ called the hurried, incomplete prayer "the worst thief," in a ḥadīth recorded by Aḥmad and al-Ḥākim:

> *"The worst thief among people is he who steals from his prayer." They asked, "O Messenger of God, how does he steal from his prayer?" He said, "He does not complete its bowing or its prostration."* (Musnad Aḥmad 22642; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī)

The one who stints in prayer is described with the very vocabulary of theft. That is how seriously the Qur'anic tradition took the principle of these three verses.

**A Practical Discipline of Overflowing**

The best defense against *taṭfīf* is a positive habit rather than a negative fear: the habit of giving slightly *more* than what is owed. The Prophet ﷺ modeled this. When he bought a pair of trousers from a seller and had him weigh the payment, he said, as recorded by Abū Dāwūd and al-Tirmidhī:

> *"Weigh, and tip the balance (arjiḥ)."* (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 3336; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 1305; graded ṣaḥīḥ)

That is, weigh the silver for payment and let it tilt in the seller's favor. The one who habitually tips the balance toward others never needs to worry about accidentally falling short, and he acquires a heart that is the opposite of the defrauder's: open-handed where the defrauder is tight-fisted, generous where he is grasping, at peace where he is anxious. In the later verses this sūrah will describe such people as the *abrār* — the openly, overflowingly righteous — and it will pour for them a wine whose measure never falls short.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:4

> **Do they not think that they will be resurrected**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Marketplace to the Grave in a Single Question**

After three verses describing an economic crime, the sūrah suddenly changes register. It does not proceed to a penalty, a regulation, or an appeal to fairness. It asks a question: *A-lā yaẓunnu ulāʾika annahum mabʿūthūn* — "Do they not think that they will be resurrected?" The abruptness is deliberate and is one of the most instructive turns in the Qur'an. It tells the reader that the root of the defrauder's crime is not in his hands but in his beliefs. The shopkeeper's thumb on the scale is connected, by a straight line, to his picture of what happens after death.

This is a general Qur'anic pattern: outward conduct is treated as the visible symptom of an inward conviction. The Qur'an does not primarily try to change behavior by changing incentives; it tries to change behavior by changing what a person *believes to be real*. A person who genuinely expects to stand before God and answer for every gram does not need a market inspector. A person who does not expect it will find a way around any inspector. Hence the question.

**The Force of "Do They Not Even Suppose?"**

The verb chosen is *yaẓunnu*, from *ẓann*, which in Arabic ordinarily means "to suppose," "to think probable," "to have an opinion" — a level of conviction below certainty (*yaqīn*). The classical commentators found this choice remarkable and drew two lessons from it.

The first is a rhetorical intensification. God does not say, "Do they not *know with certainty* that they will be resurrected?" He says, "Do they not even *suppose* it?" — as if to say: even a mere suspicion, a bare probability, that one might be resurrected and judged should be enough to stop a rational person from cheating. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and Ibn Kathīr all make this point. If a man were told, "There is a reasonable chance that you will be audited for every transaction you make, with penalties beyond your imagining," he would keep honest books even without certainty. The defrauder behaves as one who does not assign even a small probability to the Resurrection. His conduct therefore reveals a denial more complete than most people would admit in words.

The second lesson concerns the Qur'anic use of *ẓann* for something close to certainty. In several verses, *ẓann* refers to firm belief in the Hereafter: *"those who think (yaẓunnūn) that they will meet their Lord and that unto Him they will return"* (2:46); *"Truly I thought (ẓanantu) that I would meet my reckoning"* (69:20). Some commentators therefore read the verse as "Do they not *believe* that they will be resurrected?" Both readings are valid and complementary. The lesson of the first reading — that even a suspicion should suffice — is the one most exegetes emphasize, because it exposes the practical atheism hidden in dishonest conduct.

**"Those People": The Distancing Demonstrative**

The verse refers to the defrauders as *ulāʾika*, "those" — a demonstrative of distance. The rhetoric of the Qur'an frequently uses distant demonstratives either for honor (as in *"Those are upon guidance from their Lord"*, 2:5) or for contempt and estrangement. Here the sense is the latter: God speaks of the defrauders as though pointing at them from afar, disowning them, holding them up before the listener as specimens. The reader is invited to look at "those people" and be astonished — how can they behave so, when the Resurrection awaits? — and then, as always in the Qur'an, to turn the astonishment inward.

**Resurrection as the Foundation of Ethics**

The verse embodies a claim about the foundations of morality that deserves to be stated plainly. The Qur'an holds that belief in the Resurrection and Judgment is not merely one article of faith among others; it is the practical foundation of all consistent ethical conduct. Without it, morality becomes a matter of calculation: I will be honest when honesty pays, and dishonest when I can get away with it. With it, morality becomes absolute: I will be honest because I will answer for it, whether or not anyone on earth ever knows.

This is why the Qur'an so frequently pairs "belief in God" with "belief in the Last Day" as the twin foundations of faith — over twenty times the two are mentioned together. Belief in God establishes *who* is watching; belief in the Last Day establishes *that it matters*. A person could theoretically believe in a Creator who does not judge; such a belief would leave conduct unchanged. It is the Judgment that closes the gap between belief and behavior.

The Prophet ﷺ made the connection between faith and honest dealing explicit in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah:

> *"The trustworthy, honest merchant will be with the prophets, the truthful, and the martyrs."* (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 1209; graded ḥasan)

Why should an honest merchant rank with prophets and martyrs? Because his honesty, in a domain full of temptation and low risk of detection, is a daily testimony that he believes in the Unseen — that he acts as one who will be resurrected. His scale is a form of witness (*shahādah*), and the martyr (*shahīd*) is, in the root sense, a witness.

**The Logic of the Question**

The verse proceeds by what the logicians call an argument from consequence. If the defrauders believed they would be resurrected, they would not cheat. They cheat. Therefore, they do not — at the level of operative conviction — believe they will be resurrected. The Qur'an here does something precise: it uses behavior as a diagnostic of belief. This is a principle the believer can apply to himself. The question is not "What do I say I believe about the Hereafter?" but "What do my transactions, my private conduct, my treatment of those who cannot hold me accountable, reveal about what I *actually* believe?" The scale in the shop is a confession of faith more honest than any recited creed.

Al-Ghazālī and the scholars of the heart called this gap between professed and operative belief *ghaflah*, heedlessness — the condition of one who "knows" the Hereafter as a fact but does not "see" it as a reality. The defrauder is not typically a philosophical denier of the Resurrection. He may pray and fast. But at the moment of the transaction, the Resurrection is not present to his mind; he is heedless of it; and his hand does what heedlessness permits. The Qur'an's question is designed to pierce that heedlessness: *Do you not even suppose...?*

**Why the Qur'an Argues Rather Than Merely Commands**

It is worth noticing that God does not simply declare, "You will be resurrected, so stop cheating." He poses a question that invites the defrauder to reason. This is characteristic of the Qur'an's respect for human intelligence. It treats the wrongdoer as a rational agent capable of drawing conclusions, and it assumes that if he actually thought about the matter, he would change. The Qur'an frequently says of the disbelievers *"Will they not reflect?"*, *"Do they not reason?"*, *"Will they not consider?"* The implication is that sin is, at bottom, a failure of thought — an unwillingness to follow one's own beliefs to their conclusions. The defrauder is not asked to be more religious in some vague sense; he is asked to be more consistent.

**An Analogy from Ordinary Prudence**

Consider a person who is told by a doctor that there is a reasonable chance he has a serious condition and needs tests. Even without certainty, a sane person takes the tests. Consider an investor who is told there is a reasonable chance a stock will collapse. Even without certainty, a prudent investor sells. In every domain of life we act on probabilities, not certainties, and we regard as foolish the person who ignores a serious possibility because it is not proven. The Qur'an applies this universal prudence to the greatest possibility of all. If there is *any* real chance that you will stand before God and be asked about every gram and every word, the rational course is clear. The defrauder's behavior is not merely immoral; it is *imprudent* in a way he would recognize instantly in any other context. This is the sting of *a-lā yaẓunnu* — "do they not even suppose?"

**Resurrection as Bodily and Real: *Mabʿūthūn***

The word *mabʿūthūn*, "resurrected," is the passive participle of *baʿatha*, "to raise up, to send forth." The same verb is used for God's *sending* of prophets and for His *raising* of the dead — both are a "sending forth" by divine command. The Qur'an insists throughout that this raising is real and bodily, not merely a spiritual survival: *"Does man think We shall not assemble his bones? Yes indeed, We are able to fashion even his fingertips"* (75:3–4). The mention of fingertips is remarkable: the very part of the body with which the defrauder pressed the scale will be reassembled to answer for it. The Resurrection is not an abstraction; it is the return of *this* person, with *these* hands, to face what *these* hands did.

**Application: Installing the Question at the Point of Temptation**

The practical use of this verse is as a mental habit. The believer is invited to install the question *"Do I not think that I will be resurrected?"* at the precise moments when a small dishonesty presents itself: when the cashier gives too much change, when the timesheet can be rounded up, when a defect in what one is selling can be concealed, when an exaggeration will win the argument. The question is short enough to be recalled in an instant and heavy enough to stop the hand. This is what the scholars mean by *murāqabah*, the constant awareness of being watched — the practical form of *taqwā*, the driver who keeps his eyes on the road not because of the police but because of the cliff.

The sūrah will now specify what kind of Day awaits the resurrected — and the description that follows is designed to make the question of this verse impossible to shrug off.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:5

> **unto a tremendous day—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words That Carry the Weight of Eternity**

The verse is brief: *li-yawmin ʿaẓīm* — "unto a tremendous Day." Yet its brevity is a form of emphasis. The sentence begun in the previous verse — *Do they not think that they will be resurrected* — is completed not with an elaborate description but with a single adjective, *ʿaẓīm*, "tremendous, mighty, immense." The Qur'an often uses this technique: rather than exhaust the subject, it names it with a word that opens onto something too large to be described and lets the imagination stand before the door. The Day is not described; it is measured — and the measure is *ʿaẓīm*.

**The Meaning of *ʿAẓīm***

The root *ʿ-ẓ-m* gives *ʿaẓm*, "bone" — the hard, structural core of a body — and *ʿaẓamah*, "greatness, majesty, magnitude." *ʿAẓīm* describes something great in its very substance, not merely large in extent. When the Qur'an calls God *al-ʿAẓīm* (as in the Throne Verse: *"and He is the Most High, the Tremendous"*, 2:255), it means greatness in essence, beyond comparison. When it calls the Day *ʿaẓīm*, it means that the Day is great in every dimension at once: in its length, its terror, its consequences, its finality, and the magnitude of what is decided on it.

Al-Rāzī observes that the Qur'an describes the Day of Judgment with a series of adjectives across different sūrahs, each emphasizing one aspect: *"a heavy Day"* (*yawman thaqīlan*, 76:27) for its weight upon the soul; *"a grievous Day"* (*yawmin alīm*, 11:26) for its pain; *"an encompassing Day"* (*yawmin muḥīṭ*, 11:84) for its inescapability; *"a Day when there is no bargaining nor friendship nor intercession"* (2:254) for its justice; *"a Day that will make children grey-haired"* (73:17) for its terror. *ʿAẓīm*, tremendous, gathers all of these into one word. It is the summary adjective.

**Why "Tremendous" Rather Than "Terrible"**

One might expect the Qur'an, in warning defrauders, to describe the Day as terrifying or painful. Instead it chooses a word that is not, in itself, negative. *ʿAẓīm* is used for God, for the Qur'an itself (*"a tremendous Qur'an"*, 15:87), for the reward of the righteous (*"a tremendous reward"*, 4:40), and for the Throne (*"Lord of the Tremendous Throne"*, 9:129). The choice is significant. The Day is not tremendous only because of its punishments; it is tremendous because of its *significance*. It is the Day on which the meaning of everything is revealed, the Day for which the world was made, the Day on which every question of justice that history left unanswered is answered. It is tremendous for the righteous, who receive their tremendous reward, as much as for the wicked. The defrauder is being told: you are treating as negligible the most significant event in existence.

**The Connection to the Crime**

There is a precise correspondence between the smallness of the sin and the tremendousness of the Day. The defrauder's entire method depends on smallness — a small shortfall, a small deception, too small to matter. The Qur'an confronts this smallness with immensity. You are skimming grams, and a Day of infinite magnitude is coming. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the Qur'an's placing of *ʿaẓīm* here is a deliberate rebuke of the defrauder's scale of values: he has magnified the trivial (a few coins of profit) and trivialized the tremendous (his standing before God). The verse restores the correct proportions.

This inversion of scale is one of the deepest themes of the Qur'an's moral psychology. Sin, in general, works by the distortion of proportion: it makes the immediate seem large and the ultimate seem small. The Qur'an repeatedly corrects this: *"But you prefer the life of this world, while the Hereafter is better and more lasting"* (87:16–17). A tremendous Day set against a small profit is the whole argument in miniature.

**What the Qur'an Says of the Tremendousness of That Day**

Other verses expand what *ʿaẓīm* compresses. Of its cosmic upheaval:

> *"When the sun is folded up, and when the stars fall, and when the mountains are set in motion..."* (Qur'an 81:1–3)

Of its effect upon people:

> *"O mankind! Be mindful of your Lord. Truly the quaking of the Hour is a tremendous thing (shayʾun ʿaẓīm). On the Day you see it, every nursing mother will forget her nursling, and every pregnant woman will deliver her burden, and you will see people as if drunk, though they are not drunk; but the punishment of God is severe."* (Qur'an 22:1–2)

Here the very word *ʿaẓīm* is used, and its content is spelled out: a terror so great that the strongest natural bond — a mother's attachment to her nursing infant — is forgotten. Of its length and severity:

> *"The angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a Day whose measure is fifty thousand years."* (Qur'an 70:4)

The Prophet ﷺ tied this fifty-thousand-year Day directly to the sin of withholding others' due, in the ḥadīth recorded by Muslim from Abū Hurayrah concerning those who do not pay zakāh on their wealth:

> *"There is no owner of gold or silver who does not pay what is due on it, except that on the Day of Resurrection plates of fire will be prepared for him, heated in the Fire of Hell, and his side, forehead, and back will be branded with them. Whenever they cool, they are reheated, on a Day whose measure is fifty thousand years, until judgment is passed among the servants..."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 987)

The theme is the same as this sūrah's: those who kept back what belonged to others will face a Day whose length alone is beyond human conception.

**The Difference Between Knowing and Feeling the Magnitude**

Every Muslim knows, as a matter of doctrine, that the Day of Judgment is tremendous. The problem this verse addresses is not ignorance but numbness. A fact repeated often enough loses its emotional force; one can recite "a tremendous Day" a thousand times and feel nothing. The Qur'an's remedy is to place the phrase precisely where it will cut: not in an abstract theological discussion, but immediately after a description of a small, everyday sin that the reader may well have committed this week. The juxtaposition is meant to reawaken feeling. The reader who has just recognized himself in the description of the defrauder — who has thought, "I do sometimes take a little more than I give" — now reads *unto a tremendous Day* with a different heart than the one who reads it in the abstract.

The early Muslims cultivated this feeling deliberately. It is reported that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb would recite verses about the Day of Judgment and weep until his beard was wet, and that when al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī was asked why the early believers seemed so grave, he replied that they felt the Hereafter as present. The scholars called this *istiḥḍār*, making present — bringing the tremendous Day into the mind with such vividness that the hand on the scale trembles.

**The Grammar of "Unto": *Li-Yawmin***

The preposition *li* ("unto, for") in *li-yawmin ʿaẓīm* deserves a note. The defrauders are to be resurrected *for* a Day — the Day is the purpose and destination of the resurrection. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that *li* here has the sense of "for the account and reckoning of" that Day. One is not merely raised and then happens to encounter a Day; one is raised *in order to* face it. Life, death, and resurrection are all oriented toward this appointment. The Qur'an says elsewhere: *"Did you think that We created you in vain, and that you would not be returned to Us?"* (23:115). The Day is what gives the whole sequence its purpose.

**A Reflection on Proportion for Everyday Life**

The practical teaching of this verse is the restoration of proportion. Human beings constantly miscalculate what is large and what is small. We treat a slight to our ego as enormous and a slight to another's right as trivial. We treat a financial loss as a catastrophe and a spiritual loss as nothing. We agonize over a delayed flight and shrug at a delayed prayer. The word *ʿaẓīm*, set against the grams of the defrauder, is a lesson in re-weighing. The believer is invited to ask, before every choice: on the scale of the tremendous Day, how much does this actually weigh? Applied honestly, the question shrinks most of our anxieties and enlarges most of our neglected duties. It is a method for seeing the world at its true size.

The next verse will name what makes the Day tremendous above all else: not the cosmic upheaval, not the fire, but the standing of mankind before the Lord of the worlds.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:6

> **a day when mankind shall stand before the Lord of the worlds?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Heart of the Day: Standing, Not Burning**

The sentence that began in verse 4 — *Do they not think that they will be resurrected* — reaches its climax here: *yawma yaqūmu al-nāsu li-Rabbi al-ʿālamīn*, "a Day when mankind shall stand before the Lord of the worlds." Notice what the Qur'an chose as the defining feature of the tremendous Day. It did not say "a Day when the wicked are thrown into Hell" or "a Day when the earth is split open." It said: a Day when people *stand* — upright, exposed, waiting — *before the Lord of all worlds*. The Qur'an locates the tremendousness of the Day not primarily in physical terror but in the confrontation between the creature and his Creator.

This choice is psychologically exact. The deepest human fear is not pain but exposure before one whose judgment matters absolutely. A child dreads his father's disappointed face more than the punishment; an honest employee dreads the moment his employer discovers the error more than the consequences. To *stand* before the Lord of the worlds, with every hidden transaction known, is the essence of the reckoning. Everything else — the fire, the scales, the record — flows from that standing.

**The Word *Yaqūmu*: To Rise, Stand, Be Established**

The verb *yaqūmu* is from *q-w-m*, one of the richest roots in Arabic. It gives *qiyām* (standing), *qiyāmah* (the Resurrection — literally "the Standing"), *qāʾim* (one who stands, or who maintains), *istiqāmah* (uprightness), and *qawwām* (one who stands up for something). The Day of Judgment is called *Yawm al-Qiyāmah*, "the Day of Standing," from this very verb. The standing has several dimensions that the commentators draw out.

It is standing *up from the graves* — the physical resurrection. It is standing *in the assembly* — the gathering of all mankind in one place, described in the Qur'an as *"the Day when We shall gather them all together"* (6:22). It is standing *at attention* — like servants before a king, or defendants before a judge, upright and unable to sit, hide, or leave. And it is standing *for the reckoning* — the presentation of one's account. Al-Ṭabarī explains: they will stand from their graves to the place of judgment, awaiting God's decree concerning them.

The Qur'an uses the same root for the defrauder's opposite. The believer is commanded to be *qawwāmīn bi-l-qisṭ*, "steadfast maintainers of justice" (4:135). The one who *stands* for justice in this world will *stand* in safety on the Day of Standing. The defrauder, who failed to stand for justice at the scale, will stand in dread before the One who sets the scales.

**"Before the Lord of the Worlds": The Name That Ends Every Excuse**

The Qur'an could have said "before God" or "before the Judge." It said *li-Rabbi al-ʿālamīn*, "before the Lord of the worlds" — the same title with which the Fātiḥah opens: *"Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds."* The choice carries several meanings.

*Rabb* is the Lord as owner, sustainer, master, and the one who nurtures things toward their completion. Everyone will stand before the One who owned them, fed them, gave them the very hands and scales with which they cheated. The defrauder cheated using capital he did not create, in a body he did not make, for a life he did not give himself. He will stand before the Owner of all of it.

*Al-ʿālamīn*, "the worlds," is a plural encompassing every category of created being — humans, jinn, angels, animals, the cosmos in its entirety. Standing before the Lord of all worlds means standing before the One to whom every witness is available. The customer who was cheated will be there. The angels who recorded the transaction will be there. The very earth on which the shop stood will testify: *"On that Day it will tell its news"* (99:4). Before the Lord of the worlds, there is no missing witness and no unrecorded fact.

And there is a rhetorical contrast in the title. The defrauder, at the moment of his crime, considered himself a little lord of his little stall, master of his measure, in control of the transaction. On that Day he will stand as one small creature among all the worlds, before the only Lord there is.

**The Physical Reality of That Standing: The Prophetic Descriptions**

The Prophet ﷺ described the standing of mankind on that Day in terms designed to make it imaginable. The source commentary cites the ḥadīth of the sweat. In its full form, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Ibn ʿUmar, the Prophet ﷺ recited this very verse and said:

> *"'A Day when mankind shall stand before the Lord of the worlds' — until one of them will be submerged in his sweat up to the middle of his ears."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4938; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2862)

And in another narration recorded by Muslim from al-Miqdād ibn al-Aswad:

> *"On the Day of Resurrection the sun will be brought near to the creatures until it is a mile away from them. People will be in sweat according to their deeds: some of them up to their ankles, some up to their knees, some up to their waists, and some with a bridle of sweat" — and the Messenger of God ﷺ pointed to his mouth.* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2864)

Several features of these descriptions are instructive. First, the sweat is "according to their deeds" — the standing is not uniform; it is proportioned to what each person carries. The defrauder's sweat is measured, as he measured. Second, the detail of the bridle — the sweat reaching the mouth like a bit in a horse's mouth — is an image of a person silenced and controlled, unable to speak his excuses. Third, the Prophet ﷺ explicitly connected this ḥadīth to *this verse*, which tells us how the earliest listeners understood the standing: as a prolonged, agonizing wait in which the body itself confesses its terror.

There is also the ḥadīth on the length of the standing, transmitted in the source commentary with various figures — fifty thousand years, a thousand years, three hundred, forty. The Qur'an gives the figure of fifty thousand years (70:4) and elsewhere a thousand years (32:5); the commentators reconcile these as referring to different phases of the Day, or to the Day's length as experienced by different people — for the believer it will pass "like the time between two prayers," as another report has it, while for the wrongdoer it stretches beyond bearing. The Qur'an itself notes that the experience of time on that Day is relative to the soul: *"the Day they see it, it will be as if they had tarried only an evening or its morning"* (79:46).

**Standing With No Intermediary**

An essential feature of this standing is that it is *direct*. Each person stands before the Lord of the worlds himself, without advocate, translator, or screen. The Prophet ﷺ said in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim:

> *"There is not one of you but that his Lord will speak to him, with no interpreter between them. He will look to his right and see nothing but what he sent forth; he will look to his left and see nothing but what he sent forth; he will look in front of him and see nothing but the Fire before his face. So protect yourselves from the Fire, even with half a date [given in charity]."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6539; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1016)

The closing counsel of this ḥadīth is precisely relevant to the defrauder: "even with half a date." The man who withheld half a measure will wish he had given half a date. Every small thing given will stand on his right; every small thing withheld will be absent — or worse, present as a debt.

**The Justice of the Standing**

The standing before the Lord of the worlds is the guarantee that the defrauder's victims will not be forgotten. In this world, the small theft succeeds precisely because the victim never knows, or knows and cannot prove it, or proves it and cannot afford to pursue it. The customer walks away with slightly less than he paid for and never finds out. That is the intolerable injustice that the Day of Standing corrects. The Qur'an promises:

> *"And We shall set up the just scales for the Day of Resurrection, so no soul will be wronged in anything. And if there be the weight of a mustard seed, We shall bring it forth. And We suffice as Reckoner."* (Qur'an 21:47)

*The weight of a mustard seed* — the exact scale of the defrauder's theft. The Qur'an has chosen its examples with care: the tiniest weight imaginable is the unit of divine accounting. Nothing the defrauder skimmed is too small to be brought forth; nothing his victim lost is too small to be restored.

This is also, importantly, a consolation. The person who has been cheated and could do nothing about it — the worker underpaid, the tenant overcharged, the customer shortchanged, the widow whose inheritance was quietly diminished — is told by this verse that a Day is coming when the account will be settled by the Lord of the worlds Himself. The believer need not carry the burden of vengeance; he may leave it at the door of that Standing.

**Why This Verse Is the Answer to the Sūrah's Opening**

The first six verses form a single unit, and this verse closes it. The structure is: *woe* to the defrauders (v. 1); *definition* of their double standard (vv. 2–3); *diagnosis* of the root cause — they do not reckon with resurrection (v. 4); and *the reality they ignore* — a tremendous Day (v. 5) on which they will stand before the Lord of the worlds (v. 6). The argument is complete. The remedy for dishonesty is not a better law but a clearer vision of the Standing. The person who sees himself standing before *Rabb al-ʿālamīn* does not need to be told to fill the measure. He fills it because he can already feel the sweat.

**Preparing for the Standing Now**

The scholars of the heart drew a practical counsel from this verse. Since the standing before God is certain, one should rehearse it. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is reported to have said:

> *"Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed; for it will be easier for you in the reckoning tomorrow if you take account of yourselves today. And adorn yourselves for the Great Presentation — 'On that Day you will be presented; not a secret of yours will be hidden' (69:18)."* (Recorded by al-Tirmidhī in his Jāmiʿ, in the chapter on the Day of Resurrection, and by Ibn Abī al-Dunyā)

"Weigh yourselves before you are weighed" — the vocabulary of the sūrah turned into a daily practice. The believer who, each night, reviews his transactions and his measures — literal and figurative — and corrects what he can, is already standing before the Lord of the worlds voluntarily, and so will stand there on that Day with less to dread. The defrauder postpones the standing until it is imposed. The wise person brings it forward while the scales are still in his own hands.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7

> **Nay! Truly the book of the profligate is in Sijjīn.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn Marked by *Kallā***

The second section of the sūrah opens with the emphatic particle *kallā*, "Nay!" This word occurs thirty-three times in the Qur'an, all in the second half of the muṣḥaf, mostly in the Makkan sūrahs, and it always performs a decisive function: it rebukes a preceding attitude and cuts it off. Here it rebukes the defrauders' assumption, exposed in verse 4, that they will not be resurrected or that their small thefts will pass unrecorded. *Kallā* — no, it is not as you imagine. What follows is the reality that their heedlessness ignores: there is a *book*, and it is kept in a place called *Sijjīn*.

The commentators note two nuances of *kallā*. It is a word of *rebuke* (*radʿ*): "Stop! Desist from that thought and that conduct." And it is a word of *affirmation* of what follows, functioning like *ḥaqqan*, "truly." Both senses operate here. The defrauder is told to stop, and then told the truth that makes stopping necessary.

**From "Defrauders" to "Profligate": The Widening of the Lens**

Verse 1 spoke of *al-muṭaffifīn*, the defrauders. Verse 7 speaks of *al-fujjār*, the profligate or wicked. The shift is significant. Having begun with one specific sin, the sūrah now widens its lens to the whole category to which that sin belongs. The defrauder is one instance of the *fājir*; the *fājir* is the general type.

The root *f-j-r* means "to split open, to burst forth" — it gives *fajr*, the dawn, when light bursts through darkness, and *infajara*, to gush forth (as water from a rock). A *fājir* is one who has "burst through" the restraints of God's law, one whose desires flow unchecked like a broken dam. The word therefore captures not merely a person who commits a particular sin but a person whose whole orientation is toward transgression — one who has broken the boundary and no longer feels its restraint. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī defines *fujūr* as "the tearing of the veil of religion," and the Qur'an pairs it with its opposite in one of its most famous verses: *"And [by] the soul and He who fashioned it, and inspired it with its wickedness (fujūrahā) and its God-consciousness (taqwāhā)"* (91:7–8). Every soul has been given knowledge of both roads; the *fājir* is one who took the road of bursting through.

By naming the defrauder a *fājir*, the Qur'an refuses to allow his sin to be compartmentalized. He may tell himself that his short-measuring is a minor commercial matter; the Qur'an says it belongs to the same family as every other tearing of the veil, and it will be recorded in the same book.

**The Book: What Is Written and By Whom**

*Kitāb al-fujjār*, "the book of the profligate," refers to the record of their deeds. The Qur'an teaches consistently that every human action, word, and even intention is recorded by angelic scribes:

> *"And indeed, over you are keepers, noble writers, who know whatever you do."* (Qur'an 82:10–12)

> *"Not a word does he utter but that with him is an observer ready."* (Qur'an 50:18)

> *"And every small and great thing is inscribed."* (Qur'an 54:53)

This last verse is especially pertinent: *every small thing*. The defrauder relies on smallness; the record does not recognize smallness as a category of exemption. Al-Qurṭubī observes that the mention of the book here directly answers the defrauder's implicit belief that his small shortfalls are unnoticed. They are not only noticed; they are written, and the writing is kept in a designated place.

The commentators differ on whether "the book of the profligate" means the *individual* records of each wicked person — which are gathered in Sijjīn — or a *collective* register in which the names and deeds of all the wicked are inscribed. Al-Ṭabarī inclines toward the view that their deeds and their persons are registered in a place of confinement; others hold that the individual scrolls are deposited there. Both readings agree on the essential point: the record exists, it is permanent, and it is stored in a place whose name signifies its nature.

**Sijjīn: The Name and Its Meaning**

*Sijjīn* is one of the rare Qur'anic proper nouns that occurs only here (vv. 7–8). Its form is *fiʿʿīl*, an intensive pattern, derived — according to nearly all the lexicographers and commentators, including al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and Ibn Kathīr — from *sijn*, "prison." *Sijjīn*, then, means something like "the great confinement," "the deep dungeon," "the place of intense imprisonment." The pattern *fiʿʿīl* is used in Arabic for intensity: *ṣiddīq* (intensely truthful), *shirrīr* (intensely evil). So *Sijjīn* is not merely a prison but *the* prison, prison in its ultimate degree.

The imagery is exact. The defrauder confined others — he restricted, diminished, held back what was theirs. His record is now held in the place of ultimate confinement. And the prison image speaks to the nature of sin itself: the *fājir* thought he was breaking free of restraint (that is what *fujūr*, "bursting through," implies); in reality he was building his own prison. The one who "frees" himself from God's law imprisons himself in the consequences.

**Where Is Sijjīn? The Reports of the Early Scholars**

The source commentary records several views, and they are worth setting out because together they illuminate the verse.

The most widely transmitted view, reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, Kaʿb al-Aḥbār, Mujāhid, Qatādah, and others, is that Sijjīn is *in the lowest depths* — beneath the seventh earth. Ibn Kathīr cites the report that "Sijjīn is beneath the seventh earth," and connects it with the long ḥadīth of al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib on the journey of the soul after death, recorded by Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd, and others, in which the Prophet ﷺ describes how the soul of the disbeliever, after being taken by the angels, is carried up to the lowest heaven, the gates are not opened for it, and God says:

> *"Write his book in Sijjīn, in the lowest earth." Then his soul is thrown down.* (Musnad Aḥmad 18534; Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4753; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Ḥākim and others)

This ḥadīth is the clearest textual basis for the location of Sijjīn. It shows that the "book" of verse 7 is connected to the soul's fate immediately after death, and that Sijjīn is a place of descent — the opposite direction from ʿIlliyyūn, to which the righteous soul's record is raised (v. 18).

Some connected Sijjīn to *"the lowest of the low"* (*asfala sāfilīn*, 95:5) to which man is reduced when he abandons faith, and to the *"narrow place"* (*makānan ḍayyiqan*, 25:13) into which the disbelievers are thrown, chained together, in Hell. All these images share the sense of downwardness, constriction, and confinement.

A second interpretation, recorded by al-Qurṭubī, al-Qushayrī, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī, takes *Sijjīn* as a *description of the book itself* rather than a place: the book of the wicked is "a confining record," a register that binds them, "sealed" so that nothing can be erased. On this reading, verse 9 — *a book inscribed* — is the explanation of Sijjīn.

A third view, reported from some early authorities, holds that Sijjīn is a stone or rock beneath the seventh earth, or the "lowest of the earths" where the souls of the wicked are gathered. Al-Ṭabarī preferred the reading that Sijjīn is the lowest earth, the place of confinement for the records and souls of the wicked, and considered this best supported by the transmitted reports.

The soundest course, as with all such matters of the Unseen, is that of the majority of the commentators: to affirm what the texts affirm — that Sijjīn is a real place of the lowest depth and confinement where the record of the wicked is kept — without insisting on details God has not disclosed. The next verse, *And what will apprise thee of Sijjīn?*, is itself a warning against claiming to know more than has been revealed.

**Why the Record Is Kept Low**

The symbolism of the low place is not arbitrary. Throughout the Qur'an, the spatial language of height and depth carries moral meaning. God is *"the Most High"*; the righteous are "raised in degrees"; the good word "ascends to Him" (35:10). Conversely, the wicked are "brought low," and man without faith is "reduced to the lowest of the low." The *fājir* sought elevation in this world — wealth, status, the upper hand in every transaction — through means that debased him. His record is filed exactly where his conduct placed him: at the bottom.

There is also a note of humiliation. The defrauder's records — the ledgers of his profits, the accounts of his clever dealings — were, in this world, perhaps a source of pride. In the next, they are cast into the lowest prison, a filing of shame. Al-Rāzī notes that the very placement of the book is a form of judgment before the judgment.

**The Practical Weight of Knowing There Is a Book**

The verse's practical teaching is straightforward and serious. Every small thing is recorded, permanently, in a place of confinement, pending the Day of Standing. The believer who internalizes this is not paralyzed but liberated: he knows that nothing good he does is lost either (as the mirror verse about ʿIlliyyūn will affirm), and he is freed from the illusion that concealment equals safety. Consider how a person behaves when he knows a transaction is being recorded — on video, in an audit trail, in a signed contract. He becomes careful. The Qur'an asks him to recognize that *every* transaction is recorded, by writers more precise than any camera, in a book kept in Sijjīn. The one who lives with this knowledge measures honestly not from fear of the customer's suspicion, but from awareness of the scribe at his shoulder. That awareness is called *iḥsān* — to worship God as though you see Him, and if you do not see Him, to know that He sees you.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8

> **And what will apprise thee of Sijjīn?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Qur'anic Formula of Magnitude**

The verse *wa mā adrāka mā Sijjīn* — "And what will apprise thee of Sijjīn?" — belongs to a distinctive Qur'anic formula. The phrase *wa mā adrāka mā...* ("and what will make you know what ... is?") appears thirteen times in the Qur'an, always in the Makkan sūrahs, and always attached to something of immense gravity: *"And what will apprise thee of the Day of Judgment?"* (82:17–18, repeated); *"And what will apprise thee of the Night of Power?"* (97:2); *"And what will apprise thee of the Calamity?"* (101:3); *"And what will apprise thee of the steep ascent?"* (90:12); *"And what will apprise thee of the Crushing Fire?"* (104:5).

The function of the formula is to *magnify* and to *arrest*. It stops the reader before a thing whose reality exceeds ordinary knowledge, and it says: you cannot grasp this by yourself; you need to be told. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī note that it is a rhetorical device of *tafkhīm* (magnification) and *tahwīl* (inspiring awe). Applied to Sijjīn, it tells the defrauder, who took his small thefts so lightly: the place where your record is filed is a thing whose gravity you cannot begin to imagine.

**The Difference Between *Adrāka* and *Yudrīka***

The scholars of Qur'anic language, following Ibn ʿAbbās and later Sufyān ibn ʿUyaynah, observed a subtle distinction. Whenever the Qur'an says *wa mā adrāka* ("what has apprised you," in the past tense), God goes on to *explain* the thing. Whenever it says *wa mā yudrīka* ("what would apprise you," in the future or conditional), He does *not* explain it — as in *"And what would apprise thee? Perhaps the Hour is near"* (33:63) and *"And what would apprise thee? Perhaps he might be purified"* (80:3). The rule holds throughout the Qur'an. Here the form is *adrāka*, and accordingly the next verse follows with the explanation: *A book inscribed.*

This linguistic regularity is itself a small sign of the Qur'an's precision. Across twenty-three years of revelation, in sūrahs of vastly different contexts, one tense signals "explanation follows" and the other signals "this remains hidden." It is the kind of consistency that would be nearly impossible to maintain by human design in oral composition, and the early scholars pointed to it as one of the fine marks of the divine text.

**Addressed to the Prophet ﷺ — and Through Him, to Everyone**

The "thee" of the verse is, grammatically, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. The Qur'an says to its own Messenger: what could make *you* know what Sijjīn is? The point is humbling and instructive. If even the Prophet ﷺ — the most knowledgeable of creation about the Unseen, the recipient of revelation itself — is told that Sijjīn is beyond what he could know unaided, then how much more is it beyond the defrauder who does not even suppose he will be resurrected?

But as with all such addresses to the Prophet ﷺ, the address extends to every reader. The commentators regularly note that *khiṭāb al-Nabī khiṭāb li-ummatih*: what is addressed to the Prophet is addressed to his community. Every person who recites the verse is being asked: what do *you* know of Sijjīn? And the honest answer is: nothing, except what God tells me. This is the correct posture of a creature before the realities of the Unseen.

**A Lesson in the Limits of Knowledge**

The verse teaches something essential about the *epistemology* of the Hereafter — how we come to know anything about it at all. The Hereafter is *ghayb*, unseen. It cannot be reached by observation, experiment, or reasoning from analogy; human beings have no access to it except through revelation. The verse's rhetorical question emphasizes this: the only source of knowledge about Sijjīn is God's own telling. Whatever the defrauder imagines about the consequences of his conduct — and he imagines very little — is irrelevant. The reality is what God says it is.

This has a direct bearing on the defrauder's error. His entire practice rests on an unspoken confidence that he understands the consequences of his actions: a small gain, a small risk, no lasting harm. The verse strips away that confidence. You do not know what follows from the short measure. You do not know what Sijjīn is. You have been living as though the consequences were fully visible to you, when in fact the most important consequences are entirely hidden and are known only to the One who is now telling you about them.

The Qur'an makes this point about human presumption elsewhere:

> *"And they have no knowledge of it. They follow nothing but conjecture, and conjecture avails nothing against the truth."* (Qur'an 53:28)

The defrauder follows conjecture — "no one will notice, it does not matter" — against the truth of Sijjīn.

**Why God Reveals This to Us at All**

If Sijjīn is beyond human knowledge, why does God tell us of it? The answer is mercy. God reveals the existence and the nature of Sijjīn precisely so that people may avoid having their record placed there. Knowledge of consequences is the precondition of responsible choice. A road sign warning of a cliff ahead is not a threat; it is a kindness. The Qur'an's descriptions of Hell and of Sijjīn are, in this sense, the most merciful passages in the Book, because they give the traveler the information he needs before he reaches the edge — and *ṭaff*, the root of the sūrah's name, means "edge."

The Prophet ﷺ described his own role in exactly this way, in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"My likeness and the likeness of what God has sent me with is that of a man who came to a people and said, 'O people! I have seen the army with my own eyes, and I am the naked warner, so save yourselves!'"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6482; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2283)

The "naked warner" (*al-nadhīr al-ʿuryān*) in Arab custom was one who stripped off his garment and waved it to signal an imminent raid. God's telling us of Sijjīn is that warning, and the question *what will apprise thee?* is the warner's way of saying: you would never have known of this danger without me.

**The Two Readings of the Question in Relation to Verse 9**

The commentators divide over what exactly the following verse explains. On the majority view, the question *What will apprise thee of Sijjīn?* is followed by an explanation of the *book*: "[It is] a book inscribed" — meaning that the thing whose gravity you cannot imagine is the sealed, indelible record itself. On the minority view (recorded by al-Rāzī), verse 9 describes *Sijjīn* as a place whose nature is that it holds an inscribed record. Either way, the sequence performs the same movement: from the defrauder's carelessness, to the announcement of a record, to the magnification of that record's gravity, to its precise description. Each step tightens the noose of accountability.

**The Psychological Effect of Deferred Explanation**

There is a psychological wisdom in the Qur'an's method here. Rather than simply stating "the book of the wicked is a sealed record in the lowest prison," it announces a name — *Sijjīn* — pauses to ask "what do you know of it?" — and only then explains. The pause creates a moment of suspense and self-examination. In that moment, the listener confronts his own ignorance and feels the weight of what is about to be disclosed. Modern communication research confirms what the Qur'an practiced: information delivered after a question that exposes the listener's ignorance is retained far more deeply than information simply announced. The defrauder who hears "there is a place called Sijjīn — and what do you know of it?" cannot easily dismiss what follows.

**Application: Humility Before Consequences**

The practical lesson is a discipline of humility about consequences. Every person who is tempted to a hidden wrong performs a quick, unconscious calculation: what will follow from this? The verse tells us that this calculation is always incomplete, because the most important consequences lie in a realm we cannot see. The believer therefore learns to distrust his own confident assessment that "nothing will come of it" and to defer instead to the One who knows what Sijjīn is. This humility is not fearfulness; it is realism — the same realism that makes a wise traveler trust the map over his own guess about what lies over the next ridge. The map, in this case, has been drawn by the Lord of the worlds, and it marks a place called Sijjīn.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9

> **A book inscribed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Question**

Following the Qur'anic rule that *wa mā adrāka* is always followed by an explanation, this verse supplies it: *kitābun marqūm* — "a book inscribed." Two words, and the mystery of Sijjīn is not so much dissolved as sharpened. What awaits the defrauder in the lowest depths is not vagueness but *writing* — precise, legible, permanent writing. The record of his life is not a rumor or an impression; it is a document.

The commentators, as the source notes, differ on whether this describes the book of the wicked (the majority: al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Ṭabrisī) or Sijjīn itself (a minority view). On the majority reading, the sentence structure is: "The book of the wicked is in Sijjīn — and what is [that book]? — a book inscribed." On the minority reading, Sijjīn is itself described as a "writing inscribed" — a register. The difference is small; both readings insist that the essential reality is an inscribed record.

**The Meaning of *Marqūm***

The word *marqūm* is the passive participle of *raqama*, from the root *r-q-m*. The root's basic meaning is to mark, to write clearly, to inscribe with distinct characters. Its derivatives include *raqm*, a number or figure (the modern Arabic word for a numeral); *raqīm*, an inscription or tablet (as in *aṣḥāb al-kahfi wa-l-raqīm*, "the Companions of the Cave and the Inscription," 18:9); and *tarqīm*, marking or numbering. Some linguists say *raqm* originally referred to the embroidered or marked pattern on a garment — a marking woven into the fabric so it cannot be removed.

From this root the commentators derive several shades of meaning for *marqūm*, and each adds to the picture.

First, *marqūm* means *written clearly*, in large, bold, legible script — writing that cannot be misread. Al-Qatādah said: *marqūm* means *maktūb*, "written." The defrauder's deeds are not recorded in faint or ambiguous marks; they are inscribed so that there can be no dispute about what they say.

Second, *marqūm* means *sealed* (*makhtūm*), according to al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī: the writing is finalized and closed, "so that nothing can be added to it or taken from it." The sealing has two implications. Nothing can be *added*: the defrauder will not be charged with what he did not do; the record is just. And nothing can be *erased*: he cannot bribe the scribe, tear out a page, or explain it away. The seal is the guarantee of both justice and inescapability.

Third, some commentators (the source cites al-Qushayrī) understand *marqūm* as meaning that the record *distinguishes* — it marks what is good and what is evil, sorting each deed into its category, as a merchant marks goods with their prices and qualities. The record is not a heap; it is a classified ledger.

Fourth, the root's association with *numbering* suggests to some that the record is *counted*, quantified — the exact opposite of the defrauder's vague, unquantified shortfalls. He fudged the numbers; the record has the numbers exactly.

**The Irony of the Merchant's Ledger**

There is a deep irony in this verse that the language makes visible. The word *raqm* is the vocabulary of commerce. A merchant *marks* his goods, *numbers* his accounts, keeps *ledgers*. The defrauder, of all people, understood the power of the written record — he kept his own books carefully, no doubt showing profits he had obtained by short measures. Now he learns that there is another ledger, kept by scribes he cannot bribe, in which every one of his transactions is *marqūm* — marked, numbered, sealed. The instrument of his trade has become the instrument of his judgment.

The Qur'an makes the same point with even greater force in Sūrah al-Kahf, describing the moment the record is opened:

> *"And the book will be placed, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it, and they will say, 'Woe to us! What is this book that leaves neither small nor great but that it has enumerated it?' And they will find what they did present. And your Lord wrongs no one."* (Qur'an 18:49)

"Neither small nor great" — the defrauder's small thefts are exactly what the guilty will find enumerated. And the verse ends with the guarantee that makes the record trustworthy: "your Lord wrongs no one." The record is terrifying to the wicked precisely because it is *perfectly accurate*. It contains no false charges. It simply contains everything.

**The Doctrine of the Recording Angels**

This verse rests on a foundational Islamic belief: that every human being is accompanied by angels who record his deeds. The Qur'an states:

> *"When the two receivers receive, seated on the right and on the left. Not a word does he utter but that with him is an observer ready."* (Qur'an 50:17–18)

> *"And indeed, over you are keepers, noble writers, who know whatever you do."* (Qur'an 82:10–12)

The commentators explain that the angel on the right records good deeds and the angel on the left records evil deeds. The Prophet ﷺ described the mercy built into the process in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"God has written down the good deeds and the evil deeds. Then He explained them: whoever intends a good deed and does not do it, God writes it down with Him as a complete good deed; and if he intends it and does it, God writes it down with Him as ten good deeds up to seven hundred times, up to many times more. And whoever intends an evil deed and does not do it, God writes it down with Him as a complete good deed; and if he intends it and does it, God writes it down as one evil deed."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6491; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 131)

This ḥadīth transforms the meaning of "a book inscribed" for the believer. The same record that is a prison for the wicked is a treasury for the righteous — because God's accounting multiplies the good and counts evil only once. The defrauder's problem is not that the record is harsh; it is that he has filled it with evil that he thought was too small to count, and left it empty of the good he thought was too small to matter.

**The Individual Record and Its Presentation**

The "book" of this verse is connected to the individual record each person receives on the Day of Judgment. The Qur'an describes the scene:

> *"And every human being — We have fastened his record to his neck, and We shall bring forth for him on the Day of Resurrection a book he will find spread open. 'Read your book! Sufficient is your own soul this Day as a reckoner against you.'"* (Qur'an 17:13–14)

The image of the record "fastened to his neck" conveys that the record is inseparable from the person — it is, in a sense, *him*, the written form of his life. And the command *"Read your book"* tells us that the record will be legible to its subject: it is *marqūm*, clearly written. The defrauder will read his own transactions in his own record and will be his own judge. There is a profound justice in this: no external accuser is needed; the wrongdoer's own life, faithfully written, condemns him.

Then comes the moment the sūrah's later verses will describe in detail. The wicked receive their record in the left hand or behind their backs (69:25; 84:10); the righteous receive theirs in the right hand (69:19; 84:7). The book that was filed in Sijjīn is handed back to the one who wrote its contents with his deeds.

**Why an Indelible Record Is a Mercy Before It Is a Terror**

It would be a mistake to read this verse only as a threat. The doctrine of the inscribed record is, first of all, the foundation of *hope* for every person who has been wronged. The worker whose wages were skimmed and who had no recourse; the orphan whose inheritance was quietly reduced by a trustee; the customer who was cheated and never knew — all of them are told by this verse that nothing was lost. It is *marqūm*. It is sealed. It cannot be erased by the passage of time, the death of witnesses, or the power of the wrongdoer. This is the Qur'anic answer to one of the oldest human despairs: the sense that most injustice in history goes unrecorded and unpunished. It does not. It is written in a book that will be opened.

And for the wrongdoer, the record is a mercy in a different way: it is announced *now*, while there is still time. God tells the defrauder that his book is being written so that he may change what is written in it. The Qur'an promises that repentance does not merely add good deeds to the record; it *transforms* the bad ones:

> *"Except for those who repent and believe and do righteous work — for them, God will replace their evil deeds with good ones. And God is ever Forgiving and Merciful."* (Qur'an 25:70)

The seal of the record, then, is final only at death. Until then, the writing on the page is still responsive to the pen of repentance.

**The Practical Consequence: Living as a Person Whose Life Is Being Written**

The practical teaching of *kitābun marqūm* is to live as one whose every act is being clearly, permanently written. This is not paranoia; it is the ordinary attitude of a person of integrity. Consider how a professional behaves when he knows his emails are archived, his hours are logged, and his decisions are documented for audit: he becomes careful, honest, and thorough — not because he is bad, but because the presence of a record makes visible what carelessness hides. The believer extends this attitude to every moment of life, because he knows the record is not confined to the office. The scale in the shop, the words spoken in anger at home, the glance given in private, the promise made and broken — all are *marqūm*.

The response to this knowledge is not fear alone but *purposeful writing*. If the book is being written regardless, the wise person takes charge of what goes into it. He fills it with full measures given, kind words spoken, prayers completed, debts paid, faults forgiven — so that when he is told "Read your book," he reads with joy rather than dread. That is the whole difference between Sijjīn and ʿIlliyyūn, and the sūrah is about to draw it in full.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:10

> **Woe that Day to the deniers,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Woe and Its Precise Target**

The sūrah began with *wayl* pronounced upon the defrauders. Now, having established the record and the tremendous Day, it pronounces a second *wayl*: *waylun yawmaʾidhin li-l-mukadhdhibīn* — "Woe that Day to the deniers." The repetition of the word links the two groups. The defrauder of verse 1 and the denier of verse 10 are not two different people; they are the same person seen from two angles. Verse 1 described what he *does* with his hands; verse 10 describes what he *believes* in his heart. The sūrah has already told us that his cheating flowed from his failure to reckon with resurrection (v. 4); now it names that failure: he is a *mukadhdhib*, a denier.

The addition of *yawmaʾidhin*, "that Day," locates the woe in time. In verse 1 the woe was pronounced absolutely, as a standing decree. Here it is pinned to the Day of Standing described in verse 6. The woe that was decreed in this world will be *executed* on that Day.

**The Meaning of *Mukadhdhib***

The word *mukadhdhib* is the active participle of *kadhdhaba*, the second (intensive) form of the root *k-dh-b*, "to lie." The first form, *kadhaba*, means "to lie, to speak falsehood." The second form, *kadhdhaba*, means "to call something a lie, to declare it false, to reject it as untrue." A *mukadhdhib* is thus not merely someone who does not believe; he is someone who actively *labels the truth a lie*. There is a difference between the person who is uncertain, the person who has not yet heard, and the person who has heard and pronounces the message false. The Qur'an reserves *takdhīb* for the last.

This precision matters for understanding divine justice. God does not pronounce woe upon those who have simply not been reached by the message; the Qur'an states clearly, *"We never punish until We have sent a messenger"* (17:15). The woe is for those who received the message and rejected it as a lie. The next verse will specify what they denied — the Day of Judgment — and the verse after that will specify who does such denying — "every sinful transgressor."

**The Refrain of Sūrah al-Mursalāt**

As the source commentary observes, this verse is nearly identical to the refrain that runs through Sūrah al-Mursalāt (77), where *"Woe that Day to the deniers"* is repeated ten times, and it also appears in Sūrah al-Ṭūr: *"Woe that Day to the deniers, who play in idle talk"* (52:11–12). In al-Mursalāt, each repetition of the refrain follows a different proof of God's power and mercy — the creation of man from a despised fluid, the earth as a gathering place, the mountains, the sweet water — and after each proof the refrain falls like a hammer: you were shown this sign, and you denied it. Woe to you.

The echo of that refrain here does something important. It places the defrauders of Madinah and Makkah in the same category as every denier in every age. Their sin of short-measuring is not a local commercial problem; it is a particular expression of the universal human refusal to accept accountability before God. The refrain, familiar to any listener who knew al-Mursalāt, would have sounded in their ears as a summons to a much larger court.

**Denial as the Root of Every Injustice**

The placement of this verse in the sūrah's argument is instructive. Look at the sequence: (1) the defrauders and their double standard; (2) the question — do they not think they will be resurrected?; (3) the tremendous Day and the Standing; (4) the inscribed record in Sijjīn; (5) woe to the *deniers*. The sūrah has traced the crime backward to its root. The hand that shortens the measure is moved by a heart that denies the Day.

This is a general principle of Qur'anic ethics: injustice toward creation is, at its root, a denial of the Creator's accounting. The Qur'an states it most directly in Sūrah al-Māʿūn:

> *"Have you seen the one who denies the Judgment (yukadhdhibu bi-l-dīn)? That is the one who repulses the orphan and does not urge the feeding of the poor."* (Qur'an 107:1–3)

There the *takdhīb* (denial) of the Judgment is defined not by a theological statement but by *conduct*: pushing away the orphan, failing to feed the poor. The one who denies the Judgment is identified by how he treats the weak. Al-Muṭaffifīn applies the same logic to commerce: the one who denies the Judgment is identified by how he fills the measure. Denial is not primarily a belief; it is a way of living.

**The Woe Is for Denial, Not for Doubt or Struggle**

A clarification is needed here for the sincere believer who may read this verse with anxiety. The woe is pronounced upon *deniers* — those who reject the truth and call it a lie. It is not pronounced upon those who struggle with doubts, who ask questions, who find certain matters difficult to understand, or who fall into sin while still believing. The Qur'an distinguishes sharply between the believer who sins (and who is called to repentance) and the denier who rejects (and who is threatened with woe). The Companions themselves confessed moments of doubt and were reassured by the Prophet ﷺ; when some of them told him that they experienced thoughts they would rather be burned to ashes than speak, he replied, as recorded by Muslim:

> *"That is the clear sign of faith."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 132)

The very distress they felt at such thoughts proved that they were believers, not deniers. Takdhīb is the settled, willful rejection of the message, expressed in the defiant conduct of the *fājir*. It is not the passing shadow that crosses a sincere heart.

**Why the Woe Is Repeated**

Some may ask why the Qur'an repeats *wayl* rather than saying it once. The repetition serves several purposes. It marks a structural boundary: verse 1's woe opened the first section; verse 10's woe opens the sūrah's central diagnosis (vv. 10–17). It intensifies: a warning given twice is a warning meant seriously. And it clarifies: the first woe might have been heard as an economic censure; the second, addressed to *deniers*, reveals the theological heart of the matter. The Qur'an frequently uses repetition in this way — not as redundancy but as escalation, each occurrence adding a dimension the previous one lacked.

**Denial in the Modern Form**

In the seventh century, denial of the Judgment was often explicit: the Makkans mocked the idea that bones turned to dust would live again. In the modern world, denial is more often *practical* than *theoretical*. Many people would not say, if asked, that there is no accounting; they would say they believe in some form of justice beyond this life. But they live — in their business, their treatment of employees, their private conduct — exactly as if there were none. The Qur'an's diagnosis applies to them with full force, because it defines *takdhīb* by conduct. The person who professes belief in the Day of Judgment while systematically skimming from others is, in the Qur'an's terms, a practical denier — his hands contradict his tongue, and God judges by the hands.

This is a sobering standard, and it is meant to be. It denies the comfort of a merely verbal faith. It asks each person to look at his transactions, his measures, and his treatment of those who cannot hold him accountable, and to read from them what he really believes about the Day of Standing.

**The Consolation Hidden in the Warning**

Yet even this verse of woe carries a consolation. If woe belongs *that Day* to the deniers, then *that Day* also belongs to those who affirmed. The believers who were cheated, mocked, and dismissed as naive will be present on the Day when the deniers receive their woe — and the sūrah's final verses will describe them, on couches, watching. The verse tells the wronged believer: the Day on which you will be vindicated is the same Day on which the denier's woe is executed. The two are one event, and it is coming.

The next verse will specify the object of the denial, and in doing so will show that the deniers' error is not an abstract theological mistake but the rejection of the very thing that gives human life its seriousness: the Day of Judgment.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:11

> **who deny the Day of Judgment,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Naming the Object of Denial**

The previous verse pronounced woe upon *the deniers* without specifying what they deny. This verse supplies it: *alladhīna yukadhdhibūna bi-yawmi al-dīn* — "who deny the Day of Judgment." The clause is a relative one, describing the deniers of verse 10, and the specification is crucial. The Qur'an does not say "who deny God," although most deniers of the Judgment also fail in their conception of God; nor "who deny the Prophet," although they do. It names *the Day of Judgment* specifically, because it is denial of *that* Day which most directly produces the conduct condemned at the sūrah's opening. A person may affirm a Creator in the abstract and still cheat; but a person who truly affirms that he will be *judged* by that Creator, on a Day, for every measure, does not cheat.

The verb *yukadhdhibūna* is in the imperfect (present-continuous) tense, indicating an ongoing, habitual, characteristic attitude — not a single act of rejection but a way of being. They *keep on* denying. This matches the intensive participle *mukadhdhibīn* of the previous verse: their denial is their settled stance.

**The Meaning of *Yawm al-Dīn***

*Yawm al-dīn*, "the Day of Judgment," is the same phrase that appears in the Fātiḥah: *"Master of the Day of Judgment"* (1:4). The word *dīn* has a remarkable range in Arabic. It means *religion* (as in *"Truly the religion (dīn) with God is Islam,"* 3:19); it means *obedience* and *submission*; it means *custom* or *way*; and, crucially here, it means *recompense, requital, judgment* — the settling of accounts. The Arabs said *kamā tadīnu tudān*, "as you requite, you will be requited," using *dīn* for the paying back of what is owed.

These meanings are connected, and the connection illuminates the verse. *Dīn* as religion is a system of obedience to God; *dīn* as judgment is the settling of accounts for that obedience or its lack. The Day of *Dīn* is the day on which *dīn* (religion, submission) receives its *dīn* (recompense). The word thus contains, within itself, the whole moral logic of accountability: the way one lived and the requital one receives are named by a single word, because they are, in reality, a single thing seen at two moments in time.

For the defrauder, the relevance is exact. He understood *dīn* in the sense of "debt" and "requital" perfectly well in commerce; he knew what it meant to be owed and to pay back. The Qur'an tells him there is a *Yawm al-dīn*, a Day of Paying Back, on which the ledger of his life will be settled by the same principle he applied to his customers — only this time he is the one who owes.

**Why Denial of the Judgment Is the Critical Error**

The Qur'an treats belief in the Last Day as one of the pillars of faith, and this verse illustrates why it is not a peripheral doctrine. Consider what belief in the Judgment establishes:

It establishes that *actions have ultimate consequences*. Without a Judgment, the moral difference between the honest merchant and the defrauder is, in the end, only a matter of preference or social approval; both die and are forgotten. With a Judgment, the difference is eternal and absolute.

It establishes that *hidden actions matter as much as public ones*. Human justice can only judge what is seen. Divine Judgment sees everything. The defrauder's entire method relies on the gap between what is seen and what is done; the Judgment closes that gap.

It establishes that *the wronged will be vindicated*. Without a Judgment, most injustice in history is simply lost — the victims die uncompensated, the oppressors die unpunished. With a Judgment, no wrong is final.

And it establishes that *this life is a test, not a destination*. The Qur'an says: *"He who created death and life to test you, which of you is best in deed"* (67:2). Denial of the Judgment removes the test and turns life into an arena of mere acquisition — which is precisely the worldview of the defrauder, for whom the only question is how much can be gained.

Ibn al-Qayyim, in discussing the foundations of moral conduct, observes that the strength of a person's virtue is proportional to the strength of his belief in the Hereafter: weaken the second, and the first collapses; strengthen the second, and the first becomes effortless. This verse names the collapse at its source.

**How the Makkans Denied the Judgment**

The Qur'an records the arguments of those who denied the Day of Judgment, and they are worth recalling because they recur in every age. They argued from *physical impossibility*:

> *"And they say, 'When we are bones and crumbled particles, will we truly be resurrected as a new creation?'"* (Qur'an 17:49)

They argued from the *absence of precedent*:

> *"Does he promise you that when you have died and become dust and bones, you will be brought forth? Far, far off is what you are promised! There is nothing but our worldly life; we die and we live, and we will not be resurrected."* (Qur'an 23:35–37)

And they argued from *mockery*:

> *"And they say, 'When is this promise, if you are truthful?'"* (Qur'an 10:48)

The Qur'an answers each. To the argument from impossibility, it points to the first creation: *"Say: He will give them life who produced them the first time, and He is Knowing of every creation"* (36:79). To the argument from precedent, it points to the annual resurrection of dead earth by rain: *"And you see the earth barren, but when We send down upon it rain, it quivers and swells and grows something of every beautiful kind"* (22:5). To mockery, it answers with the certainty of the appointed time: *"Indeed, they see it as distant, but We see it as near"* (70:6–7).

The logic of these answers is simple: the One who made you from nothing can remake you from dust, and He demonstrates the principle every spring. Denial of the Judgment is not a rational conclusion; it is a refusal to follow a plain inference, motivated — as the next verse will say — by the desire to sin without consequence.

**The Difference Between Denying and Forgetting**

A practical nuance should be drawn here. The verse condemns those who *deny* the Day of Judgment. But many who would never deny it in words nevertheless *forget* it in practice — a condition the Qur'an calls *ghaflah*, heedlessness, and *nisyān*, forgetting. Of the people of Hell, God says: *"So taste [the punishment] because you forgot the meeting of this Day of yours; indeed, We have forgotten you"* (32:14). Forgetting is treated as culpable because the Day was announced clearly and repeatedly; to forget it is to have chosen not to keep it in mind.

The believer's task, then, is not merely to avoid *takdhīb* (denial) but to cultivate *dhikr* (remembrance) of the Day. The Prophet ﷺ counseled this directly, in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Tirmidhī and al-Nasāʾī:

> *"Remember often the destroyer of pleasures — death."* (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2307; Sunan al-Nasāʾī 1824; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

Remembrance of death is the gateway to remembrance of the Judgment. The person who keeps the Day of *Dīn* in view does not need to be watched at the scale.

**The Social Meaning of Believing in the Day of Judgment**

There is a social dimension to this verse that is easy to miss. A community in which belief in the Day of Judgment is widespread and sincere is a community in which trust is possible without constant surveillance. Every honest transaction that happens without a witness, every debt repaid that could have been evaded, every promise kept that could have been broken, is a small gift of belief in the Judgment to the social order. Conversely, a society of practical deniers must replace conscience with cameras, contracts, and courts — and these can never fully substitute for the inner witness. The Qur'an's insistence on the Day of *Dīn* is thus not only a doctrine about the future; it is the foundation of a trustworthy present.

**Reading the Verse as a Question to Oneself**

The believer who reads this verse should not only think of the Makkan deniers. He should ask: in what areas of my life do I behave as a practical denier of the Day of *Dīn*? Where do I act as though my conduct will not be requited? The honest answer usually points to exactly the domains the sūrah opened with — the small, private, unverifiable dealings where the temptation to skim is strongest. And the remedy is exactly what the sūrah prescribes: to bring the Day of Judgment vividly to mind at the point of temptation, until belief in it becomes not a doctrine recited but a presence felt.

The next verse will tell us who, in fact, denies the Day of Judgment — and its answer redirects the entire discussion from the intellect to the will.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:12

> **which none deny save every sinful transgressor.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Diagnosis of Denial: Not an Intellectual Problem but a Moral One**

This verse makes one of the most penetrating claims in the Qur'an about the psychology of unbelief. *Wa mā yukadhdhibu bihi illā kullu muʿtadin athīm* — "and none deny it save every sinful transgressor." The construction is exclusive: *none* deny the Day of Judgment *except* those of a certain moral character. Denial of the Judgment is thereby traced not to a defect of intelligence, information, or reasoning, but to a defect of *character*. The one who denies the Day of Judgment does so, the Qur'an says, because he is a transgressor and a sinner — because he *wants* there to be no Judgment.

This is a reversal of the usual way people think about belief and conduct. We ordinarily assume that beliefs come first and conduct follows: a person disbelieves in the Judgment, and therefore feels free to sin. The Qur'an reveals the hidden reverse causation: a person wishes to sin freely, and therefore disbelieves in the Judgment. The desire shapes the belief. The transgressor denies accountability because accountability would condemn what he loves to do.

**The Two Qualifiers: *Muʿtadin* and *Athīm***

The denier is described by two words, and they are chosen with care.

*Muʿtadin* is the active participle of *iʿtadā*, "to transgress, to cross a limit, to go beyond bounds, to commit aggression." The root *ʿ-d-w* gives *ʿudwān* (aggression, hostility), *ʿaduww* (enemy), and *taʿaddī* (transgression). A *muʿtadin* is one who crosses lines — the lines God has drawn around what is lawful and unlawful, and the lines that protect other people's rights. The Qur'an frequently uses this word in the context of violating the rights of others: *"and do not transgress; truly God loves not the transgressors"* (2:190). The defrauder is a *muʿtadin* in the most literal sense: he crossed the line of the brim of the measure, crossed into what belonged to another.

*Athīm* is an intensive adjective from *ithm*, "sin" — specifically the kind of sin that involves a knowing violation, a deliberate wrong that leaves a stain. *Athīm* means "deeply sinful," "habitually sinful," "one steeped in sin." It describes not a person who occasionally errs but one whose sinning is a settled disposition. The Qur'an pairs the two words elsewhere in similar contexts: *"Woe to every sinful liar (affākin athīm)"* (45:7); *"and We shall not be denied except by every ungrateful sinner"* — the pattern is consistent.

The combination is precise. *Muʿtadin* describes his conduct toward *others* — he transgresses their rights. *Athīm* describes his condition toward *God* — he is steeped in sin. Together they describe a person whose relationship with both creation and Creator is corrupted. Such a person has every motive to deny the Day of Judgment, because the Day would expose both dimensions of his corruption.

**Why the Transgressor *Needs* to Deny the Judgment**

The logic of the verse can be stated as a psychological law. A person cannot comfortably hold together two things: (a) a settled practice of wrongdoing, and (b) a vivid belief that he will be judged for it. The tension between them is intolerable. Something must give. Either the conduct changes to match the belief — which is repentance — or the belief changes to match the conduct — which is denial. The transgressor who is unwilling to give up his transgression resolves the tension by weakening, then abandoning, his belief in the Judgment.

This process is rarely conscious. Few people say to themselves, "I want to cheat, so I will now decide there is no Judgment." Rather, the belief is gradually eroded by a thousand small acts of rationalization: "It probably doesn't matter this much." "Surely God is not concerned with such small things." "Everyone does it." "Who really knows what happens after death?" Each act of sin makes the Judgment slightly less real; each diminishment of the Judgment makes the next sin slightly easier. The sūrah will describe the endpoint of this process two verses later: the heart covered with rust.

The Qur'an names this dynamic explicitly in another sūrah:

> *"Does man think that We shall not assemble his bones? Yes indeed, We are able to fashion even his fingertips. But man desires to continue in sin before him (li-yafjura amāmahu). He asks, 'When is the Day of Resurrection?'"* (Qur'an 75:3–6)

The sequence in that passage is identical to the one here: the question "When is the Day of Resurrection?" — apparently an intellectual query — is unmasked as the product of a desire: *man desires to continue in sin*. The verb there is *yafjur*, from the same root as *fujjār* (the profligate) in verse 7 of this sūrah. The skeptical question is a mask worn by the appetite.

**The Classical Commentators on This Verse**

Al-Ṭabarī explains that *muʿtadin* refers to one who transgresses the limits of God in his dealings and his conduct, and *athīm* to one who commits sins against his Lord; such a person, having no restraint, finds the doctrine of the Judgment intolerable and rejects it. Ibn Kathīr writes that the verse describes one who is "transgressing in his actions by doing what is forbidden and exceeding what is permitted, and sinful in his speech, lying when he speaks, breaking his promises, and behaving treacherously when he disputes." Al-Rāzī makes the deeper psychological point: the denial of the Judgment is not the product of a proof but of a *desire* — the desire to be free of the restraint that the Judgment imposes. He notes that no sound rational argument has ever been produced against the Resurrection; its denial is always, at bottom, an act of will.

Al-Qurṭubī draws the practical consequence: since denial flows from transgression, the way to *strengthen* faith in the Judgment is to *reduce* transgression. The heart that is not busy defending its sins is free to see the truth. This is why the early Muslims held that sin darkens the understanding and obedience illuminates it — a theme al-Shāfiʿī expressed in his famous lines complaining to his teacher Wakīʿ of his poor memory, and being counseled to abandon sins, "for knowledge is a light from God, and the light of God is not given to a sinner."

**The Doctrine Applied to Modern Skepticism**

This verse has an uncomfortable relevance to the modern intellectual landscape. Much contemporary skepticism about the afterlife presents itself as a purely rational position — the honest conclusion of a mind following evidence. The Qur'an does not deny that arguments are made; it questions their *origin*. It suggests that beneath many confident denials of accountability lies not a discovery but a preference: the wish to live without a Judge.

This is not to say that every person who struggles with belief is a transgressor; the verse speaks of settled *denial* (*takdhīb*), not of honest doubt or sincere inquiry. Nor does the Qur'an dismiss the need for arguments — it provides them abundantly. But the verse asks every skeptic to perform an honest self-examination: *would I want the Judgment to be true?* If the honest answer is "no, because of how I live," then the skeptic should recognize that his reasoning is not operating in neutral conditions. A judge who has a financial interest in the outcome of a case is disqualified from deciding it; the transgressor who has a moral interest in the non-existence of the Judgment should at least suspect his own verdict.

**The Reverse Also Holds: Righteousness Opens the Heart to Faith**

The verse's logic runs in both directions, and the positive direction is a source of hope. If transgression produces denial, then the abandonment of transgression removes the motive for denial and makes belief natural. This is why the Qur'an so often calls people to *do* right as a path to *seeing* right: *"And those who strive for Us — We will surely guide them to Our ways"* (29:69). The striving comes first; the guidance follows. A person who begins to fill the measure honestly, to pay what he owes, to restrain his hand from what is not his, finds that the Day of Judgment becomes progressively more real to him — not because he has encountered a new argument, but because he no longer has a reason to wish it away.

The Prophet ﷺ described this connection between conduct and inner light in a ḥadīth recorded by Aḥmad and Ibn Mājah:

> *"Truly the believer, when he sins, a black spot appears on his heart. If he repents, desists, and seeks forgiveness, his heart is polished. If he increases, it increases..."* (Sunan Ibn Mājah 4244; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3334; graded ḥasan)

The ḥadīth continues by citing verse 14 of this very sūrah, which shows that the Prophet ﷺ himself understood verses 12 and 14 as describing a single process: sin darkens the heart; the darkened heart denies the Judgment; the denial licenses more sin.

**The Practical Lesson: Guard Conduct to Guard Faith**

The practical teaching of this verse is that faith is not protected only by study and argument; it is protected by conduct. The person who wishes to keep his belief in the Day of Judgment strong must watch his hands as carefully as his mind. Every transgression is a small vote against the reality of the Judgment; every act of restraint is a vote for it. The defrauder who wants to know why the Hereafter feels so unreal to him should look at his scales. The believer who wants the Hereafter to feel more real should begin by giving full measure — in commerce, in worship, in relationships — and he will find, as the Qur'an promises, that God guides those who strive.

The next verse will describe the *speech* of this transgressor when the signs of God are recited to him — and it will show how a corrupted will produces a corrupted hearing.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:13

> **When Our signs are recited unto him, he says, "Fables of those of old!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**How the Transgressor Hears Revelation**

Having identified the denier as a "sinful transgressor," the sūrah now shows him in action at the decisive moment: the moment when God's signs are recited to him. *Idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā qāla asāṭīru al-awwalīn* — "When Our signs are recited unto him, he says, 'Fables of those of old!'" The verse presents a scene that recurred hundreds of times in Makkah: the Prophet ﷺ or a Companion reciting the Qur'an in the hearing of a wealthy, confident opponent, and the opponent dismissing it with a sneer and a formula.

The verb *tutlā* is passive and continuous: the signs *are being recited*, again and again. The word *āyāt*, "signs," refers here to the verses of the Qur'an, but the word's broader meaning — signs, evidences, indications — is not lost. What is recited to him is *evidence*, and his response to evidence is not counter-evidence but a label.

**The Meaning and History of "Fables of the Ancients"**

The phrase *asāṭīru al-awwalīn* deserves careful attention. *Asāṭīr* is a plural (of *usṭūrah* or *isṭārah*) from the root *s-ṭ-r*, "to write in lines" — the same root that gives *saṭr*, a line of writing, and *masṭūr*, written (as in *"By the Book written (masṭūr)"*, 52:2). *Asāṭīr* originally meant "things written down" and came to mean "written tales, legends, old stories" — the kind of material that was preserved in the books of earlier peoples: myths, epics, fanciful accounts. *Al-awwalīn* means "the first ones, the ancients, those of former times." The whole phrase therefore means "the written-down legends of ancient peoples" — what a modern speaker might call "old myths" or "bedtime stories."

This exact phrase appears nine times in the Qur'an as the standard dismissal used by the disbelievers (6:25; 8:31; 16:24; 23:83; 25:5; 27:68; 46:17; 68:15; 83:13). The most detailed version is in Sūrah al-Furqān:

> *"And they say, 'Fables of the ancients, which he has had written down, and they are dictated to him morning and evening.'"* (Qur'an 25:5)

Here the accusation is spelled out: the Qur'an, they claimed, was not revelation but a compilation of old stories the Prophet ﷺ had somehow acquired and was reciting as if new. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note that the man most associated with this slander was al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, a Qurayshī who had traveled to Persia, learned the tales of Rustam and Isfandiyār, and would sit where the Prophet ﷺ had been reciting and say, "I can tell better stories than Muhammad" — reciting the Persian epics in competition. Sūrah al-Qalam names the type: *"When Our signs are recited to him, he says, 'Fables of the ancients!'"* (68:15), immediately after describing him as *"a swearer, contemptible, a slanderer going about with calumny, a preventer of good, a transgressor (muʿtadin), sinful (athīm)"* (68:10–12) — the very words used in verse 12 of this sūrah.

**Why This Particular Insult?**

The choice of insult reveals the disbeliever's strategy. To call the Qur'an "fables of the ancients" accomplishes several things at once for the one who says it.

It *dismisses without engaging*. One does not refute a fable; one simply classifies it and moves on. The label spares the denier the labor — and the danger — of actually considering the argument.

It *insulates against the content*. Much of the Qur'an recounts the histories of earlier peoples — ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, Pharaoh — precisely as *warnings*: these people did what you are doing and were destroyed. By calling these accounts "fables," the denier neutralizes the warning. He turns a mirror into a picture-book.

It *asserts superiority*. "Fables of the ancients" implies that the speaker is modern, sophisticated, and beyond such tales — that only the primitive or the childish would take them seriously. The insult is a claim to status, which is exactly what the wealthy sinners of the sūrah's final section (vv. 29–33) will be shown displaying when they mock the believers.

And it *is itself a form of the sin the sūrah condemns*. The denier, in a sense, *short-measures the revelation*: he takes a text of immense weight and treats it as light. He is a *muṭaffif* of truth.

**The Irony of the Word "Written"**

There is an irony in the denier's choice of word that the sūrah's structure brings out. He calls the revelation *asāṭīr* — "things written in lines," old writings. But the sūrah has just told him (v. 9) that his own deeds are being *written* in *a book inscribed* (*kitābun marqūm*), kept in Sijjīn. The denier dismisses God's writing as fables; God's writing about *him* is meanwhile being kept with perfect accuracy. He will discover on the Day of Standing which writing was the fable — the revelation he mocked, or the record of his life he ignored.

**Why the Qur'an Is Not "Fables of the Ancients"**

The Qur'an answers this accusation directly in several ways that are worth recalling, because the slander has been revived in every century under new names — "borrowed legends," "recycled myths," "derivative narratives."

First, the Qur'an points to the Prophet's ﷺ unlettered condition: he did not read the books of earlier peoples and could not have compiled them:

> *"And you did not recite before it any scripture, nor did you write one with your right hand; otherwise the falsifiers would have had cause to doubt."* (Qur'an 29:48)

Second, the Qur'an explicitly frames its histories not as entertainment but as *truth with a purpose*:

> *"We relate to you the best of narratives in what We have revealed to you of this Qur'an, though before it you were among the unaware."* (Qur'an 12:3)

> *"There was certainly in their stories a lesson for those of understanding. It was not a narration invented, but a confirmation of what was before it and a detailed explanation of all things, and guidance and mercy for a people who believe."* (Qur'an 12:111)

Third, and most decisively, the Qur'an issued the challenge: if it is merely fables such as anyone could compose, then compose one sūrah like it (2:23; 10:38; 11:13). The challenge was never met — not by al-Naḍr with his Persian epics, nor by anyone since. A text that cannot be imitated by the most eloquent of the people to whom it was addressed is not a collection of familiar old stories.

Fourth, the Qur'an's accounts of earlier peoples are not the fanciful legends the label implies; they are stripped of the mythological ornament that fills the actual legends of antiquity, and they are focused with unwavering discipline on a moral and theological point. Anyone who compares the Qur'an's account of Noah or Joseph with the actual "fables of the ancients" preserved in the literature of the ancient Near East will notice the difference at once: the Qur'an removes the fables and keeps the lesson.

**The Corrupted Will Produces Corrupted Hearing**

The verse's placement immediately after verse 12 is deliberate. Verse 12 said that only the sinful transgressor denies the Judgment; verse 13 shows *how* his transgression affects his perception. When the signs are recited, he does not hear signs; he hears fables. His response is not a conclusion reached after hearing but a filter applied *before* hearing. He has already decided what the revelation is, because he has already decided what he wants to be true.

The Qur'an describes this mechanism elsewhere with great precision:

> *"And among them are those who listen to you, but We have placed over their hearts coverings, lest they understand it, and in their ears deafness. And if they should see every sign, they will not believe in it, until, when they come to you arguing with you, those who disbelieve say, 'This is nothing but fables of the ancients.'"* (Qur'an 6:25)

The sequence in that verse — coverings over the hearts, then the phrase "fables of the ancients" — anticipates exactly the sequence here: verse 13 gives the phrase, verse 14 gives the covering, the rust. The Qur'an is consistent across sūrahs in its account of how denial works: not as a failure to hear, but as a failure of the organ that interprets what is heard.

**The Modern Forms of "Fables of the Ancients"**

The denier's formula has never gone out of use; only its vocabulary has changed. "That is outdated." "That is a primitive worldview." "Those are pre-scientific ideas." "That is just religion." Each of these phrases performs the same function as *asāṭīru al-awwalīn*: it classifies in order to avoid engaging. It is a way of not listening while appearing to have listened. The Qur'an's verse is a warning to every reader — including the believer — against the habit of dismissing by labeling. The believer, too, can fall into this when confronted with a truth he does not want to hear: a sincere criticism, a verse that condemns his practice, a ḥadīth that demands more than he wishes to give. The moment one hears oneself reaching for a dismissive label rather than an honest consideration, one should suspect that the transgressor's mechanism has been activated.

**What the Believer Hears Instead**

The contrast to the denier's hearing is described in the Qur'an's portrait of the believers:

> *"The believers are only those who, when God is mentioned, their hearts tremble, and when His signs are recited to them, it increases them in faith, and upon their Lord they rely."* (Qur'an 8:2)

Same signs, same recitation — and in one heart they produce "fables of the ancients," in another a trembling that increases faith. The difference is not in the text but in the heart that receives it. The sūrah is about to explain, in one of its most famous verses, what has happened to the heart that hears fables where another hears God.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14

> **Nay! But that which they used to earn has covered their hearts with rust.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Explains the Whole Sūrah**

If one verse were to be chosen as the key to Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn, it would be this one. *Kallā bal rāna ʿalā qulūbihim mā kānū yaksibūn* — "Nay! But that which they used to earn has covered their hearts with rust." The verse answers the question the sūrah has been building toward since its first word: *why* does a person shortchange others, deny the Day of Judgment, and hear God's signs as fables? The answer is not that the truth is unclear or the evidence insufficient. The answer is that a layer has formed over the heart — a layer *made of the person's own deeds* — and this layer prevents the heart from perceiving what it would otherwise see plainly.

The verse opens with *kallā*, "Nay!" — rejecting the denier's claim that the Qur'an is "fables of the ancients." It is *not* fables; the problem is not in the text. Then *bal*, "but rather," introduces the true explanation: the problem is in the heart, and the heart's problem is self-inflicted.

**The Word *Rāna*: The Rust of the Heart**

The verb *rāna* (from the root *r-y-n*) is used only here in the Qur'an, and its meaning is vivid. Its basic sense is to cover, overwhelm, and overcome, the way rust covers iron, or the way wine overcomes the intelligence, or the way sleep overcomes a person. The noun *rayn* came to mean specifically the rust or tarnish that accumulates on metal until its shine is gone and its surface can no longer reflect. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī explained the verse: "It is sin upon sin, until the heart becomes blind and dies." Mujāhid said: "The heart is like the palm of the hand; when a person sins, a finger closes over it; when he sins again, another finger closes; until the whole hand is closed and the heart is sealed."

The Prophet ﷺ himself gave the authoritative explanation of this verse in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Tirmidhī, Ibn Mājah, Aḥmad, and al-Ḥākim, from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"Truly the servant, when he commits a sin, a black spot is placed on his heart. If he desists, seeks forgiveness, and repents, his heart is polished. But if he returns [to sin], it increases until it covers his heart. That is the rust (rān) which God mentioned: 'Nay! But that which they used to earn has covered their hearts with rust.'"* (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3334, who graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; Sunan Ibn Mājah 4244; Musnad Aḥmad 7952)

This ḥadīth is the definitive commentary on the verse, and it is precise. The rust does not appear all at once. It begins as a *single spot* from a *single sin*. It can be *removed* by repentance — the heart is "polished." But if the sin is repeated without repentance, the spots multiply and merge until the whole surface is covered. At that stage the heart can no longer receive light. This is the condition of the denier in verses 12–13: he is not a person who was born unable to perceive the truth; he is a person who *covered* his own capacity to perceive it, spot by spot, sin by sin.

**The Progressive Sealing of the Heart in the Qur'an**

The Qur'an describes several stages in the deterioration of the heart, and *rayn* is one of them. Ibn al-Qayyim and other scholars of the heart arranged them in an order of severity:

*Rayn* (rust) — a covering that forms from repeated sin; still removable by repentance.

*Ṭabʿ* (imprint, stamping) — a firmer sealing: *"Thus does God set a seal (yaṭbaʿu) upon the hearts of those who know not"* (30:59).

*Khatm* (sealing) — the closure described at the beginning of al-Baqarah: *"God has set a seal (khatama) upon their hearts and their hearing, and over their eyes is a covering"* (2:7).

*Qufl* (locking) — *"Do they not reflect upon the Qur'an, or are there locks (aqfāl) upon their hearts?"* (47:24).

*Qasāwah* (hardening) — *"Then your hearts hardened after that, and they were like stones or even harder"* (2:74).

The point of this progression is that the heart's deterioration is *gradual* and, in its early stages, *reversible*. No one wakes up one morning with a sealed heart. The seal is the last stage of a long process that began with a single unrepented sin — a single short measure, perhaps. The verse of the rust describes the process in its middle stage, when the covering is thickening but not yet locked. This is why the verse is a *warning* rather than merely a description: it is addressed to people who still have time to polish.

**"That Which They Used to Earn": The Commercial Word for Sin**

The Qur'an says the rust is made of *mā kānū yaksibūn* — "that which they used to earn." The verb *kasaba* means to earn, to acquire, to gain — it is the ordinary word for commercial acquisition. The choice is pointed in a sūrah that began with dishonest trade. The defrauder thought he was *earning* profit by his short measures. The Qur'an tells him what he was actually earning: rust on his heart. Every gram skimmed was a deposit in a different account than the one he kept.

The Qur'an frequently uses *kasaba* for the acquisition of deeds, good and bad: *"Every soul will be recompensed for what it earned"* (40:17); *"It [the soul] has what it earned, and against it what it earned"* (2:286). The metaphor is deliberate. Human life is a kind of commerce in which every action is a transaction whose proceeds accrue to the soul. The defrauder was an expert in one kind of earning and blind to the other.

The phrase *kānū yaksibūn*, with the past-continuous *kānū*, indicates habitual, repeated action. The rust is not the product of a single lapse. It is the sediment of a *practice*. This matches the ḥadīth exactly: one spot, then another, then the covering.

**The Logic of the Verse: Why Sin Blinds**

Why should wrongdoing impair *perception*? The verse asserts a causal connection between what a person does and what he is able to see, and the connection is worth understanding, because it is confirmed by ordinary human experience.

First, there is the mechanism of *rationalization*. A person who does wrong and does not repent must somehow live with what he has done. The mind, to protect itself, generates justifications: it was not really wrong, it did not really matter, everyone does it, the victim deserved it. Each justification is a small distortion of moral perception. Over time these distortions accumulate into a systematically warped view of reality, in which wrong appears right and truth appears as "fables."

Second, there is the mechanism of *desensitization*. The first time a person does a particular wrong, he feels its wrongness sharply — the conscience protests. The second time, less. By the hundredth time, not at all. The protest of conscience is a form of moral *sight*; its silencing is a form of *blindness*. What the ḥadīth calls "a black spot" is the felt residue of the first sin; the "covering" is the silence that follows the thousandth.

Third, there is the mechanism of *motivated reasoning*, already noted in verse 12. The person with a stake in the non-existence of the Judgment will find arguments against the Judgment compelling and arguments for it weak — not because of the arguments but because of the stake. His reasoning faculty is not destroyed; it is *captured*.

All three mechanisms describe a heart being covered. Modern psychology has documented each of them under various names — cognitive dissonance reduction, moral disengagement, motivated cognition — and the Qur'an described their combined effect fourteen centuries ago in a single verb: *rāna*.

**The Heart as an Organ of Perception**

The verse presupposes an understanding of the *qalb*, the heart, that must be made explicit. In the Qur'an, the heart is not merely the seat of emotion; it is the organ of spiritual *perception* and *understanding*. The Qur'an speaks of hearts that "understand" (*yafqahūna*, 7:179), hearts that "reason" (*yaʿqilūna*, 22:46), and hearts that "see": *"For it is not the eyes that are blind, but blind are the hearts within the breasts"* (22:46). The heart is what recognizes truth when it is presented — the faculty by which a person hears a verse and knows, before any argument, that this is from God.

Because the heart is an organ of perception, its condition determines what a person can *see*. A rusted mirror does not reflect; a rusted heart does not recognize. This is why the denier of verse 13 hears "fables" — his perceiving organ is covered. The Prophet ﷺ summarized the centrality of the heart in the famous ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"Truly in the body there is a morsel of flesh which, if it is sound, the whole body is sound, and if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt. Truly, it is the heart."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 52; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1599)

**The Polish: How Rust Is Removed**

The source commentary cites a famous ḥadīth on the remedy for the rusted heart, recorded by al-Bayhaqī in *Shuʿab al-Īmān* and others:

> *"For everything there is a polish, and the polish of the heart is the remembrance of God."*

And the ḥadīth of Abū Hurayrah cited above gives the mechanism: the heart "is polished" (*ṣuqila*) when the servant *"desists, seeks forgiveness, and repents."* Three actions, in order: *nazaʿa* (he pulls away from the sin), *istaghfara* (he seeks forgiveness), *tāba* (he turns back to God). The polish is not a single act but a sequence: stop, ask forgiveness, return.

The metaphor of polishing is exact. Rust on iron is removed by *friction* — by rubbing, which is effortful and repeated. So too the heart: a single "I'm sorry" does not remove a covering built by years of habit. It requires the friction of repeated repentance, repeated remembrance, repeated acts of restraint against the old pattern. The commentators note that the Qur'an's own recitation is the primary polish: *"And We send down of the Qur'an that which is a healing and a mercy for the believers"* (17:82). The very signs that the rusted heart dismissed as fables are the polish that would remove its rust — if it would only allow them to be applied.

Ibn Taymiyyah, discussing this verse in his writings on the diseases of the heart, observes that the heart is like a mirror in which the realities of things are meant to appear; sin is the tarnish that clouds it; and the remedy is a combination of *repentance* (which removes the tarnish) and *obedience* (which restores the polish and prevents new tarnish). He emphasizes that the heart's natural state (*fiṭrah*) is *clarity* — it was created to perceive the truth — and that rust is always an addition, never the original condition. This is a hopeful teaching: beneath every rusted heart is a mirror that was made to shine.

**Why God Attributes the Rust to Their Own Earning**

A theological point of great importance is embedded in this verse. God says the hearts were covered by *"that which they used to earn"* — by *their own* deeds. He does not say, in this verse, "We covered their hearts." The responsibility is placed squarely on the human agent. This answers a question that troubles many: if God seals hearts (as other verses say), how can the sealed be blamed? The answer given here is that the sealing is the *consequence* of the person's own chosen conduct. God's sealing is not arbitrary; it is the natural law He has established, by which repeated sin dulls perception — a law that operates as reliably as the law by which repeated neglect rusts iron. The person who left his heart in the rain of sin, year after year, cannot complain that God let it rust. Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr both emphasize this: the verse establishes human responsibility for the state of one's own heart.

**A Practical Discipline of Inspection**

The verse gives the believer a diagnostic tool and a daily task. The diagnostic: if the Qur'an has begun to feel like "old stories" — if the verses that once moved you now pass over you without effect, if the mention of the Judgment produces no tremor, if you find yourself reaching for dismissive labels — suspect rust. Do not blame the text; inspect the mirror. The task: polish daily. Repentance for the specific sins of the day; remembrance of God morning and evening; recitation of the Qur'an with attention; and, above all, the cessation of the *earning* that produces the rust — the small dishonesties that the sūrah began by naming. A heart polished each night does not accumulate the covering that blinds. The one who waits until the rust is thick will find the polishing harder, and the one who waits until the heart is sealed will find, as the next verse says, that he has been veiled from the very Lord he might have seen.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15

> **Nay! Surely on that Day they will be veiled from their Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First and Greatest Punishment**

Having diagnosed the rusted heart, the sūrah now names the consequences, in a sequence of three verses (15–17) that describe an escalating triple punishment. The *first* punishment — placed first because it is the gravest — is not fire. It is *separation*: *kallā innahum ʿan Rabbihim yawmaʾidhin la-maḥjūbūn* — "Nay! Surely on that Day they will be veiled from their Lord." Before the sūrah mentions Hell, it mentions the veil. The order tells us something about the true nature of loss in the Hereafter: to be cut off from God is worse than to be burned.

The verse opens again with *kallā*, the third occurrence in this passage (vv. 7, 14, 15). Each *kallā* has cut off a false hope of the deniers. The first cut off their hope that no record exists. The second cut off their claim that the Qur'an is fables. This third cuts off — what? Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī suggest it rebukes the entire disposition just described: the rusting of the heart through sin. Nay — do not continue in it, for its end is *this*. Others read it as pure emphasis: *truly, certainly*, they will be veiled.

**The Word *Maḥjūbūn*: What It Means to Be Veiled**

*Maḥjūbūn* is the passive participle of *ḥajaba*, "to veil, to screen, to bar from entry." The root gives *ḥijāb* (a veil, curtain, partition) and *ḥājib* (a doorkeeper, chamberlain — the one who controls access to a ruler). The commentators note that in classical Arabic usage, when one said that a person was *maḥjūb* from the king, one meant that he was *denied audience* — the chamberlain had turned him away at the door. He was not permitted to enter the presence. This is the image of the verse: on the Day when all mankind stands before the Lord of the worlds (v. 6), the deniers will be *turned away at the door*.

The emphasis in the Arabic is heavy: *inna* (truly) + the pronoun + *la-* (surely) attached to *maḥjūbūn*. Three layers of emphasis for a single predicate. The Qur'an is insisting on this point above the others in the sequence.

**The Two Classical Interpretations**

The source commentary records that the classical scholars understood the veil in two ways, which are complementary rather than opposed.

The first interpretation, held by al-Ṭabarī, Qatādah, and others, and recorded by al-Qurṭubī, is that they are veiled from God's *mercy* and *honor* (*karāmah*). On this reading, the verse means that the deniers will be excluded from every kindness God shows to His servants on that Day — no forgiveness, no intercession, no welcome, no mercy. They stand outside the door while the believers are received within.

The second interpretation, held by the great majority and made explicit by al-Shāfiʿī, Mālik, Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, and the scholars of the Sunnah, is that they are veiled from *seeing* God. The believers, on that Day, will be granted the vision of their Lord — the greatest of all rewards — and the disbelievers will be denied it. Al-Shāfiʿī's famous argument runs: "Since God veiled these people [the disbelievers] in His wrath, this is evidence that His friends will see Him in His good pleasure." Al-Ṭabarī records this reasoning as well, and Ibn Kathīr calls it "a fine deduction." The logic is simple: a veil is meaningful only if there is something to see. If no one could see God, saying that the disbelievers are "veiled" from Him would be no punishment at all. The veil as *punishment* implies vision as *reward*.

Both interpretations are true, and together they describe the full deprivation: the deniers will be excluded from God's mercy *and* from God's sight. The two go together, for the sight of God is the highest of His mercies.

**The Vision of God: The Reward Behind the Veil**

To grasp what the deniers lose, one must understand what the believers gain. The Qur'an states:

> *"Faces that Day will be radiant, gazing upon their Lord."* (Qur'an 75:22–23)

> *"For those who do good is the best [reward] and more."* (Qur'an 10:26)

The Prophet ﷺ explained the "more" (*ziyādah*) in a ḥadīth recorded by Muslim from Ṣuhayb:

> *"When the people of Paradise enter Paradise, God — Blessed and Exalted — will say, 'Do you desire anything more that I may give you?' They will say, 'Have You not brightened our faces? Have You not admitted us to Paradise and saved us from the Fire?' Then He will remove the veil (al-ḥijāb), and they will not have been given anything more beloved to them than the gazing upon their Lord — Mighty and Majestic." Then he recited: "For those who do good is the best reward and more."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 181)

Note the phrase: *"He will remove the veil."* The same word, *ḥijāb*, that describes the deniers' punishment in this verse describes the barrier that God lifts for the believers. The believers have it removed; the deniers have it kept in place. And the ḥadīth says the vision is *"more beloved to them than anything they had been given"* — more than Paradise itself. That is the measure of what the veil withholds.

Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from Jarīr ibn ʿAbdullāh that the Prophet ﷺ looked at the full moon one night and said:

> *"Truly you will see your Lord as you see this moon; you will have no difficulty in seeing Him. So if you are able not to be overcome in performing the prayer before sunrise and before sunset, then do so."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 554; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 633)

The connection the Prophet ﷺ made between the vision of God and the dawn and afternoon prayers is instructive: the one who guards those prayers — who does not *skim* from them, in the language of Ibn Masʿūd's commentary on this sūrah — is preparing himself for the vision. The defrauder of prayer, like the defrauder of the measure, is building a veil.

**Why the Veil Is Placed First Among the Punishments**

The sequence in verses 15–17 is: veiled from their Lord, then burned in Hell, then told "This is what you used to deny." Why is the veil first? The scholars of the heart offer the profound answer: because the greatest good in existence is the nearness of God, and therefore the greatest evil is distance from Him. The Fire is a punishment of the body; the veil is a punishment of the *soul*, and the soul is the seat of the person. A man deprived of the sight of the one he was created to know suffers a loss that no physical pain can equal.

Ibn al-Qayyim writes that the people of Hell would forget the pain of the Fire if they knew what they had lost in being veiled from God, and that the people of Paradise would forget every pleasure of Paradise when the veil is lifted. Both statements turn on the same truth: God is the goal, and everything else is means. The denier who lived for the created world — for measures of grain and coins of silver — receives, as his punishment, exactly and only the created world: fire, which is created; and never the Creator, from whom he is veiled.

**The Justice of the Veil: A Symmetry with the Rust**

There is a precise correspondence between verse 14 and verse 15. In verse 14, the deniers' hearts were *covered* (*rāna*) by their own deeds. In verse 15, they are *veiled* (*maḥjūbūn*) from their Lord. The covering they built in this world becomes the veil that shuts them out in the next. They veiled their own hearts from God's signs; God veils them from His face. The punishment is the sin made permanent.

This correspondence is not arbitrary. The vision of God on the Day of Judgment is, in the teaching of the scholars, the fruit of a *capacity* developed in this life: the capacity of the heart to know, love, and turn toward God. A person who spent his life rusting that capacity arrives on that Day without it. He is not veiled by an external decree unrelated to his life; he is veiled by his own incapacity to see, which he manufactured deed by deed. The heart that could not perceive God's signs through the covering of sin cannot perceive God through the veil of its own making.

The source commentary cites the insight of Jalāl al-Dīn al-Rūmī, who put this correspondence memorably: everyone sees the Unseen in proportion to the clarity of his heart, and that clarity depends on how much he has polished it; whoever has polished more sees more. Rūmī's statement is not a mystical addition to the Qur'an; it is a restatement of what verses 14 and 15, read together, plainly teach.

**A Human Analogy for the Veil**

Consider a son who has spent decades wronging his father — stealing from him, mocking him to others, ignoring his letters, denying his name. At the end of the father's life there is a gathering at which all the children are received, embraced, given their inheritance, and told how they were loved. The estranged son is not admitted. He is left outside the door while his brothers and sisters are inside. He can hear, faintly, the sounds of the reunion. No one is beating him; he is simply *not allowed in*. Any person who has experienced exclusion from a presence he desperately desired knows that this can be more painful than a blow. The verse describes that exclusion at the scale of eternity — and the presence from which one is excluded is not a human father but the Lord of the worlds, for whose nearness the soul was made.

**The Verse as an Argument for the Vision of God**

The theological importance of this verse in Islamic history should be noted. The question of whether believers will see God in the Hereafter was debated intensely in the early centuries. The Muʿtazilah denied the vision on rationalist grounds. The scholars of the Sunnah affirmed it, citing 75:22–23, 10:26, the ḥadīth of the moon, and *this verse*. Al-Shāfiʿī's deduction from this verse — that veiling the disbelievers implies unveiling the believers — became a standard argument, repeated by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Taymiyyah. The verse therefore stands not only as a warning to deniers but as a *promise* to believers, read in reverse: if *they* are veiled on that Day, then *you* — if you are not among them — are not.

**Application: Removing Veils Before the Veil**

The practical lesson is to remove, in this life, the veils one has placed between oneself and God — because the veil of that Day is the fixing of the veils of this one. Every sin is a small curtain; repentance draws it back. Every act of remembrance is a window opened; heedlessness closes it. The believer asks himself: what stands between me and God today? An unrepented dishonesty? A grudge? A neglected prayer? A habit of dismissing His words? Each of these is a thread in the veil. The one who spends his life drawing back curtains will find, on that Day, that the last curtain is drawn back for him by God Himself, as the ḥadīth says: *"Then He will remove the veil."* The one who spent his life adding curtains will find that the last one is not removed. The choice is made now, at the scale, at the prayer mat, at the moment the signs are recited.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:16

> **Then they will burn in Hellfire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Punishment: From the Veil to the Fire**

The sequence of consequences continues: *thumma innahum la-ṣālū al-jaḥīm* — "Then they will burn in Hellfire." The word *thumma*, "then," marks succession and, in Arabic rhetoric, often a progression in gravity or a lapse of time. Having been veiled from their Lord — turned away at the door of His presence — the deniers are then brought to the Fire. The two punishments are related as cause and effect: the one who is excluded from God's mercy has nowhere else to go but the Fire, since there is no third place. Al-Rāzī observes that the veiling is the spiritual punishment and the burning the physical one, and that the Qur'an mentions both so that no dimension of the human being — neither the soul that longed for God nor the body that served sin — is left unaccounted for.

Again the emphasis is layered: *inna* + pronoun + *la-*. The Qur'an does not present the Fire as a possibility but as a certainty for those who die in denial.

**The Word *Ṣālū*: To Enter and Endure the Fire**

The word *ṣālū* is the plural active participle of *ṣaliya*, whose meaning is more specific than simply "to burn." *Ṣaliya al-nār* means to enter the fire, to be exposed to its heat, to roast in it, to be made to feel it fully. The root is used for roasting meat over fire (*ṣalā al-laḥm*), and for warming oneself at a fire (*iṣṭalā*). The participle *ṣālin* describes one who is *in* the fire, undergoing its effects, not one who is merely near it. The Qur'an uses this root repeatedly for the punishment of the Hereafter: *"He will burn (yaṣlā) in a Fire of blazing flame"* (111:3); *"and We shall make him burn (nuṣlīhi) in Hell"* (4:115). The choice of *ṣālū* rather than a more general word for burning conveys thoroughness: they are not singed but roasted, not brushed by the fire but immersed in it.

**The Word *al-Jaḥīm*: The Fiercely Blazing Fire**

*Al-Jaḥīm* is one of the names of Hell in the Qur'an, occurring twenty-six times. It derives from *jaḥama*, "to kindle a fire intensely," and *jaḥmah*, "the fierce blazing of a fire." The Arabs called an intensely burning fire *jaḥīm*; some lexicographers note it referred specifically to a fire piled layer upon layer, feeding on itself. The name thus emphasizes the *intensity* of the Fire — its heat, its fierceness, its consuming power.

The Qur'an's other names for Hell each emphasize a different aspect: *Jahannam* (the general name, perhaps from a root meaning depth and darkness), *Saqar* (scorching), *Laẓā* (the blazing flame), *al-Ḥuṭamah* (the crushing one), *al-Saʿīr* (the fiercely kindled), *al-Hāwiyah* (the abyss). *Al-Jaḥīm* is often used in contexts contrasting with Paradise, as here, where the deniers' Jaḥīm (v. 16) is set against the righteous' *Naʿīm*, bliss (v. 22). The two words even rhyme in the Arabic, underlining the contrast.

**Why the Fire Is a Necessary Part of the Message**

Some modern readers find the Qur'an's descriptions of Hell difficult and wonder why a merciful God would speak so. Several considerations should be kept in view.

First, the Fire is *just*. It is not inflicted on the innocent, the ignorant, or those who tried and failed. It is the destination of those who, having been warned clearly, chose denial and transgression to the end. The sūrah has been careful to establish this: it named the deniers' sin (short-measuring), traced its root (denial of the Judgment), diagnosed its cause (self-inflicted rust), and only then named its consequence. The Fire arrives at the end of a chain of free choices. The Qur'an says: *"And your Lord is not ever unjust to His servants"* (41:46).

Second, the warning of the Fire is *merciful*. A warning about a real danger is the kindest thing one can give a person heading toward it. The Prophet ﷺ was sent, in his own words, as "the naked warner" — the man who runs ahead of a raid to save his people. Describing the Fire vividly is not cruelty but the opposite: it is God giving human beings every reason to avoid it while they still can. The Qur'an says of itself: *"We have made it easy in your tongue, that you may give good tidings by it to the righteous and warn by it a hostile people"* (19:97). Warning is half the purpose of revelation.

Third, the Fire is the answer to *injustice*. Without a Hell, the tyrant who murdered thousands and died comfortably in his bed, and the swindler who ruined widows and was never caught, receive exactly the same end as their victims: oblivion. The moral universe would be absurd. The Fire is the assurance that wrong does not, in the end, pay. For the victims of the defrauders, the existence of the Jaḥīm is not a difficulty but a consolation.

Fourth, the Fire is *what the denier chose*. The Qur'an describes the people of Hell as having "purchased error for guidance" (2:16) — again the commercial language. They traded. They knew the terms. The Jaḥīm is the completion of a transaction they themselves initiated.

**What the Fire Is Like: The Qur'anic and Prophetic Descriptions**

The Qur'an describes the Fire in terms designed to make it imaginable and thereby avoidable. Of its fuel:

> *"O you who believe! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, over which are angels, stern and severe, who do not disobey God in what He commands them but do what they are commanded."* (Qur'an 66:6)

Of its heat, the Prophet ﷺ said, as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"Your fire [in this world] is one seventieth part of the Fire of Hell." It was said, "O Messenger of God, this one would have been enough." He said, "It was made to exceed it by sixty-nine parts, each of them like its heat."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3265; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2843)

Of its depth, Muslim records from Abū Hurayrah that the Companions heard a crash and the Prophet ﷺ explained:

> *"That is a stone that was thrown into Hell seventy years ago, and it has only now reached its bottom."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2844)

Of the lightest punishment, al-Bukhārī and Muslim record:

> *"The person with the lightest punishment in the Fire on the Day of Resurrection will be a man under the arches of whose feet two embers will be placed, from which his brain will boil, as a cauldron boils."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6561; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 213)

These descriptions are not given to satisfy curiosity but to give weight to the choices of this life. The defrauder pressed the scale for a few grams. The Qur'an sets before him the seventy-fold fire so that he may weigh the grams correctly.

**The Fire as the Fruit of the Fire Within**

The scholars of the heart noted a continuity between the state of the denier in this world and his state in the Fire. In this world, his heart was rusted (v. 14) — rust is the slow oxidation of metal, a kind of slow burning. In the next, he burns openly. Greed, the drive behind short-measuring, is itself described in Arabic and in many languages as a fire: it consumes, it is never satisfied, it destroys what it touches. The Prophet ﷺ said, as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"If the son of Adam had a valley of gold, he would want to have two valleys, and nothing fills his mouth but the dust [of the grave]."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6439; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1048)

The insatiability of greed is a fire that burns in the breast in this life. The Jaḥīm is that fire externalized and made total. In this sense, the punishment is not foreign to the sin; it is the sin's own nature revealed. The person who spent his life burning with acquisitiveness is given, in the end, the burning he cultivated.

**The Mercy That Shadows the Verse**

Even in a verse of pure warning, the believer should hear the mercy that surrounds it. The Fire described here is the destination of those who *die* in denial. For anyone still alive, the door of repentance is open, and the Qur'an's most emphatic promise is that no sin — including a lifetime of short-measuring — is beyond God's forgiveness for the one who turns back:

> *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves! Despair not of the mercy of God. Truly God forgives all sins. Truly He is the Forgiving, the Merciful."* (Qur'an 39:53)

The very intensity of the warning is a measure of the intensity of God's desire that His servants not reach the Fire. One does not shout a warning about a danger one is indifferent to. The Prophet ﷺ described himself in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim as a man who has kindled a fire, into which moths and insects begin to fall, while he seizes them by their waists to pull them back:

> *"I am seizing you by your waists away from the Fire, and you are breaking loose from my grip."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6483; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2284)

That is the tone in which this verse should be heard: not a gloating threat but the cry of one holding people back from an edge.

**Application: Protecting Oneself and One's Household**

The verse's practical teaching flows from the Qur'anic command *"Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire"* (66:6). Protection from the Jaḥīm begins with the things the sūrah opened with: honest dealing, filling the measure, refusing the small theft that seems harmless. It extends to the household: teaching children that the invisible gram matters, that the record is kept, that the Day is real — so that they do not inherit the rust. And it is sustained by the daily practice the Prophet ﷺ prescribed, in the ḥadīth already cited on the Standing:

> *"So protect yourselves from the Fire, even with half a date."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6539)

Half a date given is the opposite of half a measure withheld. The one who trains himself in the first will not fall into the second, and will not, God willing, be among those of whom it is said: *Then they will burn in Hellfire.*

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:17

> **Then it is said unto them, "This is that which you used to deny."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Punishment: The Word of Rebuke**

The triple sequence of consequences closes with a sentence spoken to the deniers as they burn: *thumma yuqālu hādhā alladhī kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūn* — "Then it is said unto them, 'This is that which you used to deny.'" After the veil (v. 15) and the Fire (v. 16) comes the *word* — and the scholars regard this third punishment as, in its own way, the sharpest. It is the punishment of *being shown to have been wrong*: not merely to suffer, but to know, with total clarity and no possibility of appeal, that one's suffering is the direct consequence of a truth one rejected when it was offered freely.

The word *thumma*, "then," again marks progression. The veil deprives, the Fire pains, and the word *humiliates* — it adds to physical torment the mental torment of self-reproach. Al-Rāzī notes that this is the *tawbīkh* (rebuke) and *taqrīʿ* (reproach) that completes the punishment, because a punishment whose cause is understood cuts deeper than one that seems arbitrary.

**"It Is Said": The Anonymous Speaker**

The verb *yuqālu* is passive: "it is said." The speaker is not named. The commentators offer that the speakers are the keepers of Hell (*khazanat jahannam*), the angels appointed over the Fire, as in the parallel verse: *"And the keepers of Hell will say to them..."* (40:49). Others hold that the saying is from God Himself, or from any speaker, since what matters is the *content*, not the speaker. The passive voice conveys a sense of impersonal, cosmic finality: this is not one party's accusation but the verdict of reality itself. The truth they denied now speaks to them from every direction.

**"This": The Demonstrative of Presence**

The word *hādhā*, "this," is a near demonstrative — it points to what is immediately present. In verse 4 the deniers were referred to as *ulāʾika*, "those," distant and disowned. Now, in the Fire, the reality they denied is *hādhā*, "this" — right here, all around them, touching them. The rhetorical movement from the distant *those* to the present *this* traces the whole arc of the deniers' journey: from a far-off warning they could ignore to an immediate reality they cannot escape. What was "fables of the ancients" (v. 13) — remote, legendary, belonging to others — has become *this*, the burning present.

**"Which You Used to Deny": The Word That Closes the Circle**

The verb *tukadhdhibūn*, "you used to deny," is the same root that appeared in verses 10, 11, and 12 — *mukadhdhibīn*, *yukadhdhibūna*, *yukadhdhibu*. The sūrah has used this word four times in eight verses, and the final occurrence turns it against the deniers. They *denied*; now their denial is quoted back to them. The structure *kuntum ... tukadhdhibūn* (you *used to* deny) places the denial firmly in the past — a past that has now ended, because denial is impossible in the presence of the thing denied.

This closing of the circle is a recurring pattern in the Qur'an's descriptions of the Judgment. The same sentence appears in Sūrah al-Ṭūr:

> *"This is the Fire which you used to deny. Is this magic, or do you not see? Burn in it, and be patient or impatient — it is all the same for you. You are only being recompensed for what you used to do."* (Qur'an 52:14–16)

And in Sūrah al-Sajdah:

> *"So taste [the punishment] because you forgot the meeting of this Day of yours; indeed, We have forgotten you. And taste the punishment of eternity for what you used to do."* (Qur'an 32:14)

In each case, the punishment is explicitly linked to the person's own past attitude. This is essential to Qur'anic justice: no one in the Fire is confused about why he is there. The record was inscribed (v. 9); the warning was recited (v. 13); the response was denial; the consequence is *this*.

**Why Being Told Is a Punishment**

One might ask: once a person is in the Fire, what difference does it make to be told why? The answer lies in human psychology, which the Qur'an understands precisely.

Suffering that seems arbitrary can be borne with a certain defiance — one can tell oneself that one is a victim, that the punishment is unjust, that one did nothing to deserve it. This defiance is a kind of shelter. The word *"This is what you used to deny"* removes the shelter. It says: you were told; you were shown the evidence; you had the messengers, the books, the signs in yourselves and in the horizons; and you chose, freely and repeatedly, to call it all a lie. The suffering is therefore *yours* — not imposed on you from outside but earned by your own refusals. No one can be blamed but yourself.

The Qur'an describes this moment of recognition as the very substance of regret in the Hereafter:

> *"And they will say, 'Had we but listened or reasoned, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze.' And they will admit their sin, so away with the companions of the Blaze."* (Qur'an 67:10–11)

*"Had we but listened or reasoned."* This is the internal echo of the external rebuke. The word spoken to them (*This is what you used to deny*) produces the word spoken by them (*Had we but listened*). And the Qur'an says: *"They will admit their sin."* The denial is over; the confession comes — too late to help.

**Denial Undone by Sight**

There is a deep logic in the timing. In this life, faith is meaningful because the Hereafter is *unseen* (*ghayb*). Believing in what one cannot see, on the basis of evidence and trust in God's messengers, is the test of the human being. The Qur'an praises the believers as *"those who believe in the Unseen"* (2:3). On the Day of Judgment the Unseen becomes seen, and belief becomes irrelevant — everyone believes when the Fire is before them. Hence the tragedy of the denier: he denied when denial was possible (and belief was meritorious), and he believes when belief is worthless (and denial is impossible). The word *"This is what you used to deny"* marks the exact moment when the window of faith closes. The Qur'an says:

> *"On the Day when some of the signs of your Lord come, no soul will benefit from its faith if it had not believed before, or earned some good through its faith."* (Qur'an 6:158)

**The Function of This Verse for the Living**

This verse is not primarily addressed to the people of the Fire; they will hear it soon enough. It is addressed to *us* — those who can still read it before it is spoken to us. Its purpose is to let us hear, now, the sentence that will be spoken then, so that it will never need to be spoken. This is one of the Qur'an's most characteristic methods: it transports the reader forward to the Day of Judgment, lets him hear what will be said, and returns him to the present with the knowledge of what to avoid. Al-Ghazālī called this practice the "remembrance of the Hereafter" and considered it the most powerful of all motivations to righteousness — more powerful than any law, because it engages the imagination and the heart rather than merely the calculating mind.

The reader should therefore hear *hādhā alladhī kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūn* as a question: what am I, today, denying? Not necessarily in words — most readers of the Qur'an do not verbally deny the Judgment — but in practice: which truths of the Qur'an do I hear and quietly set aside as inconvenient, exaggerated, or not applicable to me? The verses on honest measure? On backbiting? On the rights of the poor? On the prayer? Each truth set aside is a "denial," and each will be present, as *hādhā*, "this," on that Day. The wisdom is to bring it near *now*, while nearness can still be met with obedience rather than with regret.

**The Contrast About to Unfold**

This verse closes the second section of the sūrah (vv. 7–17), the portrait of the *fujjār*. The portrait was structured with rigorous symmetry: their record (in Sijjīn, a sealed book), their attitude (denial of the Judgment, dismissal of the signs), their inner condition (the rusted heart), and their end (veiled, burned, rebuked). The next verse opens the third section with a single word that reverses everything: *kallā* — Nay — and then *the book of the pious is in ʿIlliyyūn*. Every element of the deniers' portrait will now be mirrored, point for point, in the portrait of the righteous. The reader who has followed the descent into Sijjīn is about to be lifted, verse by verse, into the heights.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:18

> **Nay, truly the book of the pious is in ʿIlliyyūn.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Hinge of the Sūrah: From Descent to Ascent**

With this verse the sūrah turns. Everything from verse 7 to verse 17 has been descent: the book of the wicked in the lowest prison, the rusted heart, the veil, the Fire, the rebuke. Now, with a single word — *kallā*, "Nay!" — the direction reverses: *inna kitāba al-abrāri la-fī ʿIlliyyīn*, "truly the book of the pious is in ʿIlliyyūn." The verse is the exact structural mirror of verse 7: *Nay! Truly the book of the profligate is in Sijjīn.* Same particle, same emphasis, same construction — and the opposite content. The Qur'an builds the two halves of the sūrah as a diptych, two panels facing each other, so that every feature of one is answered by a feature of the other. The reader is meant to see them side by side and to choose.

The *kallā* here has the same double function as before: it rebukes and it affirms. It rebukes the deniers' assumption that the righteous — the people they mock as "astray" (v. 32) — have nothing to show for their integrity. And it affirms with force: truly, certainly, the book of the pious is in the heights.

**Who Are the *Abrār*?**

The righteous are named *al-abrār*, the plural of *barr* (or *bārr*), from the root *b-r-r*. The root's basic meaning is *expansiveness* and *goodness*: *barr* is the wide open land (as opposed to the sea, *baḥr*), and *birr* is righteousness, goodness, piety in its broadest and most comprehensive sense. The connection between "wide land" and "goodness" is instructive: *birr* is goodness that is *expansive* — generous, open, not narrow or grudging. The *barr* is the person whose goodness is wide, who gives more than is required, whose virtue overflows.

The *abrār* are therefore the exact opposite of the *muṭaffifīn*. The defrauder is narrow: he skims, withholds, gives a fraction. The *barr* is expansive: he gives in full and beyond. The Qur'an's choice of this word for the righteous in this particular sūrah is not accidental; it names the virtue that specifically corrects the vice of the sūrah's opening.

The Qur'an's most comprehensive definition of *birr* is in Sūrah al-Baqarah:

> *"Righteousness (al-birr) is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but righteousness is one who believes in God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets; and gives wealth, despite love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask, and for freeing slaves; and establishes prayer and gives zakāh; and those who fulfill their promise when they promise; and those who are patient in poverty and hardship and during battle. Those are the ones who have been true, and it is those who are the God-fearing."* (Qur'an 2:177)

Every element of this definition contrasts with the defrauder: belief in the Last Day (which he denies), giving wealth despite loving it (he takes wealth despite others' need), fulfilling promises (his measure is a broken promise), and truthfulness (his scale is a lie). The *abrār* are those who live 2:177.

The Prophet ﷺ tied *birr* to its expansive, generous nature in a ḥadīth recorded by Muslim:

> *"Righteousness (al-birr) is good character, and sin is what wavers in your breast and you would dislike people to discover."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2553)

This definition is devastating for the defrauder: sin is *what you would dislike people to discover*. The short measure is precisely that — an act that depends on concealment. The *barr*, by contrast, has nothing to conceal; his scale can be inspected by anyone.

**ʿIlliyyūn: The Name of the Heights**

*ʿIlliyyūn* (in the genitive, *ʿIlliyyīn*, as it appears here) is one of the most linguistically remarkable words in the Qur'an. It is built on the root *ʿ-l-w*, which means *height, elevation, exaltation* — the root of *al-ʿAliyy*, "the Most High," one of God's names, and of *aʿlā*, "higher, highest," and *ʿulūw*, "loftiness." The form *ʿilliyy* is an intensive adjective meaning "extremely high, of the highest degree." The ending *-ūn/-īn* is the sound masculine plural ending, which is ordinarily used only for rational beings — persons, not places. The word is therefore grammatically anomalous: it is a *place* or *thing* given the plural form reserved for *persons*.

The grammarians and commentators addressed this anomaly, and their explanations illuminate the verse. Al-Farrāʾ and al-Zajjāj said that the Arabs sometimes used this plural form for things whose singular was unknown or unusual, to indicate *greatness* and *magnitude*, or that the plural indicates *degree upon degree*, height upon height — not one high place but many, ascending without limit. Some said the form implies that ʿIlliyyūn is "inhabited" by exalted beings — that it is not a bare place but a place of the angels brought near. The source commentary notes the range: high places, persons who sit in high places, those who dwell in the heights of mountains, and heights beyond counting. All these senses converge on a single idea: *supreme elevation*, expressed with such intensity that ordinary grammar had to be stretched to contain it.

The contrast with Sijjīn is total. *Sijjīn* is the intensive form of *prison* — confinement, depth, constriction, the lowest. *ʿIlliyyūn* is the intensive plural of *height* — expansion, elevation, openness, the highest. The books of the two parties are filed at the two extremes of the cosmos.

**Where Is ʿIlliyyūn? The Reports of the Early Scholars**

As with Sijjīn, the early authorities transmitted several views, recorded by al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that ʿIlliyyūn is Paradise itself, or that it is the seventh heaven. Kaʿb al-Aḥbār and Qatādah said it is "the right leg of the Throne" or "near the Throne." Al-Ḍaḥḥāk said it is the Lote Tree of the Furthest Boundary (*Sidrat al-Muntahā*, 53:14), which the Qur'an describes as the site of the Garden of Refuge and the limit beyond which no created being ascends. Others said it is the seventh heaven, where the souls of the believers are gathered. The source commentary records the report attributed to the Imam Muḥammad al-Bāqir: "Sijjīn is the seventh earth, and ʿIlliyyūn is the seventh heaven." Al-Qurṭubī records both the view that ʿIlliyyūn is in the seventh heaven beneath the Throne and the view that it is above the seven heavens, to the right of the Throne.

The decisive textual evidence is the ḥadīth of al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib on the journey of the soul — the same ḥadīth that establishes the location of Sijjīn — recorded by Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd, al-Nasāʾī, and Ibn Mājah, and graded authentic. In it, the Prophet ﷺ describes how the soul of the believer, after death, is taken up by angels, the gates of each heaven are opened for it, and it is escorted from heaven to heaven until it reaches the seventh, whereupon:

> *"God — Mighty and Majestic — says, 'Write the book of My servant in ʿIlliyyūn, and return him to the earth [for the questioning in the grave].'"* (Musnad Aḥmad 18534; Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4753)

This ḥadīth settles the essential point: ʿIlliyyūn is in the highest heaven, near God's Throne, and the "book" of verse 18 is written there at the very moment the believing soul completes its ascent after death. The believer's record is filed in the presence of the highest angels, in the place nearest to God's Throne. The defrauder's is filed in the lowest prison. Where one's record is kept is a preview of where one's soul is going.

**Why the Records Are Kept at the Extremes**

The placement of the two books at the opposite poles of creation is not arbitrary; it expresses a principle that runs throughout the Qur'an. Goodness *elevates*; wrongdoing *debases*. This is not merely a metaphor; it describes what happens to the human soul. The Qur'an says of good words and deeds:

> *"To Him ascends the good word, and righteous deed raises it."* (Qur'an 35:10)

Deeds have a *direction*. A righteous deed rises toward God; it is by nature elevated and elevating. A wicked deed sinks; it drags its author downward. The record is kept where the deeds naturally go. The *abrār* — whose goodness is expansive, generous, overflowing — produce deeds that ascend, and their record ascends with them to ʿIlliyyūn. The defrauder produces deeds that sink, and his record sinks to Sijjīn.

There is a further logic. The believer, in this life, *oriented himself upward*. He turned to the Most High in prayer; he sought the Hereafter over the world; he gave full measure because he looked to a reward above the transaction. His record is kept in the direction he faced. The defrauder oriented himself downward — to the earth, to its coins and grains, to the immediate and material. His record is kept in the direction he faced. Each party's book is filed in the direction of his gaze.

**The Book of the Pious: What Is Written There**

The "book" of the *abrār* is the record of their deeds — the same kind of record as the book of the *fujjār*, kept by the same recording angels (82:10–12), but with utterly different contents. What is in it? The honest measures. The full prayers. The debts paid. The wages delivered on time. The promises kept. The faults forgiven. The half-dates given in charity. The moments when the believer could have skimmed and did not. And, by the mercy described in the ḥadīth of al-Bukhārī and Muslim cited earlier, every good deed multiplied ten to seven hundred times, every intended good counted even when not performed, and every intended evil left undone written as a good deed. The book of the *abrār* is not merely a fair record; it is a record kept by a generous Accountant who tips the balance — *arjiḥ* — toward His servant.

**The Verse as Consolation and Motivation**

For the believer who has been cheated, mocked, or dismissed, this verse is a direct consolation. The defrauder took your grams; your loss is written in ʿIlliyyūn. The scoffer called you astray; your patience is written in ʿIlliyyūn. The world did not notice your honesty; it is written in ʿIlliyyūn, witnessed by the nearest angels, filed beside the Throne. Nothing you did rightly is lost, and it is kept in the highest place there is.

And for every reader, the verse is motivation: the book is being written *now*. Every day adds pages. The question is not whether one's record exists but where it will be filed — and the filing follows the deeds. The one who wants his book in ʿIlliyyūn has only to live the life of the *abrār*: expansive where the defrauder is narrow, honest where he is deceptive, full-measured where he is skimming. The next verse will pause, as it did for Sijjīn, to magnify what has just been named — and the magnification, this time, is of a height rather than a depth.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19

> **And what will apprise thee of ʿIlliyyūn?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Formula of Magnitude Applied to the Heights**

The verse repeats, for ʿIlliyyūn, the formula that verse 8 applied to Sijjīn: *wa mā adrāka mā ʿIlliyyūn* — "And what will apprise thee of ʿIlliyyūn?" The symmetry is deliberate and complete. Just as the depth of the wicked's confinement was beyond human knowledge, so the height of the righteous's elevation is beyond human knowledge. The Qur'an magnifies the reward with the same rhetorical instrument it used to magnify the punishment, so that the reader understands that the two are matched in scale: as far down as Sijjīn goes, so far up does ʿIlliyyūn rise.

The pattern *wa mā adrāka* (past tense, "what has apprised you") signals, as always in the Qur'an, that an explanation will follow — and it does, in verses 20–21: *A book inscribed, witnessed by those brought nigh.* But the pause between the naming and the explanation is itself meaningful. It is a pause of wonder.

**The Difference in Tone Between the Two Questions**

Although the words are identical, the two occurrences of *wa mā adrāka* in this sūrah carry opposite emotional colorings, and the attentive reader feels the difference. In verse 8, the question was a warning: what do you know of the prison in which your record lies? Its tone is dread. In verse 19, the question is an invitation: what do you know of the heights in which your record lies? Its tone is longing. The commentators note that the *tafkhīm* (magnification) of the formula serves *targhīb* (arousing desire) as much as *tarhīb* (arousing fear). The Qur'an is not only warning the defrauder away from Sijjīn; it is drawing the believer toward ʿIlliyyūn.

This balance between fear and hope is a defining feature of Qur'anic pedagogy. The scholars describe the believer as one who travels toward God on two wings, *khawf* (fear) and *rajāʾ* (hope), and they insist that neither wing alone will carry him: fear alone breeds despair, hope alone breeds complacency. Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn is built precisely on these two wings. Verses 7–17 are fear; verses 18–28 are hope. The two *wa mā adrāka* questions are the pivots of each.

**Why the Heights Are Beyond Our Knowledge**

The rhetorical question asserts that human beings cannot know what ʿIlliyyūn truly is except by revelation. This is worth pausing over. The reward of the righteous is repeatedly described in the Qur'an as *exceeding* human conception:

> *"No soul knows what has been hidden for them of comfort for the eyes, as a reward for what they used to do."* (Qur'an 32:17)

The Prophet ﷺ transmitted from his Lord, in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and what has never occurred to the heart of any human being."* Then he said: *Recite if you wish: "No soul knows what has been hidden for them of comfort for the eyes."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3244; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2824)

The source commentary records that Ibn ʿAbbās connected this very verse of Sūrah al-Sajdah (32:17) with the description of Tasnīm later in this sūrah, and the connection applies equally to ʿIlliyyūn: the record of the righteous is kept in a place whose reality no human heart has conceived. The words the Qur'an uses — "a book inscribed," "witnessed by those brought nigh" — are true, but they are the shadow of a reality larger than the words.

This has a practical bearing. Human beings are motivated by what they can imagine, and the imagination is limited by experience. A person who has never seen the ocean cannot be fully motivated by descriptions of it. The Qur'an, by telling us that ʿIlliyyūn exceeds our imagination, asks us to *trust* — to strive for a reward we cannot picture on the strength of the One who promises it. This trust is itself the essence of faith in the Unseen (2:3). The defrauder would not give up a visible gram for an invisible reward; the believer gives up the visible gram precisely *because* he trusts the promise of the invisible. The question *what will apprise thee of ʿIlliyyūn?* is, in effect, an invitation to that trust.

**Addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, and His Own Station**

As in verse 8, the "thee" is grammatically the Prophet ﷺ. There is a particular fittingness in asking *him* what he knows of ʿIlliyyūn, for he is the one human being who was taken up through the seven heavens on the Night Journey and Ascension (*al-Isrāʾ wa-l-Miʿrāj*) and shown the Lote Tree of the Furthest Boundary — the very place some commentators identify with ʿIlliyyūn. The Qur'an describes that vision:

> *"And he certainly saw him in another descent, at the Lote Tree of the Furthest Boundary, near which is the Garden of Refuge, when there covered the Lote Tree that which covered it. The sight did not swerve, nor did it transgress. He certainly saw of the greatest signs of his Lord."* (Qur'an 53:13–18)

Even the Prophet ﷺ, who had ascended to that height, is told: what could make you know what ʿIlliyyūn is? The point is not to diminish his knowledge but to indicate that the reality exceeds even what he was shown, or that its full significance — as the repository of the records of all the righteous throughout history, witnessed by the nearest angels — is a matter God alone comprehends. And through him, every reader is told the same: your finest imagination of the reward falls short.

**A Reflection on Human Horizons**

The verse invites a reflection on the scale of human ambition. The defrauder's horizon was the brim of a measuring cup: he could see the difference between full and almost-full, and that difference was the whole of his concern. The Qur'an sets against that tiny horizon a place whose height cannot be told. The contrast is a lesson in *aspiration*. Human beings become what they aim at. The person who aims at grams becomes a grasper of grams; the person who aims at ʿIlliyyūn becomes, gradually, a person fit for ʿIlliyyūn. The Prophet ﷺ taught his community to aim high, in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī:

> *"When you ask God, ask Him for al-Firdaws, for it is the middle of Paradise and the highest of Paradise, and above it is the Throne of the Most Merciful."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2790)

"The highest of Paradise, and above it is the Throne" — this is the region of ʿIlliyyūn. The believer is not told to be modest in his hopes; he is told to ask for the highest, because the One who gives is not limited by the smallness of the one who asks.

**Preparation for the Explanation**

The verse leaves the reader suspended, waiting to hear what ʿIlliyyūn is. The suspension does its work: it opens the heart to receive the explanation with more attention than it would give to a bare statement. When the next verses arrive — *A book inscribed, witnessed by those brought nigh* — they land in a mind that has been prepared to receive them as the answer to a question it now genuinely wants answered. That is the Qur'an's art: it does not merely inform; it creates the desire to know, and then it satisfies the desire in a way that leaves the reader wanting to *become* what he has been told about.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20

> **A book inscribed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Words, the Opposite Meaning**

The answer to the question *What will apprise thee of ʿIlliyyūn?* begins with exactly the words that answered the question about Sijjīn: *kitābun marqūm* — "a book inscribed." The verbal identity between verse 9 and verse 20 is one of the sūrah's most striking features, and it teaches a lesson that the identical wording forces upon the reader: the *mechanism* of recording is the same for the righteous and the wicked. There are not two systems of accounting, a lenient one for the pious and a strict one for the profligate. There is one system — one set of recording angels, one standard of exactitude, one indelible script — and it produces a book of shame for one party and a book of honor for the other, according solely to what each party *wrote* by his deeds.

The commentators, as the source notes, divide as they did for verse 9: most (al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Zamakhsharī) hold that *kitābun marqūm* describes the *book of the righteous*, and that the sentence structure is: "The book of the righteous is in ʿIlliyyūn — and what is that book? — a book inscribed." A minority, recorded by al-Rāzī, holds that it describes *ʿIlliyyūn itself*, in which case *kitāb* would have the sense of *kitābah*, "writing": ʿIlliyyūn is a place of inscribed writing, the register in which the deeds of the pious are recorded. On either reading the essential meaning holds: the record of the righteous is a clear, permanent, sealed inscription.

**Every Shade of *Marqūm*, Read in the Light of Mercy**

The word *marqūm* carries the same shades of meaning it carried in verse 9, but each one, applied to the book of the righteous, becomes a promise rather than a threat.

*Marqūm* as *clearly written, in bold legible script*: the good deeds of the *abrār* are not recorded faintly or doubtfully. Every honest measure, every full prayer, every kind word is written so clearly that it cannot be overlooked or disputed. The believer sometimes fears that his small acts of goodness are too insignificant to be noticed; the verse says they are written in bold.

*Marqūm* as *sealed, so nothing can be added or removed*: nothing the righteous did will be lost. The seal that terrified the wicked — because it meant their sins could not be erased — comforts the righteous, because it means their good cannot be erased either. No enemy can delete it; no passage of time can fade it; no forgetfulness — not even the believer's own forgetfulness of what he did — can remove it. The Qur'an promises: *"Truly We do not let the reward of one who does good go to waste"* (18:30).

*Marqūm* as *distinguished, classified*: the good deeds of the righteous are sorted and marked — recognized for what they are, credited to the right categories, assigned their full weight. A person's charity is recorded as charity, his patience as patience, his honesty as honesty, each with its own reward.

*Marqūm* as *numbered, counted*: the good of the righteous is *quantified*, and by the accounting the Prophet ﷺ described, the quantification is generous — ten to seven hundred times, and beyond. The number written is larger than the deed done.

**The Book of the Righteous in Other Verses**

The Qur'an describes the presentation of this book on the Day of Judgment in terms of pure joy:

> *"Then as for one who is given his record in his right hand, he will say, 'Here, read my record! Indeed, I was certain that I would meet my account.' So he will be in a pleasant life, in an elevated garden, whose fruits are near at hand. 'Eat and drink in satisfaction for what you put forth in the days past.'"* (Qur'an 69:19–24)

Several features of this passage connect to Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn. The righteous person *shows* his book to others — *"Here, read my record!"* — because he has nothing to hide; his scale, so to speak, is open to inspection. He gives the reason for his righteousness: *"I was certain that I would meet my account"* — which is exactly the belief the defrauder lacked (v. 4). And he is placed in *"an elevated garden"* — *ʿāliyah*, from the same root as *ʿIlliyyūn*. The book that was filed in the heights brings its owner to the heights.

And in Sūrah al-Inshiqāq, revealed in the same period:

> *"Then as for one who is given his record in his right hand, he will be judged with an easy account, and return to his people in happiness."* (Qur'an 84:7–9)

The "easy account" (*ḥisāban yasīran*) is explained by the Prophet ﷺ in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from ʿĀʾishah: it is the mere *presentation* (*ʿarḍ*) of the record — the person is shown his deeds and passes on, without being interrogated over each one. He said: *"Whoever is interrogated in the account is destroyed."* The righteous is spared interrogation. His book is inscribed, sealed, and presented; he reads it with joy and goes to his people.

**Why the Righteous Need to Know Their Deeds Are Recorded**

Why does the Qur'an tell the *abrār* about their book? The wicked needed to be warned that their deeds are recorded; but the righteous are not cheating — why remind them of the record? The answer reveals a deep understanding of human motivation.

The righteous person, more than anyone, is subject to a particular temptation: *discouragement*. He gives full measure and is not thanked. He works honestly while the dishonest prosper. He is patient with people who mock him. He prays when no one sees. Over years, the thought creeps in: does any of this matter? Is anyone keeping account? The Qur'an answers with *kitābun marqūm*: yes. Every act is written, in bold, sealed, in the highest place. Nothing is wasted. This knowledge is the fuel that sustains righteousness over a lifetime. The Qur'an says elsewhere:

> *"And whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it."* (Qur'an 99:7)

*An atom's weight.* The unit is the same as the defrauder's skimmed gram — and just as the gram of theft is recorded, so is the gram of goodness. The believer who knows this does not need to be seen or thanked by anyone; he is writing for a Reader who misses nothing.

**The Record and the Fear of Forgetting**

There is a further consolation in the seal. Human beings forget most of what they do. The believer, at the end of a long life, will not remember the ten thousand small honesties, the ten thousand restrained words, the ten thousand quiet prayers. He may stand before God feeling that he has done little. The inscribed book remembers what he forgot. The Qur'an describes the scene of the wicked being confronted with deeds they had forgotten — *"God has enumerated it while they forgot it"* (58:6) — and the same enumeration works in the righteous person's favor: God has counted what the believer forgot, and it is all in the book.

**The Symmetry as a Choice**

The identical phrasing of verses 9 and 20 sets the choice before the reader with unusual clarity. There will be a book. It will be inscribed. It will be sealed. These are fixed. The only variable is its *contents* and therefore its *location* — Sijjīn or ʿIlliyyūn. And the contents are determined entirely by what the reader does with his hands, his tongue, his scale, his prayer, and his heart, in the years before the seal is set. The Qur'an gives no third option. It presents the same words twice, once for each destination, and asks: which book is yours being written into?

The next verse will add the one element that the book of the wicked lacked: *witnesses* — and the witnesses of the book of the righteous are the highest beings in creation.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:21

> **witnessed by those brought nigh.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One Element the Book of the Wicked Did Not Have**

The description of the book of the righteous has, up to this point, matched the description of the book of the wicked word for word: a book, in a designated place, inscribed. Now the symmetry is deliberately broken. The book of the wicked was described in verse 9 and then the sūrah moved on; nothing further was said of it. The book of the righteous receives one additional clause: *yashhaduhu al-muqarrabūn* — "witnessed by those brought nigh." This is the honor added to the record of the *abrār* and withheld from the record of the *fujjār*. The wicked's book lies in its prison, unattended. The righteous's book is *attended* — witnessed, honored, kept in the presence of the highest beings in creation.

The verb *yashhadu* is in the present-continuous: they *witness* it, are present at it, attend it, on an ongoing basis. The root *sh-h-d* means to be present, to see with one's own eyes, and to bear testimony. All three senses are active here: the *muqarrabūn* are *present* at the book, they *see* what is written in it, and they *testify* to it.

**Who Are the *Muqarrabūn*?**

The word *muqarrabūn* is the passive participle of *qarraba*, "to bring near," from the root *q-r-b* (nearness). The *muqarrabūn* are literally "those who have been brought near" — near to God. The passive form is significant: they did not bring themselves near; God brought them. Nearness to God is a gift He bestows, not a position one seizes.

The source commentary records the two main interpretations, and both are well grounded in the Qur'an's usage of the word.

The first interpretation, held by al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabrisī among others, is that the *muqarrabūn* are the *angels brought near* — the highest ranks of angels, who dwell in the presence of God's Throne. The Qur'an uses the word for angels in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ: *"The Messiah would never disdain to be a servant of God, nor would the angels brought near (al-malāʾikatu al-muqarrabūn)"* (4:172). On this reading, the book of the righteous is kept in the seventh heaven, near the Throne, in the presence of the angels who stand closest to God, and they witness what is written in it.

The second interpretation is that the *muqarrabūn* are the *elite among the righteous* — the foremost of the believers, whom the Qur'an describes in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah as a class distinct from and above the ordinary righteous:

> *"And the foremost, the foremost — those are the ones brought near (al-muqarrabūn), in Gardens of Bliss."* (Qur'an 56:10–12)

In that sūrah, the people of Paradise are divided into two groups: the *Companions of the Right* (*aṣḥāb al-yamīn*) — the general body of the righteous — and the *muqarrabūn* — the foremost, the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the greatest of the saints. On this reading, the book of the *abrār* is witnessed by the highest human beings, who are honored with knowledge of the records of their brethren.

Many commentators, as the source notes, combine the two: the *muqarrabūn* are all who are brought near, angel and human alike. This combined reading is attractive because the sūrah itself will use *muqarrabūn* again in verse 28 — *"a spring whence drink those brought nigh"* — in a context that clearly refers to human beings in Paradise, while the angelic sense is the more natural one for witnessing a book in the seventh heaven. The Qur'an may intend both: the book of the righteous is witnessed by the highest of angels *and* known to the highest of the righteous.

**Why Witnesses Are an Honor**

Why should having one's record *witnessed* be a mark of honor? The answer connects to the deepest human needs and to the sūrah's opening theme.

In this world, the righteous person's goodness is often *unwitnessed*. The defrauder cheats in secret; but the honest merchant is also honest in secret — no one sees him fill the measure to the brim when he could have skimmed. No one sees the night prayer, the private charity, the swallowed insult, the temptation refused in an empty room. The good of the *abrār* is, by its nature, mostly invisible to human eyes. The Qur'an tells them: it is not invisible. It has witnesses — and not ordinary witnesses, but the beings nearest to God. What no human saw, the *muqarrabūn* see and attest.

There is also the matter of *honor by association*. In human societies, a document gains dignity from the rank of those who witness it: a contract witnessed by a judge, a treaty witnessed by ambassadors, a marriage witnessed by respected elders. The book of the righteous is witnessed by the highest beings in existence. This is God's way of *honoring* the deeds of His servants — of showing that He regards their small acts of honesty as worthy of attestation at the highest level.

And there is the matter of *security*. A witnessed document cannot be disputed. On the Day of Judgment, when the deniers might wish to claim that the righteous were not truly righteous, or that their deeds were not what they seemed, the *muqarrabūn* stand as witnesses. The record of the *abrār* is secured against every challenge.

**The Contrast with the Mockers' Witness**

This verse sets up a contrast that the sūrah will develop in its final section. In verses 29–33, the wicked will be shown "witnessing" the believers in a different sense — watching them, laughing at them, winking at one another, and pronouncing judgment: *"Truly these are astray!"* The wicked appointed themselves as witnesses and judges over the believers, though, as verse 33 will say, *they were not sent as guardians over them*. Against that false, mocking witness, this verse sets the true one: the believers' record is witnessed by the *muqarrabūn*. The wicked's assessment of the believers is worthless; the angels' attestation is decisive. The believer who is mocked by the powerful in this world may hold in his heart the knowledge that his book has better witnesses than they.

**The Angels' Involvement in the Believer's Life**

This verse belongs to a broader Qur'anic and prophetic picture of the angels as deeply involved in the affairs of the righteous. The angels record their deeds (82:10–12), pray for them (*"those who bear the Throne and those around it ... ask forgiveness for those who believe,"* 40:7), attend their gatherings of remembrance, and, as this verse says, witness their records. The Prophet ﷺ described the angels' attendance at the gatherings of the righteous in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"God has angels who roam the roads seeking out the people of remembrance. When they find people remembering God, they call to one another, 'Come to what you are seeking!' And they surround them with their wings up to the lowest heaven. Then their Lord asks them — though He knows best — 'What are My servants saying?'..."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6408; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2689)

The ḥadīth continues with God declaring, before the angels, that He has forgiven the people of remembrance. The angels are witnesses of the believers' goodness in this world, and this verse says they remain witnesses of its record in the next. The believer is never as alone as he feels; his unwitnessed goodness has an audience of the nearest angels.

**The Scholarly Debate and Its Resolution**

Al-Ṭabarī, after recording the various views on the *muqarrabūn*, favors the interpretation that they are the angels of each heaven, who witness the book of the righteous as it is carried up and deposited in ʿIlliyyūn — this matches the ḥadīth of al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib, in which the believer's soul is escorted by the angels of each heaven to the seventh, and its book is written there. Ibn Kathīr cites the same ḥadīth and the view that the *muqarrabūn* are the angels, while noting the alternative. The resolution most commentators adopt is that both senses are true and intended: the book is witnessed by the angels brought near at its deposition, and its owner will be in the company of the humans brought near at its presentation.

**Application: Living Before Honored Witnesses**

The practical teaching of this verse is a transformation of *audience*. Human beings are profoundly shaped by who they believe is watching. The defrauder's audience was the customer — and when the customer was not looking, he had no audience at all, and so he cheated. The believer's audience is the *muqarrabūn*: the nearest angels, the foremost of the righteous, and behind them God Himself. He is never unobserved, and his observers are of the highest rank. This transforms the quality of unwitnessed acts. The measure filled in an empty shop is filled before the angels of the seventh heaven. The prayer offered alone at night is offered before the *muqarrabūn*. The insult swallowed with no one to admire the restraint is witnessed by beings who will attest to it beside the Throne.

The believer who internalizes this does not need human recognition. He performs for a nobler audience. And he finds, as the next verses will describe, that the reward for performing before that audience is to be seated among them.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:22

> **Truly the pious shall be in bliss,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Record to the Reward**

The sūrah has described the *record* of the righteous — a book inscribed, kept in ʿIlliyyūn, witnessed by those brought near. Now it turns from the record to the *reward*: *inna al-abrāra la-fī naʿīm* — "Truly the pious shall be in bliss." This verse is the mirror of verse 16, *"Then they will burn in Hellfire"* (*la-ṣālū al-jaḥīm*). The wicked are *in* the Jaḥīm; the righteous are *in* Naʿīm. The two words rhyme in the Arabic — *jaḥīm* / *naʿīm* — and stand as the two destinations of the two books.

The construction is emphatic in the same manner as the earlier verses: *inna* (truly) + *la-* (surely) + *fī naʿīm* (in bliss). And the tense is instructive. The Arabic is a nominal sentence without a verb, which conveys *permanence* and *settled state* rather than an action in time: the righteous are not merely "going to be" in bliss; they *are* in bliss — bliss is their condition, their element, the place where they belong. Some commentators note that this timelessness allows the verse to be read as describing the righteous even now: they are in a state of inner bliss in this world, and in the fullness of bliss in the next.

**The Word *Naʿīm*: Bliss as Abundant Ease**

*Naʿīm* comes from the root *n-ʿ-m*, which carries the meanings of softness, ease, pleasantness, comfort, and blessing. From it come *niʿmah* (a blessing, a favor), *anʿama* (to bestow blessing — the verb of the Fātiḥah's "those upon whom You have bestowed blessing"), *nāʿim* (soft, delicate, comfortable), and *naʿam* (yes — the word of assent and contentment). *Naʿīm* is the state of being *enveloped* in blessing: comprehensive comfort, delight, and ease, with nothing harsh, nothing lacking, nothing that grates.

The Qur'an uses *naʿīm* as the general term for the bliss of Paradise, often in the phrase *jannāt al-naʿīm*, "Gardens of Bliss," which the source commentary lists across many sūrahs (5:65; 10:9; 22:56; 37:43; 52:17; 56:12). The word describes the *quality* of Paradise rather than its geography: Paradise is *naʿīm* — soft, easy, delightful, without a single sharp edge. This is the opposite of the sūrah's opening theme: the defrauder lived at the *edge* (*ṭaff*), always scraping, calculating, straining to keep a fraction. The righteous are in *naʿīm*, where there is no edge, no scraping, no fraction withheld.

**The *Abrār* Named Again: The Expansive Ones in the Expansive Reward**

The verse names the righteous again as *al-abrār*, and the repetition invites a reflection on the fit between the name and the reward. *Birr*, as noted in verse 18, is goodness that is *wide* — the root also gives *barr*, the wide open land. The *abrār* were expansive in this life: generous where they could have been tight, full where they could have skimmed, forgiving where they could have held a grudge. Their reward is *naʿīm*, which is *comprehensive* ease — an expansive reward for expansive people. The tight-fisted defrauder receives the tight confinement of Sijjīn; the open-handed *barr* receives the open ease of Naʿīm. The measure one gives is the measure one receives.

The Prophet ﷺ described this principle of reciprocity in a ḥadīth recorded by Muslim:

> *"Whoever relieves a believer of a hardship of the hardships of this world, God will relieve him of a hardship of the hardships of the Day of Resurrection. Whoever makes things easy for one in difficulty, God will make things easy for him in this world and the Hereafter."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2699)

*Naʿīm* is God's ease given to those who gave ease. The one who did not press the scale against his customer will not have the scale pressed against him.

**The Reality of Paradise: Physical and Spiritual**

Islamic teaching insists that the bliss of Paradise is *real* — physical as well as spiritual — and this verse's plain language supports that insistence. The *abrār* are *in* bliss, as a person is in a place, as a body is in a garden. The Qur'an describes that bliss in concrete terms: gardens, rivers, fruit, shade, garments, couches, companions, food and drink. Some have tried to read these descriptions as mere metaphors for spiritual states; the mainstream of Islamic scholarship, from the Companions onward, has affirmed their reality while acknowledging that the realities exceed the words. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the ḥadīth already cited, that Paradise contains *"what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and what has never occurred to the heart of any human being"* — which means the descriptions are true but *understated*, not false or merely figurative. Ibn ʿAbbās said: "There is nothing in Paradise that resembles anything in this world except in name."

The reason the Qur'an describes Paradise in physical terms deserves reflection. Human beings are not disembodied minds; they are souls in bodies, and their desires — for beauty, rest, companionship, taste, comfort — are not evil in themselves but are the very faculties by which they can be motivated toward good. The Qur'an does not despise these desires; it *redirects* them. It says: you desire ease, comfort, and delight — good; then obtain them in their perfect form by the road of righteousness, rather than in their corrupted form by the road of fraud. The defrauder wanted a little more comfort and stole to get it. The Qur'an offers comfort without limit to the one who refrains from stealing. It answers the same desire with a better fulfillment.

**The Spiritual Core of *Naʿīm***

At the same time, the classical scholars were clear that the *heart* of the bliss of Paradise is not physical. It is, first, the *pleasure of God* (*riḍwān*):

> *"God has promised the believing men and believing women gardens beneath which rivers flow, wherein they abide eternally, and pleasant dwellings in Gardens of Eden; but the good pleasure of God is greater. That is the great success."* (Qur'an 9:72)

*"But the good pleasure of God is greater"* — greater than the gardens and the dwellings. And, second, it is the *vision of God*, which the sūrah has already alluded to in reverse (v. 15) and will allude to again (v. 23). The ḥadīth of Ṣuhayb in Muslim says the people of Paradise are given nothing more beloved than the gazing upon their Lord. *Naʿīm*, in its fullness, is the physical ease of the Garden *and* the spiritual ease of being with the One the soul was made for. The defrauder, veiled and burned, is deprived of both; the *barr*, in *naʿīm*, has both.

**Bliss in This Life as a Foretaste**

Several scholars, including Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim, drew attention to the verbless present tense of this verse and taught that the *abrār* are in a kind of *naʿīm* even in this world. Ibn Taymiyyah's famous saying, transmitted by Ibn al-Qayyim, is: "Truly there is a paradise in this world; whoever does not enter it will not enter the paradise of the Hereafter." He meant the inner peace, contentment, and sweetness of faith that the righteous experience even amid hardship. Ibn al-Qayyim, in his *al-Wābil al-Ṣayyib*, listed the fruits of righteousness in this life: light in the face, expansion in the breast, love in the hearts of people, and a sweetness in the heart that outweighs every worldly pleasure.

This is confirmed by experience. The honest person sleeps soundly; the defrauder lies awake calculating whether he will be caught. The person who gives full measure meets his customers with an open face; the skimmer avoids their eyes. The one who has nothing to hide moves through the world at ease; the one who conceals is always tense. The *naʿīm* of the Hereafter is the completion of a *naʿīm* that begins the moment a person decides to be *barr*.

The Prophet ﷺ described the sweetness available in this life in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"Three things — whoever has them has found the sweetness of faith: that God and His Messenger are more beloved to him than anything else; that he loves a person only for the sake of God; and that he hates to return to disbelief after God has saved him from it, as he would hate to be thrown into the Fire."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 16; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 43)

**The Verse as the Turning of the Scale**

Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn began with a scale that was tilted. This verse is the scale set right. The one who gave full measure receives the fullest measure there is. The Qur'an's vision of justice is not merely that the wicked are punished, but that the righteous are *rewarded beyond their deeds* — that the balance tips, by God's generosity, in the believer's favor:

> *"Whoever brings a good deed will have ten times its like; and whoever brings an evil deed will be recompensed only with its like, and they will not be wronged."* (Qur'an 6:160)

Ten for one. The defrauder skimmed a fraction; God gives the righteous a tenfold surplus. *Naʿīm* is the name of that surplus — the overflowing measure that God pours for those who never let their own measure fall short. The verses that follow will describe this bliss in specific detail: the couches, the gazing, the radiant faces, the sealed wine, the musk, the spring called Tasnīm.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:23

> **upon couches, gazing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Detail of Bliss: Rest and Sight**

The sūrah now begins to fill in the picture of *naʿīm* with specific images, and the first is a posture and an activity: *ʿalā al-arāʾiki yanẓurūn* — "upon couches, gazing." Two words carry the whole verse. The righteous are *reclining* — at rest, secure, established — and they are *looking* — active, engaged, delighted. The combination captures the paradox of perfect bliss: total rest without boredom, total engagement without effort.

This verse occurs twice in the sūrah, here and again in verse 35, where the believers on couches are gazing in a different direction — at the fate of those who mocked them. The repetition frames the third and fourth sections of the sūrah, and the two occurrences will be compared below.

**The Word *Arāʾik*: Couches of Honor**

*Arāʾik* is the plural of *arīkah*, a word the lexicographers define with some precision: it is a raised couch or throne, usually within a canopy or curtained alcove (*ḥajalah*), adorned and cushioned, of the kind used by kings and brides. The Arabs did not call any bed an *arīkah*; the word implied elevation, adornment, privacy, and honor. It appears in the Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise in several places:

> *"They and their spouses will be in shade, reclining upon couches (arāʾik)."* (Qur'an 36:56)

> *"Reclining therein upon couches (arāʾik), they will see there neither sun nor bitter cold."* (Qur'an 76:13)

> *"Those will have Gardens of Eden beneath which rivers flow; they will be adorned therein with bracelets of gold and will wear green garments of fine silk and brocade, reclining therein upon couches (arāʾik). Excellent is the reward, and beautiful the resting place."* (Qur'an 18:31)

The image is one of *sovereignty*. In this world the righteous were often the low — the mocked, the poor, the ones the wealthy sinners laughed at (v. 29). In the next they recline on the couches of kings. The Qur'an says of the people of Paradise: *"And when you look there, you will see bliss and a great kingdom"* (76:20). The couch is the seat of that kingdom.

The couch also signifies *security*. One reclines only when one is safe — when there is no threat to stand against, no task left undone, no anxiety pressing. The defrauder, in this life, never truly reclined; he was always calculating, watching for the inspector, guarding his secret. The righteous, having lived openly, recline in the open ease of a place where nothing threatens. The Qur'an describes the people of Paradise as those to whom it is said: *"Enter it in peace, secure"* (15:46).

**The Word *Yanẓurūn*: Gazing, and at What?**

The verb *yanẓurūn* means "they look," "they gaze," "they behold." Its object is not stated, and this deliberate openness has led the commentators to offer several answers, recorded by al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabrisī, and others. The source commentary summarizes them, and each deserves to be considered.

*They gaze upon the delights of the Garden.* The righteous look out from their couches at the rivers, gardens, fruits, palaces, and companions that God has given them. The Qur'an's description in 76:20 — *"when you look there, you will see bliss and a great kingdom"* — supports this. There is a particular pleasure in *seeing* one's blessings, in surveying what one has been given, and the righteous are granted that pleasure without end.

*They gaze upon their Lord.* This is the interpretation al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī place among the foremost, and it connects this verse with the famous passage in Sūrah al-Qiyāmah:

> *"Faces that Day shall be radiant, gazing upon their Lord (ilā Rabbihā nāẓirah)."* (Qur'an 75:22–23)

The verb there — *nāẓirah* — is from the same root as *yanẓurūn* here. And this sūrah has already alluded to the vision of God in reverse, when it said that the wicked would be *veiled from their Lord* (v. 15). If the wicked are veiled, the righteous are unveiled; if the wicked cannot see, the righteous see. The very next verse — *"Thou dost recognize in their faces the splendor of bliss"* (v. 24) — echoes the "radiant faces" of 75:22, which suggests that the two passages describe the same scene: faces made radiant by gazing upon God. On this reading, the couches are the seats from which the *abrār* behold their Lord, and that beholding is the summit of their bliss.

*They gaze upon their enemies in the Fire.* Some commentators offer this, anticipating verses 34–35, where the believers are shown looking at the disbelievers being requited. The source commentary rightly notes that this interpretation, *as the primary meaning of verse 23*, sits awkwardly with the flow of the passage — which is entirely about the positive delights of the righteous — and with the prophetic descriptions of Paradise, whose joy is not primarily derived from watching suffering. The reading is better reserved for verse 35, where the context demands it.

The soundest approach, adopted by many commentators, is to recognize that the *openness* of the object is intentional. The righteous gaze at *whatever they wish* — at the Garden, at one another, at their Lord — and the absence of a stated object conveys the *freedom* and *abundance* of their sight. The Qur'an says: *"They will have whatever they wish therein, and with Us is more"* (50:35). The "more" (*mazīd*) is understood by the commentators as the vision of God. The couch-reclining, gazing believer has everything he wishes to see, and beyond it, the sight of God.

**The Symbolism of the Couch: The Soul at Rest**

The source commentary offers a spiritual reading that deserves expansion: the couches represent the state of the soul at peace, *al-nafs al-muṭmaʾinnah*, described in Sūrah al-Fajr:

> *"O soul at peace! Return to your Lord, well-pleased and well-pleasing. Enter among My servants, and enter My Garden."* (Qur'an 89:27–30)

The soul at peace is *"well-pleased"* (*rāḍiyah*) with God and *"well-pleasing"* (*marḍiyyah*) to Him — a two-way contentment. This is the inner reality that the outer image of the couch expresses. The defrauder's soul was never at peace: it was the *nafs al-ammārah*, the soul that commands to evil, always grasping. The righteous soul, having submitted its desires to God in this life, has become the soul at peace, and its reward is to *rest* — to recline — in the presence of the One it was at peace with. The couch is peace made visible.

**Rest Earned, Not Idleness Given**

There is an important nuance in the image. The couch of Paradise is not the idleness of the lazy; it is the rest of those who *labored*. The sūrah will say shortly, *"So for that let the strivers strive"* (v. 26). The recline of verse 23 follows the striving of a lifetime. The Qur'an makes the connection explicit elsewhere:

> *"Eat and drink in satisfaction for what you put forth in the days past."* (Qur'an 69:24)

*"For what you put forth."* The couch is *earned* — not in the sense that human deeds could purchase Paradise (the Prophet ﷺ said that no one enters Paradise by his deeds alone, but by God's mercy), but in the sense that God, in His generosity, attaches His mercy to the striving of His servants. The one who stood in prayer when he could have slept, who stood at the scale honestly when he could have skimmed, who stood firm under mockery when he could have caved, is given, in the end, the seat from which he need never rise.

**A Reflection on Gazing in This Life**

The verse invites a reflection on where a person directs his gaze in this life, because the gaze of the Hereafter is the continuation of the gaze of this world. The defrauder gazed at the measure, calculating how much he could withhold. The mockers of verses 29–32 gazed at the believers, winking and sneering. The believer gazed — at what? At the signs of God in the heavens and the earth; at the faces of the poor, whom he served; at the Book, which he recited; and, inwardly, at his Lord, whom he worshipped "as though he saw Him." The Prophet ﷺ defined *iḥsān*, the highest level of religion, precisely in terms of this gaze, in the famous ḥadīth of Gabriel recorded by Muslim:

> *"Iḥsān is that you worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, [know that] He sees you."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 8)

The believer who spent his life worshipping *as though he saw* God will be granted, on the couches of Paradise, to see Him in truth. The gaze of faith becomes the gaze of vision. That is the deepest meaning of *yanẓurūn* — and the next verse describes what that gazing does to the face of the one who gazes.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:24

> **Thou dost recognize in their faces the splendor of bliss.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bliss That Shows**

The description of the righteous moves from their posture (on couches) and their activity (gazing) to their *appearance*: *taʿrifu fī wujūhihim naḍrata al-naʿīm* — "Thou dost recognize in their faces the splendor of bliss." The verse makes a claim that is at once simple and profound: the bliss of the righteous is not merely something they *experience*; it is something that *shows*. It is visible to an observer. Their inner state has become their outer appearance. Anyone who looks at them *recognizes* — the verb is *taʿrifu*, "you know, you recognize" — that they are in bliss, without needing to be told.

**The Word *Naḍrah*: The Freshness of a Watered Garden**

The key word is *naḍrah*, from the root *n-ḍ-r*, whose basic meaning is *freshness, brightness, bloom, radiance* — specifically the kind of vibrant freshness seen in a plant that has just been watered, or a face flushed with health, or a color at its most vivid. The Arabs used *naḍara* of trees when they were green and lush, and of faces when they were bright with joy and well-being. *Naḍrah* is therefore not merely "brightness" in the sense of light; it is *living* brightness — the glow of vitality, freshness, and delight. Some translators render it "radiance," others "bloom," others "splendor," and each catches a facet.

The word appears in the Qur'an's description of the vision of God: *"Faces that Day shall be radiant (nāḍirah), gazing upon their Lord"* (75:22–23). The same root. And it appears in the description of the reward of the righteous in Sūrah al-Insān:

> *"So God will protect them from the evil of that Day and give them radiance (naḍrah) and happiness."* (Qur'an 76:11)

There, *naḍrah* is given to the *face* and *surūr* (happiness) to the *heart* — the outer glow and the inner joy, paired. In our verse, it is *naḍrat al-naʿīm*, "the radiance *of bliss*" — the radiance that bliss produces, the glow that comes from being in *naʿīm*.

The Prophet ﷺ used this very word in a famous supplication for those who transmit his teachings, recorded by Abū Dāwūd and al-Tirmidhī:

> *"May God make radiant (naḍḍara Allāhu) the face of one who hears something from us and conveys it as he heard it."* (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 3660; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2657; graded ṣaḥīḥ)

The scholars of ḥadīth cherished this supplication and understood *naḍḍara* to mean: may God give his face the freshness and beauty of the people of Paradise. The connection to our verse is direct.

**Why the Face?**

The Qur'an repeatedly locates the visible signs of a person's ultimate state in the *face*. On the Day of Judgment, *"some faces will be bright, and some faces will be dark"* (3:106); *"faces that Day will be humbled, laboring, weary"* (88:2–3) versus *"faces that Day will be joyful, pleased with their striving"* (88:8–9). Why the face?

Because the face is where the inner person becomes visible. It is the organ of expression — the place where joy, grief, shame, fear, and peace register without being willed. A person may control his words and even his actions, but the face betrays the heart. In this world, the defrauder's face was a mask: he smiled at his customer while cheating him, and his face concealed his heart. In the Hereafter there are no masks. The face reveals exactly what the heart contains. For the wicked, this is exposure; for the righteous, it is *vindication* — their inner goodness, long hidden, is now written on their faces for all to see.

The Qur'an notes that the righteous in this world already carry a trace of this on their faces:

> *"Their mark is on their faces from the trace of prostration."* (Qur'an 48:29)

The commentators explain that this "mark" is not primarily a physical callus but a *light* and *humility* that appears in the faces of those who prostrate much — a foretaste of the *naḍrah* of the Hereafter. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said: "Righteousness has a light in the heart, a radiance in the face, a strength in the body, an increase in provision, and love in the hearts of creatures; and sin has a darkness in the heart, a gloom in the face, a weakness in the body, a decrease in provision, and hatred in the hearts of creatures." The face, even now, reports on the heart.

**The Reversal of the Rusted Heart**

This verse is the precise mirror of verse 14. There, the wicked's *hearts* were *covered with rust* — darkened, dulled, made unable to reflect. Here, the righteous's *faces* are *radiant with bliss* — bright, fresh, shining. Rust is the loss of shine; *naḍrah* is shine at its fullest. The one whose heart was rusted by what he earned will have a dark face; the one whose heart was polished by remembrance and repentance will have a radiant one. The condition of the face in the Hereafter is the condition of the heart in this world, made visible.

This correspondence reflects a general Qur'anic principle: the Hereafter *reveals* what this world *concealed*. The Qur'an calls the Day of Judgment *"the Day when secrets will be tested"* (86:9) and *"the Day when the hidden things are made manifest."* The radiance on the faces of the righteous is the manifestation of a secret they kept for a lifetime: the secret of a heart at peace with God.

**"Thou": Who Recognizes?**

The verb *taʿrifu*, "you recognize," is in the second person singular. The source commentary notes that this can be read in two ways: as an address to the Prophet ﷺ specifically, or as a general address to any observer — "one recognizes," "anyone can see." Both are meaningful. If it is the Prophet ﷺ, the verse tells him that he will see his followers — the very ones who were mocked and impoverished in Makkah — radiant in Paradise, and this is a consolation to him. If it is general, the verse tells us that the bliss of the righteous will be *publicly visible* — to the angels, to one another, and to those who once scorned them. Some reciters, as the source notes, read the verb in the passive: *tuʿrafu*, "there is recognized in their faces" — the radiance is simply *there*, recognizable by all.

The generality is important for the sūrah's argument. The mockers of verses 29–32 looked at the believers and *saw* — or pretended to see — misguided fools. On the Day of Judgment, those same mockers will look at the believers and *recognize* — unmistakably, without possibility of denial — the radiance of bliss. Their earlier assessment will be refuted by sight.

**Bliss That Cannot Be Hidden and Need Not Be**

There is a moral beauty in the fact that the righteous's bliss *shows*. In this world, the righteous often had to *hide* their goodness — to give charity secretly, to pray privately, to conceal their faith from persecutors. Their light was, by necessity or by modesty, hidden. In the Hereafter, hiding is over. Their radiance is public, and it is public *without pride* — because it is God's gift, recognized by others as the "radiance of *bliss*," of what they were *given*, not of what they achieved by themselves. The righteous do not display their radiance; it simply shines. Al-Rāzī notes that the verse attributes the radiance to *bliss* (*naʿīm*), not to the righteous themselves, emphasizing that it is a gift bestowed, not a quality manufactured.

**A Practical Reflection: Faces in This Life**

The verse invites the believer to consider what his own face reports. There is a wisdom in the tradition that the heart writes on the face over time — that a life of anxiety, concealment, and calculation leaves one kind of mark, and a life of honesty, worship, and contentment leaves another. The Prophet ﷺ was described by his Companions as having a face like the moon, and he taught that *"your smiling in the face of your brother is charity"* (al-Tirmidhī 1956). The believer's face in this life is meant to be, in a small way, a preview of the *naḍrah* of the next: open, at peace, welcoming, free of the tension that comes from having something to hide.

The person who fills the measure honestly can look his customer in the eye. The person who prays his prayers fully rises with a settled face. The person who has nothing to conceal walks through the world with the beginnings of radiance. And the one who cultivates that radiance now — through honesty, remembrance, and the polishing of the heart — will find it perfected on the Day when *thou dost recognize in their faces the splendor of bliss*.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25

> **They are given to drink of pure wine sealed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Sight to Taste: The Drink of the Righteous**

The description of bliss now moves from what the righteous see and how they appear to what they *taste*: *yusqawna min raḥīqin makhtūm* — "They are given to drink of pure wine, sealed." The verb *yusqawna* is passive: they *are given* to drink. They do not pour for themselves, they do not labor for the drink, they do not purchase it; it is served to them. The passive voice conveys honor and ease — the righteous are *guests*, and the host is God. The Qur'an describes elsewhere that *"there will circulate among them young boys made eternal, with vessels, pitchers, and a cup from a flowing spring"* (56:17–18). The one who spent his life *serving* — giving full measure to others — is now served.

**The Word *Raḥīq*: The Purest Wine**

*Raḥīq* is a word of considerable refinement in Arabic. The lexicographers define it as the *purest* and *finest* of wine — wine without sediment, without adulteration, without any of the dregs or bitterness that ordinary wine contains. Some say it is wine that is *white* or *clear*; some that it is the *oldest* and most matured wine; some that it is wine so pure that it has no aftertaste. Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad defined it as "the choicest of wine and the finest." It occurs only here in the Qur'an. The choice of this rare and refined word, rather than the ordinary *khamr*, signals that what the righteous drink is not merely wine but wine of a category beyond anything known on earth.

The commentators emphasize that the wine of Paradise is *categorically different* from the wine of this world, which the Qur'an forbids. The Qur'an itself makes the distinction in describing the drink of the righteous:

> *"There will be circulated among them a cup from a flowing spring, white and delicious to the drinkers; no headiness is in it, nor are they intoxicated by it."* (Qur'an 37:45–47)

> *"...a cup from a flowing spring, from which they get neither headache nor intoxication."* (Qur'an 56:18–19)

> *"Therein are rivers of water unaltered, rivers of milk whose taste never changes, rivers of wine delicious to those who drink, and rivers of purified honey."* (Qur'an 47:15)

The wine of this world is forbidden precisely *because* of what it does — it clouds the mind, produces headache and nausea, leads to quarrel and disgrace, and takes a person away from the remembrance of God (5:91). The wine of Paradise has *none* of these effects: no intoxication, no headache, no quarrel, no forgetfulness. It has only the *pleasure* — the taste, the delight, the joy — stripped of every harm. This is a general principle of the Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise: every pleasure is given in its *perfected* form, with its harm removed. The believer who abstained from the wine of this world for God's sake is given the wine of the next, purified.

**The Word *Makhtūm*: Sealed**

The wine is *makhtūm*, "sealed" — from the root *kh-t-m*, which means to seal, to close, to stamp, to bring to an end (as *khātam al-nabiyyīn*, "the Seal of the Prophets," 33:40). A sealed vessel is one that has been closed and stamped so that it cannot be opened without breaking the seal. The commentators draw out several implications of the seal.

*Purity and exclusivity.* A sealed vessel has not been touched, tampered with, or opened by anyone. The wine reserved for the righteous is *theirs alone* — no hand has dipped into it, no one has drunk from it before them, nothing has contaminated it. In the ancient world, the finest wines were sealed with clay or wax and opened only for honored guests; a sealed wine was a wine kept back for someone special. The righteous are that someone.

*Preservation.* A sealed wine keeps its quality; it does not turn or spoil. The pleasures of the righteous do not degrade with time.

*Honor.* To be served from a sealed vessel — opened in one's presence — is a mark of distinction. The seal is broken *for* the guest.

The next verse will specify what the seal is made of: *musk*. This is the sūrah's own explanation of *makhtūm*, and it will be treated there. Here it suffices to note that the word "sealed" has already appeared in this sūrah in a different form: the *book* of both the wicked and the righteous was *marqūm*, which the commentators glossed as *sealed* (*makhtūm*). The righteous's record was sealed — nothing of their good could be lost; and their reward is served sealed — nothing of its purity has been compromised. There is a symmetry between the sealed record and the sealed reward.

**The Spiritual Reading of Raḥīq**

Alongside the literal meaning, which the mainstream of commentators affirm, the scholars of the heart drew out a spiritual dimension that does not replace the literal but deepens it. The Sufi commentators, cited by al-Qushayrī and others, understood the *raḥīq* as the pure love and knowledge of God — a "wine" that intoxicates the soul with joy without clouding the mind. The Prophet ﷺ spoke of the "sweetness of faith" (*ḥalāwat al-īmān*) that some believers taste in this world, and the classical scholars saw in the pure wine of Paradise the perfected form of that sweetness: the soul drinking, without veil or limit, from the presence of its Lord.

Al-Rāzī, as the source notes, held that this *raḥīq* is superior to the rivers of wine mentioned in 47:15 — that the sealed wine served individually to the righteous is a mark of special honor beyond the general provision of Paradise. He connected this with the sūrah's description of the *muqarrabūn* (the nearest) drinking from Tasnīm in its purity (v. 28), suggesting a hierarchy of drinks corresponding to a hierarchy of nearness to God.

**Why a Drink? The Wisdom of the Image**

One may ask why the Qur'an chooses *drink* as a central image of the reward. Several reasons emerge.

Thirst is the most urgent of bodily needs, and its satisfaction the most immediate of pleasures. The Arabs of the desert knew this with special intensity. To promise the righteous a *drink* is to promise the satisfaction of the deepest need.

Drink is *shared*. Throughout human cultures, to drink together is to be at peace together. The image of the righteous being served a sealed wine evokes a *feast* — a gathering of honored guests at the table of a generous host. The Qur'an describes the righteous *"reclining on couches arranged in rows"* (52:20) and *"facing one another"* (37:44) — the wine is served at a banquet of the redeemed.

And drink has a spiritual resonance the Qur'an exploits throughout: the soul *thirsts* for God as the body thirsts for water. The Qur'an speaks of the disbelievers' deeds as a mirage that the thirsty man takes for water (24:39). The righteous, who thirsted for truth in this world and were given the Qur'an, are given in the next the drink that ends all thirst.

**The Contrast with the Defrauder's Measure**

Even here, the sūrah's opening theme is not far away. The defrauder measured out grain and liquid — *kayl*, the measuring of volumes — and stinted. Now the righteous are *poured* a drink by God, and the pouring is not stinted: it is *raḥīq*, the purest; it is *makhtūm*, reserved and untouched; and, as the next verses will say, it is mixed with Tasnīm and sealed with musk. God's measure to His servants overflows. The Qur'an says of Paradise: *"a reward from your Lord, a gift well-reckoned (ʿaṭāʾan ḥisāban)"* (78:36) — a gift *measured*, but measured by the generosity of the Lord, which has no brim to fall short of.

**A Practical Reflection: What One Drinks Now**

The verse invites a simple reflection. Every person drinks *something* to satisfy his inner thirst — for meaning, for joy, for peace. The defrauder drank from the cup of gain: every skimmed gram was a sip, and it never satisfied, because greed is a thirst that grows with drinking. The mockers drank from the cup of contempt: every sneer at the believers was a sip of superiority, and it left them emptier. The believer drinks, in this world, from the cup of remembrance, prayer, Qur'an, and honest labor — a cup that gives the "sweetness of faith" now and the *raḥīq makhtūm* later. The Prophet ﷺ taught the supplication, recorded by al-Tirmidhī, that asks God for *"the pleasure of gazing upon Your Face and the longing to meet You"* — the deepest thirst, whose satisfaction is the sealed wine of the next verses.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26

> **whose seal is musk—so for that let the strivers strive—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Statements in One Verse**

This verse contains two distinct parts, each of which carries significant weight. The first completes the description of the sealed wine: *khitāmuhu misk* — "its seal is musk." The second is the sūrah's single explicit exhortation, inserted in the very middle of the description of Paradise: *wa fī dhālika fa-l-yatanāfasi al-mutanāfisūn* — "and for that let the strivers strive," or more literally, "and in that let the competitors compete." The placement is deliberate: having described the reward in enough detail to awaken desire, the Qur'an pauses to say — *this* is what is worth competing for.

**The Seal of Musk: Two Readings**

The word *khitām* means the *seal* — the material used to close and stamp a vessel — and by extension, the *end* or *conclusion* of something (the same root as *khātimah*, "conclusion"). The source commentary records two interpretations of "its seal is musk," and both are held by major authorities.

The first reading, favored by al-Ṭabarī and al-Ṭabrisī, is literal: the sealed wine is *stopped and stamped with musk*. Where the vessels of this world are sealed with clay, wax, or pitch — materials that are functional but base — the vessels of Paradise are sealed with *musk*, the most precious fragrance known to the Arabs. The seal is not something to be scraped off and discarded; it is itself a delight. Even the *closure* of the wine is a pleasure. This reading emphasizes the *totality* of the luxury of Paradise: nothing in it, not even the packaging, is merely functional or unpleasant.

The second reading, favored by al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī also records it from Ibn Masʿūd, Mujāhid, and others, takes *khitām* in the sense of *end* or *aftertaste*: "its ending is musk" — that is, when the drinker finishes the wine, its last taste and its lingering scent are musk. This reading rests on a keen observation about the wines of this world: their beginning may be pleasant, but their *end* is bitter — the aftertaste, the dregs at the bottom of the cup, and, later, the headache and nausea. The wine of Paradise reverses this: its end is *better* than its beginning; it concludes in the fragrance of musk. Ibn Masʿūd is reported to have said: "Its end is the scent of musk" (*ʿāqibatuhu rīḥu misk*). Al-Rāzī prefers this reading and notes its elegance: the drink of the righteous is one whose *conclusion* is its finest moment.

Both readings are true in their way, and they may be held together: the wine is sealed with musk, and when the seal is broken and the wine finished, its final taste is musk. Either way, the message is the same: every stage of the righteous's reward, from beginning to end, is pure delight.

**Why Musk?**

Musk (*misk*) held a unique place in the Arab imagination and in the teaching of the Prophet ﷺ. It was the most prized of all perfumes, and its scent was considered the sweetest. The Prophet ﷺ said, as recorded by Muslim:

> *"The best of your perfume is musk."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2252)

Musk appears repeatedly in the Prophet's ﷺ descriptions of Paradise. Of its soil: *"Its soil is musk"* (a ḥadīth in Muslim describing the Prophet's ﷺ vision of Paradise). Of its people: the sweat of the people of Paradise will be musk — the Prophet ﷺ said, as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, that the first group to enter Paradise will be like the full moon, *"their sweat is musk"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3245; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2834). Of the martyrs: the Prophet ﷺ said that the one wounded in God's cause will come on the Day of Resurrection with his wound *"the color of blood and the scent of musk"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2803; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1876). And of the fasting person: *"the breath of the fasting person is sweeter to God than the scent of musk"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1894; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1151).

This last ḥadīth is especially relevant. The fasting person *abstains* — from food, drink, and desire — for God's sake, and God says that even the unpleasant breath produced by his abstinence is sweeter to Him than musk. The one who abstained in this world is sealed with musk in the next. The connection between restraint here and fragrance there is a recurring thread.

**Musk as the Conclusion of a Life**

The reading "its end is musk" opens onto a broader reflection that the classical scholars pursued. The Arabic *khitām* is the same word used for the *conclusion* of a life — *ḥusn al-khitām* or *ḥusn al-khātimah*, "a good ending," is the phrase Muslims use for dying in a state of faith and righteousness. The believer's greatest concern is that the *end* of his life be good, that his last deed be pleasing to God. This verse, read at that level, promises that the *end* of the righteous — of their life, of their trial, of their striving — will be musk: fragrant, sweet, honored. Whatever bitterness they tasted along the way, the ending is sweet.

The contrast with the wicked is again exact. Their end was described in verse 17: *"Then it is said unto them, 'This is that which you used to deny.'"* Their conclusion is rebuke. The righteous's conclusion is musk.

**The Exhortation: "Let the Strivers Strive"**

The second part of the verse — *wa fī dhālika fa-l-yatanāfasi al-mutanāfisūn* — is the only direct command in the sūrah, and it is expressed in a word of great richness. The verb *tanāfasa*, in the sixth form, is derived from *nafs* (soul, self, breath) and *nafīs* (precious, valuable). *Tanāfasa fī* means to *compete* for something, to *vie* for it, to *strive against others* to obtain it — specifically something *precious* (*nafīs*), the kind of thing people compete for because they know its worth. The word implies eagerness, energy, and a healthy rivalry. Al-Zamakhsharī explains: *tanāfus* is the desire of two or more people for a precious thing, each wishing it for himself. Al-Rāghib defines it as striving in imitation of the noble — the effort to equal and surpass others in acquiring what is truly valuable.

"For *that*" — *fī dhālika* — refers to the bliss just described: the couches, the radiant faces, the sealed wine with the musk seal. The verse says: *this* is the precious thing. *This* is what deserves the competitive energy that human beings spend on lesser things.

**Why the Qur'an Endorses Competition Here**

The Qur'an is generally wary of worldly rivalry. It warns against *takāthur*, competition in piling up wealth: *"Competition in increase distracts you, until you visit the graves"* (102:1–2). It warns against envy and the desire for what others have. Yet here it explicitly *commands* competition. The resolution lies in the *object*. Competition for wealth and status is destructive because those goods are *finite*: if I have more, you have less, and the struggle breeds envy, fraud (the very sin of the sūrah's opening), and enmity. Competition for Paradise is constructive because its goods are *infinite*: my attaining it does not diminish yours, and the striving breeds not envy but mutual encouragement. The Qur'an says:

> *"Race one another toward forgiveness from your Lord and a Garden as wide as the heavens and the earth, prepared for the God-fearing."* (Qur'an 3:133)

> *"So compete in good deeds (fa-stabiqū al-khayrāt)."* (Qur'an 2:148; 5:48)

The human competitive instinct is real and powerful. The Qur'an does not try to suppress it; it *redirects* it toward the only prize worth having. The defrauder competed for grams; the Qur'an tells him to compete for musk-sealed wine. The energy is the same; the object is transformed.

**The Companions as Models of *Tanāfus***

The Companions of the Prophet ﷺ embodied this holy competition. The most famous example is recorded by al-Tirmidhī and Abū Dāwūd: when the Prophet ﷺ called for donations for the expedition to Tabūk, ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb brought half his wealth, thinking that this time he would surpass Abū Bakr. The Prophet ﷺ asked what he had left for his family; he said, "The same amount." Then Abū Bakr came with *all* his wealth, and when asked what he had left for his family, he said, "I have left them God and His Messenger." ʿUmar said: "I will never compete with you in anything again." (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1678; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3675). This is *tanāfus*: a competition in giving, where each contestant's victory enriches the other and both are elevated.

Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī said, commenting on this verse: "If you see a man competing with you in this world, compete with him in the Hereafter." And ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz is reported to have said: "My soul is ambitious. It aspired to the governorship and attained it; then to the caliphate and attained it; now it aspires to Paradise, and I hope to attain it."

**The Placement of the Exhortation**

Why does the exhortation appear *here*, in the middle of the description, rather than at the end? The commentators note that its placement is psychologically precise. The Qur'an has described enough of the reward to awaken desire — the couches, the faces, the wine, the musk — but it has not yet described the highest element, Tasnīm and the drink of the *muqarrabūn* (vv. 27–28). The exhortation falls at the moment when desire is aroused but not yet satisfied, and it says: *strive*. Then it continues with the still greater reward, as if to say: and there is *more* for those who strive harder. The exhortation is a hinge between the general reward of the *abrār* and the special reward of the *muqarrabūn* — an invitation to move from the first rank to the second.

**A Practical Reflection: Where Does Your Competitive Energy Go?**

The verse invites every reader to audit his ambition. Human beings compete constantly — for salary, position, recognition, followers, the better car, the larger house. The Qur'an does not condemn ambition; it asks about its *direction*. What would it look like to bring the same intensity, the same planning, the same daily effort that one brings to career advancement, to the pursuit of the sealed wine and the seat among the *muqarrabūn*? To *compete* — with oneself and, in the healthy sense, with one's peers — in honesty of dealing, fullness of prayer, generosity of giving, quickness of forgiveness? The verse says that the prize justifies it. The one who competes for grams gets grams, and rust, and Sijjīn. The one who competes for *that* gets musk.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27

> **and whose mixture is of Tasnīm,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wine Is Not Yet Fully Described**

After the exhortation to strive, the sūrah resumes its description of the drink of the righteous, and adds a detail that raises it still further: *wa mizājuhu min Tasnīm* — "and its mixture is of Tasnīm." The pure, sealed, musk-ended wine of verses 25–26 is *mixed* with something called Tasnīm. The Qur'an thus presents the drink of the *abrār* as a *blend*: a base of *raḥīq* (the purest wine), mixed with water from a spring of even higher origin. The next verse will identify that spring as the drink of the *muqarrabūn*, those brought nearest to God.

The grammatical antecedent of "whose" (*-hu*) is the *raḥīq* of verse 25 — the wine. The intervening verse 26 was a parenthesis (the exhortation), and now the description resumes as if uninterrupted. This confirms the reading that the exhortation was deliberately *inserted* into the description, not appended after it.

**The Word *Mizāj*: What Is Mixed In**

*Mizāj* comes from the root *m-z-j*, "to mix, to blend," and refers to that which is *mixed into* something — the admixture, the ingredient added to a drink to give it its character. The same word is used in Sūrah al-Insān for the drinks of the righteous:

> *"Truly the righteous (al-abrār) will drink from a cup whose mixture (mizājuhā) is of camphor — a spring from which the servants of God drink, making it gush forth abundantly."* (Qur'an 76:5–6)

> *"And they will be given to drink therein a cup whose mixture (mizājuhā) is of ginger — a spring therein named Salsabīl."* (Qur'an 76:17–18)

Sūrah al-Insān thus describes two admixtures — camphor and ginger — from two springs, one of them named Salsabīl. Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn adds a third: Tasnīm. The commentators note that these verses together portray a *gradation* of drinks in Paradise, each mixed from a different spring, corresponding to the different ranks of the righteous. Al-Rāzī and others observe that Sūrah al-Insān also uses the term *al-abrār* (76:5) — the same term as this sūrah — and that in both places the *abrār* drink a *mixed* cup, while a higher group drinks from the source itself.

The Arabs mixed their wine with water to soften and improve it; a fine wine was judged partly by the quality of the water it was cut with. The image here uses this custom: the finest wine (*raḥīq*) is cut with the finest water (Tasnīm). But the water, as the next verse reveals, is itself the drink of the highest rank — so the *abrār* receive, in their mixture, a portion of what the *muqarrabūn* receive pure.

**The Word *Tasnīm*: Its Root and Meanings**

*Tasnīm* occurs only here in the Qur'an. The source commentary records that some (al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī) treat it as a proper name without a derived meaning — simply the name of a spring in Paradise. But most commentators trace it to the root *s-n-m*, whose basic meaning is *height* and *elevation*: *sanām* is the hump of a camel — the highest point of its back — and *tasannama* means to climb up, to rise to the top of something. *Tasnīm*, in the second verbal noun form, would mean "raising up," "elevation," "that which comes from above." Several senses follow.

*A spring that flows from above.* The most common explanation, transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, Qatādah, and others, is that Tasnīm is a spring that *descends from a height* — it flows down to the people of Paradise from above them, pouring into their vessels and dwellings without their needing to go to it. Some reports say it flows from beneath the Throne, or from the highest part of Paradise, down into the gardens of the righteous. This connects Tasnīm with ʿIlliyyūn, the *heights* where the book of the righteous is kept: both are "from above."

*A spring that elevates.* Al-Rāzī, as the source notes, suggests that the name indicates the spring *raises* and *ennobles* those who drink from it — that its effect is elevation of rank and nobility of state. This fits the etymology (*sannama* = to raise) and the context (the *muqarrabūn*, the elevated, drink from it).

*The noblest drink of Paradise.* Al-Ṭabarī reports the general agreement that Tasnīm is the noblest and most exalted drink of Paradise — its name from *sanām*, the summit, indicating that it is the *summit* of all drinks.

Ibn ʿAbbās, as the source records, linked Tasnīm to the verse of Sūrah al-Sajdah: *"No soul knows what has been hidden for them of comfort for the eyes"* (32:17) — indicating that Tasnīm belongs to those hidden delights that no description can convey, and that the name itself, like ʿIlliyyūn, gestures toward something beyond human knowledge.

**The Spiritual Reading: Tasnīm as the Knowledge and Vision of God**

The source commentary records al-Rāzī's profound interpretation, which deserves full treatment. Al-Rāzī held that Tasnīm is, at its deepest level, "the knowledge of God and the delight of gazing upon the Face of God." His reasoning follows the sūrah's own structure. The *muqarrabūn* — those brought nearest — drink from Tasnīm *pure* (v. 28), because their entire attention is fixed on God alone; they gaze upon nothing but Him. The *abrār* — the general righteous — drink a *mixture*, because their attention is divided: sometimes they gaze upon the created delights of Paradise (the couches, the wine, the gardens), and sometimes upon the Face of God. Their drink is therefore a blend of created pleasure and divine vision, while the drink of the nearest is divine vision unmixed.

This reading does not deny the literal spring; al-Rāzī affirms the reality of Paradise's rivers. It adds a layer: the physical gradation of drinks corresponds to a spiritual gradation of *what the soul is turned toward*. The source also notes that some Sufi commentators identified Tasnīm with the highest level of spiritual love (*maḥabbah*) — the love of God for His own sake, unmixed with love of His gifts.

The Qur'an elsewhere supports the idea of gradation among the people of Paradise according to their nearness to God. Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah divides them into the *Companions of the Right* and the *Foremost, the muqarrabūn* (56:8–12). Sūrah al-Raḥmān describes two pairs of gardens, one higher than the other (55:46, 62). And the Prophet ﷺ said, as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"The people of Paradise will see the people of the chambers above them as you see a brilliant star far off on the horizon in the east or west, because of the difference in rank between them." They said, "O Messenger of God, are those the stations of the prophets that none other can attain?" He said, "Yes indeed, by the One in Whose Hand is my soul — men who believed in God and affirmed the messengers."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3256; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2831)

Tasnīm, drunk pure by the *muqarrabūn* and mixed by the *abrār*, is one of the Qur'an's images of this gradation.

**Why the Mixture Is a Kindness**

One might ask whether the *abrār* are being shortchanged — given a *mixed* drink while the *muqarrabūn* drink pure. The answer, which several commentators give, reverses the concern. The *abrār* receive, *in their mixture*, a share of the very highest drink. They are not excluded from Tasnīm; they taste it, blended. God, in His generosity, does not reserve the best exclusively for the best; He lets the whole body of the righteous share in it, according to their capacity. This is a picture of divine grace: the reward exceeds strict desert. A person who filled the measure honestly but did not reach the rank of the foremost still receives, in his cup, water from the spring of the foremost.

There is also a point of psychology. The Qur'an mentions the mixture *after* the exhortation to strive (v. 26). Having said "compete for this," it immediately shows that there are *ranks* within "this" — that the drink of the general righteous is a mixture, while a higher purity exists. The effect is to keep the striver striving: do not stop at the mixture; strive for the source. The next verse will name who reaches it.

**An Analogy for Understanding Tasnīm**

Consider a great river system. The purest water is at the spring, high in the mountains, where it emerges from the rock. As it flows down, it remains good water — it becomes the rivers and streams that irrigate the valleys — but it is mixed with what it passes. Those who live at the spring drink it in its first purity; those in the valleys drink it good and abundant but blended. Both drink from the same source; the difference is *proximity*. Tasnīm is the spring; the *muqarrabūn* live at it; the *abrār* live downstream and receive it in their wine. And the root of *muqarrabūn* is *qurb* — nearness. The whole image is about how close one has come to the source.

In this life, the source is God, and the nearness is achieved by *striving* — by the *tanāfus* of the previous verse. The one who competes for nearness in this world — through worship, honesty, remembrance, and the love of God above His gifts — is granted nearness in the next, and drinks from the spring itself. The one who is righteous but less ardent is granted the mixture. Both are in *naʿīm*; but the sūrah, having named the mixture, now names the spring, and calls the striver upward.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28

> **a spring whence drink those brought nigh.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Summit of the Third Section**

This verse is the culmination of the description of Paradise that began in verse 18. It identifies Tasnīm — *ʿaynan yashrabu bihā al-muqarrabūn* — "a spring from which drink those brought nigh." The word *ʿayn* means both "spring" and "eye" in Arabic — a spring is the *eye* of the earth, the place where water emerges — and the Qur'an uses it for the springs of Paradise throughout (76:6, 18; 88:12; 55:50, 66). Tasnīm is a spring; and it belongs, as a pure drink, to the *muqarrabūn*.

The structure of verses 25–28 is now complete. The *abrār* drink a sealed wine (v. 25), ending in musk (v. 26), mixed with Tasnīm (v. 27), which is a spring the *muqarrabūn* drink from directly (v. 28). Two ranks of the blessed have been described: the *abrār*, whose cup is a blend, and the *muqarrabūn*, whose cup is the source. This is the sūrah's final word on the reward of the righteous before it returns, in verse 29, to the mockers of this world and their fate.

**The *Muqarrabūn* Return**

The *muqarrabūn* — "those brought near" — appeared in verse 21, as the witnesses of the book of the righteous. Now they appear as the drinkers of the purest spring. The two appearances illuminate each other. Those who are near enough to God to *witness* the records kept beside His Throne are near enough to *drink* from the spring that flows from beneath it. Their nearness is the key to everything they have.

The word, as noted at verse 21, is a passive participle: they *have been brought* near. God drew them. The Qur'an's consistent teaching is that nearness to God is His gift, granted to those who seek it — *"and those who strive for Us, We will surely guide them to Our ways"* (29:69) — but never manufactured by the seeker alone. The *muqarrabūn* strove, and God brought them near.

Who are they? The Qur'an's clearest answer is in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah, which describes three groups on the Day of Judgment — the Companions of the Right, the Companions of the Left, and *"the Foremost, the Foremost — those are the ones brought near (al-muqarrabūn), in Gardens of Bliss, a multitude from the earlier peoples and a few from the later"* (56:10–14). They are the *sābiqūn*, the foremost — those who did not merely do what was required but *raced ahead*: the prophets, the truthful (*ṣiddīqūn*), the martyrs, and the righteous who loved God with an undivided heart. The Qur'an lists them elsewhere:

> *"And whoever obeys God and the Messenger — those will be with the ones upon whom God has bestowed favor: the prophets, the steadfast affirmers of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous. And excellent are those as companions."* (Qur'an 4:69)

**The Prophetic Description of the Nearest**

The Prophet ﷺ described the path to nearness to God in the famous ḥadīth qudsī recorded by al-Bukhārī, which is the most direct commentary on what makes a *muqarrab*:

> *"God says: 'Whoever shows enmity to a friend (walī) of Mine, I declare war upon him. My servant does not draw near to Me with anything more beloved to Me than what I have made obligatory upon him. And My servant continues to draw near to Me with voluntary deeds until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing with which he hears, his sight with which he sees, his hand with which he strikes, and his foot with which he walks. If he asks Me, I will surely give him; and if he seeks My protection, I will surely protect him.'"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6502)

The ḥadīth describes *taqarrub* — drawing near — as a process with two stages. The first is the *obligatory* deeds: the prayers, the fasting, the zakāh, the honest dealing — the *full measure* in every duty. The second is the *voluntary* deeds, added beyond the obligatory out of love: the night prayer, the extra fasts, the charity beyond zakāh, the remembrance beyond the prescribed. The one who persists in the second stage is *loved* by God and becomes a *walī*, a friend of God — a *muqarrab*. And the ḥadīth's description of the result — God becomes "his hearing, his sight, his hand, his foot" — is the state of a person whose every faculty is turned toward God, who perceives and acts by God's light. This is exactly the state al-Rāzī described as drinking Tasnīm pure: a person who gazes upon nothing but God.

**Why the Nearest Drink Pure**

The logic of the verse, as al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, and others explain, is one of *correspondence*. The *muqarrabūn* in this world drank *purely* from God — their worship was unmixed with the desire for reward or the fear of loss; their love was for God Himself, not for His gifts. The great early worshipper Rābiʿah al-ʿAdawiyyah is reported to have prayed: "O God, if I worship You for fear of Hell, burn me in Hell; if I worship You for hope of Paradise, exclude me from Paradise; but if I worship You for Your own sake, do not withhold from me Your eternal beauty." This is the disposition of the *muqarrab*: unmixed. And their reward corresponds: they drink the spring *unmixed*.

The *abrār*, by contrast, were sincere and righteous but their motivation was, so to speak, *mixed* — they loved God *and* hoped for His Garden; they feared Him *and* feared His Fire. This is not a fault — the Qur'an itself motivates by hope and fear — but it is a lower purity of intention. And their reward corresponds: they drink the spring *mixed* into their wine. Al-Ṭabarī records the view that the *muqarrabūn* drink Tasnīm pure while it is mixed into the drink of the Companions of the Right, and this corresponds precisely to the two levels of the righteous in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah.

The principle is one the Qur'an states directly: *"Is the reward for good anything but good?"* (55:60). The *kind* of good one does determines the *kind* of reward one receives. The pure receive the pure.

**The Spring That Comes to the Drinker**

An often-noted feature of Tasnīm, derived from its etymology ("that which comes from above"), is that it *flows down* to those who drink it — they do not need to go to it. The Qur'an describes the springs of Paradise as flowing wherever the righteous wish: *"a spring from which the servants of God drink, making it gush forth abundantly"* (76:6) — the verb there means they direct it where they will. The *muqarrabūn* do not labor for their drink; it descends to them. This is the reversal of the condition of this world, in which the seeker of God must *strive* upward — must rise for the night prayer, climb the difficult path of restraint, struggle against his own desires. In the next world, the direction reverses: what he strove upward toward now flows down to him. The *tanāfus* of verse 26 is the upward striving; Tasnīm is the downward gift.

**A Note on Grammar: *Yashrabu bihā***

The Arabic says *yashrabu bihā*, literally "drink *by* it" or "drink *with* it," rather than *yashrabu minhā*, "drink *from* it." The grammarians offer two explanations. One is that *bi* here has the sense of *min* ("from"), a known usage. The other is that the verb *yashrabu* carries the implied sense of *yarwā* ("is quenched by") or *yaltadhdhu* ("takes delight in"), so that the phrase means "by which they are quenched, in which they delight." Al-Zamakhsharī notes that *shariba bihā* conveys a fuller enjoyment than *shariba minhā* — not merely drinking *some* from the spring, but drinking in such a way that one is *satisfied and delighted by it*. The *muqarrabūn* are not merely given access to Tasnīm; they are *quenched* by it.

**The Sūrah's Vision of Reward Completed**

Standing back, one can see the full architecture of the reward described in verses 18–28. The righteous have a *record* (a book in ʿIlliyyūn, witnessed by the nearest), a *state* (bliss), a *posture* (on couches, gazing), an *appearance* (radiant faces), a *drink* (sealed pure wine, ending in musk), and — for the foremost among them — the *source* itself (Tasnīm, drunk pure by the nearest). Every dimension of the person is addressed: the record of the past, the condition of the present, the body at rest, the eyes at gaze, the face aglow, the tongue and throat refreshed. And at the summit of all of it, for those who came nearest, is a spring whose deepest meaning, as the scholars of the heart understood, is the knowledge and vision of God Himself.

Set against this, the defrauder's grams look like what they are: dust. This is the point of the whole third section. The Qur'an does not merely forbid the short measure; it shows the person who might be tempted to it what he is *trading away*. No sane person exchanges Tasnīm for a thumb on the scale. The one who sees the exchange clearly does not need to be forced to honesty; he *competes* for it.

**Application: Choosing Which Cup**

The practical teaching is an invitation to ambition of the highest kind. The believer may be content to be among the *abrār* — and that is a magnificent station. But the sūrah has just told him there is a higher one, and it has told him how it is reached: by nearness, which is achieved by fulfilling the obligations fully (no *taṭfīf* in prayer or dealing) and then going beyond them out of love. The one who wants to drink Tasnīm pure begins today: he does not merely avoid cheating; he tips the balance in others' favor. He does not merely pray the obligatory prayers; he rises in the night. He does not merely refrain from mocking the believers; he loves them for God's sake. Each step of *taqarrub* is a step closer to the spring. And the next section of the sūrah will show, by contrast, what the mockers of the believers chose instead — and where their choice left them.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:29

> **Truly those who sinned did laugh at those who believed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Return to the Earth: The Fourth Section Opens**

Having lifted the reader to the highest heaven — to ʿIlliyyūn, to the couches, to Tasnīm — the sūrah now descends abruptly to the streets of Makkah and to a scene that the first believers knew from bitter experience: *inna alladhīna ajramū kānū mina alladhīna āmanū yaḍḥakūn* — "Truly those who sinned used to laugh at those who believed." The fourth and final section of the sūrah (vv. 29–36) is about *mockery*: how the wealthy sinners treated the believers in this world, and how the positions will be reversed in the next.

The transition is not arbitrary. The sūrah began with a specific injustice — cheating in measure. It ends with another — contempt for the righteous. Both are forms of *taṭfīf* in the broad sense the commentators gave the word: the sinners *diminished* the believers, treated them as less than they were, "short-measured" their worth. And both flow from the same root: the denial of the Day of Judgment. The one who does not believe he will be judged cheats freely and mocks freely.

**The Word *Ajramū*: The Nature of Their Crime**

The sinners are called *alladhīna ajramū*, "those who committed crimes," from the root *j-r-m*. The basic meaning of *jarama* is to cut off — as one cuts fruit from a tree — and by extension, to commit a crime, to sever oneself from what is right. A *mujrim* is a criminal, one who has cut himself off from goodness and from the community of the righteous. The Qur'an uses *mujrimūn* consistently for the guilty on the Day of Judgment: *"And you will see the criminals (mujrimīn) that Day bound together in chains"* (14:49). The word here identifies the mockers not merely as unbelievers but as *criminals* — their mockery is a crime, and it will be prosecuted.

The verb is in the past tense, *ajramū*, "they committed crimes," and the following verb is in the past-continuous, *kānū ... yaḍḥakūn*, "they used to laugh." The sūrah speaks from the vantage of the Day of Judgment, looking back at what the sinners *used to do*. The mockery is over; its consequences are beginning.

**The Word *Yaḍḥakūn*: Laughter as a Weapon**

*Yaḍḥakūn*, "they laugh," is from *ḍaḥika*, ordinary laughter. But the preposition *min* ("at," literally "from") makes it laughter *at* someone — derision, mockery, the laughter that belittles. This is laughter as a social weapon. Its purpose is not joy but *dominance*: to signal to everyone present that the object of laughter is beneath consideration, that his views need not be answered, that he is a fool.

The Qur'an records that this was the standard response of the wealthy Makkans to the message. When the Prophet ﷺ recited, they laughed: *"And when they see you, they take you only in ridicule: 'Is this the one God sent as a messenger?'"* (25:41). When the believers prayed, they laughed. When the Qur'an warned of the Resurrection, they laughed: *"And when they see a sign, they ridicule"* (37:14). The laughter was systematic, and it was aimed particularly at the *poor* believers — Bilāl, ʿAmmār, Khabbāb, Ṣuhayb, and others whose low social status made them, in the Makkan aristocrats' eyes, doubly ridiculous: fools for believing, and nobodies for presuming to have views at all.

**The Occasion: The Leaders of Quraysh and the Poor Believers**

The source commentary records that "those who sinned" refers, according to many commentators, to the leaders of Quraysh — Abū Jahl, al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, and their circle — who used to mock the poorer Muslims. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī cite the report that these men would sit in their assemblies and, when ʿAmmār, Ṣuhayb, Bilāl, or Khabbāb passed, would say, "Look at these — the followers of Muhammad!" and laugh. Some reports mention specifically that when ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib passed with a group of Muslims, the hypocrites or the disbelievers mocked and winked at one another.

The Qur'an records the substance of their mockery elsewhere. They said of the believers: *"Is it these whom God has favored among us?"* (6:53) — a sneer at the idea that God would choose the poor and lowly. They demanded that the Prophet ﷺ drive the poor believers away before the nobles would consider listening, and God commanded him: *"And do not drive away those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His Face"* (6:52). And the Qur'an describes the mockery in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn in language that parallels this sūrah closely:

> *"Truly there was a party of My servants who said, 'Our Lord, we have believed, so forgive us and have mercy upon us, and You are the best of the merciful.' But you took them in mockery until they made you forget My remembrance, and you used to laugh at them. Truly I have rewarded them this Day for their patience: they are the successful."* (Qur'an 23:109–111)

That passage is the fullest parallel to verses 29–36 of our sūrah: the sinners laughed; the believers were patient; on the Day of Judgment, the believers are rewarded and the laughers are told what their laughter cost them.

**Why the Sinners Laugh: The Psychology of Mockery**

The Qur'an's placement of this verse right after the description of Paradise invites a reflection on *why* the sinners laughed, and the answer is illuminating.

They laughed because the believers *threatened* them. A person does not laugh at what is genuinely insignificant; he ignores it. Mockery is reserved for what disturbs. The believers, by their existence, were a living argument that the sinners' way of life — the fraud, the arrogance, the denial of the Judgment — might be wrong. The believers' faith, patience, and integrity were a reproach. Laughter was the sinners' way of neutralizing the reproach without answering it — the same function that "fables of the ancients" (v. 13) served against the Qur'an itself. One does not refute a fool; one laughs at him. By laughing, the sinners excused themselves from thinking.

They laughed because they measured by the *wrong scale*. Their scale was wealth, lineage, and power, and by that scale the believers — many of them poor, freed slaves, or foreigners — were nothing. The Qur'an's whole argument in this sūrah is that this scale is *false*: the true measure is the book in ʿIlliyyūn or Sijjīn, and by that measure the believers were kings and the mockers were criminals. The sinners laughed because they could not see the true scale — because, as verse 14 said, their hearts were rusted.

They laughed because laughter *felt* like superiority. There is a pleasure in derision: it makes the mocker feel large. This pleasure is one of the subtler temptations of the human soul, and the Qur'an forbids it explicitly to the believers:

> *"O you who believe! Let not a people ridicule another people; perhaps they may be better than them. Nor let women ridicule other women; perhaps they may be better than them. And do not insult one another and do not call each other by offensive nicknames."* (Qur'an 49:11)

*"Perhaps they may be better than them."* This is exactly the situation of the mockers of this sūrah: they laughed at people who were better than them by every measure that will count.

**The Believers' Experience of Being Laughed At**

For the first Muslims, this verse described a daily reality. To be laughed at by the powerful, in one's own city, for one's most sacred convictions, is a particular kind of suffering — not the sharp pain of physical persecution but the grinding humiliation of contempt. The Qur'an does not minimize this suffering. It records it, names its perpetrators as criminals, and promises its reversal. This is itself a consolation: God *saw* the laughter. It was recorded in the book. It will be answered.

The Prophet ﷺ himself endured this mockery and was consoled by God: *"And We already know that your breast is constrained by what they say"* (15:97); *"Messengers before you were also mocked"* (6:10). The consolation was not that the mockery did not hurt, but that it was *known*, *recorded*, and *temporary*.

**A Warning to Believers Against Mocking**

The verse has a second edge, which the scholars did not fail to draw. If the *sinners'* laughter at the *believers* was a crime recorded in Sijjīn, then any laughter of contempt — including a believer's laughter at another believer, or at a sinner whom God may yet guide — carries the same danger. The Prophet ﷺ said, as recorded by Muslim:

> *"It is enough evil for a person that he despises his Muslim brother."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2564)

And he warned that whoever shames his brother for a sin will not die until he commits it himself (al-Tirmidhī 2505). The believer who reads this verse should not only take comfort that his mockers will be answered; he should examine his own laughter. Contempt is a disposition of the *mujrim*; the *barr* is expansive and merciful even toward those who err.

**The Modern Face of the Verse**

The verse's applicability to contemporary life is direct. In many settings today, sincere religious commitment is met not with argument but with amused condescension — the raised eyebrow, the knowing smile, the "you don't really believe that." Believers in universities, workplaces, and media environments recognize the laughter of this verse. The Qur'an's response is not to tell the believer to laugh back, nor to be ashamed, but to hold steady in the knowledge that the laughter is *recorded*, that its authors are measured by a scale they cannot see, and that the Day is coming — as the next verses describe in detail — when the positions will be exactly reversed.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30

> **When they passed them, they would wink at one another.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Gesture of Contempt**

The sūrah now describes the mockers' behavior in closer detail, moving from open laughter to a subtler gesture: *wa idhā marrū bihim yataghāmazūn* — "And when they passed by them, they would wink at one another." The scene is drawn with the precision of an eyewitness. The believers walk through the streets or the market; the sinners pass them; and as they pass, they exchange glances, raise eyebrows, nudge one another, make the small facial signals that say, without a word, *look at these fools*. The verse captures the exact texture of social contempt as it is actually practiced: not always in loud mockery, but in the quiet, knowing gesture exchanged between insiders at the expense of the outsider.

**The Word *Yataghāmazūn*: The Language of the Eyes**

The verb *yataghāmazūn* is the sixth form of the root *gh-m-z*, and it is a word of considerable expressiveness. The base meaning of *ghamaza* is to press or squeeze with the hand — as one presses a wound to test for pain, or squeezes a fruit to test its ripeness. From this physical sense came the meaning of *pressing the eyelid*: to wink, to signal with the eye or eyebrow. And from that came the sense of *finding fault, insinuating, mocking by gesture* — *ghamaza fīhi* means to speak ill of someone by hints, to disparage him covertly. The sixth form, *taghāmaza*, is reciprocal: *they winked at one another*, exchanged the gesture back and forth.

The word therefore describes a whole social ritual: a mocking signal passed among a group at the expense of a target who sees it but cannot answer it. Al-Zamakhsharī explains: they would point at the believers with their eyes and eyebrows, mocking them, saying by gesture what they did not need to say in words. Ibn Kathīr writes: they would wink at one another in disparagement of the believers, making light of them.

**Why Winking Is Worse Than Laughter**

One might think that the wink is a milder offense than open laughter. The Qur'an lists it *second*, as if in escalation, and there is a reason. Open laughter is at least honest: it declares its contempt and can be answered. The wink is *cowardly* and *conspiratorial*. It creates a circle of the "knowing" from which the believer is excluded; it communicates contempt while maintaining deniability — "I didn't say anything"; and it enlists others in the mockery, so that the believer is not merely laughed at by one person but by a *group* that has silently agreed on his ridiculousness. The wink is the gesture of the clique, and it is more wounding than the laugh because it turns contempt into a shared social fact.

The Qur'an elsewhere condemns the person who mocks by gesture with a whole sūrah of woe: *"Woe to every slanderer and backbiter (humazatin lumazah)"* (104:1). The commentators explain that *humazah* is the one who mocks with gesture — with eye, eyebrow, hand — and *lumazah* is the one who mocks with words. Both are condemned. The wink of this verse is the *hamz* of Sūrah al-Humazah, and it carries the same woe.

**The Contrast with the Gaze of the Righteous**

This verse presents a *gaze*, and it invites comparison with the two other gazes in the sūrah. The righteous, on their couches, *gaze* (*yanẓurūn*, v. 23) — a gaze of contemplation, delight, and, at its summit, the vision of God. The sinners, in the streets, *wink* (*yataghāmazūn*) — a gaze of contempt, conspiracy, and mockery. And in verse 35, the believers will *gaze* again, this time at the fate of those who winked at them. The sūrah is, among other things, a study in *how people look at one another*. The sinners' eyes were instruments of belittling; the righteous' eyes are instruments of seeing truly. On the Day of Judgment, the difference between the two kinds of sight is made permanent: the one who winked is veiled from his Lord (v. 15); the one who was winked at gazes upon Him (v. 23).

**The Recording of Gestures**

There is a significant implication in this verse for the doctrine of the inscribed record (vv. 9, 20). The Qur'an is recording *winks* — gestures so small and fleeting that no human court would notice them, gestures that the mockers themselves would have forgotten within the hour. Yet here they are, in the eternal Book, cited as evidence on the Day of Judgment. This is the sūrah's principle of "every small thing" applied to the realm of *body language*. The defrauder thought his skimmed grams too small to be recorded; the mockers thought their winks too small to be recorded; both were wrong. The Qur'an says: *"Not a word does he utter but that with him is an observer ready"* (50:18) — and this verse extends the principle beyond words to the eyebrow.

For the believer, this is both a warning and a consolation. A warning: my own small gestures of contempt — the eye-roll, the smirk, the knowing glance exchanged about someone — are recorded with the same precision. A consolation: every wink directed at me for my faith was seen, and is written, and will be answered.

**The Social Mechanism of Exclusion**

The verse describes a mechanism that sociologists have studied under the name of *informal social control*: the use of subtle signals — glances, tones, exclusions — to enforce conformity and punish deviation. The Makkan elite used this mechanism against the believers with great effectiveness. It did not require laws or violence; a wink among the powerful was enough to mark a person as an outsider, to close doors of commerce and marriage, to make his life in the city difficult. The wink was a form of *sanction*.

The Qur'an's response is to reveal that this sanction is itself *sanctioned*. The mockers thought they were the judges — as verse 33 will say, they behaved as if they had been appointed guardians over the believers. But they were themselves under judgment. Every wink they passed was passed under the eyes of the recording angels. The social power they exercised through gesture was a borrowed, temporary power; the true power of judgment belongs to God, and it will be exercised on a Day when the winkers stand sweating before the Lord of the worlds (v. 6).

**The Believers' Response: Neither Winking Back Nor Breaking**

How were the believers to respond to the winks? The Qur'an's guidance, given in this sūrah and elsewhere, is neither retaliation nor collapse. Not retaliation: the believers are not told to wink back, to mock in return, to descend to the level of their tormentors. The Qur'an says: *"And the servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth in humility, and when the ignorant address them, they say, 'Peace'"* (25:63) — and: *"And when they pass by idle talk, they pass by with dignity"* (25:72). The believers *pass by*. The same verb — *marrū*, "they passed" — describes the sinners passing the believers in our verse; the Qur'an prescribes for the believers a *different manner* of passing: with dignity, not with contempt.

And not collapse: the believers are not to be shamed into abandoning their faith by the pressure of gesture. The Qur'an strengthens them with the knowledge of what awaits: their book in ʿIlliyyūn, their couches, their radiant faces, their sealed wine. The one who knows he will drink Tasnīm does not lose his composure over a wink.

The Prophet ﷺ, when he was mocked, was told: *"So be patient, as the messengers of firm resolve were patient"* (46:35). His Companions learned that patience from him. Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt, one of the most persecuted, once asked the Prophet ﷺ to pray for relief, and the Prophet ﷺ reminded him of the believers before them who were sawn in half and combed with iron combs without abandoning their faith, and said: *"By God, this matter will be completed"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3612). The winks of the Makkan elite were, in that perspective, a small thing to bear.

**The Reversal to Come**

The verse describes the sinners passing the believers *in this world* and winking. The sūrah will describe, in verses 34–35, the believers *in the next world* looking at the sinners from their couches. The two scenes are constructed as mirror images. In this world, the sinners are the ones moving freely, passing by, in control of the social space, exchanging glances of power; the believers are the stationary objects of their contempt. In the next, the believers are seated on thrones, gazing at leisure; the sinners are the objects of the gaze. The verb changes from *yataghāmazūn* (they wink — a gesture of contempt) to *yanẓurūn* (they gaze — a look of vindication). The reversal is exact, and it is the answer to every wink.

**A Practical Reflection**

The verse asks the reader to consider his own eyes. What do they do when someone passes who is different — more religious, less religious, poorer, stranger, less polished? Is there a wink, a glance to a companion, a small signal of superiority? The Qur'an records that signal. And what does the reader do when he is the object of such signals for his faith or his integrity? The sūrah's counsel is to pass by with dignity, to remember the record, and to keep his own gaze fixed on where it will rest on the Day when the winking is over: on the couches, gazing.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:31

> **And when they returned to their people, they would return blithely.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Scene: Mockery Carried Home**

The sūrah continues its precise portrait of the mockers by following them *home*: *wa idhā inqalabū ilā ahlihimu inqalabū fakihīn* — "And when they returned to their people, they returned in gleeful amusement." The sequence of verses 29–31 traces the full arc of the sinners' mockery through a day: in public gatherings, they laugh (v. 29); in the street, passing the believers, they wink (v. 30); and returning to their households in the evening, they arrive *fakihīn* — amused, pleased with themselves, full of jokes about what they saw. The mockery was not a momentary reaction; it was a *lifestyle*, filling their public and private hours.

**The Word *Inqalabū*: Turning Back**

The verb *inqalabū*, "they returned," is from the root *q-l-b*, which means to turn, to turn over, to revert — the root of *qalb*, the heart, so named because it constantly turns. *Inqalaba* means to turn back, to return to where one came from. The word appears twice in the verse for emphasis: *when they turned back ... they turned back amused*. The commentators note a subtle irony in the root: the sinners' *hearts* (*qulūb*) were the organ that should have turned toward God, and instead were covered with rust (v. 14); and the sinners themselves *turn back* (*inqalabū*) toward their people and their comfortable homes, amused, rather than turning toward the truth they had been shown.

The Qur'an uses this root for the final return of all people to God: *"Truly to our Lord we are returning (munqalibūn)"* (43:14; 26:50). The sinners' evening return home, amused, is set against the ultimate return that they denied. They turned back to their households; they will turn back to their Lord — and the second return will not be amusing.

**The Word *Fakihīn*: Two Readings, One Portrait**

The key word is *fakihīn*, and the source commentary notes an important variation in its recitation. In the reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim — the most widespread recitation in the Muslim world — the word is *fakihīn*, with a short vowel: "conceited," "self-satisfied," "gloating," "arrogantly pleased." In most other canonical recitations, it is *fākihīn*, with a long vowel: "amused," "jesting," "enjoying themselves," "blithe." Both readings are authentically transmitted, and, as often in the variant readings, they illuminate two facets of a single reality.

*Fākihīn* (long vowel) comes from *fākihah*, "fruit" — the pleasant thing one enjoys after a meal — and *fukāhah*, "joking, pleasant conversation." A *fākih* is one who is enjoying himself, in a good humor, telling jokes. On this reading, the sinners returned home in high spirits, entertaining their families with mocking imitations of the believers — the way people return from an outing amused by something ridiculous they saw.

*Fakihīn* (short vowel) carries the sense of *ashir* and *baṭir* — arrogant, insolent, puffed up with satisfaction. On this reading, the sinners returned home *pleased with themselves*: their mockery of the believers had confirmed their own superiority, and they carried that satisfaction home like a trophy.

Al-Ṭabarī records both readings and explains that they converge: the sinners returned amused and self-satisfied, delighting in their derision of the believers. Al-Qurṭubī cites the explanation that *fakihīn* means "rejoicing in what they have and pleased with their own state." The full portrait combines both: the sinners were *amused by the believers* and *pleased with themselves*, and the two feelings fed each other — the more ridiculous they made the believers, the more superior they felt.

**The Home as the Place Where Mockery Is Completed**

Why does the Qur'an follow the sinners home? Because it is at home, among one's own people, that the *true* attitude is revealed and *reinforced*. In public, mockery might be partly performance — a show of loyalty to one's class or tribe. At home, unobserved by outsiders, one drops the performance; and the Qur'an says that even there, the sinners were *fakihīn* — the contempt was genuine, not merely performed. Moreover, the home is where the next generation learns. The children of the Makkan elite grew up hearing their fathers return from the market laughing at Bilāl and ʿAmmār; they learned that believers were figures of fun. Mockery carried home is mockery *transmitted*. The Qur'an is showing how contempt for truth becomes a family culture.

There is also a note of *comfort* in the scene. The sinners return to their *ahl* — their households, their people, their comfortable circles. They are secure, prosperous, surrounded by family, at ease. The believers they mocked often had no such comfort: many had been cast out by their families for believing, had lost their wealth, and were harassed in the streets. The Qur'an's portrait of the sinner returning home *fakihīn* — amused and comfortable — is drawn to be reversed. In verse 34, the *believers* will be the ones at ease, on couches, and the amusement will be theirs.

**Amusement as a Spiritual Danger**

The verse names a state — *fakāhah*, lighthearted amusement — that is not, in itself, sinful. The Qur'an does not condemn joy or humor. What it condemns is amusement *at the expense of truth*: a lightness of heart that comes from treating the most serious matters as jokes. This is the disposition the Qur'an warns against throughout: *"and you were amusing yourselves (tasmadūn)"* (53:61), said of those who laugh and do not weep at the Qur'an; *"those who took their religion as amusement and play"* (7:51); *"Woe that Day to the deniers, who play in idle talk"* (52:11–12). The sinners' *fakāhah* is the mood of people who have decided that nothing is serious — that the Judgment is a fable, the believers are fools, and life is for enjoyment. It is the emotional form of denial.

The Prophet ﷺ warned his community against this mood, not by forbidding humor but by setting it in proportion. He said, as recorded by al-Tirmidhī:

> *"If you knew what I know, you would laugh little and weep much."* (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2312; also al-Bukhārī 4621 and Muslim 2359 in similar wording)

He himself smiled often and joked with his Companions, but his lightness never came at the expense of truth or of people. The difference between his humor and the sinners' *fakāhah* is the difference between joy that includes others and amusement that excludes and belittles them.

**The Qur'an's Consistent Portrait of the Comfortable Denier**

The figure described in this verse — wealthy, secure, amused, contemptuous of the believers — is one the Qur'an draws again and again, and it is worth recalling the pattern, because it shows that the verse is not about one set of Makkans but about a permanent human type.

Of the people of Noah, the chiefs said: *"We do not see you followed except by those who are the lowest of us, at first thought"* (11:27). Of the people of Shuʿayb — the very people destroyed for short-measuring, the sin of this sūrah's opening — the chiefs said: *"We will surely drive you out of our city, O Shuʿayb, and those who have believed with you"* (7:88). Of the comfortable in every age, God says:

> *"And We did not send into any city a warner except that its affluent ones said, 'Truly we are disbelievers in what you have been sent with.' And they said, 'We are greater in wealth and children, and we are not to be punished.'"* (Qur'an 34:34–35)

*"We are greater in wealth and children, and we are not to be punished."* This is the creed of the man who returns home *fakihīn*. His comfort is his proof. He reasons: if God were displeased with me, would I be this comfortable? If the believers were right, would they be this poor? The Qur'an's answer is that worldly comfort proves nothing about one's standing with God — *"Do they think that what We extend to them of wealth and children is [because] We hasten for them good things? Rather, they do not perceive"* (23:55–56) — and that the true scale will be shown on the Day when the comfortable man's book is opened in Sijjīn.

**The Believer's Perspective on the Comfortable Mocker**

For the believer who watches the mocker return home in comfort while he himself struggles, this verse offers a discipline of *perspective*. The Qur'an does not deny that the sinner is, for now, comfortable and amused. It asks the believer to see that comfort in its true dimensions: brief, borrowed, and about to be reversed. The Prophet ﷺ described this world, for the believer, as a prison, and for the disbeliever, as a paradise (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2956) — meaning that the believer's restraint feels confining now, and the disbeliever's license feels free, but the positions invert at death. The one returning home *fakihīn* is enjoying the only paradise he will ever have.

The believer is also cautioned against *envying* the mocker's ease. The Qur'an says: *"Do not extend your eyes toward what We have given for enjoyment to categories of them, the splendor of the worldly life, that We may test them thereby"* (20:131). The sinner's comfortable evening is a *test* — for him, whether it will make him grateful or arrogant; and for the believer, whether it will make him envious or steadfast.

**A Practical Reflection**

The verse invites a question about how one *returns home*. What does a person carry back to his family at the end of the day? The sinner carried mockery and self-satisfaction. The believer is invited to carry something else: gratitude for the day's provision, repentance for its failures, kindness toward those who share his home, and — if he was mocked that day — patience rather than bitterness. What one brings home is what one's household becomes. The mockers' homes became nurseries of contempt; the believers' homes, the Qur'an says elsewhere, are houses in which God's name is remembered and His light is found (24:36). The difference between the two homes is the difference between the two returns — and between the two final returns, to Sijjīn or to ʿIlliyyūn.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:32

> **And when they saw them, they said, "Truly these are astray!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Scene: The Verdict of the Mockers**

The portrait of the sinners reaches its climax not in a gesture but in a *judgment*: *wa idhā raʾawhum qālū inna hāʾulāʾi la-ḍāllūn* — "And when they saw them, they said, 'Truly these are astray!'" The progression through verses 29–32 has moved from laughter (an emotional reaction), to winking (a gestural signal), to gloating at home (a private satisfaction), and now to a *verbal verdict* pronounced with full confidence and emphasis. The sinners do not merely find the believers ridiculous; they pronounce them *misguided* — wrong about the deepest questions of existence. Mockery has hardened into a theological judgment.

**The Rhetorical Force of the Verdict**

The Arabic is strikingly emphatic: *inna* (truly) + *hāʾulāʾi* (these) + *la-* (surely) + *ḍāllūn* (astray). The sinners speak with the confidence of people who have no doubt. They use the near demonstrative *hāʾulāʾi*, "these" — pointing at the believers with dismissive familiarity, the way one points at an object. And they use the emphatic particles of *certainty* — the same particles the Qur'an itself uses for its most solemn declarations. The sinners have, in effect, borrowed the language of revelation to pronounce a counter-revelation: *truly, surely, these are the misguided ones*.

This confidence is the mark of the rusted heart (v. 14). A heart still capable of doubt might say, "Perhaps they are mistaken." The sealed heart says, "Truly these are astray," with the same certainty it applies to the price of grain. The sinners' certainty is not the fruit of investigation; it is the fruit of having stopped investigating.

**The Word *Ḍāllūn*: The Ultimate Accusation**

*Ḍāllūn* is from the root *ḍ-l-l*, "to go astray, to lose the way, to wander from the path." The Qur'an uses it as one of its central terms for the opposite of guidance (*hudā*). The Fātiḥah closes with a prayer to be kept from *"the path of those who have gone astray (al-ḍāllīn)"* (1:7). Guidance and misguidance are the two roads between which the whole Qur'an situates the human being; to call someone *ḍāll* is to place him on the road that ends in ruin.

There is a pointed irony in the sinners' choice of this word, and the commentators note it. The sinners themselves are the *ḍāllūn* by the Qur'an's own definition: they deny the Day of Judgment (v. 11), they call the revelation fables (v. 13), their hearts are rusted (v. 14). Yet they use the very term that describes them to describe the believers. This is a phenomenon the Qur'an documents repeatedly: the misguided *invert* the vocabulary of guidance, calling the guided "astray" and their own error "the way of our fathers." Pharaoh said of Moses: *"I fear that he will change your religion or that he will cause corruption in the land"* (40:26) — accusing the prophet of corruption. The people of Lot said of him and his followers: *"Expel them from your city; indeed, they are people who keep themselves pure!"* (7:82) — turning purity itself into an accusation. The inversion is a signature of the sealed heart: it sees the world upside down and describes it with perfect confidence.

**Why the Sinners Called the Believers "Astray"**

The source commentary notes that the disbelievers deemed the believers astray *for following the Prophet*. The specific content of the accusation deserves to be drawn out, because it recurs in every age.

The believers were "astray," in the sinners' eyes, because they had *abandoned the religion of their fathers*. The Makkan elite's most persistent argument was the argument from tradition: *"We found our fathers upon a way, and we are following in their footsteps"* (43:22). To leave the ancestral path was, by definition, to go astray. The Qur'an's answer was that ancestors are not proof: *"Even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided?"* (2:170).

The believers were "astray" because they had *given up worldly advantage for an invisible reward*. To the sinners, whose measure of success was wealth and status, a person who accepted poverty, ostracism, and persecution for the sake of a promised Paradise was obviously deluded. The sinners' judgment here is the same as the defrauder's judgment about the measure: only what is visible counts. The Qur'an's whole response is the third section of this sūrah — ʿIlliyyūn, the couches, Tasnīm — which shows that the believers had traded the visible for something incomparably greater.

And the believers were "astray" because they *followed a man* — Muhammad ﷺ — whom the elite regarded as an orphan of no standing, a poet or a madman or a sorcerer. *"Is this the one God sent as a messenger?"* (25:41). The Qur'an's answer was the Qur'an itself: the challenge to produce its like, which the eloquent elite could not meet.

**The Verdict Has No Authority**

The next verse will make the decisive rebuttal — *"though they were not sent as guardians over them"* — but its logic is already implicit here. The sinners pronounce a *verdict*: "astray." But who appointed them judges? On what authority do they declare the guided misguided? They have no revelation, no knowledge of the Unseen, no commission from God. Their verdict is an *opinion* dressed as a *judgment*, and the Qur'an will strip the dress away. The source commentary cites the reading that the sinners arrogated to themselves the role of witnesses against the believers — and that in the Hereafter, the roles will be reversed.

**The Believers' Response to Being Called Astray**

How should the believer respond when the confident, powerful, and comfortable pronounce him misguided? The Qur'an gives a threefold answer.

First, *do not be shaken*. The judgment of the misguided about guidance is worthless. The Qur'an says: *"And if you obey most of those on the earth, they will lead you astray from the way of God. They follow nothing but conjecture"* (6:116). The majority's confident verdict is conjecture; the believer's guidance is from God.

Second, *do not return the accusation carelessly*. The believer knows that the sinners are, in fact, the astray ones — the Qur'an says so. But the believer is not to make declaring people astray his habit, because he does not know the end of any person; the mocker of today may be the believer of tomorrow, as ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb and Khālid ibn al-Walīd were. The Prophet ﷺ said, as recorded by Muslim, that when a man declared "By God, God will not forgive so-and-so," God said: *"Who is this who swears by Me that I will not forgive so-and-so? I have forgiven him and nullified your deeds"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2621). The believer entrusts final judgments to God.

Third, *hold to the criterion*. The believer knows what guidance is because God has defined it: *"This is the Book in which there is no doubt, a guidance for the God-fearing"* (2:2). He does not measure his guidance by the approval of the sinners, but by his conformity to the Book. When he is called "astray," he checks himself against the Qur'an, not against the crowd — and finding himself on the path, he continues.

**The Psychological Function of Calling Others Astray**

There is a further insight in the verse. Why did the sinners *need* to call the believers astray? Because the believers' existence posed a question the sinners could not otherwise answer: *what if they are right?* If the believers were right, the sinners' entire way of life — the fraud, the mockery, the comfortable denial — was ruin. This possibility was intolerable. The verdict "truly these are astray" was the sinners' way of closing the question. Once the believers were classified as misguided, their faith, their patience, and their integrity could be dismissed as symptoms of delusion rather than taken as evidence of truth. The verdict was a *defense mechanism* — the same function served by "fables of the ancients" (v. 13) and by laughter (v. 29). Every strategy of the sinner in this sūrah is a strategy for *not having to think*.

This insight applies far beyond seventh-century Makkah. Whenever one finds oneself pronouncing a confident verdict on the misguidedness of people whose integrity one cannot fault — whose lives are honest, whose patience is real, whose faith produces good fruit — one should ask whether the verdict is a conclusion or a defense.

**The Reversal to Come**

The sinners *saw* the believers and *said* they were astray. On the Day of Judgment, the believers will *see* the sinners — from their couches, gazing (v. 35) — and the truth will be *shown*, not said: the sinners in the Fire, requited (v. 36). The verdict of this world, pronounced in words by those who had no authority, will be overturned by the verdict of the next, pronounced in reality by the Lord of the worlds. The next verse names precisely why the sinners' verdict never had any standing.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:33

> **Though they were not sent as guardians over them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God's Rebuttal in a Single Clause**

After four verses recording the sinners' laughter, winks, gloating, and verdict, God answers them with one sentence: *wa mā ursilū ʿalayhim ḥāfiẓīn* — "And they were not sent as guardians over them." The rebuttal is brief, dry, and complete. The sinners had appointed themselves judges of the believers' guidance; God informs them that no one made the appointment. They were not *sent* — not commissioned, not authorized, not given any role — as *guardians* over the believers. Their verdict of "astray" (v. 32) was pronounced by people who had no standing to pronounce it.

The verse is constructed in the passive: *mā ursilū*, "they were not sent." The passive raises the question: sent by *whom*? The only One who sends with authority in matters of guidance is God — He *sends* messengers (*rasūl*, from the same root, *r-s-l*). The sinners were not *sent* by God. They had no divine commission to watch over, judge, or correct the believers. The implicit contrast is with the Prophet ﷺ, who *was* sent, and who — even as a messenger — was told: *"You are not a guardian (ḥafīẓ) over them"* (42:48; 6:107). If God's own messenger was not a *ḥafīẓ* over people, in the sense of one responsible for their guidance and entitled to judge their hearts, how much less were the sinners of Makkah?

**The Word *Ḥāfiẓīn*: Guardians, Keepers, Watchers**

*Ḥāfiẓīn* is the plural of *ḥāfiẓ*, from the root *ḥ-f-ẓ*, "to guard, preserve, watch over, keep." The word has a range of senses that the commentators draw upon.

A *ḥāfiẓ* is a *guardian* — one responsible for another's welfare, as a parent for a child. The sinners were not the believers' guardians; the believers' welfare was not their charge.

A *ḥāfiẓ* is a *keeper of records* — the recording angels are called *ḥāfiẓīn*: *"And indeed, over you are keepers (ḥāfiẓīn), noble writers"* (82:10–11). The sinners were not the believers' recording angels; the believers' deeds were not theirs to note and judge. This sense connects directly to the sūrah's theme: there *are* keepers over the believers — the noble writers who inscribe their book in ʿIlliyyūn (vv. 18–21) — and the sinners are not among them.

A *ḥāfiẓ* is a *watcher* or *overseer* — one appointed to monitor conduct and enforce standards, like a market inspector. The sinners were not appointed to monitor the believers' religion.

And, as the source commentary notes, some early commentators glossed *ḥāfiẓīn* as *shāhidīn*, "witnesses": the sinners were not sent as witnesses over the believers, entitled to testify to their misguidance. Al-Ṭabrisī records that the sinners arrogated to themselves the role of witnesses *against* the believers, but in the Hereafter it is the *believers* who will bear witness — the Qur'an says the community of Muhammad ﷺ will be *"witnesses over mankind"* (2:143).

**The Principle: No One Is Appointed Judge of Another's Guidance**

The verse establishes a principle with wide application. The right to pronounce final judgment on the guidance or misguidance of a human being belongs to God alone. He has *sent* messengers to convey the criterion, and He has commanded the believers to enjoin right and forbid wrong; but He has not appointed any person as *ḥāfiẓ* over another's *inner* state, entitled to declare, with the confidence of the sinners in verse 32, "truly these are astray."

The Qur'an insists on this even for the Prophet ﷺ, in a series of verses that are remarkable for their restraint:

> *"So remind; you are only a reminder. You are not a controller (muṣayṭir) over them."* (Qur'an 88:21–22)

> *"And had your Lord willed, all those on the earth would have believed, all together. Then would you compel the people until they become believers?"* (Qur'an 10:99)

> *"Your duty is only to convey; and upon Us is the account."* (Qur'an 13:40)

If the Messenger's role was to *convey* and God's role was to *account*, then the sinners — who had no message to convey and no authority to account — were usurping a role that belonged to no creature at all.

**The Logic of the Rebuttal**

The verse's rebuttal has a precise logical structure. The sinners' verdict, "these are astray," presupposes a *standard* of guidance and an *authority* to apply it. God's answer challenges the authority: *you were not sent*. Without authority, the verdict is not a judgment but merely an opinion — and an opinion held by people whose hearts are rusted (v. 14), who deny the Judgment (v. 11), and whose book is in Sijjīn (v. 7). Their opinion of the believers' guidance carries exactly as much weight as a blind man's opinion of a painting.

There is also an implicit challenge to the sinners' *standard*. By what criterion did they judge the believers astray? By the criterion of their fathers' religion, their tribal customs, their wealth and status. But none of these is a divine standard; none was *sent*. The only sent standard is the revelation they dismissed as fables. So the sinners judged by an unsent standard, with an unsent authority, and reached an unsent verdict — three layers of presumption in a single word, "astray."

**A Warning to Believers as Well**

While the verse rebukes the sinners, the classical scholars recognized that its principle binds the believers too. The believer is *not* sent as a guardian over other believers' hearts. He may and must call to good and warn against evil; he may say of a *deed* that it is wrong; but he is not to appoint himself the judge of another's *final standing* with God, to declare fellow Muslims misguided with the emphatic confidence of the sinners in verse 32, or to make the *takfīr* (declaring others disbelievers) that the Prophet ﷺ so severely warned against:

> *"When a man says to his brother, 'O disbeliever,' it returns upon one of them."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6103; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 60)

The Companion Usāmah ibn Zayd once killed a man in battle who had pronounced the testimony of faith at the last moment, reasoning that the man said it only to save himself. The Prophet ﷺ rebuked him with a question he repeated until Usāmah wished he had not been a Muslim before that day: *"Did you split open his heart to know?"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 96). The believer is not a *ḥāfiẓ* over hearts. He was not sent to be.

This does not abolish moral judgment; the Qur'an commands the believers to distinguish right from wrong and to say so. It abolishes *presumptuous* judgment — the verdict on a person's guidance pronounced from the seat of a judge one was never given. The difference is between saying "this act is wrong, and I warn you against it" and saying "you are astray and I know your end." The first is the duty of every believer; the second is the presumption of the sinners in this sūrah.

**The Sociology of Self-Appointed Guardians**

The verse describes a phenomenon that recurs in every society: the self-appointment of the powerful as arbiters of what is normal, respectable, sane, and acceptable. The Makkan elite had no divine commission, but they had wealth, lineage, and control of the city's institutions, and they used these to position themselves as the judges of who was "astray." Every age has its equivalents — those who, by virtue of social standing, credentials, or cultural dominance, presume to decide which beliefs are reasonable and which are delusions, and who enforce the decision with laughter, winks, and verdicts.

The Qur'an's response is not to deny that such people have *power* — they clearly did — but to deny that they have *authority*. Power is the ability to impose; authority is the *right* to judge. The sinners had the first and not the second. The believer who understands this distinction is freed from the tyranny of the self-appointed guardian. He may suffer the mockery, but he does not accept the verdict, because he knows the verdict was pronounced by someone who was never sent.

**The Reversal Prepared**

The verse closes the portrait of the sinners' mockery and prepares the reversal. The sinners appointed themselves as *ḥāfiẓīn*, guardians and watchers over the believers, and they watched with contempt. On the Day of Judgment, the *true* watchers will be revealed: the recording angels, who watched the sinners' every wink and inscribed it in Sijjīn; and the believers, who will watch the sinners from their couches (v. 35). The one who set himself up as the watcher will become the watched. That is the justice the next three verses will describe.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:34

> **So that Day those who believe shall laugh at those who disbelieve,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Great Reversal**

With the particle *fa-* ("so, therefore"), the sūrah announces the consequence of everything that preceded: *fa-l-yawma alladhīna āmanū mina al-kuffāri yaḍḥakūn* — "So this Day, those who believed laugh at the disbelievers." The verse is the exact mirror of verse 29 — *"Truly those who sinned used to laugh at those who believed"* — with the subjects and objects reversed. In this world, the sinners laughed at the believers; on that Day, the believers laugh at the disbelievers. The Arabic word order even places *"at the disbelievers"* (*mina al-kuffār*) before the verb, exactly as verse 29 placed *"at those who believed"* (*mina alladhīna āmanū*) before its verb — the structure is a deliberate inversion, and any listener who heard both verses would feel the symmetry like the two pans of a scale coming level.

The word *al-yawm*, "this Day" (translated "that Day"), refers to the Day of Judgment described in verse 6 — the Day of Standing before the Lord of the worlds. The Qur'an, speaking from that Day's vantage, uses the present: *this* Day. The reversal is not a distant hope; it is spoken of as present and accomplished.

**From "Those Who Sinned" to "The Disbelievers"**

Note the change in the name of the mockers. In verse 29 they were *alladhīna ajramū*, "those who committed crimes." Here they are *al-kuffār*, "the disbelievers." The shift is significant. In this world, their mockery appeared as a *crime* — an act of contempt against the believers. On the Day of Judgment, its root is exposed: it was *kufr*, disbelief — the rejection of God and His Day. The two names describe the same people from two angles: what they *did* (crimes) and what they *were* (disbelievers). The sūrah has consistently traced conduct to conviction, and it does so once more at the end.

**Why the Believers Laugh: The Nature of Their Laughter**

The verse says the believers *laugh* at the disbelievers, and this requires careful understanding, because the laughter of the righteous in Paradise is not the same as the laughter of the sinners in this world, even though the same verb is used.

The sinners' laughter was *mockery*: cruel, contemptuous, aimed at wounding, born of a false sense of superiority. The believers' laughter is *vindication*: the natural response of those who were told they were fools and now see, with total clarity, that they were right. It is the laughter of relief, of justice witnessed, of a long trial ended in triumph. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī explain that the believers laugh because they see the truth of what they believed and the falsity of what the disbelievers claimed — they laugh at the *absurdity* of the disbelievers' former confidence, now exposed. It is not the laughter of the bully but the laughter of the vindicated.

The scholars also note that the believers' laughter is *just*, whereas the sinners' was *unjust*. The sinners laughed at people who were better than them and did not deserve it. The believers laugh at people who mocked them without cause and *do* deserve it — the laughter is *qiṣāṣ*, retribution in kind, which is the essence of divine justice: *"the recompense of an evil is an evil like it"* (42:40). The Qur'an states the principle of retributive symmetry explicitly for mockery:

> *"God mocks them (Allāhu yastahziʾu bihim) and prolongs them in their transgression, wandering blindly."* (Qur'an 2:15)

> *"Truly those who committed crimes used to laugh at those who believed ... So this Day those who believed laugh at the disbelievers."* (Qur'an 83:29, 34)

**The Ḥadīth of the Gate of Paradise**

The commentators, including Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, record a report from Abū Ṣāliḥ and others describing *how* this laughter takes place. It is said that a gate will be opened from Paradise toward the Fire, and the people of the Fire will be told, "Come out to it!" They will hasten toward the gate, and when they reach it, it will be shut in their faces — and the people of Paradise will laugh at them. This is repeated. The report is transmitted as an explanation from the early commentators rather than as a prophetic ḥadīth of the first rank, but it is consistent with what the Qur'an says elsewhere about the people of the Fire being denied relief:

> *"And the companions of the Fire will call to the companions of Paradise, 'Pour upon us some water or from whatever God has provided you.' They will say, 'Truly God has forbidden them both to the disbelievers.'"* (Qur'an 7:50)

The laughter, on this account, is the believers' response to seeing the disbelievers — who had mocked the believers' hope of Paradise — now themselves hoping for Paradise and being refused. The mockers of hope become the hopers, and their hope is mocked.

**A Reassurance: The Laughter Does Not Diminish the Believers**

Some readers are troubled by the idea of the people of Paradise laughing at the suffering of others. Several considerations resolve the difficulty.

The Qur'an describes the people of Paradise as having every trace of malice removed from their hearts: *"And We shall remove whatever is in their breasts of resentment"* (7:43; 15:47). Their laughter therefore cannot be malicious. It is not the pleasure of seeing others suffer for its own sake; it is the *satisfaction of justice* — the same satisfaction any just person feels when a tyrant is brought to account or a fraud is exposed. The Qur'an calls this satisfaction the healing of the believers' breasts: *"and He will heal the breasts of a believing people"* (9:14).

The laughter is also a *completion of the believers' bliss*. Part of the pain of being mocked in this world is the feeling that the mockery will never be answered — that the mocker will go to his grave comfortable and confident, and the mocked will go to his grave misunderstood. The Qur'an tells the believer that this is not so. The mockery *will* be answered, publicly, on the Day when all is revealed, and the believer will *see* it. This knowledge is part of what makes patience possible in this world. The laughter of verse 34 is the promise that no injustice endured for faith is final.

And the laughter is *proportionate*. The Qur'an does not describe the believers tormenting the disbelievers or taking active vengeance; it describes them *laughing* — a response exactly matched to the offense. The disbelievers laughed; the believers laugh. The scale is set level, not tilted.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Structural Key**

This verse reveals the architecture of the whole sūrah. The first section condemned the defrauder — the one who takes full measure and gives short measure. The fourth section shows God's measure: the mockers gave the believers contempt in full measure, and they receive it back in exactly equal measure. God is not a *muṭaffif*. He does not give the believers less vindication than they were denied, nor the disbelievers less humiliation than they inflicted. The scale of the Day of Judgment is perfectly balanced, and this verse is one of its pans coming to rest.

**The Consolation of the Persecuted**

For the first believers in Makkah — Bilāl, who was dragged through the streets with a rope around his neck while the elite laughed; ʿAmmār, whose parents were tortured to death before his eyes; Khabbāb, who was laid on burning coals — this verse was a promise given in the midst of their suffering. They were told: the day is coming when *you* will laugh. Not in this world, perhaps — though many of them lived to see Makkah conquered and their tormentors humbled — but on the Day that matters. And history records that they held to this promise with astonishing steadfastness. Bilāl, under torture, repeated *"Aḥad, Aḥad"* — "One, One." He was laughing, in a sense, already: he knew how the story ended.

For believers today who endure the softer mockery of the modern world — the condescension, the exclusion, the amused dismissal — the verse is the same promise. It does not call them to retaliate. It calls them to patience in the certainty that the laughter of this world is not the last laughter. The Prophet ﷺ said, as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, that on the Day of Resurrection the believers will be given their reward in full, and that this world's suffering will then seem as if it never was — a person who lived the most miserable life in this world will be dipped once in Paradise and asked whether he ever saw hardship, and he will say, *"No, by God, my Lord"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2807). The one who is dipped in Paradise forgets the mockery entirely — except, as this verse says, to laugh at it.

**A Reflection on Whose Laughter Lasts**

The verse invites every reader to ask: whose laughter lasts? The laughter of the comfortable mocker in this world is real but brief — a lifetime at most, and a lifetime is short. The laughter of the vindicated believer on that Day is eternal. Any rational person, choosing between the two, chooses the second. The defrauder chose the brief profit; the mocker chose the brief laughter; both traded the lasting for the passing. The believer, who filled the measure and bore the mockery, chose the laughter that does not end — and the next verse shows him enjoying it from the same couches on which he gazed upon his Lord.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:35

> **upon couches, gazing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Words, a Second Time**

For the second time in the sūrah, the phrase *ʿalā al-arāʾiki yanẓurūn* — "upon couches, gazing" — appears. Its first occurrence was in verse 23, in the description of the bliss of the *abrār*. Its second occurrence is here, immediately after the announcement that the believers laugh at the disbelievers. The repetition is one of the sūrah's most deliberate artistic and theological devices, and the two occurrences must be read together.

In verse 23, the context was the *enjoyment* of Paradise: the righteous, in bliss, on couches, gazing — at the Garden, at one another, at their Lord. In verse 35, the context is the *vindication* of the believers: they laugh at the disbelievers, on couches, gazing — and here, as the source commentary notes, the natural reading is that they gaze at the disbelievers and the requital they receive. The same posture, the same activity, but the object of the gaze has shifted with the context. The believers who gazed upon their Lord in delight now gaze upon their mockers in vindication. Both gazes are theirs; neither disturbs the other.

**The Couch as the Seat of Judgment Reversed**

The *arāʾik*, as discussed at verse 23, are the raised, canopied couches of kings — seats of honor, security, and sovereignty. Their reappearance here, in the context of the believers looking at the disbelievers, adds a new dimension: the couch is now also a *tribunal*. In this world, the sinners appointed themselves judges of the believers, pronounced them astray (v. 32), and did so from the seats of social power — their assemblies, their positions of wealth and influence. On the Day of Judgment, the believers are seated on the *true* seats of honor, and from those seats they look upon the ones who judged them. The self-appointed judges stand in the dock; the ones they judged sit on the thrones.

This is the reversal that the whole fourth section has been building toward. Verse 29: the sinners laughed at the believers. Verse 30: the sinners *passed by* the believers, winking — they were the mobile, powerful ones, the believers the stationary objects of contempt. Verse 31: the sinners returned to their comfortable homes. Verse 32: the sinners *saw* the believers and pronounced them astray. Now, in verses 34–35, every element is inverted: the believers laugh; the believers are *seated* in comfort and honor while the disbelievers are the objects of attention; the believers are at home in Paradise; and the believers *see* the disbelievers — not to pronounce a verdict, for God has already pronounced it, but to *witness* its execution.

**Gazing at What, Precisely?**

The commentators, as the source notes, understand the gazing in verse 35 as directed toward the disbelievers in the Fire, and toward the punishment they receive for having mocked the believers. Ibn ʿAjībah and others read it this way, and the context requires it: the verse follows immediately upon "those who believed laugh at the disbelievers," and the gazing is the medium of the laughing — they look, and they laugh at what they see.

The Qur'an describes such a scene elsewhere in detail. In Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt, a man in Paradise recalls a companion in this world who used to mock his belief in the Resurrection — *"Are you indeed of those who affirm [it]?"* — and then:

> *"He will say, 'Would you look?' And he will look and see him in the midst of the Blaze. He will say, 'By God, you almost ruined me! And had it not been for the favor of my Lord, I would have been among those brought in [to the Fire].'"* (Qur'an 37:54–57)

That passage illuminates verse 35 precisely. The believer *looks* (*iṭṭalaʿa*), *sees* his former mocker in the Fire, and responds not with cruelty but with a sober recognition of God's favor: *had it not been for the favor of my Lord, I would have been among them.* The gaze of the believer at the disbeliever is not gloating for its own sake; it is the *witnessing of justice* mixed with *gratitude for mercy*. The believer sees where he might have been, and where he is, and the difference is God's grace.

**Why God Lets the Believers See**

One might ask why the believers are granted this sight at all. Why not simply reward the believers and punish the disbelievers separately, out of each other's view? The Qur'an's answer, developed by the commentators, has several dimensions.

*Completion of justice.* Justice is not only done; it must be *seen* to be done — by the wronged party especially. The believers who were mocked in public, before witnesses, are vindicated in public, before witnesses. The mockery was a social act; its reversal is a social act. If the believers never saw their mockers requited, part of the wrong — the *public* humiliation — would remain unanswered.

*Completion of bliss.* The Qur'an says of the people of Paradise that they will *"have whatever they wish"* (16:31). Among the things a wronged person naturally wishes is to see the wrong righted. God grants it. The sight of the mockers requited is, for the believers, one of the pleasures of Paradise — not a cruel pleasure, but the deep satisfaction of a moral universe made whole.

*Completion of the mockers' punishment.* Part of the mockers' punishment is to be *seen* by those they mocked. The one who laughed at the believer from a position of power must now be looked at by that same believer from a position of infinite superiority. The Qur'an says the disbelievers will be shown the believers' state so that their regret is complete: *"and they will see the punishment, and the bonds between them will be severed"* (2:166). To be observed in one's humiliation by the one you humiliated is the exact inversion of what the mockers did, and therefore the exact measure of justice.

**The Serenity of the Gaze**

The verse says the believers gaze from *couches* — reclining, at ease. This detail matters. The believers do not rise from their seats to taunt the disbelievers, do not descend to the edge of the Fire to jeer, do not lose their composure. They *recline* and *look*. The posture conveys a serenity that is itself a form of vindication. In this world, the believers were often agitated by the mockery — hurt, provoked, tempted to answer in kind. On the couches of Paradise, they are beyond agitation. They look at their former tormentors with the calm of those whose trial is over and whose case has been decided in their favor by the Lord of the worlds. There is no need to shout when one has won.

This serenity is the Hereafter's completion of the composure the Qur'an asked of the believers in this world: *"And when the ignorant address them, they say, 'Peace'"* (25:63). The believer who could pass by the winking mockers with dignity in this world is granted, in the next, the seat from which he looks upon them with perfect peace.

**The Two Gazes Held Together**

The repetition of the phrase from verse 23 asks the reader to hold both gazes together. The believers gaze *upon their Lord* (v. 23, on the reading most commentators favor) and *upon the requital of the disbelievers* (v. 35). These are not in tension. The first is the summit of bliss; the second is a component of justice. The believer who has seen the Face of God is not diminished by also seeing the truth about those who denied Him; on the contrary, both sights confirm the same reality — that God is true, that His promise is true, that the believer's faith was true, and that the mockers' confidence was false. To see God is to see the truth positively; to see the mockers requited is to see the same truth negatively. Both are aspects of *yanẓurūn*, the gaze of those who at last see clearly.

**The Sūrah's Vision of the Scale, Completed**

With this verse, the sūrah's grand symmetry is nearly complete. It began with a scale unjustly tilted by a human hand: full measure taken, short measure given. It ends with a scale perfectly leveled by the divine hand: the mockers who gave contempt receive it in full; the believers who bore contempt receive vindication in full; the mockers who *saw* the believers and judged them are now *seen* by the believers, requited; the believers who were made to stand as objects of ridicule now *recline*, and their former judges stand before them. Every quantity given is returned in exact measure. God is not a *muṭaffif*.

The final verse of the sūrah will state this conclusion as a question — a question whose answer is the whole point of the chapter.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:36

> **Have the disbelievers been requited for that which they used to do?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Closing Question**

The sūrah that opened with a pronouncement of woe closes with a question: *hal thuwwiba al-kuffāru mā kānū yafʿalūn* — "Have the disbelievers been requited for what they used to do?" The question is grammatically an interrogative, but rhetorically it is a declaration and a conclusion. It is the kind of question a judge might ask at the end of a trial, looking around the courtroom after sentence has been carried out: *Has justice been done?* The whole sūrah is the evidence; the expected answer is *yes*.

The commentators note that the question is addressed, in the first instance, to the believers on their couches (v. 35) — as if God, or the angels, or the believers themselves, survey the scene of the disbelievers' requital and ask: has the measure been paid? And the answer, visible before them, is that it has. The question therefore functions as *taqrīr*, confirmation: an affirmation cast as an inquiry so that the listener supplies the answer himself and thereby owns it.

**The Word *Thuwwiba*: The Irony of "Reward"**

The verb *thuwwiba* is the passive of *thawwaba*, from the root *th-w-b*, whose primary meaning is *to return* — *thāba* means to come back, and *thawb*, a garment, is so called because it "returns" to the wearer, or because the woven thread returns upon itself. From the sense of "return" comes *thawāb*, "recompense" — what *returns* to a person for his deeds. Crucially, *thawāb* in ordinary Qur'anic usage means *reward*, the *good* return for good deeds: *"and the reward (thawāb) of the Hereafter is better"* (12:57); *"a reward (thawāban) from God"* (3:195).

The Qur'an's use of *thuwwiba* — "have they been *rewarded*" — for the punishment of the disbelievers is therefore deliberate irony, and the commentators from al-Zamakhsharī onward drew attention to it. The word means, literally, "have they received their return?" — and their return is the Fire. But the word normally used for a pleasant return is here used for a terrible one, and the effect is *tahakkum*, mockery: the disbelievers, who mocked the believers' hope of *thawāb*, receive their own *thawāb* — of a very different kind. The irony echoes the reversal of laughter in verse 34: the mockers are mocked, even in the choice of a word. The Qur'an uses the same device elsewhere: *"So give them tidings (bashshirhum) of a painful punishment"* (3:21; 9:34; 84:24), where the verb for "good tidings" is used for a warning of torment.

At the same time, the root sense of *return* is exact and non-ironic. The disbelievers receive precisely what *returns* to them from their own deeds. Nothing is added from outside; the punishment is the boomerang of their own conduct.

**"For What They Used to Do": The Sūrah's Refrain of Deeds**

The verse closes on the phrase *mā kānū yafʿalūn*, "what they used to do." This phrase, and its variants (*mā kānū yaksibūn*, "what they used to earn," v. 14; *mā kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūn*, "what you used to deny," v. 17), runs through the sūrah as a refrain, and it states the principle on which the whole chapter rests: requital is *for deeds*. The disbelievers are not punished arbitrarily, nor for what others did, nor for what they could not help. They are requited for *what they used to do* — the short measures, the denial, the laughter, the winks, the verdicts of "astray." The past-continuous *kānū yafʿalūn* indicates habitual, repeated conduct: a *pattern* of doing, not a single act.

This is the Qur'an's consistent doctrine of justice:

> *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."* (Qur'an 99:7–8)

> *"And you will not be recompensed except for what you used to do."* (Qur'an 37:39)

> *"And your Lord is not ever unjust to His servants."* (Qur'an 41:46)

The person in the Fire is there because of *his own doing*, recorded in *his own book*, filed in Sijjīn by *his own* earning. No one can say, on that Day, that he was wronged.

**The Source Commentary's Insight: "Yes and No"**

The source commentary offers a subtle reading of the question that deserves expansion. It observes that the answer is *yes and no*. *Yes*: the disbelievers have been requited for the evil they did — the Fire is their exact return. *No*: they have *not* been "rewarded" for what only *appeared* to be good deeds — their acts of generosity, hospitality, kinship, or courage — because these, done without faith, have *come to nothing*. The Qur'an states this repeatedly:

> *"Those are the ones whose deeds have come to naught in this world and the Hereafter, and they will have no helpers."* (Qur'an 3:22)

> *"Say: Shall We inform you of the greatest losers in respect of deeds? Those whose effort is lost in worldly life, while they think that they are doing well in work."* (Qur'an 18:103–104)

> *"And We shall turn to whatever deeds they did and make them as scattered dust."* (Qur'an 25:23)

This is a difficult teaching, and it should be understood correctly. The Qur'an does not say that the disbelievers' good acts were not *good in themselves*, or that God does not *repay* them at all — the Prophet ﷺ taught that the disbeliever who does good is repaid for it *in this world*, with provision and ease, so that on the Day of Judgment he has nothing left to his credit (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2808). What the Qur'an says is that such deeds carry no weight in the *Hereafter*, because they were not done *for God* and were not accompanied by the faith that gives deeds their eternal value. A good deed done in denial of the Judge is like a payment made in a currency the bank does not accept: real effort, no credit. The source commentary notes that the phrase *"perform righteous deeds"* occurs over fifty times in the Qur'an and is *always* joined to belief — *"those who believe and do righteous deeds."* The pairing is not accidental. Faith is what makes a deed *righteous* in the sense that counts on that Day.

So the question *"Have the disbelievers been requited for what they used to do?"* has an answer that is more precise than a simple yes: they have been requited *in full* for their evil, and their apparent good has *evaporated*, leaving the evil unbalanced. The scale is not tilted against them; it is simply that they placed nothing of lasting weight upon it.

**The Question and the Defrauder**

The sūrah's final verse returns, in its logic, to its first. The defrauder demanded full measure for himself and gave short measure to others. The closing question asks whether the disbelievers — of whom the defrauder is the opening example — have received their *full measure*. And the answer is *yes*: God gives full measure. He does not skim from the requital of the wicked, nor from the reward of the righteous. The *muṭaffif* who spent his life ensuring that others received less than their due discovers, at the end, a Judge who ensures that everyone receives exactly what is due. He wanted a world of short measures; he is requited in a world of full ones.

This is the sūrah's deepest teaching about the divine attribute of *justice* (*ʿadl*). God's justice is not an abstract balance; it is the exact *return* of every deed to its doer, in the currency of that deed. The one who gave contempt receives contempt (v. 34). The one who withheld receives withholding — he is veiled (v. 15). The one who denied receives the words *"This is what you used to deny"* (v. 17). The one who mocked the believers' hope of reward receives his own *thawāb* — the Fire. Every requital is a *return*.

**Why the Sūrah Ends with a Question Rather Than a Statement**

The choice to end with a question rather than a declaration is a final stroke of the Qur'an's pedagogy. A statement is received passively; a question demands participation. The listener must *answer*, and in answering, he commits himself. Asked *"Have the disbelievers been requited for what they used to do?"*, the believer answers *yes* — and in that *yes* he affirms the whole doctrine of the sūrah: that deeds are recorded, that the Day is real, that the scale is just, that mockery is answered, that the measure is filled. The question turns the reader from an audience into a witness.

It also leaves the reader with a question about *himself*. If the disbelievers are requited for what they used to do, then *I* will be requited for what *I* used to do. What have I been doing? What is being written in my book? Which way is my measure tilted? The sūrah's last word, *yafʿalūn* — "they do" — hangs in the air, and the reader hears it as *afʿalu* — "I do." That is the intended effect. The sūrah does not end with a period but with a mirror.

**The Complete Arc of the Sūrah**

Looking back from this final verse, the reader can see the whole architecture at once. The sūrah opened with a *woe* upon those who cheat in the smallest measures (vv. 1–3), traced that cheating to a failure to reckon with the Resurrection and the Standing before the Lord of the worlds (vv. 4–6), described the recorded book of the wicked in the lowest prison and their denial, their rusted hearts, and their triple punishment of veil, fire, and rebuke (vv. 7–17), mirrored that portrait with the recorded book of the righteous in the highest heaven and their bliss of couches, radiant faces, sealed wine, musk, and Tasnīm (vv. 18–28), returned to earth to record the mockery the sinners heaped on the believers (vv. 29–33), and closed by reversing that mockery and asking whether the measure has been paid (vv. 34–36). The chapter is a single sustained argument: *God measures justly, therefore you must measure justly; God records everything, therefore nothing you do is small; God will reverse every injustice, therefore endure with patience and do not join the unjust.*

**A Final Reflection: Living Toward the Answer**

The sūrah of the defrauders ends, fittingly, with an accounting. The practical teaching of its last verse is that every person is, at every moment, *filling the measure* that will be returned to him. The grams withheld from a customer, the minutes stolen from an employer, the effort skimmed from a prayer, the wink exchanged at a believer's expense, the verdict of "astray" pronounced without authority — each is *mā kānū yafʿalūn*, "what they used to do," and each *returns*. And equally: the full measure given when no one would have known, the prayer completed when sleep called, the mockery borne with dignity, the half-date given to the poor — each is written in ʿIlliyyūn, witnessed by the nearest angels, and each *returns*, multiplied, as sealed wine and musk and Tasnīm.

The believer who has read this sūrah with attention knows how to answer its final question, and he knows what the answer asks of him. He measures justly because he will be measured. He fills the cup because his cup will be filled. He bears the laughter because he knows whose laughter lasts. And he strives — *fa-l-yatanāfasi al-mutanāfisūn* — for the seat on the couch, the radiant face, and the spring from which the nearest drink.

*Have the disbelievers been requited for what they used to do?* Yes. And so will every soul. May God make us among those whose book is in ʿIlliyyūn, whose faces shine with the splendor of bliss, and who are given to drink, in the presence of their Lord, from a spring whence drink those brought nigh.
