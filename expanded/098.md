# Sūrah al-Bayyinah (Chapter 98) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Short Chapter Built Around a Single Word**

Sūrah al-Bayyinah, "The Clear Proof," is a chapter of only eight verses, yet it carries one of the most tightly argued sequences of reasoning in the entire Qur'an. It takes its name from the word *al-bayyinah* in its first verse — the "clear proof" whose arrival divides human history into a before and an after. The word is not decorative. It is the hinge upon which every one of the eight verses turns: the clear proof must come (v. 1); it comes in the form of a Messenger reciting purified pages (vv. 2–3); its arrival exposes divisions that were latent all along (v. 4); it never asked of humanity anything other than sincere worship, prayer, and charity (v. 5); those who reject it after seeing it descend to the lowest rank of created beings (v. 6); those who accept it and act upon it rise to the highest (v. 7); and their final destination is not merely a reward of gardens but a mutual contentment between the Lord and His servant (v. 8).

The chapter has been known by several names, which is itself a sign of how the early community read it. It is called *al-Bayyinah* after the proof; *Lam Yakun* ("They Will Not / They Were Not") after its opening two words, which is how the earliest generations often identified sūrahs before formal titles became fixed; *al-Qiyāmah* ("The Resurrection") because of its closing scenes of Hell and the Gardens of Eden; *al-Bariyyah* ("Creation" or "Created Beings") after the striking phrase repeated in verses 6 and 7; *al-Infikāk* ("The Desisting / The Separation") after the difficult word *munfakkīn* in the first verse; and *Ahl al-Kitāb* ("The People of the Book") after those it directly addresses. A chapter this short with this many names is a chapter the community has handled often and from many angles.

**Makkan or Madinan: Why the Dispute Matters**

The commentators are divided over whether al-Bayyinah was revealed in Makkah or Madinah. Many, including Ibn Kathīr and a large number of the classical authorities, treat it as Madinan; others argue that it is more widely reported as Makkan. The dispute is not a trivial matter of chronology, because the setting shapes how one hears the argument.

If the sūrah is Madinan, its opening address to "those who disbelieve among the People of the Book" reads as a direct engagement with the Jewish and Christian communities the Muslims now lived beside in Madinah — communities that had scriptures, prophetic traditions, and long-standing expectations of a coming prophet. The verse then confronts a very specific historical disappointment: those who had waited for the proof did not embrace it when it arrived.

If the sūrah is Makkan, the address is broader and more prophetic in tone: it announces in advance, to a small persecuted community, the pattern that revelation always follows — a clear proof comes, and humanity divides over it. The reference to the alms (*zakāh*) in verse 5 would then be understood in its general Qur'anic sense of purifying charity rather than the later institutionalized rate and calculation of Madinan law.

A responsible reading holds both possibilities in view. The essential meaning of the chapter does not change with either dating: revelation arrives as evidence, evidence produces responsibility, and responsibility produces two destinies. What changes is only the immediacy of the historical audience.

**The Chapter God Commanded Be Recited to a Named Companion**

No introduction to this sūrah is complete without the extraordinary report that God commanded His Prophet ﷺ to recite this specific chapter to a specific man. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim both record from Anas ibn Mālik that the Prophet ﷺ said to Ubayy ibn Kaʿb:

> *"Truly God has commanded me to recite to you: 'Those who disbelieve among the People of the Book...'"* Ubayy said: *"And He named me to you?"* He said: *"Yes."* And Ubayy wept. (al-Bukhārī 4959; Muslim 799)

In another wording, Ubayy asked, "Was I mentioned to you by the Lord of the Worlds?" and the Prophet ﷺ affirmed it, and Ubayy's tears flowed. The scholars drew several lessons from this. First, that the Qur'an is not an abstract broadcast but a personal address; a man wept because the God of the heavens had spoken his name in the counsels of revelation. Second, that reciting to others — even to those already learned, for Ubayy was among the greatest reciters of the community — is a Prophetic practice and a form of teaching in which the teacher also listens. Third, and most relevant here, that this particular chapter was singled out for that honour, which drew the early scholars' attention to its content: a summary of what all revelation has ever asked, and a verdict on what happens when that request is answered or refused.

**The Argument of the Sūrah in Miniature**

Read as a single continuous argument, the chapter reasons like this. Human beings do not abandon entrenched error on their own; something decisive must reach them from outside their closed circle (v. 1). What reaches them is not an abstraction but a person carrying a text — a Messenger reciting pages kept pure (v. 2) — and the contents of those pages are straight, self-correcting, and free of the crookedness that human speculation always develops (v. 3). Once that proof arrives, it becomes the very thing people fight over; division follows knowledge rather than preceding it, which means the cause of religious fracture is rarely ignorance and usually something else — envy, interest, pride (v. 4). Yet what was actually asked of them was strikingly simple and unchanging across all revelations: worship God alone with sincerity, incline away from every rival object of devotion, establish the prayer, and give the purifying charity (v. 5). The chapter then delivers its two verdicts: rejection of the proof after its arrival places a human being at the bottom of the scale of created things (v. 6), while faith joined to righteous action places him at the top (v. 7). And the final verse discloses the true summit of reward: not gardens as such, but *riḍwān* — God pleased with them, and they pleased with Him (v. 8).

**Why the Contrast Is Placed at the Extremes**

The sūrah's most arresting rhetorical move is its use of superlatives at both ends: *sharr al-bariyyah*, "the worst of creation," and *khayr al-bariyyah*, "the best of creation." No middle ground is described. The Qur'an frequently draws its portraits at the extremes not because most people live at the extremes but because the extremes reveal the true nature of the scale. When a physician wants to explain a disease, he describes its full course, not its mildest presentation. Al-Bayyinah is doing something similar: it shows the two ends of the human range, so that everyone living somewhere between them can see which direction his life is currently facing.

This is also why the sūrah pairs so naturally with Sūrah al-Tīn, which the Qur'an places nearby in the muṣḥaf:

> *"Truly We created man in the most beautiful stature, then We cast him to the lowest of the low — save those who believe and perform righteous deeds."* (Qur'an 95:4–6)

The same double possibility appears in both places, with the same escape clause: faith plus righteous action. The human being is uniquely able to occupy either end of creation's scale precisely because he was given the responsibility of choosing — the responsibility that angels, animals, and inanimate things were never given.

**How to Read What Follows**

What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary. Each of the eight verses is treated as an independent, fully developed discourse, in strict order, exactly as the verse-by-verse method requires. Where the source commentary discusses verses 6, 7, and 8 together, they are here separated and each given complete individual treatment. Nothing has been compressed, and no verse has been shortened because another verse discussed a related theme. Each treatment draws on the Arabic vocabulary and its roots explained in plain language, cross-references quoted from the Qur'an itself, authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections named, the readings of the classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Taymiyyah and others — and the logic, psychology, and practical application that make a fourteen-hundred-year-old text intelligible to a reader living today.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:1

> **Those who disbelieve among the People of the Book and the idolaters will not desist until the clear proof comes unto them,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse the Scholars Called One of the Hardest in the Book**

It is unusual for the Qur'an's own commentators to admit difficulty openly, but with this verse they do. Several classical authorities described *lam yakuni'lladhīna kafarū min ahli'l-kitābi wa'l-mushrikīna munfakkīna ḥattā taʾtiyahumu'l-bayyinah* as among the most difficult constructions in the whole Book. The difficulty is not in its vocabulary, all of which is ordinary Arabic, but in its logic: the sentence appears at first hearing to say something surprising, even backwards. If the disbelievers "will not desist until the clear proof comes," then what happened after the clear proof did in fact come and they still did not desist?

Working through that difficulty is not an academic exercise. It opens onto one of the central themes of Islamic belief: how divine justice deals with human beings before and after they have been given knowledge. This verse, properly understood, is a compressed statement of the principle that God does not hold anyone accountable for what they were never shown.

**The Grammar of "Munfakkīn": A Bone Coming Out of Its Socket**

The pivotal word is *munfakkīn*, the active participle of the seventh form (*infakka*) of the root *f-k-k*. The primitive verb *fakka* means to loosen, to separate, to disengage, to break something free from what it is joined to. The Arabs used it for untying a knot, releasing a captive (*fakk al-asīr* — "freeing the prisoner," a phrase the Prophet ﷺ used in his instruction to feed the hungry and free the captive), and — most vividly for the philologists — for a joint that slips out of its socket, *infakkat yaduhu*, "his hand came out of joint."

That image explains the verse. The disbelievers are being described as *joined* to their condition, locked into it the way a bone is locked into its socket. They are not going to slip free of it by themselves. Something must come to dislodge them.

Modern English translations render *munfakkīn* variously as "desisting," "abandoning [their ways]," "being released," or "parting [with disbelief]." All are attempts at the same core image: attachment that will not release on its own.

There is a further grammatical subtlety. The construction *lam yakun... munfakkīna* uses the past-tense negation *lam yakun* ("they were not / they have not been") with a participle describing an ongoing state, followed by *ḥattā* ("until"), which points forward in time. Arabic grammarians noticed that this mixes a past frame with a future limit, and much of the interpretive discussion grows from how one resolves that mixture. The most straightforward reading takes the whole statement as describing a settled condition of humanity — a standing state of affairs, not a single past event — which is why many translators render it in the present or future: "will not desist."

**Reading One: They Will Not Leave Their Disbelief Until the Proof Reaches Them**

The majority of commentators — al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī and others — take the plainest sense: those who disbelieve, whether they are People of the Book or idolaters, will not abandon their disbelief until a clear proof arrives to dislodge them.

This is a statement about human psychology as much as about revelation. Inherited belief systems are not usually abandoned through internal reflection alone. They are structures of meaning that hold a person's identity, family relationships, social standing, and moral vocabulary in place. To ask someone to leave such a structure without offering anything decisive in exchange is to ask them to step off a ledge into empty air. The Qur'an repeatedly reports the pagan Arabs' own explanation of their attachment:

> *"Nay, but they say: 'Truly we found our fathers upon a creed, and we are guided by their footsteps.'"* (Qur'an 43:22)

That is *infikāk* refused: the joint will not leave the socket. And so the verse states the condition under which movement becomes possible — the arrival of *al-bayyinah*, evidence clear enough to break the lock.

On this reading the verse is not a prediction that they *will* believe once the proof comes. It states the necessary condition for change, not the guarantee of it. A locked door requires a key; supplying the key does not force anyone to walk through.

**Reading Two: They Will Not Be Left Alone — God Will Send Them the Proof**

A second reading, recorded by several commentators, understands *munfakkīn* in the passive sense of being released, abandoned, or left to themselves. On this reading the verse says: God was not going to leave these people to their own devices — He would send them the clear proof, and only then would reward and punishment properly apply.

This reading turns the verse into a statement of divine justice rather than human stubbornness, and it aligns the verse with one of the Qur'an's most consistent legal-theological principles:

> *"And never do We punish till We have sent a messenger."* (Qur'an 17:15)

And:

> *"...messengers as bearers of glad tidings and as warners, that mankind might have no argument against God after the messengers."* (Qur'an 4:165)

And in the account of Hell's own gates:

> *"Every time a group is cast therein, its keepers will ask them, 'Did no warner come unto you?' They will say, 'Yes indeed, a warner did come unto us, but we denied and said, "God has not sent down anything..."'"* (Qur'an 67:8–9)

The internal testimony of the damned in that passage is the exact answer to the question this verse raises. Nobody arrives at the Fire able to say "I was never told." The verse before us is, on this second reading, God's own guarantee of that.

**Why Both Readings Are True at Once**

Rather than treating the two readings as rivals, the tradition's better instinct is to see them as two halves of one situation. Human beings do not release their errors without a decisive intervention (reading one). God, in His justice, does not withhold that intervention before He judges (reading two). The first describes the disease; the second describes God's provision of the remedy before the diagnosis is entered into the record.

Together they produce the moral architecture of the whole sūrah: proof arrives → responsibility begins → destinies divide. Verses 6 and 7 will deliver the two verdicts. Verse 1 makes sure the reader knows those verdicts were not issued blindly.

**"Those Who Disbelieve Among the People of the Book": A Deliberate Partitive**

The Arabic says *alladhīna kafarū min ahli'l-kitāb* — "those who disbelieve *from among* the People of the Book." The particle *min* here is partitive: it carves out a portion. The verse does not say "the People of the Book disbelieved." It identifies a subset.

This precision is characteristic of the Qur'an and is easy to miss when the sūrah is read quickly. The same Book that issues severe warnings to some of the People of the Book also says explicitly:

> *"They are not all alike. Among the People of the Book is an upright community who recite God's signs in the watches of the night, while they prostrate. They believe in God and the Last Day, enjoin right and forbid wrong, and hasten unto good deeds. And they are among the righteous."* (Qur'an 3:113–114)

The commentators differ on the scope of the reference here. Most, including Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, take it to mean disbelievers among both Jews and Christians. Some restrict it more narrowly to the Jewish community of Madinah, which fits a Madinan dating and the specific historical circumstance of expectation followed by refusal. The broader reading is preferable both grammatically and thematically, since the sūrah goes on to speak of "those who were given the Book" in the plural sense of religious history at large (v. 4).

There is a further point of definition that matters greatly for how the verse is applied. What is being negated is *kufr* — active rejection of a truth once recognized — not membership in a religious community. The root *k-f-r* means to cover, to conceal, to bury; the Arabs called a farmer *kāfir* because he covers seed with soil, and the night was called *kāfir* because it covers the horizon. To be a *kāfir* in the Qur'an's usage is to cover over a truth that has become visible. That is precisely why the verse ties the judgment to the arrival of the clear proof: you cannot cover what was never uncovered to you.

**"And the Idolaters": Two Different Failures Named Side by Side**

*Al-mushrikīn* — the idolaters, from the root *sh-r-k*, to associate or make a partner — names the second group. Their failure is different in kind from the first. The People of the Book possessed a revealed text and a prophetic lineage; their error was distortion, concealment, or refusal of the next stage of that same revelation. The idolaters of Arabia possessed no scripture and no living prophetic tradition; their error was assigning to created things the devotion owed to the Creator alone, while often acknowledging the Creator Himself:

> *"And if you asked them who created the heavens and the earth, they would surely say, 'God.'"* (Qur'an 31:25)

By listing the two groups together, the verse makes a subtle theological point: possessing scripture is no automatic protection, and lacking it is no automatic excuse. Both groups are described as needing the same thing — the clear proof — and both are held to the same standard after it arrives. Religion by inheritance and religion by absence both fail at the same test, which is the willingness to accept truth when it becomes evident.

**What Is "The Clear Proof"?**

*Al-bayyinah* comes from *bayyana*, to make clear, evident, plain, manifest. Its root sense involves separation — *bayn* means "between," and to make something *bayyin* is to make it stand apart from what surrounds it so that it can be distinguished. A clear proof is not merely a strong argument; it is something that separates truth from falsehood so that the two can no longer be confused.

Three principal identifications are offered by the commentators:

The Qur'an itself. This is the reading of the majority, and it fits the Book's own self-description:

> *"Say: 'Truly I stand upon a clear proof from my Lord, and you have denied Him.'"* (Qur'an 6:57)

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ himself. On this reading the "clear proof" is a person, which is confirmed immediately by verse 2, where the proof is expounded as "a messenger from God reciting scriptures purified" — the following verse functioning grammatically as an apposition (*badal*) explaining what *al-bayyinah* is.

The phenomenon of revelation as such — every prophet and every book sent to every community. This broader reading is supported by how the Qur'an uses *bayyināt* elsewhere: Moses came with clear proofs (2:92; 7:105), Jesus came with clear proofs (2:87; 61:6), and the general statement covers all:

> *"We have indeed sent Our messengers with clear proofs."* (Qur'an 57:25)

The three readings are complementary. In the immediate historical setting the clear proof was Muhammad ﷺ reciting the Qur'an; in the universal frame it is whatever authentic revelation reaches a community through a messenger.

**Why Evidence Must Be Clear Before Judgment Is Just**

Behind this verse lies a principle that every functioning human legal system independently recognizes: no one is justly punished for violating a law he had no way of knowing. Courts distinguish between the person who knowingly ran a red light and the person for whom no signal existed. The Qur'an builds this same principle into the very structure of divine judgment, and then goes further than any human court can: God does not merely publish the law, He sends a living person to teach it, demonstrate it, answer objections to it, and model its practice.

This is why Islamic scholars developed the concept of *iqāmat al-ḥujjah*, "the establishment of the proof," as a precondition for takfīr and for accountability in the Hereafter. Ibn Taymiyyah was especially firm on this point: a person may hold an objectively erroneous belief and still not be culpable if the proof never reached him in a form he could understand — whether because of distance, distortion, the absence of an intelligible presentation, or a genuine and non-negligent misunderstanding. The Qur'an's own vocabulary supports this caution:

> *"...that you may warn a people whose fathers were not warned, so they are heedless."* (Qur'an 36:6)

The practical implication for a Muslim today is significant and often neglected. Judging the eternal fate of individuals is not our office; we cannot see who has had the proof clearly established and who has only ever encountered Islam through caricature, hostility, or the poor conduct of its adherents. Our office is the *conveying* — clearly, patiently, and in an intelligible form. The verse describes God's provision of clarity; it does not license us to assume clarity has been achieved.

**The Psychology of the Locked Joint**

The image inside *munfakkīn* has an unusually modern resonance. Contemporary psychology describes the same phenomenon in different vocabulary: confirmation bias, in which we notice evidence that supports what we already believe; cognitive dissonance, in which the discomfort of contradictory information is resolved by discrediting the information rather than revising the belief; and sunk-cost commitment, in which the more we have invested in a position, the more expensive it becomes to abandon it.

The Qur'an describes precisely these mechanisms without the terminology. It speaks of hearts sealed (2:7), of eyes that see the signs and refuse them (7:146), and of the man who was given God's signs and *slipped out of them* — using, remarkably, a similar image of disengagement:

> *"And recite unto them the account of the one to whom We gave Our signs, but he cast them off; so Satan made him his follower, and he became one of the deviant."* (Qur'an 7:175)

The lesson is not that human beings are incapable of change. It is that change requires a force from outside the closed system — new evidence, a decisive encounter, an argument the person cannot answer from within his own framework. This is exactly why God sends messengers rather than merely inscribing truths on the sky. A message that arrives through a living human being, embedded in a community, demonstrated in conduct, and open to questioning, is far harder to file away as irrelevant than an abstract proposition.

**A Practical Analogy: The Diagnosis That Precedes Treatment**

Consider a man with a serious but symptomless illness. He continues his habits — the diet, the sedentary routine, the ignored fatigue — not out of defiance but because nothing has interrupted him. He is *munfakk*: joined to his pattern, unlikely to release it on his own. Then the scan arrives with an unambiguous result. That result is his *bayyinah*.

Notice what the scan does and does not do. It does not cure him. It does not force him to change. What it does is transfer responsibility. Before the scan, his continued habits were ignorance. After it, they are a decision. If he now refuses treatment, no one can say he was treated unfairly by the consequences.

This is the exact structure of the verse. Revelation is the scan. What follows is on us.

**Living the Verse: Are You Waiting for a Proof You Have Already Received?**

There is a searching application here for believers, not only for those addressed historically. Many of us postpone change while telling ourselves we are waiting for clarity — waiting to be more convinced, waiting for a sign, waiting for the moment when religion will feel compelling enough to compel. But if the clear proof has already come, then what we are calling "waiting for clarity" may in fact be *infikāk* refused: the joint that will not leave the socket.

The Prophet ﷺ warned against the postponement that masquerades as deliberation:

> *"Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your illness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your preoccupation, and your life before your death."* (al-Ḥākim, from Ibn ʿAbbās; graded ṣaḥīḥ by the ḥadīth scholars)

The verse says the proof must come before change is expected. Its unspoken corollary, which the rest of the sūrah will make explicit, is that once the proof has come, delay is no longer neutral.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:2

> **a messenger from God reciting scriptures purified,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Proof Turns Out to Be a Person**

The previous verse ended on a suspended note: humanity will not release its errors until *the clear proof* comes. The natural question is what form that proof will take. A miracle in the sky? A voice from the clouds? A book falling into every household? The answer given here is startling in its plainness: *rasūlun mina'llāhi yatlū ṣuḥufan muṭahharah* — "a messenger from God, reciting purified pages."

Grammatically, this verse stands in apposition (*badal*) to *al-bayyinah* in verse 1. That is, it is not a new subject but a definition of the previous one. The clear proof *is* a messenger reciting purified pages. God's chosen instrument for dislodging entrenched falsehood is a human being with a text on his tongue.

This is the decisive insight of the verse, and it deserves to be dwelt upon. Every other conceivable form of proof would have failed at one of two ends. A purely miraculous display would compel astonishment without producing understanding, and the Qur'an notes that even overwhelming signs did not produce lasting faith in earlier communities (see 5:112–115; 17:59). A purely textual revelation dropped from the sky without a human bearer would leave humanity with words but no worked example of how those words become a life. God's method joins the two: an authoritative text and a living demonstration of it, in one figure.

**"Rasūl": The Word Chosen and the Word Avoided**

*Rasūl* derives from the root *r-s-l*, whose base meaning is to send forth, to release, to let flow — the same root gives *irsāl* (dispatching) and *rasal* (a group moving gently forward, as of camels). A *rasūl* is one sent with a message; the word emphasizes commission and delegated authority, not personal greatness. In Arabic usage a *rasūl* could be a messenger between tribes or a envoy carrying a letter from a king. The Qur'an takes this ordinary term and elevates it, but it never lets go of the core implication: the messenger's authority is entirely derivative. He speaks because he was sent.

The phrase *mina'llāh*, "from God," reinforces this. The commentators note that the qualification is not redundant. Human history is full of men who claimed inspiration on their own authority; the verse insists that what makes a messenger a proof is the source of his commission, not the force of his personality.

The Qur'an is careful to keep the messenger inside the human category:

> *"Say: 'I am only a human being like you; it is revealed unto me that your God is one God.'"* (Qur'an 18:110)

And this humanity is precisely the point. A proof that human beings cannot imitate is not a proof they can follow. Because the Messenger ﷺ ate, traded, married, grieved, prayed, forgave, governed, and died, everything he taught became demonstrably livable.

**Who Is the Messenger Here?**

The majority of the commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī among them — identify the messenger with Muhammad ﷺ, which is the natural reading in the immediate context of a sūrah addressing those who resisted his prophethood.

A second reading, recorded by al-Bayḍāwī, understands *rasūl* here as the Archangel Gabriel, who is himself called a noble messenger in the Qur'an:

> *"Truly it is the word of a noble messenger, possessed of power, established in rank with the Lord of the Throne, obeyed and, moreover, trustworthy."* (Qur'an 81:19–21)

On that reading, the "purified pages" would be the heavenly record from which Gabriel conveyed the revelation. A third and broader reading takes the indefinite *rasūlun* — "a messenger," not "the messenger" — as covering the whole class of divine messengers sent to every community, consistent with:

> *"And there is no community but that a warner has passed among them."* (Qur'an 35:24)

The indefiniteness of the noun in Arabic gives real weight to this third reading. Had the verse intended a single specific individual and nothing more, the definite article was available. As it stands, the phrasing allows the historical reference (Muhammad ﷺ) and the universal principle (God's consistent method) to coexist — which is exactly how the Qur'an generally handles the relation between the final revelation and the ones before it.

**"Yatlū": Reciting as an Act of Following**

The verb *yatlū* is worth pausing on. Its root *t-l-w* carries two connected meanings: to recite aloud, and to follow behind. The two senses are joined in Arabic usage — the reciter *follows* the text word after word, as one camel follows another in a line. From the same root comes *tilāwah*, the technical term for Qur'anic recitation.

This dual meaning turns the phrase into a small piece of instruction. Recitation in Islam is not mere vocalization; it is a form of following. The one who recites is walking behind the words. This is why the Qur'an praises those who recite it *as it should be recited*:

> *"Those unto whom We have given the Book recite it with true recitation (ḥaqqa tilāwatih); it is they who believe in it."* (Qur'an 2:121)

Ibn ʿAbbās and other early authorities glossed "true recitation" as following it — treating its lawful as lawful, its forbidden as forbidden, and acting upon it. A recitation that changes nothing in the reciter has, on this understanding, not fully happened.

There is also a historical observation embedded in the choice of verb. The Prophet ﷺ was famously unlettered (*ummī*), and the Qur'an makes this part of the evidence:

> *"And you did not recite before it any Book, nor did you inscribe one with your right hand; then the falsifiers would surely have doubted."* (Qur'an 29:48)

A man who did not read, reciting for over two decades a text of unmatched linguistic power, retained and transmitted by oral memory in a society of oral experts who were his opponents and could not match it — the verb *yatlū* quietly carries all of that.

**"Ṣuḥuf": Pages, Scrolls, and What They Imply**

*Ṣuḥuf* is the plural of *ṣaḥīfah*, a sheet, page, or scroll. It is the ordinary word for a written surface — the same root that gives *muṣḥaf*, the bound copy of the Qur'an, and, in modern Arabic, *ṣaḥāfah*, journalism.

Its use here matters for two reasons. First, it establishes that revelation is a *text*, something fixed, transmissible, and quotable, not a private mystical impression that cannot be checked. Religion built on unverifiable inner states cannot be argued with, cannot be inherited accurately, and cannot function as a "clear proof" for anyone but the one who experienced it. Religion built on a text can be memorized, compared, examined, and preserved.

Second, the word connects this revelation to earlier ones. The Qur'an uses *ṣuḥuf* for the scriptures of Abraham and Moses:

> *"Truly this is in the earlier scriptures — the scriptures of Abraham and Moses."* (Qur'an 87:18–19)

And it describes the honoured pages in terms strikingly close to our verse:

> *"Nay! Truly it is a reminder — so whosoever wills, let him remember it — upon pages honoured, exalted, purified, by the hands of scribes, noble and pious."* (Qur'an 80:11–16)

The Qur'an is thus presented not as an innovation but as the latest arrival in a documented series.

**"Muṭahharah": Purified — and from What?**

*Muṭahharah* is the passive participle of *ṭahhara*, to purify, from the root *ṭ-h-r* which underlies the entire Islamic vocabulary of purity: *ṭahārah* (ritual purity), *muṭahhirūn* (those who purify themselves), and the description of the water of rain as *ṭahūr* (purifying, 25:48). The passive form is significant: the pages did not purify themselves; they were purified by God.

The commentators discuss several dimensions of this purity, and each is worth naming because each answers a different objection.

*Purified from falsehood.* The content contains no error, because its source is the One who is not subject to error:

> *"Falsehood comes not upon it from before it or from behind it; a revelation from One All-Wise, Praised."* (Qur'an 41:42)

*Purified from human interference.* Unlike texts that pass through generations of editors, the Qur'an's transmission was guaranteed at the source:

> *"Truly We have sent down the Reminder, and truly We shall preserve it."* (Qur'an 15:9)

*Purified from the touch of the impure.* The Qur'an describes its heavenly archetype as touched only by the purified:

> *"...in a Book concealed. None touch it, save the purified. A revelation from the Lord of the worlds."* (Qur'an 56:78–80)

The classical jurists derived from this verse (with supporting reports) the widely followed ruling that one should be in a state of ritual purity to handle the muṣḥaf; other scholars read the verse as describing angels rather than legislating for human beings. Both readings agree on the underlying attitude: this text is handled with reverence, not casually.

*Purified from the interference of satanic forces during its descent.* This is the reading that connects the verse to Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ:

> *"The satans did not bring it down. It is not fitting for them, nor are they able. Truly they are barred from hearing."* (Qur'an 26:210–212)

**The Preserved Tablet: The Highest Reading of "Purified Pages"**

Some commentators take *ṣuḥufan muṭahharah* as a reference to the *Lawḥ Maḥfūẓ*, the Preserved Tablet, from which all revelation descends:

> *"Nay, it is a glorious Qur'an, upon a Preserved Tablet."* (Qur'an 85:21–22)

On this reading the verse describes not the physical pages in anyone's hand but the heavenly original that the Messenger's recitation faithfully conveys. This has an important implication for how Muslims understand scripture. The written copies in our homes are not the origin of the Qur'an; they are the earthly terminus of a chain that begins with God's own knowledge. What the Messenger recites is not a summary or an interpretation of a heavenly meaning; it is the very speech, sent down in Arabic, preserved above and preserved below.

**Why God's Chosen Method Was a Human Being with a Text**

Step back and consider the design. God could have chosen any mechanism for delivering His clear proof. He chose a man, publicly known, embedded in a hostile society, reciting a text over twenty-three years.

Consider what that method achieves that no alternative could.

*It makes truth verifiable.* The text can be examined, its claims tested, its language weighed. The opponents of the Prophet ﷺ were the most linguistically sophisticated audience in human history for exactly this kind of examination, and the challenge was issued directly to them:

> *"And if you are in doubt about what We have sent down upon Our servant, then bring a sūrah like it, and call your witnesses apart from God, if you are truthful."* (Qur'an 2:23)

*It makes truth imitable.* A text alone leaves interpretation entirely open. A living exemplar shows how the words become gestures, decisions, and habits. This is why the Qur'an calls the Prophet ﷺ a "beautiful example" (*uswah ḥasanah*, 33:21) and why ʿĀʾishah, asked about his character, could answer:

> *"His character was the Qur'an."* (Muslim, in the ḥadīth of Saʿd ibn Hishām; and reported similarly in Aḥmad's Musnad)

*It makes truth transmissible.* Because the proof came as a recited text and a demonstrated practice, it could be transmitted whole by communities rather than by institutions alone: memorizers preserved the words, narrators preserved the conduct, and jurists preserved the reasoning. Fourteen centuries later a child in any country can recite the same consonantal text a child recited in Madinah.

**Practical Reflection: What Does It Mean to Live Beside a Purified Text?**

If the Qur'an is *muṭahharah* — purified — then a serious question follows for anyone who reads it: what do we bring to it?

The classical scholars insisted on preparation before recitation not out of ceremonialism but out of coherence. A pure text deserves a purified approach: physical cleanliness, an intention formed before opening, a quiet place, an unhurried pace, and the seeking of refuge that God Himself commands:

> *"So when you recite the Qur'an, seek refuge in God from Satan, the outcast."* (Qur'an 16:98)

There is also a subtler purification the verse invites. Al-Ghazzālī, in his treatment of Qur'anic recitation, observed that the greatest obstacle to receiving the Book is not ignorance of Arabic but the presence of a fixed opinion the reader is determined to defend. A reader who arrives with conclusions already formed will find in the Book only the reflection of what he brought. Purified pages ask for an emptied approach — a willingness to be corrected by what one reads.

**A Closing Analogy: The Clean Instrument**

A surgeon may possess perfect knowledge and steady hands, but if the instrument is contaminated, the operation harms rather than heals. God's guidance to humanity is delivered through an instrument that has been sterilized at every stage: the source (God), the carrier (a trustworthy angel), the recipient (a Messenger protected from fabrication), and the text itself (preserved).

This is why the response demanded is not merely intellectual agreement but a kind of trust. When you know that what reaches you has been protected at every stage, reading it stops being an act of evaluation and becomes an act of reception — the posture of a patient who has finally found a physician he does not need to second-guess.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:3

> **wherein are books upright.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words That Describe the Contents of Revelation**

*Fīhā kutubun qayyimah* — "in them are upright books." The verse is only three words in Arabic, yet it completes the definition begun two verses earlier. Verse 1 named the clear proof; verse 2 identified it as a messenger reciting purified pages; verse 3 now says what those pages contain: *kutub qayyimah*, writings that are straight, sound, enduring, and self-standing.

The progression is deliberate and moves from outside to inside. First the fact of the proof, then its carrier, then its content. A skeptic might accept that a messenger exists and that his pages are protected, and still ask the decisive question: but what is actually written in them? This verse answers.

**"Kutub": Why the Plural Inside a Single Revelation?**

*Kutub* is the plural of *kitāb*, from the root *k-t-b*, to write, to inscribe, and, in a derived sense, to prescribe or make obligatory — which is why the Qur'an uses the same root for legal obligation:

> *"O you who believe, fasting has been prescribed (kutiba) for you as it was prescribed for those before you, that haply you may be reverent."* (Qur'an 2:183)

That connection is not accidental in Arabic thought. A *kitāb* is not merely a written object; it is something *settled*, fixed, decided. When the Qur'an says *kutiba ʿalaykum*, "it is written upon you," it means the matter has been determined. So *kutub* here carries the sense of settled, authoritative writings — decrees rather than opinions.

The commentators offer several explanations for the plural. Some understand *kutub* as the individual written contents — the rulings, narratives, commands, and prohibitions contained within the pages, each of which is a "writing." Others take it as a reference to the earlier revealed books whose essential content the Qur'an confirms and preserves, since the Qur'an describes itself as a confirmer of what came before:

> *"And We have sent down unto you the Book in truth, confirming the Book that came before it and as a protector over it."* (Qur'an 5:48)

On this second reading, the purified pages contain, in essence, everything the previous scriptures truly contained. The Qur'an is not a competing volume in a library of scriptures; it is the authoritative edition that retains what was sound and removes what human hands inserted.

**"Qayyimah": The Word That Runs Through This Sūrah**

*Qayyimah* is the key term. It derives from the root *q-w-m*, to stand, to rise, to be upright — one of the most productive roots in the Qur'an. From it come *qāma* (to stand), *iqāmah* (establishing, as in *iqāmat al-ṣalāh*, establishing the prayer), *mustaqīm* (straight, as in *al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*, the straight path), *qayyim* (upright, correct, valuable), *qiyāmah* (the Standing, the Resurrection), and the divine Name *al-Qayyūm* (the Self-Subsisting, the One who stands by Himself and upholds all else).

Notice how the sūrah itself uses the root twice: here, *kutubun qayyimah*, "upright books," and again in verse 5, *dhālika dīnu'l-qayyimah*, "that is the upright religion." The books are *qayyimah* and the religion they teach is *qayyimah*. The straightness of the text produces the straightness of the way of life. This is not a rhetorical flourish; it is an argument. If you want to know whether a religion is upright, examine the text it stands on.

The lexicographers give *qayyim* a cluster of meanings that all matter here:

*Straight, without deviation.* This is the sense most commentators emphasize, and the Qur'an makes the same claim about itself explicitly at the opening of Sūrah al-Kahf:

> *"Praise be to God, who sent down the Book upon His servant, and placed no crookedness therein — [a Book] upright (qayyiman), to warn of a severe punishment from Him."* (Qur'an 18:1–2)

The word *ʿiwaj*, "crookedness," which that verse negates, is the exact opposite of *qayyim*. Crookedness in a text means inconsistency, contradiction, hidden agendas, statements bent toward the interests of a class or a party. The claim of *qayyim* is that a reader can follow any line of the Book to its end and it will not curve away from truth.

*Standing on its own, self-supporting.* A *qayyim* structure does not need external props. Applied to scripture, this means the Book carries its own authority; it does not depend for its truth on a priesthood's endorsement, a state's enforcement, or a philosophical system's approval. The Qur'an argues for itself, challenges its readers directly, and invites verification.

*Upholding and setting others straight.* Because *qayyim* is related to *qawwām* (one who maintains, sustains, manages), the word also implies an active function: these books do not merely stand, they make other things stand. A community, a marriage, a market, a legal system — all of these are set upright by an upright text.

*Valuable, enduring, of lasting worth.* An *amr qayyim* in classical usage is a matter of enduring value, not a passing fashion.

**Free From the Crookedness That Human Systems Develop**

Ibn Kathīr's explanation of *qayyimah* — that these books are free from error and mistake because they are from God — is not simply a devotional assertion. It rests on a coherent argument about origins.

Every purely human moral system, however well-intentioned, encodes the limits of its authors: the interests of their class, the assumptions of their era, the blind spots of their culture. This is visible in retrospect in every civilization. Laws that were considered self-evidently just in one century become obviously unjust in the next, not because morality changed but because the framers could not see past their own position. The bending of a system toward the interests of those who write it is what the Qur'an calls *ʿiwaj*, crookedness.

A text that comes from outside the human situation entirely — from the One who has no interests to protect, no class to belong to, no era to be limited by, and no need of anyone — is the only kind of text that could plausibly be *qayyim* in the strong sense. This is why the Qur'an ties the absence of contradiction directly to the question of authorship:

> *"Do they not consider the Qur'an? Had it been from other than God, they would surely have found much contradiction therein."* (Qur'an 4:82)

The verse issues an invitation, not a prohibition: examine it. Internal coherence across twenty-three years of revelation, delivered in fragments, in radically changing circumstances — persecution, migration, war, treaty, governance, victory — without a single retraction of principle, is precisely the sort of thing that becomes harder to fake the longer the process runs.

**Honoured Pages, Noble Scribes**

The commentators, including al-Qurṭubī, connect this verse with the description in Sūrah ʿAbasa, and the connection illuminates the physical and spiritual dignity of revelation:

> *"Nay! Truly it is a reminder — so whosoever wills, let him remember it — upon pages honoured, exalted, purified, by the hands of scribes, noble and pious."* (Qur'an 80:11–16)

Three qualities are stacked there: *mukarramah* (honoured), *marfūʿah* (exalted, raised in rank), and *muṭahharah* (purified) — the last being the very word used in the previous verse of our sūrah. And the handlers are named: *safarah*, scribes or emissaries, described as noble and pious.

The parallel with 85:21–22 completes the picture:

> *"Nay, it is a glorious Qur'an, upon a Preserved Tablet."* (Qur'an 85:21–22)

Put together, the Qur'an describes its own status at four levels: preserved in the divine knowledge (the Preserved Tablet), carried by noble angelic scribes, recited by a trustworthy Messenger, and written on pages that are handled with reverence by the community. At every stage the emphasis is the same — nothing crooked enters the chain.

**The Practical Test of an Upright Book**

There is a way to test the claim of *qayyim* that requires no scholarship at all, and the sūrah itself supplies it two verses later. If the books are upright, then what they command should be upright — and verse 5 will summarize their entire demand as: worship God sincerely, incline away from false objects of devotion, establish the prayer, give the alms.

Examine that content for crookedness. Does it privilege a tribe? No — it commands worship of the universal Creator. Does it enrich a priesthood? No — it commands charity flowing to the poor and needy. Does it flatter the powerful? No — the prayer places the ruler and the labourer shoulder to shoulder, foreheads on the same ground, five times daily. Does it exploit the vulnerable? No — the alms is a transfer of wealth from those who have to those who do not, framed as a right of the poor rather than a favour of the rich.

A crooked text, written to serve someone's interest, would look different, and history offers abundant examples of religious writings that were adjusted precisely to serve such interests. The Qur'an's own warning about that adjustment is severe:

> *"So woe unto those who write the Book with their hands, then say, 'This is from God,' that they may sell it for a paltry price. Woe unto them for what their hands have written, and woe unto them for what they earn."* (Qur'an 2:79)

The phrase "for a paltry price" identifies the motive that bends texts: the writer wanted something. The claim of *qayyim* is that no such motive touched these pages.

**Straightness as a Standard for the Self**

The root *q-w-m* is not reserved for texts. The Qur'an uses it for people, and this is where the verse becomes personal. The believer is commanded:

> *"O you who believe! Be upholders of justice (qawwāmīna bi'l-qisṭ), witnesses for God, even if it be against yourselves, or your parents and kinsfolk."* (Qur'an 4:135)

*Qawwāmīn* is from the same root as *qayyimah*. The upright book produces the upright person, and the mark of that uprightness is testing it against self-interest: justice even against yourself, even against your family. A person is *qayyim* to the exact degree that his positions do not bend toward his advantage.

And the most demanding statement of this standard is the command God gave the Prophet ﷺ himself:

> *"So remain upright (fa'staqim) as you are commanded, you and those who repent with you, and transgress not."* (Qur'an 11:112)

It is reported that the Prophet ﷺ said of this verse and its like that it had aged him. Al-Tirmidhī records:

> *"Sūrah Hūd and its sisters have turned my hair grey."* (al-Tirmidhī, from Abū Bakr; graded ḥasan)

The commentators explain that the weight lay in the word *fa'staqim* — remain straight. A book that is straight demands a life that is straight, and holding a line without deviation over a lifetime is heavier than any single act of devotion.

**A Practical Analogy: The Reference Instrument**

Engineers and surveyors work with reference standards — a level, a plumb line, a calibrated master gauge kept in controlled conditions and used to check everything else. What makes such an instrument useful is not that it is unusually clever but that it is *known not to have drifted*. If the reference itself bends, every measurement made with it is quietly wrong, and the error compounds through the whole structure.

*Kutubun qayyimah* is the claim that revelation is that reference. The reason it matters is practical: a community that measures its ethics against its own current opinion has no way to detect when its opinion has drifted, because the ruler and the object being measured bend together. A fixed reference makes correction possible. It allows a person to discover that he is wrong — which is the one discovery that self-referential moral systems can never reliably produce.

**Reading With the Grain of an Upright Text**

The final practical implication concerns how we read. If the Book is straight, then when a reader finds an apparent crookedness in it — a verse that seems harsh, a ruling that seems strange, a passage that seems to sit awkwardly with another — the disciplined response is to suspect one's own understanding before suspecting the text. This is not intellectual cowardice; it is the ordinary procedure of any serious learner. A student who finds an apparent error in a well-established proof first checks his own working.

The classical scholars institutionalized this as a discipline: study the language, learn the context of revelation, consult those who came before, compare the verse with its parallels elsewhere in the Book, and hold judgment where knowledge is incomplete. The result is the vast literature of tafsīr, which is at bottom fourteen centuries of readers assuming the Book is straight and working patiently until their own understanding straightened out to match it.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:4

> **Those who were given the Book did not become divided until after the clear proof had come unto them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Diagnoses Religious Division**

This verse makes a claim that sounds counterintuitive until it is examined, and then becomes one of the most penetrating observations in the Qur'an about religious communities. *Wa mā tafarraqa'lladhīna ūtu'l-kitāba illā min baʿdi mā jāʾathumu'l-bayyinah* — "And those who were given the Book did not become divided except after the clear proof had come unto them."

The expected sequence would be the opposite. We assume that people divide because they lack information, and that when the truth finally arrives, the disputes end. This verse states the reverse: division came *after* clarity, not before it. The Qur'an makes the same point elsewhere with an added diagnosis of the cause:

> *"And We gave them clear proofs from the Command. And they differed not till after knowledge had come unto them, out of envy among themselves."* (Qur'an 45:17)

And again:

> *"Mankind was one community; then God sent the prophets as bearers of glad tidings and as warners, and He sent down with them the Book in truth, to judge among mankind concerning that wherein they differed. And none differed concerning it, save those who were given it, after clear proofs had come to them, out of envy among themselves."* (Qur'an 2:213)

The repeated phrase is decisive: *baghyan baynahum*, "out of envy" or "out of mutual transgression." The Qur'an is not saying that revelation causes division. It is saying that revelation *exposes* a division that was already present in human hearts but had no occasion to surface.

**"Tafarraqa": Splitting Into Parties**

The verb *tafarraqa* comes from the root *f-r-q*, to separate, to split, to distinguish. The same root gives *furqān* (the criterion that separates truth from falsehood, a name of the Qur'an), *firqah* (a sect or party), *farīq* (a group or faction), and *tafriqah* (causing division).

The fifth-form verb *tafarraqa* implies a process, something that happens gradually and to a body that was previously one. It is not a description of two groups who never had anything in common; it is the description of a single community coming apart into factions. This matters, because the verse is not talking about the natural diversity of nations. It is talking about internal fracture within a people who received one message.

The Qur'an consistently forbids this fracture:

> *"And hold fast to God's rope, all together, and be not divided (wa lā tafarraqū). And remember God's Blessing upon you, when you were enemies and He joined your hearts, such that you became brothers by His Blessing."* (Qur'an 3:103)

And it warns against imitating the pattern this verse describes:

> *"And be not like those who became divided and differed after the clear proofs had come unto them; it is they who shall have a great punishment."* (Qur'an 3:105)

That verse is essentially a command not to repeat the history that al-Bayyinah 98:4 records. The pattern is presented as a known, documented failure mode of religious communities, and the Muslim community is told explicitly to avoid it — which strongly implies that it is capable of falling into it.

**Why Clarity Provokes Division: The Logic of the Claim**

The mechanism the verse describes is worth reconstructing carefully, because it is not obvious.

Before a clear proof arrives, disagreement is cheap. Everyone holds vague positions, no one can be definitively shown to be wrong, and the community coexists in a kind of comfortable haze. There is no cost to error, because error cannot be identified. People may be nominally united simply because nothing has forced them to take a position.

When a clear proof arrives, this changes instantly. Now positions can be tested. Now some people are demonstrably right and others demonstrably wrong. Now a scholar who built his reputation on a doctrine must publicly abandon it or defend it. Now a family with generations of investment in a practice must choose. Now a class whose income depends on a religious arrangement discovers that the arrangement has no basis.

Clarity, in other words, imposes costs — and it is the unwillingness to pay those costs, not the absence of light, that produces sects.

This is precisely what the Qur'an means by *baghyan baynahum*. The root *b-gh-y* covers envy, rivalry, and overstepping bounds. The division was not intellectual but moral. People did not misunderstand; they resented. They resented that the truth came through someone else, or that it required them to yield, or that accepting it meant losing status.

**The Historical Case the Verse Points To**

The commentators identify a specific historical application. Al-Rāzī and others note that among the People of the Book there were those who awaited a coming prophet, who described him, who used the expectation as a source of authority — and who then rejected him when he arrived through a lineage they did not expect. The Qur'an describes this with painful precision:

> *"And when there came to them a Book from God, confirming that which they had with them — and aforetime they used to ask for victory over those who disbelieve — so when there came to them that which they recognized, they disbelieved in it. So may the curse of God be upon the disbelievers."* (Qur'an 2:89)

The phrase *mā ʿarafū*, "that which they recognized," is the sharpest word in the passage. It was not unfamiliarity that produced the rejection. Recognition and rejection occurred together, which is the definition of *kufr* in its root sense of covering something over.

And the Qur'an describes the intensity of that recognition elsewhere:

> *"Those unto whom We have given the Book recognize him as they recognize their own children. Yet truly a group among them conceal the truth, knowing."* (Qur'an 2:146)

The analogy of recognizing one's own children is not chosen casually. It describes a recognition that is immediate, certain, and impossible to be mistaken about.

**A Second Reading: The Corruption of Revelation Into Sects**

Ibn Kathīr and others read the verse more broadly as a reference to the fragmentation of earlier religious communities into competing sects (*firaq*) after receiving a unified revelation. The Qur'an notes this repeatedly:

> *"And had your Lord willed, He would have made mankind one community; but they cease not to differ."* (Qur'an 11:118)

> *"Truly this Qur'an recounts to the Children of Israel most of that concerning which they differ."* (Qur'an 27:76)

> *"And they did not become divided until after knowledge had come unto them, out of envy among themselves."* (Qur'an 42:14, with similar wording at 41:45 and 45:17)

In this connection the commentators cite the well-known ḥadīth of the seventy-three sects:

> *"Verily, the Jews divided into seventy-one sects. And verily, the Christians divided into seventy-two sects. And this community will divide into seventy-three sects, all of them in the Fire except one."* They said: *"Who are they, O Messenger of God?"* He said: *"[Those who follow] what I and my Companions are upon."* (Recorded by Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī, Ibn Mājah and others; the ḥadīth is cited by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī in their commentary on this verse)

Several careful points must be made about this report, because it is frequently misused. First, its transmission has been discussed at length by ḥadīth scholars; the core of it is widely accepted, while some wordings are weaker than others. Second, and more importantly, the scholars of the tradition warned against the use to which sectarians immediately put it — namely, as a weapon by which every faction declares itself the saved one and everyone else damned. Al-Shāṭibī, among others, observed that this ḥadīth was revealed as a warning to keep the community from splitting, not as a licence for splitting further by excommunicating rivals.

Third, the Prophet's own answer defines the saved group not by a party name but by an allegiance: what he and his Companions were upon. That is a standard of practice and creed, not a label. Anyone who claims the label while abandoning the standard has not been included by the ḥadīth; anyone who holds to the standard is included regardless of what he is called.

**"After Clarity" as a Recurring Human Pattern**

The verse's diagnosis is not confined to religion, which is one reason it remains so illuminating. The same structure appears wherever knowledge collides with interest.

Consider a workplace where an audit reveals a clear irregularity. Before the audit, everyone worked side by side. After it, factions form instantly — those who want the finding acted upon, those who want it buried, those who attack the auditor's motives, those who quietly change the subject. The audit did not create the conflict; it revealed which people were prepared to pay the cost of truth.

Consider a family where a will is read. Before the reading, the family is one. After it, positions harden. The document did not manufacture the greed.

Consider a field of science in which a decisive experiment settles a long-running question. The result does not always produce agreement; it often produces entrenchment among those whose careers were built on the losing side, and the sociologists of science have documented how often a paradigm changes only when its defenders retire.

In every case the pattern is the same: clarity is not neutral. It forces a choice, and choices reveal character. This is exactly why the Qur'an ties division to *baghy* — envy and overreach — rather than to ignorance.

**What This Means for Divine Justice**

There is an important theological consequence here, and it links this verse back to verse 1. If people divided only *after* the clear proof came, then their division cannot be blamed on God's failure to guide. The evidence was supplied. The fracture came from what people did with it.

This preserves the Qur'an's consistent teaching on human responsibility. God guides those who seek guidance and leaves in error those who prefer it:

> *"Thereby He misleads many and guides many; and He misleads none but the iniquitous."* (Qur'an 2:26)

The last clause is the key: the misguidance follows the iniquity; it does not precede it. Similarly here, the division follows the arrival of the proof, and its cause is named as envy, not as any deficiency in the proof itself.

**Guarding Against the Same Failure Today**

The severity of the Qur'an's language about earlier communities is not a license for complacency. Verse 3:105 explicitly warns Muslims not to become like them, which means the pattern is available to us.

Several practical safeguards follow from the verse's own diagnosis.

*Distinguish disagreement from division.* The Companions differed on many questions of interpretation and law without fracturing into hostile parties. The Qur'an condemns *tafarruq*, not *ikhtilāf*. The classical schools of law disagreed on thousands of rulings while praying behind one another and marrying into one another's families. Division occurs when disagreement becomes an identity to defend rather than a question to investigate.

*Watch for the interest behind the position.* Since the Qur'an names envy as the engine, the most useful question in any religious dispute is not "who is right?" but, first, "what would I lose if I were wrong?" A person who cannot answer that question honestly is not in a position to reason clearly.

*Return contested matters to the sources.* The Qur'an prescribes the procedure directly:

> *"O you who believe! Obey God and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. And if you differ in anything, refer it to God and the Messenger, if you believe in God and the Last Day. That is better and fairer in outcome."* (Qur'an 4:59)

*Hold to the community.* The Prophet ﷺ tied divine help to cohesion:

> *"God's hand is with the congregation."* (al-Tirmidhī, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

And he described the believers with an image that makes fracture unthinkable:

> *"The believers, in their mutual love, mercy, and compassion, are like a single body: when one limb suffers, the whole body responds with sleeplessness and fever."* (al-Bukhārī 6011; Muslim 2586)

**A Sobering Conclusion**

The most uncomfortable implication of this verse is that receiving the truth does not guarantee benefiting from it. The communities described here had the Book. They had the clear proof. And the very thing that should have united them became the occasion of their fragmentation, because they brought to it hearts that wanted something other than the truth.

The corrective is stated in the next verse, and the placement is exact. Having diagnosed the disease of division, the sūrah immediately prescribes the cure: *ikhlāṣ*, sincerity — worshipping God with the religion made entirely His. Envy divides because envy is about position. Sincerity unites because sincerity has no position to defend.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:5

> **They were not commanded but to worship God, devoting religion entirely to Him, as ḥanīfs, and to perform the prayer, and to give the alms — that is the upright religion.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whole of Religion in One Sentence**

After diagnosing the fracture of earlier communities, the sūrah turns to what those communities were actually asked to do — and the answer is remarkable for its brevity. *Wa mā umirū illā li-yaʿbudu'llāha mukhliṣīna lahu'l-dīna ḥunafāʾa wa yuqīmu'l-ṣalāta wa yuʾtu'l-zakāta wa dhālika dīnu'l-qayyimah.* Four requirements, one summary judgment.

The construction is an exclusive one: *mā... illā*, "they were not commanded except to..." This is Arabic's strongest way of restricting a statement. It does not merely say that this is what they were commanded; it says that this and nothing else was the substance of the command. Whatever additions, complications, ceremonies, hierarchies, exclusions, and doctrinal elaborations later accumulated, the original instruction was this short.

Al-Ṭabarī draws the natural inference: the exclusivity implies a reproach. If they were commanded only this, and yet they divided into sects, then their divisions arose from things they were never commanded at all. They added objectives and practices to a religion that had been given to them complete and simple, and the additions became the ground on which they fought.

Al-Zamakhsharī and others note a further implication: the verse is also a defence of the new revelation against the charge of novelty. The Prophet ﷺ was accused of bringing something unprecedented. The verse answers that he brought nothing but what every prophet brought. The core has never changed.

**"Li-yaʿbudū Allāh": The Meaning of Worship**

The first and governing requirement is *li-yaʿbudū Allāh* — that they worship God. The verb comes from *ʿ-b-d*, whose noun *ʿabd* means a servant or slave, and whose derived noun *ʿibādah* is the general Qur'anic term for worship.

The root's literal field is instructive. The Arabs described a path smoothed and made pliant by the feet of travellers as *ṭarīq muʿabbad* — a "worn," yielding road. The connection to worship is exactly that: an *ʿabd* is one whose resistance has been worn down before his Master, one who has become pliant to the divine will rather than rigid before it.

Worship in the Qur'an is therefore far broader than ritual. Ibn Taymiyyah's widely quoted definition captures the classical understanding:

> *"Worship is a comprehensive term for everything God loves and is pleased with, of words and deeds, inward and outward."*

That definition includes prayer and fasting, but also honesty in trade, kindness to parents, restraining anger, removing harm from a path, and providing for one's family. The Qur'an itself states the purpose of human existence in these terms:

> *"And I did not create jinn and mankind except to worship Me."* (Qur'an 51:56)

Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have glossed *li-yaʿbudūn* in that verse as *li-yaʿrifūn*, "that they may know Me" — worship being the practical form that knowledge of God takes.

The logic of the priority is worth stating plainly. Every human being serves something. The one who does not serve God serves his appetite, his reputation, his career, his fear, or another human being's approval. The Qur'an describes this substitution directly:

> *"Have you seen the one who takes his caprice as his god?"* (Qur'an 25:43)

The question religion asks is therefore not whether you will serve, but *whom*. And there is a psychological asymmetry in the answer: every created master is finite, changeable, and eventually unable to reward you, while the One who is *al-Ṣamad* — the eternally sought-after upon whom all depend — never runs out. Servitude to God is, paradoxically, the only servitude that produces freedom, because it releases a person from every other claim.

**"Mukhliṣīna lahu'l-dīn": Sincerity as the Refining of Ore**

*Mukhliṣīn* is the active participle of *akhlaṣa*, from the root *kh-l-ṣ*, whose base meaning is to be pure, clear, unmixed, free of adulteration. The Arabs used *khāliṣ* for milk that had been strained clean of any admixture, and for gold refined of its ore. *Khallaṣa* means to rescue or extract something from what is entangling it.

So *ikhlāṣ* — sincerity — is not primarily a warm feeling. It is a process of extraction: pulling the act of worship free from every motive that is not God.

This is why the Qur'an makes it the condition of acceptance:

> *"So worship God, devoting religion entirely to Him. Truly, sincere religion belongs to God alone."* (Qur'an 39:2–3)

And why the Prophet ﷺ made intention the measure of every deed:

> *"Deeds are only by intentions, and every man shall have only what he intended. So whoever's migration was for God and His Messenger, his migration was for God and His Messenger; and whoever's migration was for some worldly gain or a woman he would marry, his migration was for that for which he migrated."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907)

The scholars placed this ḥadīth at the head of their collections deliberately. It establishes that the visible form of an act does not determine its value. Two men perform the identical prayer; one is worshipping God, the other is maintaining a reputation. The bodies are indistinguishable and the outcomes are opposite.

The most severe warning on this point concerns the first three cases judged on the Day of Resurrection — a martyr, a scholar and reciter, and a generous philanthropist — each of whom is told that he did what he did so that people would call him brave, learned, or generous, and that it was said. Muslim records the ending:

> *"Then he was dragged upon his face until he was cast into the Fire."* (Muslim 1905, from Abū Hurayrah)

That report is disturbing by design. The three men performed the most admired deeds in any society, and their deeds were nullified by a hidden motive. The lesson is not that public good works are suspect, but that *ikhlāṣ* is the difference between an act that ascends and an act that collapses.

The Prophet ﷺ also named the subtler danger:

> *"The thing I fear most for you is the lesser idolatry."* They asked: *"And what is the lesser idolatry, O Messenger of God?"* He said: *"Showing off (al-riyāʾ)."* (Aḥmad, Musnad, from Maḥmūd ibn Labīd; graded good by the ḥadīth scholars)

The commentators note two dimensions of *ikhlāṣ* in this verse: purifying the *religion* for God — meaning that one's belief, practice, and loyalty admit no partner — and purifying the *self*, meaning that one's inner motives are cleaned out. The two are connected: idolatry in creed is the visible form of a heart divided between competing claims.

**"Ḥunafāʾ": The Word That Means Leaning Away**

*Ḥunafāʾ* is the plural of *ḥanīf*, from the verb *ḥanifa*, meaning to incline or lean. In pre-Islamic Arabic the related word *aḥnaf* described someone whose feet turned inward — a physical leaning.

The Qur'an takes this image of inclination and gives it a spiritual direction: a *ḥanīf* is one who leans away from idolatry and toward pure monotheism. The word implies motion and orientation rather than a static label. It describes someone facing a particular way.

The Qur'an's paradigm of the *ḥanīf* is Abraham:

> *"Abraham was not a Jew, nor a Christian; rather he was a ḥanīf, one who submits (muslim), and he was not of the idolaters."* (Qur'an 3:67)

> *"Say: 'Nay, [ours is] the creed of Abraham, a ḥanīf, and he was not of the idolaters.'"* (Qur'an 2:135)

The choice of Abraham is a masterstroke of argument in the Madinan context. Jews and Christians both claimed him; the idolaters of Makkah claimed descent from him and custodianship of the house he built. The Qur'an's response is to point out the obvious: Abraham preceded all of the later formations, and what he had was not a sectarian identity but an orientation — away from everything false, toward the One.

The Qur'an ties this orientation to human nature itself:

> *"So set your face toward the religion as a ḥanīf — the primordial nature (fiṭrah) of God upon which He originated mankind. There is no altering the creation of God. That is the upright religion (al-dīn al-qayyim), but most of mankind know not."* (Qur'an 30:30)

Notice that this verse contains both key terms of our sūrah: *ḥanīf* and *al-dīn al-qayyim*. The two passages interpret each other. The upright religion is not an external imposition on human nature; it is the recovery of an original inclination.

The Prophet ﷺ described that original state:

> *"Every child is born upon the fiṭrah; it is his parents who make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian."* (al-Bukhārī 1358; Muslim 2658)

And in a ḥadīth qudsī recorded by Muslim:

> *"I created My servants ḥunafāʾ, all of them; then the devils came to them and turned them away from their religion."* (Muslim 2865, from ʿIyāḍ ibn Ḥimār)

This gives the word a hopeful edge that is easy to miss. To become a *ḥanīf* is not to acquire something foreign but to return to something native. Conversion, in this frame, is homecoming.

**"Yuqīmu'l-Ṣalāh": Establishing, Not Merely Performing**

The third requirement is *wa yuqīmu'l-ṣalāh*. The verb is *aqāma*, from the root *q-w-m* that runs through this entire sūrah — the same root as *qayyimah*. It means to make something stand, to set it upright, to establish it firmly.

The Qur'an almost never says "do the prayer" or "perform the prayer." It says *establish* it. The grammatical choice is a whole theology of worship. To establish something is to give it a fixed place in the structure of a life: at set times, with its conditions met, maintained through inconvenience, and standing upright rather than leaning.

*Ṣalāh* itself comes from a root associated with connection and with supplication; the Qur'an uses the same root for God's own *ṣalāh* upon the believers, meaning His blessing and mercy (33:43). The prayer is thus, at root, a link.

The Prophet ﷺ described its function with an image any city-dweller understands:

> *"Tell me: if there were a river at the door of one of you in which he bathed five times a day, would any dirt remain on him?"* They said: *"No dirt would remain on him at all."* He said: *"That is the likeness of the five prayers: God wipes away sins with them."* (al-Bukhārī 528; Muslim 667)

He also named it the first item on the ledger:

> *"The first thing for which a servant will be held accountable on the Day of Resurrection is his prayer. If it is sound, he has succeeded and prospered; if it is corrupt, he has failed and lost."* (al-Tirmidhī, al-Nasāʾī, from Abū Hurayrah; graded authentic)

And he identified the pillar it forms:

> *"The head of the matter is Islam, its pillar is the prayer, and the summit of its hump is jihād in the way of God."* (al-Tirmidhī, from Muʿādh ibn Jabal; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The Qur'an explains the prayer's psychological work:

> *"Truly the prayer prevents indecency and wrong."* (Qur'an 29:45)

This is a claim that can be tested against ordinary human experience. Habits are formed by repetition at fixed intervals; character is formed by habits. A person who interrupts his day five times to stand before God is repeatedly re-anchored in a frame that makes wrongdoing harder. It is difficult to sustain a life of exploitation when, several times daily, you must place your forehead on the ground before the One who sees the exploitation. Where the prayer does not prevent wrongdoing, the classical scholars said, it is the establishment (*iqāmah*) that is defective, not the promise.

Notice, finally, why the prayer appears in a verse describing what *all* communities were commanded. Prayer is not an Islamic innovation. Abraham prays for it to be established in his descendants (14:40); Ishmael commands his people to it (19:55); Jesus in the cradle says God enjoined it upon him (19:31); Moses is commanded to establish it (20:14); and the Children of Israel were charged with it (2:83).

**"Yuʾtu'l-Zakāh": Charity as Purification of Wealth**

The fourth requirement is *wa yuʾtu'l-zakāh*. The root *z-k-w* carries two joined meanings: purification and growth. The Arabs used *zakā al-zarʿ* for a crop that flourished. The Qur'an uses the root for the purification of the soul:

> *"He has prospered who purifies it (zakkāhā), and he has failed who buries it."* (Qur'an 91:9–10)

The dual meaning is the whole doctrine. Giving away a portion of wealth purifies both the wealth and the giver, and — counterintuitively — causes increase rather than loss:

> *"Take alms from their wealth to cleanse them and purify them thereby."* (Qur'an 9:103)

> *"Satan promises you poverty and enjoins indecency upon you, while God promises you forgiveness from Him, and bounty."* (Qur'an 2:268)

> *"The likeness of those who spend their wealth in the way of God is that of a grain that grows seven ears, in every ear a hundred grains."* (Qur'an 2:261)

And the Prophet ﷺ stated the paradox as a rule:

> *"Charity does not decrease wealth; God increases a servant in honour when he pardons; and no one humbles himself for God's sake but that God raises him."* (Muslim 2588, from Abū Hurayrah)

The commentators, including Ibn Kathīr, note that *zakāh* in this verse — especially if the sūrah is Makkan, revealed before the formal rates and thresholds of Madinan law were fixed — should be understood in its broad sense: every act of generosity toward the poor and needy. Like prayer, the alms was enjoined on earlier communities: the Children of Israel (2:83), Ishmael (19:55), Jesus (19:31), and the prophets generally (21:73).

**Why These Two Acts and No Others?**

The most instructive question about this verse is why, out of all the practices of religion, *these two* were named alongside sincere monotheism.

The answer becomes clear when you see what each one costs. Prayer costs *time* — the most non-renewable resource a person has, and the one that competes most directly with ambition. Alms costs *wealth* — the resource that most directly represents security, status, and control. Between them, they claim the two things human beings most instinctively hoard.

They also cover the two directions of religious life. Prayer is the vertical axis, the servant's relationship with his Lord. Alms is the horizontal axis, the servant's relationship with other people. Any religion that has only the vertical becomes a private mysticism indifferent to the hungry; any religion that has only the horizontal becomes social work that has forgotten God. The Qur'an refuses to separate them, pairing the two more than two dozen times.

There is a further point. Both are *measurable*. A person can claim any inward state he likes, but whether he prayed at dawn and whether he gave from his wealth are matters of fact. Islam anchors sincerity — which is invisible — to acts that are visible, so that the claim of faith is always accompanied by evidence.

**"Dhālika Dīnu'l-Qayyimah": The Verdict**

The verse closes with a summary: *and that is the upright religion*. The construction *dīnu'l-qayyimah* has been analysed in several ways by the grammarians — most simply as the religion of the upright community, or the religion of the upright books mentioned in verse 3, with the noun understood.

Either way, the sūrah's key root returns. The books were *qayyimah* (v. 3); the religion is *al-qayyimah* (v. 5). Straight text, straight path. And the demonstrative *dhālika*, "that," points backward at the four items just listed, as if drawing a line beneath them and writing: this — no more, no less — is the religion that stands upright.

**The Simplicity That Was Lost, and the Warning It Carries**

Al-Ṭabarī's reading — that earlier communities complicated what had been given to them simply — is not an accusation to be aimed comfortably outward. Every religious community drifts in the same direction, and the mechanism is well understood: secondary matters become identity markers, identity markers become boundaries, boundaries become the thing people defend most fiercely, and eventually a person can be judged more by his allegiance to the boundary than by his sincerity toward God.

The Prophet ﷺ warned about this pressure repeatedly:

> *"Religion is ease, and no one makes the religion hard upon himself except that it overcomes him."* (al-Bukhārī 39)

> *"Beware of extremism in religion, for those before you were destroyed only by extremism in religion."* (al-Nasāʾī, Ibn Mājah, from Ibn ʿAbbās; graded authentic)

The corrective this verse offers is a kind of periodic audit. Ask of any religious energy you are expending: does it trace back to one of these four — sincere worship of God, orientation away from false objects of devotion, established prayer, and giving to those in need? If it does, it is the upright religion. If it does not, it may still be permissible, but it is not the thing that was commanded, and it should never be allowed to displace the thing that was.

**A Practical Analogy: The Load-Bearing Walls**

Anyone who has renovated an old building learns to distinguish load-bearing walls from partitions. Partitions can be moved, redesigned, painted, or removed to suit a family's taste. Load-bearing walls hold up the roof. Confusing the two is how buildings collapse — either by removing a wall that was holding everything up, or by refusing to move a partition because it has always been there.

Verse 5 is the Qur'an marking the load-bearing walls of religion: sincere worship, monotheistic orientation, established prayer, obligatory giving. The vast body of Islamic devotional and legal life builds upon these, and much of it is genuinely valuable. But when a community forgets which walls are structural, two failures follow at once: people begin removing what holds the roof up, and they begin dying to defend a partition.

The sūrah's placement of this verse immediately after the verse on division is therefore exact. Communities fractured because they forgot how short the original command was.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:6

> **Truly the disbelievers among the People of the Book and the idolaters are in the Fire of Hell, abiding therein; it is they who are the worst of creation.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict After the Evidence**

The sūrah has been building a case with the discipline of a legal argument. The proof was necessary (v. 1); the proof arrived in the form of a Messenger with purified pages (v. 2); the contents were upright (v. 3); division followed clarity, not ignorance (v. 4); the actual demand was simple and unchanged across all revelations (v. 5). Only now, with every premise established, does the sūrah pronounce sentence.

This ordering matters enormously for how the verse should be read. It is not an outburst of hostility toward outsiders. It is the conclusion of an argument in which the conditions of fairness were laid out first. Nobody in this verse is condemned for being born into the wrong community; the subject throughout is *alladhīna kafarū* — those who *actively rejected* — and verse 1 established that this rejection is only meaningful after the clear proof has reached them.

**The Repetition That Frames the Sūrah**

The opening phrase of this verse repeats the opening phrase of verse 1 almost word for word: *inna'lladhīna kafarū min ahli'l-kitābi wa'l-mushrikīn* — "truly those who disbelieve among the People of the Book and the idolaters."

Classical rhetoricians called this device *radd al-ʿajuz ʿalā al-ṣadr*, the return of the end to the beginning. The effect is to close a circle. The sūrah opened by saying that these groups would not release their position until the proof came. It now says what happened when it came: they still refused. The same subject appears in both frames — before the evidence, and after the judgment — so the reader can see the entire arc of their case in a single chapter.

Note also the shift in particle. Verse 1 began with the negation *lam yakun*. Verse 6 begins with *inna*, the particle of emphasis and certainty, usually rendered "truly" or "indeed." The grammar itself moves from suspense to settlement.

**"Fī Nāri Jahannama Khālidīna Fīhā": The Language of the Sentence**

*Jahannam* is the most frequent Qur'anic name for the abode of punishment. Philologists have discussed its origin — some deriving it from the Arabic root *j-h-m*, connected to a frowning, grim, forbidding aspect (*jahm al-wajh*, a scowling face), and others treating it as an Arabized form of the Hebrew *ge-hinnom*, the valley outside Jerusalem associated with child sacrifice and later with burning refuse. Both etymologies converge on the same connotation: a place of grimness and burning, associated with the consequences of grave wrong.

*Khālidīna fīhā*, "abiding therein," comes from *kh-l-d*, a root denoting permanence and remaining. The Arabs used it for something that lasts a very long time, and the Qur'an uses it both for the Fire and for the Garden. Where the Qur'an intends absolute unendingness, it often adds the reinforcement *abadan* ("forever") — as it does in the very next mention of the Garden in verse 8 of this sūrah: *khālidīna fīhā abadan*.

Some commentators have found significance in that asymmetry within this sūrah: the punishment is described as *khālidīna fīhā*, while the reward is described as *khālidīna fīhā abadan*, with the extra word of permanence attached to mercy. Whether or not one presses the distinction, it harmonizes with the ḥadīth qudsī recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"When God completed the creation, He wrote in His Book — and it is with Him above the Throne — 'Truly My Mercy prevails over My Wrath.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3194; Muslim 2751)

The general principle of the tradition is that punishment is proportionate and mercy is expansive; the door of repentance stands open until death arrives, and the Qur'an's most emphatic invitation is addressed to those who have most to fear:

> *"Say: 'O My servants who have wronged their own souls! Despair not of God's Mercy. Truly God forgives all sins. Truly He is the Forgiving, the Merciful.'"* (Qur'an 39:53)

The verse before us describes the destination of those who do not take that door. It does not remove the door.

**"Sharr al-Bariyyah": The Worst of Creation**

The final clause is the one that arrests every reader. *Ulāʾika hum sharru'l-bariyyah* — "it is they who are the worst of creation."

*Bariyyah* comes from the root *b-r-ʾ*, meaning to create, to originate, to bring into being from nothing — the same root as the divine Name *al-Bāriʾ*, the Originator (59:24). The word *bariyyah* therefore means the totality of created beings. Some philologists connected it instead to *barā*, dust or soil, giving the sense "those made from the earth"; the two readings are compatible in meaning here.

The scope of the phrase is what shocks. Not the worst among human beings — the worst among *created things*. Beneath the beasts, beneath the insects, beneath the inanimate objects that occupy their assigned station without deviation.

The pronoun structure intensifies it further: *ulāʾika hum*, with the separating pronoun *hum* inserted for emphasis and specification. Arabic grammarians call this *ḍamīr al-faṣl*, and its function is restrictive — "it is *they*, precisely they, who are the worst of creation."

**How Can a Human Being Fall Below an Animal?**

The claim seems extreme until one considers what makes it possible, and here the Qur'an's own reasoning is precise and consistent.

An animal fulfils its nature completely. A bee makes honey; a horse runs; a bird migrates on schedule. None of them can fail, because none of them was given the capacity to choose against their design. They occupy their rank in creation perfectly.

The human being alone was given the capacity to refuse. That capacity is precisely what makes him potentially the highest of creatures — and necessarily what makes him potentially the lowest. Value and risk rise together. The Qur'an describes the acceptance of this responsibility in dramatic terms:

> *"Truly We offered the Trust unto the heavens and the earth and the mountains, but they refused to bear it, and were wary of it. Yet man bore it; truly he has proven himself an ignorant wrongdoer."* (Qur'an 33:72)

And it draws the comparison with animals explicitly, more than once:

> *"And We have indeed made many among jinn and mankind for Hell: they have hearts with which they understand not; they have eyes with which they see not; and they have ears with which they hear not. Such as these are like cattle. Nay, they are even further astray. It is they who are heedless."* (Qur'an 7:179)

> *"Or do you suppose that most of them hear or understand? They are but as cattle. Nay, they are further astray from the way."* (Qur'an 25:44)

The logic in both passages is identical, and it is not arbitrary contempt. Cattle lack the faculties; they cannot be blamed for not using what they were never given. A human being possesses hearing, sight, and a mind capable of recognizing evidence — and lets them lie idle in the one matter that determines his eternal state. He is therefore worse off than the animal, because the animal is fulfilling its nature while he is betraying his.

Consider a practical version of the comparison. A blind man who fails to see a warning sign is not blamed. A sighted man who sees it and drives on is not merely as bad as the blind man; he is worse, because he had what was needed and refused to use it. The faculties are the aggravating factor.

**The Qur'an's Ranking of Creatures**

The Qur'an offers a scale, and the verse before us is one of its two poles. At the top:

> *"We have indeed honoured the Children of Adam, and carried them over land and sea, and provided them with good things, and favoured them above many We have created."* (Qur'an 17:70)

At the bottom, in the passage the source commentary rightly cites:

> *"Truly We created man in the most beautiful stature, then We cast him to the lowest of the low — save those who believe and perform righteous deeds; for theirs shall be a reward unceasing."* (Qur'an 95:4–6)

The human being is thus the only creature with a range. Angels are fixed in obedience; animals are fixed in instinct; humans move. This is the single most important fact about human dignity in Islamic thought: it is not automatic, it is not inherited, and it is not distributed by lineage. It is *earned or forfeited* by what a person does with the faculties he was given.

And here the Qur'an's most explicit statement on the criterion of rank belongs:

> *"O mankind! Truly We created you from a male and a female, and We made you peoples and tribes that you may come to know one another. Surely the most noble of you before God are the most reverent of you."* (Qur'an 49:13)

The Prophet ﷺ made the same point in his Farewell Sermon:

> *"O people! Truly your Lord is One and your father is one. There is no superiority of an Arab over a non-Arab, nor of a non-Arab over an Arab; nor of a white man over a black man, nor of a black man over a white man — except by piety."* (Aḥmad, Musnad, from the sermon at the Farewell Pilgrimage)

Read together with our verse, the doctrine is coherent: rank in creation is real, it is steep, and the only currency in it is faith joined to conduct.

**What This Verse Does Not License**

Because this is among the sternest verdicts in the Qur'an, it must be handled with the care the tradition itself applied.

*It is a description of a category, not a licence to assign individuals to it.* The Sunni tradition has been consistently cautious about declaring specific living persons to be among the eternally condemned. The reason is epistemic: we cannot see whether the clear proof was genuinely established for a given person, and we cannot see how a person will end. The Prophet ﷺ warned:

> *"A man may do the deeds of the people of Paradise, as it appears to people, while he is of the people of the Fire; and a man may do the deeds of the people of the Fire, as it appears to people, while he is of the people of Paradise. Deeds are only by their endings."* (al-Bukhārī 6607; Muslim 112)

If the ending is decisive and the ending is hidden, then final verdicts on individuals are simply outside human competence.

*It does not authorize mistreatment.* The Qur'an's ruling on conduct toward non-Muslims is governed by an entirely separate principle:

> *"God does not forbid you, regarding those who did not fight you on account of religion and did not expel you from your homes, from being kind to them and acting justly toward them. Truly God loves the just."* (Qur'an 60:8)

And the standard of justice is not suspended by disagreement:

> *"Let not the hatred of a people lead you to be unjust. Be just; that is nearer to reverence."* (Qur'an 5:8)

The Prophet ﷺ was similarly explicit about the protected non-Muslim:

> *"Whoever kills one under covenant will not smell the fragrance of Paradise, though its fragrance is found at a distance of forty years' travel."* (al-Bukhārī 3166)

Eschatological verdicts belong to God; social conduct is governed by justice and mercy. Confusing the two categories has been a recurring failure of religious communities — and, as verse 4 taught, precisely the sort of confusion that fractures them.

*It is not an ethnic or communal statement.* The partitive *min* in "those who disbelieve *from among* the People of the Book" was analysed under verse 1. The same Book that issues this warning also praises the upright among them (3:113–114) and permits marriage and shared meals with them (5:5) — arrangements that would be unintelligible if the verse were a blanket communal condemnation.

**The Mirror This Verse Holds Up to the Believer**

The most useful way for a Muslim to read a verse like this is not outward but inward. The Qur'an consistently uses portraits of failure as warnings to those reading, not as trophies for them.

The traits condemned here are not exotic: seeing evidence and covering it over; recognizing truth and refusing it because acceptance is costly; possessing faculties and refusing to use them. None of these require membership in another religion. A Muslim can look at a clear ruling and decide not to see it. A Muslim can recognize an obligation and bury it under rationalization. The disease named in this verse — the deliberate covering over of a recognized truth — is available to anyone.

The early generations understood this. It is reported that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb asked Ḥudhayfah, the Companion entrusted with the names of the hypocrites of Madinah, whether he was among them. A man of ʿUmar's rank asking that question is the model of how these verses are meant to be received: with self-examination rather than self-congratulation.

**Why the Punishment Is Just: The Logic of Consequence**

Finally, the coherence of the verse depends on seeing punishment as consequence rather than as arbitrary retaliation.

Throughout the Qur'an, the Fire is presented as something people bring upon themselves:

> *"That is for what your hands sent forth, and because God is not unjust toward His servants."* (Qur'an 3:182)

> *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it."* (Qur'an 99:7–8)

A life spent covering truth is a life spent training a soul into a particular shape. The Hereafter, on the Qur'an's account, is the unveiling of what a soul has actually become:

> *"On the Day when secrets are tried."* (Qur'an 86:9)

Consider the analogy of a patient who is warned repeatedly, offered treatment freely, and refuses it for thirty years. When the disease arrives at its final stage, the outcome is not an injustice inflicted by the physician. It is the natural terminus of a chosen course. This is why the verse follows verse 1 rather than preceding it: God established the proof before He established the consequence.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:7

> **Truly those who believe and perform righteous deeds, it is they who are the best of creation.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counterweight**

Immediately after placing one group at the bottom of the scale of created beings, the sūrah places another at the top, and it does so using an exactly mirrored sentence. Verse 6 ended: *ulāʾika hum sharru'l-bariyyah*, "it is they who are the worst of creation." Verse 7 ends: *ulāʾika hum khayru'l-bariyyah*, "it is they who are the best of creation." Same construction, same emphatic pronoun *hum*, same noun *bariyyah* — only the middle word changes, from *sharr* to *khayr*.

This is deliberate parallelism, and it carries an argument. The two verses are the two ends of a single measuring rod. They tell the reader that the same faculties, the same species, and the same lifespan can produce either outcome, and that nothing in between the two poles is described because what matters is which direction a person is travelling.

The Qur'an uses this technique often — light and darkness, the blind and the seeing, the living and the dead — not to deny that most people live in the middle, but to make the two directions visible so that a person can locate himself on the road.

**Only Two Requirements**

What lifts a human being to the top of creation, according to this verse, is stated in five Arabic words: *alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilu'l-ṣāliḥāt* — "those who believe and perform righteous deeds."

The economy of this is worth noticing. The verse does not require nobility of birth, exceptional intelligence, wealth, physical strength, scholarly credentials, or public prominence. It names an inner conviction and an outer practice. Both are available to a labourer, a mother, an illiterate villager, a prisoner, and a scholar alike.

This pairing — faith and righteous action — occurs together in the Qur'an approximately sixty times. Such a repetition is not accidental. It is the Book's fixed formula for salvation, and the fixity is itself a doctrine: neither element functions alone.

**"Āmanū": What Belief Actually Means**

*Āmanū* comes from the root *ʾ-m-n*, which produces *amn* (security), *amānah* (trust, a deposit entrusted to someone), and *amīn* (trustworthy — the title the Makkans gave Muhammad ﷺ before his prophethood). *Īmān*, the noun, is therefore not merely intellectual assent. It is the establishment of security and trust between a servant and his Lord.

The connection between the root's meanings is illuminating. A person who genuinely believes in God arrives at *amn*, inner security, because he has entrusted (*amānah*) his affairs to One who is capable and merciful. This is why the Qur'an ties faith directly to tranquillity:

> *"Those who believe and whose hearts find rest in the remembrance of God. Truly, in the remembrance of God do hearts find rest."* (Qur'an 13:28)

The scholars of creed defined *īmān* in a formula preserved from the earliest generations: affirmation in the heart, declaration with the tongue, and action with the limbs — increasing with obedience and decreasing with disobedience. That definition already contains the second half of our verse, which is why some of the early scholars noted that the addition of "and performed righteous deeds" in the Qur'an is an explication of faith rather than a separate condition tacked on.

The Prophet ﷺ described the branching structure of faith:

> *"Faith has some seventy-odd branches. The highest of them is the statement 'there is no god but God,' and the lowest of them is removing something harmful from the road. And modesty is a branch of faith."* (al-Bukhārī 9; Muslim 35)

Notice the range of that ḥadīth: it runs from the supreme declaration of monotheism down to clearing a stone off a path. Both are named as faith. This is the Qur'anic vision of religion made concrete — a continuum from creed to civic decency, all of it counted.

**"ʿAmilu'l-Ṣāliḥāt": Deeds That Repair**

*Ṣāliḥāt* comes from the root *ṣ-l-ḥ*, meaning to be sound, wholesome, fit for purpose, and — importantly — to *repair* what is broken. From it come *iṣlāḥ* (reform, reconciliation, putting right), *ṣulḥ* (peacemaking), and *maṣlaḥah* (benefit, the public good).

This root has an opposite that the Qur'an uses constantly: *fasād*, corruption or spoilage, from *f-s-d*. The pairing tells us what a righteous deed is. It is not merely a ritual act; it is an act that *repairs* rather than spoils — that leaves a person, a relationship, a community, or a piece of the world in better condition than it found it.

The Qur'an describes the mission of the prophets in exactly these terms. Shuʿayb says to his people:

> *"I desire naught but reform (al-iṣlāḥ), so far as I am able."* (Qur'an 11:88)

And the Qur'an characterizes the failure of nations as *fasād fī'l-arḍ*, corruption in the land.

The scholars specified two conditions for an act to qualify as *ṣāliḥ*: that it be done sincerely for God, and that it conform to what God has legislated. Al-Fuḍayl ibn ʿIyāḍ (d. 187/803) explained the Qur'anic phrase "the best in deeds" (67:2) with a formulation the tradition never tired of quoting:

> *"[The best deed is] the most sincere and the most correct."* They asked: *"O Abū ʿAlī, what is the most sincere and the most correct?"* He said: *"If a deed is sincere but not correct, it is not accepted; and if it is correct but not sincere, it is not accepted — until it is both sincere and correct. Sincere means done purely for God, and correct means done in accordance with the Sunnah."*

Both conditions are needed for a reason that is easy to grasp. A person who invents a devotion out of enthusiasm may be sincere, but he is following his own taste rather than God's instruction, which quietly places his judgment above revelation. A person who performs the prescribed acts to be admired is doing the right thing for an idol. Between the two conditions, the whole territory of self-deception is closed off.

**Why Faith Without Action, and Action Without Faith, Both Fail**

The insistence on both halves is one of the most practically important teachings in the Qur'an, and the reasoning behind it is worth spelling out.

*Faith without action is a claim without evidence.* Convictions that never alter behaviour are not convictions; they are opinions we enjoy holding. Every human being knows this from ordinary life: a man who says he loves his family but never appears, never provides, never listens, has an emotion rather than a commitment. The Qur'an confronts this directly:

> *"O you who believe! Why do you say what you do not do? It is most hateful in the sight of God that you say what you do not do."* (Qur'an 61:2–3)

*Action without faith lacks an orientation.* Good works performed with no reference to God are still genuinely beneficial to their recipients — the Qur'an nowhere denies that a well dug by an unbeliever gives water. But as regards the Hereafter, a deed derives its ultimate value from the direction in which it is offered. A journey undertaken with tremendous energy in the wrong direction covers ground without arriving.

The Prophet ﷺ was asked about a man of pre-Islamic Arabia famed for hospitality and generosity, and his answer, recorded by Muslim, was that such a man never once said "My Lord, forgive me my sins on the Day of Judgment" (Muslim 214). The tradition preserves that answer without cruelty; it simply states the principle that the Hereafter is entered through the door of faith.

The relationship between the two is better understood as organic than as arithmetic. Faith is a root; deeds are the fruit. A root that never fruits is dead wood; fruit without a root is decoration that rots within days. The Qur'an uses precisely this image:

> *"Do you not see how God sets forth a parable? A good word is as a good tree: its roots firm and its branches in the sky. It brings forth its fruit in every season, by the leave of its Lord."* (Qur'an 14:24–25)

**Better Than the Angels? A Classical Debate**

This verse became the anchor of a serious theological discussion among the classical scholars: are righteous human beings superior to the angels?

Those who argued that they are — and Ibn Kathīr reports this position, as do others — reasoned from the very word *bariyyah*. If believers are the best of *all created beings*, and angels are created beings, then the ranking includes them. Their supporting argument is moral rather than merely textual: the angels obey without the pull of appetite, without the whisper of Satan, without fatigue, doubt, or the fear of death. As the Qur'an describes them:

> *"They disobey not God in what He commands them, and they do what they are commanded."* (Qur'an 66:6)

The human being obeys against resistance — against desire, against social pressure, against exhaustion, against uncertainty. An act that costs something is, on this reasoning, worth more than an act that costs nothing.

Those who argued the opposite pointed to the angels' nearness to God, their unbroken worship, and their freedom from sin.

The tradition largely settled into a nuanced position: the perfected among the righteous believers — the prophets and the greatest of the saints — attain a rank surpassing that of many angels, while the angels in general surpass ordinary human beings. What matters for the reader of this verse is the underlying insight, which is not competitive but explanatory: the possibility of failure is exactly what gives human obedience its weight. Struggle is not an obstacle to your rank. It is the reason a rank is possible at all.

**Best of Their Age, Best in This World**

Some commentators, cited by al-Qurṭubī, read *khayr al-bariyyah* in a relative sense: the best of the created beings of their own era, comparable to the address to the Children of Israel:

> *"Remember My Blessing which I bestowed upon you, and that I favoured you above the worlds."* (Qur'an 2:47)

That verse plainly means favour within a particular historical period, since the same community is elsewhere reproached severely. Read this way, the verse says that in any age, the believers who act righteously are the best of the people of that age — a claim that does not require comparison with angels at all.

The commentators also transmit prophetic reports about "the best of creation" that direct the phrase toward concrete, unglamorous piety. Ibn Kathīr records this among them:

> *"Shall I not inform you of the best of creation?"* They said: *"Of course, O Messenger of God."* He said: *"A man who takes the reins of his horse in the way of God, and whenever there is a cry of alarm, he mounts it. Shall I not inform you of the best of creation?"* They said: *"Of course."* He said: *"A man who has a flock of sheep, and he establishes the prayer and gives the alms. Shall I not inform you of the worst of creation?"* They said: *"Of course."* He said: *"The one who is asked for the sake of God, yet does not give for His sake."*

Two portraits of the best, and they are strikingly ordinary: a man ready to defend his community when the alarm is raised, and a shepherd in the hills who keeps up his prayers and pays what is due from his flock. No title, no office, no visibility. The third portrait — the worst — is equally telling: someone who is asked in God's name and refuses. Not a tyrant, not a persecutor, just a closed hand.

There is also the report cited by both Shiite and Sunni sources, mentioned in the source commentary, in which the Prophet ﷺ said to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib regarding "the best of creation" that it referred to him and his followers on the Day of Resurrection, "content, contenting" — the last two words being a quotation of 89:28, and anticipating the mutual contentment that verse 8 of this sūrah will describe. Whatever position one takes on the grading of that report, its function in the commentaries is to attach the abstract superlative to a recognizable human example.

**The Democratic Nature of This Verse**

One of the most striking features of the verse is what it does *not* say. It does not name a tribe, a lineage, a language, a homeland, or a social class. Membership in "the best of creation" is defined entirely by two things a person can begin doing today.

This has real social consequences, and the first Muslim community demonstrated them. Bilāl ibn Rabāḥ, a formerly enslaved Abyssinian, called the prayer from the roof of the Kaʿbah — the ritual centre of a society organized by lineage. Salmān al-Fārisī, a Persian who had passed through several religious communities searching for the truth, was declared by the Prophet ﷺ to be "of us, the family of the house." Ṣuhayb the Byzantine, Zayd ibn Ḥārithah the freed slave placed in command over aristocrats — the roster of early Islam reads as a systematic dismantling of inherited rank.

That was not sentimentality. It was the sociology implied by a verse like this one. If the top of the created order is reached by faith and righteous action, then every inherited hierarchy is provisional, and any community that reintroduces such hierarchies is contradicting the criterion by which it claims to live.

**Living the Verse: Small Deeds and the Long Line**

Because the requirement is faith plus righteous action, and because "righteous action" spans everything from the declaration of God's oneness to removing an obstacle from a road, the path to the rank described here is made of ordinary material.

The Prophet ﷺ emphasized consistency over intensity:

> *"The most beloved of deeds to God are the most constant, even if few."* (al-Bukhārī 6464; Muslim 783, from ʿĀʾishah)

And he refused to let anyone dismiss a small good:

> *"Do not belittle any act of kindness, even that you meet your brother with a cheerful face."* (Muslim 2626, from Abū Dharr)

> *"Guard yourself against the Fire even with half a date; and whoever does not find that, then with a good word."* (al-Bukhārī 6023; Muslim 1016)

The practical wisdom in these reports is considerable. People fail at religion far more often through discouragement than through temptation. A person who believes that only spectacular deeds count will attempt one, exhaust himself, fail, and quit. A person who understands that a smile, half a date, a removed stone, and a steady prayer are all entered into the same ledger will keep going for fifty years — and a fifty-year accumulation of small consistencies is what actually produces the character this verse describes.

The verse's own placement supports this reading. It comes immediately after verse 5, which listed the requirements: sincere worship, monotheistic orientation, established prayer, given alms. The "best of creation" of verse 7 are simply the people who did what verse 5 asked.

## Sūrah al-Bayyinah 98:8

> **Their reward is with their Lord, Gardens of Eden with rivers running below, abiding therein forever, God being content with them and they being content with Him. That is for whosoever fears his Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Closing Verse and Its Ascending Order**

The sūrah ends with a single verse that climbs through five stages, each higher than the last: a reward that is *with their Lord*; Gardens of Eden watered by running rivers; permanence without end; the mutual contentment of God and His servants; and finally the one condition that qualifies a person for all of it.

The arrangement is not random. It moves from where the reward is kept, to what it consists of, to how long it lasts, to what it ultimately *is* — and then closes the sūrah by naming the door through which one enters.

**"Jazāʾuhum ʿinda Rabbihim": Kept in the Best of Hands**

*Jazāʾ* means recompense, a return given for something — the word covers both reward and punishment, since both are returns. Here it is qualified by *ʿinda Rabbihim*, "with their Lord."

The preposition *ʿinda* denotes presence, custody, nearness. To say that something is *ʿinda* someone is to say it is held in their keeping. The Qur'an uses this construction to distinguish what is stored with God from what is held in the world:

> *"That which is with you is spent, and that which is with God endures."* (Qur'an 16:96)

Consider the reassurance in that. Everything a person earns in this world is held in unreliable custody: savings can be devalued, property can be seized, reputations can be destroyed by a rumour, and health cannot be stored at all. The believer's deeds, by contrast, are described as being deposited with the One who neither forgets nor becomes insolvent:

> *"Truly God wrongs not so much as an atom's weight; and if it be a good deed, He will multiply it and grant a great reward from His Presence."* (Qur'an 4:40)

The choice of the name *Rabb* — Lord, Sustainer, Nurturer, from a root meaning to raise something in stages until it reaches completion — rather than a name of majesty or power is also deliberate. It is the name of gradual nurturing. The One who brought them through their whole development is the One holding the outcome. And the suffix is *Rabbihim*, "*their* Lord," a possessive of relationship. This is not a distant administrator settling accounts but the Sustainer who has been with them the whole way.

**"Jannātu ʿAdn": Gardens of Permanent Residence**

*Jannāt* is the plural of *jannah*, from the root *j-n-n*, meaning to cover, conceal, or veil. The same root gives *janīn* (a foetus, concealed in the womb), *jinn* (beings hidden from sight), *junūn* (madness, where reason is veiled), and *junnah* (a shield). A *jannah* is thus, at root, a garden so densely planted that its ground is concealed by foliage — for a people of the desert, an image of overwhelming abundance.

The plural is significant: gardens, not a garden. The tradition describes multiple levels, and the Prophet ﷺ encouraged his community to aim high:

> *"When you ask God, ask Him for al-Firdaws, for it is the middle of Paradise and the highest part of Paradise, and above it is the Throne of the Merciful, and from it spring forth the rivers of Paradise."* (al-Bukhārī 2790, from Abū Hurayrah)

*ʿAdn* comes from a root meaning to remain, to stay fixed, to reside permanently — the same root gives *maʿdin*, a mine, meaning the place where a mineral permanently resides. "Gardens of Eden" therefore means, in the Arabic, gardens of permanent residence.

That definition addresses a real anxiety. Every earthly delight is shadowed by the knowledge that it will end: the holiday has a return date, the health has a limit, the loved company will be separated by death. Much of the pain of pleasure in this world is the awareness of its expiry. *ʿAdn* removes the expiry.

**"Tajrī min Taḥtihā al-Anhār": The Image of Running Water**

The phrase "with rivers running below" recurs throughout the Qur'an as the standard description of the Garden. Its power depends on knowing the audience. To a people living in a landscape where water was the scarcest and most contested resource, where wells were fought over and tribal territory was measured by access to springs, the image of gardens with rivers *running beneath them* — continuous, unmanaged, inexhaustible — was the most vivid possible picture of security and abundance.

*Tajrī*, "running," is a present-tense verb of continuous action; the water never stops flowing, and it requires no irrigation, no labour, no rationing.

The Qur'an also insists that the reality exceeds the image. In a ḥadīth qudsī recorded in both Ṣaḥīḥs:

> *"God says: 'I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and what has never occurred to the heart of a human being.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3244; Muslim 2824, from Abū Hurayrah)

And the Qur'an says of the descriptions themselves:

> *"No soul knows what comfort of the eyes is kept hidden for them as a recompense for that which they used to do."* (Qur'an 32:17)

The classical commentators drew the appropriate conclusion: the descriptions are true, but they function as pointers toward a reality beyond the reach of description. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that there is nothing in the Garden resembling this world except the names.

**"Khālidīna Fīhā Abadan": Permanence Reinforced**

*Khālidīna fīhā* — abiding therein — is here reinforced by *abadan*, forever. As noted in the commentary on verse 6, the Qur'an frequently attaches this additional word of unending duration to the description of the Garden, and the commentators have long observed the emphasis it gives to mercy.

The doubling is not redundant. It answers the deepest fear that would otherwise remain even in paradise: the fear of losing it. A joy that can be lost is never fully enjoyed, because part of the mind is always guarding it. The Qur'an closes that gap explicitly, quoting the residents themselves:

> *"And they will say: 'Praise be to God, who has removed all sorrow from us. Truly our Lord is Forgiving, Thankful — He who out of His Bounty has settled us in the Abode of Everlasting Life, wherein no toil befalls us, nor does weariness touch us therein.'"* (Qur'an 35:34–35)

And in the most direct statement of security:

> *"They taste not death therein, save the first death."* (Qur'an 44:56)

The Prophet ﷺ described the announcement made to the people of the Garden:

> *"It will be called out: 'O people of Paradise! You shall live and never die; you shall remain healthy and never fall ill; you shall remain young and never grow old; you shall enjoy and never be wearied.'"* (Muslim 2837, from Abū Saʿīd and Abū Hurayrah)

**"Raḍiya'llāhu ʿanhum wa Raḍū ʿanhu": The Summit of the Sūrah**

Here is the phrase that makes this verse extraordinary, and the tradition has always recognized it as the true climax of the sūrah — greater than the gardens, greater than the rivers, greater even than the permanence. *Raḍiya'llāhu ʿanhum wa raḍū ʿanhu* — "God being content with them, and they being content with Him."

*Riḍā* comes from the root *r-ḍ-w*, meaning contentment, acceptance, approval, being well-pleased. It is not the same as merely granting something. A judge may award a payment without approving of the recipient; a wealthy man may fund a stranger with no relationship at all. *Riḍā* is relational: it means the other party is *pleased with you*.

Notice the reciprocity, which is the heart of the phrase. Not only is God pleased with them; they are pleased with Him. Both directions are named, and the Qur'an repeats this exact pairing in its highest passages:

> *"God is content with them and they are content with Him. That is the great triumph."* (Qur'an 5:119)

> *"And the first to lead the way, of the Emigrants and the Helpers, and those who followed them in excellence — God is content with them and they are content with Him, and He has prepared for them Gardens with rivers running below, to abide therein forever. That is the great triumph."* (Qur'an 9:100)

> *"God is content with them and they are content with Him. They are the party of God. Truly it is the party of God who are the successful."* (Qur'an 58:22)

And the call addressed to the soul at the end of Sūrah al-Fajr:

> *"O soul at peace! Return unto your Lord, content, contenting. Enter among My servants. And enter My Garden."* (Qur'an 89:27–30)

There the two words appear as a single description of the arriving soul: *rāḍiyatan marḍiyyah* — pleased and pleasing.

The reason this ranks above the gardens is that the gardens are things given, while *riḍā* is a relationship achieved. The Prophet ﷺ described a moment in the Garden that makes the hierarchy explicit. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record that when the people of Paradise have entered it, God will ask whether they wish for anything more; they will answer that He has given them everything and saved them from the Fire, and then:

> *"He will say: 'I shall grant you something better than that.' They will say: 'Our Lord, what could be better than that?' He will say: 'I bestow upon you My good pleasure (riḍwānī), and I shall never be displeased with you thereafter.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6549; Muslim 2829, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

Better than the Garden. The gifts were the setting; the approval of the Giver is the reward.

The commentators mentioned by the source add the observation, transmitted among the spiritual authorities, that the Garden of Contentment (*jannat al-riḍwān*) is ranked just below the Garden of the Essence (*jannat al-dhāt*) — the vision of God Himself, which the Qur'an promises:

> *"Faces, that Day, shall be radiant, gazing upon their Lord."* (Qur'an 75:22–23)

**The Second Half of the Phrase: "And They Being Content with Him"**

The clause that is easiest to skip over is the one that speaks most directly to how a person lives now. It is not difficult to imagine wanting God to be pleased with us. But the verse also says the believers are pleased *with Him* — and this is a description that begins in this world, not only in the next.

To be content with God means to accept His decree without resentment: His timing when it is slow, His portion when it is small, His answer when it is no, His taking when it is painful. Anyone who has lived through loss knows how demanding this is, and the Qur'an does not pretend otherwise; it commands patience precisely because the situations that require it are hard.

The Prophet ﷺ tied this contentment directly to the taste of faith:

> *"He has tasted the sweetness of faith who is content with God as Lord, with Islam as religion, and with Muhammad as Messenger."* (Muslim 34, from al-ʿAbbās ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib)

And he described the believer's condition in both directions of fortune:

> *"How wonderful is the affair of the believer! All his affairs are good, and that is for no one but the believer: if something pleasing happens to him he is grateful, and that is good for him; and if something harmful happens to him he is patient, and that is good for him."* (Muslim 2999, from Ṣuhayb)

There is also a psychological observation worth making here. The verse names contentment as the highest reward, and every honest examination of human experience confirms that contentment, not acquisition, is what people are actually after. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Riches are not in the abundance of possessions; rather, riches are the contentment of the soul."* (al-Bukhārī 6446; Muslim 1051, from Abū Hurayrah)

A person who obtains everything he wanted and remains restless has obtained nothing. A person who has little and is content has obtained what the wealthy man was chasing. The Hereafter's supreme prize is the perfection of the state that even now determines whether a life feels full or empty.

**"Dhālika li-man Khashiya Rabbah": The Door and Its Key**

The sūrah closes by naming the qualification: *dhālika li-man khashiya rabbah* — "that is for whosoever fears his Lord."

The verb is *khashiya*, and the choice of word repays attention. Arabic has several terms for fear. *Khawf* is the general word — the instinctive fear of harm, the fear a person feels of a fire, an illness, or an enemy. *Khashyah* is more specific: the scholars define it as a fear that arises from *knowledge* of the greatness of the one feared, mixed with reverence and awe. It is the fear of a person who knows what he is standing before.

The Qur'an makes this connection explicit:

> *"Only those of His servants who have knowledge fear (yakhshā) God."* (Qur'an 35:28)

Fear born of ignorance is panic and produces flight. *Khashyah* born of knowledge produces care — the careful attention of someone who understands what is at stake and therefore watches where he steps.

This is precisely the well-known analogy for *taqwā*: a person walking a narrow path through thorn bushes at night, or driving a mountain road in poor visibility. He is not paralyzed with terror; he is *attentive*. He slows at the bends, he keeps both hands on the wheel, he does not check his phone. His awareness of danger is not a burden on the journey — it is what allows him to complete it. Awareness prevents avoidable harm.

Note also the object of the fear: *rabbah*, "his Lord" — not "the Punisher," not "the Overpowering." Even in the clause about fear, the sūrah uses the name of nurture and care. The believer's *khashyah* is not the dread of a tyrant; it is the reverence owed to the One who raised him from nothing and to whom he is answerable.

**Why Fear Is Named as the Condition for a Reward of Contentment**

There is an apparent tension in the verse's structure, and resolving it discloses the sūrah's final wisdom. The reward described is mutual contentment — the most intimate and secure state imaginable. The condition attached to it is fear. Why should fear be the door to contentment?

The answer is that *khashyah* is what keeps a person on the road long enough to arrive. Consider the alternative. Someone who feels no accountability drifts: he postpones, he compromises in small matters, he allows the gap between belief and behaviour to widen year by year, and he does so without alarm, because nothing in him registers the drift as dangerous. Fear of God is the internal alarm that catches the drift early.

The Qur'an makes the same connection between fear and outcome elsewhere:

> *"But as for the one who feared the standing before his Lord and restrained the soul from caprice, truly the Garden shall be the refuge."* (Qur'an 79:40–41)

And the Prophet ﷺ described the reward of a fear that is internal rather than performed, in the ḥadīth of the seven whom God shades on the Day when there is no shade but His:

> *"...and a man who is called by a woman of beauty and standing, and he says: 'I fear God'; and a man who gives in charity so secretly that his left hand does not know what his right hand has given; and a man who remembers God in solitude and his eyes overflow."* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031, from Abū Hurayrah)

Every one of those seven cases involves an act performed where no one is watching. That is what *khashyah* produces: integrity in private, which is the only reliable evidence that a person's religion is directed at God rather than at an audience — the *ikhlāṣ* that verse 5 of this sūrah demanded.

**The Whole Sūrah in a Single Line of Reasoning**

With this verse the argument of al-Bayyinah closes exactly where it began, and the shape of the whole becomes visible.

Human beings do not release entrenched error on their own, so God sends a clear proof (v. 1). The proof is a Messenger reciting purified pages (v. 2) whose contents are straight and self-standing (v. 3). Clarity, rather than ending disputes, exposes the envy that fractures communities (v. 4) — even though what was commanded was only ever sincere worship, monotheistic orientation, established prayer, and giving to the poor (v. 5). Those who cover the proof after seeing it fall to the bottom of created things (v. 6); those who believe and repair the world rise to the top (v. 7). And what waits at the top is not merely a garden but the mutual contentment of the Lord and His servant — available to anyone who lives with the reverent awareness that he is answerable to the One who sustains him (v. 8).

Eight verses; a complete account of revelation, responsibility, and destiny. It is not difficult to see why God commanded His Messenger ﷺ to recite this particular chapter to Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, nor why Ubayy wept when he heard that he had been named.
