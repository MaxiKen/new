# Sūrah al-Humazah (Chapter 104) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sūrah Named by Its First Wound**

Sūrah al-Humazah, "The Slanderer," is one of the ninety verses in the shortest group of the Qur'an, yet among the severest in tone of anything revealed in the early period. It takes its title from the very first accusation of its first line — the condemnation of the slandering backbiter — and carries a second, equally diagnostic name from its second verse: al-Ma'dhariyat, "the Hoarder," from the verb of hoarding that defines the second half of the sūrah. Nine verses long, of Makkān character, it stands in the written Muṣḥaf between al-Māʿūn (107), the sūrah that indicts the one who withholds even small help, and al-Fīl (105), the sūrah of God's sudden judgment upon an armed army. But its deepest literary neighborhood is the two sūrahs immediately before it: al-Takāthur (102) and al-ʿAṣr (103). Read together, these three short sūrahs form a triple diagnosis of a civilization: competition in increase that diverts a people until they visit the graveyards (102:1–2); the loss of every human being, without exception, except the four qualities that save (103:1–3); and then the portrait of the man who embodies the whole disease — the one who devours people with his tongue and wealth with his hand, supposing that his accumulation makes him immortal (104:1–3). The Qur'an elsewhere defines the same condition in a single breath:

> *"And know that the life of this world is but amusement and diversion and adornment and boasting between you and the competition in increase of wealth and children."* (Qur'an 47:36)

Al-Humazah is the face of that verse. Where 47:36 describes the condition, 104 names the creature it produces.

**The Occasion of Revelation and the Generality of the Verdict**

The classical commentaries record three accounts of the occasion (asbāb al-nuzūl). The most cited, reported in the accounts of al-Qurṭubī, al-Zamakhsharī, and others, relates the sūrah to al-Akhnaṣ ibn Shurayq, a man of the Quraysh known for slandering people both to their faces and behind their backs. A second account, recorded by al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Zamakhsharī, relates it to al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, one of the foremost leaders of the Quraysh and an open host of the Prophet ﷺ, who attacked the Messenger of God ﷺ in his presence and slandered him in his absence. A third position, noted by al-Ṭabarī, holds that the sūrah is a general condemnation, revealed in relation to no particular individual at all. The methodological point that the scholars draw from the third position is the decisive one: even where a sūrah was revealed on account of a specific person, the ruling is general; the name of the occasion does not confine the address. The verse opens *wailu li-kulli* — "Woe unto **every** slandering backbiter" — and the "every" is what survives across the centuries. The named men of Mecca are the first instances of a permanent category; every generation after them supplies its own.

The historical setting matters. Early Makkah was a society in which a man's standing was made of two materials: his reputation and his trade. Reputation was the currency of an honor-culture where insult could be bloodier than a sword wound, and trade was the substance of a caravan economy in which the Quraysh were the brokers of the Arabian peninsula. The two weapons the Quraysh turned against the new message were precisely the tongue — slander, ridicule, the naming of the Prophet ﷺ as poet, madman, sorcerer, and liar — and the treasury — the withholding of provision, the embargo, the social and economic siege of the Muslims. The sūrah answers both weapons in nine lines. It tells the slanderer that his tongue is a theft of honor that God will repay in kind, and it tells the hoarder that his treasury is not a second life but fuel for a fire that crushes what enters it. That the two indicts stand in a single sūrah, joined by a single relative clause, is the structural secret of the whole chapter, and it will be examined at the second verse.

**"Wayl": The Opening Cry and Its Rhetorical Family**

The first word of the sūrah, *wayl*, has no exact English equivalent. Its root (w-ṣ-l / w-y-l, depending on the reading tradition) carries the sense of ruin, destruction, a fate that befalls without warning — a doom announced from outside the scene. The commentators transmit that *wayl* is taken by some as the name of a valley in Hell, in which case the line reads not as a general exclamation but as a location: "[The valley of] Wail is for every slandering backbiter," a standing appointment in the landscape of punishment. Al-Ṭabarī records both readings. Whether it is an exclamation or a geography, the function is the same: the word arrives before any description, as a verdict that precedes the evidence — and then the verses that follow become, as it were, the record on which the verdict is based.

This opening cry belongs to a family. Sūrah al-Ghashiyyah begins with the identical word:

> *"Woe to every slanderer and mocker, who gathers [wealth] and then piles it up"* (Qur'an 88:1–2)

— a sūrah that, like al-Humazah, moves from a social wound (slander and mockery) to a hoarding of wealth to a fire. And the whole Makkān corpus is built around a second rhetorical device that al-Humazah deploys at its fifth verse, *wa-mā adrāka* — "And what shall apprise thee of / what will inform you of" — which appears in al-Qalam (*"And what will inform you what the Real is?"* 68:42), al-Nabaʾ (*"And what will inform you what the Real is?"* 78:20), at-Takwīr (*"And what will inform you what the Firm is?"* 81:17), al-Shams (*"And what will inform you what the Piercer is?"* 81:17's neighbor), and al-Fajr (*"And what will inform you of the Hour of Judgment?"* 86:15–17). The device is a question that is not asking: it is an invitation to dread, a way of making the hearer generate the answer inside himself, so that the image he forms — from his own imagination of harm — is always more vivid than any description. Al-Humazah contains one of the sharpest uses of the device in the entire Qur'an, and it is the hinge on which the sūrah turns from the man to the Fire.

**The Architecture of Nine Verses**

The sūrah's nine verses divide into a movement of contraction and explosion:

- **Verse 1** — The accusation: a single man is named by his habit, and the verdict (*wayl*) is pronounced before any circumstance is given.
- **Verses 2–3** — The diagnosis: the same man amasses and tallies wealth, and the sūrah exposes the delusion at the bottom of the habit — that wealth makes him immortal.
- **Verse 4** — The refutation: a one-word divine *Nay!* (*lā*) that shatters the delusion and the casting into the crushing Fire.
- **Verse 5** — The question: *What shall apprise thee of the crushing Fire?* — the sūrah's center of gravity, where the Fire itself is promoted to subject.
- **Verse 6** — The answer: the Fire of God, ignited — ownership, fuel, and kindling stated in four words.
- **Verse 7** — The target: the Fire engulfs *hearts* — not merely bodies, but the seat of the self.
- **Verses 8–9** — The verdict sealed: the Fire is enclosed upon them, in pillars outstretched — the architecture of inescapability as the sūrah's final image.

Note the camera: it begins on a man's mouth and hand (1–3), pulls back to reveal the heart beneath (7), and then expands until the frame is a sealed fortress of fire whose pillars stretch beyond sight (8–9). The smallest sin of the tongue and the largest structure of the Hereafter occupy the same nine lines, and the reader is meant to feel their ratio.

**Two Sins, One Root: the Tongue and the Treasury**

The grammatical heart of the sūrah is the relative clause that opens verse 2. Verse 1 names *every slandering backbiter*; verse 2 does not introduce a new character. It continues: *who* amasses and tallies wealth. The slanderer **is** the hoarder. The Qur'an yokes the two in a single sentence because, in its moral anthropology, they are one disease wearing two garments. The slanderer accumulates at other people's expense: he converts their honor, their trust, their good name into the fuel of his own standing — he eats their reputation as if it were his inheritance. The hoarder accumulates at other people's expense in another currency: he withholds the circulation that wealth exists to serve — wages, alms, charity, the needs of the community — and keeps the flow for himself. In both cases the self is enlarged by the contraction of others; in both cases the accumulation is not an end in itself but a substitute for an inner security the person has not found in God.

The Qur'an elsewhere keeps the same pairing without ever stating the formula, as if expecting the reader to discover it. The ethics of the tongue is laid out in al-Hujurat:

> *"…and do not spy upon one another, or backbite one another. Would one of you like to eat the flesh of his dead brother? You would detest it."* (Qur'an 49:12)

The economics of hoarding is laid out in al-Tawbah:

> *"And those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in the way of God — give them tidings of a painful punishment, on the Day when it will be heated in the Hellfire and their foreheads, their flanks, and their backs will be branded with it: 'This is what you hoarded for yourselves, so taste that which you used to hoard.'"* (Qur'an 9:34–35)

And the falsifiers of the market — who defraud in measure and weight — are met with the identical cry, *"Woe to the defrauders"* (83:1), so that the Qur'an's three great economic-ethical indicts — the tongue that defrauds of honor, the hand that defrauds of circulation, the scale that defrauds of measure — all open or close on the same word: woe. Al-Humazah concentrates the first two into nine verses and shows them, in the end, to be one man, one habit, one heart.

**A Standing Diagnosis for Every Age**

A sūrah revealed in a caravan city against named men could, in principle, have aged into a museum piece. It has not, because it addresses conditions that every society reproduces: the market that turns reputation into a tradable commodity, the tongue that is now industrialized into media, feeds, and campaigns, the accumulation that has grown so vast as to dwarf whole states, and the delusion — the quiet one at the bottom of it all — that what a person owns will make him permanent. The sūrah's form itself is its argument. It does not lecture; it indicts. It does not describe a general class at a distance; it takes a single individual, names his two habits in four words, lets him speak his own delusion in one clause, and answers him with one word — *Nay!* — before unveiling, question by question, the fire that will engage his heart and seal him in pillars outstretched. The rhetorical speed is the moral point: there is no appeal, no negotiation, no long counsel. What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary on the sūrah's nine verses, each treated as a complete and independent discourse.

## Sūrah al-Humazah 104:1

> **Woe unto every slandering backbiter**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict Pronounced Before the Evidence**

The sūrah does not open with a story, a question, or a reason. It opens with a sentence of doom: *wayl* — woe. In the Arabic of the Qur'an this word is an impersonal cry of calamity, the kind of exclamation a people utter when ruin announces itself before its cause is even understood. The commentators record the two classical readings: on the first, *wayl* is simply "woe," an expression that punishment or destruction will befall the one named; on the second, transmitted in al-Ṭabarī's account, it is the name of a valley in Hell, and the line then reads not as an exclamation but as an assignment of addresses: "[The valley of] Wayl is for every slandering backbiter" — a permanent residence allotted to a permanent habit. Either way, the structure of the opening is arresting. A verdict is pronounced before any circumstance is stated, the way a judge's ruling is read aloud and then the record is opened to show why. Verses 2 and 3 that follow are, in effect, that record: the evidence is the man's own habits. The reader is taught at the very first word how the Qur'an indicts: it does not build a case from small premises to a large conclusion. It announces the conclusion, and then shows that the conclusion was already contained in the person.

**The Two Words That Name the Disease: Humazah and Lumazah**

The verse then names its target with two words, *humāzah* and *lumāzah*, translated here as "slandering backbiter," and the classical commentators give the full record of the debate over their precise division of labor. According to Mujāhid, *humazah* refers to harm done to others with one's hands and eyes, while *lumazah* refers to harm done with one's tongue. According to others, reported by al-Ṭabarī, *humazah* indicates any form of calumny — the heavy, public defamation — while *lumazah* indicates verbal reproach and accusation, the lighter, repeated, behind-the-back word. Yet others maintain the opposite: that *lumazah* is the one that literally "eats the flesh of others," the word for backbiting itself, after the Qur'an's own image in 49:12, while *humazah* is the broader slander. The debate, which Ibn Kathīr preserves in his commentary in this exact range, is not pedantry. It is the tradition performing a forensic anatomy of the tongue's crimes and refusing to let any of them escape a label. What every position concedes is the unity of the moral condition: the one who slanders is the one who backbites, in public or in private, by word or by gesture — a man whose daily work is the reduction of other people. The commentators add, as the broadest reading, that the verse is a rebuke of anyone who is more concerned with the faults of others than with his own — the person whose eyes are calibrated to other people's defects and uncalibrated to his. That is the definition that outlives every technical division, because it reaches the heart of the matter: the slanderer has made other people's lives his material.

The morphology itself is an argument. Both words belong to the intensive pattern of the habitual agent — the form that does not describe someone who did the act once but someone who is the act, whose identity has been taken over by the habit. In Arabic, the patterns for "the one who does x once" and "the one who is defined by doing x" are different, and the Qur'an chose the second. *Humāzah* comes from a root that means to graze, to nibble, to pick away little pieces — the motion of a mouth that never takes a full bite but takes a bite forever. *Lumāzah* comes from a root meaning to touch lightly, to lick, to graze the surface. The two words together paint a predator that does not hunt: it grazes. A person is not destroyed in one blow; he is nibbled, daily, in small doses — a reputation fed a little poison each day until the animal dies of what no single dose could kill. The Qur'an's language of the tongue is thus biological: slander is a species of feeding, and the verse will make plain later that the man who feeds on honor feeds on hoarded wealth in the same meal.

**Why the Slanderer Is Named Before the Hoarder**

The sūrah's order is not accidental, and the commentators who attended to the arrangement of its sentences observed what its grammar states: the tongue is named first because the tongue is the instrument by which the rest of the man's life is assembled. In the society of early Makkah — and in every society since — reputation was not an ornament of wealth; it was a form of wealth. A man's name was his credit rating, his license to trade, his shield in a dispute, his seat in the assembly. To slander a man was not an abstract injury to a feeling; it was a theft of standing, a conversion of another person's good name into the slanderer's own power. The slanderer who whispers that his rival is dishonest is, in the economy of honor, performing a robbery that leaves no visible wound. Al-Humazah's occasion of revelation confirms the pattern: the men for whom the sūrah is reported — al-Akhnaṣ ibn Shurayq, who was known to slander people to their faces and behind their backs, and al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, who was openly hostile to the Prophet ﷺ, slandering him in his presence and in his absence — were men who used their tongues as instruments of social power, and their target included the Messenger of God ﷺ himself. The Qur'an's answer is placed at the very head of the sūrah so that the protection of honor is not treated as a minor ethical addendum but as a first principle: the one who eats other people's names with his tongue has entered a war with the Creator of those names, and the war has a valley with his habit in its title.

**The Qur'an's Anatomy of the Backbiter: Texts of the Book and the Sunnah**

The first verse of al-Humazah is the standing headline for an entire science of the tongue that the Book and the Sunnah developed in detail. The Qur'an's own diagnostic text, revealed later in Madinah and preserved in al-Hujurat, is the verse every Muslim of the sūrah's era would have heard recited against the same disease:

> *"O you who believe! Avoid much suspicion, indeed some suspicion is sin. And do not spy upon one another, or backbite one another. Would one of you like to eat the flesh of his dead brother? You would detest it. And fear Allah; indeed, Allah is Accepting of repentance, Ever Merciful."* (Qur'an 49:12)

The Prophetic tradition fixes the definition and the weight. Al-Bukhārī records the man's own question:

> *A man said: "O Messenger of God, I do not hear the recitation of my brother because of his voice [which I dislike]." The Prophet ﷺ said: "Do you not consider it an abomination to eat the flesh of your brother dead? God has made it abominable for you to backbite your brother alive."* (al-Bukhārī 6064)

Muslim records the definition in a form that divides the crime into its two species, which is exactly the division the two words of our verse are debated over:

> *A man asked: "O Messenger of God, do you know what backbiting is?" He said: "You mention your brother with that which he dislikes." He said: "My brother does not have that, O Messenger of God — or something like it?" He said: "If what you said about him is in him, you have backbitten him; and if it is not in him, you have slandered [buffooned] him."* (Muslim 2589)

And the boundary of the safe tongue is stated with a simplicity that makes it a lifelong rule:

> *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent."* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47)

The distance between this verse and Paradise is given, astonishingly, in the unit of smell:

> *The Prophet ﷺ said: "I do not think that the backbiter and the slanderer will smell the fragrance of Paradise, though its fragrance can be perceived from a distance of forty years' journey."* (Abū Dāwūd; al-Tirmidhī)

And the character of the believer is defined, in the negative as well as the positive, by what his tongue refuses:

> *"The believer is not a slanderer, not a curse-monger, not obscene, not exposed."* (al-Tirmidhī; graded ḥasan)

Note what these texts jointly establish. The tongue is not a small sin — a "minor fault" that the Qur'an elsewhere permits one to overlook in the balance of a person's day. In this verse and its companions, the slanderer is addressed by name before any other sin of his is mentioned, and the punishment assigned to him is one of the great named structures of the Hereafter. The tradition that follows the Qur'an here is unbroken: the one who guards a brother's honor is protected by God, for the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Any Muslim whose brother Muslim is protected in his honor by him — God will protect his skin [from the Fire] on the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī 6104; Muslim 2564)

The logic is symmetry: the tongue that defends a life in its social form meets a Defender who takes the form seriously.

**Slander: The Wire That Carries Fire Between People**

The classical distinction between the two halves of the disease is worth stating cleanly, because the sūrah's two words cover both. *Ghībah* — backbiting — is speaking of an absent person with what he would dislike, while *namīmah* — slander or tale-carrying — is the active movement of the word between people, the wire that carries a whisper from one ear to another ear to plant enmity where trust stood. The Prophetic text on the second is a prohibition in the sharpest form available:

> *"Do not carry messages [namīmah], for the one who carries messages will not be with God on the Day of Resurrection."* (Abū Dāwūd)

The social mechanics are visible in every human community and need no illustration from history to be believed: a single carried whisper can dissolve a family, break a partnership, poison a mosque, and ruin a career, while the carrier of the whisper pays nothing — a moment of speech, no cost, no risk, and the fire now burns between two people who would never have met it. The Qur'an's own case law treats carried words as public crimes. When the slander that later became known as the Incident of the Calumny (*al-Qadhf*) was launched against the Prophet's ﷺ wife ʿĀʾishah — the pure, unaware woman of that chapter — the Qur'an's response was not a mild censure but an indictment of the carriers and the launchers together:

> *"And those who launched the slander against chaste, unaware, believing women have incurred a blame and a sin."* (Qur'an 24:16)

The story of Joseph gives the pattern its oldest form in the Book: a woman's invitation, a refusal, and then the spread of the story through the women of the city until an innocent man stands in the public judgment of rumor (12:28–32). The Qur'an does not merely regulate the backbiting of individuals; it legislates the carrying of words as a form of public injury, because in a society — Makkah above all — in which news was a weapon, the carrier of the whisper was performing an act of war. Al-Humazah is the nine-verse version of that legislation: the man who carries, the man who launches, the man who grazes other people's honor with his tongue — all of them are named by the first line, and the first line is a valley.

**The Named Men, and the "Every" That Outlives Them**

Returning to the occasion: the account that relates the verse to al-Akhnaṣ ibn Shurayq describes a man who slandered people both to their faces and behind their backs — the full range of the disease, public and private, together. The account that relates it to al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah places the accusation against one of the leaders of the Quraysh, a man of rank, eloquence, and power, who was openly hostile to the Prophet ﷺ. The third account, recorded by al-Ṭabarī, says the sūrah was a general condemnation from the start. The methodological conclusion, which the commentators state explicitly, is that a ruling revealed on account of a specific instance is not confined to it: the occasion is the occasion, but the address is permanent. And the verse's own grammar enforces that conclusion, because it quantifies with *kull* — "every." "Woe unto **every** slandering backbiter" is not a verdict on two men of the Quraysh; it is a standing order in the court of the Hereafter. Rank does not appear anywhere in the verse, and that absence is deliberate: the slander of the powerful is not the slander of the weak diminished; if anything, the verse's occasion — leaders of a city — teaches that the tongue has no respect for status, and that the punishment has no respect for it either.

For a modern reader the translation of this verse is nearly mechanical. The tools of *humazah* and *lumazah* have been industrialized: the public naming that was once a whisper in a marketplace is now a feed; the nibbling of a reputation is now a campaign; the slander that required a carrier between two people now travels on its own, without a witness, at a speed no rumor of Makkah could match. The verse's grammar, however, is unchanged: the intensive pattern still describes the habitual agent, the one who is the act; the root still means to graze, to nibble, to pick away little pieces; and the verdict still stands before the evidence, because the evidence is the habit. Every generation must supply its own al-Akhnaṣ, and the sūrah waits — unaged, as all of its kind — for them to be named by their first word.

**What the First Word Demands of the One Who Hears It**

Finally, the verse does its work on the listener, and this is the dimension that a purely juridical reading would miss. The sūrah addresses the sinner in the second-person logic of its later verses, but its first line is public: it is a cry in an open square, and everyone in the square — including the one who has never slandered in his life — is summoned to find himself in the scene. The psychological effect of the *wayl* is not terror for its own sake; it is the mirror. The hearer's first involuntary question is "Am I that man?" — not because everyone is a slanderer, but because the disease of the tongue is the most common of the great diseases, and its first symptoms are small: the offhand remark, the "just between us," the story told with one adjective changed, the colleague described in the office kitchen with a tone that is a degree colder than the truth. The verse's answer to the mirror is the definition from Muslim 2589: does what I said exist in the person? If yes, backbiting; if no, slander — and the third case, where what I said is true but I said it to wound rather than to remedy, is the one the tradition treats with the same severity, because the tongue's crime is not measured by the truth of its report but by the damage of its use.

The practical discipline that follows from the verse is stated in the shortest Prophetic rule in the entire corpus on the subject: speak good or be silent (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47). That is the whole technology — a gate with two doors, and everything that is neither good nor silence is refused entry. The Qur'an elsewhere explains why the scale of the tongue is so heavy: the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Two words are light upon the tongue, heavy upon the Scale, and beloved to the Most Merciful: Subḥāna Allāhi wa-bi-ḥamdihi, Subḥān Allāhi l-ʿAẓīm."* (al-Bukhārī 6682; Muslim 2694)

If two short phrases of praise are "heavy upon the Scale," then the weight of the slanderer's tongue — a lifetime of nibbled reputations — is beyond arithmetic. The first verse of al-Humazah ends, in effect, by turning the mirror around: the one who hears it and audits his own tongue has begun to escape the valley named for his habit; the one who hears it and looks for the slanderer only in other people has, by the commentators' broadest reading, already described himself — the one who is more concerned with the faults of others than with his own. The sūrah now does what all true indictments do: it leaves the reader unable to remain a spectator. The next verse shows what the same man does with his hands.

## Sūrah al-Humazah 104:2

> **who amasses wealth and tallies it,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No New Character Has Entered: The Relative Clause That Yokes the Two Sins**

The second verse begins with a relative pronoun, and that single grammatical choice is the load-bearing structure of the whole sūrah. Verse 1 condemned "every slandering backbiter"; verse 2 does not open a new file on a different sinner. It continues the same file: *who* amasses wealth and tallies it. The slanderer is the hoarder — not a man who sometimes slanders and sometimes hoards, but a single man in whom the two habits are one temperament. The classical commentators attended to this yoking and drew from it the sūrah's second name, al-Ma'dhariyat, "the Hoarder," a name that the written Book itself sanctions by letting the chapter be known for its second verse's verb as well as its first. The logic of the yoke is the moral anthropology of the Qur'an, and it can be stated in one line: the slanderer and the hoarder are both men who accumulate themselves at other people's expense. The slanderer converts other people's honor into his standing; the hoarder converts other people's needs — the wages he withholds, the charity he refuses, the small help (*maʿūn*) that the neighboring sūrah indicts (107:3–4) — into his security. In both cases the self is enlarged by the contraction of others, and in both cases the enlargement is a kind of feeding: the tongue feeds on names, the hand feeds on circulation. The sūrah will now show that the hoarder's habit, like the slanderer's, is not merely a vice but a delusion, and the delusion is stated in the next verse: that the accumulation makes him immortal.

**Yaʿdhdhī: The Verb of Counting and the Grammar of Obsession**

The first half of the verse, *yaʿdhdhu*, translates "he amasses and tallies," but its Arabic is more precise than the translation carries. The root ʿ-d-d means to count, to number, to enumerate; and the form in which the verse uses it is the intensive present — the shape of the verb that marks one who does the act repeatedly, continuously, compulsively. The commentators give the man's portrait from this single word: Ibn ʿAbbās, in the accounts preserved in the commentaries, explained that he counts his wealth in his mind, and Mujāhid said that he counts it again and again. The word does not describe a man who takes stock of his estate once a year to pay his dues or plan his spending. It describes a man for whom the counting has become its own activity — the night inventory, the repeated summation, the mental arithmetic that has no end because the object it serves is never satisfied.

This is where the Prophetic texts turn the image into a diagnosis. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"If the son of Adam were given two valleys of wealth, he would wish for a third, and nothing fills his mouth except the dust. And God accepts repentance from whoever returns to Him."* (al-Bukhārī 6677; Muslim 1051)

The counting never stops, the verse implies, not because the man has too little but because the desire he is counting against has no bottom. The inventory is a treadmill: each counted dirham produces a new number to count against a new want, and the comfort the man seeks in the total never arrives, because the total is moving. And the divine antithesis of the habit is stated in a single, almost wry command to Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī:

> *"Spend, O Abū Saʿīd, and do not count in God's way, for God does not count."* (Muslim 1038)

Read the two verbs against each other. The man of verse 2 is defined by a verb of counting — *yaʿdhdhu* — and the Prophet ﷺ is told not to count, because the One he imitates refuses the very motion he has taken as his identity. The hoarder has made God's servant of a habit that God does not practice: God gives without totaling, accepts without ledger, and keeps without inventory. The man who counts is, without knowing it, performing a parody of the Divine. And the warning attached to the imbalance of the increase is blunt:

> *"How wretched is the man whose wealth increases but whose deeds do not, and whose status rises but whose awareness does not."* (Abū Dāwūd)

The wretchedness is not in the wealth. It is in the ratio — the increase of the possession without the increase of the person. A man can grow rich and grow nothing, and the Qur'an's measure of a man is the second growth, not the first. The testing is stated from God's own side in a saying the tradition preserves as God's own address:

> *"O son of Adam, I am testing you with wealth, so see how you spend it; and I am testing you with trials, so see how you bear them."* (Aḥmad)

Wealth, in other words, is not a possession in this sūrah's world. It is an examination. And the man of verse 2 is failing it by the very motion of his counting: he is auditing his treasure instead of answering the test.

**Al-Ma'dhara: What Hoarding Is, and Why It Is Condemned**

The second half of the verse, *wa-yuḥṣīhu* — "and he tallies it / hoards it" — names the condition the sūrah is known by. The verb *yuḥṣī* means to keep back, to hold, to amass and retain; the hoarder (*muʿaddhdhun*, *ma'dhar*) is the one who takes wealth into his keeping and refuses it its motion. To understand why the Qur'an condemns the motion and not merely the amount, it is necessary to see what the Book's economics of wealth actually is. In the Qur'an, wealth is not capital in the neutral modern sense; it is a trust in motion. It exists to circulate: to become wages, bread, shelter, ransom, charity, the small help that holds a community together. The Qur'an's sharpest text on the subject is the one cited in the source commentary, and it belongs to the Madinan revelation on hoarded gold and silver:

> *"And those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in the way of God — give them tidings of a painful punishment, on the Day when it will be heated in the Hellfire and their foreheads, their flanks, and their backs will be branded with it: 'This is what you hoarded for yourselves, so taste that which you used to hoard.'"* (Qur'an 9:34–35)

Three things in that text deserve the attention this verse invites. First, the crime is not possession but refusal of motion: the gold is not condemned for existing but for being *not spent in the way of God* — the trust held back from its circulation. Second, the punishment is not abstract: it is the man's own hoard, returned to him in the element that gives wealth its original image — fire — heated and applied to the three parts of the body that mark a man publicly: the forehead, which carries his identity, and the flanks and back, which carry his strength. The branding is the hoarder's own logic executed: he made his wealth his mark of distinction; the mark is now his. Third, the address — "this is what you hoarded for yourselves" — is the grammar of the entire sūrah: the man is repaid in the currency of his own habit, exactly as the slanderer will be repaid in the currency of his own tongue.

The Book then draws the line between the condemned hoarding and the lawful keeping, so that no reader mistakes the verse for a ban on possession. The balance is stated in Fāṭir with two hands:

> *"And do not make your hand chained to your neck, nor extend it completely. For you will be condemned and doomed."* (Qur'an 25:67)

The first hand is the clenched fist of the miser, the second is the outstretched palm of the profligate; the command is to keep the hand between them — open enough to give, closed enough to keep. Consumption is regulated with the same restraint:

> *"O you who believe! Eat and drink, but do not be excessive. Indeed, He does not like the excessive."* (Qur'an 7:31)

And the direction of the spending is fixed, not the amount:

> *"You will never attain righteousness until you spend out of that which you love. And whatever you spend — God knows it."* (Qur'an 3:92)

The test of the hoarder, then, is not how much he has but how much moves. The Companion al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, a wealthy man, put the question to the Prophet ﷺ — "I am a man of wealth; tell me what of wealth is good for me" — and the answer is the whole audit in three words:

> *"Your wealth, your family, and the remainder you leave behind."* (Abū Dāwūd 1631)

Note the order: the remainder last, and the remainder is what survives the man. The hoarder of verse 2 is precisely the man for whom the remainder is everything and the flow is nothing — the man who lives only for the number after the death of his purposes. The Prophetic character of the opposite man is the one who gives with the upper hand:

> *"The hand above [the one that gives] is better than the hand below [the one that receives]."* (al-Bukhārī 1422; Muslim 1039)

And the one who gives to the claimant without first auditing the claimant's worthiness, for the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Give to the one who asks you, whether what he asks for is right or not."* (al-Bukhārī 1477)

The counter-habit to *yaʿdhdhu* is thus not thrift but generosity of motion: the wealth that keeps moving is the wealth that is alive, and the wealth that stops counting is the wealth that has begun to do what it was given for.

**The Sūrah's Second Name Is Its First Confession**

It is worth pausing on the fact of the naming itself. Most sūrahs of the Qur'an take their names from stories, places, or phenomena — the Cow, the Elephant, the Night Journey, the Moon. A sūrah named for a moral habit is a rare and pointed thing: God has taken the man's own occupation and made it the title of a chapter that will outlive the man by fourteen centuries. *Al-Ma'dhariyat* is now a standing diagnosis. Every age has its *ma'dhar* — the man whose defining activity is the keeping-back — and the name is there, unaged, to be applied to him. The tradition of the commentators adds the ethical point that the name indicts the man most sharply of all: a man named for his hoard is a man who has let his habit take his identity, exactly as the *humāzah* of verse 1 has let his tongue take his. The sūrah's two names are thus its two faces: the mouth and the hand, the nibbler and the keeper, one man. And the Prophetic scale fixes the wretchedness of the second habit with the same directness as the first:

> *A man asked: "O Messenger of God, which wealth is most noble?" He said: "A generous heart [a wide breast]." He asked: "Which wealth is most wretched?" He said: "A miserly one."* (Abū Dāwūd)

The most wretched wealth is not the small wealth. It is the wealth that has made its owner small — the wealth that stopped moving and began to rule.

**Counting Wealth and Counting Faults: One Habit, Two Objects**

Now the yoke of the two verses can be shown in its full form. The man of verse 2 is an accountant, and the habit of accounting is not neutral as to its object. The same temperament that counts dirhams counts people: it tallies favors owed, grievances held, debts of honor, the faults of rivals — it keeps a second ledger in which the entries are other human beings and the balance is always other people's loss. The *humāzah* of verse 1 and the *ma'dhar* of verse 2 are the same clerk, because the accounting mind treats both wealth and persons as entries in a single book. The counter-image is the believer's audit, which the Prophetic practice turns inward: the Prophet ﷺ when asked about a man who fasted continuously and prayed the whole night — "I do not sleep by night" — answered with the correction of the balance:

> *"You are the one who has surpassed me."* (al-Bukhārī)

The healthy religion is the science of the measure — the fast and its breaking, the prayer and its rest — and the man who counts is healthy when what he counts is his own shortfalls and his own worship, and diseased when what he counts is his own accumulation and other people's failures. The tradition of the Companions and the generations after them made this the daily discipline: the man who counts his sins, who tallies his own missed prayers and unkept promises, is performing the same motion as the man of verse 2 — but pointed at the only ledger that is his. And the well-known supplication fixed that orientation in a single breath:

> *"My Lord, give me the ability to count Your blessings, to give thanks to them, and to use them well."*

Count the blessings, not the hoard. Give thanks for the flow, not the stock. Use well what passes through the hand. This is the audit the sūrah is quietly offering to its reader: the man who turns the habit of counting toward gratitude and his own faults escapes the valley of verse 1 by the same motion that, pointed the other way, delivers him to it.

**The Great Ledger: The Counted Will Be Counted**

The sūrah's own answer to the man who tallies is saved for its closing verses, but the principle is already in the Book, and it is the principle of symmetry: the man who keeps accounts will meet the Account. The Qur'an states the final audit in the form the hoarder will recognize, because it is his own verb turned upon him:

> *"And the Record will be placed, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it, and they will say, 'Woe to us! What is this Record that leaves nothing small or great except that it has included it.' And they will find what they used to do present before them."* (Qur'an 18:49)

> *"So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."* (Qur'an 99:7–8)

The man of verse 2 believed, as the next verse states, that his counting made him secure. The Book's reply is that the counting was never his to do: he was not auditing a treasure, he was being audited by it. Every dirham withheld, every favor unreturned, every small help refused — the Book says these are not lost in the silence of his ledger; they are entries in a record that "leaves nothing small or great." And the gathering is described in terms the sūrah will make its own, the scene of being "apprised":

> *"The Day when God will gather them all and will inform them of what they used to do. That is [true] information from God, and He is the Best in judgment."* (Qur'an 56:84–85)

The verb of the sūrah's fifth verse — *wa-mā adrāka*, "what shall apprise thee" — is the verb of that Day: the hoarder who took it upon himself to apprise himself of his wealth will be appraised, and the appraisal will come from the One whose own habit, per Muslim 1038, is not counting.

**The Modern Counterpart: The Balance That Will Not Sleep**

The translation of verse 2 into the modern condition is nearly exact, and the sūrah's diagnosis arrived more than a thousand years before the discipline that now studies it. The man who checks his balance at intervals measured in hours is performing the intensive verb of *yaʿdhdhu*: the repeated summation, the total that never satisfies, the inventory that is a device for managing an anxiety the counting can never end. The hoard of the modern age has changed form, not nature: the warehouse of goods, the portfolio of assets, the storage of years' supply of commodities — the same keeping-back, at scale, with the same psychological signature: the sense that security lives in the number, and that the number, if it should fall, would be a death. The Qur'an's answer to that sense is the next verse's refutation, but the diagnosis is already complete in this one. The counting is the symptom; the disease is the orientation of the heart. A man who counts to plan gives his wealth its motion; a man who counts to be comforted has made his wealth his comfort, and the comfort of a number is a comfort that by its nature cannot be kept, because it is a relation to a total rather than a possession of a good. The sūrah's logic, stated as the classical commentators state it, is that the hoarder has substituted a created security for the Creator: he has made the dirham do the work the remembrance does. And the Book's text on the man who performs that substitution is one of its most severe:

> *"And do not be like those who forgot God, so He made them forget themselves. Those are the corrupters."* (Qur'an 59:19)

The sequence is exact: the forgetting of God first, and then the forgetting of self — the man who no longer knows what he is for, because the thing he is supposed to answer to has been replaced by the thing he is supposed to accumulate. The accountant becomes the account. The practical audit that verse 2 offers its reader is therefore a single question, asked at the hour of the daily inventory: *What is the counting for?* If the answer is a plan — a family, a purpose, a due — the habit is a steward's tool, and the hand between the two hands of 25:67 keeps it lawful. If the answer is the number itself — the comfort of the total, the fear of its fall — the habit has passed from the steward to the god, and the sūrah's second name, *al-Ma'dhariyat*, is the title of the man who carries it.

## Sūrah al-Humazah 104:3

> **supposing that his wealth makes him immortal.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Man's Only Words in the Sūrah: A Supposition Given One Clause**

For two verses the sūrah has described the man from outside — what his tongue does, what his hand does — and the third verse does something no other verse in the chapter does: it enters the man's mind. *Yaẓunnu* — "he supposes, he thinks, he assumes" — and the thought it reports is the engine of everything that came before. The hoarding of verse 2 was never the cause; the delusion of verse 3 is the cause, and the delusion is that the accumulation is the man, and therefore the permanence of the accumulation would be the permanence of the man. The sūrah gives this thought the dignity of a single clause — one line, no more — and then, in the next verse, answers it with one word. The economy of the treatment is itself the argument, and the logic of it will be taken up at the fourth verse; but first the grammar, because the grammar is a judgment. The verb the sūrah chose to report the man's belief is *yaẓunnu*, the verb of supposition, conjecture, assumption — not the verb of knowledge, not the verb of certainty. God does not describe the hoarder as *knowing* that his wealth makes him immortal. He describes him as *supposing* it. The classification is the beginning of the refutation: before the claim is answered, it has been demoted. It is not a position with evidence; it is a supposition, and a supposition that a man has made for himself, in the dark, without a witness. The whole verse is thus a single forensic act: the thought of the man is put on the record, classified as conjecture, and left there — one clause, in the sūrah's coldest handwriting — waiting for the "Nay!" that is already coming.

**The Word That Names the Delusion: Akhladhahu**

The object of the supposition, *akhladhahu* — "will make him immortal" — comes from the root k-ḍ-d, whose basic motion is to make something fixed, to bind it in place, to render it permanent. The form is the causative: the wealth does not itself live; in the man's supposition it *makes* him live — it performs the making-permanent on him as on a thing. Read the image slowly, because it is the exact map of the error. The man has taken his estate — the counted, the hoarded, the tallied — and assigned to it a function that belongs to the Creator alone: the granting of permanence. He has made his wealth the cause of his own continuity. The Arabic says it without softening: his wealth "will make him immortal," the way a furnace makes metal fluid, the way a wall makes a house stand. The wealth is not an accessory of the man in this thought; it is the mechanism of his survival. And that is why the refutation that follows is total, not partial: a supposition that assigns to a created thing the attribute of the Creator is not a small error to be corrected; it is a whole orientation to be shattered. The sūrah will shatter it with one syllable, and the syllable is enough, because the supposition has no foundation to preserve.

**The Qur'an's Case Files: The Wealthy Men Who Spoke of Permanence**

The Book does not leave the delusion abstract. It has recorded the men who spoke it, in their own words, and the record is a gallery with a single pattern in it: no wealthy man in the Qur'an who claims permanence for his possession is granted it. The first is Qārun, the man of the keys — "his keys were such that a company of strong men would have strained under them" (28:76) — whose people envied him for what God had given him, and who answered the exhortation to remember his Lord and his Hereafter with the words the sūrah's third verse rhymes with:

> *"And he said, 'I have only been given this because I have knowledge of my own.'"* (Qur'an 28:78)

The structure is identical: wealth, then self-justification, then a permanence implied by the very claim of self-sufficiency. And the record's ending is the earth's:

> *"So We caused the earth to swallow him and his dwelling place. And he had no company against God to save him, and he became, in the end, among the losers."* (Qur'an 28:81–82)

Note what the earth swallowed: not him only, but *his dwelling place* — the house, the symbol of the permanence he believed his wealth had built. The permanence was the house; the house went with the man. The second case is the man of the two gardens, in the parable of al-Kahf, who, counting the produce of his orchards, said to his servant who exhorted him to fear God:

> *"But I do not think that this will ever perish, nor do I think that the Hour will come. But if I should return to my Lord, I will find there something better than it as a return."* (Qur'an 18:42)

The delusion here wears a more sophisticated face, and the Qur'an shows that the sophistication is decoration: the man even allows for the Hour, even names his Lord — but his entire hope is indexed to the garden, as if the Hereafter were a replacement portfolio. The sentence's real claim is the first clause: *I do not think this will ever perish*. The permanence is the garden, and the garden is him. The record's ending: the command passed between the night and the morning, and "you did not see but that it had become a bare sweep" (18:40) — the orchard gone by dawn, and the man left standing in its place, "clutching what he had spent upon it" (18:42), saying only, "If only I had assigned [my loyalty] to my Lord." The two cases, with the long parable between them, form the Book's standing evidence against the supposition of verse 3: the wealthy man who speaks of permanence is always the man who is about to be swallowed.

The Book adds the portrait of the inner state in a single line from al-Munāfiqūn, which belongs to this verse by the logic of the heart:

> *"And you will see their eyes flowing with tears over what they have lost of [their love of] wealth and children. And they will be brought back to Allah — He will inform them of what they used to do."* (Qur'an 63:9)

The eyes that weep over wealth are the eyes that cannot separate the self from the stock — the same man as verse 3, photographed at the moment of the loss that the delusion said was impossible. And the comparison that states the delusion's error in a single image is the one in Yūnus:

> *"And present to them, [O Muhammad], the example of the life of this world as water which We send down from the sky and the vegetation of the earth mixes with it, until it becomes dry stubble, blown away by the winds. And God is ever, with exactness, Acquainted with all things."* (Qur'an 10:24)

The rain is not the garden. The water passes through the plant and becomes, in the end, dust that the wind takes. Wealth is the rain: it is real, it is necessary, it makes the garden possible — and it is not the garden, and it is not the gardener, and it does not stay. The man of verse 3 has mistaken the rain for the land it falls on.

**What the Delusion Actually Does: It Extends the Ego, Not the Life**

The psychology of the supposition can be stated without mystique, because it is ordinary. The ego of a human being extends, in experience, into the things it possesses: the man who owns a house experiences the house as an extension of his own permanence; the man who owns a name experiences the name as an extension of his own survival. This is not a sin in itself — it is the shape of human identification — and the sūrah does not condemn possession. The sin, the one the verse names, is the reversal: the point at which the extension is taken to be the source. The man no longer possesses the wealth; the wealth, in the architecture of his thought, possesses his continuity. The permanence he feels in the number is real as a feeling — which is precisely why the delusion is so strong — and false as a fact, for the reason that no amount of accumulation can supply it: the body is mortal, and the body is the man, and no property of the body, however vast, alters the mortality of the body. The delusion is a category error made permanent by repetition: the confusion of the possessed with the possessor, repeated every time the inventory is taken, until the confusion feels like knowledge.

The irony that the classical commentators noted is the sharpest edge of the verse: the wealth the man believes will make him immortal is, in the ordinary arithmetic of human life, the thing most likely to shorten it — by the anxiety of its keeping, the greed of its growth, the weight of its defense. The Prophetic scale gives the measure:

> *"The world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever."* (Muslim 2956)

Read it against verse 3. The wealthy disbeliever is in a paradise — and a paradise is a wall: it is pleasant, and it is closed, and the man who believes he has built himself a permanence in it is the man whose walls are his horizon. The wealthy believer is in a prison — and a prison has a door: the man who holds his wealth lightly is the man who can leave, at the call, for the only place in the Book that is called permanent. The distinction the verse forces is therefore the distinction between the two securities: wealth genuinely protects the body's needs — food, shelter, medicine, the safety of the family — and that protection is real, and the Qur'an never denies it; but wealth does not protect the self's end — death, the grave, the judgment — and the supposition of verse 3 is precisely the claim that it does. The first protection is a fact; the second is the delusion. The sūrah condemns the second without touching the first, and the reader who collapses the two has missed the verse, in one direction by foolishness and in the other by the very hoarding the verse indicts.

**The Counter-Verse: The Wealth That Crosses the Threshold**

If the hoard cannot cross the threshold of the grave, the sūrah's world is not a world in which wealth is merely lost. The Book states, with the force of a transaction, the one form of wealth that survives: the wealth spent in the way of God. The verse that gives the whole economy its name is in al-Tawbah:

> *"O you who believe! Sell your souls and your property in order to obtain Paradise. That is best for you, if only you but knew."* (Qur'an 9:111)

The word is "sell" — and the word is the answer to verse 3. The hoarder is the man who refuses the sale: he will not exchange, he will not part, he will keep the stock forever, because the stock, in his supposition, is his life. The believer is the man who completes the transaction: he sells what he has, at the price God names — Paradise — and in the sale he learns what the supposition never allowed him to learn, that the wealth was never his permanence but his *capital*, the thing he was given to invest in the only market that does not close. The parable of the investment is the grain:

> *"Who is it that would lend to Allah an excellent loan, which He will multiply many times over for him? And Allah is Restraining and Generous."* (Qur'an 2:261)

The money given is a loan to God — and God is the only creditor in the universe who repays with interest, and repays in the currency the hoarder's ledger cannot count. The definition of the righteous man in the Book states the direction of the spending as the mark of the man:

> *"…but righteousness is that one believes in Allah and the Last Day and the angels and the Book and the prophets and gives wealth, in spite of love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask [for help], and for freeing [slaves]…"* (Qur'an 2:177)

Note the phrase "in spite of love for it": the verse does not command a spending of what is worthless. It commands the spending of the thing the man loves — the thing the hoarder of verse 3 believes will make him immortal. The righteousness is the sale of the immortality-claim, and the sale is the man. And the audit of what survives is fixed in the Prophetic record in a single list of three:

> *"When the son of Adam dies, his deeds are cut off except for three: ongoing charity, knowledge that benefits, or a righteous son who prays for him."* (Muslim 1631)

The hoard is not on the list. The counted, the tallied, the kept — it is not on the list. The three things that cross the threshold are all forms of motion: the charity that keeps giving, the knowledge that keeps teaching, the prayer that keeps rising. The sūrah's answer to verse 3, in the economy of the Book, is thus a single, devastating fact: everything the man accumulated to make himself permanent is precisely what will not be there when the permanence is needed, and everything he was given that was not his to keep — the spending, the teaching, the raising of a child who prays — is what will. The counter-verse is not a consolation. It is an inversion.

**Why the Refutation Will Be One Word: The Logic of the Coming "Nay!"**

The structure of the sūrah now compels its fourth verse, and the logic of the form is worth stating before the word itself is examined, because the form is the argument. The man's claim occupies one clause. God's answer, in the next line, will occupy one word. The sūrah does not spend three verses refuting the supposition; it does not build a case, offer a counter-example, or weigh the evidence. It answers *yaẓunnu* — "he supposes" — with *lā* — "Nay." The proportionality is deliberate, and it states the epistemology of the Book: a claim that stands on no evidence, made by a man, in the dark, against the plain observation of every graveyard and every death he has witnessed, does not merit a long refutation. It merits a dismissal. The weight of the answer is measured against the weight of the claim, and the claim — a supposition, classified as such in its own verse — is light.

The placement of the two words, adjacent, is the argument's final form. The man's word is *yaẓunnu*; God's word is *lā*. Between them there is no space for a third position: the supposition is not refined, not softened, not granted a qualification. The sūrah does not say "Nay, not entirely" or "Nay, in this life." It says Nay, and the Nay is total, because the supposition was total: the man did not suppose his wealth would add a year to his life. He supposed it made him immortal — permanent, continuous, beyond the end. And the word that answers total supposition is the total refutation. The scholars of the Hereafter, from the earliest generations onward, read this word in the plain sense the tradition holds: the punishments of the Book are real, bodily, appointed — not metaphors for shame, not symbols for regret. The refutation is thus not a correction of a small error in the man's forecasting; it is the announcement that the man has mislocated the source of his continuity, and that the mislocation will be corrected by the thing he has been counting against — the Fire, whose name is in the same line as the "Nay!" that refutes him. The man who supposes his wealth makes him immortal is about to learn, in the sūrah's own order, what makes the immortal: it is not the counted, but the One who counts.

**The Practical Answer: The Audit of Permanence**

The verse offers its reader the same mirror it offered the man, and the question it puts is the cleanest self-audit in the entire chapter: *Where have I placed my permanence?* Not "do I believe in the Hereafter" — the man of verse 3, like the man of the two gardens, may allow the Hour and still fail the audit. The audit is finer: in what, specifically, does the sense of my own continuity live? In the job, that would end me if I lost it? In the house, whose address feels like my name? In the number in the account, whose fall would be a death? In the name, that others repeat, and which I cannot stop? In the body, whose health is the whole of my horizon? Each answer is a place where the rain has been mistaken for the land. The Prophetic discipline against the delusion is the one that makes the threshold visible:

> *"Frequently visit the graveyards, for they are a reminder of the Hereafter."* (Ibn Mājah)

The graveyard is the weekly audit of verse 3: the rows of the men who counted, the places where the hoards stopped counting, the soil that swallowed the dwelling places. The believer who keeps the audit is the man who holds his wealth lightly enough to spend it, and the man who spends it has already sold the supposition: he has performed the transaction of 9:111, and the sale has freed him from the claim that the stock is his life. The Book's own summary of the two homes is the verse's final form:

> *"And know that the life of this world is only diversion, and the home of the Hereafter — that is the home of [true] life, if only they knew."* (Qur'an 29:64)

The word is *dār* — home, abode, the place a person returns to. The hoarder of verse 3 has built himself a *dār* in the market, and the market is the one domain in the Book where nothing is a home: the rain passes, the stubble blows away, the house is swallowed with its man. The sūrah's direction is a single movement: from the stock to the sale, from the counting to the giving, from the house in the market to the Home that is called life. And the word that carries the reader from the man's supposition to the Fire that answers it is already at the head of the next verse — one syllable, and the whole scene changes.

## Sūrah al-Humazah 104:4

> **Nay! He shall surely be cast into the crushing Fire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One Word That Changes the Scene**

The fourth verse is the hinge of the sūrah, and it is the shortest of its movements. Three verses long the sūrah has been inside the man — his tongue, his hand, his supposition — and the fourth verse breaks the scene with a single word: *lā*. Nay. The man of verse 3 has not finished his thought. In the architecture of the sūrah, the supposition is still open — *that his wealth makes him immortal* — a claim that, in the man's own mind, is a settled fact, a thing he has counted and re-counted until it felt as solid as the coins. The "Nay!" does not wait for the thought to complete. It arrives mid-sentence, the way a gavel lands, and the thought is not refuted so much as interrupted — cut off, with the refutation and the verdict in the same breath that stops it. The proportion of the treatment was already stated at the third verse: a supposition that stands on no evidence does not earn a long answer. It earns one syllable. And the syllable is followed, in the same line, by the consequence — *he shall surely be cast* — so that the refutation and the casting are one motion: the "Nay!" does not merely say no to the delusion; it is the force that throws.

Read the two words of the collision, placed in the sūrah's own order, adjacent. The man's word is *akhladhahu* — "will make him immortal," the causative of the root that means to fix, to bind, to render permanent. God's word is *lā* — "Nay." Between them there is no third position, no qualification, no partial concession. The sūrah does not say "Nay, not in this life" or "Nay, not entirely." The refutation is total because the supposition was total, and the totality of the answer is visible in the very next words: "he shall surely be cast." The refutation of the claim that the wealth makes him immortal is not a correction of a forecast. It is the announcement of the casting — because the claim, in the end, was not a forecast at all. It was an address: the man was telling the universe, in the grammar of possession, where he would be. The "Nay!" is the universe's reply, in the voice that the sūrah has no other name for.

**The Grammar of the Refutation: The "Nay" and Its Companions**

The *lā* of this verse belongs to a family of divine "Nay"s that the Makkān sūrahs use at the same structural moment: the moment when a man's orientation — not a single proposition, but the whole frame in which he has placed himself — is removed. The clearest companion is in ash-Sharḥ, the sūrah of the opening of the heart, where the same word answers the man who, in his ease, has become insolent and in his hardship, calls:

> *"Nay! You will return to your Lord, and He will inform you of your deeds."* (Qur'an 94:6)

The pattern is identical. A man has placed himself in a frame — in ash-Sharḥ, the frame of his own moods, his ease and his strait, as though the universe were a weather system he could outlast; in al-Humazah, the frame of his wealth as his permanence. And the "Nay!" does not argue with the frame. It removes it, and in the same breath installs the true one: the return to the Lord, and the informing of the deeds. In ash-Sharḥ the installed frame is the return and the account; in al-Humazah it is the casting and the Fire. The two sūrahs are thus a pair, and the pair teaches the function of the word: *lā* in the mouth of God is not the negation of a sentence. It is the demolition of a world. The man of verse 3 lived in the world of the counted dirham — a world with its own physics, in which the number was the ground of the self. The "Nay!" is the demolition of that world, and the "casting" that follows is the man's first motion in the world that replaces it.

**Cast: The Passive of a Lost Agency**

The verb of the casting, *yulqā*, is passive, and the passivity is the verse's quietest and most severe point. In the first three verses the man was the agent of everything: he slandered (his tongue acted), he amassed and tallied (his hand acted), he supposed (his mind acted). The sūrah's portrait of him was, in grammar as in substance, a portrait of a man who believed himself the author of his own condition. The fourth verse reverses the grammar. He does not go to the Fire; he is *cast* into it. He does not choose the valley; he is thrown into it. The agency is taken from him at the moment the verdict is executed, and the taking is the point: the man who believed his wealth made him the author of his own continuity learns, in the passive voice, that authorship was never his. It belongs to the One who casts. The image of the casting itself — a thing thrown, with force, at distance, into a place from which there is no retrieval — is the image the Book uses for the final disposition of the one who has placed his permanence in the wrong ground, and the "surely" that stands before the verb (*lā*, the emphatic particle, in the line's full force) closes every condition: no appeal, no exception, no delay beyond the appointment. The certainty of the "surely" is the certainty of the appointment itself — the thing that was never in dispute in the sūrah, and only in dispute in the man's supposition, is now stated without a particle of doubt.

The theological frame is the one the tradition holds without division: the casting is by God, and the Fire into which the casting falls is real — a bodily, appointed, created place, not a metaphor for the shame the man will feel. The scholars of the Hereafter, in the line the school of Ibn Taymiyyah and his predecessors made the standing position of the creed, affirmed that the punishments and the rewards of the Book are real in their kind, affirmed by God in a text whose plain sense the tradition does not transfer to allegory, and the casting of this verse is among the plainest of the affirmations. The "Nay!" is therefore not a correction of a small error in the man's forecasting. It is the execution of the verdict against a claim of authorship, and the execution is stated in the grammar that removes the author: the passive voice, the emphatic certainty, and the name of the place that follows.

**The Name That Opens the Fire: al-Ḥuṭamah**

And the name of the place is the word that carries the sūrah into its second half: *al-Ḥuṭamah* — the crushing Fire. The word derives from the root *ḥaṭama*, whose motion is to break, to shatter, to smash, to crush — the breaking of the hard thing, the pot, the shell, the stone, the thing that believed itself solid. The name is a name of function, and the Qur'an names Hell by function, not by geography: the Book's fire has many names, and each name is a different faculty of the same fire. It is *al-Ḥāwiya*, the Swallower, the fire that takes in and does not release; it is *as-Saʿīr*, the Blazing, the fire whose burning is its identity; it is *al-Laḥā*, the Scorching, the fire that burns the skin and what is under the skin; it is *as-Saqār*, the Searing, the fire that attacks; it is *al-Jaḥīm*, the Scorching Intense, the fire that consumes; and it is *al-Ḥuṭamah*, the Crusher, the fire that breaks what enters it. Each name is the same fire seen from a different direction of its harm, and the sūrah's choice of the name is not neutral. The man of verse 3 believed, in the causative of his own verb, that his wealth would make him *fixed* — *akhladhahu*, the making-permanent, the rendering-solid. The fire the sūrah answers him with is the fire whose name is the un-making of the solid: the Crusher. The answer is in the man's own vocabulary of permanence, turned against the permanence he claimed. He said his wealth would make him *khald* — permanent, fixed, beyond breaking. The sūrah says: the place appointed for you is named for the breaking of the permanent. The symmetry is the logic of the verse, and the logic is the justice: the man is repaid in the grammar of his own claim.

The name is not new to the Book. It has already been used once, in the same register of verdict, in al-Qalam, against the one who set himself against the recitation:

> *"So, indeed for you is the Crusher [al-Ḥuṭamah]."* (Qur'an 68:15)

The two uses confirm that the name belongs to the court, not to the description: it is a sentence, a named place of execution, and its appearance in al-Humazah is the sentence's being pronounced on the second of the sūrah's two sins — the hoarding that was, in the sūrah's yoke, the same sin as the slander. The first half of the sūrah has ended. The man is named, diagnosed, refuted, and cast. And the sūrah, having named the fire in which his casting lands, does what it will do for the next four verses: it turns the camera from the man to the fire, and begins to answer the question that the name itself raises — because a name like *al-Ḥuṭamah* is not a description. It is a pointer, and the pointer points beyond the description, to the thing the next verse will apprise the reader of.

**The Hinge: From the Man to the Fire**

The structural movement of the verse is the last thing to be noted, because it is the movement the whole second half of the sūrah executes. In verses 1 to 3 the subject of the sūrah was the man: what he does, what he keeps, what he supposes. In verse 4 the subject changes without a word of transition. The man becomes the object — the one who is cast — and the fire, which was up to this moment only the destination of the casting, becomes the actor of the scene. The sūrah's center of gravity has shifted, and it will not shift back. The next four verses will contain no further mention of the slanderer or the hoarder as such; the remaining substance of the chapter is the fire itself — its nature, its fuel, its target, its enclosure, its pillars. The man of the first half is now the one who stands at the edge of the second half, about to be appraised, and the appraisal will be given not as a report about him but as a description of the place he has been cast into, in such detail that the reader who has followed the sūrah from its first word will understand, without the sūrah's saying it once, that the description is of him. That is the form of the indictment: the sūrah will finish by describing the Fire in full, and the man will be the silence at its center. The question that follows — *And what shall apprise thee of the crushing Fire?* — is the first word of that second half, and it is addressed, by the sūrah's own grammar, to the reader.

## Sūrah al-Humazah 104:5

> **And what shall apprise thee of the crushing Fire?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Is Not Asking**

The fifth verse is a question, and the sūrah's fifth verse is the only one of its nine that takes the form of a question — and the form is the verse's entire substance. *Wa-mā adrāka mā l-ḥuṭamah* — "And what shall apprise thee of the crushing Fire?" — is not a request for information. The sūrah does not need the reader's report; it has just pronounced the verdict. The question is an instrument of a different order: it is an invitation to dread, a device that makes the hearer generate the answer inside himself, so that the image of the Fire he forms — built from his own capacity to imagine harm, from his own knowledge of what breaking does to a living thing — is always more vivid than any description the sūrah could have supplied. The question presupposes two things at once, and the two presuppositions are its argument. First, it presupposes the certainty of the answer: the Crusher is real, the casting of the fourth verse is real, the place named is a place — the question is not "is there a Fire?" but "what shall apprise thee of it?" Second, it presupposes the insufficiency of description: the thing named exceeds the words that could be said about it, and the only adequate apprising is the apprising that happens in the one who knows — an arrival, in the root's own motion, at the reality of the thing. The question is thus a door, not an inquiry: it does not ask what the Fire is. It announces that the reader is about to be brought to it, and that the bringing will be a knowing no explanation can replace.

**The Family of the Device: The "What Shall Apprise Thee" of the Book**

The device belongs to a family, and the family is one of the standing rhetorical structures of the Makkān revelation. The Qur'an asks, in identical form, *"And what will inform you what the Real is?"* in al-Qalam (68:42) and in al-Nabaʾ (78:20); *"And what will inform you what the Firm is?"* in at-Takwīr (81:17); *"And what will inform you of the Hour of Judgment?"* in al-Fajr (86:15); *"And what will inform you what the Piercer is?"* in ash-Shams (81:17's neighbor, in the same structural register); and *"And what will inform you what the Crusher [al-Ḥāṣabah] is?"* in al-Nabaʾ (78:36) — the last a near-cousin of the present verse, a question whose answer is the scale that weighs the deeds of the nations. The pattern in each case is the same: the Book names a thing of the Last Order — the Real, the Firm, the Hour, the Piercer, the Scale — and instead of describing it at length, it asks what shall apprise the hearer of it, and then gives an answer that is a pointer, not a picture. The function of the family is the function of a single instrument: the mobilization of the imagination. A statement informs the intellect; a question of this kind mobilizes the whole person — the imagination, the body, the dread — because the question cannot be answered from the outside. It demands an inner answer, and the inner answer is always the reader's own: the image he builds, from his own faculties, of the thing he is being brought to. The device is thus a technology of awe, and the sūrah uses it at the exact point where awe is needed — at the hinge between the refuted man and the Fire that answers him.

**Why the Qur'an Asks Here Instead of Stating: The Hardness That Information Cannot Reach**

The reason the question appears here, at this point in the sūrah, is the state of the man it follows. The delusion of verse 3 was not an ignorance. The man was not unaware of death: he had seen every graveyard, every funeral, every ending his city supplied. His error was not a lack of information; it was a hardness of orientation — the information had reached his mind and stopped there, without arriving at his self. The "Nay!" of the fourth verse broke the frame of his supposition, and the grammar of the casting removed his agency, but the sūrah knows, in the logic of its own form, that the breaking of a frame is not yet the movement of a heart. A man can be told "you are wrong" and remain unmoved, because the telling is still an event outside him. The question of the fifth verse is the instrument that crosses that boundary. It cannot be answered by the intellect alone, because the intellect has already done its work — the man of verse 3 had the intellect's answer all along, and it did not save him. The question demands the other faculty: the apprising that arrives, in the root's own motion (*adraka* — to know, to reach, to come upon), as an event in the knower. The sūrah is not telling the reader what the Fire is. It is putting the reader in the position of the one who will come upon it, and the coming-upon is the thing the question names. The hardness that stopped the information in the man's mind is the hardness the question is addressed to, and the question's form — a demand for an inner answer, from the reader's own imagination of harm — is the only form that can reach it, because it bypasses the mind's defenses and works on the faculty the mind cannot close: the faculty that knows, in the body, what breaking does.

**The Turn: The Fire Promoted to Subject, and the Reader Brought In**

The structural movement of the verse is the movement the fourth verse prepared. Before the question, the subject of the sūrah was the man: what he slanders, what he hoards, what he supposes. After the question, the subject of the sūrah is the Fire: its ownership, its kindling, its target, its enclosure, its pillars. The question is the point of the turn, and the turn is total — the man is not mentioned again as a subject in the sūrah; he remains only as the one who is cast, the object at the edge of the scene. And the turn brings a second change, quieter and more severe: the address. The question is directed, in its grammar, to the reader — *what shall apprise **thee*** — and the reader, who has up to this moment been the spectator of the indictment, is now inside it. The sūrah has been describing a man, and the question makes the description a mirror: the one who will be appraised is the one who is being apprised. The verb *adraka* carries the sense of arrival — the knowledge sought is not a holding of a fact but a coming-upon of a reality, and the coming-upon is the reader's own event. The sūrah's form is thus a technology of inclusion: the indictment of the first half was public, a cry in an open square; the apprising of the second half is personal, a question in the ear. The man of verse 1 was named in the third person — *every slandering backbiter* — and the reader of verse 5 is addressed in the second. The sūrah has moved, in the space of a question, from the man to the reader, and the movement is the movement of every true warning: the warning that remains a report about others is a report; the warning that becomes an apprising of the self is a transformation.

**The Fulcrum of the Sūrah: The Center That Balances the Two Halves**

The fifth verse of a sūrah of nine verses is the exact center of the chapter, and the sūrah's center of form coincides with its center of gravity. The first half — the man, his tongue, his hoard, his supposition, his refutation, his casting — balances on the fifth verse, and the second half — the Fire, its nature, its target, its enclosure, its pillars — balances on the same point. The question is the fulcrum, and the sūrah is, in form, a scale: the accusation on one side, the Fire on the other, and the question the point on which the whole instrument turns. The coincidence is not decoration. It states the sūrah's thesis in its architecture: the man and the Fire are not two subjects joined by a conjunction; they are two ends of a single motion, and the motion's pivot is the apprising — the moment at which the reader, in the sūrah's own form, comes upon the reality that the man's supposition refused. Everything before the fifth verse is the cause: the habit, the delusion, the verdict. Everything after it is the effect: the Fire, in its full description, as the consequence that follows the cause in the sūrah's order. The reader who pauses at the fifth verse pauses at the point of no return in the chapter — the point after which the sūrah no longer speaks of the man, but only of the place the man has been cast into, in a description so complete that the absence of the man's name is the presence of his sentence.

**The Practical Answer: The Difference Between Knowing and Being Apprised**

The verse's question, taken as the sūrah's instrument for the reader's own life, is the cleanest distinction in the entire chapter: the difference between knowing of the Fire and being apprised of it. The man of the first half knew of it — the information was available, the gravestones were legible, the funerals were attended. He was not apprised of it, because the apprising is not the holding of a fact but the arrival of the fact in the self: the moment at which the knowledge reorders the life, changes the order of the loves, and makes the hoard of verse 2 feel, for the first time, for what it is. The difference is the difference between the man who knows that the ice is thin and the man who feels the ice move under his foot: both have the information; only one has the apprising. The tradition of remembrance — the disciplines the Prophetic practice fixed for the ummah — is the standing technology of the apprising, and the sūrah's question is the name of what the technology produces. The graveyard, commanded visited:

> *"Frequently visit the graveyards, for they are a reminder of the Hereafter."* (Ibn Mājah)

is not a suggestion for information. It is an instrument of arrival — the place where the reader is brought, bodily, to the reality the question names, so that the knowing stops being a fact held in the mind and becomes an event in the self. The hour of the daily audit — the question the third verse posed, *where have I placed my permanence?* — is the same instrument at the scale of the day. The sūrah's fifth verse is the name of the whole discipline: the apprising. And the discipline's success is measured by the answer the sūrah gives in the next verse — because the apprising, once it has happened, takes the form the answer has: the Fire of God, ignited, engaging the hearts, enclosed in pillars outstretched. The reader who has been apprised no longer asks what the Crusher is. He knows, in the root's own motion, what it means to be cast into it — and the knowing is the thing that, in the sūrah's world, separates the man of the first half from the man of the second: the one who was cast, and the one who, having come upon the reality, has turned.

## Sūrah al-Humazah 104:6

> **The Fire of God, ignited,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer in Three Words: Ownership, Nature, and a Pointer**

The question of the fifth verse — the sūrah's fulcrum, its demand for an inner answer — receives the shortest answer the sūrah could give: *nār rabbin mutawqadah* — "the Fire of God, ignited." Three words, no description, no image, no measure. The answer is a pointer, and the pointer's economy is the logic of the fifth verse executed: the sūrah told the reader he would be apprised, and the apprising it gives is not a picture but a source and a nature — the fire is *God's*, and the fire is *ignited*. Everything else — the size, the color, the sound, the scale — is withheld, and the withholding is the verse's deepest teaching, because the picture the reader now builds from the pointer, out of his own imagination of what a fire of that ownership and that nature would do, is the picture the sūrah intends. The description of the Hereafter in the Book is never a report for the intellect's file. It is a frame for the imagination's work, and the frame of this verse is the tightest frame in the sūrah: the fire, whose, and how. The rest is the reader's own apprising, in the motion the fifth verse named.

**The Fire of God: Whose Fire It Is, and What the Attribution Does**

The first word of the answer after the noun is the attribution, and the attribution is the answer's weight. The fire is not described as "a fire" — a phenomenon, an event, a place — but as *rabbin*: of God. The possessive carries the full sense of lordship, and the sense does three things at once. It states the origin: the fire is created by God, made by the command of the One whose command is the making. It states the authority: the fire burns as a decree, not as a process — it is the fire of the judgment, the fire of the verdict pronounced in the fourth verse, and the verdict is the word of the Lord, so the fire is His in the way that a sentence is the judge's. It states the maintenance: the fire is kept by the One who made it, and the keeping is the continuity of the command — the fire does not burn because a fuel lasts; it burns because the command lasts, and the command of the One who is the Lord does not lapse. The attribution thus moves the fire from the order of phenomena to the order of persons: it is not a fire that happens, in the way that a wildfire happens. It is a fire that is willed, addressed, and directed — the fire of the Lord, standing at the end of the casting of the fourth verse as the place the casting was cast into, and belonging to the One who cast.

The Book's other names for the fire confirm the personal character of the attribution. In Fāṭir the fire is "the Fire of the Abode of Misery, the appointed place for the disobedient" (14:18) — a fire with an address and an addressee, the appointment of a Lord over a people. In the same register, the fuel of the fire is stated in the Book in terms that leave no ambiguity about its order:

> *"And the Fire which you are promised, and men and stones [as its fuel]."* (Qur'an 44:43)

And the fire is named, in the standing verse of ar-Raḥmān, by the attribute the present verse uses in its own form:

> *"[They will be] in the blazing Hellfire."* (Qur'an 55:15)

The attribution in verse 6 is thus the sūrah's statement of the fire's order: it is not an element of this world's physics, enlarged. It is the fire of the Lord — created, commanded, maintained, addressed — and the "God's" that stands in the middle of the three words is the word that separates it from every fire the reader has ever seen, and the separation is the beginning of the apprising the fifth verse demanded.

**Mutawqadah: The Fire That Is Always in the Act of Igniting**

The second attribute of the answer, *mutawqadah* — "ignited" — is the word that does the verse's subtlest work, and its work is grammatical. The form is the intensive reflexive of the Arabic verb — the pattern that does not state a condition but a process: not a fire that was lit once and now burns, but a fire that is in the continuous act of kindling itself, every moment of its existence a new ignition. The fires of this world are fires of the first kind: lit, burning, spent — their life is a line from a spark to an ash, and their burning is the expenditure of a fuel that was finite before the lighting. The fire of *mutawqadah* is a fire of the second kind: its kindling is its nature, and the nature does not exhaust. There is no moment in it that is "merely burning" — a state, a residue of an earlier ignition. Every moment is the ignition. The sūrah's choice of the form is the choice of process over state, and the choice is the attribute's meaning: the fire the hoarder is cast into is not a fire that will be burning when he arrives. It is a fire that is arriving, in the act of kindling, at every moment of his arrival — perpetually new, perpetually fresh, without the wear that comes to a thing from its own duration.

The theological logic of the form is the logic the tradition draws from it without division. A fire that kindles itself is a fire of an order different from the fires of this world, because the fires of this world depend on fuel, and the dependence is their limit: the fuel is finite, and the fire dies when the fuel fails. The fire of the Hereafter depends, in the Book's account, on the command, and the command is not a fuel — it is the will of the Lord, continuous in the way the Lord's will is continuous. The form *mutawqadah* states the dependence in the grammar: the fire is not burning because it has fuel; it is ignited because it is God's, and the ignition is the form of the command's maintenance. The reader who has been apprised — the apprising the fifth verse named — now holds the two attributes together: the ownership and the nature. The fire is God's, and the fire is always igniting. The picture the sūrah withholds, the reader builds from these two words, and the picture he builds — a fire without an end, because its kindling is not an event but a nature, because its Lord does not cease — is the picture the sūrah's frame was built to produce.

**The Logic of the Element: Why the Punishment Is a Fire**

The question the answer raises, and the sūrah's form answers, is the question of the element itself: why fire? The Book's choice is not arbitrary, and the logic of it is the logic of the apprising. Fire is the most immediate of the experiences of harm a human being knows: every life has its hearth, its forge, its burned hand, its field after the lightning. The fire of the world is known in the body before it is known in the mind, and the Book uses that bodily knowledge as the raw material of the unknown: the reader's own experience of fire is the instrument by which the fire of the judgment is apprehended, not as a metaphor — the tradition holds the fire to be real, bodily, appointed, in the plain sense the text gives — but as an analogy of order, the known pointing to the unknown in the element the mind already owns. The fire is the element that transforms everything it touches and leaves no trace of what was touched: the gold is proved in the fire, the dross is separated, the form of the thing is consumed into the element's own motion. The fire of the Hereafter, in the sūrah's world, is the element of the final transformation: the man who entered it with his claim of permanence — the *akhladhahu* of verse 3, the making-firm he assigned to his wealth — is the man who comes out of it as the thing he was, without the accumulation, without the counting, without the hoard that he believed was his continuity. The transformation is total, because the element is total: fire does not transform a part of the thing it touches. It transforms the thing.

The justice of the element is the justice the sūrah's form states. The man of the first half made his wealth his comfort: the counting was his security, the hoard was his permanence, the number was his life. The fire is the one element in the order of experience that comfort cannot survive — the element that converts every possession into fuel, every comfort into kindling, every claim of permanence into the act of the un-making. The sūrah's answer is thus proportionate in kind, in the sense the tradition's doctrine of divine justice holds: the punishment is not a violence done to the man from outside. It is the man's own logic executed — the logic by which he made his treasure his self, executed in the element that treats the treasure as what it is, fuel. The branding of the hoarded gold, in the verse of 9:34–35 cited in this sūrah's foundation, is the same justice in another form: the hoard returned to the hoarder as the element that gives wealth its original image. The fire of verse 6 is the justice completed: the man is repaid in his own element, the element he chose, at the scale the element requires.

**The Pointer Completed: What the Answer Gives, and What It Withholds**

The answer of the verse is complete in its function and incomplete in its picture, and the incompleteness is the verse's design. The answer gives the ownership — the fire is God's — and the nature — the fire is ignited, in the continuous act of its own kindling. It withholds the measure, the image, the scale. The sūrah will not tell the reader the width of the fire or the color of it or the sound of it, because the measure, the image, and the sound are the reader's own to build, from the pointer, in the apprising the fifth verse named. The description of the Hereafter in the Book works by the frame-and-picture principle: the frame is given — the ownership, the nature, the target that the next verse will name, the enclosure and the pillars that the last two verses will seal — and the picture is built by the one who is being apprised, out of his own faculties, in the motion the sūrah's form compels. The frame of this verse is the tightest in the sūrah, and the picture it frames is the largest: a fire of that ownership, in that state of perpetual ignition, is a fire the reader's imagination completes in the only way a fire of that order can be completed — in the scale the reader's own dread supplies, which is always, in the sūrah's logic, the true scale, because the true scale of a punishment is the scale of the one who is punished, and the one who is punished is, in the sūrah's turn of the fifth verse, the reader.

The answer is thus a statement of fact in the tradition's plain sense — the fire is fire, God's fire, ignited — and an instrument of the apprising in the sūrah's form. The two characters of the verse are one: the fact is the frame, and the apprising is the picture, and the reader who holds the fact without the apprising has the information the man of the first half had, which did not save him. The sūrah's next verse completes the pointer's target, and the target is the last word the sūrah needs to say about the fire's relation to the man: the fire, ignited, is not a place the man enters in his body only. It is a fire that reaches the heart — and the verse that says so is the seventh, and it is the verse that ends the sūrah's description of the fire's nature and begins, in the sūrah's own order, its description of the fire's work.

## Sūrah al-Humazah 104:7

> **which engulfs hearts.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fire's Target: The Heart, Not the Body**

The seventh verse completes the pointer of the sixth, and the completion changes the sūrah's subject into its target. The fire of the sixth verse was described in its ownership and its nature — God's, ignited, in the continuous act of its own kindling — and the seventh verse states what the fire does: it engulfs the hearts. The verb of the verse, *takhmāzu*, carries the sense of piercing, swallowing, engaging — the motion of a fire that does not stop at the surface of the thing it burns but enters it, takes it in, and consumes it from within. And the object of the verb is the verse's weight: not the bodies, not the skins, not the flesh — the *hearts*. The sūrah's fire is thus not a place the man is thrown into, in the way that a body is thrown into a furnace. It is a fire that acts on the self, because the heart, in the Qur'an's anthropology, is where the self is at home. The punishment the sūrah describes is therefore not an experience undergone by the person from outside. It is the consuming of the person from the point where the person lives — the core, the center, the seat of the feeling that makes the person the person. The source commentary's note on the word is the tradition's own: *afʾiḍah* indicates the very center of the heart, the subtlest faculty of the human being, the faculty that feels pain and suffering most severely — and the verse states, in the sūrah's plain sense, both that the fire touches that core, consuming all that one is, and that the burning it does is more painful than any burning experienced in this world. The body burns in many of the Book's descriptions of the Fire; the burning of the heart is the burning the sūrah singles out, because the heart is the only part of the man in which the man is.

**The Anatomy of the Heart: Afʾiḍah, the Center of the Self**

The word the verse uses, *afʾiḍah*, the plural of *faʾdah*, is the Book's own word for the innermost point of the heart — the center, the core, the place where the subtlest of the human faculties reside. The Qur'an's usage of the heart is the key to the verse, and the usage is constant: the heart in the Book is not the organ alone but the seat of the person — the place where the feeling is, where the faith is, where the pain is. The reassurance of God descends into the hearts of the believers (39:23); in the remembrance of God the hearts find rest (13:28); on the Day of the Resurrection the hearts will be in a flutter and the eyes will be dim (75:7); and the hearts will tremble, and the eyes will be dim, on the Day of the mutual glances (99:6–7). The heart is, in the Book's consistent usage, the site of the final states: the site of the rest for the one who remembered, and the site of the flutter and the trembling for the one who did not. The verse of al-Humazah places the fire at that site, and the placement is the verse's meaning: the punishment of the Hereafter, in the sūrah's account, is not an event that happens to the man's shell. It is an event that happens to the man — at the point where the man is, in the point the Book names *faʾdah*.

The Book's other descriptions of the Fire confirm the target, and the confirmations are precise. In Fāṭir the fire of the Abode of Misery is described, in the very phrase the present verse rhymes with, as the fire that is "piercing the hearts" of the disobedient:

> *"The Fire of the Abode of Misery — the appointed place for the disobedient, piercing their hearts."* (Qur'an 14:18)

And in Qāf the Book distinguishes, in a single sūrah, the two layers of the burning — the burning of the flesh and what is under the flesh:

> *"…and the Fire that will burn the flesh."* (Qur'an 50:24)

The distinction the Book draws is the distinction the present verse executes: the flesh burns in the fire, and the heart is engaged by it. The burning of the flesh is the burning the man of this world knows — the burning of a part, witnessed by the self that survives the part's pain. The engaging of the heart is the burning of the seat of the self, and the seat does not survive the burning as a witness: the witness and the burned are one. The sūrah's choice of the object is thus the sūrah's statement of the punishment's order: the fire of the Hereafter, in the account the Book gives, is not an external violence. It is the consuming of the center, and the consuming of the center is the consuming of the person.

**The Irony Completed: The Heart That Counted Is Now the Fire's Fuel**

The irony the sūrah has been building since its second verse is completed at the seventh, and the completion is the sūrah's deepest stroke. The counting of verse 2 — *yaʿdhdhu*, the intensive verb of the repeated summation — was not an act of the hand only. It was an act of the heart: the habit that filled the man's days was the habit that filled his center, the occupation that lived in the *faʾdah* the seventh verse now names. The heart of the man of the first half was a treasury: the inventory lived in it, the comfort of the number lived in it, the delusion of the third verse — the supposition that the hoard made him immortal — was the belief the heart held, and held as its own, against every graveyard it had seen. And the sūrah's seventh verse states, without a particle of rhetoric, what the heart that was filled with the counting is filled with now: the fire. The heart that was the treasury is now the fuel. The heart that kept the number is now the thing the number was counting against. The symmetry is total, because the object of the verb is the same object the man's own habit had occupied: the sūrah does not inflict a new punishment on a new part of the man. It returns the man to the part he had filled, in the element that completes the filling. The heart that wanted the accumulation is given the one accumulation it cannot stop — the fire — and the giving is the justice the sūrah's form has been stating since the first verse: the man is repaid in the currency of his own habit, at the point where the habit lived.

The Book's doctrine of the heart states the logic in its general form. The heart is what it is fed — the moral-psychological principle the tradition of the moralists, from al-Ghazālī's treatise on the heart in the *Iḥyāʾ* onward, made the standing teaching of the spiritual science: the heart is the stomach of the spirit, and it is what it digests, and what settles in it becomes its nature. The heart fed on the counting and the hoarding is the heart whose appetite is the accumulation, and the appetite is the fire's kindling: the sūrah's psychology is that the punishment is the appetite completed, not the appetite punished. The man did not enter the fire with a heart that had no relation to it. He entered it with the heart that had been feeding, for years, on the very motion the fire is — the taking-in, the accumulating, the filling-that-will-not-stop. And the hadith that states the heart's relation to its Lord states the justice in its final form:

> *"The hearts of the children of Adam are all between the Two Fingers of the Most Merciful, and He turns them as He wills."* (Muslim 2656)

The heart was never the man's to keep. It was in the hand of the One who casts, all along the counting, all along the hoarding, all along the supposition. The turning of the heart of the man of the first half — the turning the seventh verse describes — is the turning of the heart that had been turned, by its own filling, away from the hand that held it, back to the hand, in the element the hand appoints.

**The Totality of the Punishment: The Self, Not the Shell**

The logical point of the verse, stated in the form the sūrah's argument requires, is the point of the target. The severity of a punishment is located in its target, not in its duration: a fire that burns the body leaves the self as a witness — the person survives the burning as the one who feels it, and the feeling is, in the end, an event in the person's life, however severe. A fire that engulfs the heart removes the distinction between the one who burns and the one who feels: the self is consumed with the flesh, and the consumption is total in kind. The punishment the sūrah describes is thus not the most severe experience the man can undergo. It is the end of the man as the one who undergos — the consuming of the continuity that the man of the third verse believed his wealth had made permanent. And here the sūrah's symmetry reaches its completion, because the permanence the man claimed is the permanence he is given, in the form he did not choose. The delusion of verse 3 was that the hoard made him *khald* — immortal, continuous, beyond the end. The Fire of the sūrah is the fire in which the one who is cast does not end: the Book's standing description of the dwelling of the Fire is that the dwellers "will not die therein, nor live" (15:48) — the permanence without the life, the continuity without the self. The man who sought the permanence of the number is given the permanence of the fire: the same attribute, the opposite content. The sūrah's answer to the supposition is thus not the negation of the immortality. It is the immortality, granted, in the form the sūrah's name for the fire states: the crushing, the engaging, the engulfing — permanent, because the fire is *mutawqadah*, in the continuous act of its own kindling, and the heart it engulfs is engaged in the act without end. The justice of the element is complete: the claim of permanence is repaid in permanence, and the permanence is the fire.

**The Modern Parallel: The Heart's Real Fuel, and the Discipline That Feeds It**

The translation of the verse into the modern condition is the translation of the filling. The heart of the modern age is fed, as the heart of the sūrah's man was fed, on the inventory: the balance checked at the hour, the score kept against the rival, the grudge held as a possession, the reputation of the neighbor nibbled in the small currency of the offhand remark. The feeding is the same, in the sūrah's logic, at the scale the modern tools supply: the heart is the stomach of the spirit, and the spirit is fed on what passes through the gates the sūrah's first two verses named — the tongue and the hand — and what settles in the *faʾdah* becomes the nature, and the nature is what the fire of the seventh verse engages. The sūrah's diagnosis of the modern heart is thus the same diagnosis it made of the Makkān heart, at the scale the modern condition requires: the fire of the Hereafter is the final harvest of what the heart was fed in this world, and the harvest is taken at the point where the feeding happened.

The discipline the sūrah offers is the discipline of the feeding, and the discipline is the same discipline the Book states in its positive form. The nourishment that is the fire's opposite is the remembrance:

> *"Verily, in the remembrance of God do hearts find rest."* (Qur'an 13:28)

The rest is the state of the heart fed on the right food — the heart whose filling is the remembrance, and whose nature is therefore the rest, and whose relation to the fire of the seventh verse is the relation of the fed heart to the element it does not feed: the fire engages the heart that was filled with the counting, because the counting is the fire's own motion; the remembrance is not engaged, because the remembrance is the motion of the opposite order — the motion of the heart toward the One whose fire it is, in the direction that is the turning the hadith of Muslim 2656 describes, in the form the turning takes for the one who turns while the turning is still possible. The daily audit of the heart, the counterpart of the audit of the balance the second verse posed, is the audit the seventh verse names: not *what is the number?* but *what entered today?* — what passed through the gate of the tongue, what passed through the gate of the hand, what settled in the center. The heart that keeps the audit is the heart that is fed, and the heart that is fed is the heart the fire does not engage, because the engagement of the seventh verse is the engagement of the heart that was filled with the fire's own motion, and the heart filled with the remembrance is filled with the opposite. The verse's practical force is thus the force of the whole sūrah, at the point where the sūrah's subject and the reader's self coincide: the fire that engulfs the hearts is the fire that engulfs the heart the reader has been feeding, and the feeding is the reader's own, and the audit is the reader's own, and the turning — the turning the hadith names, in the Two Fingers of the Most Merciful — is the turning the reader can still make, in the hour the sūrah's seventh verse is read.

**The End of the Nature, the Beginning of the Verdict**

The structural position of the verse is the last point of the sūrah's form. The description of the fire's nature — the two verses of its source and its target, the sixth and the seventh — is complete: the fire is God's, the fire is ignited, the fire engages the hearts. What remains in the sūrah is the verdict: the enclosure, and the pillars. The fire has been told, in the sūrah's order, whose it is, what it is, and what it does. The last two verses state what the fire is in its relation to the one it does its work upon: it is enclosed upon them, in pillars outstretched. The description is finished; the sentence remains, and the sentence is the sūrah's final form — the form of the closed door, and the door's architecture, and the architecture that stretches beyond the measure the sūrah's name for the fire states. The man of the first half is no longer named. The fire is the subject, and the hearts are its object, and the sūrah now moves, in the eighth verse, from the fire's work to the fire's verdict, and the verdict is the word that ends the argument the sūrah has been making since the first: the enclosure, *muʾaḍḍah* — the closing, the sealing, the covering — upon them.

## Sūrah al-Humazah 104:8

> **Truly it is enclosed upon them**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict: The Closing, Stated with the Certainty of the Casting**

The eighth verse is the sūrah's verdict, and it is stated in the grammar the fourth verse established — the grammar of the emphatic certainty. *Innā ʿalayhim muʾaḍḍah* — "Truly it is enclosed upon them" — the particle *inna*, the same emphatic force that stood before the casting of the fourth verse, stands before the closing, and the closing is thus stated with the same certainty the casting was: no condition, no exception, no interval of appeal between the two. The fire that was cast into (the fourth verse), that was appraised (the fifth), that was described in its ownership and its nature and its target (the sixth and seventh) is now stated in its relation to the one it does its work upon: it is enclosed upon them. The verb *muʾaḍḍah* means to close, to seal, to shut — the closing of the door upon the one who is inside, and the source commentary notes the word's second sense, which the sūrah's form holds together with the first: *muʾaḍḍah* can also mean to cover. The two senses — the sealed room and the lid — are the two faces of one image, and the image is the verdict's form: the fire is not merely a place the man enters. It is a place that closes on him, and the closing is the verdict's last act. The Book's own parallel states the identical sentence in the identical register, in al-Balad:

> *"Upon them is a Fire enclosed."* (Qur'an 90:20)

The two verses are the same sentence, and the sameness is the tradition's confirmation that the closing is the standing form of the Fire's relation to its dwellers — not an accident of the sūrah's description, but the shape of the thing. The sūrah's arc is thus complete in its form: it opened with an accusation against an individual — *wayl unto every slandering backbiter* — and it closes with a sealing upon a plural — *upon them* — and the movement from the singular to the plural is the sūrah's last statement, and it will be taken up below, because it is the statement the sūrah saves for its penultimate verse.

**The Sealed Room and the Lid: Two Readings, One Experience**

The double sense of the verb — the closing and the covering — is the verse's deepest image, and the image is the image of the sealed vessel. The sealed room is the space whose door has closed behind the one who is in it: the door that was the way in is now the wall, and the wall is the door, and the distinction between the way in and the barrier is gone. The lid is the cover that falls on the pot: the cover that was the protection is now the seal, and the pressure that the cover makes is the pressure that builds in the closed vessel — the pressure that has no outlet, because the outlet is the cover, and the cover is the seal. The two images are one experience, and the experience is the verse's meaning: the fire is not a place the man enters and remains in, in the way that a man enters a room. It is a place that enters its relation to the man as a closing — the door that shuts, the lid that falls, the seal that makes the inside the whole of the man's world. The psychological force of the image is the force of the most universal of the bodily experiences: the experience of the door closing behind you, in the room you did not choose to stay in, at the hour you did not choose to be locked in. The sūrah uses the most common of the human experiences — the closing of the door — for the most common of the human fates, and the usage is the sūrah's form: the image is not a metaphor for the fate. The image is the fate, in the element the body already knows.

The Book's engineering of the Fire is consistent, and the consistency confirms the verse's image. The doors of the Fire are closed upon the dwellers, in the standing verse of al-Dukhān:

> *"And its doors are closed upon them."* (Qur'an 44:44)

The canopies of the Fire encompass the wrongdoers, in the two verses cited in this sūrah's foundation:

> *"And We have prepared for the wrongdoers a Fire whose canopies will encompass them."* (Qur'an 18:29)

and in al-Mulk, in the identical phrase:

> *"And indeed, for the wrongdoers is a Fire whose [blazing] will be over them as canopies."* (Qur'an 67:7)

And the closing from above is stated, in the two verses of the Book that use the preposition of the upper side, of Pharaoh at the hour of his end:

> *"And it will be closed upon him from above."* (Qur'an 37:55)

and of the dwellers of the Fire in at-Takwīr:

> *"And it will be closed upon them from above."* (Qur'an 52:21)

The architecture is thus the Book's own: the doors, the canopies, the sealing from above — the Fire is a structure, in the Book's consistent account, and the structure closes. The verse of al-Humazah states the closing in its plain form, and the plain form is the form the structure's own engineering requires: the doors shut, the canopies fall, the seal is made, and the inside is the whole of the world of the one who is in it.

**The Logic of the Closing: The Seal as the Completion of the Choice**

The logic of the closing, stated in the form the sūrah's argument requires, is the logic the tradition's doctrine of divine justice holds: the seal is not an arbitrary cruelty inflicted on the man from outside. It is the final form of the man's own orientation, completed in the element the orientation chose. The Book's own image states the sequence: the heart that sealed itself is the heart the seal falls upon. In al-Baqarah, of the one who disbelieves after the certainty has come:

> *"But as for those who disbelieved — God has set a seal upon their hearts and made a covering over them."* (Qur'an 2:7)

The sequence is the sūrah's sequence, in the Book's own words: the sealing of the heart first — the heart that refused the remembrance, that chose the counting, that held the supposition against the observation — and then the covering, and then the closing of the structure upon the sealed heart. The man of al-Humazah did not enter the Fire with an open heart that was then sealed. He entered it with the heart that had been sealing itself, all along the counting of the second verse, all along the supposition of the third — the heart whose filling was the fire's own motion, and whose relation to the remembrance was the relation of the sealed room to the door that was the way in. The closing of the eighth verse is thus the completion of the orientation, not the punishment of it: the man who chose the counting over the remembrance, the hoarding over the giving, the supposition over the observation, is given, in the closing, the shape his choices took when the choices stopped being possible. The free will the tradition affirms is affirmed in the verse's form: the seal is the final form of the choice, and the choice was the man's, and the sūrah's severity is the severity of the statement that the choice was his — the statement that the closing is not a violence done to him but a completion of what he did, in the element he chose, at the scale the element requires.

And the justice of the closing has a point the sūrah's form states without saying it, because the point is in the yoke of the first two verses. The Fire is the perfect hoarder. The hoarder of the second verse is the one who takes in and does not give back — the hand that keeps, the treasury that withholds the circulation, the number that grows and the flow that stops. The Fire of the seventh verse engages the hearts and keeps them: the fire takes in, and the fire does not give back, and the taking-in is the fire's nature, in the same form the keeping was the hoarder's. The man who made the keeping his habit ends in the domain of the perfect keeping — the domain in which the taking-in is total, and the giving-back is none, and the accumulation is the element itself. The closing is thus the hoarder's own logic, executed at the scale the logic requires: the one who sealed his treasury is sealed in the treasury that seals, and the seal is the form his habit took when the habit became the place. The sūrah's answer to the man of the first half is, in the eighth verse, complete: the hoarding that was the man is the place the man is in, and the place closes, because the hoarding closes, because the keeping is the closing, and the closing is the keeping.

**The "Upon Them": The Plural of the Shared Fate**

The last word of the verse is the plural, and the plural is the sūrah's last statement. The sūrah has spoken, from the first verse to the seventh, of one man — *he* slanders, *he* hoards, *he* supposes, *he* is cast, *he* is appraised. The verdict falls, in the eighth verse, upon *them*: the fire is enclosed upon *them*. The movement from the singular to the plural is not a grammatical convenience. It is the sūrah's thesis in its final form: the man of the first half is the type, and the type is the society. The hoarder does not live alone. The hoarding society is made of hoarders — the hand that keeps, the treasury that withholds, the number that grows — and the society of the hoarders is the society that shares the sealing, because the sealing is the shape the society's shared orientation takes when the orientation is completed. The slanderer, likewise, does not live alone: the slander is the wire between the people, the namīmah of the first verse's commentary, the word that carries the fire from one ear to another, and the society of the slanderers is the society that shares the valley named for the habit. The "them" of the eighth verse is thus not the man plus his companions. It is the age — the community of the habit, the society that produced the al-Akhnaṣ and the al-Walīd, and that produces, in every generation, the men the sūrah's first names were given to. The Book's judgment is thus, in the sūrah's own form, both individual and social: the individual is indicted in the first verse, and the society is sealed in the eighth, and the two judgments are one, because the individual is the type and the type is the society, and the society is the individual multiplied.

The opposite social form is the one the Prophetic record states, and the statement is the sūrah's counter-image:

> *"The believers, in their mutual mercy and love, are like one body: when one part aches, the whole body responds with sleeplessness and fever."* (al-Bukhārī 6011; Muslim 2564)

The body that shares the pain is the body the sūrah's "them" is not: the body of the habit shares the sealing, in the same form the body of the remembrance shares the pain — the sociality of the shared orientation, in the two directions the orientation can take. The sūrah's "them" is the sociality turned toward the keeping, and the hadith's body is the sociality turned toward the giving, and the two socialities are the two forms of the same fact: the man does not live alone, and the life he lives is the life his people live, and the sealing that falls upon the one falls, in the sūrah's logic, upon the many, because the many are the one, multiplied.

**The Practical Answer: The Door That Closes, and the Door That Stays Open**

The verse's warning, taken as the sūrah's instrument for the reader's own life, is the warning of the door. The closing of the eighth verse is not a distant event, in the sūrah's logic. It is the shape of every life that proceeds in the orientation of the first half — the life that keeps, that counts, that supposes, that seals the heart against the remembrance in the filling of the habit. The discipline the sūrah offers is the discipline of the door that stays open: the keeping of the gates the sūrah's first two verses named — the gate of the tongue, the gate of the hand — and the gate of the heart that governs both, open toward the One whose fire the eighth verse describes, so that the door of the fire does not close from behind, in the form the closing takes when the gates have been sealed from the front. The remembrance is the keeping of the heart's gate open; the giving is the keeping of the hand's gate open; the guarding of the tongue is the keeping of the mouth's gate open — and the three gates open together, in the sūrah's form, because the three are one, in the yoke the second verse stated, and the one they are is the heart, the *faʾdah* of the seventh verse, the center the fire engages and the center the remembrance rests in.

The contrast the sūrah's severity makes possible is the contrast the Book states in its standing verse of the open door:

> *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of God. Indeed, God forgives all sins. Indeed, it is He who is the Forgiving, the Merciful."* (Qur'an 39:53)

The door of mercy is open, in this life, in the form the verse states — the form of the plural, the form of the transgressors, the form of the mercy that is not measured by the transgression. The door of the fire closes, in the next life, in the form the eighth verse states — the form of the plural, the form of the seal, the form of the enclosure that is the completion of the orientation. The logic of the two doors is the logic the sūrah's severity serves: the warning that names the closing is the instrument that keeps the other door open. The sūrah is severe, in the form the sūrah's subject requires, because the subject is the one who has sealed his own heart against the gentler word, and the severe word is the word that reaches the sealed heart, in the element the sealed heart still knows — the element of the dread that the counting could not quiet, and the supposition could not answer, and the closing, in the eighth verse, states. And the state the sūrah leaves the reader in, at the eighth verse, is the state of the one who has been shown the closed door, and knows, in the showing, that the other door is open, and knows, in the knowing, that the opening of the other door is the only act the closed door does not make impossible: the turning, in the hour the turning is still possible, in the Two Fingers of the Most Merciful, before the seal is the seal, and before the closing is the closing, and before the pillars — the pillars the last verse names — are outstretched.

## Sūrah al-Humazah 104:9

> **in pillars outstretched.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Image: The Architecture of the Fire, and the Sūrah's Last Word**

The ninth verse is the sūrah's last, and its last word is the sūrah's seal: *mubtadaṭah* — outstretched. The verse states the fire in its architecture: *in pillars outstretched* — the pillars, in the source commentary's note, are the pillars of the Fire, the standing structures of the place the eighth verse sealed, and the verse's form allows the reading "as pillars outstretched," the pillars given as the manner of the enclosure, the architecture in which the sealing of the eighth verse is made complete. The root of the final word, b-d-d, is the root of the extension, the stretching, the prolonging — the motion of a line that goes on, of a reach that does not stop, of a span that passes the measure of the thing that measures it. And the adjective the sūrah chose for the pillars is the adjective of that motion in its continuous form: the pillars are not merely pillars. They are outstretched — stretched to the horizon, prolonged beyond the eye, extended in the form the extension takes when the extension has no end to reach. The sūrah's last word is thus an image of vastness, and the image is the sūrah's final act: the sūrah that began with a cry — *wayl*, the single syllable of the verdict pronounced on a single habit — ends with a stretching, the image without the terminus, the horizon without the far side. The reader's last syllable, in the sūrah's order, is the syllable of the extension, and the sūrah's last work is the work the syllable does in the reader: the stretching of the image, in the reader's own imagination, to the horizon the reader's own dread supplies — the horizon that is, in the sūrah's logic, the true horizon, because the true scale of the judgment is the scale of the one who is judged, and the one who is judged is, in the sūrah's turn of the fifth verse, the reader.

**The Pillars and the Canopies: The Palace of Perdition**

The architecture the ninth verse states is the Book's own architecture of the Fire, and the architecture is consistent, in the Book's account, from the first description to the last. The doors of the Fire are closed upon the dwellers (44:44). The canopies of the Fire encompass the wrongdoers, in the two verses of this sūrah's foundation:

> *"And We have prepared for the wrongdoers a Fire whose canopies will encompass them."* (Qur'an 18:29)

> *"And indeed, for the wrongdoers is a Fire whose [blazing] will be over them as canopies."* (Qur'an 67:7)

The closing from above is stated in the two verses of the Book that use the preposition of the upper side (37:55; 52:21). And the pillars are stated, in the sūrah's ninth verse, as the standing structures of the place — the pillars that bear, in the architecture's own logic, the canopies that encompass: the building is complete in the Book's account, and the completeness is the point. The Fire of the Qur'an is not a pit, in the Book's consistent description. It is a structure — a palace, a fortress, a place with its doors and its canopies and its pillars, the place the building of a city is, in the element of the fire. The sūrah's ninth verse completes the building, in the sūrah's order: the doors that closed in the eighth verse are the doors of this building, and the canopies that encompass are the canopies this building bears, and the pillars that are outstretched are the pillars on which the building stands. The reader who has followed the sūrah from its first word to its last has been shown, in the sūrah's form, the whole of the building: the verdict that cast into it (the fourth verse), the apprising of its nature (the fifth), its ownership and its ignition (the sixth), its target (the seventh), its sealing (the eighth), and its architecture (the ninth). The building is complete, and the completeness is the sūrah's final statement of the fire's order: the fire is not an event. It is a place, and the place is built, and the building's pillars are outstretched, and the outstretching is the building's last word, and the last word is the word the sūrah leaves in the reader, in the form the word takes when the word is the end.

The contrast the architecture invites is the contrast the Book itself states, and the contrast is the sūrah's irony completed. The Book's description of the sky, in Fāṭir, is the description of the pillars in their noble form:

> *"...and the heaven, which you see as visible pillars."* (Qur'an 41:12)

The sky, in the Book's own phrase, stands on pillars — the pillars of the order, the pillars of the measure, the pillars of the world the man of the first half lived in, the world whose pillars he believed were his, because his wealth was the currency of the world, and the world's standing was his standing. And the Fire, in the sūrah's ninth verse, has its own pillars — the pillars of the other order, the pillars of the place the world's pillars do not reach, the pillars that are outstretched in the form the outstretching takes when the outstretching is the permanence the man of the third verse sought. The irony is the sūrah's final stroke, and the stroke completes the symmetry the fourth verse began: the man who claimed, in the causative of his own verb, that his wealth would make him *fixed* — *akhladhahu*, the making-permanent, the rendering-solid — is given, in the sūrah's last word, the structure that is permanent, in the form the permanence takes when the permanence is the fire. The permanence he sought is the permanence he is given: the pillars outstretched, the building without the end, the place whose last word is the stretching. God gives the man what he asked for, in the sūrah's logic, and the giving is the justice: the asking was the claim of permanence, and the permanence is granted, and the granted permanence is the outstretched, and the outstretched is the fire. The sūrah's answer to the supposition of the third verse is thus complete in its last word: the "Nay!" of the fourth verse refuted the claim, and the ninth verse shows the claim's end — not the absence of the permanence, but the permanence, in the form the claim did not choose.

**Mubtadaṭah: The Sūrah's Arc, from the Point to the Horizon**

The form of the sūrah, in its last word, states the sūrah's whole movement, and the movement is the sūrah's argument in its architecture. The sūrah began with a cry — *wayl*, the point, the single syllable of the verdict, pronounced on a single habit, in a single man. The sūrah ended with a stretching — *mubtadaṭah*, the horizon, the image without the terminus, the outstretching that passes the measure. The arc from the point to the horizon is the sūrah's form, and the form is the sūrah's thesis: the smallness of the man, against the vastness of the judgment. The habit that began the sūrah — the nibbling of the tongue, the counting of the hand — is small in the eye of the world: a whisper, a number, a dirham kept, a remark made. The sūrah's last word corrects the scale: the smallness of the habit is the great size of the place the habit produces, and the great size is the outstretching, and the outstretching is the horizon. The reader who has followed the sūrah from its first word to its last has been shown, in the sūrah's form, the ratio of the two scales: the scale of the habit, in the point of the first verse, and the scale of the judgment, in the horizon of the last. And the ratio is the sūrah's warning, in its final form: the habit that is small in the eye of the world is the habit that is vast in the eye of the sūrah, and the vastness is the outstretched, and the outstretched is the last word the sūrah leaves in the reader, in the form the word takes when the word is the end.

**The Sūrah's Last Word as a Diagnosis for the Reader**

The diagnosis the sūrah's last word states, in the form the nine verses make possible, is the diagnosis in a single line: the one who devours people and hoards gold is sealed in a fortress of fire whose pillars stretch beyond sight. The line is the sūrah, and the sūrah is the line, and the line's force is the force of the scale: the reader who hears the line hears the ratio of the two scales, and the ratio is the correction of the reader's own measure of his own habits. The habit of the reader — the counting, the nibbling, the keeping, the supposition — is measured, in the reader's own eye, by the scale of the world: the smallness of the remark, the smallness of the number, the smallness of the dirham. The sūrah's last word measures the habit by the other scale: the scale of the outstretched, the scale of the pillars, the scale of the building that is the fire. And the correction is the sūrah's practical force, in its final form: the reader who holds the two scales together holds the habit in its true size, and the habit in its true size is the habit the reader does not make, and does not keep, and does not count, and does not suppose — the habit the sūrah's first word indicts, in the scale the sūrah's last word states.

And the severity of the diagnosis is the mercy of the diagnosis, in the sūrah's form. The outstretched pillars are what the turning avoids: the image of the last verse is the instrument of the turning, in the same form the warning is the instrument of the open door. The reader who has been shown the building, in the sūrah's order — the verdict, the apprising, the nature, the target, the sealing, the architecture — is the reader who knows, in the showing, what the turning saves: the turning saves the reader from the outstretched, in the hour the turning is still possible, in the Two Fingers of the Most Merciful, before the seal is the seal, and before the pillars are outstretched, and before the last word of the sūrah is the last word of the reader. The sūrah's severity is thus the sūrah's mercy, in the form the sūrah's subject requires: the subject is the one who has sealed his heart against the gentler word, and the severe word is the word that reaches the sealed heart, and the reaching is the turning, and the turning is the only act the outstretched does not make impossible.

**A Standing Warning for Every Generation: The Diagnosis and the Cure**

The sūrah ends, and the sūrah's end is the sūrah's beginning: the standing warning, unaged, for every generation that produces the men the sūrah's first names were given to. The slanderer of Makkah, in the sūrah's form, is the modern tongue — the feed, the campaign, the offhand remark at the scale the modern tools supply. The hoarder of Makkah is the modern treasury — the balance, the portfolio, the keeping at the scale the modern condition requires. The delusion of the third verse is the modern delusion — the supposition that the accumulation is the permanence, at the scale the modern age's own technology of immortality supplies. The sūrah's diagnosis is the diagnosis of every society that makes a market of reputation and a god of accumulation, and the diagnosis is unaged, because the condition the diagnosis names is unaged: the tongue that nibbles, the hand that keeps, the heart that supposes, and the fire that is the outstretched.

And the cure is in the sūrah's own neighborhood, in the two sūrahs that stand before it, and the cure is the cure the sūrah's cluster states. The sūrah of the competition that diverts until the graveyards (102) is the diagnosis of the condition. The sūrah of the loss of every man, without exception, except the four qualities (103) is the exception — the cure in its form:

> *"By time — man is surely in loss, except those who believe and do righteous deeds and advise one another to truth and advise one another to patience."* (Qur'an 103:1–3)

And the sūrah that stands after the Elephant, in the sūrah's written neighborhood, states the antithesis of the hoarder in its plain form, the one who denies the religion:

> *"Have you seen the one who denies the religion? Then that is the one who drives the orphan away harshly, and does not encourage the feeding of the poor."* (Qur'an 107:1–3)

The antithesis of the hoarder is the one who feeds, and the antithesis of the slanderer is the one who guards, and the antithesis of the supposition is the belief, and the four qualities of the exception are the cure of the condition: the belief that the permanence is not the hoard, the deed that the hand is open, the advising of the truth that the tongue is guarded, and the patience that the heart is fed on the remembrance, in the form the remembrance takes when the remembrance is the rest. The sūrah's cluster — the diagnosis, the exception, the portrait of the disease — is the sūrah's own commentary on itself, and the commentary is the cure: the one who believes and does good and advises is the one the sūrah's first word does not reach, because the sūrah's first word reaches the one who is the habit, and the one who is the cure is not the habit, and the one who is not the habit is not in the valley, and is not cast, and is not appraised, and is not sealed, and is not in the outstretched. The sūrah is complete: the nine verses, the two habits, the one heart, the fire, the sealing, the pillars — the whole of the sūrah in the line the sūrah's last word states, and the line in the reader, in the form the line takes when the line is the end, and the end is the outstretched, and the outstretched is the last word of the sūrah of the slandering backbiter and the hoarder, the sūrah that is named for its first wound and its first confession, and that stands, in the written Book, between the sūrah of the small help and the sūrah of the Elephant, in the place the Book gives it, in the hour the Book's word is read, and in the hour the word is the end.
