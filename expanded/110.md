# Sūrah an-Naṣr (Chapter 110) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Verses That Close Twenty-Three Years of Revelation**

Sūrah an-Naṣr — "The Victory," or more precisely "The Divine Support" — is among the shortest chapters of the Qur'an, yet it carries the weight of an entire prophetic biography. Its three verses were, in the judgment of the majority of exegetes, the last complete sūrah to be revealed to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ before his death. Whatever individual verses may have come down after it, no complete chapter did. This gives an-Naṣr a unique station in the structure of the Book: it is the sūrah of the ending — the chapter in which God tells His Messenger, in the gentlest possible way, that the work is finished, that the harvest is visible, and that the time of departure is near. To recite it is to stand, as it were, at the bedside of revelation itself.

The Arabic word *naṣr* comes from a root (*n-ṣ-r*) whose basic meaning is to help, to come to the aid of someone who cannot prevail alone, and — by extension — to grant him victory. From the same root the Qur'an draws the divine name *al-Naṣīr*, "the Helper": *"...sufficient is your Lord as a guide and a helper (wa-kafā bi-rabbika hādīan wa-naṣīrā)"* (Qur'an 25:31). The sūrah's title is therefore not merely "Victory" in the cold military sense; it is the name for God's act of standing by His servants when every earthly calculation says they should have been crushed. That act had been the hidden engine of the Prophet's entire mission, and this sūrah gathers it into a single word placed at the head of the chapter, the way a banner is planted on a summit.

**The Last Complete Sūrah and the Question of When It Came Down**

The chronology of this sūrah has been discussed with unusual care by the classical exegetes, precisely because its meaning changes so much depending on when it arrived. The majority agree that it is a late Madinan sūrah and that it was the last complete sūrah to be revealed. Within that agreement, several specific timings were proposed and transmitted. A strong body of opinion holds that it was revealed at the plain of Minā — the valley that lies between Mount ʿArafāt and Makkah — on the tenth of Dhuʾl-Ḥijjah during the Prophet's Farewell Pilgrimage in the year 10/632, either the day after the verse of completion was revealed at ʿArafāt — *"This day I have perfected for you your religion and completed My favor upon you"* (Qur'an 5:3) — or shortly before it. Others maintain that it was revealed in Madinah after the Prophet had returned from the Farewell Pilgrimage. Those who hold the Madinan view, and many of those who hold the Minā view, agree that at least one verse came down after this sūrah: the awe-filled warning of Sūrah al-Baqarah, *"And be conscious of the Day when you shall be returned to God; then every soul shall be paid in full what it has earned, and none shall be wronged"* (Qur'an 2:281) — which many reports identify as the last verse of the entire Qur'an to be revealed, after which the Prophet ﷺ lived, by some accounts, only days.

A minority position, recorded by al-Ṭabarī, places the sūrah after the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyah in 6/628. Al-Wāḥidī, in his classic work on the occasions of revelation, reports that it came down when the Prophet set out for the Battle of Ḥunayn in 8/630, and adds the striking detail that the Prophet lived only two years after that battle. The same report transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās a scene of extraordinary intimacy: when the sūrah was revealed, the Prophet called his daughter Fāṭimah and her husband ʿAlī and said to them:

> *"O ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib! O Fāṭimah! God's Help and Victory have come. I have seen people entering the religion of God in throngs. I therefore hymn the praises of my Lord and seek forgiveness of Him, for He is Ever Relenting."* (transmitted in the asbāb al-nuzūl literature, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The detail that he summoned the two people dearest to him — the daughter he called "a piece of me," and the cousin who had been at his side since childhood — tells its own story. A man who receives news that his work is complete and his term is drawing near does not first address the crowds; he first gathers his household. The verse, on this reading, was not only a public announcement about history but a private farewell within the Prophet's own home.

**"The Farewell Sūrah": The Name Given by Ibn Masʿūd**

Because of this character, ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd — one of the earliest Muslims, one of the first to recite the Qur'an openly in Makkah, and a man the Prophet ﷺ called to recite for him — is reported to have given the sūrah a second name: *Sūrat al-Tawdīʿ*, "the Sūrah of Taking Leave." Through this sūrah, Ibn ʿAbbās explains, the Prophet was informed that his death was near. The exegetical tradition has preserved this reading with such strength that it effectively became the sūrah's identity. It is a remarkable fact of the Qur'anic sciences: the last complete chapter of God's Book is, at its heart, a preparation for death — not the death of an enemy or a tyrant, but of the Messenger himself, and it contains no note of tragedy. It mentions victory, human beings streaming toward their Lord, praise, forgiveness, and a divine name of constant mercy. The Qur'an, which began with the command *"Read!"* (96:1) spoken to an unlettered man in a cave, ends its complete revelation with a whisper of homecoming.

**The Council of Badr and the Youth Who Saw Furthest**

The most celebrated transmitted scene connected with this sūrah is the one preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, the second caliph, used to include the young Ibn ʿAbbās in his consultations with the senior Companions who had fought at Badr. Some of the elders wondered aloud why a mere youth should be admitted to their company when they had sons of his age. ʿUmar, who never flattered anyone, told them they knew the young man's rank. Then one day he put a question to the assembly, as though administering an examination: what do you make of the sūrah, *"When God's Help and Victory come"*? The elders answered with what the surface offers: that when God grants His Prophet help and victory, he is commanded to praise God and seek His forgiveness. Ibn ʿAbbās said nothing. ʿUmar turned to him directly. And the youth — he was still only in his teens when the Prophet died, yet he would become known as *Turjumān al-Qurʾān*, "the Interpreter of the Qur'an" — gave the reading that has governed the sūrah's understanding ever since:

> *"It is the taking leave of the Messenger of God ﷺ. God informed him: 'When God's Help and Victory come' — that is the sign of your appointed term — 'so hymn the praise of your Lord and seek forgiveness from Him; He is Ever Relenting.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3627; also transmitted in his Tafsīr of the sūrah)

And ʿUmar — the man whose word could shake palaces — said: "I know of it no more than what you say." The scene deserves slow reflection. The two readings do not actually contradict one another; the elders answered what the sūrah *commands*, and Ibn ʿAbbās answered what it *means* — why that command comes precisely at that moment. But the exchange fixed several lessons in the tradition: that the young may see what the old miss; that rank in Islam is measured by understanding, not age or seniority; and that a ruler secure enough to be corrected by a youth is a ruler secure in truth. Behind Ibn ʿAbbās's insight lay a childhood prayer: he reported that the Prophet ﷺ once embraced him and prayed, *"O God, grant him understanding of the religion and teach him the interpretation (taʾwīl)"* — a prayer preserved in the Musnad of Aḥmad and graded authentic by al-Ḥākim. The entire science of tafsīr is, in one sense, the ongoing answer to that prayer.

**A Biography in Miniature: The Architecture of Three Verses**

Read as a whole, the sūrah traces the entire arc of the prophetic mission in three movements. The first verse names God's act — help and opening — which had sustained the mission from Badr to Ḥunayn. The second verse names the visible result — mankind entering God's religion in throngs — the harvest of twenty-three years of preaching, patience, persecution, and war. The third verse names the only fitting response — praise and forgiveness-seeking — and then seals everything with a name of God, *al-Tawwāb*, the Ever-Relenting, as though the final word of the complete revelation were not an image of triumph at all but an image of the open door of return. The classical commentators, al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī among them, all noted how the sūrah answers the whole Makkan drama: the man who was once stoned at Ṭāʾif, boycotted in the valley of Abū Ṭālib, and driven from his city now sees the same city opened and the tribes that fought him coming to him in delegations. In this the sūrah functions like the final scene of a long drama, in which every earlier scene is suddenly illuminated.

There is also a quiet signature in the arrangement of the Qur'an itself. In the muṣḥaf, an-Naṣr is followed by Sūrat al-Masad (111) — the short Makkan chapter foretelling the ruin of Abū Lahab, the Prophet's arch-enemy — and preceded by Sūrat al-Kāfirūn (109), *"To you your religion, to me mine."* The closing triptych of the Book thus reads: separation from unbelief, victory and mass entry into the faith, and the end of the implacable enemy. And within the sūrah, the final complete revelation closes not with a law but with an attribute — He is Ever-Relenting — so that the Book's last complete message to the community is, before anything else, an assurance about God's mercy toward those who turn back to Him.

**How the Earliest Community Lived the Sūrah**

The sūrah did not remain ink on parchment; it became practice within days. ʿĀʾishah, the Prophet's wife, reported that in the period after this revelation the Messenger of God ﷺ used to say constantly in the bowing and prostration of his prayers:

> *"Glory be to Thee, O God, our Lord, and by Thy praise; O God, forgive me."* (Muslim 484)

She explained that he was *interpreting the Qur'an* — that is, consciously acting out verse 3 of this very sūrah: the glorification (*sabbīḥ*) in his bowing, the seeking of forgiveness (*istighfār*) in his prostration. Ibn ʿAbbās, transmitting through al-Bukhārī's Tafsīr of this sūrah, drew the same map: the command of tasbīḥ falls in the bowing, the command of forgiveness-seeking in the prostration. Thus the last complete sūrah was immediately woven into the daily liturgy of every prayer, and it remains there: every time a worshipper bows and prostrates reciting the formula the Prophet used, the farewell sūrah of the Qur'an is being enacted in living posture. A revelation about the end of a mission thereby became a permanent habit of the community the mission left behind.

**How to Read What Follows**

What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary in the mandated method: each of the three verses is treated as a complete, independent discourse — with the Arabic vocabulary and roots unpacked simply, the Qur'anic cross-references quoted in their actual wording, the authentic ḥadīth cited with its collection identified, the views of the classical exegetes (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Taymiyyah and others) set out where relevant, the occasion of revelation and its historical context restored, and the logical, psychological, and practical meaning drawn out for the reader of today. Short sūrahs are not shallow sūrahs: the Qur'an itself compares some of its shortest chapters to mountains, and the tradition records that the Prophet ﷺ would sometimes stand long in the night prayer reciting a single short chapter as though it were the whole Book. An-Naṣr has been called the sūrah of an ending; it is equally, as will be seen, a manual for what every human being must do whenever God's help arrives in his own small life — which is, in the end, the only ending any of us is guaranteed to share with the Prophet ﷺ.

## Sūrah an-Naṣr 110:1

> **When God's Help and Victory come**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence of Three Words That Contains a History**

The Arabic of the opening is astonishingly compact: *Idhā jāʾa naṣru Allāhi wa-al-fatḥ* — "When there comes God's help and the opening." Four words in the original, and within them the entire biography of a mission. There is no subject announcing a decree, no ornamental preamble, no oath, no letters of the alphabet — the sūrah simply begins in the middle of an event, the way a man arrives breathless with news and speaks first of the thing itself. Al-Ṭabarī, in his commentary on this verse, transmits the understanding of the early generations that this is the language of someone reporting a matter already understood between speaker and listener: God and His Messenger need no introduction to each other's subject. The verse is addressed to a man who had waited twenty-some years for these words, and the words know they have been waited for.

The structure is a conditional: *idhā* — "when" — followed by a verb in the perfect tense, *jāʾa* — "has come." Classical Arabic rhetoric has a precise device here that must be understood, because the entire force of the verse depends on it: the Arabs used the past tense for a future event when they wanted to say that the event is *certain* — so certain that one may speak of it as though it had already happened. This is sometimes called the perfect of realization (*taḥqīq*). The Qur'an uses the same device at the head of Sūrah al-Naḥl: *"The command of God has come (atā amru Allāhi), so do not seek to hasten it"* (Qur'an 16:1) — a command still future, described as already arrived. In our verse, the victory is not a hope or a possibility; it is a fact which the future merely has to catch up with. The listener receives the sentence the way one receives a confirmed departure time, not a weather forecast.

**Naṣr: The Arabic of Rescue and Vindication**

The first noun, *naṣr*, deserves a slow look, because the sūrah is named after it. The root *n-ṣ-r* means to come to the aid of one who is overmatched — to support, strengthen, and stand with someone until he prevails. It carries the sense of rescue as much as of triumph. The one who receives it is *manṣūr*, "vindicated"; the one who gives it is *naṣīr*, "a helper." The Qur'an uses the noun form of God in this exact sense: *"And sufficient is your Lord as a guide and a helper (wa-kafā bi-rabbika hādīan wa-naṣīrā)"* (Qur'an 25:31), and *"God knows your enemies better; and God suffices as a protector, and God suffices as a helper (wa-kafā bi-Allāhi naṣirā)"* (Qur'an 4:45). The word family appears constantly in the Qur'an's account of the struggle between the first Muslim community and its enemies, and it is never abstract. It describes God's concrete interventions at Badr, at the Trench, at Ḥudaybiyah's aftermath, and at the conquest of Makkah.

Now observe the grammar of attribution. The verse does not say "when victory comes" in the abstract; it says *naṣru Allāh* — "God's help" — a possessive construction (*iḍāfa*) that binds the victory to its Owner. This is the Qur'an's relentless theological grammar, and it is everywhere in the Book. At Badr, when the Muslims were three hundred-odd against a force perhaps three times their number, the Qur'an insisted: *"God had already given you victory at Badr, when you were weak and humbled (qad nasarakumu Allāhu bi-Badrin wa-antum adhillah)"* (Qur'an 3:123). It then drew the lesson into a maxim that no army, committee, or genius can outgrow: *"Victory comes only from God (wa-mā al-naṣru illā min ʿindi Allāhi), the Mighty, the Wise"* (Qur'an 3:126). And it sealed the point with a sentence that removes human credit from the decisive moments of history altogether:

> *"It was not you who slew them, but God who slew them; and it was not you who threw when you threw, but God who threw."* (Qur'an 8:17)

The verse under commentary opens with that same grammar: the very first word after "when" is *God's* — His help, His victory, His act. Before any human being is mentioned at all, before any response is commanded, the sūrah fixes the ownership of the achievement. Everything that follows in the chapter — the crowds, the praise, the forgiveness — flows from that single attribution.

**The Logic of Attributing Victory to God Alone**

It is worth asking *why* the Qur'an is so insistent on this point, because to a modern ear the attribution can sound like religious formality. The answer lies in the psychology of success, which the Qur'an diagnoses with unsparing accuracy. Failure humbles; success intoxicates. The surges of confidence that follow a victory are precisely when human beings begin to believe in their own self-sufficiency, and the Qur'an names this danger in one of its earliest revelations: *"Indeed, man rebels (la-yatghā) as soon as he sees himself self-sufficient (an yarāhu istaghnā)"* (Qur'an 96:6–7). The word *ṭughyān* — rebellion, overstepping, tyranny — is attached not to the wicked in their wickedness but to the *successful* in their success. The sequence is ancient and repeatable: a people are weak and dependent, God opens a door for them, they pass through, and within a generation they attribute the door to their own hands — and then they become what they once suffered under.

Attributing the victory to God is therefore not a courtesy; it is a survival mechanism. The Qur'an makes the causality explicit in a verse that functions as the covenant behind our sūrah: *"O you who believe, if you help God, He will help you and plant your feet firm (in tanṣurū Allāha yanṣurkum wa-yuthabbit aqdāmakum)"* (Qur'an 47:7) — and, with the other face of the same coin, *"If God helps you, none can overcome you; and if He forsakes you, who is there to help you after Him? (wa-in yakhudhka Allāhu fa-man dhā alladhī yanṣuruka min baʿdih?)"* (Qur'an 3:160). Human effort is real — the Qur'an never erases it — but it is the *condition* of help, never the *source* of it. An analogy may make the principle tangible. A patient who survives a critical illness through the skill of surgeons does not, on recovery, credit his own white blood cells for the survival and dismiss the surgeons. Every honest accounting of a complex outcome traces it through layers of enabling causes to a final anchor. The Qur'an simply insists on following the chain to its end: behind the strategy is the mind, behind the mind the life that sustains it, behind the life the One who gave it — *"God, there is no god but He, the Living, the Self-Subsisting"* (Qur'an 2:255). At the moment of victory, when every lesser cause is clamoring for credit, the sūrah begins with the anchor.

**Fatḥ: When One Word Means Opening, Victory, and Decree at Once**

The second noun, *al-fatḥ*, is one of the richest words in the Qur'anic vocabulary. Its root *f-t-ḥ* means "to open," and from it the Qur'an builds an astonishing range of meanings, all present by implication here. The first is literal: the opening of a city — the conquest of Makkah, which the tradition almost universally reads into this verse. The second is victory itself, by extension: an enemy's closed fist is "opened," his power pried loose. The third is the opening of locked things in general — hearts, provisions, destinies: *"What God opens (mā yaftaḥu Allāhu) for mankind of mercy, none can withhold"* (Qur'an 35:2). The fourth is adjudication — the "opening" of a dispute, the handing down of a decisive verdict: the prophets of old pray, *"Decide between us and our people with truth, and You are the best of deciders (wa-anta khayru al-fātiḥīn)"* (Qur'an 7:89). And the fifth is the opening of a chapter — the very word the Arabs use for the Fātiḥah, "the Opening," and the word *fātiḥah* itself.

Every one of these senses enriches the verse. God's opening of Makkah was simultaneously a military victory, an unbolting of a city locked against Him for twenty-one years, a verdict rendered in history between truth and its deniers, and the opening of a new era — the era of the throngs. The Qur'an had already consecrated the word in the strongest possible way at Ḥudaybiyah. In 6/628, the Muslims set out for ʿumrah and were blocked; the expedition ended not in battle but in a treaty whose terms — to the Muslims then, and especially to ʿUmar — looked like humiliation: they would return home without performing the pilgrimage, and a truce of ten years was signed on terms favorable to Quraysh. It was precisely then, on the road home, that God revealed:

> *"Indeed, We have granted you a manifest opening (innā fatḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnā)."* (Qur'an 48:1)

The sīrah preserves the scene: Companions bewildered, ʿUmar voicing his anguish at the terms — and revelation descending to name the apparent setback *the manifest victory*. Within two years the meaning was visible to everyone: the treaty broke the boycott, allowed the tribes to hear Islam without fear of reprisal, and set the stage for Makkah itself. When Quraysh later breached the treaty, the road to the conquest lay open. This is the divine pedagogy hidden in the word *fatḥ*: what human beings see as a locked door is often God's hand already on the bolt. The Prophet's own vision had been fulfilled to the letter, as the Qur'an itself attests: *"God has indeed fulfilled the vision of His Messenger in truth: you shall enter the Sacred Mosque, God willing, in security, heads shaven or hair shortened, without fear"* (Qur'an 48:27).

**The Reading That Doubles the Letter: Ibn ʿAbbās and "Izzā Jāʾa"**

The commentary tradition preserves a fascinating non-canonical reading of this very word *idhā*. Ibn ʿAbbās — the same Companion whose interpretation fixed the sūrah's farewell meaning — is reported in the commentary literature, recorded by al-Qurṭubī, to have read the opening with a doubled consonant: *Izzā jāʾa naṣru Allāhi wa-al-fatḥ*. The doubled form intensifies. Some of the early grammarians explained it as emphasis upon the immediacy of the event — "as soon as God's help comes" — the doubling miming the very intensity it describes. Others heard in it a note of completion: "it has indeed already come." The canonical reading of the ten reciters remains *idhā*, but the transmitted variant is not a curiosity; it is a window into how the first generations pressed on this verse with their whole weight, feeling in it the tremor of a completed era. When a text announces that something is finished, readers who lived through the finishing reach for stronger grammar.

**Which Victory Is Meant? The Debate and Its Harmony**

The exegetes differed — instructively — over which historical event the verse's two nouns point to. Each reading has a face, and together they form a layered harmony rather than a dispute.

The majority — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī among them — read the verse in the past tense, as an allusion to the conquest of Makkah in Ramadan of 8/630. On this reading, the verse is a description of what had already come: God's help at Ḥunayn immediately after the conquest, the consolidation of Arabia, and the tide of conversions that followed. A second opinion, transmitted by al-Ṭabarī and followed by al-Ālūsī, sees the reference to the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyah of 6/628 — the *fatḥan mubīnā* of Sūrah 48 — so that this sūrah's "opening" is the same opening the Qur'an had already so named. A third group, recorded by al-Qurṭubī and al-Ālūsī, read the verb in the future tense and saw a prophecy still standing: the conquests by which Islam would spread beyond Arabia after the Prophet's death — Persia, the Levant, Egypt — all of them still in the future when the verse came down. And beyond the historical readings lies the continuous one: attaining Paradise is the final victory, and every believer's death in a state of faith is, for him, the true conquest of Makkah. In this vein the mystical commentator al-Baqlī writes:

> *"The Victory (fatḥ) is the opening (infitāḥ) of the doors of arrival and the unveiling of the lights of Beauty and Majesty."*

The readings do not compete; they nest. The conquest of Makkah is the manifest instance; Ḥudaybiyah is its seed; the later conquests are its fruit; and the individual soul's arrival at its Lord is its meaning. The Qur'an itself teaches this way of reading, describing its verses as *mutashābihāt* that "resemble" one another (cf. 3:7) — its events functioning at once as history and as pattern.

**"God's Help" in the Turning Points of the Sīrah**

To feel the weight of *naṣru Allāh* in this verse, one must walk briefly through the mission it summarizes. At Badr (2/624), the decisive battle of Islam's existence, the Muslims were badly outnumbered; the Qur'an describes God's help in terms of unseen forces and a firming of hearts: *"And God had already given you victory at Badr, when you were weak and humbled. So be conscious of God, that you may be grateful"* (Qur'an 3:123). At the Trench (5/627), a confederation of ten thousand besieged Madinah, and the siege dissolved not by sword but by wind and invisible hosts: *"O you who believe, remember God's favor upon you when hosts came against you, and We sent against them a wind and hosts you did not see"* (Qur'an 33:9). At Ḥudaybiyah (6/628), the "defeat" that the Qur'an named a victory. At Ḥunayn (8/630), when a sudden rout panicked the Muslims in the very hour of their new power after Makkah's conquest, the Qur'an again marked who turned the day: *"…then God sent down His tranquility upon His Messenger and upon the believers, and sent down hosts you did not see"* (Qur'an 9:26). Notice the pattern across every scale of crisis: the material cause is never the decisive one, and the Qur'an never lets the Muslims forget it. Sūrah an-Naṣr, coming at the end, is the summing of all these interventions under one heading — *naṣru Allāhi wa-al-fatḥ* — the ledger's final column.

**The Law of Hardship Before Opening**

Why, one may ask, did God structure the mission this way — thirteen years of persecution in Makkah, years of hunger, boycott, and war in Madinah — before the opening came? The Qur'an answers the question directly, and its answer is a law of the moral universe:

> *"Or did you think that you would enter the Garden while there had not yet come upon you the like of what came upon those who passed away before you? Affliction and adversity touched them, and they were shaken until the Messenger and those who believed with him said, 'When comes God's help?' Ah, surely God's help is near."* (Qur'an 2:214)

The verse is startling: even the Messenger of God reaches the point of asking "when?" — and the question is not rebuked but answered with nearness. The delay of *naṣr* is not negligence; it is formation. Steel is not made by sparing the ore the furnace. The community that would one day receive the world in throngs had first to be a community that had learned to depend on nothing but God — because the throngs would bring wealth, power, and celebrity, each of which corrodes an unformed soul. The Qur'an states the principle in a pair of verses so merciful they are recited at every Muslim funeral: *"So truly, with hardship comes ease — truly, with hardship comes ease"* (Qur'an 94:5–6). The Arabic repeats the noun *al-ʿusr* with the definite article both times — it is the *same* hardship — but the ease is mentioned twice, and the commentators noted the arithmetic of hope: one hardship, two eases, because the ease that follows a difficulty contains, within itself, the memory of relief that fortifies for the next trial. The opening verse of an-Naṣr is the promise of 2:214 and 94:5–6 kept, in history, before the eyes of the Messenger himself.

**Why the Verse Is Conditional at All**

One final grammatical observation yields a theological ocean. The verse does not say "God's help has come, full stop"; it says *when* God's help comes. The conditional does two things. First, it binds the community's expectations to God's timing rather than its demands — victory arrives when *He* sends it, not when impatience schedules it, and the interval between the two is precisely where faith is exercised. Second, and more subtly, it makes the verse portable: the conditional form reaches beyond the Prophet's lifetime to every subsequent "opening" the community or any believer will experience. God has bound Himself, in the Qur'an's own words, to a standing law: *"…and God will surely help him who helps Him (wa-layanaṣuranna Allāhu man yanṣuruhu); truly God is Strong, Mighty"* (Qur'an 22:40) — a verse revealed, remarkably, in defense of the very monasteries and churches of other faith communities, showing how wide God's help extends along the axis of justice. The conditionality is thus an invitation: the verse's "when" is addressed, across the centuries, to anyone who chooses to be part of the condition. The command that follows in verse 3 will show the other half of the covenant — what the helped one owes when the help arrives.

**Standing Under the Verse Today**

The verse is not a museum piece about the seventh century; it is a discipline for every life in which something finally breaks open. The believer who attributes his recovery, his provision, his cleared name, his reconciled family, or his surviving city to God's *naṣr* is not practicing false modesty; he is reading the causal chain to its anchor. And the attribution has a practical yield: the person who credits God at the summit is far harder to corrupt at the summit. Leaders who whisper "this was God's help" over their successes keep, on average, the humility that their subordinates desperately need them to keep; organizations that institutionalize gratitude keep the cultures that survive success. The verse's opening word *idhā* still stands at the start of every one of these moments, waiting — as it waited for the Prophet ﷺ — to be filled in.

## Sūrah an-Naṣr 110:2

> **and you see mankind entering God's religion in throngs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Ear of Report to the Eye of Witness**

The second verse opens with two small Arabic words that carry a whole theology of certainty: *wa-raʾayta* — "and you see." The verb is in the imperfect tense, the tense of ongoing and future action, and it is addressed to a single listener: the Prophet ﷺ. Everything in the mission until now had rested on report — promises recited, prophecies trusted, setbacks absorbed on the strength of unseen assurances. The Qur'an had told the Muslims at their weakest: *"God has promised those of you who believe and do righteous deeds that He will surely make them successors on the earth, as He made those before them successors"* (Qur'an 24:55) — a promise the first hearers received with their ears while burying their dead. Here, at last, the promise crosses from the ear to the eye. The Prophet is told he will *see*. In the ladder of knowledge that Islamic epistemology has always recognized — the report of a trustworthy informant (*khabar*), then inference, then eyewitness seeing (*ruʾyah*) — the verse moves the Prophet to the top rung concerning the harvest of his own mission. God does not merely keep His word; He lets His servant watch Him keep it. There is a courtesy in this, an intimacy: the farmer is not simply mailed the harvest statistics; he is brought to the field.

The particle *wa* — "and" — is also doing quiet work. It welds verse 2 to verse 1 so tightly that many reciters experience the three verses as one breath: when the help comes *and you see* the throngs entering — then do the command of verse 3. The seeing is not incidental to the command; it is the visible fact that makes the command intelligible. Praise and forgiveness are the response of a man who has *watched* what God's help produces.

**Al-Nās: The Qur'an's Most Democratic Word**

The object of the seeing is *al-nās* — "mankind," "the people." The word is deliberately unbounded. Not "the Arabs of such-and-such tribe," not "the poor who followed you," not "your Companions," but the generic noun that in Qur'anic usage sweeps across all humanity: it is *al-nās* whom the Qur'an addresses in its most universal invocations — *"O mankind (yā ayyuhā al-nās), worship your Lord who created you and those before you, that you may be conscious"* (Qur'an 2:21); it is *al-nās* from whom the Prophet's own commission is described as inseparable: *"We did not send you except to all mankind (illā kāffatan li-l-nās), as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner"* (Qur'an 34:28); and it is over *al-nās* that his mission is given its universal mandate: *"Say: O mankind, I am the Messenger of God to you all"* (Qur'an 7:158).

The exegetes, whose views the source text preserves, explored the word's reach. Some held that *al-nās* here indicates the Arabs in general; others specified a large body from Yemen who had accepted Islam shortly before; others kept the word's full span. The disagreement itself is fruitful. If the verse meant only one generation of Arabs, the sūrah would be an obituary for a moment; if it means mankind as mankind, then the verb of the verse — as the next section will show — stretches its arms across centuries. And the deeper point stands on the Qur'an's own authority: a religion whose Prophet is sent to all mankind (34:28) and described as *"a mercy to the worlds"* (Qur'an 21:107) cannot have its final victory-portrait painted in tribal colors. The throngs of the verse are, by the Book's own logic, humanity itself beginning to move.

**Yadkhulūna: The Tense of a Movement Still in Motion**

The grammar of "entering" — *yadkhulūna*, an imperfect verb, again the tense of the unfinished — is one of the most quietly remarkable features of the verse. The language does not say "mankind has entered," a closed report; it says mankind *is entering, enters, will be entering* — the picture is of a stream, not a lake. The classical exegetes drew the natural consequence, and al-Qurṭubī and al-Ālūsī record it: read in the future, the verse also embraces the conquests and conversions after the Prophet's death. The imperfect tense simply refuses to stop at the year 11/632. And history has kept verifying the tense. Within two decades of the Prophet's death, the community of Madinah had spread through the two superpowers' territories — the Persian empire fell at al-Qādisiyyah and Nahāvand, the Byzantine provinces of Syria and Egypt opened; then North Africa, then Spain, then Central Asia and the Indus valley; then, centuries later, carried not by armies at all but by merchants and scholars, the faith entered the Malay archipelago — today Indonesia, the most populous Muslim land on earth — and the vast sweep of West Africa along the trade routes of the Sahara. The Qur'an had announced the trajectory in words the community recited long before it could have verified them:

> *"He it is who sent His Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth, to make it prevail over all religion (li-yuẓhirahu ʿalā al-dīni kullih), though the idolaters be averse."* (Qur'an 61:9; cf. 9:33; 48:28)

The verse of an-Naṣr is that announcement's pastoral close-up: not the map of empires but the eye of a watching Prophet and the image of people walking in.

**Fī Dīni Allāhi: Entering a House Whose Owner Is Named**

The preposition in *yadkhulūna fī dīni Allāhi* — "entering *into* God's religion" — deserves its own pause. One can "accept" an idea or "adopt" a creed, but the Qur'an consistently pictures submission as *entry*, as though religion were a building with a threshold. The same imagery stands in one of al-Baqarah's great commands: *"O you who believe, enter into submission wholly (udkhulū fī al-silmi kāffatan), and do not follow the footsteps of Satan"* (Qur'an 2:208) — where the picture is not signing a document but walking through a doorway, with the choice to bring the whole self inside or loiter on the step. The Qur'an elsewhere completes the architectural metaphor with the word *dār*, "abode": the Hereafter has a *"Home of Peace (dār al-salām)"* to which God calls (Qur'an 10:25), and human life divides, in the Book's idiom, into abodes of guidance and error. To enter God's religion is thus not to add an opinion; it is to take up residence in a house whose rooms are worship, law, ethics, and hope.

And the house is *named after its Owner*: *dīn Allāh* — God's religion, not Muhammad's. The Qur'an had insisted on this from both directions. Positively: *"Truly, the religion with God is submission (inna al-dīna ʿinda Allāhi al-islām)"* (Qur'an 3:19) — the way of life in its final form is God's own institution, continuous in essence with what He gave Abraham, Moses, and Jesus; the Prophet is its courier, not its author: *"I only follow what is revealed to me"* (Qur'an 46:9). And negatively, the Qur'an rebukes the very idea of proprietary religion: *"And they say, 'None shall enter the Garden except one who is a Jew or a Christian.' Those are their hopes. Say: Produce your proof, if you are truthful"* (Qur'an 2:111); it cuts through every branding of faith with the scalpel, *"Truly, those who believe, and the Jews, and the Christians, and the Sabians — whoever believes in God and the Last Day and does righteousness — they have their reward with their Lord"* (Qur'an 2:62) in its Makkan counterpart 2:112's essence; and it describes the prophets of old as *muslimūn*, those who submitted, before the final revelation ever named a community. The word *dīn* itself, from the root *d-y-n*, carries in Arabic the senses of obedience, custom, and reckoning — a way of life that binds now and is settled later; the "religion" of this verse is therefore not a Sunday garment but an accounting relationship with One who will audit it. When the throngs enter *God's* religion, they enter a house that existed before them and whose Landlord outlives them — which is precisely why, as the sūrah's farewell undertone reminds us, the death of the Messenger would not close the door behind him. The door belongs to God, and it stayed open.

**Afwājan: The Vocabulary of Waves**

The final word of the verse, *afwājan* — "in throngs," "in troop after troop" — is the plural of *fawj*: a body of men arriving as a unit, a distinct company large enough to be seen coming before it arrives. The plural piles image on image: not one delegation but many, not a queue of individuals but wave upon wave, each with its dust and its noise. The Qur'an uses the word once elsewhere, in a scene of cosmic scale, and the echo is instructive:

> *"The Day the Trumpet is blown and you come in throngs (wa-yeʾtūna afwājā)."* (Qur'an 78:18)

On the Day of Resurrection, humanity arrives in *afwāj* — companies streaming toward the Judgment. In our sūrah, humanity streams in *afwāj* toward God's religion. The shared word quietly yokes the two scenes: every entry into the faith in this world is a rehearsal of the great assembly of the next; the direction of the crowd on the Last Day is being chosen, company by company, now.

Against this word-picture the commentators set the memory of how different things had been. Before the conquest of Makkah — so the exegetes observe, among them those cited in the source tradition — people entered Islam one, two, a handful at a time, and often in secret. In the first years in Makkah the faith lived in whispers: a merchant here, a slave there, a youth disowned by his family, converts smuggling themselves out to Abyssinia in two separate migrations to escape torture. Bilāl was dragged onto the burning sand; ʿAmmār's mother was killed; Yāsir and Sumayyah, the first martyrs of Islam, were pierced through for refusing to curse the Prophet. Conversion, for thirteen years, was not a movement one joined; it was a sentence one signed. Even in Madinah the growth was hard-earned, treaty by treaty, battle by battle, letter by letter to the kings of the earth. And then — the conquest, Ḥunayn, the Arabia-wide settlements of 9 and 10 AH — and the word has to change from "one or two" to *afwājan*. The contrast is the verse's own built-in miracle-witness: the Prophet lived to see both economies of conversion, the era of the individual and the era of the throng, and only One who rules history could have timed the switch.

**The Historical Fulfillment: The Year of Delegations**

The historical books give the verse its full illustration. After the conquest of Makkah (8/630) and the campaigns that secured it, the year 9 AH earned its recorded name: *ʿām al-wufūd*, "the Year of Delegations." From every region of Arabia — the provinces of Yemen, the tribes of Najd, the highlands of Ḥaḍramawt, the oases of the interior — delegations arrived in Madinah, season after season, to declare the entry of their peoples into the faith. The chroniclers of the sīrah and the ṭabaqāt, Ibn Saʿd foremost among them, devote entire books to cataloguing them: Thaqīf of Ṭāʾif, whose siege of the Prophet had once shed his blood, came and entered; Hawāzin, beaten at Ḥunayn, came and entered; the proud horsemen of Tamīm came, and their encounter with the Muslims' own orator Thābit ibn Qays and poet Ḥassān ibn Thābit in the Prophet's mosque became a set-piece of Arab literary history; the delegation of Kinda arrived in silk and gold to find the Prophet sitting in the dust with his Companions; the Christian princes of Najrān came and were lodged in the mosque itself, praying undisturbed in their own manner — an episode the Qur'an had already prepared for: *"And dispute not with the People of the Book except in the fairest manner"* (Qur'an 29:46) in the broader covenant of courtesy. Ibn ʿAbbās, whom the caliphs would later consult as the interpreter of the Book, was a young man in Madinah watching this horizon-wide procession file in — the very spectacle the verse had promised his master: *wa-raʾayta al-nāsa yadkhulūna fī dīni Allāhi afwājā*.

Why did the dam break precisely then? The commentators, and the source text with them, give the sociological key: as long as Makkah — the sanctuary city, the home of the Kaʿbah, the hub of Arabian religion — stood with Quraysh against the Prophet, the tribal mind read Islam as a local, losing bid; the chiefs who waited on events were waiting, as chiefs do, for the risk calculus to tip. When the Prophet entered the city of his birth not as a vengeful exile but as a liberator who had taken it with scarcely a sword drawn; when he cleansed the Kaʿbah of its idols and proclaimed *"Truth has come and falsehood has vanished"* (Qur'an 17:81) over its precincts; when the man they had stoned at Ṭāʾif forgave the city that stoned him — the final obstacle in the Arabian imagination fell. What changed was not merely the map but the *moral* map: the old order had visibly ended without the sky falling, and the man at its center had visibly refused to become a tyrant in victory. The throngs followed the evidence.

**Quality and Quantity: Reading the Throngs Without Naivety**

An honest commentary must also ask the harder question: were all the entrants sincere? The Qur'an itself, revealed into this very era, distinguishes grades of belief: it names *"the Bedouins who say, 'We believe' (qul aslamnā — say: you have not believed; rather say, 'we have submitted')"* (Qur'an 49:14) — an exact portrait of the throng-era entrant whose entry was, at least at first, external alignment rather than inward conviction. And the test was not long in coming: when the Prophet ﷺ died in 11/632, much of Arabia rose in apostasy — the *ridda* — and it fell to Abū Bakr, the gentlest of the elders, to hold the community together with a firmness that startled even ʿUmar. The lesson embedded in that trial is the sūrah's hidden realism: the verse announces *entry*, and entry is where the spiritual life begins, not where it is guaranteed. God's book had said from the earliest days: *"Of mankind are those who say, 'We believe in God,' but if they are afflicted in God's cause they take the affliction of mankind as God's punishment"* (Qur'an 29:10) — belief, once professed, must then be *lived into*. The first community held because the individual-era core — the men and women formed in the furnace of Makkah — and the truly Godward among the throngs stood together; the roots survived the storm that stripped the leaves. For every later age the application is sobering: the health of a religious community is measured not by the size of its crowds but by the depth of its roots, and roots grow slowly, one soul at a time — which is why the era of secret conversions was not the mission's weakness but its foundation.

**The Quiet Miracle in the Grammar: Faith Moves, It Is Not Dragged**

Return, finally, to the verb: *yadkhulūna* — "they enter." The grammatical subject is the people themselves. They are not "brought in," "conquered into," "registered," or "dragged"; the verb is active and voluntary. This is not a small stylistic point; it is the whole Qur'anic doctrine of conversion compressed into a mood and a voice. The Book forbids coercion in terms that ended religious conquest-by-compulsion as a matter of divine law: *"There is no compulsion in religion (lā ikrāha fī al-dīni); the right course has become distinct from error"* (Qur'an 2:256). It asks the Prophet, rhetorically, whether he could manufacture belief by force: *"Would you then compel mankind (a-fa-anta tukrihu al-nāsa) until they become believers?"* (Qur'an 10:99). And it teaches that hearts are turned by God alone: *"Whom God wills to guide, He opens his breast to submission; and whom He wills to confound, He makes his breast narrow and constricted"* (Qur'an 6:125). History bore out the grammar: the throngs of the Year of Delegations were not defeated armies marched into a faith; they were delegations that *traveled to* the Prophet — some across the whole peninsula — to make their declaration. The same pattern would repeat wherever the faith truly spread: entered by merchants along the monsoon routes, by scholars along the Silk Road, by the sheer conduct of believers whose honesty had to be asked about. A modern reader may verify the principle at any scale: belief that is chosen endures pressure; belief that is imposed evaporates with the imposer. The Qur'an built its world-community on the durable kind.

**The Verse as a Standing Commission**

For the community that outlived the Prophet, this verse has always carried a quiet imperative: the *seeing* was his; the *entering* continues to be mankind's; and between the two stands, in every generation, the community whose conduct is the door through which the throngs walk. The Qur'an defines the ideal doorkeeper with precision, in a verse addressed to the Prophet but describing the method of all who followed him: *"It was by the mercy of God that you were lenient with them (fa-bi-mā raḥmatin mina Allāhi linta lahum); had you been harsh and hard of heart, they would have dispersed from around you (la-infaddū min ḥawlik)"* (Qur'an 3:159). Harshness disperses; it is the one command in the verse stated as a counterfactual — even the Messenger's severity would have emptied his own circle. The same Book crowns the calling of every believer with a question no calling outranks: *"And who is better in speech than one who calls to God and does right and says, 'I am of those who submit' (wa-man aḥsanu qawlan mimman daʿā ilā Allāhi wa-ʿamila ṣāliḥan wa-qāla innanī mina al-muslimīn)?"* (Qur'an 41:33). The verse of the throngs thus remains, fourteen centuries on, a commission and a mirror: if the age of the throngs is in any sense still open — and the imperfect tense, and the movements of peoples into the faith across the modern world, suggest it is — then every Muslim stands either as a door through which someone may enter, or as a wall at which someone turns away. The Prophet saw the crowd from the pulpit of Minā; the crowd, in every generation, still sees the Muslims first.

## Sūrah an-Naṣr 110:3

> **Hymn the praise of thy Lord, and seek forgiveness from Him. Truly He is Ever Relenting.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command That Follows the Victory: Praise Before Celebration**

The Arabic opening is one word: *fa-sabbīḥ* — "so glorify!" — and the little prefix *fa* is load-bearing. In Arabic rhetoric, the *fa* of sequence (*faʾ al-taʿqīb*) ties a consequence to its cause with immediacy: when — the moment — God's help comes and you see the throngs, *then*, at once, this is what happens next. The verse thereby stages a small drama of response. A conquest has occurred; a man stands at the summit of his life's work; the world is at his feet; every human instinct says the next scene is the celebration — the triumphal procession, the distribution of honors, the monument. Revelation writes a different stage direction: praise of God and the seeking of forgiveness. It is one of the most quietly revolutionary sentences in scripture, and the Prophet ﷺ enacted it to the letter. The sīrah records that on the day he entered Makkah — the city that had mocked him, starved him, expelled him, and killed his followers — he came in with his head bowed low, so low, the chroniclers say, that his beard nearly touched his saddle-bow: an emperor's posture inverted into a servant's. He forgave the population with words that consciously borrowed the pardon Joseph spoke to the brothers who had sold him — *"No blame upon you this day" (lā tathrība ʿalaykumu al-yawm)* (Qur'an 12:92) — and released them with the words, "Go, you are free," as preserved in the sīrah and Dalāʾil literature. That is what *fa-sabbīḥ* looked like in a human body.

**Sabbīḥ: A Feminine Ending and a Masculine Addressee**

The morphology of the command rewards attention. The verb *sabbaḥa* — to glorify, to declare transcendent — forms its imperative to a male addressee as *sabbih*; yet here the text reads *sabbīḥ*, with the feminine ending. The addressee is the Prophet, a man, so the form asks for explanation, and the classical grammarians and exegetes gave several complementary ones. Al-Farrāʾ and al-Zajjāj, the pioneers of Qur'anic grammar, identified this as the feminine imperative of intensity and address-softening (muwāṭaʾah) — a form of command that in Arabic can strike the ear with a special firmness or urgency, the doubled consonant and the long vowel stretching the summons. Others read the addressee as the Prophet's *nafs*, his soul — a feminine noun in Arabic — as though the command pierces past the public man to the private self: the soul itself is summoned to glorify, not merely the tongue. Still others noted that the same root elsewhere addresses the heavens and the earth, femininely conceived, and heard in the form an echo of cosmic address — as though the command given to the Messenger merges his voice with the voice of creation. Whichever analysis one prefers, the effect on the ear is the same: the imperative is marked, deliberate, heightened. Scripture does not ask; it does not suggest; at the moment of victory it issues one clear summons with extra weight on it.

It is worth noticing, too, that this is not the first such command in the Prophet's life. Years earlier, amid the mockery of the Quraysh, the same instruction had come in identical words addressed to him: *"Then glorify with the praise of your Lord and be among those who prostrate (fa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika wa-kun mina al-sājidīn)"* (Qur'an 15:98) — the command then being consolation in defeat's climate. Now, in an-Naṣr, the same phrase returns as coronation in victory's climate. The two verses bookend the Prophet's public career and teach its most personal lesson: the same act — glorifying God — steadies the servant in humiliation and sanctifies him in triumph; only the circumstances change, never the anchor.

**Tasbīḥ Bi-Ḥamd: Two Acts of the Tongue in One Phrase**

The command's object is a prepositional phrase of enormous density: *bi-ḥamdi rabbika* — "with the praise of your Lord." The full construction, *sabbih bi-ḥamd*, joins two distinct acts of worship into one motion. *Tasbīḥ* — from the root *s-b-ḥ* — is the declaration that God is *beyond* everything: beyond likeness, defect, partner, limit, and every description creatures can devise; its liturgical expression, *subḥān Allāh*, has been glossed by the tradition as "Glory to God above all that they attribute to Him." *Ḥamd* is praise-with-gratitude, the affirmation of God's perfections as they touch the praiser — His generosity, His help, His guidance; its liturgical expression, *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh*, is the Qur'an's own first verse after His name. Joined, the phrase performs a double motion of the soul: God is infinitely above all my descriptions (*tasbīḥ*) — and everything I can truly say of Him is already more than I deserve (*ḥamd*). This is why the scholars, as the source commentary notes, see in *sabbih bi-ḥamd* "the recognition that God is beyond all that anyone can attribute to Him": praise given while remembering that the One praised exceeds the praising. The Qur'an wields the same compound phrase across the whole register of revelation — in the mouths of the angels bearing the Throne: *"…and we glorify with Your praise (nu-sabbiḥu bi-ḥamdika)"* (Qur'an 40:7); in the Psalms-shaped instruction to David: *"…and glorify with the praise of your Lord before the rising of the sun and before its setting"* (Qur'an 20:130, cf. 50:39; 52:48–49); and, most breathtakingly, in the description of the entire cosmos at work:

> *"The seven heavens and the earth, and all within them, glorify Him; and there is nothing that does not glorify with His praise (wa-in min shayʾin illā yusabbiḥu bi-ḥamdih), though you do not understand their glorification."* (Qur'an 17:44)

When the Prophet is commanded at his moment of victory to glorify with praise, he is being told to step, knowingly, into a chorus that is already continuous — the electrons and the whales, the falling water and the orbiting stars. The sūrah's moment of maximum human achievement is folded back into the universe's permanent occupation. Vanity has nowhere to stand.

**"Thy Lord": The Naming at the Moment of Intimacy**

The verse does not say "glorify God" in the abstract; it says *rabbika* — *your* Lord. The noun *rabb* names the one who creates, owns, sustains, educates, and manages — a Lord in the fullest, warmest sense: the master who raises what he owns, as a guardian raises a ward. And the possessive pronoun is a compressed biography. The one commanded to glorify *his* Lord is the orphan of ʿAbd Allāh, the son of Āminah, the boy of the Banū Saʿd, the shepherd of Makkah's hills — of whom God had said, in one of the tenderest interrogations in scripture:

> *"Did He not find you an orphan, and shelter you? And find you astray, and guide you? And find you in want, and enrich you?"* (Qur'an 93:6–8)

The *rabb* who commanded the glorification is the same *rabb* who sheltered the orphan, guided the seeker, enriched the poor man, raised the reviled into the honored, and — the verse implies — who will now receive him back. It is the same possessive comfort the Qur'an had given the Prophet's closest friend in the cave on the night of the Hijrah, when bounty-hunters stood at the mouth of their hiding place: *"Do not grieve; God is with us (lā taḥzan inna Allāha maʿanā)"* (Qur'an 9:40). From the cave to the conquest, the pronoun had not changed: *your* Lord — who was yours in the cave and is yours at the summit. For every believer, the grammar is transferable: whatever summit you stand on, the command to glorify names the One who carried you up it, and the naming itself is half the cure for pride.

**Istighfār at the Summit: Why the Prophet Was Told to Seek Forgiveness**

Then comes the phrase that has exercised the commentators more than any other in the sūrah: *wa-staghfirhu* — "and seek forgiveness from Him." On its face it is astonishing. This is the Messenger whom the Qur'an describes as addressed directly, *"God has pardoned you (ʿafā Allāhu ʿanka) — why did you grant them leave?"* (Qur'an 9:43) for no sin at all; whose past and future God had forgiven, as ʿĀʾishah herself testified; whom the Book presents as *"an excellent example (uswatun ḥasanah)"* (Qur'an 33:21). Why is such a man commanded, precisely at the moment of triumph, to seek forgiveness?

The classical explanations — those of al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, and the wider tradition — converge on a layered answer. First, *istighfār as the completion of gratitude*: no servant, not even the Prophet, renders thanks equal to the blessing received, and the consciousness of that shortfall is itself the highest gratitude; the asking of forgiveness at the summit is the confession that even perfect service cannot balance infinite kindness — the Prophet ﷺ himself defined the station in a single question. ʿĀʾishah reported that he would stand in the night prayer until his feet swelled, and when she asked why he did so though God had forgiven him, he answered:

> *"Shall I not be a grateful servant (a-fa-lā akūnu ʿabdan shakūrā)?"* (Muslim 2820)

Second, *istighfār as instruction by example*: the leader of the community models, in his own person, the discipline the followers will need in every age — for if the sinless Prophet sought forgiveness at his victory, what excuse has any governor, general, CEO, or parent at theirs? The Qur'an had stated the pedagogy of the Prophet's example explicitly: *"Indeed, in the Messenger of God you have an excellent example, for whoever hopes in God and the Last Day and remembers God much"* (Qur'an 33:21). Third, *istighfār as a shield at the moment of danger*: success is the trial that failure never is — the Qur'an warns, *"…man rebels as soon as he sees himself self-sufficient"* (Qur'an 96:6–7) — and the commanded confession re-humbles the servant in the very hour the world rises to flatter him. And fourth, as Ibn ʿAbbās's farewell-reading taught: the seeking of forgiveness is the soul's preparation for departure — the last polishing before the meeting. The Prophet's practice confirmed all four layers: he sought forgiveness constantly, not seasonally. He said:

> *"O people, repent to God and seek His forgiveness, for I repent to Him a hundred times a day."* (Muslim 2702)

and, as al-Bukhārī transmits from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"By God, I seek God's forgiveness and turn to Him in repentance more than seventy times a day."* (al-Bukhārī 6307)

ʿĀʾishah, asked what the Prophet did when alone, gave the summary of his entire inner life: *"The Messenger of God ﷺ used to remember God in every state (kāna yadhkuru Allāha ʿalā kulli aḥwānih)."* (Muslim 373) A hundred, seventy — the numbers are not in tension; they describe a man for whom returning to God was breathing, and the sūrah's command merely made that breath audible at history's volume.

**The Ḥamd–Istighfār Pair Enacted in the Prayer Itself**

The tradition preserved something rarer than interpretation: it preserved the *sound* of the Prophet implementing this verse. ʿĀʾishah reported that in the period after an-Naṣr was revealed — the phrase is "in his bowing and his prostration" — he used to say frequently:

> *"Glory be to Thee, O God, our Lord, and by Thy praise; O God, forgive me (Subḥānaka Allāhumma rabbanā wa-bi-ḥamdika, Allāhumma-ghfir lī)."* (Muslim 484; al-Bukhārī transmits it likewise in his Tafsīr of this sūrah)

She explained that he was *interpreting the Qur'an* — meaning, as Ibn ʿAbbās expounded, that the command of tasbīḥ found its home in the bowing of the prayer and the command of istighfār found its home in the prostration: the verse distributed into the body's postures of submission. The insight transforms the way one sees the daily prayer. In rukūʿ, the body folds itself into the posture of a creature before its Creator — the moment for *subḥānaka*, "Glory to Thee," the declaration of God's transcendence; in sujūd, the body descends to its lowest, most intimate, most humble station — the moment for *Allāhumma-ghfir lī*, "O God, forgive me," the plea of return. Every unit of every prayer since has carried the farewell sūrah in its muscles. Al-Qurṭubī, as the source commentary records, took the correspondence one step further: some of the scholars, he transmits, read "hymn the praise of your Lord" as simply meaning *pray* — since the prayer is the very institution in which glorification and forgiveness-seeking are performed in their canonical form. On that reading, the last command of the last complete sūrah is, at bottom, a command to worship — to let the summit of history resolve into the prostration of the creature. And that, in the Qur'an's own idiom, is exactly what prophets do at exactly such moments: the command to the Prophet's father-figure Abraham was, when the finality of the mission dawned: *"…and be among those who prostrate"* (Qur'an 15:98).

**Why Victory Demands Forgiveness-Seeking: The Psychology of Success**

Step back from the exegesis and observe the verse as a piece of moral engineering — because that is what it is. Success intoxicates. It does so through three specific mechanisms that psychology and scripture agree on. First, *self-attribution*: the successful mind quietly rewrites history so that outcomes trace to its own decisions — the very error the sūrah's first verse (naṣru *Allāh*) was written to prevent. Second, *contempt for the defeated*: the victor begins to read his win as a verdict on persons rather than circumstances, and the mercy that winning should produce curdles into arrogance — the Qur'an's history of Israel's scriptures warns precisely of this turn: *"…and do not exult in what He has given you above others"* in the counsel's spirit, and the Prophet's own conduct at Makkah — free pardon, head bowed — was the commanded antidote. Third, *entitlement*: the blessing received becomes the baseline expected, and gratitude dies of inflation — against which the Qur'an legislates the law of increase: *"If you are grateful, I will surely increase you (la-in shakartum la-azīdannakum)"* (Qur'an 14:7), a verse whose reverse reading is a warning: gratitude is not merely the *response* to blessing but the *condition* of its continuation. The scholars of the tradition, Ibn Taymiyyah prominent among them, formalized gratitude into a three-part discipline — gratitude of the heart (knowing the blessing is from God), of the tongue (declaring it, as in *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh*), and of the limbs (using every blessing in obedience to its Giver). The verse of an-Naṣr compresses all three into two imperatives: the tongue's praise, the heart's return, and — by the very act of asking forgiveness for possible shortfalls in thankfulness — the humility that keeps the limbs honest.

There is, finally, a subtle logic in the *pairing* of the two commands that repay thought. Praise without forgiveness-seeking can curdle into smugness — the gratitude of those who secretly believe themselves deserving. Forgiveness-seeking without praise can curdle into despair — the penitence of those who have forgotten the generosity of the One they petition. Together they calibrate: God is infinitely worthy of praise (so the falling-short is real), and infinitely willing to relent (so the return is always worth making). Which is exactly why the verse does not end with the commands. It ends with a reason.

**Innahu Kāna Tawwābā: The Name That Makes Return Always Possible**

The sūrah's final words are its deepest: *inna-hu kāna tawwābā* — "truly, He is Ever-Relenting." Every particle carries weight. *Inna* asserts: this is not a hope but a fact about the divine nature. *Kāna* — "He is," in the Arabic perfect — the grammarians note that *kāna* applied to God's attributes denotes permanence: He *is and has always been and will always be* this; the attribute is not a mood that passes but a name that abides. And *tawwāb* — an intensive form (*faʿʿāl*) that in Arabic grammar conveys repetition and abundance — names the One who relents *again and again and again*: not once per sinner, but once per return; a mercy with no expiry and no quota. The Qur'an pairs this name with mercy wherever it crowns a call to return: *"…and know that God is Ever-Relenting, Wise (inna Allāha tawwābun ḥakīm)"*; *"…God is Ever-Relenting, Merciful (tawwābun raḥīm)"* — the pairings include the judgment-scene of mutual suspicion: *"…and God is Ever-Relenting, Merciful"* (Qur'an 49:12), and the throne-bearers' theology: *"…God is the Ever-Relenting, the Merciful"* (Qur'an 9:104), and the first forgiveness ever granted: *"Then Adam received words from his Lord, and He relented toward him; truly He is the Ever-Relenting, the Merciful (innahu huwa al-tawwābu al-raḥīm)"* (Qur'an 2:37).

That last citation opens the doctrine hidden in the name. In Arabic, the word for man's repentance and God's relenting is the same: *tawbah*, from the root meaning "to turn" — man turns back *to* God in regret, and God turns *toward* man in acceptance. The Qur'an plays the reciprocity with exquisite precision: *"But whoever repents after his wrongdoing and makes amends, God will relent toward him (fa-inna Allāha yatūbu ʿalayhi); truly God is Forgiving, Merciful"* (Qur'an 5:39). And, crucially, the tradition — as the source commentary observes — teaches that the divine turn is not merely reactive but *preceding*: God turns toward the servant first, enabling the servant's turn, as the verse of the three stranded at Ṭabūk states outright: *"…then He relented toward them, that they might repent (fa-tāba ʿalayhim li-yatūbū)"* (Qur'an 9:118) — the order of the verbs is the theology. Repentance, in the Qur'anic economy, is less a scheme the servant executes than a door the Lord holds open. The Prophet ﷺ transmitted God's own description of that door in terms of almost heartbreaking extravagance:

> *"God stretches out His hand by night to accept the repentance of the day's wrongdoer, and stretches out His hand by day to accept the repentance of the night's wrongdoer — until the sun rises from the west."* (Muslim 2759)

and again:

> *"God is more joyful at the repentance of one of His servants than one of you who, traveling through a desolate land, finds his camel — carrying his food and drink — escaped, despairs of ever finding it, lies down in despair, and then wakes to find it standing beside him."* (al-Bukhārī 6309; Muslim 2747)

Read in that light, the final clause of the final complete sūrah is the Qur'an's farewell gift: the last thing the complete revelation wishes fixed in the community's mind is not a rule, a border, or a warning, but a name — *al-Tawwāb* — and the guarantee it carries: that the door the throngs entered by will remain open behind them, and behind everyone after them, for as long as there is a night and a day in which to turn. The Prophet ﷺ summed up the human side of the covenant: *"Every son of Adam errs, and the best of those who err are the ones who repent (khayru al-khaṭṭāʾīna al-tawwābūn)."* (al-Tirmidhī 2499, graded ḥasan) And the tradition never tired of listing what the returning itself unlocks, even in this world — Noah's people were told:

> *"Ask forgiveness of your Lord; truly He is Ever-Forgiving: He will send down upon you rain in abundance, and give you increase in wealth and children, and make for you gardens and rivers."* (Qur'an 71:10–12)

— while the Prophet ﷺ promised: *"Whoever persists in seeking forgiveness, God will appoint for him relief from every distress, a way out of every hardship, and provision from where he does not reckon."* (Abū Dāwūd 1518) The istighfār commanded at the summit is thus revealed as less about guilt than about *orientation*: the continuous turning of the whole person toward the Source of every opening — which is why it could be commanded, without paradox, to the sinless.

**The Arc from "Read" to "Return": The Sūrah as the Qur'an's Signature**

Set the sūrah back into the whole Book it completes, and its shape becomes unmistakable. The first words ever revealed to the Prophet ﷺ in the cave of Ḥirāʾ were: *"Read, in the name of your Lord who created (iqraʾ bi-ismi rabbika alladhī khalaq)"* (Qur'an 96:1) — a command to *receive*. The last complete sūrah's substance is a command to *return* — praise given back, forgiveness sought back, the whole relationship closing its circle like a breath released after a long inhale. Between the two commands lie twenty-three years: thirteen of patient endurance in Makkah, ten of building and struggle in Madinah, the revelation descending verse by verse through every weather of the mission — consolation in grief (after Uḥud and after the deaths of Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib), correction after mistakes (as at Badr's aftermath, in 8:67–69), law for a civilization, light for the inward life. And now the Qur'an, in its last complete chapter, performs what the sīrah transmits the Prophet ﷺ said about himself and God's servants: this is the moment the mission's ledger is read. Ibn ʿAbbās's reading stands as the seal on it all: through this sūrah the Prophet was informed that his death was near; the command of praise-and-forgiveness is both the summary of servanthood and its final preparation; and the Book's last complete word about God is *tawwāb*. Within roughly eighty days of the Farewell Pilgrimage the Prophet ﷺ fell ill; within days of the last verse — by many reports 2:281 — he left the world, his head in ʿĀʾishah's lap, the community he had gathered standing at the door of the very legacy the sūrah had told him was complete: *"…this day have I perfected for you your religion and completed My favor upon you"* (Qur'an 5:3). The man who received the sūrah implemented it until his final prayers: glorifying, praising, seeking forgiveness — *"…interpreting the Qur'an."*

**Living the Last Sūrah: A Rule for Every Blessing**

The farewell sūrah was the Prophet's, but its pattern is God's, and God does not deal in patterns for one man only. The Qur'an teaches by events and then hands the community the principle. An-Naṣr's principle, distilled: *whenever God's help arrives and you witness what it produces, the correct response is never self-congratulation — it is praise of the Source and immediate return to Him.* Every human life contains its small an-Naṣrs. The examination passed after years of failure; the health restored after a frightening diagnosis; the marriage rescued from the edge; the business that finally stabilizes; the child who comes home; the addiction broken; the reputation cleared after years of suspicion. In each, the verse's three beats replay: there *was* a help that no strategy alone explains (*naṣru Allāh*); there is something now *seen* that was only promised (the harvest visible); and there is a fork in the road — the moment of triumph, where the self leans forward to take the bow — and the sūrah's command stands at the fork like a signpost: *sabbīḥ… wa-staghfirhu.* The believer who has trained the tongue in that moment — *"al-ḥamdu li-Llāh"* said before the announcement of success, *istighfār* said in the privacy after it — has carried the Prophet's farewell discipline into the twenty-first century. An analogy from the road: the driver who survives a dangerous mountain pass does well to thank the One who built his reflexes before admiring his own steering; the pass was God's, the reflexes were God's, and the arrival — that too was *fatḥ*. The Qur'an's own closing instruction to the Beloved, in the sūrah that seals his story, is simply the permanent rule of arriving: give the praise, seek the pardon, and know the One you are dealing with:

> *"…hymn the praise of your Lord, and seek forgiveness from Him. Truly He is Ever-Relenting."*
