# Sūrah al-Inshiqāq (Chapter 84) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter of the Rent Sky**

Al-Inshiqāq, "The Rending Asunder," takes its name from its opening verb — *inshaqqat*, "is rent asunder" — which describes the sky splitting open at the end of time. It is a Makkan sūrah of twenty-five verses, revealed, according to the prevailing view of the Qur'anic historians, in the middle phase of the Makkan period, the years in which the Prophet ﷺ and his small community bore the organized hostility of the Quraysh while the revelations about the Hereafter were descending upon them in their most concentrated form. Its place in the final thirtieth part of the Book is deliberate. It belongs to that family of short, intense Makkan sūrahs — al-Takwīr, al-Infiṭār, al-Inshiqāq, al-Ṭāriq — that compress the entire summons of the Prophet ﷺ into a handful of towering truths: that the world will end, that the graves will be opened, that every deed has been recorded, that every soul will meet its Lord, and that the human journey terminates in one of two abodes. Nothing else is permitted to dilute these truths here. The sūrah is an arrow, not a library; and its single point is the certainty of the return.

**The Name and Its Near-Twins**

The word *inshiqāq* comes from the root *sh-q-q*, "to split, to cleave, to tear apart," and its cognates map the whole emotional terrain of the sūrah. From the same root comes *mashaqqah*, hardship, toil — because hardship is that which splits a person's strength and scatters his composure — and the connection is no accident, for this sūrah is precisely about the toil (*kadḥ*, v. 6) that splits a life open and the final cleaving that splits the world open. The title's near-twin is Sūrah 82, al-Infiṭār, "The Cleaving," and the commentators never tired of placing the two side by side. In al-Infiṭār the sky is *cleft* (*infatarat*, 82:1) and the seas burst forth; in al-Inshiqāq the sky is *rent* (*inshaqqat*, 84:1) and the earth is stretched out and emptied. One sūrah gives the breaking from above; the other adds the overturning from below. Between them they frame the same event — the dissolution of the present cosmic order — seen from its two poles.

**Where the Sūrah Sits in the Sequence of the Book**

The sūrah also completes a three-chapter arc that the careful reader of the final portion of the Qur'an will recognize. Sūrah 82, al-Infiṭār, introduced the noble scribes — the guardian angels who record every deed (82:10–12). Sūrah 83, al-Muṭaffifīn, then introduced the *book* in which the record is gathered, contrasting "the book of the profligate" deposited in *Sijjīn* (83:7–9) with "the book of the pious" preserved in *ʿIlliyyūn* (83:18–21). Sūrah 84 now takes the final step in the same progression: it shows the book being *placed in human hands* — into the right hand of one, behind the back of another — and it traces the two receptions to their two conclusions, an easy reckoning and a joyful return on one side, a cry of destruction and a blazing Fire on the other. The recording angels of 82, the ledger of 83, and the verdict of 84: read in sequence, the three sūrahs are a single unfolding revelation about the record that no human being escapes.

**The Architecture of the Sūrah**

The twenty-five verses fall into three movements, and the seams between them are visible. The first movement (vv. 1–5) is cosmic and voiceless: the sky is rent, it hearkens to its Lord, the earth is stretched out, casts forth what is in it, and hearkens too. There are no human beings on stage in these five verses — only sky and earth, obeying — and that absence is the point, as the commentary on verse 1 will show. The second movement (vv. 6–15) turns suddenly to the human being, addressed directly in the singular — *yā ayyuhā al-insān*, "O mankind!" — and unfolds the contrast between two recipients of the book. The third movement (vv. 16–25) is sealed with a triple oath — by the twilight, by the night and what it enshrouds, by the moon when full — that leads into the declaration of stage-by-stage ascent, the rebuke of unbelief, and the final pairing of a painful punishment with a reward unceasing. The sūrah thus moves from the end of the cosmos, to the account of the soul, to the oath that binds both together.

**The Twin Pole of This Sūrah: Labor and Return**

If one word had to be chosen as the sūrah's hinge, it would be the verb *kādiḥ* in verse 6 — "laboring, toiling" — for the entire chapter is built upon the image of the human being as a worker whose every movement is, whether he knows it or not, a movement toward a meeting. The Arabic carries the fatigue and exertion of the image: *kadḥ* is not leisurely walking but labor that leaves its mark, the striving that wears a man out and shows in his face and frame. The sūrah's first hearers in Makkah knew exhaustion intimately — the merchant's caravan, the shepherd's day, the mother's night, the slave's load — and the revelation seized that universal experience and turned it into theology: you are already toiling, it says; the only question is toward whom, and what you will meet at the end of the toil. The easy reckoning of verse 8 and the joyful return of verse 9 are the answer given to the toil that was aimed aright; the cry of verse 11 and the Blaze of verse 12 are the answer to the toil that was not.

**The Makkan Signature: The Oath of Rejection**

Finally, a formal feature marks the sūrah as Makkan to its bones. Verse 16 opens with *fa-lā uqsimu* — literally "Nay! I swear," a construction unique to the Qur'an in which the "Nay" (lā) is not a negation of the oath but a rebuke of the deniers before the oath is sworn. The formula appears again and again in the Makkan sūrahs (cf. 56:75; 69:38; 70:40; 75:1; 90:1) and nowhere in the long Madinan legislation. It is the voice of a speaker pushed to the edge of patience: "Nay — you who deny — I swear by the twilight, by the night, by the moon...". The oath, the direct address, the cosmic scene, the abrupt shifts of scale — all of it is the rhetorical signature of the Makkan years, when the message needed no statute but a verdict, and the verdict needed to be announced with the full weight of heaven and earth standing witness.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the twenty-five verses as an independent, complete discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order, exactly as the method requires. Every verse is quoted individually from the sūrah's text, then expanded through the Arabic language, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿAbbās, and others), the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, the logic of the teaching, and its bearing upon the life of the modern reader. Verses that the source commentary groups together — 1–2, 10–12, and 13–14 — are here separated and each given its own full treatment. Nothing has been compressed for the sake of length, for this sūrah is itself a lesson in the opposite: it teaches that the shortest span of life is heavy enough to fill a book, and that a book heavy enough to be opened on the Last Day deserves a reading that does not hurry.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:1

> **When the sky is rent asunder,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Word of the End**

The sūrah opens, without preface, with a scene: *idhā al-samāʾu-nshaqqat* — "when the sky is rent asunder." The opening particle *idhā*, "when," is the signature of the Qur'an's apocalyptic passages. It is not a conditional that hangs in doubt — *if* the sky splits — but a time-marker that assumes the event as settled fact, the way one says "when the sun sets" or "when winter comes." The event is certain; only its hour is withheld. The commentators dwell on the grammar because the creed is folded into it: the Day of Judgment is announced here not as a possibility to be argued but as an appointment already fixed, and the only honest question a listener can ask about it is not "will it happen?" but "when?" — to which the Qur'an elsewhere replies with the famous answer that the Prophet ﷺ was instructed to give, that its knowledge rests with God alone (7:187; 79:42–44).

**The Anatomy of a Splitting: What *Inshiqāq* Means**

*Inshiqāq* is the seventh form of the root *sh-q-q*, which means to split or cleave a thing along its length so that two parts open out of one. The same root gives the Qur'an the sea "splitting" for Moses and his people (*fa-nshaqqa*, 26:63), the earth "splitting" open to yield its vegetation and its springs (cf. 50:9; 80:26), and — most tenderly — the womb and the birth it splits toward, as when the Qur'an says of a human being's first emergence that he came forth from a *shaqq*, a cleft (cf. 19:16–17; 86:6). In every case the root carries the same visual logic: something whole and closed is opened along a seam, and what was hidden within is exposed. The sky in this verse is the supreme instance of that logic. The firmament that has arched over human heads for all of history — the one feature of the world that no king could build, no army could breach, and no eye could see past — is now opened like a garment torn down the middle. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī record that the sūrah's first hearers understood this not as metaphor but as report: the sky is a real created thing, and what is created can be dissolved by the One who created it.

**The Sky as a Preserved Roof: What Is Being Torn**

To feel the verse's force, one must first register what the sky is in the Qur'an's own vocabulary. It is described as a *roof*, *saqfan*, that is *preserved*, *maḥfūẓan* (21:32); as a *canopy* stretched without pillars, which "God raised ... and set in place the balance" (55:7; cf. 13:2). It is the great constant, the one backdrop that never changes, the ceiling under which every human drama is enacted. The Qur'an elsewhere asks its hearers to look at it and find no flaw: "Do they not look at the sky above them — how We built it and adorned it, and there are no cracks in it?" (50:6). Precisely because the sky is the symbol of permanence, its splitting is the symbol of the end of permanence. On that Day the order that seemed unbreakable breaks, and the verse's unstated premise lands with full weight: if the sky — the most stable thing a human being has ever seen — can be opened by a command, then no stability anywhere, least of all the apparent stability of a comfortable life built in forgetfulness, is beyond the reach of its Creator. The same scene is spread across the final portion of the Qur'an in a chorus of variations: "And the sky will be cleft asunder (*inshaqqat*), for that Day it will be frail" (69:16); "And the sky will be opened, becoming gates" (78:19); "The Day when the sky will be like molten brass" (70:8); and the unforgettable image, "when the sky is rent asunder and hearkens to its Lord, and becomes crimson like red hide" (55:37; cf. 25:25, 82:1).

**The Unspoken Companion: Why Earth Waits Until Verse 3**

The verse mentions the sky alone and pauses. The sūrah could have said "when the sky is rent asunder and the earth is stretched out" in one breath; instead it lets the sky's splitting stand alone for a full verse, and reserves the earth for verses 3–4. The commentators read the pause as deliberate art. The sky is the higher witness, the ceiling of the visible world; it is named first because the drama descends from above, and because in the Qur'an's account the command of dissolution comes down from the highest heaven. There is also a subtler point that the spiritual commentators draw out: the sky is the emblem of everything a human being looks *up* to — and on that Day, what men looked up to and trusted besides God is precisely what collapses first. The rent sky is the death of every false ceiling, every substitute permanence, every created thing in which a heart placed the security that belongs to the Creator alone.

**The Moral Logic of an Ending: Why a Rent Sky Is a Mercy**

To a modern mind the apocalyptic opening may read as mere destruction; the sūrah intends it as the opposite — as the dismantling of a stage, not the ruining of a home. A theater is dismantled when the play is over; a courtroom is cleared when judgment is to be delivered; a journey ends when the destination is reached. The Qur'an repeatedly frames the end of the world in exactly this way: not as annihilation but as *arrival*, the removal of the temporary so that the permanent may stand revealed. "And when the earth is spread and casts out what is in it and is emptied, and hearkens to its Lord — then, O man, you will be, laboring toward your Lord, one who meets Him" (cf. 99:1–8). The sky that sheltered the heedless is rent precisely so that no shelter remains but God — and the commentary tradition is unanimous that this is a mercy disguised as terror, the door of the next life opening after the closing of this one. The first hearers in Makkah, who were told by the Quraysh that death is the end of everything, were given here the Qur'an's standing reply: death is not the end of everything; it is the end of the sky — and the beginning of the meeting.

**Living with a Ceiling That Will Not Last**

The practical yield of this verse for a modern believer is a reorientation of what is called "security." Human beings build their lives under a sense of permanence — careers, properties, reputations, plans spanning decades — and the rent sky is the standing reminder that every permanence is leased, not owned. The believer is not asked to despise the world; he is asked not to *anchor* in it. The Prophet ﷺ put the same truth in the most vivid image he ever used about the world's share of permanence, when he passed by a kid goat that had died and was of little value and said:

> *"By Him in Whose hand is my soul, this world is more insignificant to God than this (dead kid) was to its owners."* (Muslim 2957)

The verse does not call for anxiety, then, but for the specific calm that comes from holding temporary things loosely and trusting the One who holds them all. The sky that God raised will one day be folded by the same hand — and the heart that knew this in advance has already learned to say, with the Qur'an, "to God we belong, and to Him we return" (2:156).

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:2

> **and hearkens unto its Lord, as in truth it must;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**When a Ceiling Listens**

The second verse completes the first with a phrase that has no parallel in ordinary speech: *wa adhinat li-rabbihā wa ḥuqqat* — "and hearkens unto its Lord, as in truth it must." The verb *adhinat* is from *udhn*, the ear, and literally means "to lend an ear, to listen and comply." The sky, which was just described as splitting open, is now described as *listening* — and not listening idly, but listening in the mode of obedience, the ear turned toward the command it has no thought of resisting. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī both gloss the meaning with transparent simplicity: the sky obeys its Lord's command to split, and it does so because it is *worthy of that obedience and bound to it*, for it is created and its Creator's command has a claim upon it that nothing in it can refuse. The whole cosmic event of verse 1 is thus reframed in a single word: the rending of the sky is not an accident of collapsing physics but an act of *listening* — the highest creature of the visible world hearing its Lord and complying.

**The Grammar of Compulsion and Consent**

The second clause, *wa ḥuqqat*, is built on the verb *ḥaqqa*, "to be true, to be due, to be binding," and the commentators unpack two meanings that fuse into one. The first is: the sky *deserved* this — it was fitting and right that it obey, the way it is fitting that a debt be paid and that a promise be kept. The second is: the sky *was made to* — the passive sense by which the command of God is an inescapable claim, a *ḥaqq* that cannot be evaded. Arabic readers in the sūrah's first audience heard both at once, and the tradition preserved both as true together. There is no contradiction here because in the Qur'an's understanding of creation, the deepest fittingness and the deepest necessity are one thing: a creature's whole being is a response to its Creator, so that its obedience is simultaneously its free nature and its unavoidable condition. Ibn Kathīr notes that this is precisely the opposite of the human situation as the sūrah will unfold it — for the sky and the earth obey *by their very being*, without a struggle, while the human being is the one creature whose obedience is placed in his own hands, to be chosen or refused. The mute heavens, in other words, will turn out to have a reliability that the talking creature sometimes lacks.

**A Universal Law Stated Elsewhere in the Book**

The verse is not an isolated image; it is one instance of a doctrine the Qur'an states as a law of all existence. The nearest and fullest statement comes at the start of Sūrah Fuṣṣilat, where God is described as addressing the heaven and the earth together:

> *"Then He turned to the heaven while it was smoke and said to it and to the earth: 'Come, willingly or unwillingly!' They said: 'We come willingly.'"* (Qur'an 41:11)

Here the two created masses *speak*, and what they say is the voluntary answer — *"We come willingly"* — which the sūrah now shows being enacted at history's end, when the sky hears the command to split and complies. The Qur'an's cosmology is thereby made visible: every created thing, from the heaven down to the pebble, is in a state of obedience toward God, its existence a continuous "yes" to the command that sustains it. The famous verse of the ants' valley, where an ant warns her colony of Solomon's army (27:18), and the verse that declares there is not a thing but exalts God with praise (17:44), belong to the same vision. The sky's hearkening in 84:2 is simply that vision applied to the largest thing a human being can see.

**The Contrast That the Sūrah Is Building**

The commentators linger here because the verse is a silent set-up for what follows. Verses 1–2 show sky, and verses 3–5 will show earth — both hearing and obeying — before verse 6 suddenly turns to *al-insān*, the human being. The architecture of the opening is a deliberate contrast between the obedient cosmos and the accountable human. The sky has no choice, and therefore no reckoning; it obeys because it must, and the sūrah says of its obedience only that it was *right* (*ḥuqqat*). The human being, by contrast, *has* a choice, and therefore *has* a reckoning — and the whole middle section of the sūrah (vv. 6–15) will follow the consequences of that difference to their two ends. Ibn Kathīr's commentary makes the implied rebuke explicit: the heavens and the earth submit to their Lord's command with perfect obedience, while the human being, who was honored above them and given the power to choose, is the one creature in the visible world who sometimes refuses. The mute sky, in its single silent act of splitting, becomes the sūrah's first preacher.

**Hearing as the Deepest Form of Being**

Finally, the verse teaches something about the nature of obedience itself by putting it in the language of the ear. In the Qur'an's psychology, hearing is the gateway of faith: the revelation is called *samʿ*, "hearing," when it is spoken of as something heard (cf. 18:101; 32:12), and the believer is repeatedly described as the one who "listens" — "those who listen to the word and follow the best of it" (39:18), "give glad tidings to My servants who listen to the word and follow the best of it" (39:17–18). The sky "hearkening" is therefore not a figure of speech imported from human affairs; it is the Qur'an's root metaphor for the creature's right relation to its Creator, shown in its purest form. To exist is, in the deepest sense, to hear — and to obey what one has heard. The human being who reads this verse is being shown, in the sky, the one thing required of himself: a listening that becomes compliance. Everything else in the sūrah — the labor, the book, the reckoning, the two ends — is the story of whether that listening happened.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:3

> **and when the earth is stretched out,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Leveling of the Stage**

After the sky is rent, the scene drops to the earth: *wa idhā al-arḍu muddat* — "and when the earth is stretched out." The verb *muddat*, from *madda*, "to extend, spread, stretch," is the same word used for spreading a carpet, unfolding a scroll, or stretching a rope to its full length. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī explain the image: the earth is *flattened and extended*, its mountains leveled and its uneven ground made smooth, until the whole globe is one vast, level plain. The Qur'an describes this leveling elsewhere in verses the commentators routinely join to this one: "And they ask you about the mountains; say: My Lord will scatter them utterly, and leave it (the earth) a level, empty plain — you will see in it no curve and no unevenness" (20:105–107); "And the earth will shine with the light of its Lord, and the Book will be set in place" (39:69). The purpose of the leveling is stated in still another verse, the one that most directly complements the present scene: "the Day We will make the mountains move, and you will see the earth a plain, and We will gather them and not leave out a single one of them" (18:47).

**Why the Earth Must Be Leveled**

The reason for the leveling is both practical and symbolic, and the commentators supply both. Practically, the earth is leveled so that it can serve as the gathering-ground of the entire human race from Adam until the last soul: a single plain on which all of humanity can be assembled together, none hidden in a valley, none lost behind a mountain, none beyond a horizon — "a single plain" in the words of one reading of 20:106, "and We will gather them and not leave out a single one of them" (18:47). The imagery answers a question every thoughtful person has asked: how could every human being who ever lived be gathered for judgment, scattered as they are across continents and centuries and buried in earth and sea? The answer is that the very ground of the gathering will be made ready for it — leveled, widened, and cleared of every obstacle, so that nothing stands between any soul and the place where it will stand before its Lord. Symbolically, the leveling undoes the hiding places of the world. The mountains that men trusted as fortresses, the terrain in which the fugitive hid, the walls behind which the tyrant plotted, the graves in which the dead were concealed — all of it is flattened into one exposed, unshadowed field where "nothing of them will be hidden from God" (40:16).

**A Fitting Sequel to the Rent Sky**

The verse's placement continues the descent begun in verse 1. The sky, the ceiling, is torn; now the earth, the floor, is stretched. The two great constants of human experience — the roof above and the ground beneath — are both transformed in the same moment, and the effect is to leave a human being with no familiar coordinates at all. Al-Rāzī and others note the deliberate symmetry: the sky is *split* (a movement of opening outward), and the earth is *spread* (a movement of extending outward); one opens from within, the other flattens from above, and together they dismantle the container of the world so that its contents — including every buried body and every concealed secret — can be brought out. The sūrah is staging, in five verses, the complete overturning of the visible order, and the order of the staging matters: sky first, earth second, because the command descends from above and the disclosure rises from below.

**The Earth That Will Testify**

The verse also prepares the way for the next one by establishing that the earth will not merely be a passive floor but an active witness and deliverer. In the Qur'an's account, the earth is not neutral about what it has held: "And the earth will shine with the light of its Lord" (39:69); "That Day it will relate its news — because your Lord has inspired it" (99:4–5). The stretched-out earth of 84:3 is the same earth that, in verse 4, will "cast forth what is in it," and in Sūrah al-Zalzalah will "relate its news" of everything done upon it. The leveling is thus not the erasure of the earth's memory but the preparation for its testimony. Every place a human being ever stood is being smoothed into one surface precisely because the record of what happened on that surface is about to be read out.

**The Level Field as a Mirror of Divine Justice**

One further dimension of the image deserves emphasis, because it is the one with the most direct moral content: the gathering on a level plain is the Qur'an's most vivid emblem of the equality of all human beings before God. On the day of gathering there will be no elevation for the rich, no platform for the ruler, no privileged slope for the noble lineage — "all of them will come to Him alone on the Day of Resurrection" (19:95), "each of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone" (19:80, 95). The level earth is the architecture of divine justice: the ground itself refuses to honor any rank but the rank of deeds. Kings and beggars, oppressors and oppressed, will stand on the same flat plain, in the same dust, under the same opened sky — and the only difference that will then matter is the difference the sūrah's next section unfolds: which hand receives the book. For a modern world obsessed with elevation — status, platforms, rankings, heights of every kind — the verse is a quiet leveller: before God, the ground is flat, and what a human being is worth there is exactly what he has done.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:4

> **and casts forth what is in it, emptying itself,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Earth Gives Up Its Dead**

*Wa alqat mā fīhā wa takhallat* — "and casts forth what is in it, emptying itself." Two verbs, each carrying its own precise weight. The first, *alqat* (from *alqā*, "to cast, throw, fling out"), describes the earth throwing out its contents with the vigor of one who discards a burden; the second, *takhallat* (from *takhallā*, "to empty oneself, to be left vacant, to disgorge completely"), describes the result — the earth left *hollow, vacant, completely emptied*. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī all identify the primary content being cast forth: the dead. This is the emptying of the graves. The bodies and the souls of every human being that the earth ever received are thrown up and out, so that the earth is left with nothing inside it — a vessel drained to its last drop. The commentators sometimes add the companion meaning noted in the source: that the earth also casts forth everything *hidden* — the treasures men buried, the secrets men concealed in the ground, the bodies of the murdered, everything that was ever committed to the earth's keeping — for the day of disclosure is total.

**The Grave as a Trust That Must Be Returned**

The verse's logic rests on the Qur'an's understanding of burial as a *deposit*. The human being did not disappear at death; he was *entrusted* to the earth, and the earth is a trustee that must one day return its trust. The Qur'an elsewhere poses the question with an image that reads like a guarantee of this verse: "Does man think that We shall not gather his bones? Yes — We are able even to proportion his fingertips" (75:3–4). And the Qur'an makes the casting-forth a universal summons in Sūrah al-Zalzalah, the nearest twin of this passage: "And the earth casts out its burdens (*akhrajati al-arḍu athqālahā*)" (99:2). The same event appears in the imagery of a single cry: "It is but a single cry — and behold, they are all brought before Us" (36:53); "And the Trumpet will be blown, and behold, they will come forth from the graves hastening to their Lord" (36:51; cf. 50:41–42; 54:7; 79:13–14). The earth that human beings used as the final container for everything they wanted to forget is, in the Qur'an's account, only a holding place — and the holding ends.

**The Wordplay the Ear Is Meant to Catch**

The Arabic of this verse and its neighbor carries an auditory design that the first hearers would have caught immediately. *Alqat ... wa takhallat* is a pair of verbs in the same rhythmic pattern, ending in the same closing syllable, one active and one resulting — and the effect, as the rhetoricians note, is the sound of a full vessel being turned over and shaken empty. The sūrah's opening verses are built of these paired, echoing verbs — *inshaqqat ... ḥuqqat* (vv. 1–2), *muddat ... alqat ... takhallat ... ḥuqqat* (vv. 3–5) — a continuous chain of final-*t* rhymes that the earliest listeners described as having the force of hammer-strokes. The form is not decoration; it is the rhetoric of finality. Each verse lands with the closed sound of a door shutting, and the whole opening passage is a drumbeat announcing that the era of the world has closed.

**Emptiness as Disclosure: What the Vacancy Means**

The commentators pause over *takhallat*, the emptying, because it carries a meaning that English "emptying" only half-renders. When the earth is *left vacant*, nothing remains to conceal. The vacancy is the disclosure: once the earth has cast out every body and every secret, there is no longer any interior, any hidden chamber, any place where a fact can lie buried. The Qur'an joins this emptying to the exposure of the hidden in the same breath elsewhere: "On the Day when the earth is exchanged for another earth, and the heavens, and they come forth before God, the One, the Irresistible" (14:48). The theological point is that *secrecy itself is a temporary condition* — a loan of the present order that will be called in. What a person hid in the ground, or in the dark, or in the innermost chamber of the heart, is all ultimately the same kind of thing: something held inside a container that will be opened. The emptying earth is the Qur'an's way of saying that every container is scheduled to be opened, and the only question is whether the opening will be a relief or a catastrophe — which is precisely the question the sūrah answers in its two portraits of verses 7–9 and 10–12.

**The Resurrection as Restoration, Not Improvisation**

Finally, this verse delivers the sūrah's first full answer to the great objection of the Makkan deniers, who said that a human being dispersed into the earth as dust and bone could never be gathered again. The verse's answer is that the dispersal was never a loss: the earth itself has held the deposit, and the same Lord who commanded the earth to receive will command it to give back. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the logical reply explicit, from the creation of the first human being: "He it is Who begins creation and then repeats it — and that is easier for Him" (30:27); "Does man not see that We created him from a drop, and behold, he is an open adversary — and he strikes a likeness for Us and forgets his own creation, saying: 'Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?' Say: He will give them life Who produced them the first time" (36:77–79). If the first creation required nothing but a command, the second creation requires no more; and the emptied earth is simply the visible moment at which the command is obeyed. For the modern reader who has absorbed a picture of the world in which the dead simply dissolve into the soil forever, this verse is the Qur'an's counter-picture, stated with maximum compression: the earth does not consume its dead; it *stores* them, and the storage has a release date.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:5

> **and hearkens unto its Lord, as in truth it must.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exact Repetition and Why It Matters**

Verse 5 repeats verse 2 word for word: *wa adhinat li-rabbihā wa ḥuqqat* — "and hearkens unto its Lord, as in truth it must." The same clause is attached first to the sky (v. 2) and now to the earth (v. 5), and the Qur'an's principle — that nothing is repeated without purpose — demands that the repetition be read as a design rather than a refrain. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr all note what the symmetry accomplishes: the two halves of the visible world, the heavens above and the earth below, are here shown performing the identical act of obedience in the identical language. The ceiling and the floor of human existence both hearken; both comply; both are *ḥuqqat* — bound by right and deserving of their obedience. The frame of the world is closed by obedience on every side, and the human being at its center is left standing inside a universe that obeys while he alone remains the one accountable question mark. The repetition is thus the sūrah's way of surrounding the human being — literally, in the structure of the verses, above and below — with the example he is about to be asked to follow or to refuse.

**The Chiasmus of the Opening Five Verses**

The commentators of the rhetorical school observe further that the five opening verses form a carefully balanced structure, a *chiasmus* in which the elements are arranged as a mirror. The sky splits (v. 1), the sky hearkens (v. 2); the earth is stretched (v. 3), the earth casts forth and empties (v. 4), the earth hearkens (v. 5). Read in order, the sequence pairs the high with the low and crowns each with the same act of hearkening, so that the passage opens with the sky's splitting and closes with the earth's obedience — destruction at the top, compliance at the bottom, and between them the whole span of the created order reduced to a single lesson. The classical rhetoricians called such repetition of a closing phrase *radd al-ʿajuz ʿalā al-ṣadr*, "returning the end upon the beginning," and they counted it among the Qur'an's distinctive beauties. Here its function is not beauty alone but emphasis: the obedience of creation is stated twice so that no hearer can miss it, and so that when the sūrah turns in verse 6 to the one creature who can *choose* disobedience, the contrast will be framed on both sides.

**One Obedience, Two Modes: Sky and Earth Compared**

Though the words are identical, the tradition finds a delicate difference in what each obedience entails, a difference rooted in the different natures of sky and earth. The sky's hearkening in verse 2 follows its *splitting* — its obedience is an act of opening, of giving way from above. The earth's hearkening in verse 5 follows its *emptying* — its obedience is an act of giving up, of releasing what it held from below. One opens, the other releases; one surrenders its structure, the other surrenders its contents. Al-Rāzī gathers the two into a single composite image: the whole world, top and bottom, is shown obeying its Lord's command in the exact manner its own nature makes possible — and the human being is thereby taught that obedience does not require a single uniform posture but the surrender of whatever each creature is and holds. For a human being, the parallel surrender is named later in the sūrah itself: the labor of verse 6, aimed at the Lord, is the human form of the sky's opening and the earth's release.

**The Verse as a Seal on the Cosmic Argument**

Taken together, verses 1–5 constitute a complete and self-contained argument, and verse 5 is its seal. The argument runs: the world is not permanent; it will be undone by command; and its undoing will itself be an act of obedience, because everything God created obeys Him. If all of that is true — and the verses state it as settled fact, not as speculation — then the ground is prepared for the question the sūrah has been driving toward from its first word: and what about *you*? The human being who reads these five verses is placed inside a world whose every part is aligned to its Maker, and is then confronted, in the very next verse, with his own alignment: *"O mankind! Truly thou art laboring unto thy Lord laboriously, and shall meet Him"* (84:6). The cosmic section is not a digression on astronomy; it is the stage-setting for the human drama, and verse 5 closes the stage-setting with the sound of every bolt locked into place.

**Hearing as the Door the Sūrah Opens Next**

One last function of the repetition deserves note. By ending the opening passage on the note of *hearing* — the sky hears, the earth hears — the sūrah is quietly handing the reader the disposition it will require of him. The believer is defined in the Qur'an as a hearer: "and they say, 'We hear and we obey'" (2:285); "those who listen to the word and follow the best of it" (39:18). The sky and the earth, which cannot refuse, hear and obey; the human being, who can refuse, is now summoned to hear the same call and to *choose* the same obedience. The entire remainder of the sūrah — the book, the reckoning, the two ends — is the content of what the human ear is meant to receive. Verse 5 thus ends the cosmic overture and, in the same breath, turns the reader's ear toward what follows: the announcement that he, too, is laboring toward a meeting.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:6

> **O mankind! Truly thou art laboring unto thy Lord laboriously, and shall meet Him!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sudden Turn from Cosmos to Soul**

Five verses of cosmic upheaval — sky rent, earth emptied — and then, without transition, the direct address: *yā ayyuhā al-insān* — "O mankind! O human being!" The singular is deliberate. The cosmos of the opening verses was spoken of in the third person, as a scene laid out for inspection; now the sūrah wheels around and speaks to a single human being, to *you*, in the second person. The rhetorical shift — what Arabic calls *iltifāt*, the "turning" — is the sūrah's whole strategy compressed into one word. The universe is the backdrop; the human being is the subject; and the moment the backdrop is in place, the camera swings to the one creature for whom the backdrop was prepared. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both note that the address reaches every reader individually: *al-insān* here is not a crowd but a name for each member of the crowd, so that no one can read the verse and feel himself addressed in general while exempted in particular. The verse is, in the technical language of the commentators, the pivot of the sūrah — everything before it prepares it, everything after it unfolds it.

**Kadḥ: The Anatomy of Human Toil**

The verse's central word is *kādiḥ* — "laboring, toiling" — and the commentators take care with its root because the whole sūrah turns on it. *Kadḥ* denotes labor that is strenuous and prolonged, the kind of exertion that leaves its trace on the one who performs it — a word applied in Arabic to the work of the hands that roughens the skin and to the striving that exhausts the frame. The intensive construction *kādiḥun ... kadḥan* ("laboring ... laboriously," the verbal noun *kadḥan* reinforcing the active participle *kādiḥun*) is the Arabic pattern of emphasis by repetition: the toil is stressed twice so that no hearer can mistake its reality. Ibn ʿAbbās, whose gloss the source preserves, captured the point in a single sentence that the later commentators repeat:

> *"Whatever deed you do, you will meet God with it — whether it is good or bad."* (Ibn ʿAbbās, as transmitted by Ibn Kathīr)

The grammar makes the same point as the gloss: the phrase *ilā rabbika* — "unto thy Lord" — attaches the toil not to a mere result but to a *destination*. The human being is not merely laboring *at* something; he is laboring *toward* something — and that something is his Lord. Every effort, every day of work, every ambition pursued and every habit indulged, is a movement along a road whose terminus is God.

**The Metaphor That Organizes the Whole Sūrah**

The image of labor-and-journey is the sūrah's master metaphor, and everything that follows is its unpacking. A human being's life is a stretch of toil — *kadḥ* — and the toil is inherently directional: it carries the laborer somewhere. The commentators draw out the three stages implied in the verse. First, the fact of the toil: no human being is idle; even stillness is a kind of movement, and the one who thinks he is doing nothing is merely toiling toward nothing. Second, the direction of the toil: the verse says the toil is *unto the Lord* — not because every laborer intends God, but because every road of effort terminates at the meeting with God regardless of what the laborer intended, the way every river terminates in the sea regardless of what name the river gives itself. Third, the meeting: *fa-mulāqīh* — "and thou shalt meet Him." The emphatic structure makes the meeting certain and personal; it is not an idea or an event one hears about but an encounter one undergoes. The believer's task, the sūrah will show, is to bring the *direction* of his toil into line with its *destination*.

**What Maybudī Saw: Every Moment a Step**

The source commentary preserves the reading of the Persian exegete Maybudī, who understood the verse as an injunction to mindfulness precisely because every moment is a step closer to the meeting. The insight deserves its full statement. If a person is always laboring toward a meeting with God, then there is no neutral time, no off-duty hour, no moment of a life that is not travel. The verse thus abolishes the modern division of life into "sacred" and "secular" at its root: the commute, the desk, the kitchen, the leisure, the argument, the quiet hour before sleep — all of it is *kadḥ ilā rabbika*, toil unto the Lord, and all of it is being walked toward a meeting. This is why the Prophet ﷺ taught that even the smallest and most ordinary deeds carry weight: removing a harmful thing from the road is a charity (al-Bukhārī 2989; Muslim 1009), and a morsel placed in a wife's mouth is an act of devotion (al-Bukhārī 56; Muslim 1628). The verse does not ask a person to abandon his labor for worship; it asks him to *worship with* his labor, by directing it.

**The Two Travelers the Verse Prepares**

The verse deliberately postpones the outcome of the meeting, and the postponement is itself the sūrah's artistry. It does not say "and shall meet Him — in joy" or "in ruin"; it says only *fa-mulāqīh*, "and shall meet Him," and lets the tension hang. The next four verses will then answer the unspoken question by splitting humanity into its two receipts: the one whose book comes in the right hand and whose meeting is joy (vv. 7–9), and the one whose book comes from behind and whose meeting is a cry of destruction (vv. 10–12). The verse is thus a doorway with two rooms behind it, and the sūrah's whole moral force is that every reader must pass through the doorway — for everyone labors, everyone is traveling, everyone will meet — but which room awaits is determined by the direction of the toil that the verse has just named. Ibn Kathīr notes the exact correspondence: the believer who "labors unto his Lord" by obeying Him meets the Lord of the right hand, while the one who labored for his own desire meets the Lord of the cry of ruin — yet both, without exception, meet.

**The Psychological Reorientation of a Life**

Finally, the verse is a psychological instrument as much as a theological statement, and the tradition treated it as such. A human being who has absorbed this verse cannot regard his life as an open-ended accumulation of days; he regards it as a *commute*. The difference is total. The commuter organizes everything — sleep, speed, baggage, route — by the destination; the stroller organizes everything by whim. The verse installs the destination at the center of the day's arithmetic, and the Qur'an elsewhere shows what the arithmetic produces: the believer who says "my prayer, my sacrifice, my living, and my dying are for God, Lord of the worlds" (6:162), and the famous supplication attributed to the Prophet's ﷺ teaching that a person's toil be made the very form of his worship. The modern reader, whose culture tells him he is "working toward retirement" or "building a career" or "spending his time," is offered here a single correction: he is working toward a meeting. The only question the verse leaves open — and the sūrah will spend its remaining verses pressing it — is whether the work has been shaped, from this moment on, by the meeting it is moving toward.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:7

> **As for one who is given his book in his right hand,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Verdict Pronounced**

The sūrah now answers the tension of verse 6 by dividing the meeting into its two possible outcomes, and it begins with the blessed one: *fa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahu bi-yamīnih* — "As for one who is given his book in his right hand." The structure *ammā ... fa-* ("as for ... then") is the Qur'an's cleanest instrument of division, and it cuts humanity into two files with a single stroke. This verse names the first file and, by its very wording, tells the reader almost everything he needs to know. The human being of verse 6 — who labors and will meet his Lord — here *meets* the record of his labor in the form of a written book, and the book is placed in his *right hand*. Every element of the sentence is weight-bearing: the book is real, the hand is specific, and the placement is the verdict before the verdict.

**The Book: What a Life Becomes**

The word *kitāb* — book, record, written document — has already been threaded through the three preceding sūrahs of this family, and the sūrah assumes its reader knows what it is. The book is the written form of a human life: the complete, itemized record of everything a person did, said, intended, and was, preserved by the recording angels whose existence Sūrah 82 announced ("and indeed over you are guardians — noble, writing — they know what you do," 82:10–12). The Qur'an returns to this record again and again, always with the same staggering feature — its completeness: "And the Book will be placed, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it, and they will say, 'Woe to us! What is this book that leaves out nothing, small or great, but has enumerated it?' And they will find what they did present, and your Lord does not wrong anyone" (18:49). The commentators on this verse (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī) emphasize the psychology of the book: it is the objectification of a life, the self a person built day by day, handed back to him as a thing he can hold. Nothing is more intimate than one's own biography — and on that Day, the biography is the evidence.

**Why the Right Hand? The Symbolism of Light and Truth**

The right hand is specified because in the Qur'an's symbolic language, and in the universal human intuition it draws upon, the right side is the side of honor, truth, light, and good omen. The source commentary notes the striking parallel with the Latin *sinister* — "left" — from which the English word "sinister" descends: across unrelated languages and cultures, humanity has associated the right with fortune and the left with ill. The Qur'an elevates this intuition into a precise eschatological sign. The *aṣḥāb al-yamīn* — "the companions of the right" — are the saved throughout Sūrah 56 (56:27–40, 90–91), and the receipt of the book in the right hand is there described with the same vocabulary as here (cf. 69:19; 17:71). Ibn ʿAbbās and the early commentators explain the meaning beneath the symbol: the right hand receives the book because the book's contents are *light* — truth lived openly, deeds done in the daylight of faith — and light is what the right hand is fit to carry. The book in the right hand is thus not an arbitrary assignment but a *correspondence*: the man whose life was aligned with truth receives his truth-facing record in the hand of honor. He is not handed an award; he is handed *his own life*, and his own life turns out to be the award.

**The Moment of Presentation: What It Feels Like**

The sūrah does not dramatize the scene — it states it — but the companion verses in the Qur'an fill in the experience, and the commentators assemble them here. In Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah the recipient of the right hand speaks the words the blessed one of this sūrah is thinking: "As for him who is given his book in his right hand, he will say: 'Here, read my book! I was certain that I would meet my account'" (69:19–20). The cry — *hāʾumu-qraʾū kitābiyah*, "Here, read my book!" — is the sound of a life vindicated: the man is not afraid of the open record but eager for it, because the record is the proof of everything he hoped for. And in Sūrah al-Inshiqāq's own companion verse (v. 9), the outcome is joy. The tradition adds the most luminous detail of all, in the long authenticated narrative of the soul's journey at death transmitted from al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib and recorded by Aḥmad and Abū Dāwūd: the believing soul, as it rises, is greeted by the angels, perfumed, welcomed by the hosts of heaven as "a good soul from a good body," and promised the Garden — the book in the right hand at the Resurrection is the final, public confirmation of a welcome that began at the deathbed. The joy of verse 9 is thus not a surprise sprung at the last moment; it is the completion of a friendship with the record that the believer maintained all along.

**The Grammar of Grace: Passive Receipt, Active Desert**

One subtle feature of the verse deserves note, because it guards a doctrine. The book is *given* — *ūtiya*, in the passive, "is given" — not seized or earned by claim. The commentators observe that even the saved do not walk up and take their book; it is handed to them, and the passive verb keeps the act of giving in God's hand. This is the Qur'an's characteristic balance between works and grace: the blessed one *did* labor (v. 6), and his labor is the content of the book, but the placement of the book in the right hand is still a gift of the One who rewards far beyond the deed. The sūrah will make the imbalance explicit at its close, where the believers are promised "a reward unceasing" (v. 25) — not a wage, but a reward, and a reward that never runs out. The right-hand book is the first glimpse of that disproportion: the human being contributes a lifetime of toil, and God returns it as a book of light in the hand of honor, to be followed by a joy that has no end.

**The Mirror for the Reader**

Finally, the verse is placed here as a mirror, not merely as information. Every reader is being invited, in the present tense, to ask which hand is being prepared. The sūrah does not permit neutrality: the same *fa-ammā* that introduces the blessed one will, in verse 10, introduce his opposite, and between the two there is no third file. The practical question the verse puts to a modern reader is the oldest and simplest in the tradition: *what is my life's record accumulating into?* The right-hand book is built one deed at a time, in obscurity, by the labor of verse 6 aimed at its Lord — and the verse's comfort is that the building is possible for anyone, at any hour, since the smallest good deed, done sincerely, is already a line in the book that will one day be handed over in the right hand.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:8

> **he will be reckoned with an easy reckoning**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Kinds of Reckoning**

*Fa-sawfa yuḥāsabu ḥisāban yasīrā* — "he will be reckoned with an easy reckoning." The word *yuḥāsabu* is from *ḥisāb*, accounting, and the image is drawn directly from the world of the ledger, the merchant's register, the settling of accounts between debtor and creditor. The Qur'an's first audience in Makkah was a trading city, and the vocabulary of commerce was the common language of its daily life; the revelation speaks to them — and through them to every generation — in the language of the balance-sheet. But the verse immediately distinguishes two kinds of reckoning, and the distinction is the whole point of the word *yasīrā* — "easy, light, gentle." The reckoning of the blessed is not the absence of a reckoning but its transformation: it is an account that is *settled easily*, the way a matter that was always in order is reviewed quickly, rather than the account that is torn open item by item with nothing found to offset it.

**The Prophet's ﷺ Own Explanation: Reckoning vs. Presentation**

The most important commentary on this verse is a ḥadīth, and the sūrah's whole understanding of the "easy reckoning" rests upon it. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever is brought to account on the Day of Resurrection will be punished."* — Upon this ʿĀʾishah asked: "But has God not said, 'As for one who is given his book in his right hand, he will be reckoned with an easy reckoning'?" The Prophet ﷺ replied: *"That is not the Reckoning; rather, that is the presentation (al-ʿarḍ). Whoever is interrogated in the Reckoning on the Day of Resurrection will be punished."* (al-Bukhārī 4939; Muslim 2876)

The exchange is a small masterpiece of the prophetic method, and the commentators (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī) draw from it a distinction that has governed Muslim understanding of the Last Day ever since. There is a *presentation* of deeds — *al-ʿarḍ* — in which a person's record is shown to him, its sins acknowledged and then pardoned and concealed by God's mercy, without the soul being dragged through an interrogation. And there is the *reckoning* proper — *al-ḥisāb* — in which the account is scrutinized in full, the soul is questioned, and the outcome is punitive. The "easy reckoning" of this verse, the Prophet ﷺ teaches, is the first kind: it is not that the believer's sins are unexamined, but that God, having forgiven them, spares him the ordeal of the detailed interrogation. It is the difference between a case dismissed with mercy and a case prosecuted to judgment.

**Why the Believer's Account Is Easy: The Logic of Mercy**

The ḥadīth raises a question the commentators answer carefully: on what basis is the believer's account made easy, if his book contains the same human weight of error as anyone's? The answer is the Qur'an's doctrine of the covering of sins. God describes Himself as *al-Ghafūr*, *al-ʿAfuww*, *al-Sattār* — the Forgiving, the Pardoning, the Concealer — and the Prophet ﷺ painted the scene of the believer's private judgment in a ḥadīth qudsī of extraordinary tenderness:

> *"God will bring the believer near and place His covering over him and conceal him, and say: 'Do you know such-and-such a sin? Do you know such-and-such a sin?' He will say: 'Yes, my Lord,' until he has confessed his sins and thinks himself destroyed. Then He will say: 'I concealed them for you in the world, and I forgive you for them today.' Then he will be given the book of his good deeds."* (al-Bukhārī 2441; Muslim 2768)

This is the "easy reckoning" in its inner reality: the sins are *known*, the servant *confesses*, and the Lord *conceals and forgives*. The ease is not the ease of a clean record — no human record is clean — but the ease of a settled debt, forgiven by the Creditor. Ibn Kathīr notes the profound comfort here: the believer's account is easy precisely because it is *merciful*, and the mercy is not a contradiction of justice but its crown, exercised by the same Lord whose justice is perfect.

**The Presentation of Deeds in the Ḥadīth Literature**

The tradition fills out the picture of *al-ʿarḍ* — the presentation — with details that the commentators gather under this verse. In the long ḥadīth of the intercession recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, the believers are described as being gathered, their deeds presented, and their sins concealed, until they cross the bridge into the Garden without the torment of the full interrogation. The Prophet ﷺ also taught that among the first to enter the Garden will be a man whose deeds, when presented, are found to be few — yet God, out of His generosity, grants him entry (cf. the ḥadīth of the card, al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah, in which the word *Lā ilāha illā Allāh* outweighs ninety-nine scrolls of sins). The cumulative picture the tradition paints is one of an asymmetry deliberately built into the divine economy: the believer's small, sincere good is received with a generosity out of all proportion to its size, while his confessed sin is covered. The "easy reckoning" is that asymmetry made into a verdict.

**What the Verse Requires of the Living**

But the verse is not a license, and the commentators are emphatic on the point: the easy reckoning has a condition, and the condition is the life that produced the right-hand book. The believer who is spared the interrogation is the one who *lived* as though the account were coming — who repented before death, restored what he owed men, and kept his account with God in a state of continual settlement. The Prophet ﷺ taught the principle that governs the whole matter:

> *"The wise one is he who brings his soul to account and works for what comes after death."* (al-Tirmidhī 2459; Ibn Mājah 4260, from Shaddād ibn Aws — graded ḥasan)

And his ﷺ great successor ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (d. 23/644) turned it into the most quoted maxim of Muslim self-governance:

> *"Bring yourselves to account before you are brought to account, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed for you."*

The logic is the logic of every honest business: the account that is *reviewed daily* is the account that is *settled easily* at the year's end. A believer who audits himself nightly — in the practice the tradition calls *muḥāsabah* — arrives at the final audit with his book already known to him, already confessed, already in order, and the ease of the Last Day is the fruit of the discipline of all the days before it. The verse's "easy reckoning" is thus not a lucky escape but the natural harvest of a life that never let its ledger lapse.

**Ease as the Believer's Atmosphere, Not His Escape**

Finally, the verse corrects a widespread modern misreading of the Hereafter as a courtroom of terror for everyone. The sūrah is at pains to show that the believer's experience of the Day is characterized, at every stage, by *ease* — the word *yasīr* here answering the *kadḥ* (toil) of verse 6 with a deliberate tenderness. The toil is real, but it terminates in ease; the labor is heavy, but the reckoning is light. The Prophet ﷺ condensed this entire economy into a promise recorded by Muslim: when the people of the Garden have entered it, a caller will announce that they shall never die, never fall ill, never grow old, and never sorrow — and God will say to them, "I have made My good pleasure binding upon you, and I shall never be angry with you after this" (cf. Muslim, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī). The easy reckoning is the first installment of that permanent ease. The believer who reads verse 8 should hear in it not a loophole but a climate — the climate of mercy in which his whole relationship with God is conducted, from the first repentance to the final presentation.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:9

> **and will return to his people joyful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Destination of the Blessed**

The easy reckoning of verse 8 resolves into a homecoming: *wa yanqalibu ilā ahlihi masrūrā* — "and he will return to his people joyful." The verb *yanqalibu* (from *qalaba*, "to turn, return") is the verb of a traveler who turns back toward home after a completed journey, and *masrūrā* (from *surūr*, joy, gladness) is a joy that is settled and inward — the joy of arrival, not the excitement of anticipation. The sūrah is closing the arc it opened in verse 6: the human being who was *laboring unto his Lord* now *returns* — the labor completed, the meeting passed, the book received, the reckoning easy — and the word for the return is homecoming. The laborer of verse 6 was a stranger in the world; the recipient of verse 9 is coming home.

**Who Are "His People"? The Three Readings of *Ahlihi***

The commentators devote careful attention to the phrase *ahlihi* — "his people, his family" — because the identity of the family the blessed one returns to is itself a promise. Al-Zamakhsharī and the source commentary record three readings, each a layer of the same joy. The first: his *literal family* — the parents, spouse, and children who were believers, reunited in Paradise, in fulfillment of the Qur'an's repeated promise that the pious will be joined with their righteous kin: "and those who believed and whose descendants followed them in faith — We will join their descendants with them" (52:21); "Gardens of Eden which they enter, along with whoever was righteous among their fathers, spouses, and descendants" (13:23); "and the angels enter upon them from every gate: 'Peace be upon you for what you patiently endured — and excellent is the final home'" (13:23–24). The second: his *spiritual people* — the company of believers among whom he lived and worshipped, his brothers and sisters in faith, gathered in the Garden as they had gathered in the mosque. The third, and most expansive: the *people of Paradise* — the community of the saved as a whole, the great family of the righteous across all generations, in which every blessed soul finds kin. Al-Qurṭubī notes that all three are true at once, and the verse intends the widest embrace: the believer who feared that death would separate him from those he loved is here promised the opposite — reunion, in a company that has only grown.

**Joy as the Fruit of a Right-Hand Life**

The joy named in the verse is not an arbitrary reward bolted onto a good life; it is the *internal consequence* of the life itself, made visible. The man whose book came in his right hand is the man who lived for the meeting — who believed, labored, repented, and kept his account — and joy is simply what such a life *becomes* when its hidden labor is finally confirmed. The sūrah is precise about the sequence: easy reckoning (v. 8) *then* return to his people *then* joy (v. 9). The joy is not the cause of anything; it is the harvest. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the same joy in the exact same vocabulary of the homecoming traveler: "So their Lord answered them: 'I do not allow the work of any worker among you to be lost... and We will surely admit them into Gardens beneath which rivers flow' " (3:195); and in the voice of the blessed themselves: "And they will say, 'Praise be to God, who has fulfilled His promise to us and made us inherit the earth, dwelling in the Garden wherever we wish — and how excellent is the reward of the workers!'" (39:74). The word "workers" — *al-ʿāmilīn* — deliberately echoes the "laboring" of verse 6: the joy of the Garden is the wage of the toil, and the verse wants the reader to feel the link between the two.

**The Welcome That Begins Before the Judgment**

The tradition adds a dimension that lifts the verse out of the abstract: the believer's homecoming to his people begins, in a sense, at the moment of death, long before the Resurrection. In the long and widely transmitted narration of al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib, recorded by Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd, and al-Ḥākim, the Prophet ﷺ described the death of the believer: the angels descend with white faces "as though their faces were suns," carrying a shroud and perfume from the Garden; the soul is drawn out as gently as a drop leaves a waterskin; it is carried up through the heavens, and at each gate the angels ask who this pure soul is and answer one another with the best of the names he was known by on earth; and it is announced, "write his book in ʿIlliyyūn," and he is shown his place in the Garden, and he is told, "this is your place until God resurrects you." The "return to his people" of verse 9 is the public and permanent version of that private welcome — the believer is never alone at any point of the journey, and the sūrah's word *ahlihi* gathers the whole angelic and human company that has been waiting for him.

**The Mirror of Verse 13**

The reader is meant to hold verse 9 against verse 13, and the sūrah plants the two like facing mirrors. The blessed one *returns to his people joyful* (*masrūrā*); the condemned one *was* joyful among his people (*masrūrā*, v. 13) — the identical word in two opposite tenses. The joy of the believer is *future and abiding* — he returns *to* it — while the joy of the heedless was *past and spent* — it lies behind him, in the world he mistook for home. The single shared word is the sūrah's most economical commentary on itself: joy is a destination or it is a memory, and the whole difference between the two fates is whether a person's joy was stored up ahead of him in the meeting with God, or spent behind him in the world's entertainments. The believer, in other words, is the one who *deferred* his joy into the one place it could never be lost — and verse 9 is the moment the deferral pays out.

**Home, Understood Correctly**

Finally, the verse teaches the believer the true meaning of home. The modern world speaks of "home" as a place of origin, a childhood house, a nation — all of them temporary and all of them losable. The Qur'an quietly relocates the word: *ahlihi*, his people, are those with whom he will dwell forever, and the "return" is not backward to a lost origin but forward to a permanent one. This is why the Prophet ﷺ described the believer's relation to this world in a saying the source commentary records:

> *"The world is the prison of the believer and the garden of the disbeliever."* (Muslim 2956)

A prison is not a home; it is a place one is *released from*. The believer who internalizes verse 9 stops grieving every temporary parting with a despairing finality, because the Qur'an has promised him a reunion that outlasts every separation. The parent who loses a righteous child, the spouse who buries a believing partner, the friend parted by distance or death — all are given here a single consolation: the journey ends in a return, and the return is to one's people, joyful.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:10

> **But as for one who is given his book from behind his back,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second File: The Book from Behind the Back**

The *fa-ammā* that opened the blessed file in verse 7 now opens its opposite: *wa ammā man ūtiya kitābahu warāʾa ẓahrih* — "but as for one who is given his book from behind his back." The structure is a diptych, two panels set side by side, and the sūrah's artistry is in the single detail that differentiates them: the book is not placed in a left hand opposite the right, but *behind the back*. The condemned man receives his record from a direction he cannot see, in a position of the deepest possible humiliation — the position of one who dare not face what is coming, whose record is handed to him from behind like a warrant served on a fugitive who has turned away. The commentators pause over this image precisely because it is stronger than a simple "left hand" would have been: the left hand is a symbol; the back is a *posture*, and the posture is that of a man who spent his whole life turned away from God, and now finds his book — and his fate — arriving from behind him.

**Reconciling This Verse with Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah**

The reader of the Qur'an notices a seeming difference between this verse and its companion in Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah, which says: "And as for him who is given his book in his left hand, he will say, 'Would that I had not been given my book!'" (69:25). The commentators resolve the two accounts without difficulty, and their reconciliation is recorded by Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Zamakhsharī: on the Day of Resurrection the disbeliever's *left hand* will be bound behind his back, so that the book is received from behind his back *into* the left hand — the two verses describing the same event from its two sides, one naming the hand that receives, the other naming the position in which the hand is held. Both details together paint the complete picture: the right hand, free and forward, receives the book of light; the left hand, bound and backward, receives the book of darkness. The binding is itself a commentary — the man who spent his life reaching forward toward forbidden things, with his right hand free for every disobedience, finds his instruments of sin bound and useless at the one moment he might have wanted them for anything else.

**The Record He Hid Behind**

There is a deeper reason the book comes from behind, and the spiritual commentators draw it out: the disbeliever's record is the thing he spent his life *putting behind him*. The deeds recorded in that book — the sins, the denials, the neglected duties, the wronged neighbors, the years lived in forgetfulness of the meeting — are precisely the things he refused to face while alive, pushing them behind his back day by day, telling himself there would be no reckoning and no return. The Qur'an describes this habit of the heedless with the same spatial image: "Nay, man will be a clear witness against himself, even if he presents his excuses" (75:14–15); and in the parable of the one who "turned his back" — *wallā mustadbiran* — in the story of the rich man of the two gardens (cf. 96:13; 79:22). On the Last Day, the direction of a life is made literal: what was pushed behind for a lifetime arrives from behind at the end. The book from behind the back is the geometry of a soul's lifetime of turning away, finally completed.

**The First Word of the Verse: "But"**

The verse opens with *wa* — "and/but" — and the contrast it marks deserves emphasis. The sūrah is not describing two independent fates but one division, and the word binds them: wherever there is a right-hand book, there is also its opposite, and the reader cannot have one panel of the diptych without the other. Ibn Kathīr notes that the Qur'an's method here is the method of the warning and the glad tiding joined in a single breath, the way a physician describes both the outcome of treatment and the outcome of neglect. The believer reads verse 10 not to gloat over anyone but to be *made serious*: the same record-keeping that will vindicate the righteous will convict the heedless, and the two are not separable. The verse is thus an instrument of *khawf* — the reverent fear that steadies the heart — placed immediately after the instrument of *rajāʾ* (hope) in verse 9, so that the reader's hope is never allowed to curdle into presumption. The balance of hope and fear is the Qur'an's constant pedagogy, and the *wa* of verse 10 is its hinge.

**The Horror, Stated Without Drama**

It is characteristic of the sūrah's method that the verse states the catastrophe with complete restraint. There is no description of flames here, no enumeration of torments — only a book, a position, and the two short verses that follow (the cry and the Blaze). The restraint is deliberate: the sūrah lets the reader's own imagination supply the weight, and the commentators note that the most terrifying verses in the Qur'an are often the most understated, because understatement forces the hearer to complete the scene himself. The man who receives a book from behind his back, unable to see it, knowing what must be in it, is a figure the reader is made to inhabit for one verse — and it is the inhabiting, not any description, that does the sūrah's work. The verse's single image is worth a thousand catalogues of torment precisely because it is *human*: it is the moment a person's whole lifetime of turning away is handed back to him, and he must take it, from behind.

**The Lesson Turned Toward the Reader**

Finally, the verse's practical teaching is the mirror of verse 7's. If the right-hand book is built by deeds done in the daylight of faith, the book from behind the back is built by deeds done in the darkness of denial — and the reader is asked, now, which book his present life is accumulating. The sūrah does not permit the comfortable thought that the back is only for others: the Qur'an's own portrait of the heedless includes the warning that a person can drift into the turned-away posture without ever declaring it — "and the day when the wrongdoer will bite his hands, saying, 'Would that I had taken a path with the Messenger!'" (25:27). The verse therefore functions, like verse 7, as a present-tense mirror: it asks each reader to turn around, while turning around is still possible, and to look at what he has been pushing behind his back. The sūrah will now follow the man of the back into his end — the cry of verse 11 and the Blaze of verse 12 — and the reader follows, warned.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:11

> **he will cry out for destruction,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word the Condemned Man Will Say**

*Fa-sawfa yadʿū thubūrā* — "he will cry out for destruction." The verb *yadʿū* means to call out, to invoke, to summon — and what he summons is *thubūr*, destruction, ruin, perdition, the utter and final loss of oneself. The same word and the same cry appear in Sūrah al-Furqān, where the inhabitants of the Fire are described in the full scene: "And the Day the wrongdoer bites his hands, saying: 'Would that I had taken a path with the Messenger! Woe to me! Would that I had not taken so-and-so as a friend!'" (25:27–28) — and then: "when they are cast into a narrow place of it, bound together, they will call out there for destruction (*daʿaw hunālika thubūrā*). 'Do not call out this Day for one destruction, but call out for much destruction!'" (25:13–14). The cry of this verse is thus not a word the sūrah invents but a word the Qur'an has already placed in the mouths of the damned: *thubūr* is the prayer of the one who has lost everything and now asks, with all that remains of his voice, for the only end he can imagine — to be annihilated, to cease to be.

**The Irony of the Cry**

The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr) all note the bitter irony built into the verse. The word *yadʿū* — "he will call, he will pray" — is the same verb used for *duʿāʾ*, the believer's supplication. The believer spent his life calling upon God for mercy; the condemned man now calls for destruction, and his call is itself a testimony against him. Even more searing is the irony that his request can never be granted: the Qur'an elsewhere answers the cry explicitly — "And they will call, 'O Mālik, let your Lord put an end to us!' He will say, 'Indeed, you will remain'" (43:77). The one thing the man of the back most desperately wants — to be undone — is the one thing that is now forever out of reach, because the soul, once created, is not destroyed but either saved or punished. The cry for destruction is thus a prayer that heaven will never answer, and the refusal is the measure of the calamity: the man would rather not exist than exist as he now does, and yet exist he must.

**Why the Cry Comes at This Point in the Sūrah**

The placement of the verse is precise. In verse 10 the book arrives from behind the back; in verse 11, before any torment is even named, the man cries out. The sūrah is making a psychological point that the commentators dwell on: the condemnation of the heedless does not begin with fire — it begins with *knowledge*. The moment the book is in his hands (behind his back), the man *knows*, with a certainty no denial can now touch, what his life amounted to and what awaits it. The cry of *thubūr* is not a reaction to pain; it is a reaction to *truth finally recognized*. The Qur'an elsewhere confirms that the first torment of the damned is recognition itself: "And the record will be placed, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it, and they will say: 'Woe to us! What is this book that leaves out nothing small or great but has enumerated it?'" (18:49). The cry is the sound of that recognition — and the sūrah wants the reader to understand that the Fire, terrible as it is, is only the continuation of an inward catastrophe that has already begun.

**The Tragic Reversal of the Laborer**

The verse must also be read against verse 6, the sūrah's hinge. The man of verse 6 was *laboring* — *kādiḥun* — and the sūrah never said he was idle. The heedless man of verses 10–12 labored too: he labored for wealth, for pleasure, for status, for his own desire, with the same *kadḥ* the believer gave to his Lord. The cry of *thubūr* is the endpoint of that other labor: all the toil of a lifetime, every night of striving, every ambition pursued, has culminated in a man praying for his own annihilation. Ibn Kathīr's commentary makes the correspondence explicit: the believer's toil ends in the easy reckoning and the joyful return, while the disbeliever's toil ends in the cry and the Blaze — and the *direction* of the toil, not its quantity, is what separated them. The verse is thus the sūrah's most concentrated warning about wasted effort: no amount of labor is itself redemptive; a lifetime of energy aimed at the wrong destination is a lifetime that ends in the prayer for destruction.

**The Cry as a Door the Living Can Still Use**

Finally, the tradition turns the verse, with characteristic mercy, into an instrument for the living. The cry of *thubūr* is what a person must never have to say — and the way never to have to say it is to say, now, the opposite word: *tawbah*, turning back, repentance. The Qur'an pairs the two words across its pages: the heedless cry *thubūr* at the end, while the believer, at any moment before the end, can cry *tawbah* and be received — "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God's mercy; indeed, God forgives all sins" (39:53). The sūrah places this verse in front of the reader not as a sentence pronounced over anyone but as a warning sounded for everyone: the cry is recorded in the Qur'an so that it may be *replaced* in the reader's mouth by its opposite while replacement is still possible. The man of the back is not yet anyone in particular; he is a possibility that every heedless life is tending toward, and the verse exists so that the reader, hearing the cry from the page, will turn back before he ever has to utter it.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:12

> **and will enter a Blaze.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Terminal of the Turned-Away Life**

*Wa yaṣlā saʿīrā* — "and he will enter a Blaze." The verb *yaṣlā* (from *ṣaliya*, "to be exposed to fire, to roast, to burn") is the Qur'an's precise word for enduring fire, and *saʿīr* is one of the Qur'an's names for Hell — literally "the blazing, the kindled," a fire that has been lit and fed and is now in full flame. The verse closes the three-verse arc of the condemned with maximum brevity: book from behind the back (v. 10), cry for destruction (v. 11), entrance into the Blaze (v. 12). The sūrah does not linger on the description; it simply states the terminus, and the commentators observe that the brevity is itself the rhetoric — after the cry of verse 11, nothing more needs to be said, and the single word *saʿīrā* lands like the slamming of the gate. The destination is reached; the toil of verse 6, misdirected, has run its full course.

**The Book and the Blaze: Why the Record Comes First**

The sequence of the three verses is theologically exact, and the commentators mark it: the book (v. 10) comes before the Blaze (v. 12) because the punishment is *established by the record*. The man is not cast into the Fire arbitrarily; he is entered into it *by what the book contains*. The Qur'an states the principle as a universal rule of that Day: "This Day no soul will be wronged in anything, and you will not be recompensed except for what you used to do" (36:54); "And the record will be placed, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it... and they will find what they did present, and your Lord does not wrong anyone" (18:49). The Blaze, in other words, is not an external penalty bolted onto a life but the *consequence* of a life — the book is the cause, and the Fire is the effect, the way a debt acknowledged is a debt owed, and a debt owed and unpaid is a debt collected. The sūrah's whole diptych is built on this correspondence: the right-hand book *becomes* the easy reckoning and the joy, and the book from behind *becomes* the cry and the Blaze. Each receives the shape his own life took.

**A Fire the Qur'an Never Lets Us Forget**

Though the sūrah is brief on the Blaze, the Qur'an is not, and the commentators read this verse in the light of the full portrait the Book paints elsewhere. The *saʿīr* is the fire "whose fuel is people and stones, prepared for the disbelievers" (2:24); it is the fire that "has been kindled" and "leaps over the hearts" (104:6–7); it is the fire of which God asks, "and what will make you know what the Crushing Fire is? It is the kindled fire of God, which rises over the hearts" (104:4–7). It has seven gates (15:44), its drink is boiling water and its shade is of black smoke (56:42–44), and its inhabitants are told in the verse that most directly answers the cry of verse 11: "Do not call out this Day for one destruction, but call out for much destruction" (25:14). The Prophet ﷺ described its distance from any earthly fire with a comparison that the commentators on this verse record:

> *"Your fire, which the children of Adam kindle, is one of seventy parts of the fire of Hell."* They said, "O Messenger of God, would it not have sufficed?" He said: *"It has been given sixty-nine times more than it, each part as hot as it."* (al-Bukhārī 3265; Muslim 2843)

The point of the comparison is not to inspire despair but to convey *proportion*: the Fire is not an exaggerated earthly flame but a created thing of a different order, prepared by the same Lord who prepared the Garden, and the sūrah's one word *saʿīrā* is a pointer to everything the Qur'an says about it elsewhere.

**The Missing Word: "His" Blaze**

A grammatical detail in the Arabic deserves note, for it carries a doctrine. The verse does not say "he will enter *the* Blaze" as though the Fire were a single fixed chamber; the commentators observe that the Qur'an also speaks of each person's recompense as, in a sense, *his own* — "indeed, the Fire will be his shelter" (cf. 92:14; 79:39), "for them is a painful punishment" (cf. 2:10). The individualizing of the punishment is part of the Qur'an's doctrine of exact justice: no soul bears the burden of another (6:164; 35:18), and each is recompensed for what *he* earned. The man of verse 12 enters a Blaze that is, in the deepest sense, the product of his own record — not a mass sentence but a personal one. This is why the Qur'an can say, in a verse the commentators join to this one, "that is their recompense because they disbelieved in Our signs" (cf. 3:21; 45:8) — the punishment is always traced back, verse after verse, to a specific life and its specific deeds, because the justice of God is not collective but individual, and the individual is exactly the one the sūrah has been addressing since verse 6.

**The Verse as Mercy, in the Only Way a Warning Can Be**

A modern reader may ask what place such a verse has in a message of mercy, and the tradition's answer is worth stating plainly: the warning *is* the mercy. A God who is truly good does not leave a human being unwarned about the end of the road he is traveling; the verse exists so that no one reaches verse 12's destination by surprise. The Prophet ﷺ described his own mission in exactly these terms — "I am a warner and a bringer of glad tidings" (cf. 11:2; 34:28) — and he wept for his community in the night prayer precisely because he had been shown what this verse states. The sūrah's strategy throughout is to pair the two panels — the joy and the Blaze — so that the reader is pulled by hope and checked by fear at the same time, the two forces that the classical scholars of the heart, from al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī to al-Ghazzālī, called the wings of the believer's journey. Verse 12 is one wing. Read alone it is terror; read with verse 9 it is the sobering counterweight that makes the joy real, and the warning that makes the turn — *tawbah* — urgent while the turn is still possible. The sūrah's final section will now drive the point home with oaths, so that no reader can claim he was not told.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:13

> **Truly among his people he was joyful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Backstory of the Condemned Man**

Having delivered the verdict in verses 10–12, the sūrah now steps backward in time to explain it: *innahu kāna fī ahlihi masrūrā* — "truly, among his people he was joyful." The particle *inna* ("truly, indeed") opens an explanation — *this is why* the book came from behind his back, *this is why* he cries for destruction and enters the Blaze. The verse is the sūrah's single-word diagnosis of a ruined life: the man was *joyful* — *masrūrā*, the same word the sūrah used for the believer's joy in verse 9 — but his joy was *among his people*, in this world, spent on its pleasures, its approvals, and its entertainments. The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr) all read "his people" here as his family and circle in the life of this world — the company among whom he strutted and laughed, in the world whose approval he chased and whose enjoyments he consumed. The verse, they note, is the portrait of the heedless man of the world: the one for whom home, joy, and security were all located *here*, on this side of the grave, with no reserve laid up for the other side.

**The Single Word That Mirrors Verse 9**

The sūrah's artistry is nowhere more economical than in the repetition of *masrūrā* — "joyful" — in verse 9 and verse 13. The believer *will return to his people joyful* (future, abiding); the heedless *was joyful among his people* (past, spent). The one word, placed in the two panels of the diptych, carries the whole moral of the sūrah. Joy is not the problem — the sūrah never condemns joy, and the believer's own end is described as joy. The problem is the *location* of the joy. The believer placed his joy in front of him, in the meeting with God, and so his joy is future and unlosable; the heedless placed his joy behind him, in the world, and so his joy is past and gone. Al-Qurṭubī draws the contrast with the sharpness of a proverb: the believer's joy *awaits* him, while the heedless man's joy *betrays* him — it abandons him at exactly the moment he needs it, leaving him to face verse 11 and verse 12 with empty hands. The tense of a single verb, past against future, is the sūrah's whole eschatology.

**"Among His People": The Company That Concealed the Truth**

The phrase *fī ahlihi* — "among his people, in the midst of his family" — deserves its full weight, because it names the social dimension of heedlessness. The man was joyful *among his people* — his joy was a social joy, the joy of being approved, admired, envied, and entertained within a circle that shared his forgetfulness. The Qur'an repeatedly traces the ruin of the heedless to their *company*: the wrongdoer of Sūrah al-Furqān bites his hands and cries "would that I had not taken so-and-so as a friend" (25:28); the inhabitants of the Fire confess, "we obeyed our masters and our great ones, and they led us astray from the way" (33:67); and the believers are warned of the company that "makes you forget the remembrance of your Lord" (cf. 58:19). The verse is thus a quiet social warning: the circle in which a person is *joyful* is often the circle in which he is *forgetful*, and the family or friends who make the world feel like home are, for many, the very company that made the meeting with God seem unreal. The sūrah is not condemning family love — it is exposing family *complacency*, the shared, comfortable unbelief of a circle that never disturbs itself.

**The Psychology of Deferred Joy**

The verse is also a study in a psychology that is as modern as it is ancient: the human default of preferring a small joy now over a great joy later. The heedless man of verse 13 is the man who could not — or would not — defer. Every life is a series of such choices: the joy of the moment against the joy of the account. The Qur'an's diagnosis is that the man of the world systematically took the first and let the second lapse, until his joy was entirely *spent* and nothing remained but the bill. The Prophet ﷺ taught the economics of this choice in a saying that the commentators on this passage invoke:

> *"The world is the prison of the believer and the garden of the disbeliever."* (Muslim 2956)

The image is exact: the disbeliever treats the world as his garden — the place where he is joyful — while the believer treats it as a prison, a place of restraint through which he passes. The restraint is not misery; it is the deferral that verse 13's subject refused. The believer's joy is not less than the heedless man's — it is *later*, and therefore *more*, the way a harvest is more than a handful of green grain eaten in the field.

**The Warning Turned Inward**

Finally, the verse turns its mirror on every reader, and the tradition insists on the personal application. The joy "among one's people" is not a distant historical vice; it is the daily atmosphere in which any comfortable life can drift. The question the verse plants is not "do you know such people?" but "where is *my* joy located?" The Qur'an elsewhere gives the believer a diagnostic test in the same vocabulary: "And if they had believed in God and the Prophet and what was revealed to him, they would not have taken them as allies" (cf. 5:81) — and, more pointedly, the verse that measures faith by what a person prefers: "Say: If your fathers, your sons, your brothers, your spouses, your clan, the wealth you have acquired, the trade you fear will decline, and the homes you love are dearer to you than God and His Messenger and striving in His way, then wait until God brings His command" (9:24). Verse 13 is that test in miniature: it does not forbid joy among one's people; it forbids joy *that stops there*, joy with no reserve beyond the circle, joy that makes the world feel so final that the meeting of verse 6 is forgotten. The believer's correction is not to renounce joy but to *re-anchor* it — to be joyful among his people in the world while storing his real joy, by his deeds and his direction, in the home to which verse 9 promises he will return.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:14

> **Truly he thought that he would never return.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Root of the Ruin: A Thought, Not a Deed**

The sūrah now descends from the man's outward joy to its inward source: *innahu ẓanna an lan yaḥūr* — "truly he thought that he would never return." The word *ẓanna* means "he thought, he supposed, he reckoned" — and it names the true disease, which was never a single sin but a *conviction*. The man of verses 10–13 did not stumble into ruin through weakness; he lived his whole heedless joy on the basis of a belief — the belief that death was the end, that there would be no return, no resurrection, no reckoning. The verb *yaḥūr* (from *ḥāra*, "to return, to come back") is rare and vivid: it is the return of one who has gone away and comes back to the place he left. The sūrah is saying, with devastating precision, that the man's ruin was built on a single wrong idea — and that the idea was not a private doubt but the governing assumption of his entire life. Every act of heedless joy in verse 13 was, in the end, an act of faith in verse 14's denial.

**The Qur'an's Portrait of the Denying Mind**

The Qur'an returns to this wrong "thought" again and again, because it is the intellectual root of all heedlessness. The deniers of every generation are quoted with the same sentence: "They said: 'There is nothing but our life of this world; we die and we live, and nothing destroys us except time'" (45:24); "And they say: 'There is none but our life of this world, and we shall not be resurrected'" (6:29); "What! When we have died and become dust and bones, shall we indeed be raised again?" (23:82). The Qur'an's counter-argument is always the same and always elegant: the one who denies the return is the one who has never thought seriously about *beginnings* — "Does man not see that We created him from a drop, and behold, he is an open adversary? And he strikes a likeness for Us and forgets his own creation, saying: 'Who will give life to the bones when they are decayed?' Say: He will give them life who produced them the first time, and He is, of every creation, Knowing" (36:77–79). The "thought" of verse 14 is thus, in the Qur'an's analysis, an *inconsistency* — a mind that accepts the miracle of its own first creation while denying that the same power can repeat it. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī both read the verse in this light: the man's denial was never a conclusion of evidence but a *wish* dressed up as a theory — he "thought" there would be no return because he *wanted* there to be no return, and the want came first, the thought second.

**The Connection to Verse 6: The Meeting He Wouldn't Believe**

The verse is the exact counterpoint of verse 6, and the sūrah means the reader to feel the echo. Verse 6 declared, to the same "O mankind": *innaka kādiḥun ilā rabbika kadḥan fa-mulāqīh* — "thou art laboring unto thy Lord laboriously, and shall meet Him." Verse 14 reports the heedless man's reply, in the same *inna* form: *innahu ẓanna an lan yaḥūr* — "truly he thought that he would never return." The whole drama of the sūrah is compressed into the distance between these two verses: God declares the meeting; man disbelieves it; and the disbelieving is itself the crime that the book from behind the back records. The commentators note the bitter precision: the man was *told* — verse 6 addressed him directly, in the second person — and yet he *thought* otherwise, and lived as though the warning had never been given. The ruin of verse 12 is thus never presented as ignorance but as *disbelief of what had been declared* — a thought maintained against the evidence, in the comfort of the circle described in verse 13.

**The Psychology of the Impossible Return**

The verse is also a study in a specific psychological state that the Qur'an names with clinical accuracy: the state of the one who *cannot imagine* a return because his entire sense of reality has been shaped by what he can see. The man "thought he would never return" in the way a fish "thinks" there is no dry land — not by argument but by the sheer enclosure of his horizon. The Qur'an's response to this state, throughout its pages, is to break the enclosure with the spectacle of nature: "And among His signs is that you see the earth barren; then when We send down water upon it, it quivers and swells. Indeed, He who gives it life is the Giver of life to the dead" (41:39); "Look then at the traces of God's mercy, how He revives the earth after its death — indeed, that is the Reviver of the dead" (30:50). The revival of the dead earth every spring is the standing, visible refutation of the "thought" of verse 14 — a yearly demonstration, played out in every field the denier passes, that return after death is not a fantasy but the pattern of the world itself. The sūrah's own oaths, which begin in verse 16, will pick up exactly this argument from nature, swearing by the twilight, the night, and the full moon as witnesses against the denier's "thought."

**The Danger of a Life Built on a Supposition**

Finally, the verse carries a warning for the modern reader that is sharper now than ever. The man of verse 14 built an entire life — his joys, his priorities, his neglects, his cruelties — on a *supposition* (*ẓann*), on the unproved assumption that the meeting would never come. The Qur'an elsewhere generalizes the warning from one man to the human condition: "They have no knowledge of that; they follow only supposition, and supposition avails nothing against the truth" (53:28). The modern world has made this supposition fashionable, elevating a materialist picture of the world to the status of settled fact and organizing whole societies around the assumption that death is the end. The verse's warning is not that such people are evil but that they are *gambling with the unprovable as though it were certain* — and the stakes are the meeting of verse 6. The believer's intellectual honesty, by contrast, is to weigh the possibility of the return with the seriousness its consequences demand. The sūrah does not ask for blind faith; it asks for the removal of a blind *assumption*. The man of verse 14 did not know there would be no return — he *thought* it, and staked eternity on the thought. The verse, and the oaths that follow it, exist to make that stake visible before it is collected.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:15

> **Nay! Truly his Lord was watchful of him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Nay" — The Particle That Overturns a Thought**

The verse opens with *balā* — "Nay! Yes, indeed!" — the particle of emphatic contradiction, and the commentators note that it is aimed straight at the "thought" of verse 14. The man thought he would never return; the verse answers: *Nay — you will indeed return* — and then, instead of merely asserting the return, it adds the reason that makes the return certain: *inna rabbahu kāna bihi baṣīrā* — "truly his Lord was ever watchful of him." Al-Ṭabarī's gloss, preserved in the source, is the classical reading: the man will in fact return, for God has been aware of all his actions from the moment He created him until the moment He resurrects him. The logic is the Qur'an's most characteristic argument for the Hereafter: the return is not a new decision God makes at the end of history but the *inevitable completion* of an observation that has been continuous from the beginning. A God who has watched every deed cannot end the matter by letting the deeds scatter into nothing; the watching implies the accounting.

**Baṣīr: The Ever-Watchful**

The word *baṣīr* is one of God's Beautiful Names, usually rendered "the All-Seeing," and its form here — *kāna bihi baṣīrā* — deserves careful unpacking. The verb *kāna* ("was") with the past tense does not limit God's seeing to the past; in Arabic this construction expresses a state that was continuous and unbroken — "He was, and ever remained, watchful of him." The preposition *bihi* ("of him, over him") attaches the seeing to the specific man: God's watchfulness is not a general surveillance of the crowd but a particular, individual regard for this one human being, the same *singular* the sūrah has addressed since verse 6. And *baṣīr* itself is the intensive form of the seer — not one who catches a glimpse but one whose seeing is total, constant, and penetrating. The verse is thus the Qur'an's compact statement of the doctrine the tradition calls *murāqabah*: that no human being is ever unwatched, that the eye of God is upon each soul individually, and that the privacy a person feels in his most hidden hour is, in the strict sense, an illusion.

**The Qur'an's Geography of God's Nearness**

The verse gathers into one word a theme the Qur'an states elsewhere with an intimacy that the commentators never fail to quote here. "And We have already created man, and We know what his soul whispers to him, and We are nearer to him than his jugular vein" (50:16). "He knows the stealthy glance and what the breasts conceal" (40:19). "Is there not in the breasts of the people a covering over Our reminder?" (cf. 11:5) — the verse that describes men folding up their breasts to hide from God, and answers: "Unquestionably, when they wrap themselves up, He knows what they conceal and what they declare — indeed, He is Knowing of what is within the breasts" (11:5). And the verse that most completely states the doctrine: "There is no secret consultation of three but He is their fourth, nor of five but He is their sixth, nor of fewer or more but He is with them wherever they are" (58:7). The watchfulness of verse 15 is not the cold observation of a distant record-keeper; it is the *nearness* of a Lord who is closer than the vein, who hears the whisper before it is spoken, and who has never once lost sight of the soul He created. The man who "thought he would never return" was, all along, standing in the direct gaze of the One to whom he will return.

**Why Watchfulness Implies Reckoning**

The verse's argument deserves to be stated in its full logical form, because it is the Qur'an's most powerful rational proof of the Hereafter, and the commentators draw it out explicitly. If God has been watchful of every deed — if nothing, not a whisper, not a glance, not a concealed intention, has escaped His sight — then the watchfulness cannot be without purpose. A witness who records everything and then lets it all dissolve into nothing would be a witness whose record was a vanity; a judge who notes every act and never convenes a court would be a judge who mocks his own notation. The very perfection of God's seeing *demands* the completion of the seeing in an accounting. Ibn Kathīr makes the point through the verse's own grammar: the watchfulness is stated as the *reason* (*inna*, "truly") for overturning the man's "thought" of no-return — because He was ever watchful, therefore there must be a return, for the watched deeds must be answered. The Hereafter, in this reading, is not an extra doctrine added to belief in God's knowledge; it is the *logical consequence* of that knowledge, the moral necessity of a universe whose every detail is seen.

**The Doctrine of Murāqabah: Living as the Watched**

The tradition did not leave this verse as theology; it converted it into a discipline with a name — *murāqabah*, the awareness of being watched — and built it into the fabric of Muslim life. The Prophet ﷺ defined the highest station of worship, *iḥsān*, in exactly the terms of this verse, in the celebrated ḥadīth of Gabriel recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"Iḥsān is that you worship God as though you see Him; for if you do not see Him, then indeed He sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8)

The discipline is the believer's answer to verse 15: since God *is* watchful, the believer *lives as* the watched — and the living changes everything. A worker performs differently under the employer's eye; a student studies differently under the examiner's gaze; a child behaves differently in the presence of a parent. The believer who has internalized verse 15 carries the highest possible form of that awareness — the awareness of a witness who never blinks, never forgets, and can never be deceived — into every hour of his life, and the result is the steady, quiet integrity the Qur'an elsewhere calls *taqwā*. The verse is thus the sūrah's hinge from warning to discipline: the man who thought himself unwatched is the ruined man; the man who knows himself watched is the saved one — and the difference between them is precisely the word *baṣīr*.

**The Verse as Comfort and as Caution**

Finally, the verse cuts both ways, and the sūrah intends it to. For the believer, the watchfulness is the deepest comfort in the Qur'an: the Lord who is ever-watchful is the Lord who saw the tears no one else saw, the patience no one else credited, the good deed done in secret that no other eye recorded — and whose book will therefore be full of precisely those hidden treasures when it is handed over in the right hand. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the charity given in secret is among the deeds most beloved to God, and that among the seven whom God shades on the Day of judgment is "a man who gives charity and conceals it so that his left hand does not know what his right hand gives" (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031). For the heedless, the same watchfulness is the caution that should unsettle every private moment — for the eye that misses nothing has also missed none of the hidden things the man of verse 13 hid from his circle. The verse stands at the center of the sūrah's two panels like a single lamp: the same light that will illuminate the believer's hidden good illuminates the heedless man's hidden evil, and neither man's book is empty. The only question the verse leaves open — and the oaths that follow will press it — is which man the reader will choose to be under the gaze that never lifts.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:16

> **So I swear by the twilight,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath of Rejection and Its Meaning**

With verse 16 the sūrah turns from narrative to oath: *fa-lā uqsimu bi-sh-shafaq* — "So — nay — I swear by the twilight." The formula *fa-lā uqsimu* is one of the Qur'an's most distinctive constructions, and the commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, Ibn Kathīr) all pause over it. The particle *lā* here is not the negation of the oath but an emphatic interjection of rebuke — "Nay!" — thrown at the deniers described in verse 14, so that the full sense is: "Nay — you who think there is no return — I swear by the twilight..." The construction occurs again and again in the Makkan sūrahs (56:75; 69:38; 70:40; 75:1; 90:1), always at moments when the Qur'an is answering the deniers of the Resurrection, and it is the rhetorical signature of a speaker pushed to the edge: the denial is so flagrant, so contrary to the evidence all around, that the speaker swears by that very evidence to silence it. The oath is thus not a flourish but an argument — the twilight, the night, and the moon of the next verses are being summoned as *witnesses* in the case against the man who "thought he would never return."

**Why God Swears, and by What**

The oath also raises a question the commentators address directly: why does God swear at all, and why by created things? The first question is answered by the rules of Arabic rhetoric: the oath (*qasam*) is the device by which a speaker affirms a matter of great importance, and the greatness of the matter here — the certainty of the return — justifies the most solemn form of affirmation the language possesses. The second question is answered by the theology of the Qur'an: when God swears by a created thing, He is not *honoring the thing as a deity* but *pointing to it as a sign* — each sworn-by object is a witness to some attribute of God that the matter in question requires. The twilight, the night, and the moon are not randomly chosen; they are, as the next verses will show, a deliberate sequence of witnesses to the very doctrine the deniers rejected — change, gathering, and return. The classical principle, recorded by Ibn al-Qayyim and others, is that God swears by His creatures to draw attention to the *sign* they contain: the oath is an act of teaching, not an act of reliance on anything but God.

**What Is the Twilight? The Debate over *al-Shafaq***

The word *shafaq* denotes the redness — the afterglow — that remains in the western sky after the sun has set, and the commentators record a precise disagreement about its exact extent, because the word had acquired a technical sense in the law of prayer-times. One view, transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and held by the Ḥanafī school, is that *al-shafaq* is the *whiteness* that lingers in the sky after the redness has faded; the other, held by the Shāfiʿī and Mālikī schools and transmitted from many Companions, is that it is the *redness* itself. Al-Qurṭubī treats the question at length in his commentary because it determines the end of the time for the sunset prayer (Maghrib) and the entry of the night prayer (ʿIshāʾ): on the first view, the Maghrib window extends until the whiteness fades; on the second, only until the redness fades. Ibn Kathīr, following the reading of the verse's plain sense, identifies the *shafaq* as "the remaining redness and light of the sun after its setting, at the beginning of the night, until it is close to actual nighttime" — the last ember of the day's fire, glowing on the horizon. For the purposes of the sūrah's argument, the exact juristic boundary matters less than the *image*: the twilight is the threshold moment between day and night, the hour when the world is neither one thing nor the other, the visible border between two states.

**The Twilight as a Witness to Change**

It is precisely as a threshold that the twilight is summoned as the first witness. The man of verse 14 "thought he would never return" — his denial was, at bottom, a denial of *change*, a belief that the world he knew was permanent and that death was a final state with no transition after it. The twilight is the standing refutation: every single evening, before the eyes of every human being, the day passes into night through a boundary state — the afterglow — and then the night passes back into day through the dawn. The whole sky is a machine of transition, and the twilight is its most delicate moving part, the visible proof that states give way to one another, that nothing in the created world is final, that every end is a threshold to a beginning. The Qur'an makes the same argument from the dawn elsewhere: "By the dawn, and the ten nights" (89:1–2); "By the night when it covers, and the day when it shines" (92:1–2). The twilight swears, in effect, that the end of the day is not the end of light — and the sūrah is about to apply that witnessed truth to the end of a human life.

**The Emotional Register of the Afterglow**

Finally, the choice of the twilight carries an emotional weight that the sūrah's first hearers would have felt immediately. The afterglow is the most beautiful and the most *melancholy* hour of the day — the hour of return from the fields and the markets, the hour when the day's work is done and the household gathers, the hour when the Arab traveler watched the light die and thought of the brevity of all things. To swear by the twilight is to swear by the hour of *reckoning with the day* — the moment of summing up — and the sūrah's argument rides on that feeling: just as every day ends in a twilight that settles the day's account, so every life ends in a threshold that settles the life's account. The man of verse 13, who was "joyful among his people," is the man who never stopped at the twilight to consider that the day was ending; the believer is the one for whom the evening afterglow has always been a small, daily rehearsal of the larger return. The verse thus recruits the most ordinary of sights — the red western sky — into the service of the sūrah's single message, and does so with the solemnity of an oath.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:17

> **by the night and what it enshrouds,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Night as the Gatherer**

The second oath: *wa-l-layli wa mā wasaq* — "and by the night and what it enshrouds." The verb *wasaqa* is the precise word the verse turns on, and the commentators treat it at length because its meaning is both concrete and symbolic. In the language of the desert Arabs, *wasaqa* meant to *gather* — specifically, to gather animals together into one herd or fold, the way a shepherd collects his scattered flocks into a single enclosure as evening falls. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī all record this primary sense: the night *gathers* what the day had scattered — the people, the animals, the birds, the travelers — drawing them all in from their dispersion and enclosing them under its darkness. The word thus paints a picture: as darkness falls, the whole living world is collected, creature by creature, into the night's single fold. The night is the great herdsman, and the dusk is its gathering-time.

**From Herding to Enshrouding: The Full Range of *Wasaqa***

The lexicographers extend the root's range, and each extension feeds the verse's argument. *Wasaqa* can also mean to *load and carry* — a camel *wasq* is its full load — and the night, on this sense, is the carrier that takes up the day's creatures and carries them through the dark hours. It can mean to *cover and envelop* — hence the translation "enshrouds" — for the night wraps the world in its darkness the way a garment wraps a body. And it can mean to *assemble, compress, and make into a whole* — the gathering of scattered parts into one. All these senses converge on the same root image, and the sūrah chose the word deliberately: the night is the visible demonstration of *gathering* — the nightly proof, played out over every horizon, that what is scattered can be brought together again. This is exactly the point the sūrah needs, for the man of verse 14 denied that the scattered dead could be gathered; the night answers him, every evening, by gathering the scattered living.

**The Witness the Night Bears**

The night is summoned as the second witness in the sūrah's case, and its testimony follows the twilight's. The twilight testified to *change* — the passing of one state into another. The night now testifies to *gathering* — the collecting of the dispersed into one. Together the two oaths carry the full argument for the Resurrection: states change (so death is not final), and what is scattered can be gathered (so the dispersed bodies and souls of the dead can be reassembled). The Qur'an makes the same argument from the night's gathering in its other oaths: "By the night when it covers, and the day when it shines" (92:1–2); "By the night as it closes in, and the dawn as it breathes" (81:17–18); and, most explicitly for the Resurrection, "And We made the night a garment, and made the day for livelihood" (78:10–11) — the night's enfolding darkness is itself an image of the grave's enclosure, and the day's return after it is the image of the rising. The night of this verse is not a decoration of the oath; it is the argument, witnessed nightly by every human being who has ever watched darkness fall.

**The Night as the Time of the Soul's Return**

The tradition also hears in the oath a note about *where* the gathering matters most. The night is the time when the outer world is gathered away and the inner world gathers itself: the distractions of the day are folded up, the markets close, the roads empty, and the human being is left with what the Qur'an calls the *self* and its *whispers*. It is no accident that the Qur'an's deepest revelations came by night, that the Prophet's ﷺ most intense worship was the night prayer, and that the sūrah of the night journey — al-Isrāʾ — begins "by the night." The night is the hour of *ḥisāb* in the inner sense, the time when a soul, alone in the dark, gathers up its scattered day and reckons with it. The oath "by the night and what it enshrouds" thus also points inward: just as the night gathers the creatures, so the believer's night gathers his conscience, and the practice of nightly self-reckoning — *muḥāsabah* — is the human echo of the night's own gathering work. The man of verse 13, joyful among his people all day, never used the night for what it was made for; the believer is the one for whom the night's gathering is a daily return to himself before the final return to God.

**The Symbolic Power the Source Commentary Names**

The source commentary, following the classical tradition, draws the oath's full symbolic power to the surface: the night gathers all things together by encompassing them in darkness, "just as all things will be brought back to God in the end." This is the sūrah's true point, and it deserves to be stated as the climax of the verse. The nightly gathering of creatures into the fold of darkness is a *rehearsal* of the final gathering of all humanity into the presence of God — the day the Qur'an describes in the words the commentators join to this verse: "the Day We will gather the righteous to the Compassionate as a delegation, and drive the guilty to Hell as a thirsty herd" (19:85–86); "Say: 'Indeed, the first and the last will be gathered for the appointment of a known Day'" (56:49–50). What the night does every evening on the scale of a landscape, God will do once on the scale of history: collect every scattered creature into one fold. The oath swears by the smaller gathering to the certainty of the greater — and the man who watches the night fall and still denies the gathering is, in the sūrah's quiet judgment, refusing to read the sign that is spread over his own head every night of his life.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:18

> **and by the moon when full;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Witness: The Moon at Its Fullness**

*Wa-l-qamari idhā-ttasaq* — "and by the moon when it is full." The verb *ittasaqa* is from the very same root, *w-s-q*, that produced *wasaqa* in the preceding verse — "enshrouds, gathers" — and the commentators are unanimous that the sūrah chose the echo deliberately. The moon is said to *ittasaqa* when it has gathered itself into completeness: when its scattered crescents have accumulated, night after night, into the perfect full disc. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī note the exact force of the shared root: the night *gathers* (*wasaqa*) the creatures, and the moon *gathers itself* (*ittasaqa*) into fullness — the same idea of assembling dispersed parts into a whole, now applied to the most visible object in the night sky. The two verses, sharing one root, make one point: gathering is the law of the universe, written across the darkness in the nightly flocking of creatures and in the monthly filling of the moon.

**The Moon's Phases as a Syllabus in Change**

The moon is summoned as the third witness, and its testimony completes the argument the three oaths have been building. The twilight testified to the transition between states; the night testified to the gathering of the scattered; the full moon now testifies to the *progression through stages* — the slow, certain movement from thin crescent to full disc and back again. The Qur'an elsewhere states the moon's lesson explicitly, in the verse the commentators routinely pair with this oath: "And the moon — We have determined for it phases, until it returns like the old date-stalk. The sun is not to overtake the moon, nor the night to outrun the day; each, in an orbit, is swimming" (36:39–40). The moon never skips a phase, never reverses, never stops: it moves through its appointed stages with a regularity that no human schedule can match, and then *returns* to where it began. The man of verse 14 "thought he would never return" — and the moon, which he has watched return a thousand times, is the standing witness against him: if the moon returns, and the seasons return, and the night returns after every day, then the human being's return is not an impossibility but the same law applied to himself.

**The Full Moon and the Fullness of a Life**

The sūrah chose the moon *at its fullness* rather than the moon at any other phase, and the choice carries a meaning the commentators draw out. The full moon is the moon's *completion* — the moment at which its scattered light has been gathered into a single, complete, unshadowed disc. The image is the sūrah's emblem of the completed human being and the completed account. A human life, like the moon, passes through phases — infancy, youth, maturity, age — and like the moon, it is moving toward a fullness: the moment when all its scattered days have been gathered into the single completed record that verse 7 and verse 10 describe being placed in the hand. Ibn Kathīr notes the parallel the sūrah is quietly drawing: just as the moon "gathers itself" (*ittasaqa*) into the full disc, so the book of deeds "gathers" a life into a single record, and the reckoning of the Last Day is the fullness of every life — the point at which what was dispersed across years is assembled and seen whole. The full moon, bright and complete in the dark, is the visible image of that final completeness.

**The Root *W-S-Q* as the Sūrah's Hidden Thread**

The shared root of verses 17 and 18 is worth pausing over, because it is the kind of detail in which the sūrah's unity of argument lives. *Wasaqa* and *ittasaqa* both come from *w-s-q*, the root of gathering, loading, and completing — and the two oaths, back to back, drive the root home twice: the night *gathers*, the moon *gathers itself*. The sūrah is not merely swearing by two pretty objects; it is swearing, twice, by the *act of gathering*, in the two different forms the universe displays it — the gathering of many creatures into one fold, and the gathering of one object's scattered light into one whole. The third oath thus does not add a new theme so much as perfect the second, and together the two verses form the sūrah's most sustained argument for the Resurrection: what the night does to the living each evening, and what the moon does to itself each month, God will do to humanity once, at the end of time — gather every scattered soul into the single fold of the final assembly. The denier's "thought" of no return is answered not with a counter-assertion but with a triple oath sworn on the very processes of gathering that the denier cannot deny because he watches them every day.

**The Witnesses and the Day**

Finally, the three oaths of verses 16–18 must be read as one composite scene, for they were chosen to fit together into a single picture of a *threshold day*. The twilight is the day's end; the night is the world's gathering; the full moon is the completed light. Taken together they describe the very texture of the evening hour — the red western sky fading, the darkness gathering the world, the full moon rising to its completed brightness — and the sūrah swears by this whole evening scene as a single witness. The point is that the scene is *ordinary* and *universal*: every human being, in every age, has stood at this threshold and watched these three signs perform their gathering. The oath's power lies in that ordinariness — the evidence for the Resurrection has been playing out nightly over every human head since the first moon rose, and the man of verse 14 has seen it all and still "thought" otherwise. The sūrah will now turn from the witnesses to the declaration they support — the stage-by-stage journey of verse 19 — and the reader, having stood with the oaths under the evening sky, is prepared to hear it.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:19

> **thou wilt surely journey from stage to stage.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conclusion the Oaths Were Sworn To Support**

Three oaths — the twilight, the night, the full moon — now release their burden in a single declaration: *la-tarkabunna ṭabaqan ʿan ṭabaq* — "thou shalt surely journey from stage to stage." This is the *jawāb al-qasam*, the "answer to the oath," the clause for whose sake the oaths were sworn, and the commentators note its emphatic grammar: the *lām* of *la-tarkabunna* and the doubled *nūn* of emphasis bind the statement with the strongest affirmative the language can produce. It is not a possibility or a hope but an oath-sworn certainty. The verb *tarkabunna* (from *rakiba*, "to mount, to ride") is the verb of the traveler mounting his mount — the image is of a rider who boards one stage of the journey, then another, then another, never allowed to remain where he is. And *ṭabaqan ʿan ṭabaq* — "stage upon stage, layer upon layer" — is the Qur'an's image for the ladder of states a human being is carried through, each stage leading inexorably to the next. The man of verse 14, who "thought he would never return," is answered here with the sūrah's central certainty: he will not merely return — he will *ride* through stage after stage, in a journey whose every step was sworn by the twilight, the night, and the moon.

**The Many Readings of the Stages**

The commentators record a remarkable range of interpretations of *ṭabaqan ʿan ṭabaq*, and the range itself is part of the verse's depth, for the sūrah has chosen a phrase broad enough to hold every level of the human journey at once. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī transmit the readings as a gathered harvest. The first reading is the *stages of this worldly life*: the human being passes from a drop to a clot to a lump to a formed body, then from infancy to childhood to youth to maturity to old age — "weaned after he was breast-fed, and an old man after he was a young man," in the vivid gloss the source preserves from the early exegetes. The second reading is the *alternation of fortune*: the human being rides "ease after difficulty, difficulty after ease, wealth after poverty, poverty after wealth, health after sickness, and sickness after health" — the inescapable succession of conditions that no life escapes. The third reading is the *states of existence*: from non-existence to existence, from life to death, from death to the resurrection, and from the resurrection to the reckoning and the final abode — the complete arc from before the beginning to after the end. The fourth, recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās, is the *posthumous* journey specifically: the stages after death, through the grave, the resurrection, the gathering, the standing, the account, and the crossing. All four readings are true at once, and the verse, by its deliberate breadth, contains them all: a human being is a traveler through stages at every level of his existence, and the oaths are sworn to the totality of the journey.

**The Stages of the Womb: The Prophet's ﷺ Confirmation**

The first of these readings — the stages of creation — receives an extraordinary confirmation in the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ, and the commentators join the ḥadīth to this verse as its commentary. ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd narrated that the Messenger of God ﷺ, the truthful and the truth-telling, said:

> *"Verily, the creation of each one of you is brought together in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop; then he is a clinging clot for the like of that; then he is a lump of flesh for the like of that. Then the angel is sent to him and breathes the spirit into him, and is commanded with four words: to write his provision, his life-span, his deeds, and whether he will be wretched or blessed."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643)

The ḥadīth is a stage-by-stage map of the very first "journey from stage to stage" any human being undertakes — from drop to clot to lump to ensouled being — and it ends with the recording of the four words that will follow the traveler through every later stage of the sūrah: provision, life-span, deeds, and final end. The verse thus reaches backward to the womb and forward to the book of verses 7 and 10, and the journey it swears by is the single continuous road that begins before birth and does not end at death.

**"That Concerns Your Prophet": The Ascension Reading**

The source commentary records a further, luminous reading: that the verse is addressed in the second-person singular to the Prophet ﷺ himself, and that the "stages" are the degrees of his own ascent — the moving from degree to degree of nearness to God, culminating in the Night Ascension (the *Miʿrāj*, cf. 17:1). Ibn ʿAbbās, asked about this verse, is reported to have said simply, "That concerns your Prophet." The reading is not in conflict with the others; it is their summit. The Prophet ﷺ is the human being who rode the stages to their highest point, and the verse's second-person address is deliberately open, so that it can carry both the general meaning — every human being rides the stages — and the particular meaning — the Prophet ﷺ rode them to their summit on the night he was carried from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque and raised through the heavens. The journey of verse 19 is thus, in its fullest range, the road that every soul travels and that the Prophet ﷺ, the guide of souls, traveled first and farthest.

**The Logical Force of the Verse**

The verse's argumentative force deserves to be stated in its logical form, because it is the climax of the sūrah's reasoning and the commentators draw it out with care. The denier of verse 14 said, in effect, "there is no return." The oaths of verses 16–18 presented three witnessed facts: the twilight passes, the night gathers, the moon completes itself and returns. The verse then draws the conclusion the oaths were sworn to secure: if the whole universe is a system of stages — if nothing stands still, if every state passes into another, if even the moon returns after waning — then the human being's claim to be the one exception, the one creature whose journey stops forever at death, is an anomaly with no support in anything he can see. The verse's logic is the logic of induction from the universal to the particular: *everything* rides from stage to stage; therefore *you* will ride from stage to stage. The man who watches the moon complete its phases and return, and still thinks he himself will not return, is denying for himself the one law he concedes for the moon — and the sūrah swears the oaths precisely to make that inconsistency inescapable.

**The Warning and the Hope in One Road**

Finally, the verse is the sūrah's hinge between warning and hope, and it must be read with both faces. As warning, it tells the heedless man of verse 13 that his journey is not over — that the joy he spent among his people is a stage, not a terminus, and that stages lie ahead of him that he has refused to prepare for. As hope, it tells the believer that his own difficult stage — the toil of verse 6, the prison of the world — is equally not a terminus, that the road continues upward, and that the "easy reckoning" and the "joyful return" of verses 8–9 are stages further along the very road he is now walking. The Prophet ﷺ taught his community to see every hardship as a stage with an end, and to ride the stages with patience: "Know that victory comes with patience, relief with distress, and ease with hardship" (recorded by Aḥmad and al-Tirmidhī from Ibn ʿAbbās). The verse's single most practical gift is this reorientation of perspective: whatever stage you are on — grief, illness, poverty, or the long ordinary toil of a faithful life — it is a *stage*, sworn by the heavens to pass into the next, and the journey's direction is set not by the stage but by the rider. The oaths, the witnesses, and the certainty now stand complete; the sūrah turns, in its final movement, to the question the journey makes unavoidable — why, with all this witnessed, do they still not believe?

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:20

> **So what ails them that they believe not,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rhetorical Question of Amazement**

*Fa-mā lahum lā yuʾminūn* — "So what ails them that they believe not?" The verse is a question, and the commentators note that it is the question of *amazement* — the astonished interrogation of a speaker who has presented evidence after evidence and finds his hearers still unmoved. The *fa-* ("so") ties the question to everything that has come before: given the rent sky, the emptied earth, the certain labor and the meeting, the two books, and the triple oath sworn on the twilight, the night, and the moon — *so what ails them?* The construction *mā lahum* (literally "what is with them?") is the Arabic idiom for diagnosing an obstruction: what defect, what illness, what barrier stands between these people and belief? The sūrah does not answer its own question here; it lets it hang, and the silence is deliberate — for the next verses will supply the answer piece by piece, until the final diagnosis is pronounced in verse 22: "Nay, those who disbelieve deny."

**The Logic of the Question: Evidence Is Not the Obstacle**

The question carries a hidden premise the commentators make explicit: the evidence is not the problem. The sūrah has not been arguing to people who lack signs — it has been arguing to people *surrounded* by signs, who watch the twilight and the night and the moon every evening, who carry in their own bodies the stages of creation the verse has just named, and who have been addressed directly by the revelation itself. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both draw the point: the question is asked in amazement precisely because the case for belief is, from the standpoint of evidence, already complete — which means the obstruction to belief must lie *not in the evidence but in the believer*. The question thus performs a diagnosis by exclusion: if the signs are clear, and the message is clear, and the witnesses are universal, then what remains to explain unbelief is something in the heart, not something in the proof. The verse is the sūrah's pivot from argument to *analysis of the arguer* — it stops proving and begins to ask why the proof is refused.

**The Question as a Mirror for Every Age**

The question's second-person force is aimed, historically, at the Quraysh who heard the sūrah and still denied — but the commentators are emphatic that the question is permanent. It is asked of every generation, including the reader's own, and it is asked in the present tense: not "what ailed them" but "what ails them." The verse thus turns the sūrah's whole accumulated argument into a standing question that no age can escape: after all that has been shown — after the universe's stages, the witnessed gatherings, the recorded books — what excuse remains? The question's genius is that it does not demand an answer so much as expose the *absence* of one: there is no good answer, and the hearer knows it, and the knowing is itself the beginning of the turn the sūrah is driving toward. A question that cannot be honestly answered is more persuasive than a hundred assertions, and the sūrah knows it: the verse leaves the denier standing in the witness box with nothing to say.

**The Psychology of the Obstructed Heart**

The tradition supplies the deeper diagnosis that the verse's question gestures toward. The Qur'an's account of unbelief is consistently that the obstruction is *in the will* rather than the intellect — that people do not believe not because they cannot see but because they do not *want* to see, since seeing would obligate a change they are unwilling to make. The Qur'an names the mechanism in verse after verse: "Then when the truth came to them from Us, they said, 'Why was he not given the like of what Moses was given?' Did they not disbelieve in what Moses was given before? ... Say: 'Whatever you are, bring the proof'" (cf. 28:48) — and, most precisely, the verse that answers "what ails them": "And what prevented the people from believing when guidance came to them, except that they said, 'Has God sent a human messenger?'" (17:94). The obstruction, in every case, is not a gap in the evidence but a *claim* the heart is unwilling to release — pride, comfort, the love of the world, the fear of the change belief would demand. The sūrah's question "what ails them" is thus a diagnostic question in the medical sense: it treats unbelief as a *condition* with a *cause*, and the cause is moral, seated in the will.

**From Question to the Next Sign: The Qur'an Itself**

The question also prepares the transition to the next verse, and the commentators note the structure: verse 20 asks why they do not believe, and verse 21 will ask why they do not prostrate when the Qur'an is recited. The two questions are a *ladder of signs*. The sūrah has argued from the signs of the created world — the sky, the earth, the twilight, the night, the moon. Verse 20's question comes at the end of that argument and asks why the cosmic signs have not sufficed. Verse 21 will then say, in effect: if the signs of the world have not moved you, here is a sign of a different order — the Qur'an itself — and what ails you before *that* sign? The sūrah is moving from the outer witness to the inner witness, from the book of nature to the Book of revelation, and verse 20 is the hinge: having exhausted the testimony of the universe, it now turns to the testimony of the recited word. The question that hangs here — "what ails them?" — is about to be pressed against the most intimate of all the signs, the scripture placed on their own tongues and before their own ears.

**The Question Turned on the Reader**

Finally, the verse's practical force is its refusal to let the reader remain a spectator. The question "what ails them" becomes, in the act of reading, the question "what ails *me*?" — and the sūrah's method throughout has been to address each reader in the singular, so that the crowd of "them" dissolves and the single hearer remains. For the believer, the question is an invitation to *check his own obstructions*: the places where evidence has been shown and still the heart hesitates, the sins rationalized, the truths postponed, the "later" that never arrives. For the one who has not believed, the question is the sūrah's gentlest and most disarming pressure — a question rather than a threat, inviting the hearer to search himself for the real obstruction, which is the first step to removing it. The verse thus ends the sūrah's argument not with a verdict but with a *consultation*, and the next verse will deepen the consultation by placing the Qur'an itself before the hearer as the final, unanswerable sign.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:21

> **and that when the Quran is recited unto them, they do not prostrate?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Question: The Sign of the Recited Word**

The question begun in verse 20 completes itself here: *wa idhā quriʾa ʿalayhimu-l-qurʾānu lā yasjudūn* — "and that when the Qur'an is recited unto them, they do not prostrate?" The sūrah has moved from the cosmic signs to the supreme sign, and the commentators mark the progression with care. The twilight, the night, and the moon are witnesses *outside* the hearer; the Qur'an is the witness *addressed to* him — placed directly upon his hearing, in his own language, by a messenger of his own kind. The sūrah's argument, in effect, climbs: if the signs of the world did not move you (v. 20), then the sign of the Qur'an should have — for this sign is not read in the sky but recited into the ear, and it carries within it the very truths the sky merely hints at. The question "what ails them that they do not prostrate" is the sūrah's most pointed rebuke: a word that reduces mountains to humility (cf. 59:21) leaves these hearers standing upright in indifference.

**Why Prostration? The Logic of the Gesture**

The verb *yasjudūn* — "they prostrate" — is chosen with precision, and the commentators unpack its two dimensions. Prostration (*sujūd*) is the bodily gesture of complete humility: the placing of the highest part of the body — the face, the forehead — upon the ground, in acknowledgment of a greatness that lowers the self. The Qur'an's claim is that the *natural* response to hearing God's word is exactly this: the word is so manifestly from a source above the human that the hearer, recognizing it, is pulled downward in humility. The Qur'an states the claim as a universal law of its own effect: "If We had sent down this Qur'an upon a mountain, you would have seen it humbled, split asunder from the fear of God — and these parables We strike for mankind, that they may reflect" (59:21). A mountain would prostrate; the stone would humble itself; and yet these hearers, to whom the word is recited, remain standing. The question's force is the contrast between the response the word *deserves* — prostration — and the response it *receives* — nothing. The verse is thus measuring their unbelief not by their arguments but by their *posture*: the body that does not bow has already told the truth about the heart.

**The Prostration of Recitation: A Lived Practice**

The verse is also, for the Muslim community, a legal and devotional landmark, because it is one of the verses of the Qur'an after which the *prostration of recitation* (*sajdat al-tilāwah*) is performed. The source commentary notes the classical discussion: most scholars count this among the fifteen (or, by other counts, fourteen) places in the Qur'an where a prostration is prescribed upon recitation, while Imam Mālik did not hold it obligatory here, though other Mālikī jurists did. The practice rests on the Prophet's ﷺ own example, and the tradition records it specifically for this sūrah. Abū Salamah ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān narrated:

> *"Abū Hurayrah recited to us 'When the sky is rent asunder' and prostrated in it; and when he finished he told us that the Messenger of God ﷺ prostrated in it."* (Muslim 578)

The ḥadīth is a small window into the living reality behind the verse: the first community did not merely *read* this sūrah — they *prostrated* at this very point, because the verse, read as an instruction and not merely a rebuke, teaches that hearing the Qur'an should culminate in prostration. The question "why do they not prostrate?" is thus also, in the community's practice, a standing summons to the reader: you who recite this verse, do not be of those it rebukes — prostrate. The prostration of recitation is the believer's answer, enacted with his body, to the very question the verse asks.

**The Qur'an as the Final Witness**

The sūrah's placing of the Qur'an at the climax of its argument deserves emphasis, because it completes a progression that runs through the whole chapter. The sūrah began with the *sky* — the largest visible sign; passed to the *earth*, the twilight, the night, and the moon — the daily and nightly signs; and now arrives at the *Qur'an* — the recited sign, the one sign that does not merely point to the truth but *speaks* it. The commentators note the hierarchy: nature shows *that* there is a Creator and *that* there is a return, but the Qur'an *tells* the hearer who the Creator is, what the return is, and what he must do. The refusal to prostrate at the Qur'an is therefore graver than the refusal to read the sky, because it is the refusal of the clearest and most direct of all the signs. This is why the Qur'an elsewhere declares its own proof-character in the strongest terms: "Say: 'If mankind and the jinn gathered to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like of it, even if they were to one another assistants'" (17:88); and "And if you are in doubt concerning what We sent down upon Our servant, then bring a sūrah like it" (2:23). The verse's question — why do they not prostrate? — is asked in the presence of a sign whose like cannot be produced, and the failure to prostrate is the measure of a heart closed not to a riddle but to a demonstration.

**The Two Meanings of Prostration and Their Unity**

The source commentary records that the early exegetes understood *yasjudūn* in two ways — as the prostration of *prayer* and as the prostration of *humility* — and the two are not rivals but one meaning seen from two sides. As prayer, the verse rebukes those who, hearing the Qur'an's summons to worship, do not pray; as humility, it rebukes those who, hearing the word of their Creator, do not humble themselves. The bodily prostration and the inward humility are, in the Qur'an's anthropology, one act: the body's bowing is the outward form of the heart's submission, and the heart's submission is the inward reality of the body's bowing. The hearers of the sūrah are condemned on both counts at once — they neither bow their bodies in prayer nor bend their hearts in humility — and the single word *yasjudūn* holds the whole failure. The believer, by contrast, is defined by the same word in its positive form, in the Qur'an's portrait of those who hear and respond: "Only those believe in Our signs who, when they are reminded of them, fall down in prostration and glorify the praise of their Lord, and are not arrogant" (32:15). The verse is the negative of that photograph, and the sūrah wants the reader to see both.

**The Posture That Divides Humanity**

Finally, the verse's deepest lesson is that prostration is the line that divides the sūrah's two files of humanity, and the sūrah has been building toward this line since verse 6. The believer is the one who *prostrates* — who bows his body and heart to the Lord toward whom he labors. The heedless man of verses 13–14 is the one who *remains standing* — upright in his joy among his people, unbowed in his "thought" of no return. The whole sūrah is, in this light, an argument for a single gesture: the journey of verse 19, sworn by the heavens, has one destination, and the destination is reached on the forehead — in the prostration that acknowledges the Lord of the journey. The verse's question is thus the sūrah's most personal appeal: after the rent sky and the emptied earth, after the oaths and the stages, what remains to a human being but to prostrate? And the failure to do so, the sūrah will now say, is not a matter of insufficient evidence but of the heart's refusal — the denial that verse 22 names.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:22

> **Nay! Those who disbelieve deny.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Diagnosis at Last**

The sūrah has asked its two questions — what ails them that they believe not, and that they do not prostrate? — and now it answers them with a single, blunt sentence: *bal alladhīna kafarū yukadhdhibūn* — "Nay! Those who disbelieve deny." The *bal* ("nay, rather") is the particle of *correction*: it does not merely answer the questions, it *corrects the premise* behind them. The questions implied that something merely "ailed" the hearers — an obstruction, a deficiency, a gap that might be filled. The answer strips away that charitable framing: it is not that they *cannot* believe; it is that they *deny*. The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr) draw out the weight of the distinction. *Kufr* (disbelief) is here paired with *takdhīb* (denial, rejection) — and the pairing is the diagnosis: their unbelief is not the absence of conviction but the active rejection of a truth that has been made known. The verb *yukadhdhibūn* is in the present/future tense, marking it as an ongoing, deliberate, habitual act — they *keep on* denying, as a settled posture, not a passing hesitation.

**Denial as a Choice, Not a Condition**

The verse's central insight is that denial is *volitional*, and the tradition is emphatic on the point. The Qur'an consistently presents the disbeliever's problem not as a lack of evidence — the sūrah has spent twenty verses showing that evidence surrounds him — but as a refusal rooted in the will. The word *kadhdhaba* ("he denied, he called a lie") appears throughout the Qur'an as the characteristic verb of the ruined nations: the people of Noah "denied the messengers" (26:105), ʿĀd "denied" (26:123), Thamūd "denied" (26:141), and the pattern is always the same — the truth came to them *clearly*, and they *called it a lie*. The Qur'an's most precise statement of the mechanism comes in the verse that answers, in effect, the very question of verse 20: "And they denied them — though their souls were convinced of them — out of injustice and arrogance" (27:14). Here the anatomy of denial is laid open: the souls were *convinced*, and the denial was performed *despite* the conviction, driven by injustice (*ẓulm*) and arrogance (*ʿuluww*). The sūrah's "Nay, those who disbelieve deny" is the same anatomy in miniature: the obstacle is not epistemic but moral, and the heart that denies is not ignorant but *rebellious*.

**The Present-Tense Denial and Its Object**

The grammar of *yukadhdhibūn* deserves one more look, because it names the object of the denial by implication. What are they denying? Everything the sūrah has established: the rent sky and the emptied earth (vv. 1–5), the labor and the meeting (v. 6), the book and the reckoning (vv. 7–12), the watchful Lord (v. 15), and the stage-by-stage journey sworn by the oaths (vv. 16–19). The denial is thus not a single proposition rejected but a *whole worldview refused* — and refused, the commentators note, precisely because accepting it would cost the denier the one thing he will not surrender: the freedom to live as he pleases, the joy among his people of verse 13, the unaccountable life of verse 14. The verse is the sūrah's conclusion about the human heart: where the truth collides with the will's desires, the will does not quietly lose — it *denies*, and the denial is the will's last and most vigorous self-defense. This is why the sūrah does not conclude its argument with more proof but with a *diagnosis*: the proof was never the issue.

**The Two Refusals Joined: Belief and Prostration**

The verse also ties together the two questions of verses 20–21 into one refusal. The denial of belief (v. 20's question) and the refusal of prostration (v. 21's question) are not two failures but one, for the sūrah has defined belief and prostration as a single act seen from within and without. The denier refuses both at once: he denies in his heart and remains standing in his body, and the *yukadhdhibūn* of this verse is the single verb that covers the single refusal. The commentators note the sūrah's economy: the whole condition of the disbeliever is compressed into one word — *denial* — and the whole condition of the believer, by contrast, was compressed in the earlier verses into one gesture — *prostration*. The two files of humanity, first divided in verses 7 and 10 by the hand that receives the book, are here divided again by the response to the word: the believer bows, the denier denies. Between bowing and denying there is no middle posture, and the sūrah leaves no room for a spectator's neutrality.

**The Verse's Mercy in Its Severity**

A final point must be made, because the sūrah's severity here is easily misread. The verse is not pronouncing a sentence that closes the door; it is stating a *diagnosis* that opens one. The whole purpose of naming the disease — "they deny" — is to make the remedy possible, for a disease unnamed cannot be treated. The Qur'an's method throughout is to expose the will's refusal so that the will, seeing itself, may turn — and the turn (*tawbah*) is always left open, as the sūrah's own closing verses will confirm by promising "a reward unceasing" to "those who believe and perform righteous deeds" (v. 25). The verse's bluntness is thus the bluntness of a physician, not a judge: it tells the patient the truth about his condition precisely because the condition can change. And the change, the tradition insists, is available at any moment before the meeting of verse 6 — "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God's mercy" (39:53). The denier of verse 22 is not a fixed species; he is a human being at a moment, and the sūrah, having diagnosed the moment, immediately turns to what God knows about what lies beneath it — the hidden contents of the denying heart, which verse 23 now brings into the light.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:23

> **But God knows best what they conceal.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Hidden Depths Beneath the Denial**

*Wa-llāhu aʿlamu bimā yūʿūn* — "And God knows best what they conceal." The denial of verse 22 was outward; this verse turns to what lies beneath it, and the verb *yūʿūn* is the key. It comes from the root *w-ʿ-y*, "to contain, to hold, to gather into a vessel," and it names the *inner hoarding* of the heart — the thoughts, intentions, secrets, and hidden resentments that a person gathers and keeps inside himself, the way a container holds its contents and gives out nothing. Al-Ṭabarī and the source commentary identify what is being concealed: polytheism in all its forms, and denial — the hidden unbelief that the tongue may not state but the heart harbors, the secret conviction, the buried hatred of the truth, the private scheming that never reaches the light. The verse declares that none of it is hidden from God: the container is transparent to its Maker. What a human being conceals from every other human being — and even, in the end, from himself — is fully, permanently, and exhaustively known to the One who formed the heart that conceals it.

**The Root *W-ʿ-Y* and the Image of the Vessel**

The commentators pause over the word's image, because it is one of the Qur'an's most precise metaphors for the inner life. The heart is a *vessel* (*wiʿāʾ* is a container, a receptacle), and a human being spends his life *filling* it — with knowledge or with ignorance, with faith or with its opposite, with love or with malice. The word *yūʿūn* pictures the disbelievers as vessels that have been filled with denial and are now *holding* it, sealed, away from every eye but God's. The same root appears in the Qur'an's praise of the receptive heart — "and that ears that retain (*wāʿiyah*) may retain it" (69:12) — and in the Prophet's ﷺ celebrated prayer for the vessel that holds and transmits: he said of the one who hears his words, retains them, and conveys them, "may God brighten the face of a man who hears my speech, retains it, and delivers it as he heard it" (recorded by al-Tirmidhī and others). The heart, in this vocabulary, is defined by what it *contains* — and this verse declares that the contents are God's to know, whether the vessel gives them out or not.

**The Qur'an's Doctrine of the Open Breast**

The verse is one instance of a doctrine the Qur'an states with relentless clarity: the interior of a human being is an open book to God, however sealed it is to men. The commentators gather the companion verses here: "And whether you conceal what is in your breasts or reveal it, God knows it" (3:29); "And We have already created man, and We know what his soul whispers to him — and We are nearer to him than his jugular vein" (50:16); "He knows the stealthy glance and what the breasts conceal" (40:19); and the verse that most directly matches this one's rebuke: "And they hide their breasts from Him — do they not know that He knows what they conceal and what they declare?" (cf. 11:5). The point is not merely omniscience in the abstract but *intimacy*: the concealment that works so perfectly against other human beings — the polite smile, the guarded word, the carefully managed appearance — has never once worked against God, and the disbelievers' elaborate secrecy is, in the sūrah's dry observation, secrecy from witnesses who never existed. The verse is thus both a comfort and a terror: a comfort to the one whose hidden good is seen, and a terror to the one whose hidden evil he thought was his own.

**The Specific Concealment: What the Deniers Hoard**

The source commentary, following al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī, specifies what the verse's *mā yūʿūn* refers to: the disbelievers conceal polytheism and denial within their breasts. But the specification deserves expansion, for the Qur'an elsewhere catalogues the hidden contents of the denying heart with unsettling precision. They conceal their *enmity* to the Prophet ﷺ while smiling in his presence (cf. 3:119); they conceal their *plots* against the believers (cf. 8:30); they conceal their *schadenfreude* at the believers' setbacks and their private rejoicing at their own immunity (cf. 3:120; 9:50); and above all they conceal the *inward acknowledgment* of the truth that their pride forbids them to state — the very conviction of 27:14, "though their souls were convinced." The verse's point is that this whole hidden economy — the stored resentments, the unspoken recognitions, the private calculations — is itemized before God as plainly as if it were written across the sky. The concealment is real only against other creatures; against the Creator, the vessel's seal does not exist.

**Why the Verse Is Placed Here**

The placement of the verse is a stroke of the sūrah's structure, and the commentators note its function. Verse 22 pronounced the diagnosis — "they deny" — and this verse immediately adds the qualifier that saves the diagnosis from being a human judgment: *God knows best* what they conceal. The sūrah is teaching its readers the limits of their own knowledge even as it declares God's. Human beings can see the outward denial and the refusal to prostrate, but the inward contents — the secret sincerity of a heart that might yet turn, or the secret depth of a malice that has not yet surfaced — belong to God alone. The verse thus performs two tasks at once: it *rebukes* the deniers, who forget that their interior is exposed, and it *restrains* the believers, who might otherwise imagine themselves able to read the interiors of others. The judgment of hearts is God's alone, and the sūrah hands it to Him even as it describes the denial. This is the Qur'an's characteristic balance: the certainty of God's knowledge is asserted, and the certainty of human knowledge is refused, in the same breath.

**The Two Concealments and Their Two Ends**

Finally, the verse completes a contrast the sūrah has been building since its center, and the contrast is between the two kinds of hiddenness — God's and man's. The man of verse 14 concealed his "thought" of no return, and the man of verse 13 concealed his heedlessness under a surface of joy; they hoarded their hiddenness as though it were security. But the sūrah has already shown, in verse 15, that God *sees* — and now it states it with the superlative *aʿlamu*, "knows best." The hidden things of men are not hidden from God, while the hidden things of God — the hour of the meeting, the contents of the book, the timing of the end — are hidden from men. The asymmetry is the whole theology of the sūrah: man conceals and is seen; God conceals and sees. And the believer's wisdom is to align himself with that asymmetry — to live as one who is seen, storing up the good that verse 25 will reward, rather than the denial that this verse exposes. The sūrah now moves to its final movement: the command to the Prophet ﷺ to deliver the news, and the single exception that divides the two endings forever.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:24

> **So give them glad tidings of a painful punishment,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command Wrapped in Irony**

*Fa-bashshirhum bi-ʿadhābin alīm* — "So give them glad tidings of a painful punishment." The verse is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, and its single verb carries the sūrah's whole rhetorical method into its final movement. *Bashshir* — "give glad tidings" — is the verb of the *bushrā*, the good news, the word used throughout the Qur'an for the announcement of salvation, mercy, and Paradise: the angels "gave glad tidings" to Zechariah of John (cf. 3:39), to Mary of Jesus (3:45), and to Abraham of Isaac (11:71); the believers are "given glad tidings" of the Garden (cf. 2:25; 10:2). To use this verb of a *punishment* is a deliberate and devastating irony, and the commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr) all note the device: the good news is announced with the vocabulary of good news, and the collision between the word and its content is the point. The deniers, who so loved to mock the Prophet's ﷺ warnings — turning them into entertainment, as the Makkan sūrahs record — are now to be *congratulated*, in the same breath that their congratulation is named: the news is theirs, and the news is pain.

**The Grammar of the Announcement**

The command form *bashshir* ("give glad tidings") is worth its full grammatical weight. It is an imperative addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, and the *fa-* ("so") ties it to everything that has preceded: *since* the denial is established (v. 22) and the concealment is known (v. 23), *therefore* announce to them their news. The imperative is not a wish or a prediction; it is a *charge* — the Prophet ﷺ is commanded to deliver this message as his message, to say it to their faces, and the irony of the verb is part of the delivery. The commentators also note the sūrah's characteristic economy in the object: *ʿadhābin alīm* — "a painful punishment" — with the adjective *alīm* (painful) built on the intensive pattern, so that the punishment is not merely one that *causes* pain but one that is *full of* pain, pain as its defining quality. The sūrah does not need to describe the punishment; the adjective and the ironic verb together do the work of a paragraph, and the single short sentence lands with the force of a verdict read in a courtroom.

**The Companions of the Irony Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

This verse is not the only place the Qur'an wields the irony of "glad tidings," and the commentators gather the companion passages to show it is a deliberate device rather than an accident of one verse. The same construction appears in the command concerning the hoarders of gold and silver: "give them glad tidings of a painful punishment" (9:34); in the threat to those who deny God's signs: "give them glad tidings of a painful punishment" (3:21); in the warning to those who take their religion in jest: "give them glad tidings of a painful punishment" (45:8); and in the sūrah of the Women: "give the hypocrites the glad tidings that for them is a painful punishment" (4:138). The recurring device teaches the reader something about the Qur'an's tone: it is capable of a scalding irony precisely because it is fundamentally a book of mercy, and the irony is reserved for those who have treated the *genuine* good news with contempt. The Prophet ﷺ, who was sent as "a mercy to the worlds" (21:107) and described himself as a bringer of glad tidings, is here instructed to turn the word itself against those who mocked the word — the most refined form of the warning a text can deliver.

**The Timing: A Warning That Leaves the Door Open**

The sūrah's placement of this command, immediately before its final verse, is a matter of careful structure, and the commentators note the deliberate suspense. The "glad tidings of a painful punishment" of verse 24 is not the last word of the sūrah — verse 25 immediately follows with its single great exception: "save those who believe and perform righteous deeds." The sūrah thus ends not on the punishment but on the exception, and the commentators record the two readings of the exception's connection. On one reading, the exception is *continuous* with the command — "give them glad tidings of a painful punishment, *save* those who believe..." — so that the verse, read whole, holds both the warning and its escape hatch within a single breath, and the door of repentance stands open even inside the announcement of the punishment. On the other reading, recorded by al-Qurṭubī and al-Zamakhsharī, the exception marks the *contrast* between the two files of humanity — the believers, whose news is the unceasing reward, set against the deniers, whose news is the pain. Both readings are true to the sūrah's method, which has never once, from verse 6 to this verse, allowed the warning to be spoken without its companion promise. The "glad tidings of a painful punishment" is thus not a sentence that seals; it is a *warning that warns*, and a warning that warns is, by definition, an act of mercy addressed to those who can still turn.

**The Messenger's Burden and Ours**

Finally, the verse deserves to be read for what it reveals about the messenger who received it. The command to "give glad tidings of a painful punishment" is, humanly speaking, an almost unbearable charge — to walk among one's own people, one's own kin, and announce to their faces the news of their ruin. The Prophet ﷺ bore this burden for twenty-three years, and the sūrahs of Makkah record the cost of it: the taunts, the rejection, the years in which the very people he was sent to save called him a liar and demanded that he hasten the punishment he warned of (cf. 8:32; 22:47). The verse shows that the Prophet's ﷺ gentleness — his famous reluctance to curse even his persecutors, his prayer that his people be guided — was not a softening of the message but a *bearing* of it: he delivered the warning exactly as commanded, and he delivered it with a heart that wept over its recipients. The reader of the verse is thus given a measure of the prophetic office: the man commanded to announce the punishment was the same man who, the tradition records, said of his community with tenderness, "O God, guide my people, for they do not know." The verse's irony is the Qur'an's; the mercy behind the irony is the messenger's — and both point to the single exception that the sūrah will now, at its very end, hold open.

## Sūrah al-Inshiqāq 84:25

> **save those who believe and perform righteous deeds; theirs shall be a reward unceasing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception That Ends the Sūrah**

The sūrah closes with its single great exception: *illā alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti lahum ajrun ghayru mamnūn* — "save those who believe and perform righteous deeds; theirs shall be a reward unceasing." The word *illā* ("save, except") is the hinge on which the whole sūrah turns at its final moment: after the rent sky, the emptied earth, the two books, the two ends, the oaths, and the warning — the door is left standing open, and the exception names those who will pass through it. The commentators record the two readings of the connection, and both are true at once. On one reading, the exception qualifies the command of verse 24: the "glad tidings of a painful punishment" are *not* for the believers, whose news is altogether different. On the other, recorded by al-Qurṭubī and al-Zamakhsharī, the exception distinguishes the fate of the believers from the fate of the deniers — the sūrah's two files, held apart to the very last line. The sūrah thus ends exactly as it began: not with a single verdict over humanity but with the division that has organized it from verse 6 onward, the division between the one who prostrates and the one who denies.

**The Twin Conditions: Faith and Righteous Deeds**

The exception names its conditions in the sūrah's characteristic pairing: *āmanū* (they believed) and *ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* (they performed righteous deeds). The two are inseparable in the Qur'an, which never tires of joining them — the phrase "those who believe and do righteous deeds" recurs as one of the Book's most frequent refrains — and the commentators draw out the logic of the pairing. Belief without deeds is an unfulfilled claim; deeds without belief are an unpaid wage. The *īmān* is the root and the direction; the *ʿamal ṣāliḥ* is the fruit and the proof. The sūrah's own structure has prepared this pairing: verse 6 established that the human being *labors* (*kadḥ*), and the whole question of the sūrah has been what direction the labor takes. The believers of this final verse are the laborers of verse 6 whose toil was *aimed at the Lord* — their belief directed the labor, and their deeds were the labor itself. The word *ṣāliḥāt* ("righteous deeds," literally "deeds that are sound, whole, set aright") carries the root of *ṣ-l-ḥ*, "to be sound and wholesome" — the deeds that are not merely performed but performed *aright*, in the manner and for the sake that makes them whole. The exception is thus not a loophole but a description: the saved are those whose inner belief and outer work are both, together, sound.

**The Reward That Cannot Be Cut Off**

The sūrah's final phrase is *ajrun ghayru mamnūn* — "a reward unceasing" — and the commentators linger over its two words because they carry the sūrah's whole promise of permanence. *Ajr* is the wage, the hire — the same word a laborer receives for his toil, and the sūrah chose it deliberately to echo the *kadḥ* of verse 6: the believer's reward is the *wage of his labor*, paid in full by the Lord toward whom he labored. *Ghayru mamnūn* — "not cut off, not interrupted" — is built on the root *m-n-n*, which in this context means "to cut, to sever," the same root from which comes the cutting of a rope and the severing of a favor. The reward is described as that which is *never severed*: not interrupted by death, not depleted by time, not withdrawn by any change of circumstance — a wage that keeps being paid, an unending stream that no drought can dry. The commentators note the deliberate contrast with the world's rewards, every one of which is *mamnūn* — cut off at death, subject to loss, theft, decay, and the final severing of the grave. Against every wage that runs out, the sūrah sets one wage that never does; against the heedless man's joy, which was *past and spent* (v. 13), it sets the believer's reward, which is *future and unending*.

**The Companion Verses and the Full Picture of the Reward**

The sūrah's final phrase is not unique; it is one chord in a harmony the Qur'an repeats, and the commentators gather the companion verses to complete the picture. The identical phrase appears in Sūrah Fuṣṣilat: "Indeed, those who say, 'Our Lord is God,' and then remain upright — the angels descend upon them... and give them glad tidings of the Garden... for them is a reward unceasing" (41:8, 30–32); and in Sūrah al-Tīn: "save those who believe and do righteous deeds — for them is a reward unceasing" (95:6). To these the tradition adds the verses that describe the reward's content: "Gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein forever" (cf. 2:25; 98:8); "they will have whatever they wish therein, and with Us is more" (50:35); and the verse that the commentators on this passage love most, because it names the reward's crown: "And the end of their call therein will be, 'Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds'" (10:10). The "reward unceasing" is thus not a bare number written in a ledger; it is the Garden, the abiding, the company of verse 9's "people," and the unending praise — a wage whose payment is itself the highest joy, paid forever.

**The Return of the Whole Sūrah in One Phrase**

The final verse is the sūrah's gathering of its own threads, and the reader is meant to feel the threads close. The "believers" of this verse are the ones who *prostrated* (v. 21) instead of denying (v. 22); their "righteous deeds" are the *labor* of verse 6, aimed aright; their "reward" is the *right-hand book* (v. 7), the *easy reckoning* (v. 8), and the *joyful return* (v. 9) made permanent; and the word *ghayru mamnūn* — "unsevered" — answers, at the very last, the severing the sūrah has threatened from its first verse: the rent sky and the emptied earth undo the world, but the believer's reward is the one thing in the sūrah that *cannot* be undone. The heedless man's joy was severed by death; the believer's reward is unsevered by everything. The sūrah that opened with the destruction of the heavens closes with the one permanence that survives the destruction — and the whole argument of the chapter is in that arc: every permanence in the world will be rent, and the only thing worth building is the thing that outlasts the rending.

**The Door Left Open**

Finally, the verse must be read as the sūrah's final act of mercy, because the sūrah has placed the exception at its *end* — the last word of the chapter is not the punishment but the promise. A reader who has followed the sūrah from the rent sky to this final line has passed through the terror and arrived at the open door, and the door is open to *whoever* believes and does righteous deeds — no lineage required, no past held against him, for the exception's conditions are the only conditions, and they are conditions anyone can meet at any moment before the meeting of verse 6. The Qur'an states the same open door in its most famous verse of hope, which the commentators join here: "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God's mercy — indeed, God forgives all sins" (39:53). The sūrah's twenty-five verses are thus, in the end, a single movement from the end of the world to the door of the Garden — and the door, named in the last line, stands open for the reader who will take it. The labor of verse 6 continues; the stages of verse 19 still turn; and the reward unceasing, promised to those who believe and do good, is the wage that no rent sky and no emptied earth can ever touch.
