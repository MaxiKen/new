# Sūrah al-Ṭāriq (Chapter 86) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Short Sūrah Built Like a Legal Argument**

Sūrah al-Ṭāriq is one of the compact Makkan chapters of the last thirtieth of the Qur'an. It has only seventeen verses, and the whole of it can be recited in less than a minute. Yet its architecture is remarkably complete. It opens with an oath (vv. 1–3), states the fact the oath guarantees (v. 4), turns to the human listener with a command to reflect (vv. 5–7), draws a conclusion from that reflection (v. 8), describes the Day toward which that conclusion points (vv. 9–10), swears a second oath (vv. 11–12), states a second guaranteed fact — this time about the Qur'an itself (vv. 13–14), and closes with a statement about the plotting of the opponents and the counsel given to the Prophet ﷺ (vv. 15–17). Oath, claim, evidence, conclusion, warning, oath, claim, verdict, counsel: the sūrah is built like a carefully drafted argument in which no step is missing.

This structure is not accidental. The early Makkan revelations were addressed to an audience that acknowledged God as Creator but denied that He would raise the dead and hold them to account. The sūrah answers that denial by two lines of proof drawn from what the listener already knows — his own bodily origin (vv. 5–8) and the yearly return of rain and vegetation (vv. 11–12) — and then it states the consequence: the Word that announces this is decisive and no jest, and those who scheme against it will be answered by a scheme they cannot escape.

**The Name and Its Root**

The sūrah is named after the word *al-ṭāriq* in its first verse. The root *ṭ-r-q* carries the basic sense of striking or knocking. From it come *ṭarīq*, "a road" (because it is beaten down by feet), *miṭraqah*, "a hammer" (because it strikes), and *ṭāriq*, "one who comes at night" — because in Arab custom a traveler who arrives after dark finds the doors shut and must knock. The Prophet ﷺ himself used the word in this sense. He forbade a man returning from a long journey to *yaṭruqu* his family at night — to come knocking unannounced — so that the wife might have time to prepare and no suspicion be created:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ used to forbid a man from coming to his family at night (an yaṭruqa ahlahu laylan)."* (al-Bukhārī 1801; Muslim 715)

Because stars appear at night and vanish by day, the star is called *al-ṭāriq*, "the night-comer." The sūrah then defines the word itself in verse 3 as "the piercing star," which settles the reference. The title thus points toward the sky, toward the night, and toward something that arrives and pierces — an apt emblem for a revelation that came to a heedless people in the darkness of their ignorance and pierced it with light.

**Where It Falls in the Order of Revelation**

Al-Ṭāriq belongs to the early Makkan period. Scholars of the order of revelation commonly place it after Sūrah al-Balad (90) and before Sūrah al-Qamar (54), though such orderings are approximate and rest on reports of varying strength. Its theme, style, and short rhythmic verses match its sister sūrahs of that era — al-Burūj (85), which immediately precedes it in the muṣḥaf and which also opens with an oath by the sky; al-Aʿlā (87), which follows it; and al-Infiṭār and al-Inshiqāq, which similarly move from cosmic signs to the Resurrection.

**The Prophet's Use of This Sūrah in Prayer**

The Companions preserved the memory of which sūrahs the Prophet ﷺ favored in which prayers. Jābir ibn Samurah reported:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ used to recite in the noon and afternoon prayers 'By the sky and the night-comer' and 'By the sky possessing the constellations' and the like."* (Abū Dāwūd 805; al-Tirmidhī 307, who graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; al-Nasāʾī 979)

Ẓuhr and ʿAṣr are the two daytime prayers recited silently; the Prophet ﷺ chose for them sūrahs of this length and this weight — short enough for a congregation in the heat of the working day, weighty enough to fill the silent recitation with meaning. It is also reported that when the Prophet ﷺ visited the tribe of Thaqīf during the period of the Pilgrimage, he recited this sūrah to them, and one of their men later said he memorized it while still a polytheist and recited it after his acceptance of Islam (Aḥmad 17942, from Khālid ibn Abī Jabal al-ʿAdwānī). The sūrah was therefore a public proclamation from the beginning, not a private meditation.

**Sequence of Themes**

The sūrah moves through five stations:

1. **Oath and claim (vv. 1–4):** By the sky and the piercing star — every soul has a guardian watching over it.
2. **Evidence from human origin (vv. 5–8):** Let man look at what he was created from: a fluid ejected from between the loins and the ribs. The One who made him from that can certainly bring him back.
3. **The Day of return (vv. 9–10):** On that Day the secrets will be tested, and man will have neither power of his own nor a helper.
4. **Oath and second claim (vv. 11–14):** By the sky that returns rain and the earth that splits open for growth — this Qur'an is a decisive Word, not a jest.
5. **The scheming and the counsel (vv. 15–17):** They plot; God plots. So be gentle with the disbelievers, give them a little respite.

Each station will be treated below verse by verse, with every verse given as an independent discourse, in obedience to the method this project requires.

**Why This Sūrah Matters Today**

Three convictions run through it that the modern reader needs as much as the ancient one. First, that a person is *watched* — that no life is unobserved, and therefore no life is meaningless. Second, that the body's humble origin is not a reason for despair but a proof of the power that will restore it. Third, that the Word of God is *decisive* — that in a world of endless opinion and noise there is a speech that settles matters, and that it is not a game. The sūrah addresses the modern anxieties of anonymity, materialism, and relativism with the same brevity and force with which it addressed the Quraysh.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:1

> **By the sky and that which comes by night.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grammar of a Divine Oath**

The sūrah opens with the letter *wāw*, the "wāw of oath" (*wāw al-qasam*), joined to *al-samāʾ*, "the sky," and then to *al-ṭāriq*, "the night-comer." In Arabic, an oath requires two things: the thing sworn by (*al-muqsam bihi*) and the thing sworn to (*al-muqsam ʿalayhi*, also called *jawāb al-qasam*, "the answer of the oath"). Here the thing sworn by is the sky and the night-comer; the answer of the oath comes in verse 4: "Over every soul there is a guardian." Verses 2 and 3 are inserted between them — an explanation of what the night-comer is — so that the listener understands the majesty of the witness before he hears the testimony.

The Qur'an regularly swears by created things: the dawn (89:1), the sun and its brightness (91:1), the fig and the olive (95:1), the star when it sets (53:1), the pen (68:1). The scholars explained the wisdom of this at length. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī both note that when God swears by a created thing, He does so to draw attention to it as a *sign* — a thing worth looking at, whose existence and order testify to its Maker. The oath is thus not merely a rhetorical flourish; it is an invitation to reflection folded into an assertion of certainty.

**Why Human Beings May Not Swear by Creation**

A distinction must be made at the outset, because it touches on creed. God may swear by whatever He wills, for He is the Owner of all things and every created thing is a sign of Him. Human beings, by contrast, are forbidden to swear by anything but God, because swearing by a thing implies exalting it, and none is to be exalted in that way besides God. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever swears by other than God has committed disbelief or associated partners (with Him)."* (al-Tirmidhī 1535; Abū Dāwūd 3251; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Ḥākim and others)

And:

> *"Indeed God forbids you to swear by your fathers. Whoever swears, let him swear by God or keep silent."* (al-Bukhārī 6108; Muslim 1646)

Ibn Taymiyyah explained the asymmetry clearly: the Creator's oath by a creature is an exaltation of the sign that points to Himself; a creature's oath by another creature is an exaltation of the creature in its own right, which is the seed of *shirk*. So when we read "By the sky," we are not being taught to swear by the sky; we are being taught that the sky is worth swearing by *for the One who made it*, and that its message deserves our attention.

**"The Sky": Roof, Sign, and Source**

*Al-samāʾ* in Arabic denotes whatever is above — the root *s-m-w* means height and elevation. The Qur'an speaks of the sky under several images: as a "protected roof" (*saqfan maḥfūẓan*, 21:32), as a "structure" (*bināʾ*, 2:22), as a thing raised and "spread out" (*al-samāʾ banaynāhā bi-aydin wa-innā la-mūsiʿūn*, "We built the heaven with power, and indeed We are expanding it," 51:47), and as a canopy adorned with lamps (67:5). By naming the sky first, the sūrah places the listener beneath something vast and ordered which he did not make and cannot alter. This is a deliberate psychological placement. A person who begins by looking up is already smaller in his own estimation than one who begins by looking at himself.

In this sūrah the sky will be sworn by twice — here as the seat of the night-comer, and in verse 11 as the "sky of return" that sends back the rain. The two oaths bracket the argument: the sky as the place of the star that pierces darkness, and the sky as the source of the rain that brings the dead earth to life. Both point to the theme of the sūrah: light after darkness, life after death, and return.

**"That Which Comes by Night": The Word and Its Image**

*Al-ṭāriq* is the active participle of *ṭaraqa*, "to knock, to strike, to come by night." The classical lexicographers, and al-Ṭabarī after them, explain that the Arabs called anything that appeared at night a *ṭāriq*, because a visitor arriving after dark would have to knock on the closed door. Stars are *ṭawāriq* because they appear at night and disappear by day; the star does not come *to* you, but it comes *upon* the night, and it arrives into your sight as the darkness falls, just as a night visitor arrives at your door.

Al-Ṭabarī writes:

> *"God swore by the sky and by that which appears in it at night — and the word ṭāriq is used for whatever comes to you by night, because it 'knocks.' And the stars are called ṭāriq because they appear at night."* (Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī on 86:1)

The pre-Islamic poets used the word in this sense. Hind bint ʿUtbah is recorded reciting on the day of Uḥud: *naḥnu banātu ṭāriq* — "we are daughters of the star," meaning of a lineage as lofty as the night-star. The image was therefore vivid and familiar to the first hearers.

Some commentators offered a second, more intimate reading. Bayḍāwī notes that since the word means "night-comer," it may allude to *anything* that arrives in the night — and the deepest thing that arrives in the night is revelation itself, which came to the Prophet ﷺ in Ramadan's Night of Power, or the inspiration and insight that visit the heart in the stillness of the night. This reading does not replace the plain sense, which verse 3 fixes as the star, but it enriches it: the night-comer is that which breaks in upon darkness with light, and the Qur'an is precisely such a thing.

**The Two Witnesses Paired**

Why pair the sky with the night-comer rather than with the sun or the moon? The answer lies in the theme to be proven. Verse 4 will say that every soul has a guardian watching over it. The night is the time when people imagine themselves unobserved. The star that appears in the night is a visible reminder that the darkness is not empty: something high is present, something is looking down, something pierces the gloom. The sky and the night-star are the perfect witnesses to a claim about unseen watching, because they themselves are watchers over the night that no darkness can hide.

Al-Qurṭubī draws attention to this correspondence, noting that just as the star pierces the night so that it cannot conceal what is under it from the sky, so the guardian angel pierces the concealment of the human heart so that nothing done in secret is hidden from God.

**The Night as the Testing Ground of Sincerity**

There is a moral logic to the choice of night imagery. Human beings are most truly themselves when they believe no one sees them. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the hypocrite as one who "hides from people but does not hide from God, while He is with them when they plot at night speech He does not approve" (4:108). The night is the theater of secret sin and also of secret devotion — of the burglar and of the worshipper who rises to pray while the household sleeps (51:17–18; 73:6). By opening with the night-comer, the sūrah quietly announces that the night is not a curtain. What a person does in the dark will be treated in the sūrah's ninth verse as "secrets" that are "tested" on the Day of Return.

The Prophet ﷺ described the highest level of religion (*iḥsān*) as living as though under observation:

> *"Iḥsān is that you worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, then indeed He sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8)

Verse 1, by invoking the night-star, prepares the heart for this teaching: the watching is real even when the watched one does not perceive the watcher — just as the star's light reaches the earth whether or not anyone lifts his eyes to it.

**A Practical Reflection**

A person who works in an office where every keystroke is logged behaves differently from one who thinks his activity is private. The sūrah teaches that the entire world is such an office, but with a Watcher who is just, merciful, and knows intentions as well as acts. The difference between the two attitudes is the difference between a life of carefulness and a life of drift. To look at the night sky and to feel the presence of that which comes by night is to begin the movement from drift to carefulness. This is why the Qur'an repeatedly makes the observation of the sky an act of worship: "Indeed in the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the alternation of night and day, are signs for people of understanding — those who remember God standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth" (3:190–191).

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 1 lays down two witnesses — the sky and the star that arrives with night — before a claim that will be stated in verse 4. It teaches that oaths by creation belong to God alone, that the sky is a sign to be looked at rather than merely lived under, and that the night — the time of supposed concealment — is in truth the time when a piercing light is most visible. All of this is preparation for the sūrah's central assertion: no soul is unwatched.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:2

> **And what will apprise thee of that which comes by night?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Formula of Magnification**

*Wa-mā adrāka mā al-ṭāriq* — "And what will make you know what the night-comer is?" This is a fixed Qur'anic formula, occurring some thirteen times, always with the same effect: it pauses the discourse, magnifies the thing just mentioned, and prepares the listener for a definition. The same construction introduces the Night of Power (97:2), the Crushing Fire (104:5), the Striking Calamity (101:3), the Day of Judgment (82:17–18), Sijjīn (83:8), ʿIlliyyūn (83:19), the Steep Ascent (90:12), and Saqar (74:27).

The commentators observed a fine distinction preserved by Ibn ʿAbbās and reported by al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr: wherever the Qur'an says *mā adrāka* ("what will make you know"), God then informs the Prophet ﷺ of the thing; wherever it says *mā yudrīka* ("what would make you know" — e.g., 33:63; 42:17; 80:3), He does not inform him. Here the formula is *mā adrāka*, and the answer follows immediately in verse 3: "It is the piercing star." The formula is therefore a promise of definition, not an expression of ignorance.

**Why God Asks a Question He Will Answer**

The rhetorical question serves several functions at once. Grammatically it is an interrogative; rhetorically it is an exclamation of magnitude — "How great a thing is the night-comer!" Pedagogically it does what every skilled teacher does: it makes the student aware that he does not fully know a thing he thought he knew, so that he is ready to receive knowledge about it.

The Quraysh knew the stars intimately. They navigated by them, told the seasons by them, and named their children after them. The question "What will make you know what the night-comer is?" is therefore addressed to experts and tells them that their expertise is superficial. They knew the star's position; they did not know its meaning. The question opens a gap between familiarity and understanding, and the sūrah will fill that gap with a statement about the human soul.

Al-Rāzī comments that the formula is used only for matters of great weight, and asks why the night-star should be counted among them. His answer is that the star, though a commonplace sight, is a mighty sign: its size, its distance, its precision of movement, and its ability to guide the traveler through utter darkness all bespeak a wisdom and a power that the casual observer overlooks. The question is an instruction to stop overlooking it.

**The Address to the Prophet ﷺ and to Every Reader**

*Adrāka* — "made you know" — is in the second person singular. The immediate addressee is the Prophet ﷺ, and through him every reciter. There is tenderness in this. God does not say "what will make *them* know," as though the disbelievers were the only ones in need of instruction; He addresses His own Messenger, teaching him first so that the teaching reaches others through him. Every reader who recites the sūrah takes up the position of the one being addressed and is invited to admit, "I do not fully know what this is; teach me."

This posture — the admission of not-knowing before God — is the foundation of all learning in Islam. Al-Ghazālī observed that the greatest obstacle to knowledge is the assumption that one already possesses it. The formula *mā adrāka* dismantles that assumption in a single breath. It is, in miniature, what the whole Qur'an does to the human being: it takes a thing he believed he understood — the sky, the rain, his own body, death — and reveals that he never really looked at it.

**Knowledge Comes Only by Being Told**

The verb *adrā* is the causative of *darā*, "to know, to perceive." The causative form means "to make someone know, to inform." The verse thus contains an unstated but important principle of epistemology: there are things the human being cannot know unless he is informed of them. Reason and observation can take a person far — they can tell him that the star exists, how bright it is, when it rises — but they cannot tell him what the star *means* in the design of God, nor that its Maker has set a guardian over every soul. That kind of knowledge comes only by *ikhbār*, being told, and the One who tells is God, through revelation.

The Qur'an makes this point about itself repeatedly: "And thus We have revealed to you a spirit of Our command. You did not know what the Book was, nor faith, but We made it a light by which We guide whom We will of Our servants" (42:52). The Prophet ﷺ did not know until he was made to know; and the sūrah, by asking "what will make you know," is reminding the listener that what follows is not human speculation but divine information.

**The Pause That Creates Reverence**

There is also an experiential dimension. When the reciter reaches this verse, he pauses. The oath of verse 1 has been sworn; the answer has not yet come; and now a question hangs in the air. This pause is a small enactment of reverence. The Qur'an frequently slows its own pace at moments of gravity so that the heart may catch up with the tongue. The Companions understood this rhythm. Ibn Masʿūd said of the recitation of the Qur'an: "Do not scatter it like the scattering of sand, nor rush through it like the rushing of poetry; stop at its wonders and move the hearts by it" (reported by al-Bayhaqī in *Shuʿab al-Īmān*). Verse 2 is one of those built-in stopping places.

**The Relationship to the Coming Claim**

Finally, the magnification of the night-comer is not for the star's sake alone. The sūrah is about to say that every soul has a guardian over it. By first magnifying the witness, God magnifies the testimony. If the thing sworn by is great, the thing sworn to is greater. The Quraysh were being told: you who revere the star, know that the star is only a witness; the matter it testifies to — the watching over your souls and your return to God — is what truly deserves your awe.

**Application**

In everyday terms, verse 2 teaches the believer to hold his knowledge humbly. A physician knows the body, an engineer knows the bridge, an astronomer knows the star — but each of them, if he is wise, knows that he does not know what these things *are* in the fullest sense, nor what they are *for* in the intention of their Creator. The Qur'an's question, "What will make you know?", is an invitation to add to technical competence the deeper knowledge that comes from being taught by the One who made the thing. This does not diminish human knowledge; it completes it.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:3

> **It is the piercing star.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Given: A Star That Pierces**

*Al-najm al-thāqib* — "the piercing star." The question of verse 2 is answered in three words. *Najm* means a star (root *n-j-m*, "to appear, to rise" — the same root gives the word for a plant that rises from the ground, as in 55:6, "and the stars and the trees prostrate," where some read *al-najm* as "the stemless plant"). *Thāqib* is the active participle of *thaqaba*, "to pierce, to bore a hole, to penetrate." A *mithqab* is a drill. The star is called "piercing" because its light seems to bore through the darkness — and, in another shade of the word, because it is brilliant. The Arabs said of a bright star or a blazing fire that it *thaqaba*, meaning that its light was intense and penetrating.

Al-Ṭabarī explains:

> *"Al-thāqib is the luminous one (al-muḍīʾ). The Arabs say of the fire when it blazes brightly, 'It has pierced' (thaqabat), and of the star when it shines intensely, 'It is piercing.'"* (Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī on 86:3)

Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said simply, "al-thāqib means the luminous one (al-muḍīʾ)." Mujāhid said, "the one that blazes (yatawahhaj)." The consensus on the meaning of the adjective is clear: intensity of light that penetrates the darkness.

**Which Star? The Range of Classical Opinion**

The definite article — *the* piercing star — raised the question of whether a specific star is meant. The source commentary notes that most say it is the Pleiades (*al-Thurayyā*), while others mention Saturn (*Zuḥal*), Venus, or a meteor, and a minority say it is any bright star. Each view had its reasons.

The Pleiades were the most-watched cluster in Arabian sky-lore; the Arabs called it simply *al-Najm*, "the Star," without qualification, and the same word in 53:1 ("By the star when it sets") was read by some as the Pleiades. Those who chose Saturn did so because of a linguistic argument: Saturn is the highest of the visible planets in the old cosmology, and its light was said to "pierce" the seven heavens to reach the earth; Ibn Zayd and others transmitted this. Those who said "meteor" (*shihāb*) connected it to 37:10 — "except one who snatches a fragment, and there follows him a piercing flame (*shihāb thāqib*)" — where the very same adjective is used for the fiery missile that drives off the eavesdropping devils.

Al-Ṭabarī, after surveying these, prefers the general reading: God did not specify a star, so the word should be taken in its general sense as "any star that pierces the darkness with its light." His reasoning is a model of exegetical restraint: where God generalizes, the commentator should not narrow without proof. Al-Qurṭubī records the various opinions and inclines to the same view, while noting that the article may simply be the article of genus (*al-jins*) — "the star as a kind."

For the purposes of the sūrah's argument, the identity does not change the message. Whatever star it is, it is a body of overwhelming brilliance and distance whose light reaches the earth by night, and it was chosen as witness because of what it does: it pierces.

**The Meteor Connection and the Guarding of Heaven**

The connection to 37:10 deserves a moment of attention, because the adjective *thāqib* is used in the Qur'an only twice — here and there. In Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt God says:

> *"Indeed We have adorned the nearest heaven with the adornment of the stars, and as a guard against every rebellious devil. They cannot listen to the exalted assembly and are pelted from every side — driven away, and for them is a lasting punishment — except one who snatches a fragment, and there follows him a piercing flame."* (Qur'an 37:6–10)

Here the stars appear in two roles: as adornment and as guard. The heavens are *protected* (*maḥfūẓ*), and the piercing flame is one of the instruments of that protection. This is striking because the very next verse of our sūrah will say that every soul has a *ḥāfiẓ* — a guardian, from the same root *ḥ-f-ẓ* as the "protected" heaven. The piercing star that guards the heaven from unseen intruders is called upon as witness to the guarding of the human soul by unseen guardians. The sky is guarded; the soul is guarded; and the star testifies to both.

**Light That Overcomes Darkness: The Symbolic Reading**

The source commentary notes al-Biqāʿī's observation that the star, which pierces the darkness, can be seen as an allusion to knowledge overcoming ignorance. This reading has deep roots. The Qur'an calls itself light (5:15; 42:52) and calls disbelief darkness (2:257; 24:40). Revelation came to a people described as being "in manifest error" (62:2) — a night of the intellect and the spirit. The Prophet ﷺ was sent as "a shining lamp" (*sirājan munīran*, 33:46). The piercing star, then, is a natural emblem of the revealed Word, which will be described at the end of this same sūrah as "a decisive Word" — one that cuts and separates as the star's light cuts the dark.

The believer, too, is meant to become a kind of piercing light. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"My Companions are like the stars: whichever of them you follow, you will be guided."*

This particular report is weak in its chain (it is discussed by Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr and Ibn Ḥazm, who rejected it), and it should not be attributed to the Prophet ﷺ with certainty; but the image it uses — the righteous as stars — is echoed in authentic sources. In Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The stars are a security for the sky; when the stars go, there comes upon the sky what has been promised. And I am a security for my Companions; when I go, there comes upon my Companions what has been promised. And my Companions are a security for my community; when my Companions go, there comes upon my community what has been promised."* (Muslim 2531)

The star, therefore, is both a physical sign and a moral one: a source of light and orientation in darkness, whose disappearance signals a coming upheaval.

**The Star as Guide and the Sūrah's Argument**

The Qur'an mentions elsewhere that God made the stars "so that you may be guided by them in the darknesses of land and sea" (6:97) and that "by the star they are guided" (16:16). In a world without maps or compasses, the star was the difference between reaching home and dying in the desert. It was the traveler's hope in the night.

This practical function feeds directly into the sūrah's theme. A traveler who trusts the star does not see his destination; he sees only a point of light and follows it in faith that the light is fixed and true. In the same way, the sūrah asks the listener to trust the piercing light of revelation about a destination he cannot yet see — the Day when secrets are tested — and to steer by it. Denial of the Resurrection is like a traveler who, because he cannot see the city, refuses to follow the star.

**A Cosmological Note**

The word "piercing" invites a modern reflection which the classical scholars did not have the means to make but which their principle of reading signs permits. The light of the stars we see at night has traveled for years, centuries, or millennia through emptiness before reaching the eye. Some of the stars whose light pierces our night may no longer exist. The star, then, is an emblem of a message sent long ago that arrives at its destination in perfect fidelity — much like the Word of God preserved on the Guarded Tablet and delivered, verse by verse, to a man in a cave. One need not press the analogy, but it illustrates what the Qur'an means by calling the star a sign: it is a thing that, properly looked at, opens onto meanings far beyond itself.

**The Definiteness of the Answer and the Certainty of the Claim**

Grammatically, verse 3 is a nominal sentence: *[huwa] al-najm al-thāqib*, "[It is] the piercing star." The definiteness of both noun and adjective gives the answer a tone of finality. The question of verse 2 has been answered with no ambiguity. This definiteness carries forward: the thing about to be sworn to — the guardian over every soul — is stated with the same certainty. The rhetoric moves from certain witness to certain testimony.

**Practical Meaning**

For the believer, this verse trains a habit: to see the ordinary as extraordinary. He has seen stars thousands of times; the sūrah tells him he has not yet *seen* one until he sees it as a piercing light set by God as adornment, guard, guide, and witness. The Companion Abū al-Dardāʾ used to say, "Reflection for an hour is better than standing in prayer for a night" — a statement transmitted in the works of Ibn Abī Shaybah and others as the saying of the Companions and early scholars. Verse 3 hands the believer an object for such reflection that is available on any clear night, free of charge, from any corner of the earth.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:4

> **Over every soul there is a guardian.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer of the Oath**

*In kullu nafsin lammā ʿalayhā ḥāfiẓ* — "There is no soul but that over it is a guardian." This is the *jawāb al-qasam*, the statement the oaths of verses 1–3 were sworn to guarantee. Everything before it was preparation; this is the claim.

The Arabic construction is emphatic and was the subject of careful grammatical discussion. In the reading of the majority of the seven reciters (Ibn Kathīr, Nāfiʿ, Abū ʿAmr, al-Kisāʾī), *lammā* is read light — *lamā* — and the sentence means "Indeed every soul, surely over it is a guardian," with *in* being the lightened form of *inna* ("indeed") and the *lām* being the emphatic *lām*. In the reading of ʿĀṣim, Ibn ʿĀmir, and Ḥamzah, *lammā* is read heavy and functions as a particle of exception meaning "except," so that the sentence means "There is no soul except that over it is a guardian" — *in* being negative, as in 86:4 and also in 36:32 and 43:35. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Farrāʾ discuss both readings, and both yield the same meaning: universality. No soul is excluded.

The phrase *kullu nafsin*, "every soul," is comprehensive. It includes the believer and the disbeliever, the king and the slave, the newborn and the dying, the one who remembers God and the one who has forgotten Him. There is no soul so lofty that it is above watching and none so obscure that it escapes it.

**Who Is the Guardian? Two Readings and Their Harmony**

*Ḥāfiẓ* is the active participle of *ḥafiẓa*, "to guard, to preserve, to keep, to memorize." A *ḥāfiẓ* of the Qur'an is one who has preserved it in memory; the "Guarded Tablet" (*al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ*) is preserved from alteration. The word here has been understood in two principal ways, both supported by the Qur'an.

The first reading, transmitted from Qatādah and adopted by al-Ṭabarī, is that the guardian is the angel (or angels) who *record* the deeds of the soul. The Qur'an states this elsewhere with the same root:

> *"And indeed, over you are guardians (ḥāfiẓīn), noble writers, who know what you do."* (Qur'an 82:10–12)

> *"When the two receivers receive, seated on the right and on the left. He utters not a word but that with him is an observer ready."* (Qur'an 50:17–18)

> *"Or do they think that We do not hear their secrets and their private conversations? Yes, and Our messengers are with them, writing."* (Qur'an 43:80)

Ibn Kathīr, commenting on our verse, cites these and writes:

> *"That is, over every soul is a guardian from God who protects it from calamities, as He said: 'For him are successive angels before and behind him who protect him by the command of God' (13:11)."* (Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on 86:4)

This second reading — the guardian as *protector* — is also grounded in the text. The Qur'an says:

> *"And He is the Subduer over His servants, and He sends over you guardians (ḥafaẓah) until, when death comes to one of you, Our messengers take him, and they do not fail."* (Qur'an 6:61)

> *"For him are successive [angels] before and behind him who guard him by the command of God."* (Qur'an 13:11)

The angels, in this reading, keep the human being from harm that God has not decreed for him — from the falling stone, the unseen pit, the whispering of Satan — until his appointed term arrives; when the decree comes, they withdraw and let it pass.

The source commentary suggests the protection here may be specifically against the whispers of Satan and the jinn (114:4–6). This too has support: the Prophet ﷺ said that every person has been assigned a companion from the jinn and a companion from the angels (Muslim 2814), the one inciting to evil and the other to good.

Al-Qurṭubī, and after him many, saw no need to choose. The angel who records is the angel who protects; a single guardianship has two faces. It *preserves the person* from what is not written for him and *preserves the record* of what he does. The word *ḥāfiẓ* encompasses both, and the Qur'an uses the same root for both functions. The most complete reading is therefore the widest: every soul is under a watching that both shelters and witnesses.

**God Himself as the Ultimate Guardian**

Some commentators, including al-Rāzī, add that the ultimate *Ḥāfiẓ* is God Himself, and the angels are merely His instruments. The Qur'an gives God this name: "God is the best Guardian (*khayrun ḥāfiẓan*), and He is the most merciful of the merciful" (12:64); "Indeed my Lord is over all things a Guardian (*ḥafīẓ*)" (11:57). The angels neither protect nor record by their own power; they do so "by the command of God" (13:11). The verse thus opens onto the doctrine of divine knowledge and preservation: nothing escapes God, and the angelic guardianship is one visible arm of a watching that is total.

**The Hadith of the Alternating Angels**

The reality of angelic guardianship was described by the Prophet ﷺ in a report of the highest authenticity:

> *"Angels come to you in succession by night and by day, and they meet together at the time of the Fajr prayer and the ʿAṣr prayer. Then those who have stayed with you ascend, and their Lord asks them — though He knows best about them — 'In what state did you leave My servants?' They reply, 'We left them praying and we came to them while they were praying.'"* (al-Bukhārī 555; Muslim 632)

This report adds texture to our verse. The guardianship is continuous — the shifts change but the watching does not lapse. It is *reported*: what the guardians observe is carried up. And the question God asks — "In what state did you leave My servants?" — reveals the tenderness of the arrangement: the Lord who needs no report asks for one, as a means of honoring the praying servant before the heavenly assembly.

**The Logic of the Argument**

Why does the sūrah swear such a solemn oath to establish this? Because the whole of the sūrah's message about the Resurrection rests on it. If no one is watching and no record is kept, then the idea of a Day when "secrets are tested" (v. 9) is meaningless — one cannot test what was never observed. The doctrine of guardianship is the epistemological foundation of the Judgment: the record exists, so the reckoning can occur. The Quraysh's denial of the Resurrection implicitly assumed that their lives were unrecorded. Verse 4 removes that assumption first, before the sūrah argues for the power to resurrect (v. 8).

There is also a logical fittingness to the fact that the same power which sustains a person's existence would also register it. A being who did not create himself, who does not keep his own heart beating, who does not know what will happen to him in the next hour, is plainly held in existence by another; and it would be strange for that Sustainer to hold him in being without knowing what he does with the being He grants. Ibn Taymiyyah made a related point: the denial of divine knowledge of particulars is a greater absurdity than the denial of resurrection, for it denies to the Creator the very knowledge by which He creates and sustains.

**The Psychology of Being Watched**

Modern behavioral research has repeatedly shown that people behave more honestly when they believe they are observed, even by a picture of eyes on a wall. The Qur'an does not need such studies, but they confirm its insight into human nature. What the sūrah offers, however, is not the anxious surveillance of a camera but the guardianship of a *ḥāfiẓ* — a keeper whose watching is at once protective and accountable. This transforms the psychology entirely.

Consider the difference between a child watched by a security camera and a child watched by a loving parent. The camera creates fear and evasion; the parent's watching creates security and, ideally, a desire to please. The verse's choice of the word *ḥāfiẓ* — guardian, preserver — rather than *raqīb* (watcher) alone, situates the human being in the second relationship. He is watched *for his good*. His deeds are preserved so that not one atom of good he does is lost (99:7), and he himself is preserved from every harm not decreed for him.

The Prophet ﷺ taught the young Ibn ʿAbbās this lesson in a single sentence:

> *"Be mindful of God, and He will guard you (yaḥfaẓka). Be mindful of God, and you will find Him before you."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516, who graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The same root — *ḥ-f-ẓ* — links the human duty and the divine reward: guard God's limits, and God's guardianship over you is intensified.

**Social and Ethical Implications**

A society whose members believe in a *ḥāfiẓ* over every soul is a society in which private conduct and public conduct converge. The gap between what a person does when seen and what he does when unseen — which is the space in which corruption, fraud, and betrayal live — is closed. The Qur'an describes the righteous as those who "fear their Lord in the unseen (*bi-l-ghayb*)" (21:49; 35:18; 36:11; 50:33; 67:12) — that is, when no human eye is upon them. Verse 4 is the doctrinal root of that virtue.

This has consequences that ripple outward: the merchant who does not shortchange when the customer is not looking; the official who does not take the bribe no one would discover; the spouse who is faithful in the absence of the other; the student who does not cheat when the invigilator leaves the room. Each of these is a practical enactment of the belief that "over every soul there is a guardian."

**An Analogy**

Imagine a lone hiker on a mountain at night. He believes himself completely alone. He does not know that a rescue team with night-vision equipment is tracking his every step from a ridge above — ready to intervene if he strays toward a cliff and recording his path for the report they will file. His aloneness is an illusion. Whether he feels comforted or exposed by the knowledge of that team depends entirely on what he is doing on the mountain. The sūrah's opening oath by the night-star is precisely this: the piercing light on the ridge, reminding the hiker that the darkness is not what it seems.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 4 is the heart of the sūrah's first movement. It states, under solemn oath, that no soul is unwatched. The guardian is the angel who records and protects, acting by the command of God, who is Himself the ultimate Guardian. The doctrine supplies the foundation for the Judgment described later in the sūrah, reshapes human psychology from evasion to mindfulness, and binds private and public conduct into one. It is both a warning to the heedless and a consolation to the believer: you are never alone, and nothing you do for God is ever lost.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:5

> **So let man consider that from which he was created.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn from Sky to Self**

*Fa-l-yanẓur al-insānu mimma khuliq* — "So let man look at what he was created from." The particle *fa-* ("so, then") marks a consequence. Having established that every soul is guarded and watched, the sūrah now turns the listener's gaze from the sky to his own body. The logic is: since you are watched and will be brought back, and since you doubt the bringing-back, look at how you were brought *forth* in the first place. The proof of the second creation lies in the first.

This is one of the Qur'an's most characteristic modes of argument. It does not ask the doubter to accept a mystery on authority; it asks him to look at something he cannot deny — his own existence — and to reason from it. The same command appears with different objects elsewhere: "Let man look at his food" (80:24); "Do they not look at the camels, how they were created?" (88:17); "Let them look at the sky above them, how We built it" (50:6). The verb is always *naẓara* — to look, and by extension to consider, to reflect. The Qur'an is a book that constantly commands looking.

**"Man" — the Universal Address**

*Al-insān* is the human being as such — not the Arab, not the disbeliever, but every child of Adam. The word is often linked by lexicographers to *uns* (intimacy, sociability) or to *nasiya* (to forget). Both etymologies suit the verse: the human being is the creature made for intimacy with God who nonetheless forgets his origin. The command to "look" is a remedy for forgetting.

The context, as al-Ṭabarī notes, is the denier of the Resurrection: "Let this human being who denies the raising of the dead look at what he was created from, and he will know that the One who created him from a despised fluid is able to bring him back." But the universal term means that the command is also addressed to the believer, who needs the same reflection for a different reason — not to cure denial but to cultivate humility and gratitude.

**"Let Him Look": The Command to Reflect**

The imperative *fa-l-yanẓur* is in the third person — a command about man rather than to him directly — which gives it the tone of a general decree: this is what the human being *ought* to do. Reflection (*naẓar*, *tafakkur*) is, in Islam, a religious duty and an act of worship, not merely a philosophical pastime. The Qur'an praises those who "reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth" (3:191) and censures those who pass by the signs "turning away from them" (12:105).

The scholars distinguished between seeing and looking. Every person *sees* that he exists; few *look* at how. The command here is to the second. Al-Rāzī explains that the *naẓar* commanded is *naẓar al-istidlāl* — the looking that draws inferences — as opposed to *naẓar al-ʿayn*, the mere looking of the eye. A man may see his own hand every day without once asking what it is made of, how it came to be, and what that implies.

**"That From Which He Was Created": The Object of Reflection**

*Mimma khuliq* — "from what he was created." The verb is passive: *khuliqa*, "he was created." He did not create himself, and no one claims he did. The passive voice quietly disposes of the possibility that the human being is self-originated, and the question then becomes: from what, and by whom? The next verses answer the "from what" — a gushing fluid — and thereby imply the "by whom," since a fluid does not organize itself into a thinking being.

The Qur'an elsewhere gives the full sequence of human origin:

> *"And indeed We created man from an extract of clay. Then We placed him as a drop in a firm lodging. Then We made the drop into a clinging thing, and We made the clinging thing into a lump, and We made the lump into bones, and We clothed the bones with flesh; then We produced him as another creation. So blessed is God, the best of creators."* (Qur'an 23:12–14)

The command to look "at what he was created from" is an invitation to walk back down this sequence: from the adult, to the infant, to the embryo, to the drop, to the food that formed the drop, to the earth from which the food grew. Every step of that descent is a step toward humility, and every step is a witness to the power that reversed it.

**The Alternative Reading: Created For What**

The source commentary notes a second understanding, found in the spiritual commentaries: that the verse can also be read as a call to consider *what man was created for*. The Arabic *mimma* means "from what," but in the wider Qur'anic context of purpose (51:56: "I did not create jinn and mankind except to worship Me"), the reflection on one's origin naturally leads to reflection on one's end. A thing's origin and its purpose are bound together: the one who knows he was made *by* God will ask what he was made *for*.

Maybudī's passage, quoted in the source, deserves to be dwelt on:

> *"God created a face fit for prostration, eyes fit for taking heed, a body fit for service, a heart fit for knowledge, and a secret core fit for love. So remember God's blessing upon you (2:231), for He adorned your tongues with the testimony of faith, your hearts with knowledge and joy, and your bodies with service and worship."*

This is a reading of the body itself as a catalogue of purposes: each faculty is made *for* something, and the "looking" the verse commands is a looking that discovers those purposes and fulfills them. The forehead exists to touch the ground before God; the eyes exist to see signs; the heart exists to know. Reflection on origin thus becomes a summons to vocation.

**The Logic Made Plain**

The argument, stated simply, is this. If someone tells you that a certain builder cannot build a house, and you know that the same builder has already built that exact house once before from nothing but a heap of raw materials, the claim refutes itself. The doubters of Makkah accepted that God created them (the Qur'an records their admission: "If you ask them who created them, they will surely say, 'God,'" 43:87). Yet they denied that He could re-create them. The sūrah exposes the inconsistency by pointing to the first creation as the greater feat: producing a hearing, seeing, reasoning human being from a formless fluid is more remarkable than restoring one whose pattern already exists. The Qur'an makes this explicit elsewhere: "He it is who originates creation and then repeats it, and that is easier for Him" (30:27).

**The Humility Reflection Produces**

There is a second purpose to the command beyond refuting denial: it produces *humility*. The human being is capable of an astonishing arrogance — building towers, conquering peoples, declaring himself independent of God. The Qur'an confronts this with the reminder of his origin: "Does man not see that We created him from a drop, and behold, he is an open adversary?" (36:77). "Was he not a drop of fluid ejected? Then he was a clinging thing, and He created and proportioned him" (75:37–38). The word "consider" in verse 5 is a needle to puncture pride. A man who truly looks at what he was made from cannot strut.

The Prophet ﷺ himself was struck by this. When he passed by a grave or spoke of death, he reminded people of the meanness of their origin and their end. He said:

> *"The son of Adam says, 'My wealth, my wealth!' But do you have of your wealth anything except what you ate and consumed, or wore and wore out, or gave in charity and sent forward?"* (Muslim 2959)

And ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is reported to have said: "Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed." The looking commanded in verse 5 is the beginning of that self-accounting.

**A Modern Application**

Modern embryology allows the reflection commanded here to be carried out in astonishing detail. A person can now *see* — with instruments the Quraysh never dreamed of — the single cell from which he came, the stages of its division and differentiation, the folding of tissue into heart and brain. The scientific description does not replace the Qur'anic command; it enriches it. The more precisely one sees the mechanism, the more forcefully the question presses: who designed this? And who, having designed it once, would be incapable of reassembling it?

At the same time, the verse guards against a modern error: the belief that describing a process explains it away. Knowing *how* the embryo develops is not knowing *why* it develops or *who* ordained that it should. The Qur'an asks man to look not merely at the mechanism but *through* it, to the Maker.

**Practical Steps of "Looking"**

How does one obey this verse in daily life? The scholars of spiritual discipline suggested regular *tafakkur*: sitting quietly and tracing one's existence backward — the food that became one's body, the parents who did not choose one's features, the years in which one could neither speak nor stand, the moment before which one did not exist at all. Such reflection done sincerely produces three fruits: gratitude for the gift of existence, humility before its Giver, and certainty about the return — for the One who made me from nothing is not hindered by remaking me from dust.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 5 pivots the sūrah from cosmic witness to personal evidence. It commands the human being to look — with the eye of inference, not merely of sight — at his own origin, in order to draw from it the certainty of his return, the humility proper to his station, and the recognition of his purpose. It refutes the denier by his own body, and it instructs the believer in the daily worship of reflection.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:6

> **He was created from a gushing fluid,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to "From What?"**

*Khuliqa min māʾin dāfiq* — "He was created from a fluid that gushes." Verse 5 commanded man to look at what he was created from; verse 6 tells him what he will find. The answer is deliberately humbling in its plainness. Not from light, not from fire, not from noble substance — from a fluid.

*Māʾ* is the ordinary word for water or any liquid. The Qur'an uses it for the reproductive fluid in several places: "Did We not create you from a despised fluid (*māʾin mahīn*)?" (77:20); "Then He made his progeny from an extract of a despised fluid" (32:8); "And God created every living creature from water" (24:45). Here the fluid is qualified as *dāfiq*.

**The Word Dāfiq: Gushing, Poured, Ejected**

*Dāfiq* is the active participle of *dafaqa*, "to pour out, to gush, to be discharged with force." Grammatically it is in the active form — "gushing" — though the fluid does not gush by its own act but is caused to gush. The grammarians of Baṣra and Kūfa discussed this. Al-Farrāʾ and after him al-Ṭabarī explain that the Arabs commonly use the active participle in the sense of the passive when the meaning is clear — as in *ʿīshatin rāḍiyah*, "a pleasing life" (69:21), literally "a pleased life," meaning *marḍiyyah*, "one that is pleased with." So *māʾin dāfiq* means *madfūq*, "poured out, ejected." Others, cited by al-Qurṭubī, preferred to keep the active sense and read it as "a fluid that has the attribute of gushing" (*dhū dafq*), like *lābin* ("possessor of milk") and *tāmir* ("possessor of dates").

Whichever grammatical route is taken, the physical picture is the same: the fluid is expelled with force, not seeping but propelled. This is an accurate description of the male emission, and the classical commentators took it in this sense. Several of them, following reports from Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatādah, understood the "fluid" to be the combined fluid of both the man and the woman, since the Qur'an speaks elsewhere of man being created from "a drop of mixed fluid (*nuṭfatin amshāj*)" (76:2), and a hadith in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records the Prophet ﷺ speaking of the fluid of the man and the fluid of the woman (Muslim 315). The singular *māʾ* here can encompass both, as the singular is often used generically.

**Why God Describes the Origin So Frankly**

The Qur'an does not shy from the biological facts of human origin. It names the drop (*nuṭfah*), the clinging thing (*ʿalaqah*), the lump (*muḍghah*), and here the gushing fluid. Modern readers sometimes find this frankness surprising in a sacred text. The classical scholars saw its purpose clearly: it is meant to *humble*. Al-Rāzī writes that God reminds man of the baseness of his beginning so that he may not be arrogant, and of the wonder of his development so that he may not be ungrateful. A being who was recently a fluid no one would wish to touch has no ground for haughtiness toward his Creator or his fellow creatures.

The Qur'an makes this connection explicit:

> *"Does man not see that We created him from a drop — and behold, he is an open adversary! And he presents an argument against Us and forgets his own creation. He says, 'Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?' Say: 'He will give them life who produced them the first time, and He is Knowing of all creation.'"* (Qur'an 36:77–79)

The sequence in this passage is precisely the sequence of our sūrah: the origin from a drop, the arrogance of denial, the forgetting of one's own creation, and the answer from the first creation. Verse 6 of al-Ṭāriq is the "drop" of that argument.

**The Occasion and the Objection**

The Makkan deniers reasoned from the visible: bones rot, bodies decay, therefore resurrection is impossible. The Qur'an reasons from the same visible realm but further back: a fluid becomes a man, therefore the transformation of matter into life is not only possible but has already happened to *you*. The gushing fluid is more remote from a living human being than dust is from a decayed corpse. If the greater gap was crossed, the lesser one cannot be an obstacle.

Ibn Kathīr, commenting on the parallel verse in 75:37, sums up the argument in a sentence: "The One who created this creation from this weak fluid is surely able to bring it back after its death." The very meanness of the origin, far from being an embarrassment, is the strength of the proof.

**A Reflection on the Word "Water"**

That the Qur'an calls the reproductive fluid *māʾ*, "water," has a resonance with its broader teaching that "We made from water every living thing" (21:30). Water is the medium of life throughout creation; the human being is no exception. The choice of the word draws a line from the rain of the sky — which verse 11 will invoke — to the fluid of human origin. Both are water; both are sent and caused to flow by God; both are the means by which dead things are brought to life. The dead earth receives rain and puts forth vegetation; the womb receives the fluid and puts forth a child. The parallel is one of the structural threads of the sūrah, tying its first oath (about the origin of man) to its second (about the return of rain).

**Divine Power in the Ordinary**

There is a subtle theological lesson in the choice of a common and even uncomfortable example. God's power is often imagined to be displayed only in the extraordinary — the splitting of the sea, the fire that did not burn Abraham. The Qur'an insists that it is displayed at least as fully in the ordinary: in the fluid that becomes a person, the seed that becomes a tree, the cloud that becomes rain. The ordinary is, in fact, the greater miracle, because it happens constantly and universally, and because familiarity blinds us to it. Verse 6 takes the most ordinary fact of human existence — that every person came from a discharge of fluid — and holds it up as a proof of the power to raise the dead. If a person can be brought to see the miracle in this, he can see it in anything.

**The Ethics of Remembering One's Origin**

The frank mention of the fluid has an ethical fruit as well as a theological one. A person who reflects on it cannot sustain contempt for others, since all share the same origin. Racism, classism, and every form of pride in birth collapse before the fact that every human being — the noble and the humble, the rich and the poor — began the same way. The Prophet ﷺ said in his Farewell Sermon:

> *"O people, indeed your Lord is one and your father is one. There is no superiority of an Arab over a non-Arab, nor of a non-Arab over an Arab, nor of a white over a black, nor of a black over a white, except by piety."* (Aḥmad 23489; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī and others)

This teaching rests on the shared origin that verse 6 states. If all are from the same fluid and the same dust, the only remaining ground of distinction is what each does with the life given — which is exactly what the guardian of verse 4 records.

**Modern Confirmation and Its Limits**

Modern reproductive biology confirms the description in its essentials: the fluid is ejected with force, and from it — combined with the contribution of the mother — the human being develops. Some contemporary writers have made much of the correspondence between Qur'anic descriptions and embryology. The classical scholars would counsel balance here. The Qur'an is not a biology textbook and does not stake its truth on the details of any scientific model; its purpose in verse 6 is to humble, to prove, and to remind. That its plain statement sits comfortably with what is now known is a mercy, but the verse's authority rests on its being the Word of God, not on scientific endorsement.

**Application**

Practically, the believer can use this verse as a remedy for two spiritual diseases. When he feels pride rising — in his intellect, his wealth, his appearance, his lineage — he recalls that he was a gushing fluid. When he feels doubt about the Hereafter creeping in — the cold voice that says the dead cannot rise — he recalls the same fact: the One who made me from *that* can make me again from *this*. One verse, two medicines.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 6 answers the command of verse 5 with a blunt statement of origin: man came from a fluid expelled with force. The grammatical form was discussed by the scholars but the sense is agreed. The verse humbles the proud, refutes the denier of resurrection by his own body, links the fluid of human origin to the water that gives life to all things, and levels every human hierarchy save that of piety.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:7

> **issuing from between the loins and the pelvic arch.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Completing the Description**

*Yakhruju min bayni al-ṣulbi wa-l-tarāʾib* — "it comes out from between the ṣulb and the tarāʾib." This verse completes the sentence begun in verse 6. The fluid that gushes is now located: it emerges from between two named regions of the body. The verb *yakhruju*, "it comes out, emerges," is in the present tense, describing a continuing reality rather than a one-time event — this is how human beings are, and continue to be, brought forth.

**The Two Terms: Ṣulb and Tarāʾib**

*Al-ṣulb* means, at its root, hardness and firmness (*ṣalābah*). In anatomical usage it denotes the backbone — the spine, and more specifically the lumbar region, the loins. The Qur'an uses it for the male line of descent: "the wives of your sons who are from your loins (*min aṣlābikum*)" (4:23), and "your loins (*ẓuhūr*)" in 7:172, where the Children of Adam are drawn forth from the backs of their fathers. In Arabic usage generally, *ṣulb* refers to the man, and a "son of the ṣulb" is a direct male descendant.

*Al-tarāʾib* is the plural of *tarībah*. Its meaning was the subject of extensive lexical discussion. The most widely held view among the early authorities — Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrimah, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Mujāhid, Qatādah, and al-Ḍaḥḥāk — is that the *tarāʾib* are the bones of the upper chest, the region where a woman wears her necklace. Al-Ṭabarī transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās: "The *tarāʾib* are the place of the necklace." Pre-Islamic poetry supports this: the poet al-Mussayyab ibn ʿAlas described a woman "whose *tarāʾib* gleamed like a polished mirror," clearly referring to the upper chest. Other opinions recorded by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī include: the area between the shoulders and chest; the four ribs on each side of the lower chest; the region between the two breasts; the hands, feet, and eyes (from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr in one report); and, from al-Ḍaḥḥāk, the area between the breasts and the legs. Al-Ṭabarī, after listing them, prefers the majority view — the upper chest, where the necklace rests — because it is the meaning attested in the language of the Arabs.

**Two Ways of Reading the Anatomy**

The source commentary records the two principal interpretations. The first, transmitted by al-Ṭabarī from several authorities, is that *ṣulb* refers to the man and *tarāʾib* to the woman: the fluid comes from between the loins of the man and the chest of the woman, meaning that the child is formed from the contribution of both parents. On this reading, "between" (*bayna*) is used in the sense of "from the pair of" — as when one says "goodness came from between Zayd and ʿAmr," meaning from both of them. This interpretation fits the Qur'anic statement that man is created from "a mixed drop" (76:2), and it harmonizes with the Prophet's ﷺ words:

> *"The fluid of the man is white and the fluid of the woman is yellow. When they meet, and the man's fluid prevails over the woman's, the child is male by God's permission; and when the woman's fluid prevails over the man's, the child is female by God's permission."* (Muslim 315)

The second interpretation, also recorded by al-Ṭabarī and preferred by some, restricts both terms to the male: *ṣulb* is his backbone and *tarāʾib* his ribs or chest, and the verse describes the region of the male body from which the fluid issues. On this reading, the verse is describing the region of origin — the trunk of the body, between the back and the front — from which the emission is drawn.

Both readings were held by respected scholars, and al-Ṭabarī does not insist on one to the exclusion of the other. What matters for the sūrah's purpose is that the fluid is located within the body, comes forth from a specific and enclosed region, and does so by no design of the person himself.

**The Meaning of "Between"**

The preposition *bayna*, "between," has been read in different ways. In the two-parent reading, it means "from the two of." In the single-body reading, it means "from the region lying between" — the fluid is produced in the trunk, which lies between the spine and the chest. There is also a reading, found in al-Rāzī and others, that emphasizes the *concealment* of the origin: the fluid comes from a hidden place, deep between bone and bone, unseen and unreachable. The human being cannot see, control, or fully understand the process by which his own seed is formed. This concealment is itself an argument: what a person does not even control in himself, he certainly did not create.

**Modern Physiology and the Classical Readings**

A modern reader may ask how the description relates to what is now known. The reproductive organs are formed in the embryo in the region of the lower back, near the kidneys, and descend during development; their blood supply and nerve supply originate from the lumbar region of the spine and the aorta, in the region between the backbone and the lower ribs. The emission itself is a process involving the whole trunk — the nervous signals from the spine, the muscles of the pelvis and abdomen. The verse's location of the fluid's origin "between the loins and the ribs/chest" is thus consistent with a general description of the trunk as the seat of the process, and with the two-parent reading as a statement that both mother and father contribute.

The classical scholars would counsel that the verse should not be forced into agreement with any particular anatomical model, ancient or modern. Its purpose is not to teach anatomy but to point to a fact everyone can acknowledge — that the human being emerges from a hidden, enclosed, and humble origin within the body — and to draw from that fact the conclusion of verse 8. Attempts to read detailed physiology into the verse, whether Galenic or modern, exceed its intent. What is sound is that nothing in the verse contradicts what is known, and its general picture is accurate.

**The Theological Weight of the Detail**

Why does the sūrah specify the region at all? It would have been sufficient to say "from a fluid." The specification serves the argument in two ways.

First, it deepens the humility. Not only is the origin a fluid, but a fluid drawn from the inner, hidden regions of the body — from between bones, in darkness. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the development of the child as taking place "in three darknesses" (39:6) — the darkness of the belly, of the womb, and of the membrane. The origin is dark and hidden at every stage. The human being who emerges into light and thinks himself self-made has forgotten the darknesses from which he was drawn.

Second, it emphasizes the involuntary character of the process. No person decides to produce this fluid, forms it, or directs it. It "comes out" — the verb is intransitive, and the human being is not its agent. The Qur'an asks, in a parallel passage: "Have you seen that which you emit? Is it you who create it, or are We the Creator?" (56:58–59). The question answers itself. If the human being does not create even the fluid that comes out of him, still less did he create himself from it.

**Guarding Modesty in Sacred Speech**

A note on tone is appropriate. The Qur'an speaks of these matters with complete frankness and complete dignity. It does not use crude words, nor does it euphemize to the point of obscurity. The terms *ṣulb* and *tarāʾib* are anatomical, precise, and free of vulgarity. This is a model for how believers should discuss the body and reproduction: without shame at the facts, because God created them; and without coarseness, because they are matters of the sacred process by which life is given. The Companions asked the Prophet ﷺ about the most intimate matters, and he answered them plainly, saying "God is not shy of the truth" (al-Bukhārī 130; Muslim 313).

**The Verse as Part of the Proof**

Read together, verses 5–7 build one sentence of proof: *Let man look at what he was created from — he was created from a gushing fluid — that comes out from between the loins and the chest.* Each phrase lowers the human being another step: from a person, to a fluid, to a fluid from a hidden region. And at the bottom of the descent, verse 8 will spring the argument: "Indeed He is able to bring him back." The lower the origin, the higher the power that raised a human being from it, and the more certain the power to raise him again.

**Practical Meaning**

For the believer, this verse is a reminder to hold the body in humility and gratitude at once. Humility, because its origin is hidden and humble; gratitude, because from that origin God fashioned the eyes that read this verse and the mind that understands it. Reflecting on it should soften the heart toward parents, through whom — by God's decree — one came into being, and toward all human beings, who share the same hidden origin. And it should quiet the whisper that says the dead cannot rise, by pointing to the greater wonder that has already occurred in every living person.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 7 completes the description of human origin by locating the gushing fluid between the loins and the chest — read either as the man and the woman, or as the trunk of the male body. The terms were carefully discussed by the classical lexicographers; both readings serve the sūrah's aim. The detail deepens the humility of the origin, stresses that the process is involuntary and hidden, models dignified speech about the body, and sets up the conclusion of the next verse.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:8

> **Truly He is able to bring him back,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conclusion the Evidence Was Gathered For**

*Innahu ʿalā rajʿihi la-qādir* — "Indeed, He is surely able to return him." Everything from verse 5 onward has been building toward this sentence. The human being was told to look at his origin; he was shown the fluid and its hidden source; and now the inference is drawn: the One who did that can do this. The sentence is emphasized in three ways: by *inna* ("indeed"), by the emphatic *lām* on *la-qādir* ("surely able"), and by placing the object before the predicate — *ʿalā rajʿihi* comes before *la-qādir*, "over his return, He is surely able" — which in Arabic gives the object prominence. God is not merely able in general; He is able *over this very thing*: the bringing-back.

**The Word Rajʿ and Its Family**

*Rajʿ* is the verbal noun of *rajaʿa*, "to return, to come back." It occurs in this sūrah twice: here, and in verse 11, where the sky is called *dhāt al-rajʿ*, "possessing return" — the sky that returns rain. The repetition is deliberate. The return of man from the grave and the return of rain from the sky are described by the same word, and the second is offered as evidence for the first. Elsewhere the Qur'an uses this root for the Resurrection frequently: "To Him is your return (*marjiʿukum*), all of you" (10:4); "Indeed to your Lord is the return (*al-rujʿā*)" (96:8); "Indeed to God we belong and indeed to Him we are returning (*rājiʿūn*)" (2:156).

The pronoun in *rajʿihi*, "his return," refers to man (*al-insān*) from verse 5. The verse thus says that God is able to return the human being — that is, to bring him back to life after death.

**The Primary Interpretation: Resurrection**

The overwhelming majority of the commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī — understand "bring him back" as the raising of the dead on the Day of Resurrection. Al-Ṭabarī writes:

> *"Indeed God, who created man from a gushing fluid, is surely able to return him after his death and his becoming dust, alive as he was before his death."* (Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī on 86:8)

Ibn Kathīr says:

> *"That is, to return this man who was created from a gushing fluid — He is able to return him to the Hereafter, because the One who is able to originate is able to repeat."* (Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on 86:8)

This reading is confirmed by the following verse, which specifies the time: "on the Day when secrets are tested." The return is to the Judgment.

**Other Readings Recorded by the Scholars**

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī record additional interpretations from the early authorities, which were held as possible meanings of the pronoun and the verb. Mujāhid reported two views: that God is able to return the fluid back into the loins from which it came; or that He is able to return the man into the fluid from which he was made — that is, to reverse the process of creation. ʿIkrimah and al-Ḍaḥḥāk said: to return the man from old age to youth, or from youth to old age. Ibn Zayd said: to return him to life after death. Al-Ṭabarī, weighing these, chooses the last — the Resurrection — as the most fitting, because the following verse mentions the Day when secrets are tested, and because the whole thrust of the passage is the answer to the deniers of the raising of the dead.

The minority readings are not without value. They express the same underlying principle: the One who controls a process forward controls it backward. He who turned a fluid into a man can turn the man back into a fluid, or the old man back into a youth, or the dust back into a body. The particular application the sūrah intends is the last, but the principle is general.

**The Logical Form of the Argument**

The argument of verses 5–8 is what logicians call an argument *a fortiori* — from the stronger case to the weaker. It runs:

1. Creating a human being from a fluid is a greater act than re-creating him from his remains (because in the first case there was no prior pattern, and the material was further from the result).
2. God has demonstrably done the greater act — you yourself are the proof.
3. Therefore God can certainly do the lesser act.

The Qur'an makes this argument in many places, always with the same structure:

> *"And he presents an argument against Us and forgets his own creation. He says, 'Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?' Say: 'He will give them life who produced them the first time, and He is Knowing of all creation.'"* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

> *"O people, if you are in doubt about the Resurrection — then indeed We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging thing, then from a lump, formed and unformed, that We may make clear to you."* (Qur'an 22:5)

> *"Does man think that We will not assemble his bones? Yes — We are able to reconstruct even his fingertips."* (Qur'an 75:3–4)

> *"He it is who originates creation, then repeats it — and that is easier for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27)

> *"Say: 'He will give them life who produced them the first time.'"* (Qur'an 36:79)

The phrase in 30:27, "and that is easier for Him" (*wa-huwa ahwanu ʿalayh*), was discussed by the scholars, since nothing is difficult for God and so nothing is, strictly, "easier." Their answer: the expression speaks according to human reasoning — *for you*, repeating a thing is easier than originating it; so by your own standard, the Resurrection should be more credible to you than your own birth, which you do not doubt.

**Why the Makkans Denied It**

The source commentary notes that many pre-Islamic Arabs accepted God as Creator but denied that He could resurrect the dead. The Qur'an records their words: "When we are dead and have become dust and bones, shall we indeed be resurrected?" (23:82; 37:16; 56:47); "Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?" (36:78); "Shall we be returned to our former state, when we have become decayed bones?" (79:10–11). Their denial was not primarily philosophical; it was the denial of people who did not want to be held to account. The Qur'an diagnoses this: "Rather, man wishes to continue in sin ahead of him. He asks, 'When is the Day of Resurrection?'" (75:5–6). The intellectual objection was a cover for a moral preference.

This is why the sūrah's argument is aimed not merely at the intellect but at the whole person. It shows the inconsistency of the denier's position (he accepts the greater and denies the lesser), and it then names the real issue in verse 9: the Day when *secrets* are tested. The denier does not fear a logical contradiction; he fears the exposure of what he has hidden.

**Divine Power (Qudrah) as an Article of Creed**

The verse ends with *qādir*, "able, powerful." *Al-Qādir* and *al-Qadīr* are among the Names of God, and the affirmation of His comprehensive power (*qudrah*) over all things is a pillar of Islamic belief: "Indeed God is over all things powerful" (*inna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr*) occurs dozens of times in the Qur'an. Belief in the Resurrection is, in the end, a corollary of belief in divine power. To say "God cannot raise the dead" is to say "there is something God cannot do," which is a denial of an attribute of God.

Ibn Taymiyyah observed that the Qur'an's arguments for the Resurrection are of three kinds: from the first creation (as here), from the revival of the dead earth (as in verse 11), and from the creation of the heavens and the earth, which is greater than the creation of man (40:57). All three reduce to the same principle: the demonstrated power of God over the greater proves His power over the lesser. The arguments are not merely rhetorical; they are demonstrations, and the sūrah expects its listener to be convinced by them.

**The Comfort of the Verse for the Believer**

For the denier, the verse is a refutation; for the believer, it is a consolation. The one who has lost a parent, a child, a friend, hears in "He is able to bring him back" a promise, not a threat. The dead are not gone into nothing; they are held by the One who made them, and He will return them. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the believer at the grave says: "Peace be upon you, O people of the abodes, believers and Muslims; and indeed we, if God wills, will join you" (Muslim 249). Death is a separation, not an annihilation, and the return is certain.

**An Analogy**

A skilled architect who designed and built a magnificent building from raw stone, timber, and sand — with no prior model to copy — is later told that the building has collapsed into rubble. Would anyone say, "He cannot rebuild it"? He has the plans; he has the skill; he has already done the harder thing, which was to conceive it and raise it from nothing. Rebuilding from the rubble is, if anything, simpler. The human body's "plans" are with God, who "knows what the earth diminishes of them" (50:4) — that is, He knows every particle the earth has consumed. Nothing is lost, and nothing is beyond His power to reassemble.

**Application**

This verse should be a daily anchor. When the mind wanders toward doubt about death and what follows, the believer returns to what he cannot doubt: he exists, and he was once a drop. The certainty of the first fact transfers, by the sūrah's reasoning, to the certainty of the return. And when he considers his own death, he does so as a return to the One who made him — not as a fall into the void. This is why the Qur'an teaches the believer to say at every loss: "Indeed to God we belong, and indeed to Him we return" (2:156).

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 8 draws the conclusion from the evidence of verses 5–7: the God who created man from a humble fluid is certainly able to bring him back after death. The scholars recorded minority readings (returning the fluid, or returning youth), but the primary meaning — the Resurrection — is fixed by the following verse. The argument is *a fortiori*, from the greater act to the lesser; it addresses both the intellect and the will of the denier; it affirms the creedal doctrine of divine power; and it consoles the believer with the certainty that death is a return, not an end.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:9

> **on the Day when secrets are tested,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fixing the Time of the Return**

*Yawma tublā al-sarāʾir* — "on the Day the secrets are tested." Verse 8 said that God is able to bring man back; verse 9 says when. The word *yawma*, "on the Day," is grammatically attached to *rajʿihi*, "his return," in the previous verse: He is able to return him *on the Day when secrets are tested*. This attachment settles the interpretation of verse 8 in favor of the Resurrection, as al-Ṭabarī noted, because the Day of testing secrets is the Day of Judgment and no other.

**The Word Sarāʾir: What Is Hidden Within**

*Sarāʾir* is the plural of *sarīrah*, from the root *s-r-r*, which means concealment, secrecy, and also — in another branch — the inner joy of the heart (*surūr*). A *sirr* is a secret; a *sarīrah* is that which a person keeps hidden within himself: his beliefs, his intentions, his motives, his private conduct, and his inner state. The word encompasses everything about a person that is not visible on the surface. The Qur'an speaks of God's knowledge of this domain repeatedly: "He knows the secret (*al-sirr*) and what is even more hidden" (20:7); "Whether you conceal what is in your breasts or reveal it, God knows it" (3:29); "Indeed He knows the secret and the private conversation" (9:78).

Al-Zamakhsharī, cited in the source commentary, explains *sarāʾir* as all that is hidden in hearts and minds — beliefs, intentions, and the like — together with all the deeds a person has done in secret. Al-Qurṭubī adds that it includes both good and evil: the concealed act of charity and the concealed act of betrayal, the secret sincerity and the secret hypocrisy.

**The Word Tublā: Tested, Examined, Brought to Light**

*Tublā* is the passive of *balā*, "to test, to try, to examine." The same root gives *balāʾ*, "trial," and *ibtilāʾ*, "testing." In the Qur'an God "tests" people in this life: "We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger" (2:155). But the testing on the Day of Judgment is of a different kind. Here it means: to examine, to bring out, to make manifest, to expose what was hidden so that its true nature is revealed. The commentators gloss *tublā* with *tukhtabar* ("is examined"), *tuẓhar* ("is made apparent"), and *tukshaf* ("is uncovered"). The image is that of an assayer testing metal in fire to reveal whether it is gold or dross.

The secrets are tested in the sense that they are brought out from concealment and their real quality is determined. What a person hid because he was ashamed of it will appear; what a person hid out of humility will also appear. The believer who gave in secret will find his secret charity displayed; the hypocrite who prayed for show will find his hollow inner state displayed.

**The Hadith of Muʿādh: Secrets Are Your Deeds**

The source commentary preserves a report from Muʿādh ibn Jabal, transmitted by al-Ṭabrisī and others. Muʿādh asked the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"What are these secrets for which the servants will be tested in the Hereafter?" He replied: "Your secrets are your deeds — prayer, fasting, alms, ablution, the major ablution, and every obligatory act. All deeds are hidden secrets. For if a person wills, he says, 'I have prayed,' though he has not prayed; and if he wills, he says, 'I have made ablution,' though he has not. And this is the meaning of God's Word: 'on the Day when secrets are tested.'"*

This report is transmitted in the tafsīr literature with a weak chain and should not be cited as an established hadith of the Prophet ﷺ; but its explanation matches the teaching of authentic texts and was accepted by the commentators as sound in meaning. Its insight is important: *every act of worship is, at bottom, a secret*. No one can verify that another person has truly prayed, truly fasted, truly made ablution. These acts can be feigned. A person can stand in the prayer line with his heart absent, or claim a fast he broke in private. The only Witness to the reality of worship is God, and the only Day on which its reality is verified is the Day of testing secrets.

**The Two Kinds of Secret**

The scholars distinguished two categories of *sarāʾir*, and the verse covers both.

The first is the secret of the *heart*: belief and disbelief, sincerity and hypocrisy, love and hatred, intention and motive. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Indeed God does not look at your bodies or your forms, but He looks at your hearts."* (Muslim 2564)

And:

> *"Actions are only by intentions, and every person shall have only what he intended."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907)

On the Day of testing, the intention behind each act will be exposed. The same act — a large donation — done for God's sake and done for reputation will be revealed as two utterly different things.

The second is the secret of *conduct*: deeds done when no one was watching. The Qur'an says the mouths will be sealed and the hands and feet will testify (36:65), and the skins will speak (41:20–21). The Prophet ﷺ warned:

> *"I certainly know of people from my community who will come on the Day of Resurrection with good deeds like the mountains of Tihāmah, white — and God will make them scattered dust." Thawbān said, "O Messenger of God, describe them to us so that we do not become of them unknowingly." He said: "They are your brothers and of your skin; they take of the night as you take — but they are people who, when they are alone with the prohibitions of God, violate them."* (Ibn Mājah 4245; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī)

This hadith is the sharpest commentary on verse 9. Those men had public deeds like mountains, but their *secrets* — what they did alone — destroyed them. The Day of testing is the Day the private life is weighed alongside the public.

**The Link to Verse 4**

Verse 9 closes a circle opened at verse 4. There the sūrah said that every soul has a guardian over it — a recorder and protector. Here it says that what the guardian recorded will be tested. The two verses are the beginning and end of a single process: observation now, examination then. Between them stand the verses about the human origin, which prove that the One who observes can also raise up the observed to be examined. The sūrah is thus a tightly-argued unit: you are watched (v. 4), you will be brought back (v. 8), and what was watched will be tested (v. 9).

**Why Secrets, and Not Deeds?**

The verse could have said "the Day when deeds are examined." Instead it says *secrets*. The choice is precise. Public deeds are already known and require no exposure. What the Day of Judgment uniquely does is bring out what was *hidden*. The Qur'an describes that Day as the one on which "what is in the breasts is obtained" (100:10), "that which is in the graves is scattered forth" (100:9), and "you will be exposed; no secret of yours will be hidden" (69:18). It is called *yawm al-ḥisāb*, the Day of Reckoning, and a reckoning is meaningless if the ledger is incomplete. The ledger is completed by the exposure of secrets.

This has a dual effect on the listener. It terrifies the one whose hidden life is worse than his public one, and it comforts the one whose hidden life is better than his public one. Many righteous people are misjudged in this world — accused, slandered, unseen in their devotion. The Day of testing secrets is their vindication. Many wicked people are honored in this world — praised, followed, admired. The Day of testing secrets is their exposure. The verse is thus an expression of divine justice: the final judgment will be based on the whole truth, not on appearances.

**The Psychology of the Concealed Self**

Every human being carries a hidden self — a store of thoughts, memories, desires, and deeds that he shows to no one. Modern psychology has long recognized the strain this creates: the gap between the presented self and the actual self is a source of anxiety and, in extreme cases, of breakdown. The Qur'an's teaching is that this gap will one day be closed *completely and publicly*, and it invites the believer to close it *now, voluntarily*, so that on that Day there is nothing to fear. The way to close it is twofold: to bring the hidden self into conformity with the shown self through repentance and reform, and to bring the shown self into honesty by refusing to pretend to a virtue one does not have.

The Companions and early Muslims cultivated this. Ibn al-Mubārak said, in a saying preserved in the biographical literature: "Whoever's secret and public are the same is upright (*ʿadl*); whoever's secret is better than his public — that is excellence (*faḍl*); and whoever's public is better than his secret — that is injustice (*jawr*)." The believer aims for the second category and flees from the third.

**A Practical Exercise**

One way to live this verse is to conduct, at the end of each day, a brief "testing of secrets" upon oneself before God tests them: What did I do today that I would not want seen? What did I intend that was not for God? What did I pretend to be that I am not? ʿUmar's counsel — "Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account" — is the application of verse 9 to daily life. The believer who does this regularly finds that the Day of testing loses its terror, because there is nothing left in his store that has not already been brought out, repented of, and reformed.

**The Concealment of Good**

The verse also encourages the *concealment of good deeds*. If secrets will be tested and displayed, then a secret good deed is not lost — it is stored for the most public display of all. The Prophet ﷺ listed among the seven whom God shelters on the Day of no shelter: "a man who gives charity and hides it, so that his left hand does not know what his right hand gives" (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031). Verse 9 gives such a man his reward: his hidden charity is among the secrets that will be brought out on that Day — not to his shame but to his honor.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 9 names the Day of the return: the Day when all that was hidden in hearts and in private conduct is examined and brought to light. The word *sarāʾir* covers beliefs, intentions, and secret deeds; *tublā* means tested and exposed as an assayer tests metal. The verse completes the logic of verse 4 (the guardian records) and verse 8 (God brings back), anchors the doctrine of divine justice in complete knowledge, warns the one whose private life is worse than his public one, consoles the one whose private life is better, and calls the believer to make his secret and his public one — before the Day on which they are made one for him.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:10

> **whereat man has neither strength nor helper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Human Condition on That Day**

*Fa-mā lahu min quwwatin wa-lā nāṣir* — "Then he will have no strength and no helper." The *fa-* connects this to the previous verse: on the Day the secrets are tested, *then* man will find himself with nothing. The pronoun "he" returns to *al-insān*, the human being of verse 5 — the same one who was commanded to look at his origin, who was created from a fluid, and who will be brought back. His story is completed here: from a fluid, to a life, to a return, to a standing in which he has neither power nor helper.

The verse names two things a person might rely on in a crisis and denies both. *Quwwah* is strength — one's own power, ability, resources. *Nāṣir* is a helper — external support, an ally, an advocate. In this world, when a person faces a threat, he either resists it by his own strength or calls on someone else to defend him. On the Day of testing, both routes are closed.

**The Construction and Its Emphasis**

The phrase *mā lahu min quwwatin* uses *min* — the "min of comprehensive negation" (*min al-istighrāqiyyah*) — which turns "he has no strength" into "he has no strength *whatsoever*, not even the smallest amount." The same applies to *nāṣir*: not a single helper of any kind. The negation is total.

The two nouns are indefinite (*quwwatin*, *nāṣirin*), which in a negative context indicates generality: no strength of *any* type — physical, financial, political, intellectual — and no helper of *any* type — friend, relative, patron, intercessor without permission, or false deity.

**Strength: Why One's Own Power Fails**

In this world, human strength is real but borrowed. A person walks, works, and defends himself with a body he did not make, sustained by a heart he does not control. The Qur'an reminds him that even this borrowed strength has a curve: "God is He who created you from weakness, then made after weakness strength, then made after strength weakness and white hair" (30:54). On the Day of Judgment, the borrowing ends. The human being is raised in a state of complete dependence: "The Day when no soul will possess anything for another soul, and the command that Day is God's" (82:19).

Al-Ṭabarī explains the verse:

> *"On that Day this man will have no strength by which he can defend himself from God's punishment, and no helper who can help him against God."* (Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī on 86:10)

The strength that fails is specifically the strength to *resist the consequence of the testing*. When the secrets are exposed and the verdict is given, no one can push back against it, flee from it, or bargain around it. "Where is the escape?" the Qur'an asks on that Day; "No — there is no refuge" (75:10–11).

**Helper: Why Every Ally Fails**

The second denial is of a helper. In Arab society, and in every society, the helper was the ultimate security — the tribe that would avenge you, the patron who would shield you, the ally who would stand with you. Makkan polytheism itself was built on the idea of helpers: the idols were understood as intercessors and protectors who would plead for their devotees before God. The Qur'an dismantles this at every turn: "They worship besides God what neither harms them nor benefits them, and they say, 'These are our intercessors with God'" (10:18). On the Day of testing, the idols will be nowhere, and their worshippers will say: "We had no intercessors, nor any close friend" (26:100–101).

The source commentary cross-references the Qur'an's repeated teaching that God alone is the helper and that none can avail another against Him: "You have besides God no protector and no helper" (2:107; 9:116; 29:22); "no soul will avail another soul at all, nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor will compensation be taken from it, nor will they be helped" (2:48; 2:123). The vocabulary of these passages — *walī*, *naṣīr*, *shafāʿah*, *ʿadl* — covers every form of external rescue, and every one is denied to the one who arrives without faith.

**What About Intercession?**

A necessary clarification: Islam affirms intercession (*shafāʿah*) on the Day of Judgment — the intercession of the Prophet ﷺ, of the angels, of the righteous — but only by God's permission and only for those with whom He is pleased: "Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission?" (2:255); "They cannot intercede except for one with whom He is pleased" (21:28). The verse's denial of a "helper" therefore does not contradict the doctrine of intercession; it denies *independent* help — help that could force or bargain with God, help that a person could rely on as a right. The believer hopes for the Prophet's intercession as a mercy from God, not as a lever against Him. The disbeliever, who rejected God's messenger, has no such hope: "So the intercession of intercessors will not benefit them" (74:48).

Ibn Kathīr, in his commentary on related verses, makes the point clearly: intercession is a gift God grants to whom He wills for whom He wills; it is not a helper the person brings with him.

**Standing Alone: The Hadith of the Individual Reckoning**

The Prophet ﷺ described the solitude of that standing in words that make verse 10 vivid:

> *"There is none of you but that his Lord will speak to him, with no interpreter between them. He will look to his right and see nothing but what he sent forward; he will look to his left and see nothing but what he sent forward; he will look in front of him and see nothing but the Fire before his face. So protect yourselves from the Fire, even with half a date."* (al-Bukhārī 7512; Muslim 1016)

No interpreter, no advocate, no shield. To the right and left, only one's own deeds. This is what "no strength and no helper" looks like: a person standing before his Lord with nothing but what he did.

And on the same theme:

> *"The son of Adam's feet will not move on the Day of Resurrection until he is asked about five things: his life and how he spent it, his youth and how he used it, his wealth — where he earned it and on what he spent it — and what he did with what he knew."* (al-Tirmidhī 2416; graded ḥasan)

**The Logic: Why Total Helplessness Is Just**

Why should the human being be brought to a state of total powerlessness? Is this not harsh? The answer is that it is the *removal of illusions*, not the imposition of a new condition. The human being never had strength of his own or a helper besides God; he only imagined he did. In this world, the imagination is permitted, as a test: will he recognize his dependence or deny it? On the Day of Judgment the imagination is dissolved, and the reality that was always true is made plain. The Qur'an describes that Day as the day when "the matter will appear to them from God which they had not reckoned" (39:47), and "you will see them exposed before it, humbled by disgrace" (42:45).

There is a further justice in it. In this world, the strong and well-connected often escape accountability — the rich man's lawyers, the powerful man's allies, the tyrant's army. Verse 10 declares that on the Day of testing, no such escape exists. Every person stands equal, stripped of strength and allies, judged on the secrets alone. This is the only condition under which perfect justice is possible. The verse is thus not cruelty but the guarantee of fairness: the weak who were oppressed and the strong who oppressed will stand identically, and only the record will speak.

**The Psychology of Dependence**

There is a mercy in the verse for those who receive it rightly. A person who lives believing that his security lies in his own strength and his network of helpers lives in perpetual anxiety, because both are fragile: strength fails with age and illness; helpers fail with changing interests. The Qur'an offers a different foundation: dependence on the One who does not fail. "Whoever relies on God, He is sufficient for him" (65:3). The believer who has internalized verse 10 does not wait until the Day of Judgment to discover that his strength and helpers are nothing; he discovers it now, and transfers his reliance to God, and finds a peace that the self-reliant never know.

The Prophet ﷺ taught the supplication: "O Living, O Self-Sustaining, by Your mercy I seek help; rectify all my affairs and do not leave me to myself for the blink of an eye" (al-Nasāʾī in *ʿAmal al-Yawm wa-l-Laylah*; al-Ḥākim; graded ḥasan). "Do not leave me to myself" is verse 10 turned into prayer: I know I have no strength of my own, so do not leave me to it.

**The One Helper Who Remains**

The verse denies all helpers *except* the One who is doing the testing. This is the sūrah's hidden invitation. If there is no strength and no helper on that Day, then the only sane course is to secure, now, the favor of the Judge Himself — to make Him one's helper before the Day on which He is the only one. The Qur'an says: "God is your Protector, and He is the best of helpers" (3:150), and "Hold fast to God; He is your Protector — the best Protector and the best Helper" (22:78). The one who has God as helper in this world is not among those of whom verse 10 speaks, because the verse describes the *disbeliever's* standing, in which he has cut himself off from the only Helper there is.

**An Analogy**

Consider a man who has spent his life accumulating strength and allies — muscles, money, contacts, favors owed. He is confident that whatever comes, he can handle it or someone will handle it for him. Then he is diagnosed with an illness that no strength can fight and no contact can cure. Suddenly he sees what was always true: his strength was a loan, and his helpers were limited. Some men in this position despair; others turn, at last, to God. The Qur'an's aim in verse 10 is to bring about that turning *before* the illness — before the Day — by describing in advance the condition every human being will reach.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 10 completes the portrait of the human being on the Day of testing: stripped of his own strength and of every helper. The negation is total and general. It reflects the reality that all human strength is borrowed and all human helpers are temporary; it guarantees the justice of the Judgment by placing everyone on the same footing; it does not contradict the doctrine of permitted intercession, which is God's gift and not man's lever; and it invites the listener to make God his Helper now, so that on the Day when there is no other helper, he is not alone.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:11

> **By the resurgent sky,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Oath Begins**

*Wa-l-samāʾi dhāt al-rajʿ* — "By the sky possessing return." The sūrah, having completed its first movement (oath, claim, evidence, conclusion, Day), now begins a second oath. It is again by the sky — the same word, *al-samāʾ*, as in verse 1 — but with a different attribute. In verse 1 the sky was the seat of the piercing star; here it is the sky "of return." The answer of this oath will come in verse 13: "Indeed it is a decisive Word."

The structure of the sūrah is now visible as a diptych: two oaths by the sky, each with an earthly or human partner, each guaranteeing a claim. The first pair (sky and star) guaranteed the guardianship over souls; the second pair (sky and earth) will guarantee the truth of the Qur'an. The sky is the constant witness; what changes is what it testifies to.

**The Word Rajʿ: The Same Word as Resurrection**

The attribute is *dhāt al-rajʿ*, "possessor of return." *Rajʿ* is the very word used in verse 8 for the bringing-back of man: *innahu ʿalā rajʿihi la-qādir*, "He is able over his *return*." The repetition is the hinge of the whole sūrah. The sky *returns* — and so God is able to *return* man. The word does double duty: it names the natural phenomenon and the theological claim, and by naming both with one word it makes the first the proof of the second.

**What Returns? The Interpretations**

The commentators offered several understandings of what the sky "returns," and, as the source commentary notes, the majority — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabrisī, and others — understood it as *rain*.

Ibn ʿAbbās said: *dhāt al-rajʿ* means *dhāt al-maṭar*, "possessor of rain." Qatādah said: "It returns the sustenance of the servants every year; were it not for that, they and their livestock would perish." Al-Ṭabarī, transmitting these, explains that rain is called *rajʿ* because it comes back again and again — the sky sends it, the earth absorbs it, the sun draws it up, and the sky sends it back. The Arabs, he adds, called rain *rajʿ* as an omen of good, hoping it would return.

Ibn Kathīr writes:

> *"Ibn ʿAbbās said: al-rajʿ is rain. And it is said: it is the clouds that carry the rain. Ibn ʿAbbās also said: dhāt al-rajʿ means it rains and then rains again. Qatādah said: it returns the sustenance of the servants every year — were it not for that, they would perish."* (Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on 86:11)

A second interpretation, recorded by al-Ṭabarī, is that the "return" refers to the celestial bodies — the sun, moon, and stars — which set and return, disappear and reappear, in ceaseless cycles. The sky "returns" them every day and every night. A third, mentioned by some, is that the sky returns whatever ascends to it — the angels return with the deeds of the servants; the vapors ascend and return as rain. All three readings share the idea of *cyclical return*, and all serve the argument.

**Rain as the Master Sign of Resurrection**

The choice of rain as the primary meaning is not arbitrary. Rain is the Qur'an's favorite proof of the Resurrection, invoked more often than any other natural phenomenon. The pattern is always the same: dead earth, rain descends, life emerges — "thus is the Resurrection."

> *"And it is God who sends the winds, and they stir the clouds, and We drive them to a dead land and give life thereby to the earth after its death. Thus is the Resurrection."* (Qur'an 35:9)

> *"And you see the earth barren; but when We send down upon it rain, it quivers and swells and grows something of every beautiful kind. That is because God is the Truth, and because He gives life to the dead, and because He is over all things powerful, and because the Hour is coming — no doubt about it — and because God will resurrect those in the graves."* (Qur'an 22:5–7)

> *"And We send down from the sky blessed rain and produce thereby gardens and grain for harvest ... and We give life thereby to a dead land. Thus is the emergence (al-khurūj)."* (Qur'an 50:9–11)

> *"And among His signs is that you see the earth humbled; but when We send down upon it rain, it quivers and grows. Indeed He who gives it life is the Giver of life to the dead. Indeed He is over all things powerful."* (Qur'an 41:39)

The word *al-khurūj*, "the emergence," in 50:11 is the Qur'an's term for the coming-forth of the dead from their graves, and it is used there of plants emerging from the soil. Our sūrah, by swearing by the returning sky and (in the next verse) the splitting earth, is compressing this entire argument into two lines.

**The Two Proofs of the Sūrah Compared**

The sūrah now offers its second proof of the return. The first (vv. 5–8) was drawn from the human body: from a fluid, a man; therefore from dust, a man again. The second (vv. 11–12) is drawn from the earth: from dead soil and returning rain, life; therefore from dead bodies and God's command, life again. The first proof is *internal* — the listener looks at himself. The second is *external* — he looks at the world. Together they leave him no place to stand in his denial: whether he looks inward or outward, he sees the same thing — the bringing of life from what was lifeless.

Al-Rāzī observed that the Qur'an's arguments for the Resurrection are drawn from the things most visible and most frequent, so that no one can claim ignorance. Everyone has a body; everyone has seen rain. The proofs are not esoteric.

**The Sky as Sustainer: Qatādah's Insight**

Qatādah's gloss — "it returns the sustenance of the servants every year; were it not for that, they and their livestock would perish" — opens a dimension beyond the argument for Resurrection. The returning sky is also the *sustaining* sky. Every mouthful a human being eats traces back to rain: the grain, the fruit, the grass the animal ate, the water the fish swam in. The human being is thus wholly dependent on a cycle he did not design and cannot control. The Qur'an asks: "Have you seen the water that you drink? Is it you who sent it down from the clouds, or are We the Sender? If We willed, We could make it bitter; so why are you not grateful?" (56:68–70).

The oath by the returning sky is therefore also a reminder of *dependence*. The being who cannot make it rain and would die without rain is the same being who, in verse 10, will stand with no strength and no helper. He has never had strength of his own; the sky's return is proof of that every year.

**The Regularity of Return as a Sign of a Sustainer**

The word *rajʿ* emphasizes not merely that rain falls but that it *returns* — reliably, seasonally, in a cycle. Regularity is itself a sign. A single event may be attributed to chance; a cycle that repeats for millennia points to a Maintainer. The Qur'an uses this logic repeatedly: the alternation of night and day (3:190), the phases of the moon (36:39), the orbits of sun and moon that "run to an appointed term" (13:2). The returning sky joins this list. Its dependability is a mercy — imagine a world in which rain might never return — and it is a proof of the One who *decreed* its return: "And We sent down from the sky water in measure and settled it in the earth; and indeed We are able to take it away" (23:18).

**The Emotional Register of Rain**

Rain in the Arabian environment was not merely a natural process; it was life and death. A year without rain meant famine. The Prophet ﷺ would go out and pray for rain (*ṣalāt al-istisqāʾ*) in times of drought, and when it came he would expose part of his body to it and say, "It has just come from its Lord" (Muslim 898). He would say when seeing rain: "O God, a beneficial downpour" (al-Bukhārī 1032). The Companions felt the return of rain as a direct act of divine mercy. The oath in verse 11 invokes this whole emotional register: the God who, out of mercy, returns the rain that keeps you alive is the God who, in justice and mercy, will return you.

**The Double Witness of the Sky**

We may now see why the sūrah swears by the sky twice. In verse 1 the sky bears the piercing star — a light in darkness, a witness to the *knowledge* that watches every soul. In verse 11 the sky bears the returning rain — life in deadness, a witness to the *power* that will return every soul. Knowledge and power are the two attributes of God on which the Judgment rests: He must *know* what everyone did (the guardian, the star) and He must be *able* to raise everyone to answer for it (the return, the rain). The sky testifies to both.

**Practical Reflection**

The believer is meant to read the sky. When rain falls, he should see not only water but *rajʿ* — return — and remember his own return. When drought ends, he should see the earth's resurrection and remember his own. The Prophet ﷺ trained his Companions in this seeing; the Qur'an trains every reader. A person who has practiced this will find, as the years pass and the rains return, that the doctrine of Resurrection has moved from an article he affirms to a reality he expects, as naturally as he expects the next rain.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 11 opens the sūrah's second oath: by the sky that returns — rain, according to most; the cycling stars, according to others. The word *rajʿ* deliberately echoes the "return" of man in verse 8, making the sky's return the proof of man's. Rain is the Qur'an's master sign of Resurrection, invoked in dozens of passages; its regularity points to a Sustainer; its necessity points to human dependence; and its arrival was felt by the first community as mercy from God. The sky thus testifies, in its two oaths, to both the knowledge and the power on which the Judgment rests.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:12

> **and by the furrowed earth,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Earth Joins the Oath**

*Wa-l-arḍi dhāt al-ṣadʿ* — "and by the earth possessing the split." The sky of verse 11 is now paired with the earth. Sky and earth are the Qur'an's great pair, mentioned together hundreds of times; here they are sworn by together as the two halves of one process. The sky sends down; the earth opens up. Between them, life appears.

**The Word Ṣadʿ: Splitting, Cracking, Cleaving**

*Ṣadʿ* is the verbal noun of *ṣadaʿa*, "to split, to cleave, to crack open." A *ṣadʿ* in a wall is a crack; a *ṣadʿ* in the earth is a fissure. The same root appears in the famous command to the Prophet ﷺ: *fa-ṣdaʿ bi-mā tuʾmar*, "So proclaim openly what you are commanded" (15:94) — literally, "split with what you are commanded," meaning: declare it so clearly that it cleaves through opposition as a crack cleaves stone. And *ṣudāʿ* is a headache — a splitting of the head.

Applied to the earth, *dhāt al-ṣadʿ* means the earth that *splits open*. Splits for what? The commentators are nearly unanimous: for the plants. Ibn ʿAbbās said: "It splits open for vegetation." Mujāhid, Qatādah, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, and al-Suddī said the same. Al-Ṭabarī summarizes:

> *"The earth that splits open for vegetation, and for the trees and the fruits and the rivers."* (Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī on 86:12)

Ibn Kathīr adds: "That is, it splits for the plants," and cross-references the passage in Sūrah ʿAbasa where the same process is described: "Then We split the earth with a splitting (*shaqaqnā al-arḍa shaqqan*) and caused to grow in it grain, and grapes, and herbage, and olive, and palm, and dense gardens, and fruit, and grass — a provision for you and your livestock" (80:26–32).

A minority of commentators added other kinds of splitting: the earth splits for the springs and rivers that burst from it; the earth splits for the dead who emerge from their graves on the Day of Resurrection ("the Day the earth splits away from them (*tashaqqaqu al-arḍu ʿanhum*) rapidly" — 50:44); and the earth splits under the plough. All of these are true, and all are forms of the earth opening to release what was hidden within it. But the primary meaning, fixed by the pairing with the rain-sending sky, is the splitting for growth.

**The Image: A Seed Breaking the Ground**

Anyone who has watched a seed germinate knows the image. The soil is flat, hard, apparently dead. Then a crack appears, and a pale shoot pushes up, sometimes lifting a clod of earth many times its own weight. The earth *splits* to let it through. This is one of the most ordinary sights in the world and one of the most extraordinary: something that was buried, that looked dead, that was covered over, comes up alive — and the earth, which held it, breaks open to release it.

The Qur'an sees in this the perfect image of the Resurrection, and says so directly: "And you see the earth barren; then when We send down upon it rain, it quivers and swells and grows something of every beautiful kind ... and because God will resurrect those in the graves" (22:5–7). The Prophet ﷺ made the same comparison:

> *"Then God will send down from the sky a rain, and they will grow as vegetation grows. There is nothing of a human being that does not decay except one bone — the tailbone — and from it the creation will be assembled on the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī 4814; Muslim 2955)

The hadith is remarkable: the dead will be raised *by rain*, and they will *grow* like plants. The oath by the returning sky and the splitting earth is thus not merely a metaphor for the Resurrection; according to this hadith, it describes the very means by which God will bring it about.

**Sky and Earth as One Argument**

Verses 11 and 12 must be read together. The sky alone, returning rain, would prove nothing if the earth did not respond; the earth alone, splitting, would show nothing if the sky did not first send down water. It is the *pairing* that constitutes the proof. Dead earth plus returning water equals life. The Qur'an uses this pairing over and over: "He sends down rain from the sky and gives life to the earth after its death" (30:24); "God sends down water from the sky and thereby gives life to the earth after its lifelessness. Indeed in that is a sign for a people who hear" (16:65).

The sūrah's argument is this: the earth you walk on is, by itself, dead. Every spring you watch God bring it to life by sending water from above. You do not doubt this; you depend on it. Now consider the graves in that same earth: God will send His command from above, and the earth will split, and what was buried will come up alive. The process you accept for seeds you deny for bodies — yet it is the same earth, the same Lord, the same splitting.

**The Word Ṣadʿ and the Openness of the Proof**

There may also be a play on the root's other meaning. *Ṣadaʿa* means not only "to split" but "to declare openly" (as in 15:94). The earth that splits is the earth that *declares* — that shows openly, every year, what God can do. The proof is not hidden; it is split open before every eye. The denier of the Resurrection cannot claim that the evidence was concealed from him. It breaks through the ground in front of his house.

**The Earth as Womb and Grave**

There is a deep correspondence between the earth and the human body in this sūrah. Verses 6–7 described the hidden origin of man in the body; verses 11–12 describe the hidden origin of plants in the earth. Both involve water (the fluid; the rain); both involve a dark, enclosed place (between the loins and the ribs; beneath the soil); both involve an emergence (the child; the shoot). The Qur'an makes the earth a womb elsewhere: "From it We created you, and into it We will return you, and from it We will bring you forth another time" (20:55). The earth that splits for the seed is the earth that will split for the dead — and it is the earth from which the human being was first made. The sūrah's two proofs converge: body and earth are one argument, told twice.

**Sign Value and the Duty of Gratitude**

Verse 12, like verse 11, is also a reminder of provision. The earth splits *for you*: "a provision for you and your livestock" (80:32). Every meal is the fruit of an earth that split open. The Qur'an asks: "Have you seen what you sow? Is it you who make it grow, or are We the Grower? If We willed, We could make it debris, and you would remain in wonder" (56:63–65). The oath thus demands two responses from the listener: *certainty* about the Resurrection (the argument) and *gratitude* for sustenance (the gift). A person who eats bread and denies the Resurrection has taken the earth's splitting as provision and refused it as proof.

**Ibn Taymiyyah on the Analogy of Revival**

Ibn Taymiyyah, discussing the Qur'an's proofs of the Resurrection, noted that the revival of the earth is in some ways the most powerful of them, because it involves the *re-creation of living things from apparently dead matter in the same place* — which is exactly what the Resurrection involves. The seed that was dry and inert becomes a living plant; the dead land becomes a garden. The analogy is not loose but exact in its essentials: dead matter, divine command, water, emergence of life. He observed that the disbelievers' objection — "how can bones live after they have decayed?" — is answered every spring in a manner they themselves witness and never dispute.

**A Reflection for Modern Life**

Most people today live cut off from the earth's splitting. Food arrives in packages; soil is something under pavement. The sūrah's oath is an invitation to restore the connection: to plant a seed, to watch the ground crack, to see what the Qur'an means. Many who have done this — who have kept a garden or a single pot on a windowsill — report that the doctrine of the Resurrection became more tangible to them through it. The Qur'an's proofs are meant to be *seen*, not merely read about. A believer who has watched the earth split open has an answer to doubt that no argument can give him: he has seen the dead rise, in miniature, with his own eyes.

**The Answer of the Oath Approaches**

With the sky of return and the earth of splitting, the second oath is complete. The listener has been shown the cycle of death and life that surrounds him every year. Now the sūrah will state what this cycle guarantees: not, this time, a statement about man, but a statement about the Word that has been telling him all this. Verse 13 will say: "Indeed it is a decisive Word." The sky and earth that split open with life bear witness that the Qur'an, which announces the greater splitting-open of the graves, is truth and not jest.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 12 completes the second oath by pairing the returning sky with the splitting earth — the earth that cracks open for vegetation, and, by extension, for springs and for the dead on the Last Day. The root *ṣ-d-ʿ* means both "to split" and "to declare openly," and the earth's splitting is God's open declaration of His power to bring life from death. Sky and earth together form one argument, confirmed by the Prophet's ﷺ description of the dead growing like plants after rain. The verse demands certainty and gratitude, connects the earth's womb-like opening to the human body's hidden origin, and prepares the ground for the sūrah's declaration about its own decisive Word.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:13

> **truly it is indeed a decisive Word.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer of the Second Oath**

*Innahu la-qawlun faṣl* — "Indeed it is a decisive Word." This is the *jawāb* — the answer — of the oath sworn in verses 11–12. By the sky that returns rain and the earth that splits with life, God swears: *it* is a decisive Word. The sentence is emphasized twice, by *inna* and by the emphatic *lām*: not merely "it is a decisive Word" but "indeed, it is surely a decisive Word." God is confirming, under oath and with emphasis, the character of His speech.

**What Does "It" Refer To?**

The pronoun *-hu*, "it," has no explicit antecedent in the immediately preceding verses, and the commentators discussed its reference. The overwhelming majority — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī — say that it refers to *the Qur'an*, which is understood from context: the whole sūrah is Qur'an, and what is being sworn to is the truth of what has just been said about the guardian, the origin, the return, and the testing. Al-Ṭabarī writes:

> *"Indeed this Qur'an is a decisive Word that separates between truth and falsehood, by its clarification and its exposition."* (Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī on 86:13)

The source commentary also notes a second reading: that "it" refers to the *Day of Judgment*, or to God's word about the Day, or to God's word in general — that the promise of the Resurrection is a decisive matter that separates good from evil. This reading draws on the Qur'an's naming of the Day as *yawm al-faṣl*, "the Day of Decision/Separation" (37:21; 44:40; 77:13, 14, 38; 78:17). The two readings are not opposed. The Qur'an is the Word that announces the Day; the Day is the event that fulfills the Word; and both are *faṣl* — decisive, separating.

The preferred reading, following the majority, is that the pronoun refers to the Qur'an, and specifically to the discourse of this very sūrah — the argument for the guardianship and the Resurrection that has just been made. Having argued, God now certifies the argument: what you have heard is not speculation; it is a decisive Word.

**The Word Faṣl: Cutting, Separating, Deciding**

*Faṣl* comes from the root *f-ṣ-l*, whose basic meaning is to separate one thing from another, to cut apart, to make a division. A *fāṣil* is a divider; a *mafṣil* is a joint (where one bone is separated from another); *faṣl* in a book is a chapter (a section separated from others); and *faṣl al-khiṭāb* is "decisive speech" — the ability to give a judgment that settles a dispute, which God granted to David: "And We strengthened his kingdom and gave him wisdom and decisive speech (*faṣl al-khiṭāb*)" (38:20).

Here *faṣl* is used as an adjective, or as a verbal noun standing for an adjective: *qawlun faṣl* means "a word that separates," "a word that decides." The commentators gloss it in several complementary ways:

- **Separating truth from falsehood.** Ibn ʿAbbās said: *faṣl* means *ḥaqq*, "truth." Al-Ṭabarī: it separates between truth and falsehood. Mujāhid: it separates (*yafṣilu*) between right and wrong.
- **Decisive, settled, final.** Al-Qurṭubī: it is a *ḥukm*, a judgment that settles the matter, from which there is no appeal.
- **Serious and definitive, as opposed to jest** — which is what the next verse will make explicit: "and it is not for amusement."

The Prophet ﷺ described the Qur'an with this very word. In the hadith reported by ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, transmitted by al-Tirmidhī and cited in the source commentary, he said of the Qur'an:

> *"In it is the news of what was before you, the judgment of what is among you, and the tidings of what is after you. It is the decisive [Word] (huwa al-faṣl), not jest. Whoever abandons it out of arrogance, God will break him; and whoever seeks guidance in other than it, God will lead him astray. It is the firm rope of God, the wise reminder, and the straight path."* (al-Tirmidhī 2906; its chain is weak, but its meaning is confirmed by the Qur'an itself in this verse and the next)

The phrase *huwa al-faṣl laysa bi-l-hazl* — "it is the decisive, not the jest" — is precisely the content of verses 13–14 of our sūrah.

**Why the Qur'an Is "Decisive"**

What makes a word decisive? Three things, and the Qur'an possesses all of them.

First, *clarity*. A decisive word does not leave the listener uncertain about what is meant. The Qur'an calls itself *mubīn*, "clear" (5:15; 12:1; 26:2), and *tibyānan li-kulli shayʾ*, "a clarification of all things" (16:89). Its central claims — one God, accountability, the Resurrection — are stated with such plainness that no reader can mistake them.

Second, *authority*. A decisive word comes from one who has the right and the power to decide. A judge's verdict is decisive because he has the authority; a bystander's opinion is not. The Qur'an is decisive because it is the speech of God, "whom no one can overturn in His judgment" (13:41). Its statements about the unseen are not guesses but reports from the One who knows.

Third, *finality*. A decisive word ends the matter. After it, there is nothing more to say. The Qur'an is "the seal" of revelation; its Prophet is "the seal of the prophets" (33:40); and its verdict on the questions it addresses — is there a God? will there be a Judgment? — is final: "The Word of your Lord has been fulfilled in truth and justice; none can alter His words" (6:115).

Al-Rāzī adds a fourth feature: a decisive word *separates people*. Once it is spoken, those who accept it and those who reject it are divided into two parties. The Qur'an did exactly this in Makkah: it split the Quraysh into believers and deniers, families into two camps. It is *faṣl* in this social sense too — the Furqān, "the Criterion" (25:1), that separates people according to their response to it.

**Decisive, Not Ambiguous: The Answer to Relativism**

There is a modern application of great importance. The contemporary world is skeptical of decisive words. It prefers to say that all views are perspectives, all truths are relative, and no text can settle anything. The Qur'an, under oath, claims the opposite for itself: it is a Word that *decides*. It does not offer itself as one opinion among many about God and the Hereafter; it offers itself as the verdict.

This is not arrogance but honesty about its nature. If God has spoken, then His speech is by definition decisive, because He knows what is true and has the authority to declare it. A revelation that presented itself as merely "one perspective" would be denying its own source. The Qur'an's insistence on its decisiveness is a consequence of its claim to be from God, and the oath by the sky and the earth is God's guarantee of that claim.

For the believer, this decisiveness is a mercy. In a world of endless argument, he has a ground to stand on. He does not have to rebuild his understanding of existence every morning from the latest opinion; he has a Word that has decided the essential questions. This does not close his mind — the Qur'an commands reflection more than any other book — but it gives his reflection a fixed point.

**The Oath and the Content: Why Sky and Earth Guarantee the Word**

Why does God swear by the returning sky and the splitting earth *specifically* to guarantee that the Qur'an is decisive? The connection is fitting on several levels.

The sky and the earth are themselves decisive. The rain returns without fail; the earth splits without exception. Nature does not jest. When God's word goes out to the sky — "send rain" — and to the earth — "bring forth" — they obey decisively. The Qur'an is God's word to *human beings*, and it is as decisive as His word to the elements. The difference is only that the sky and earth cannot disobey, while human beings can — but their disobedience does not make the Word any less decisive; it only makes them the ones who will be cut off by it.

There is also a connection of content. The sky and earth prove the Resurrection by their yearly cycle; the Qur'an is the Word that *announces* the Resurrection. The sign and the statement agree. God swears by the sign that the statement is true. It is as if He says: look at the rain and the crops — that is My decisive act; now hear the Qur'an — that is My decisive Word; they say the same thing.

**The Day of Decision**

The alternative reading — that "it" refers to the Day of Judgment — deserves its own development, because the Qur'an calls that Day *yawm al-faṣl* repeatedly:

> *"This is the Day of Decision (yawm al-faṣl) which you used to deny."* (Qur'an 37:21)

> *"Indeed the Day of Decision is the appointed time for them all."* (Qur'an 44:40)

> *"For what Day was it postponed? For the Day of Decision. And what will make you know what the Day of Decision is? Woe that Day to the deniers."* (Qur'an 77:12–15)

> *"Indeed the Day of Decision is an appointed time."* (Qur'an 78:17)

The Day is called *faṣl* because on it God separates: the believers from the disbelievers, the truthful from the liars, the people of the Garden from the people of the Fire. "And [it will be said], 'Separate yourselves today, O criminals'" (36:59). The two readings of verse 13 thus converge: the Qur'an is the decisive Word that announces the Day of Decision. To reject the Word is to be on the wrong side of the Day.

**The Psychological Effect of a Decisive Word**

Human beings need decisive words. A child needs a parent who says "no" and means it; a society needs laws that are enforced; a patient needs a diagnosis that is clear. Ambiguity in essential matters produces anxiety, paralysis, and manipulation. The Qur'an's decisiveness about the things that matter most — God, death, judgment, right and wrong — is a source of psychological stability for the one who accepts it. He knows where he stands. He knows what will happen. He knows what is required of him. This certainty frees him to act, where the doubter is frozen.

At the same time, the decisive Word is a warning to the one who plays with it. The next verse will state: "and it is not for amusement." The Quraysh treated the Qur'an as a matter for debate, mockery, and evening entertainment. Verse 13 tells them that the thing they are playing with is a verdict — and a verdict that will be executed.

**Practical Application**

The believer should approach the Qur'an as a decisive Word — not as literature to be admired, nor as a text to be argued about, but as a verdict to be accepted and lived. When it says a thing is forbidden, the matter is decided. When it says the Resurrection will occur, the matter is decided. The Companions understood this. When a verse was revealed, they did not debate it; they implemented it — the prohibition of wine, the command of hijab, the ruling on inheritance. ʿĀʾishah said of them that when the verses of Sūrah al-Nūr were revealed, the women tore their wrappers and covered themselves at once (al-Bukhārī 4758). That is the response of people who heard the Qur'an as *qawl faṣl*.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 13 is the answer of the sūrah's second oath: by the returning sky and the splitting earth, the Qur'an — and the Day it announces — is a decisive Word. *Faṣl* means cutting, separating, and deciding; the Qur'an separates truth from falsehood, is authoritative and final, and divides people by their response to it. Its decisiveness is a consequence of its divine source, a mercy to the believer seeking a fixed point, an answer to relativism, and a warning to those who treat it lightly. The sky and earth, which obey God's word without fail, are the fitting witnesses to a Word that human beings are commanded — but not compelled — to obey.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:14

> **And it is not for amusement.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Negative Complement to the Decisive Word**

*Wa-mā huwa bi-l-hazl* — "and it is not jest." Verse 13 stated positively what the Qur'an is: decisive. Verse 14 states negatively what it is not: amusement, play, jest. The Qur'an frequently pairs an affirmation with the denial of its opposite, so that no shadow of misunderstanding remains: "God is the Truth, and what they call upon besides Him is falsehood" (22:62); "the right course has become clear from the wrong" (2:256). Here: decisive, and not jest. The two verses together form a single sealed description.

The construction *mā huwa bi-l-hazl* uses the *bāʾ* of emphasis after a negation (*bāʾ al-taʾkīd*), which intensifies the denial: "it is not *at all* jest." The article on *al-hazl* makes it the genus: not any kind of jest, of any degree.

**The Word Hazl: Jest, Play, Triviality**

*Hazl* is the opposite of *jidd*, "seriousness." It denotes joking, frivolity, speech not meant in earnest, talk that is for entertainment rather than for truth. The Arabs said *hazala fī kalāmihi*, "he jested in his speech," of one who did not intend his words to be taken literally or acted upon. *Hazl* can be innocent (a light joke among friends) or contemptuous (mocking someone), but in all cases it is speech with no binding consequence — the speaker does not stake anything on it and the hearer need not respond to it.

The Qur'an denies that it is such speech. It is not for entertainment, not for passing the evening, not a matter of opinion to be tossed about, not a story to be enjoyed and forgotten. It is *jidd* — earnest, binding, consequential — and the consequence is the Judgment.

**Why This Denial Was Necessary**

The denial answers a specific attitude of the Makkan opponents. The Qur'an records that they treated revelation as amusement:

> *"And among the people is he who buys diverting speech (lahw al-ḥadīth) to lead astray from the way of God without knowledge, and takes it in ridicule."* (Qur'an 31:6)

> *"And when Our verses are recited to them, they say, 'We have heard. If we willed, we could say the like of this. This is nothing but legends of the former peoples.'"* (Qur'an 8:31)

> *"They approach not any new reminder from their Lord except that they listen to it while they are at play (yalʿabūn), their hearts distracted."* (Qur'an 21:2–3)

Al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, one of the leaders of Quraysh, would follow the Prophet ﷺ when he had finished reciting and say to the audience, "Come, I will tell you better stories than his" — and he would recite the tales of the Persian kings Rustam and Isfandiyār. The Quraysh regarded the Qur'an as one more entertainment competing in the marketplace of stories. Verse 14 cuts through this attitude: what you take as an evening's diversion is a verdict on your eternal state.

There was also the specific mockery of the Resurrection. The Qur'an records them saying, in jest, "When we have died and become dust and bones, shall we be resurrected? We and our forefathers? This is nothing but the legends of the former peoples" (23:82–83). They found the doctrine laughable. The sūrah, having proven it from their own bodies and from the earth beneath their feet, now tells them that the matter is no joke.

**Seriousness as a Divine Attribute of Speech**

The verse teaches something about God's speech in general. God does not jest. His words are not idle. The Qur'an says: "And We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them in play (*lāʿibīn*). Had We intended to take a diversion, We could have taken it from Ourselves — if We were to do so" (21:16–17). "Did you think that We created you in vain (*ʿabathan*), and that you would not be returned to Us?" (23:115). Creation is not play; revelation is not play; the Judgment is not play. The universe is an earnest matter, and the Word that explains it is earnest.

This attribute of divine speech has a consequence for how it is to be received. What is said in jest may be answered in jest; what is said in earnest demands an earnest response. If a friend jokes that your house is on fire, you may laugh; if a fireman says it, you run. The Quraysh's error was to respond to God's earnest Word as though it were a jester's. The sūrah corrects the category.

**The Prophet's Own Seriousness — and His Truthful Humor**

The Prophet ﷺ embodied the earnestness of the Word. When the Qur'an was recited, his Companions described him as being in a state of gravity; when the verses of warning came down, they could see the effect on his face. Yet the Prophet ﷺ was not humorless. He joked with his Companions — but always truthfully. He said:

> *"I do joke, but I say nothing but the truth."* (al-Ṭabarānī in al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Jāmiʿ 2494)

When an old woman asked him to pray that she enter Paradise, he told her, "No old woman will enter Paradise," and when she wept, he explained that God would make her young again — reciting "Indeed We have produced them in a new creation and made them virgins" (56:35–36) (al-Tirmidhī in *al-Shamāʾil*). His humor never departed from truth. This is the model for a community that has received a Word that is "not jest": lightness of manner is permitted; falsehood, even in jest, is not. He also warned:

> *"Woe to the one who speaks and lies to make people laugh; woe to him, woe to him."* (Abū Dāwūd 4990; al-Tirmidhī 2315; graded ḥasan)

**The Danger of Trivializing Religion**

Verse 14 has a sharp application for every age, including our own. Religion can be trivialized in many ways: by turning it into entertainment, by reducing it to cultural performance, by treating its rulings as optional suggestions, by mocking it openly, or by mocking it "harmlessly" among friends. The Qur'an treats the last of these with particular severity. When some men on the expedition to Tabūk made jokes about the reciters of the Qur'an, saying that they were the greediest and most cowardly of men, and then pleaded, "We were only conversing and playing," God revealed:

> *"Say: 'Was it God and His verses and His Messenger that you were mocking?' Make no excuse; you have disbelieved after your belief."* (Qur'an 9:65–66)

Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Taymiyyah both derive from this passage that mocking any part of the religion — even "in play" — is a form of disbelief, because it treats as *hazl* what God has declared to be *faṣl*. Verse 14 is the foundation of that ruling: the Qur'an is not a thing one may make jokes of, because it is not itself a joke.

**The Positive Side: The Dignity of Earnestness**

The verse does not merely prohibit; it ennobles. To be addressed by a Word that is *not* jest is to be taken seriously by God. He did not send a trifle to trifling creatures; He sent a decisive, earnest Word to beings capable of understanding it and answerable for their response. The human being's dignity is bound up with the seriousness of the Word addressed to him. A being who could be adequately addressed by jokes would be a being of no consequence. The Qur'an's earnestness is a measure of the human being's worth in the sight of God.

This is why the believer's relationship with the Qur'an should be marked by *reverence* (*hayba*). The Companions would tremble when reciting it; ʿUmar was heard weeping in the prayer from the back rows; the early Muslims would not read it while lounging carelessly. Not because the Qur'an is grim — it contains passages of overwhelming joy and beauty — but because it is real. Reverence is the proper response to reality.

**Seriousness Without Grimness**

A balance must be struck, and the Prophet ﷺ struck it. The Word is not jest; but the community that carries it is not forbidden joy. Islam is not a religion of long faces. The Prophet ﷺ smiled more than any man, according to his Companions; he played with children; he raced with his wife ʿĀʾishah. The seriousness of the Word does not mean the joylessness of life; it means that the *foundations* are firm — that beneath the laughter, the play, and the ordinary pleasures of life, there is a bedrock that is never treated lightly: God is real, death is coming, the Judgment is decisive. On that bedrock, a person can build a joyful life without fear that the ground will give way.

**A Reflection on Modern Attention**

Perhaps no age has been more inclined to treat everything as entertainment than our own. The devices in our hands turn the most serious matters — war, death, faith — into scrollable content, consumed and forgotten within seconds. Verse 14 speaks with special force here. The Qur'an refuses to be content. It is a decisive Word, and it demands the kind of attention one gives to a verdict, a diagnosis, a will. The believer who wishes to obey this verse must, at minimum, carve out a space in his day where he receives the Word *as* Word — undistracted, unhurried, in earnest — and does not let it become one more item in the stream.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 14 completes the description begun in verse 13 by denying the Qur'an's opposite: it is not jest, play, or amusement. The denial answers the Makkan habit of treating revelation as entertainment and the Resurrection as a joke. It reveals that earnestness is an attribute of God's speech, as of His creation. It grounds the ruling that mocking religion is a grave sin, models a humor that never departs from truth, ennobles the human being by the seriousness of the Word addressed to him, and calls the believer to receive the Qur'an with reverence in an age that turns everything into content.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:15

> **Truly they are devising a scheme,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Word to Its Opponents**

*Innahum yakīdūna kaydan* — "Indeed they are plotting a plot." The sūrah has made its argument and certified its Word. Now it turns to the response of those who heard it. They did not answer the argument; they *schemed* against it. The pronoun "they" refers to the disbelievers of Quraysh — understood from context, since they are the audience of every Makkan sūrah and the ones described throughout the Qur'an as plotting against the Prophet ﷺ.

The sentence is emphasized by *inna* and by the cognate accusative (*mafʿūl muṭlaq*): *yakīdūna kaydan*, "they plot a plotting." In Arabic, repeating the verb's own noun as its object intensifies and confirms: they are plotting *in earnest*, with real plots, not merely idle hostility. There is a rhetorical irony here. The previous verse said the Qur'an is "not jest." This verse says their plotting is real and serious. Both sides are in earnest — and the next verse will show whose earnestness prevails.

**The Word Kayd: Plotting, Scheming, Stratagem**

*Kayd* denotes a hidden scheme to bring harm upon someone — a plan carried out by indirection and concealment rather than open confrontation. It is close in meaning to *makr* (cunning, plotting), and the Qur'an uses both for the enemies of the prophets. *Kayd* can be blameworthy, when its aim is unjust, or praiseworthy, when it is a just strategy against an unjust enemy — as in Joseph's *kayd* to detain his brother: "Thus We planned (*kidnā*) for Joseph" (12:76).

Here the plotting of the disbelievers is blameworthy: it is directed against the truth, against the Messenger, and against the believers. What did it consist of?

**The Content of the Quraysh's Scheming**

The Qur'an and the biographical literature record the forms it took. They plotted to discredit the message: calling the Prophet ﷺ a poet, a soothsayer, a madman, a sorcerer — and coordinating which label to use at the pilgrimage season, as al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah did (74:18–25). They plotted to distract from it: al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith's stories, the musicians hired to drown out recitation. They plotted to pressure the Prophet ﷺ: offering him wealth, kingship, and women if he would stop, and threatening his uncle Abū Ṭālib. They plotted to break the believers: torturing Bilāl, Sumayyah, ʿAmmār, and Khabbāb; boycotting the clans of Hāshim and al-Muṭṭalib for three years. They plotted to destroy the message at its source: the assembly at Dār al-Nadwah, where they agreed to have a man from each clan strike the Prophet ﷺ simultaneously so that the blood-guilt would be spread among all the tribes. The Qur'an refers to this last plot directly:

> *"And [remember] when those who disbelieved plotted against you to restrain you, or kill you, or expel you. And they plot, and God plots; and God is the best of plotters."* (Qur'an 8:30)

The verse in al-Ṭāriq, revealed earlier in Makkah, anticipates all of this. "They are plotting" describes a continuous state: the scheming was ongoing throughout the Makkan period, in a hundred forms.

**The Pattern Across Prophetic History**

The source commentary rightly notes that the Quraysh's plotting places them in a lineage of schemers against the prophets. The Qur'an tells of the brothers of Joseph, who "plotted" (*kayd*) to cast him into the well (12:5); of Pharaoh's magicians and Pharaoh himself, who "plotted" against Moses (20:60, 64; 40:25, 37); of the people of Hūd (11:55) and of Noah (71:22, "they plotted a mighty plot"); of the nine men of Thamūd who swore to kill Ṣāliḥ by night (27:48–50: "They plotted a plot, and We plotted a plot, while they perceived not"); and of the enemies of Abraham, who "intended a plot against him, but We made them the greatest losers" (21:70). The vocabulary is constant. In every generation the opponents of truth respond not with argument but with *kayd* — and in every generation the outcome is the same.

The Makkan verses of the Qur'an frequently narrated these histories to the Prophet ﷺ, and the purpose was partly consolation: what is happening to you happened to every messenger, and every plot failed. Verse 15 fits this pattern of assurance. It tells the Prophet ﷺ, in effect: yes, they are plotting — as they always have.

**Why Do Deniers Resort to Plotting?**

There is a psychological and logical insight in the sequence of the sūrah. The Qur'an presented an argument (vv. 5–8, 11–12) and certified it as decisive (vv. 13–14). Those who could not refute the argument turned to plotting. This is a recurring pattern in human affairs: when a position cannot be defended by reason, its defenders resort to force, deception, and distraction. The Qur'an makes the point explicitly about the Quraysh: "And they say, 'Do not listen to this Qur'an, and make noise during it, so that perhaps you may overcome'" (41:26) — the scheme of *noise* against the scheme of *argument*.

The commentators observed that the move from denial to plotting reveals the true nature of the denial. If the Quraysh had honestly believed the Qur'an was jest, they would have ignored it, as one ignores a jester. That they plotted against it with all their resources shows that they sensed its power and feared it. Plotting is the tribute that falsehood pays to a truth it cannot answer.

**The Grammar of Continuity**

The verb *yakīdūna* is in the present tense (*muḍāriʿ*), indicating ongoing action: they *are plotting*, continuously. This applies beyond the Quraysh. Every generation of believers has faced schemes against the faith — sometimes violent, sometimes subtle: the slander of the Prophet ﷺ, the distortion of the Qur'an's meaning, the seduction of believers away from practice, the mockery of the pious. The verse's present tense makes it a permanent description: those who reject the Word will always plot against it. The believer should not be surprised or dismayed by this; the Qur'an told him in advance.

**The Believer's Response to Plotting**

What should the believer do when he perceives plotting against the truth? The sūrah's answer comes in the next two verses: God plots too (v. 16), so be patient and give them respite (v. 17). But the Qur'an elsewhere gives the fuller prescription:

> *"If you are patient and God-fearing, their plot will not harm you at all. Indeed God encompasses what they do."* (Qur'an 3:120)

Patience (*ṣabr*) and God-consciousness (*taqwā*) are the believer's shield against *kayd*. The reasoning is simple: a plot works by exploiting weakness — fear, greed, impatience, division. A person who is patient cannot be provoked into rash action; a person who is God-fearing cannot be bribed or seduced. Against such a person, the plotter has no purchase.

This does not mean passivity. The Prophet ﷺ took every lawful precaution: he sent the believers to Abyssinia, accepted the protection of Abū Ṭālib and later of al-Muṭʿim ibn ʿAdī, planned the Hijrah with care, and used ʿAlī as a decoy in his bed on the night of the assassination plot. Trust in God's counter-plot does not exclude prudence; it excludes *anxiety*. The believer does what he can and leaves the outcome to the One whose plot is firm.

**The Comfort in the Verse**

There is comfort in being *told* that one's enemies are plotting. The unknown is more frightening than the known. God informs His Prophet ﷺ that scheming is underway, and by informing him, He signals that He knows the scheme in every detail — and, by implication, that He has already accounted for it. The Prophet ﷺ is not walking blind into ambush; his Lord sees the ambush and has told him. The believer facing hidden hostility can take the same comfort: "God knows what they conceal and what they declare" (2:77), and nothing they plan escapes His knowledge or His power.

**Application in Everyday Life**

Not every believer faces the plots of a Quraysh. But every believer faces smaller forms of *kayd*: the colleague who undermines him behind his back, the whisper of Satan that schemes to make him abandon a good habit, the social pressure that schemes to make him ashamed of his faith. The Qur'an's own description of Satan's activity uses the same word: "Indeed the plot of Satan has ever been weak" (4:76). The response is the same at every scale: patience, God-consciousness, prudence, and trust — and the certainty, given in the next verse, that every plot is answered.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 15 turns from the decisive Word to those who resist it: the disbelievers are plotting a real, ongoing scheme. The word *kayd* denotes hidden stratagem; its content was the campaign of slander, distraction, pressure, persecution, and attempted assassination that the Quraysh waged against the Prophet ﷺ. The verse places them in the lineage of every people who plotted against a prophet, exposes plotting as the resort of those who cannot answer an argument, gives the believer advance notice so that he is neither surprised nor afraid, and sets up the divine response of the next verse.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:16

> **and I, too, am devising a scheme.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Divine Response in Parallel Form**

*Wa-akīdu kaydan* — "and I plot a plot." The response to the disbelievers' scheming is stated in a form that mirrors it exactly: they *yakīdūna kaydan*; God *akīdu kaydan*. The same verb, the same cognate accusative, the same emphasis. This deliberate parallelism (*mushākalah*) is a feature of Qur'anic style: God describes His response in the terms of the action it answers, so that the correspondence between deed and recompense is unmistakable. The same device appears in "They plot and God plots" (8:30), "They deceive God, but He is deceiving them" (4:142), "They forgot God, so He forgot them" (9:67), and "God mocks them" (2:15).

The shift to the first person singular — *akīdu*, "I plot" — is striking. Until now the sūrah has spoken of God in the third person ("He is able," v. 8). Now God speaks directly: *I*. The effect is to bring the full weight of divine personal involvement into the confrontation. It is no longer an abstract power that opposes the schemers; it is God Himself, speaking, saying *I am plotting against you*.

**How Can Plotting Be Attributed to God?**

This question occupied the theologians and commentators, and it must be answered carefully, because it touches on divine attributes. *Kayd*, as applied to human beings, often implies deception, weakness, and injustice — the resort of one who cannot achieve his aim openly. None of this applies to God. So in what sense does He "plot"?

The classical answer, articulated by al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and formulated with precision by Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim, is as follows. Attributes such as *kayd* and *makr* are not attributed to God absolutely, as names by which He is called (one does not say "God is a plotter" as one says "God is Merciful"). They are attributed to Him *in response*, as recompense for those who plot — that is, in a context that makes clear their meaning is *just requital in kind*. In such a context, *kayd* denotes: bringing about the ruin of the schemer by means he does not perceive, in a manner that fits his own scheme, and with perfect justice. This is not a defect but a perfection, for it is praiseworthy to defeat the unjust plotter through means he cannot foresee. The Qur'an itself says "God is the best of plotters" (*khayr al-mākirīn*, 3:54; 8:30) — that is, the best of those who requite plotting.

Ibn al-Qayyim writes in *al-Ṣawāʿiq al-Mursalah* and elsewhere that God's *makr* and *kayd* are of two kinds of perfection: they are *just* (directed only at the unjust), and they are *wise* (achieving the ruin of the schemer through the very means of his scheme). Human plotting is blameworthy because it is usually unjust and deceptive; divine "plotting" is praiseworthy because it is just requital carried out with wisdom and knowledge. The word is the same; the reality is entirely different.

**What God's Plot Consists Of**

The commentators describe God's *kayd* against the disbelievers as taking several forms, all of which the Qur'an confirms.

First, *istidrāj* — gradual leading-on. God grants the schemer success, wealth, and ease, so that he becomes more confident and more deeply committed to his path, until the punishment overtakes him from a direction he did not expect: "We will lead them on gradually (*sa-nastadrijuhum*) from where they do not know. And I give them respite; indeed My plot (*kaydī*) is firm" (7:182–183; 68:44–45). This passage is the closest parallel to our verse, and it links God's *kayd* directly to *respite* — which is exactly what verse 17 will command.

Second, *turning the plot back on the plotter*. "The evil plot encompasses only its own people" (35:43). The brothers of Joseph plotted to remove him, and their plot placed him on the throne of Egypt. Pharaoh killed the sons of Israel to prevent a deliverer, and raised the deliverer in his own palace. The Quraysh drove the Prophet ﷺ from Makkah to destroy his mission, and the Hijrah was the beginning of the mission's triumph. The plot of Dār al-Nadwah, where they agreed to kill him, was the direct cause of the Hijrah, and the Hijrah was the direct cause of their eventual defeat. "They planned, and God planned; and God is the best of planners" (8:30).

Third, *confusing their counsel*. God causes the plotters to disagree, miscalculate, and undermine one another. At Badr, the Quraysh marched out confident, and their own overconfidence — with the dream shown to the Prophet ﷺ that made them seem few (8:43–44) — led them into the defeat that broke their power.

Fourth, *the ultimate exposure*. On the Day of testing secrets (v. 9), every plot will be revealed and every plotter held to account. The scheming that seemed clever in the world will be seen for what it was.

**"God's Plot Is Firm"**

The phrase in 7:183 and 68:45 — *inna kaydī matīn*, "indeed My plot is firm" — sets the divine plot apart from every human one. Human plots are fragile: they depend on secrecy that can be broken, on allies who can defect, on circumstances that can change. God's plot is *matīn* — firm, solid, unbreakable — because He knows every variable, controls every circumstance, and cannot be surprised. The Quraysh's plot was known to Him before they conceived it; His plot is unknown to them until it is executed. The asymmetry is total.

Al-Rāzī draws out the logic. A plot succeeds by the plotter's *knowing more* than his victim. But no one can know more than God, and God knows everything about the plotter. Therefore no plot against God's cause can succeed, and every plot of God's against the unjust must succeed. Verse 16 is thus not merely a threat but a statement of logical necessity: given who God is, the outcome of the contest is fixed.

**The Consolation of the Prophet ﷺ**

The verse was revealed at a time when the Prophet ﷺ and a handful of believers faced the whole might of Quraysh — their wealth, their tribal power, their control of the sanctuary. Humanly, the plotters held every card. Verse 16 tells the Prophet ﷺ that the contest is not between him and them but between them and God. He is not the one who must out-plot the Quraysh; God is. This transfers the burden from human shoulders to divine ones, and it is the foundation of the calm the Prophet ﷺ displayed throughout the persecution. When Abū Bakr, in the cave of Thawr, whispered that the pursuers had only to look down to see them, the Prophet ﷺ replied: "What do you think of two, of whom God is the third?" (al-Bukhārī 3653; Muslim 2381). That is the confidence of verse 16.

Later Qur'anic passages make the consolation explicit: "So do not grieve over them, nor be in distress over what they plot" (16:127; 27:70); "And be patient, and your patience is only through God, and do not grieve over them, and do not be distressed by what they plot" (16:127).

**The Fear the Verse Should Produce**

For the schemer, the verse is a warning of the gravest kind. He is plotting against One who is plotting against him — and whose plot is firm. The Qur'an asks: "Do they feel secure from the plan (*makr*) of God? None feels secure from the plan of God except the losing people" (7:99). The scholars of the heart taught that a believer should never feel entirely secure from God's *makr* — not because God is capricious, but because a person may be sinning while imagining he is safe, and the ease he enjoys may be the leading-on that precedes the seizure. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī said: "Whoever is given expanse and does not see that he is being plotted against has no understanding."

This is the balance of Islamic spirituality: hope (*rajāʾ*) and fear (*khawf*), like two wings. Verse 16 supplies the fear: do not be deceived by your success against the truth; the One you scheme against has a scheme for you.

**Justice, Not Cruelty**

It is important to see that the divine plot is directed *only* at those who plot against the truth. It is not a general statement that God deceives people. It is requital, measure for measure. The Qur'an's principle is: "The recompense of an evil is an evil like it" (42:40). Those who scheme in secret against God's Messenger are answered by a scheme in secret against them. Those who deal openly and honestly with the truth — even if they initially reject it — are not the subject of this verse. Many of the Quraysh who opposed the Prophet ﷺ openly, without treachery, later became Muslims and Companions; the divine plot did not consume them, because they were not plotters in the sense the verse intends.

**Application for the Believer**

Two applications follow. First, the believer should never plot unjustly against anyone, because he knows that God answers plotting with plotting, and "the evil plot encompasses only its own people" (35:43). The one who digs a pit for his brother falls into it — this is not merely a proverb but a divine law. Second, the believer who is the target of unjust plotting should hand the matter to God, with prudence but without anxiety, because God has undertaken to answer plots against His servants: "Indeed God defends those who believe" (22:38). The Prophet's ﷺ supplication when facing enemies was: "O God, we place You at their throats, and we seek refuge in You from their evils" (Abū Dāwūd 1537; graded ṣaḥīḥ). That is the practical form of trusting verse 16.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 16 answers the plotting of the disbelievers with God's own declaration, in the first person: "I too am plotting." The parallel form marks the recompense as measure-for-measure; the attribution of *kayd* to God is understood, per the classical theologians, as just requital carried out with perfect knowledge and wisdom — not deception or weakness. God's plot takes the forms of gradual leading-on, turning the plot back on the plotter, confusing the plotters' counsel, and final exposure; it is "firm" where human plots are fragile. The verse consoles the Prophet ﷺ and the believers by transferring the contest to God, warns the schemer that he is opposed by One he cannot outwit, and teaches the believer never to plot unjustly and never to fear the plots of others.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:17

> **So be gentle with the disbelievers; grant them respite for a while.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Closing Counsel**

*Fa-mahhil al-kāfirīna amhilhum ruwaydā* — "So give the disbelievers respite; grant them respite gently." The sūrah ends not with a threat but with an instruction to the Prophet ﷺ, and through him to every believer who faces the opposition of those who reject the truth. Having declared that God has His own plot against the plotters, God tells His Messenger what *he* is to do meanwhile: wait; do not rush; leave them for a short time.

The *fa-* ("so") makes this the consequence of verse 16. *Because* I am plotting, *you* need not hurry. The matter is in hand. Your task is patience.

**The Words: Mahhil, Amhil, Ruwaydā**

Three words in this short verse carry the meaning, and all three point in the same direction.

*Mahhil* is the imperative of *mahhala* (form II), and *amhil* is the imperative of *amhala* (form IV); both derive from the root *m-h-l*, which means slowness, deliberateness, and giving time. *Muhlah* is a period of grace or delay. The two verbs are near-synonyms, and their repetition within one short verse is for emphasis and variation: "give them time — yes, give them time." Some grammarians noted a subtle difference: *mahhala* (form II) suggests a gradual, repeated granting of delay, while *amhala* (form IV) suggests the act of granting delay; together they cover both the act and its continuation.

*Ruwaydā* is a diminutive form (*taṣghīr*) from the root *r-w-d*, meaning gentleness, slowness, and ease. As an adverb it means "a little," "gently," "slowly," "for a short while." Al-Ṭabarī explains it as *qalīlan*, "a little," and the sense is "grant them a short respite." Al-Qurṭubī notes the tenderness of the diminutive: it softens the command, giving it the tone of "just a little while more." The diminutive also implies that the respite is *brief* — not endless tolerance, but a short delay before the matter is settled.

The verse therefore says: "Give them time, give them time — just a little." The threefold expression of delay, capped by a word meaning "a little," produces a precise meaning: *wait, but not for long*.

**Who Are "the Disbelievers"?**

*Al-kāfirīn* — "the disbelievers" — in the Makkan context are the Quraysh who rejected the Prophet ﷺ and were plotting against him. The word is definite and specific: not disbelievers in general throughout history, but *these* disbelievers, the plotters of verse 15. The counsel of respite is thus not a general policy toward all non-Muslims for all time; it is a specific instruction about how to bear the hostility of a specific group during a specific period — and, by extension, a principle for how believers should bear the hostility of those who plot against them while God's decree unfolds.

**What Does "Respite" Mean Here?**

The commentators understood the command in complementary ways.

First, it is a command to *restrain from retaliation*. In Makkah the believers were forbidden to fight; their instruction was patience and forbearance. Verse 17 is one of the verses of this period that told the Prophet ﷺ to leave the disbelievers alone — not to seek revenge, not to call down punishment, not to take matters into his own hands.

Second, it is a command to *stop hastening the outcome*. The source commentary notes that the disbelievers themselves demanded that the punishment come quickly, in mockery: "O God, if this is the truth from You, then rain down upon us stones from the sky" (8:32); "Bring us what you promise us, if you are of the truthful" (7:70; 11:32; 46:22). The Prophet ﷺ, for his part, longed to see God's promise fulfilled and the persecution ended. The verse tells him: do not wish for haste; God's timing is perfect. The Qur'an says elsewhere: "So be patient, as the messengers of firm resolve were patient, and do not seek to hasten for them" (46:35).

Third, it is a reassurance in the form of a command. "Give them a little respite" implies: their time is short. Al-Ṭabarī paraphrases: "Give them a little time, until the term God has appointed for them arrives; do not hasten, for their term is near." The command carries within it a promise.

**Respite as Divine Method**

Verse 17 reflects a consistent divine method that the Qur'an describes repeatedly. God does not seize the wrongdoer at once; He gives time. The purposes of this delay are several, and understanding them dissolves the impatience that the verse addresses.

The delay is a *mercy*, because it allows the opportunity of repentance: "And your Lord is the Forgiving, the Possessor of Mercy. If He were to seize them for what they have earned, He would have hastened the punishment for them; but they have an appointed time from which they will never find an escape" (18:58). Many of the very Quraysh who plotted against the Prophet ﷺ — Khālid ibn al-Walīd, ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, Abū Sufyān, ʿIkrimah the son of Abū Jahl — later became Muslims. Had the punishment been hastened, they would have died in disbelief. The respite of verse 17 was the space in which their guidance became possible.

The delay is a *test*, because it distinguishes those who persist in wrongdoing from those who turn back: "And if God were to punish people for what they have earned, He would not leave on its surface any creature; but He defers them to an appointed term" (35:45).

The delay is a *part of the plot* of verse 16 — the leading-on that lets the wrongdoer become confident until the seizure comes from where he does not expect: "And I give them respite; indeed My plot is firm" (7:183). The commentators note that verse 17's *amhil* is the very word used in 7:183 (*umlī lahum*, "I give them respite") and 68:45. The Prophet ﷺ is told to grant respite because God Himself is granting it, and the granting is itself a strategy.

And the delay is a *just* proportion, because the punishment when it comes will be exact, and the delay ensures that the wrongdoer has had every opportunity: "Do not think that God is unaware of what the wrongdoers do. He only delays them for a Day when eyes will stare in horror" (14:42).

**The Prophet's ﷺ Embodiment of the Verse**

The Prophet ﷺ lived verse 17 with perfect fidelity. In Ṭāʾif, after being stoned and driven out, the angel of the mountains offered to crush the city between the two mountains, and he replied: "No; rather I hope that God will bring forth from their descendants those who worship God alone" (al-Bukhārī 3231; Muslim 1795). That is respite. At the conquest of Makkah, standing before the very people who had plotted his death for twenty years, he said: "Go, for you are free" (al-Bayhaqī, *al-Sunan al-Kubrā*; Ibn Hishām). That is the fruit of respite. Every day of the Makkan period, when he had every provocation to curse them and every human reason to despair, he gave them time — and the time was not wasted, because within it a nation was guided.

**"For a While": The Term Is Short**

The source commentary notes two views on when the respite ended: some said at the Battle of Badr, in 2 AH, when the leaders of the plotting Quraysh — Abū Jahl, Umayyah ibn Khalaf, ʿUtbah, Shaybah, al-Walīd — were killed in a single day; others said at the Day of Resurrection, when all will be judged. Both are correct in their sphere. The worldly term of the plotters was short indeed: from the revelation of this sūrah in early Makkah to Badr was little more than a decade, and for men who imagined themselves permanent, a decade is *ruwaydā* — a little while. The ultimate term is the Day of testing secrets, and by the measure of eternity all of worldly life is *ruwaydā*.

Ibn Kathīr comments:

> *"That is: wait for them, and do not hasten; you will see what befalls them of punishment, torment, and destruction — as God says, 'We let them enjoy for a little; then We will force them into a massive punishment' (31:24)."* (Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on 86:17)

**The Wisdom of the Believer's Patience**

Why does God command the believer to give respite, rather than simply telling him that respite is being given? Because the command *shapes the believer*. A person who has been wronged and told merely to wait may wait bitterly, nursing his desire for vengeance. A person who is commanded to *grant* respite — to actively extend time to his enemy — is being trained in magnanimity. He becomes a participant in God's mercy rather than a spectator of God's justice. The verse does not say "they will be given respite"; it says "*you* give them respite." The Prophet ﷺ is made an agent of the delay, and this is his honor: he shares in the divine patience.

This has practical implications for anyone wronged. The urge to retaliate immediately is one of the strongest in human nature, and one of the most destructive. It produces cycles of vengeance, it clouds judgment, and it usurps a role — final justice — that belongs to God. The verse teaches a different path: do what is prudent, refrain from what is rash, and let the matter unfold. Not because the wrong does not matter — it matters enough that God Himself has undertaken to answer it — but because the timing and the manner of the answer are His.

**Gentleness and Firmness Together**

The translation "be gentle" for *mahhil* captures something real in the verse: the diminutive *ruwaydā*, the softness of the repeated verbs. There is gentleness in the command. But the gentleness is not weakness. The gentleness is the gentleness of one who is *certain of the outcome*. A person who knows he will win can afford to be patient; only the anxious are harsh. The sūrah has established, under two oaths, that God watches, that He will raise, that His Word is decisive, and that His plot is firm. From that certainty flows the calm of verse 17. The believer's gentleness with his opponents is the outward form of his inner confidence in God.

**The Sūrah's Return to Its Beginning**

The sūrah began with the night-comer — the star that appears in darkness and pierces it. It ends with respite — a little more darkness before the dawn. The two ends meet. The believer in Makkah lived in the night of persecution; the sūrah told him at the start that the night is watched, and it tells him at the end that the night is short. Between these, it proved the return of all things — man from dust, rain from the sky, plants from the earth — and certified the Word that announces it. The final counsel is the practical application of everything before it: since all this is true, wait a little. The morning is coming, and the star that pierces the night is already visible.

**Practical Application Today**

Believers today face opposition of many kinds — sometimes hostility, sometimes mockery, sometimes systematic scheming. Verse 17 teaches: do not despair, do not rage, do not seek to hasten God's judgment upon anyone, and do not take into your hands what belongs to God. Do what is lawful and wise to protect yourself and the truth; then grant respite. Continue to hope for the guidance of those who oppose you, as the Prophet ﷺ hoped for the people of Ṭāʾif. And know that the time is *ruwaydā* — short. Every tyrant has fallen; every plot against the truth has unraveled; and every soul will stand on the Day when the secrets are tested with no strength and no helper. In the meantime, the believer's task is not to win the contest — God has already undertaken that — but to be patient, gentle, and firm, and to keep reciting the decisive Word that is not jest.

**Summary of the Verse**

Verse 17 closes the sūrah with a command to the Prophet ﷺ to grant the disbelievers a brief respite: *mahhil*, *amhil*, *ruwaydā* — give them time, give them time, a little. It forbids hasty retaliation and the hastening of God's judgment, reassures by implying that the plotters' term is short (fulfilled at Badr, and finally on the Day of Judgment), and reflects the divine method of respite as mercy, test, strategy, and justice. The Prophet ﷺ embodied it from Ṭāʾif to the conquest of Makkah. The command trains the believer in magnanimity, expresses the gentleness that flows from certainty, and brings the sūrah full circle: the night is watched, the night is short, and the dawn is sure.
