# Sūrah al-Ṭāriq (Chapter 86) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Knock at the Door of the Night**

Sūrah al-Ṭāriq is the eighty-sixth chapter of the Qur'an, a compact text of seventeen verses that occupies a place in the written muṣḥaf between Sūrah al-Burūj, "The Constellations," and Sūrah al-Aʿlā, "The Most High." It belongs to the early Makkan period; the source record judges it most likely revealed after Sūrah al-Balad (chapter 90), which sets it within the intense middle stretch of revelation, when the Qur'an's address was aimed chiefly at the Quraysh of Makkah — a small audience, mostly hostile, being invited for the first time in their history to weigh the claims of prophethood, resurrection, and final judgment. The sūrah takes its name from its first oath: *al-ṭāriq*, rendered in the source translation as "that which comes by night," and traditionally rendered also as "the Night Visitant" and "the Morning Star." The name is built from the Arabic root *ṭ-r-q*, whose basic sense is to pound or strike. From it comes *ṭarīq*, a road or path — the way that is pounded hard and compact by the feet that walk it. And from the same root comes the noun of agent, *ṭāriq*: one who arrives at night. The connection is a small window into the texture of old Arabian life: in a land of tribal suspicion and open raiding, doors were barred after dark, and anyone who came to a house at night had to announce himself by knocking, by *ṭarq*, upon the door. The Night Visitant is thus the one who strikes upon what is closed — and in the imagery of this sūrah, that act of knocking upon a shut door becomes a portrait of how truth, death, and judgment arrive upon a sealed and sleeping world.

**An Early Word to a City in the Dark**

The sūrah's historical moment matters for nearly every verse that follows. The Makkans of the first decade of revelation did not, by and large, deny that God exists, or that He had created the heavens and the earth; what they refused — sometimes with mockery, sometimes with genuine bewilderment — was the claim that the dead would be raised and judged. The Qur'an preserves their objection in their own quoted words: *"There is nothing but our first life; we die and we live, and we shall not be raised again"* (45:24). To ears formed on that assumption, the message of resurrection sounded like a tall tale, and the messenger who insisted upon it became, in their eyes, a poet, a sorcerer, or a man possessed. At the same time, the small community that believed him was under mounting pressure, and the chieftains of Quraysh had already begun to gather in council to decide what to do with this man and his movement — the scheming that the sūrah's final verses address directly. Sūrah al-Ṭāriq answers both situations at once. To the intellectual objection it offers evidence, not speculation: it points to the star that pierces the dark, to the gushing drop from which every human being came, to the rain that returns from the sky and the seed that splits in the earth — familiar, universal, nightly-and-seasonally-repeated proofs that the One who initiates can also restore. To the political pressure it offers quiet and formidable assurance: they plot, and God plots, and the believers are told, at the very end, to grant them respite.

**Two Oaths, One Verdict: the Architecture of the Sūrah**

The sūrah is built as a ring of oaths. It opens with a sworn pair — *the sky and the Night Visitant* (vv. 1–3) — and the answer to that oath runs from verse 4 through verse 10: every soul has a guardian over it; let man consider the gushing fluid from which he was created; He who created him is able to return him; on the Day when secrets are tested he will have neither strength nor helper. Then the oath returns in a second pair — *the resurgent sky and the furrowed earth* (vv. 11–12) — and the answer to that oath runs from verse 13 to verse 17: it is a decisive Word, no matter for jest; they are plotting, and God is plotting; so grant them respite a little while. The classical commentators, reading the sūrah's structure closely, observed that the object of all four oaths — the thing sworn about, the *muqsam ʿalayhi* — is disclosed at verse 13: this Book, this message, and the Day it announces are *qawl faṣl*, a decisive Word. The heavens, the star, the rain, and the seed are thus not decorations; they are witnesses summoned in pairs to certify a verdict that is then delivered in plain speech. The effect is of a courtroom in which the entire cosmos is called to testify before the sentence is read.

**Small Signs Summoned to Prove a Great Truth**

There is a deliberate modesty in the sūrah's choice of evidence. It does not argue from the exotic or the inaccessible. It argues from what every human being has seen with the unaided eye: a star shining through the night, the origin of a child, the coming of rain, the cracking of soil into green shoots. This is a consistent Qur'anic method — *"We shall show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves, until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth"* (41:53) — and here it is deployed with special tightness: the greatest claim in religion, bodily resurrection and judgment, is argued from the humblest materials in human experience. The logic is that of the nearer proof for the further fact: the One who has already done something small and familiar can hardly be doubted for something that differs from it only in repetition. Creation was the first time; resurrection is the second; and the entire visible world, this sūrah insists, is an engine of second times — rain returns, seeds return, the stars return each night, and the sky and earth between them perform, endlessly and in public, the very act the Makkans declared impossible.

**The Sūrah on the Prophet's Tongue**

The introductory record of the source notes that the Prophet ﷺ would often recite this sūrah and the one before it, Sūrah al-Burūj, as part of the two afternoon prayers — Ẓuhr and ʿAsr — a practice transmitted in the commentaries' introductions to this chapter. The detail is worth pausing upon. These are the middle prayers of a working day, prayed in the thick of labor and heat, when minds are occupied with markets, errands, and disputes; and into those crowded hours the Prophet placed a sūrah whose whole business is watchfulness — the star that watches from the sky, the guardian that watches over every soul, the Day when hidden things are brought to test, the plot watched by the Plotter. For a community being watched and plotted against by its own city, the recitation was both comfort and formation: it trained the believers to see every ordinary afternoon as standing inside the same frame as the Night Visitant and the Day of Decision. What the sūrah teaches by its content, its liturgical use taught by rhythm: the unseen watchers, and the unseen outcome, are present realities in the most ordinary hours.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the seventeen verses of Sūrah al-Ṭāriq as an independent discourse, in strict order. Where the source commentary binds verses together — verses 1–2, verses 6–7, verses 11–12, and verses 15–16 — they are here separated, and each verse receives its own complete treatment, since the mandated method requires that no verse be compressed into a group or shortened for the sake of the whole. Each section quotes the verse, then expands it through the Arabic language — roots, morphology, and idiom explained simply — the classical exegetes including al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Tustarī, Maybudī, and Ālūsī; authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified; Qur'anic cross-references with the relevant text quoted; and the logical and psychological structure of the verse's teaching, carried through to its consequences in ordinary modern life. Before the first verse, the sūrah opens as nearly all sūrahs do with the basmalah — *In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful* — and its presence here carries its own lesson: a chapter whose matter is watchfulness, judgment, and the divine scheme against the schemers is nonetheless entered through the two names of mercy, *al-Raḥmān* and *al-Raḥīm*, teaching the reciter from the threshold that the God who tests secrets and ends respite is first, and finally, the Merciful — that the whole architecture of warning stands inside, and serves, an architecture of mercy.

The sūrah is short enough to be memorized in a day and deep enough to occupy a lifetime; the pages that follow are an attempt to honor both facts.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:1

> **By the sky and that which comes by night.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grammar of a Divine Oath**

The sūrah opens with the particle *wa* — "by" — the Arabic oath-prefix that grammarians call the *wāw al-qasam*. When this particle is set before a noun in the accusative, as here before *al-samāʾ* (the sky), the construction is a solemn swearing, the equivalent of raising a hand in court. It is a form God uses frequently in the shorter Makkan sūrahs, and always at moments of weight: oaths in the Qur'an are never decorative; they are summons to attention, a divine hand laid on the listener's shoulder before an announcement of consequence. Two features of this particular oath deserve notice. First, God swears *by His creatures* — by the sky and by the night arrival — and this is His prerogative alone: the Owner may swear by what He owns. For His servants the reverse legislation was given, when the Prophet ﷺ found ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb swearing, in the old pre-Islamic manner, by his father, and said:

> *"God forbids you to swear by your fathers. Whoever must swear, let him swear by God, or else keep silent."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Ibn ʿUmar)

The logic is exact: an oath stakes everything on the thing sworn by, and nothing in creation is great enough to bear that weight except the One who made it — while God, who needs nothing, may swear by anything He has made, and by swearing He does not diminish it but hallows it. When God swears by the sky, the sky is honored, not used. Second, the oath is plural in its implications but universal in its witness: every listener under every sky has stood beneath one. The very first word of the sūrah's argument is thus a fact that no Makkan polytheist, philosopher, or skeptic could dispute.

**A Word Built from the Sound of Knocking**

The second object of the oath, *al-ṭāriq*, is one of the most quietly brilliant word-choices in the Qur'an. Its root, *ṭ-r-q*, means to pound, hammer, or strike — the sound of knocking itself seems embedded in the consonants. From this root the Arabs derived *ṭarīq*, a path or road, because a road is precisely the ground that has been pounded and compacted by passing feet. And from it again comes *ṭāriq*, the one who comes at night — because in the Arabia of the era, as in most pre-modern worlds, doors were shut and barred after dark, and a nocturnal traveler could not simply walk in: he had to knock, to pound, to strike upon the door and announce himself to the household within. *Al-ṭāriq* is therefore not merely "something that appears at night"; it is the one who *arrives upon the closed and the sleeping*, the one who breaks in upon enclosure. The verse swears by the sky and by whatever, or whoever, comes knocking upon it out of the night. Classical opinion then divides, as verse 2 will show, over the identity of this knocker: the great majority hear in it the stars, which appear in the evening, vanish with the dawn, and knock, as it were, with their light upon the darkness; al-Biqāʿī records both readings — a specific star, or all the heavenly bodies that appear by night and disappear by day; and Ālūsī notes the minority view that any glittering star may be meant, since each in its way strikes upon the dark. Behind all these readings stands the same root-image: light striking upon closure.

**Why Begin with the Sky and Its Messenger?**

The choice of the sky as the first witness is deliberate strategy. The sūrah's destination, disclosed by verse 5 onward, is the resurrection of the human body — a claim its first hearers found absurd. Before the argument can touch their bodies, it must reawaken their eyes. And the sky was, for the Makkan audience, the most eloquent object in existence: they navigated by it, timed their caravans and their agriculture by it, built their poetry upon it, and — crucially — already acknowledged its Lord. The Qur'an repeatedly swears by the sky and its inhabitants when it wishes to establish a matter of the unseen upon visible ground: *"By the star when it sets"* (53:1); *"I swear by the positions of the stars — and it is a mighty oath, if you but knew"* (56:75–76). The stars, in the Qur'an's own idiom, are the lamps of the lowest heaven and the guardians of its borders: *"And We adorned the lowest heaven with lamps and made them a guard"* (37:6). To swear by the sky and its night arrival was therefore to summon a witness already credited in the courtroom of Makkan minds. The believer and the idolater alike had stood under that sky and felt its vastness; the oath requires only that they now think about what they have always seen.

**The Night Arrival as Forewarning**

There is a deeper fitness in the image that will only fully unfold as the sūrah proceeds. A *ṭāriq* — a night arrival — has three properties that this sūrah is about to apply to the human soul's situation. He comes *unannounced*: the household sleeping behind the barred door did not schedule his knock. He comes *to what is closed*: knocking is the act of one who stands outside a sealed door. And his knock *demands a response*: doors exist to be either opened or defended. Verse 4 will reveal the watcher over every soul; verse 9 will reveal the Day when what is sealed in hearts is brought out and tested; verse 17 will close the sūrah with the respite of those who forget that an arrival is appointed. In that architecture, the Night Visitant of verse 1 is the sūrah's overture: something is coming, at an hour you do not know, upon the door you imagine shut. The pre-Islamic Arab lying awake at a knock in the night experienced a small, familiar version of what every soul will experience at the knock of death and the summons of judgment. The Qur'an so often builds its largest theology out of such small, shared human moments — and here, at the very threshold of the sūrah, the entire doctrine of the unexpected arrival is compressed into a single active participle.

**Modern Eyes and the Knocking Stars**

For most of human history the nightly appearance of the stars was one of the most regular experiences of every living person; the sūrah's opening witness was, for fourteen centuries, verifiable by any listener on any clear night. Ours is the first generation in which that is substantially untrue: city lights have drowned the sky for a majority of the earth's population, and the piercing star of verse 3 is now, for many, a rumor. The loss is not merely aesthetic but religious in the Qur'an's sense, for the sky is counted among God's signs, and signs exist to be seen. Interestingly, the modern science of astronomy has, in its own idiom, restored something of the old wonder: astronomers call stars and events that suddenly appear and fade — novae, supernovae, comets sweeping in from the dark — "transients," which is precisely the semantic field of *ṭāriq*: that which arrives. Instruments now detect these arrivals nightly, in numbers no ancient sky-watcher ever imagined. The believer who reads verse 1 today may therefore take the oath in both registers: the recovered naked-eye sky of a dark countryside, where the old experience of the nightly visitant can still be had; and the instrumented sky, which registers arrivals out of the darkness at every hour. In both, the same sworn truth stands — the heavens are not an empty dome but a place of comings, and the God who points to them as witnesses is announcing, from the sūrah's first words, that something is coming for the listener too.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:2

> **And what will apprise thee of that which comes by night?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Magnifies**

No sooner has the oath named the Night Visitant than the Qur'an interposes a question that withholds the name again: *wa-mā adrāka mā al-ṭāriq* — "and what will apprise thee of the Night Visitant?" The construction is one of the Qur'an's most recognizable rhetorical instruments, the magnifying interrogative. The verb *adrā* (from the root *d-r-w/d-r-y*, to know, to be apprised) in this form means to cause to know, to inform; the question is therefore not a request for information but a declaration of magnitude: *how could you even be told what it is?* The Qur'an uses this exact formula in a small family of places, always before realities at the edge of human grasp: *"The Striker! What is the Striker? And what will apprise thee of the Striker?"* (101:1–3); *"The Inevitable! What is the Inevitable? And what will apprise thee of the Inevitable?"* (69:1–3); *"And what will apprise thee of the Day of Recompense?"* (82:17–18); *"And what will apprise thee of the Crusher?"* (104:4–5); *"What will appraise thee of the steep pass?"* (90:12); *"And what will apprise thee of the Night of Power?"* (97:2). The pattern is consistent: the formula never stands before trivia. It marks a reality whose full dimensions exceed the listener's present categories — the Day of Judgment, Hell's abyss, the immeasurable worth of a single night of destiny. That the Night Visitant is introduced in the same register tells the reader instantly that this is no idle star: something of the Day it heralds already hangs about it.

**Adrāka versus Yudrīka: A Subtle Distinction**

The classical scholars of Qur'anic style noticed a delicate grammatical pattern in these formulas, and it bears directly on this verse. Where the Qur'an says *wa-mā adrāka* — using the past-tense form, "what appraised thee" — the explanation is then *given*: the answer follows, and the Prophet is thereby actually informed, as will happen here in verse 3: the question is asked and then satisfied, *it is the piercing star*. Where it says *wa-mā yudrīka* — using the subjunctive form, "what would apprise thee" — the matter is characteristically left undisclosed or only hinted, as in the case of the hour of judgment, of which the Prophet is told plainly that no one knows it. The difference is between a mystery about to be graciously opened and a mystery that will remain sealed. Sūrah al-Ṭāriq stands in the first class. The question of verse 2 is therefore not a wall but a door: the Qur'an first magnifies the object beyond the listener's natural reach, and then, in the very next breath, opens it — a sequence of deprivation followed by gift that is itself a miniature of revelation's whole economy. God teaches the servant first that he does not know, and only then hands him the knowing.

**A Pause Before the Answer**

Considered as rhetoric, the question functions as a held breath. The oath of verse 1 has struck the door; verse 2 stands, as it were, in the silence between the knock and the opening — a pause of one verse in which the listener's imagination is left to reach for the name and fail. This is the ancient herald's art: to announce a title, pause, and then proclaim it; the pause is what makes the proclamation land. Ibn Kathīr and other commentators observe that such questions serve to fix the attention of the audience upon what follows, impressing upon them that they are about to receive something they could not have produced themselves. The Qur'an, revealed into a culture that prized oral performance above almost all else, here out-performs the performers at their own art: where the poet would use the pause to glorify himself, the revelation uses it to glorify its subject. The Makkan listener who came to the recitation expecting entertainment found himself, at verse 2, standing at a door he could not name, waiting for something he could not predict — which is precisely the position the sūrah wants the human soul to recognize as its permanent condition.

**Night as the Hour of Divine Visitation**

The insistence on *night* — the knocker comes *by night* — is not neutral scenery. In the Qur'an's sacred geography, the night is repeatedly the hour at which the unseen visits the seen. The first revelation descended at night, in the cave of Ḥirāʾ: *"Read, in the name of thy Lord who created"* (96:1). The Qur'an as a whole was sent down in the Night of Power: *"We sent it down on the Night of Power. And what will apprise thee of the Night of Power? The Night of Power is better than a thousand months"* (97:1–3) — and note that even that descent was introduced by the same magnifying formula. The Prophet's Night Journey to the heavens occurred by night: *"Glory be to Him who carried His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque"* (17:1). Angels descend by night; mercy is sought in the last third of the night, of which the Prophet ﷺ said in the well-known transmitted tradition that God descends nearest to His servants in that hour, asking *who is calling upon Me, that I may answer him?* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah). Against this background, a "Night Visitant" carries a resonance far above astronomy: the night is when heaven knocks upon earth. The earliest community, hunted and mocked by day, could hear in verse 2 the assurance that their Lord's most decisive interventions had always come in darkness — and that the darkness they were passing through was therefore not abandonment but the customary hour before arrival.

**Epistemic Humility as Worship**

Finally, the question enacts a theology of knowledge. Before it tells the listener anything, it asks him to measure the distance between what he knows and what is knowable only by disclosure. The Makkans prided themselves on knowing — the genealogies, the stars, the roads, the markets; their caravans navigated by precisely the sky this sūrah swears by, as Sūrah Quraysh's own testimony to the winter and summer journeys shows. Yet here, at the first metaphysical claim, the Qur'an asks: *what would even apprise you?* — and the honest answer is: nothing you own; only what is told you. This is the standing position of the creature before the Creator's news: not ignorance in general, but the specific, disciplined awareness that the deepest facts — the origin of the soul, its return, its judgment — are not deducible from a caravan route. They arrive from outside, as a knock arrives from outside a closed door. The verse thus trains, in a single line, the first virtue of the listener: the humility that stands ready to receive. And the readiness is immediately rewarded — for the next word the listener hears is the answer itself.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:3

> **It is the piercing star.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Star That Drills Through the Dark**

With verse 3 the withheld answer is disclosed: *al-najm al-thāqib*, "the piercing star." The word *najm* is the ordinary Arabic term for a star, and in some classical usages for any heavenly body visible as a point of light, including what modern astronomy would classify as a planet. The operative word is the adjective. *Thāqib* is the active participle of the root *th-q-b*, whose core meaning is to pierce, bore, or perforate — the action of an awl through leather, of a drill through wood, of a lance through armor; the noun *thaqb* means a hole. A star is here named, with startling exactness, *the thing that makes holes in the dark*. The image is not of gentle illumination but of penetration: the night sky is pictured as a covering, and the star as a point of light that pierces through it. Any person who has stood in an open desert night knows the phenomenological truth of the image — the stars do not flood the sky as the sun floods the day; each one is a single bright puncture in an otherwise total blackness. The Qur'an takes the most familiar nightly sight and, with one adjective, teaches its hearers to see what they had always looked at: the dark is not empty or seamless; it is pierced, at countless points, from somewhere beyond it.

**The Same Word for the Flame That Strikes the Eavesdropper**

The adjective is not an isolated flourish; the Qur'an uses it once more, in a context that enriches this verse considerably. In Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt, describing the guarding of the lowest heaven, it says of the satans who attempt to steal a hearing of the divine assembly:

> *"…save such as snatches a fragment, whereupon a piercing flame pursues him."* (Qur'an 37:10)

There the pursuing flame is *shihābun thāqib* — a piercing blaze — built from the very same root. The two passages share a theology of light as an active, punitive, unerring agent: light in the Qur'an is not merely what enables seeing, but what strikes, hunts, and transfixes. Against the background of 37:10, the piercing star of 86:3 is no passive ornament; it belongs to the sky's appointed machinery of scrutiny and interception. The listener who connects the two — and the early audience, for whom the whole revealed corpus was being memorized verse by verse, was uniquely positioned to connect them — hears the sūrah's theme sounding already in its imagery: the sky watches, its lights are agents, and nothing crosses its order unmarked. That theme will be made explicit in the very next verse, where every soul is declared to have a guardian: the macrocosm of the guarded heavens and the microcosm of the guarded soul are set side by side, each interpreting the other.

**Which Star? The Spectrum of the Commentators**

The classical commentators divided, with productive freedom, over the star's identity. The majority reading cited in the source identifies it with the Pleiades — *al-thurayyā* — the celebrated cluster whose rising and setting the Arabs used to mark seasons and rains, and whose brilliance in the desert sky made it a natural candidate for the sharpest puncture in the dark. Others proposed Saturn; others Venus, which as the morning star literally "knocks" upon the night before the dawn, appearing low in the eastern sky in the final hour of darkness and then vanishing before the sun — fitting the *ṭāriq* of verse 1 with uncanny precision. Others still proposed a meteor, a shooting star, which pierces visibly and briefly and connects directly to the *shihāb thāqib* of 37:10; and Ālūsī records the minority view that the piercing star is any and every star that glitters, since each in its own degree strikes light through the dark. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported in the exegetical tradition to have read the term in this comprehensive sense: every star that comes by night is a ṭāriq. The disagreement itself is instructive. The Qur'an deliberately did not name a specific star; and the fact that pious, learned, Arabic-speaking interpreters could propose several candidates — all of them visible nightly to the original audience — suggests that the phrase was heard the way a well-aimed image is heard: each star in the sky is *a* piercing star; the heavens as such are the punctured veil. The verse's referent is, in the deepest sense, the whole nightly sky performing the act of piercing.

**Light from Far Away Still Arrives**

There is a quiet argument folded into the image. The star's light is old and its source remote; between the listener and the star lies a distance no Makkan could cross; yet the light arrives, enters the eye, and is *there* — present, punctual, every clear night. Ālūsī's gloss preserved in the source makes the application: in every interpretation the star is called upon because it *pierces the dark*, and al-Biqāʿī hears in that piercing an allusion to the overcoming of ignorance. The message that had come to Makkah was of exactly this kind: its Source beyond all reach, its light nonetheless arriving nightly in the recitation, striking through the accumulated darkness of centuries of unbelief — a darkness, the Qur'an elsewhere insists, that is not innocent but willful: *"God is the Guardian of those who believe; He brings them out of the darknesses into the light"* (2:257). The piercing star is thus also a portrait of revelation itself: the Qur'an came into the night of Jāhiliyyah as a star comes into the night sky — from beyond, with light, one verse at a time, puncturing where it landed. And like starlight, it could be refused only by shutting the eye: the darkness could not extinguish it, only fail to receive it.

**A Sign We Have Nearly Lost**

The verse assumes an audience that sees the sky. For the first fourteen centuries of this sūrah's life, that assumption held nearly everywhere on earth; today it fails for a large part of humanity. Artificial light has washed the stars from urban skies, and with them a nightly, free, universal sermon. The loss gives the verse a fresh application: recovering the piercing star is now, for many people, a deliberate act — driving beyond the glow, waiting for a clear dark, looking up. Those who do are often astonished at what the sūrah's first audience knew as ordinary: the sky is not black cloth but a pierced veil, crowded with arrival. The believer who stands under such a sky finds that verse 3 has prepared the experience in advance: this is the *najm thāqib*, the nightly witness, still keeping its oath-keeping appointment after three thousand years of human noise; and the God who points to it says, in effect, what He says of all His signs: *"We shall show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves, until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth"* (41:53). The horizon's sign is here; the sūrah now turns, in verse 4, from the sky's watcher to the watcher over every soul.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:4

> **Over every soul there is a guardian.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Unnoticed Companion**

The oath of verses 1–3 is followed immediately by its first consequence, introduced without connective as a weighty declaration: *in kullu nafsin lammā ʿalayhā ḥāfiẓ* — "verily, every soul — over it is a guardian." Each word is loaded. *Kullu nafsin*: every soul, without exception — believer and denier, Prophet and persecutor, the tribe's noble and its slave; the universality is absolute and is itself the point, for the sūrah is about to address people who assumed their wealth and lineage placed them beyond accounting. *Lammā* is an emphatic particle that rivets the predication: not merely "there is," but "most surely, for all that." *Ḥāfiẓ* is the active participle from the root *ḥ-f-ẓ*, whose semantic field covers guarding, keeping, preserving, watching, and maintaining from loss — the word from which come *ḥifẓ* (memorization, the keeping of the Qur'an in the chest) and *maḥfūẓ* (that which is protected, the guarded Tablet of 85:22 in the sūrah just preceding). A *ḥāfiẓ* is one engaged in continuous guarding — the participle denotes ongoing occupation, not a single act. The sentence thus states, with the brevity of law, a doctrine that reorganizes the whole of life: no soul exists unaccompanied; over each stands one whose function is to keep.

**Scribes and Shields: The Two Angelic Readings**

The classical commentators transmitted two principal identifications of the guardian, both angelic. The first reads the *ḥāfiẓ* as the recording angels, the scribes of deeds. This is the reading of the explicit parallel passages: *"And over you are watchers — noble scribes — who know whatever you do"* (82:10–12); *"Not a word does he utter, but beside him is an observer ready"* (50:18); *"Do they reckon that We hear not their secrets and private counsels? Yes indeed — and Our messengers are beside them, writing"* (43:80). On this reading the guardian is the accountant: a full, unsleeping record of every act and word, compiled against the Day when, as verse 9 of this sūrah will announce, the secrets are tested. The second reading understands the guardian as the protecting angel — the appointed defenders of the human being: *"He is the Omnipotent over His servants, and He sends forth guardians over you"* (6:61); *"For each there are those who succeed one another, before him and behind him, guarding him by God's command"* (13:11). On this reading the guardian is the shield: the soul is so valuable in God's economy that angels are posted around it, warding off harm that was not decreed. The two readings are not rivals so much as the two halves of a single doctrine — *preservation in both directions*: the soul is guarded from what would destroy it before its time, and guarded for the account that must be rendered after it. Al-Qurṭubī, surveying the views, records alongside these the understanding that the ultimate Guardian is God Himself, whose knowledge never sleeps and from whose keeping nothing is absent: *"And He is with you wherever you are"* (57:4); *"Not an atom's weight in the heavens or the earth escapes Him"* (34:3). The angels guard by His command; the guarding is finally His.

**The Watchers Who Gather at Dawn and Late Afternoon**

The Prophet ﷺ described the watchers' routine in a report of great tenderness, transmitted in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"The angels take turns among you by night and by day, and they all meet at the dawn prayer and the late afternoon prayer. Then those who stayed the night among you ascend, and their Lord asks them — though He knows best — 'In what state did you leave My servants?' They say: 'We left them praying, and we came to them praying.'"* (al-Bukhārī 555; Muslim 632)

Every detail rewards reflection. The angels *alternate* — the watch never lapses, one shift relieving another. They *meet at Fajr and ʿAsr* — the two prayers that bound the working day, which are therefore the moments at which the record of the night is closed and the record of the day is opened. And God *asks, though He knows best* — the question is not for information but for honor: He causes the watchers to testify to the watched servant's faithfulness in the very court of heaven. The verse before us, standing between the night-oath and the judgment-warning of this sūrah, places the listener inside that routine: over every soul, this very hour, the shift is standing; the record is open; the report is being written exactly as it is lived.

**From Surveillance to Companionship**

The modern mind hears "guardian over every soul" as surveillance, and recoils — yet the same modern mind lives cheerfully inside a surveillance more total than any ancient could have imagined: cameras, tracking, data profiles, permanent searchable records of every purchase and message. The verse invites a comparison that is entirely to its advantage. A corporate database watches without loving, records without understanding motive, and forgives nothing; nothing recorded in it can ever be edited, and its keeper does not know the heart. The guardian of verse 4 watches with comprehension — he knows the deed *and* the state of the soul that did it — and reports to a Lord whose final disposition toward the servant is mercy: *"Your Lord has inscribed for Himself mercy"* (6:54). For the believer, the doctrine is therefore transformed from threat into company. This is exactly the transformation the Prophet ﷺ taught when the angel Gabriel asked him, in the celebrated hadith of Gabriel, about excellence (*iḥsān*), and he answered:

> *"That you worship God as though you see Him; for if you do not see Him, surely He sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8 — the hadith of Jibrīl)

The unremitting eye, received in faith, becomes the permanent audience of the sincere — the reason a believer can be honest when no human is watching, gentle when no one would know of his harshness, and constant when hypocrisy would pay better. The watchfulness that terrifies the corrupt conscience steadies the clean one.

**Why This Verse Stands Here**

The placement is precise. The sūrah has sworn by the sky and by what comes in the night — the hour, par excellence, when people believe themselves unseen: *"They conceal themselves from people, but they cannot conceal themselves from God"* (4:108). Verse 4 arrives exactly at that point to close the escape route: the darkness in which the deed hides is lit at a frequency no eye perceives. Then verses 5–8 will descend into the origin of the soul, and verses 9–10 into its trial — and the guardian will be standing there too, his record ready. The Makkan dignitary who plotted by night against the believers, the merchant who cheated when the market was dark, the quiet idolater of the heart who imagined his inner mockery of the Prophet was his own private property — all are told, in five words, the same thing: *there is, over you, one who keeps.* The verse does not say the guardian is hostile; it does not even say he records. It says only that he is *there* — and lets each soul ask itself, in the honesty of the night, what it is like to be kept.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:5

> **So let man consider that from which he was created.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Imperative of Looking**

The Arabic opens with *fa-yanẓur* — "so let him look" — a jussive imperative of the verb *naẓara*, to look, to behold, to examine. It is a command, and its object is given in the next phrase: *mimmā khuliqa* — from what thing he was created. The Qur'an does not merely invite reflection; it legislates it. Looking — attentive, directed, honest looking — is here made an act of religion, on a par with the obligations more usually associated with scripture. The same imperative rings through the Qur'an's most famous passages of reflection: *"Say: Behold what is in the heavens and the earth"* (10:101); *"And on the earth are signs for those of certainty, and within yourselves — do you not then see?"* (51:20–21); *"In the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are signs for people of understanding — those who remember God standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth: 'Our Lord, You have not created this in vain!'"* (3:190–191). Two features of the command deserve notice. First, its direction: not upward at the stars this time, but *inward* — the sūrah has sworn by the sky, and now turns the eye to the body itself, teaching that the human creature is one of the horizons of God's signs. Second, its specificity: not "let him consider that he was created," but *from what* — the question is material and concrete, and the answer, in the next verses, will be equally concrete. Faith here is not asked to soar; it is asked to look down, at its own origin.

**Insān: The Creature Who Forgets**

The word for man is *al-insān*, and the choice is pointed. Classical scholarship debated the word's derivation, and two main lines emerged: from *uns* — intimacy, sociability, being-at-ease — because the human being is the creature of company and familiarity; or from *nisyān* — forgetting — because the human being is, of his nature, forgetful. The Qur'an's usage gives real weight to the second: *"We commanded Adam before, but he forgot, and We found in him no resolve"* (20:115); *"Be not like those who forgot God, so He made them forget themselves"* (59:19). If the derivation from forgetting is heard — and most of the classical lexicographers gave it standing — then the command of this verse is precisely the medicine for the disease named in its addressee: man, the forgetter, is commanded to remember; the creature whose origin he has forgotten is told to go and look at it again. Al-Ṭabarī's comment on this verse, preserved in the source, sets the command in its argumentative context: it is addressed especially to *those who deny the Resurrection* — let them reflect upon the manner in which God created them in the first place. The forgetter has forgotten not only where he came from but what that origin proves; the verse sends him back to the evidence.

**The Buried Verb: "He Was Created"**

The grammar conceals a sermon. The verb *khuliqa* is passive — "he was created" — and the Agent, though known to every listener, is not named in the sentence. Arabic permits the passive precisely for such effects: the doer is understood, and His very omission from the syntax of the sentence mirrors the way He has been omitted from the psychology of the creature. Man remembers himself, his works, his name, his lineage — and forgets the hand that made him. The passive verb performs that forgetting; the imperative that follows (*let him consider*) commands its cure. And the cure is not a mystical trance but a fact-check: go and look at what you came from. There is also mercy in the grammar: the same passive that indicts the forgetful soul protects it from pride — the sentence cannot be recast to say "he created himself" in any voice, active or otherwise. The door to self-authorship is closed at the level of the language itself.

**The Argument Already Begun Elsewhere**

This verse has a twin, nearly word for word, in Sūrah ʿAbasa: *"From what thing did He create him? From a drop He created him and proportioned him"* (80:18–19) — a passage likewise aimed at the denier of resurrection, and likewise moving immediately from the drop to God's power to restore. The pattern — origin cited as proof of return — is one of the Qur'an's standard arguments, deployed in many passages: *"Does not man remember that We created him before, when he was nothing?"* (19:67); *"He strikes out for Us a likeness and forgets his own creation. He says: 'Who will give life to the bones when they are decayed?' Say: 'He will give them life Who created them the first time; and He knows every creation'"* (36:78–79). The logic is a fortiori — from the lesser to the greater. If God accomplished the astonishing thing once, from nothing, without any prior model, His accomplishing it a second time, from existing material, cannot be harder; as the Qur'an states it with deliberate bluntness: *"He it is Who originates creation, then brings it back — and that is easier for Him"* (30:27). Verse 5 is thus the hinge on which the whole sūrah turns: the sky-oaths of verses 1–3 now deliver the human being to the examination stand, and the next three verses will present the evidence — a fluid, a source, an anatomy — before verse 8 draws the conclusion: He is able to bring him back.

**Created from Something — for Something**

The source commentary adds a second dimension to the command, one the verse implies but does not state: to consider *from what* one was created leads naturally to asking *for what*. The Qur'an answers that question in its own voice: *"I created jinn and mankind only that they might worship Me"* (51:56). The two questions are inseparable, because origin and purpose are given together in the same act of making; and the Sufi exegete Maybudī, quoted in the source, unfolds the purpose in a passage of luminous precision:

> *"God created a face fit for prostration, eyes fit for taking heed, a body fit for service, a heart fit for knowledge, and a secret core fit for love. So remember God's blessing upon you [2:231], for He adorned your tongues with the shahādah, your hearts with knowledge and joy, and your bodies with service and worship."* (Maybudī)

The reading turns the imperative into a mirror: to look at what you were made from is also to see what you were made for — every limb a tool shaped for its task, the whole body an instrument whose music is servanthood. The one who considers his origin honestly will find his orientation restored along with his humility.

**Looking Inward as Moderns Look Outward**

Modern humanity looks with unprecedented power outward — into cells, genomes, embryonic development — and has largely stopped looking inward at what the seeing means. The verse's command is strikingly contemporary in its implied method: *consider the evidence of your own beginning*, an evidence now displayable on a screen, from the single fertilized cell to the fully formed infant. The believer loses nothing by that inspection; the sūrah ordered it fourteen centuries before the ultrasound. What the ultrasound cannot supply is the conclusion the verse commands: that this origin, humble and unchosen, is the standing proof of a Maker, and therefore of a return. Every birth room in the world daily exhibits what Makkah's elders called impossible — a complete human being emerging from a hidden, minute beginning. The verse asks only that man grant to his own future what he watches, applauding, in every delivery ward: that what emerges from the dark and the small is not limited by the dark and the small. Let man consider — *fa-yanẓur* — and the consideration, honestly completed, humbles the proud, steadies the doubting, and silences the scoffing, all in one look.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:6

> **He was created from a gushing fluid,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Gushes: Dāfiq**

The answer to the command of verse 5 arrives in the bluntest possible terms: *khuliqa min māʾin dāfiq* — "he was created from a gushing fluid." *Māʾ* is the ordinary word for water or fluid; *dāfiq* is the active-cognate participle from the root *d-f-q*, to pour forth, to gush, to flow out with force — the same root from which Arabic takes *difq*, a torrential outpouring. The form is vividly specific: not fluid at rest, not a still pool, but fluid *in the act of gushing* — emitted, poured out, on the move. The grammatical shape matters: an active participle denotes a doer or a thing characterized by ongoing action, and here it characterizes the fluid at the very moment of its emission. The Qur'an could have used a softer or more abstract word; it chose the most physical and least dignified one available. There is a deliberate fierceness in this vocabulary, and the early listeners felt it — the sūrah has just commanded man, the forgetter, the proud, the resurrection-denying elder of Quraysh, to look at precisely this: the seminal fluid, gushing, from which he began. The theological purpose of the bluntness will become clear at once: nothing deflates the human claim to self-made grandeur like an honest look at chapter one of one's biography.

**The Qur'an's Honest Speech About Humble Origins**

This plainness of speech is not incidental; it is a consistent Qur'anic strategy against human arrogance, and it repeats with almost aggressive consistency. The fluid of this verse is elsewhere in the Qur'an called, exactly, *despised*: *"Then He made his progeny from an extract of a despised fluid"* (32:8); *"Did We not create you from a base fluid?"* (77:20); *"He created man from a drop of fluid, and behold, he is a manifest opponent"* (16:4). That last verse states the irony this sūrah is exploiting: from a drop — an open antagonist. The pattern of argument is always the same: the greater the pretension, the more pointedly the Qur'an names the origin. It told the sceptic who demanded proof of the raising of the dead to remember that he himself had once been *nothing at all* (19:67); it told the quarrelsome rejecters that they began as *"a drop of emitted semen"* (75:37). And in the sūrah immediately preceding this one in the muṣḥaf, the guardians of revelation are themselves described as God's *"honored envoys"* (80:15-16) — while man, who dares to disbelieve what they bring, is reminded in the same passage that he was created *"from a drop He created and proportioned"* (80:18–19). The lesson the vocabulary teaches is anthropology: whatever the human being becomes, he begins as something he would blush to have on his hands — and the Qur'an says so, not to degrade man, but to place his dignity where it actually lies. Man is not great because of his origin; his origin is lowly, and his greatness, where real, is entirely a gift of the One who worked the lowliness upward. Speech that hides the origin flatters; speech that names it liberates — from the exhausting performance of being self-made.

**The Drop in the Qur'an's Consistent Vocabulary**

Sūrah al-Ṭāriq's *gushing fluid* belongs to a full, stable embryological vocabulary the Qur'an deploys across many passages — *nuṭfa*, the drop; *ʿalaqa*, the clinging clot; *muḍgha*, the chewed-looking lump — a sequence nowhere stated more completely than in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn:

> *"We created man from an extract of clay; then We made him a drop in a secure lodging; then We made the drop into a clot, and We made the clot into a lump, and We made the lump into bones, and We clothed the bones in flesh; then We brought him forth as another creation. So blessed be God, the best of creators!"* (Qur'an 23:12–14)

The phrase *another creation* at the sequence's end carries the whole argument of this sūrah in three words: each stage in the passage from drop to human being is a *creation* — a new thing that was not there before, brought out of what was there — so that the human body is, by the testimony of its own history, a standing series of resurrections. The one who passed from nonexistence to drop, drop to clot, clot to formed child, has no logical ground for doubting a further passage from dust to risen body; he has already been brought forth, stage after stage, from what he previously was not. Sūrah al-Ṭāriq cites the first stage of the series — the gushing fluid — precisely because it is the humblest and therefore the most probative: the distance from *that* to the man now standing in his cloak and pride is the greatest distance in the whole sequence, and God crossed it without difficulty.

**From One Cell to a Universe of Cells**

The verse's invitation to *look* has, in the modern world, an answering technology. What the Qur'an names a gushing fluid is, in today's description, a fluid containing vast numbers of cells, of which ordinarily a single one fertilizes the ovum; that single fertilized cell then divides and differentiates — in the language of the Qur'an's own sequence, clings, lumps, bones, flesh — until it is a human being of some tens of trillions of cells, with a heart, hands, eyes that see, and a mind that reads sūrahs about its own beginning. The believer gains, in this inspection, exactly what the verse commanded: not new doctrine, but a renewed astonishment at the documented scale of the crossing. And the doctrinal conclusion strengthens rather than weakens with detail: the more precisely science describes the mechanism, the more staggering the product, and the less plausible the suggestion that the outcome was ever *nothing to speak of*. The verse does not teach embryology; it teaches *ibrah* — passage from the seen to the lesson — through embryology. The Qur'an's way is to point at the observable and ask the soul to read its Maker's signature there; the modern lens, far from removing the observable, has magnified the signature.

**The Arrogance Cure**

Finally, the verse functions as it was designed to function in its original theater: as therapy for the specific spiritual disease of the Makkan elite — *ṭughyān*, the overweening that grows with wealth and rank, of which the Qur'an says: *"Man transgresses when he sees himself self-sufficient"* (96:6–7), a passage revealed in the same early period and aimed at the same audience. Psychology recognizes the mechanism: status builds a narrative of self-authorship; self-authorship hardens into entitlement; entitlement finds the claim of judgment intolerable — *who is above me to judge me?* The gushing fluid is the permanent counter-narrative, and it is issued to every generation in the space of four words. Whatever a man's title, portfolio, following, or self-assessment, he began as something unchosen, unwilled, and undignified, poured forth and hidden in darkness; and nothing that happened afterward happened without the sustained, moment-by-moment upholding of the One who first drew him out of the drop. The man who can say this to himself, unhurried and without self-contempt, is immune both to arrogance and to despair — the two diseases the knowledge of lowly origin, rightly taken, cures together: he is too small to be proud, and too deliberately made to be worthless. Verse 5 said *let him consider*; verse 6 supplies the material for the consideration; verse 7 will add the anatomical detail, and verse 8 will state the conclusion that the material forces.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:7

> **issuing from between the loins and the pelvic arch.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Anatomical Words and Their Ancient Glosses**

Verse 7 completes the sentence of verse 6 by locating the origin of the gushing fluid: *yakhruju min bayni al-ṣulbi wa'l-tarāʾib* — "issuing from between the loins and the pelvic arch." The two anatomical terms are the verse's substance. *Ṣulb* (plural *aṣlāb*) is the backbone, the loins — the strong column of the lower back; in Qur'anic usage it is the regular word for the region in which the human being is carried in the loins of his fathers: *"God created you from dust, then from a drop; then He made you pairs"* (35:11), and *"and God brought you forth from the loins (aṣlāb) of your fathers when you were nothing"* — the idiom the Qur'an uses elsewhere for the generational chain. *Tarāʾib* is more debated: it is a plural form whose singular sense centers on the upper chest and its bones — the breastbone and the arch of ribs; and in the exegetical tradition the most poetic gloss is the one reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and his school: the *tarāʾib* are *the place of the necklace* — the upper chest of a woman where her necklace rests. The gloss matters, because on it the verse becomes a geography of two bodies: the fluid issues from between the loins of the man and the chest of the woman — that is, from the coming together of the two, the man's contribution and the woman's, whose union is the child. This is the reading the source records from al-Ṭabarī: the loins and the pelvic arch as the respective parts of the man and the woman, whose sperm and egg form the fetus. The other principal reading, which the source likewise transmits from al-Ṭabarī and al-Tustarī, restricts both terms to the male: the region of the man from which his fluid gushes. The Arabic, characteristically, accommodates both readings without deciding them; what neither reading surrenders is the essential picture: the origin of every human being is not a single point but a *between* — a convergence, an issuing-forth from a meeting.

**The Meeting of Two Lineages**

Read in the first sense, the verse quietly announces one of the Qur'an's repeated wonders: every human being is a meeting of two histories. The drop does not belong to one person; it is the confluence of a lineage carried in the loins of the fathers and a lineage carried in the bodies of the mothers — two long rivers, as it were, joining in a single confluence that is the child. The Qur'an states the dual contribution explicitly elsewhere: *"And that He creates the two kinds, male and female, from a drop of fluid when it is emitted"* (53:45–46) — the emitted drop, in that verse, the shared origin of both sexes of the child. *"He it is Who shaped you in the wombs as He wills"* (3:6); *"He fashioned the two pairs, male and female, from a drop when it is emitted"* (75:37–39). For the sūrah's argument, the two-bodied origin adds force to the humility already commanded in verse 6: man is not merely from a humble fluid, he is from a fluid that required *two other people* — and behind them four, and behind them eight — an unbroken, un-self-authored chain of meetings stretching back to the first couple. No one chose his loins or his tarāʾib; no one convened his own existence. The verse, before it says a word about resurrection, has already abolished the myth of the self-made man at the level of grammar: the human being is, originically, a *between*, a creature of relation before he is anything else.

**The One Who Knows the Hidden Origin Knows the Hidden End**

There is an argument from intimacy folded into the verse's precision. The location named — between the loins and the pelvic arch — is one of the most concealed, unobserved, and unobservable places in human experience: no eye, in the entire first era of the revelation, had ever seen the moment this verse points to. That the Qur'an names the hidden origin so specifically is a claim, in itself, about the Speaker: the One who reveals the verse is One who was *there* — who knows, with the knowledge of the Maker, the exact topography of the act of making. The Qur'an makes the same claim about the womb in general: *"God knows what every female bears, and every increase and decrease of the wombs; with Him everything has a measure"* (13:8). And it draws the exact conclusion this sūrah is building toward, in a passage that runs parallel to our own verses:

> *"Was he not a drop of emitted semen, then a clot, which He created and fashioned, and made of it the two kinds, male and female? Is not that One able to give life to the dead?"* (Qur'an 75:37–40)

The logic binds the two hidden places together: the One who knows and works within the hidden origin (the loins, the womb) necessarily knows and can work within the hidden end (the grave, the decayed bone). Both are darkness to us and daylight to Him. The sūrah's verse 9 will bring the symmetry to its climax — the Day when *secrets* (sarāʾir) are tested — and the reader should feel the thread already: the God who names the body's most secret origin is announcing, symmetrically, that He will name the heart's most secret contents. Nothing in the human being, physical or moral, is hidden from the One who describes his making.

**Modern Ears and an Old Precision**

Modern readers, taught by biology, sometimes hear in the verse a resonance with facts unknown at the time of revelation: in the human embryo, the organs that will produce the reproductive cells arise in precisely the region between the backbone and the upper abdomen, behind the organs of the loins, in the territory where the kidneys are formed — the gonads begin their development anchored in that upper retroperitoneal region, and only later descend, in the male, to their mature position, trailing their blood supply, which originates from the aorta between the kidneys — that is, from between the backbone and the chest. Those who note this correspondence are entitled to their wonder, and some careful students of the Qur'an have advanced it; but honesty about the exegetical history is the better part of reverence. The classical readings given above — the two-body reading, the one-body reading — were articulated by the earliest commentators from the plain sense of the Arabic, centuries before anatomy could either confirm or complicate them, and none of them depends on modern knowledge. The soundest position combines gratitude and restraint: the verse's own claim, on any reading, is that God names the hidden place of man's origin exactly; the modern findings, whatever else they show, do not embarrass that claim — a phrase of the fourteenth century that no one then could check still stands inspection by instruments its hearers never imagined. A word that survives being looked at more and more closely for a thousand years is, at minimum, unlike ordinary speech.

**The Dignity of Being Known So Exactly**

It remains to ask what this precision is *for*, devotionally — and the answer is in the direction of comfort as much as of humbling. To be known in one's origin, exactly and before witnesses, is to have never been lost. No human being is an accident of history, a surplus birth, a chance convergence: the One who names the loins and the tarāʾib names *this* meeting, *this* child. The Qur'an's doctrine of the individual providence of God rests here: *"And when your Lord took from the children of Adam, from their loins, their seed, and made them testify of themselves: 'Am I not your Lord?' They said: 'Yes, we testify'"* (7:172) — the same anatomical word, *aṣlāb*, bearing the weight of the covenant itself. Man was known, named, and covenanted in the very place this sūrah describes. The knowledge that humbles the arrogant is the same knowledge that steadies the insignificant: you were not too hidden, too small, or too far back for God to know exactly where you came from — and the God of that knowledge is the one about whom verse 8 will now state the consequence: He is able to bring you back.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:8

> **Truly He is able to bring him back,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rajʿ: The Theology of Return**

Verse 8 draws the conclusion the evidence of verses 5–7 was assembled to force: *innahu ʿalā rajʿihi la-qadīr* — "truly He is able to bring him back." The key noun is *rajʿ*, from the root *r-j-ʿ*, to return, to go back, to restore to a former place. Theologically this is richer than "revive": the resurrection is not described as the manufacture of a new thing but as the *return* of an existing traveler to his point of departure. The human being is, in the Qur'an's persistent picture, a creature in transit — sent forth from God, sojourning in the world, and going back to God: *"O soul at rest, return to your Lord, content and contenting — enter among My servants, and enter My Garden"* (89:27–30) — the very word of return again, *irjiʿī*. Death, in this idiom, is not annihilation but the turning of the road; and the Day of Resurrection is the arrival at the beginning. The emphasis structure of the Arabic is double — *inna…la-* — the strongest form of asseveration: no dubiety remains on the divine side of the sentence. The only remaining question, which the verse's grammar quietly leaves open, is on the human side: whether the listener will order his life as one who is returning. And *qadīr*, the last word — from *q-d-r*, power joined with measured extent — asserts not merely that God *can* in principle, but that the power is His in full, practiced, proven measure.

**The A Fortiori Logic the Qur'an Repeats**

The argument of this verse is the argument the Qur'an deploys more than any other against resurrection-denial, and it deserves to be seen as a piece of logic rather than a piece of rhetoric. Its form is *a fortiori* — from the stronger to the weaker, or in this case from the harder to the easier. Creating from nothing is harder than reassembling what already existed; therefore the One who did the first can do the second. The Qur'an states the inference outright: *"He it is Who originates creation, then brings it back — and that is easier for Him"* (30:27); *"Does not man remember that We created him before, when he was nothing?"* (19:67); *"Say: He will give them life Who created them the first time; and He knows every creation"* (36:79); *"Do they not see that God, Who created the heavens and the earth and was not wearied by their creation, is able to give life to the dead?"* (46:33). Verse 6 supplied the premise for this sūrah's version of the argument: the gushing fluid. The Makkan skeptic who admitted the sky, the earth, the camel, and his own body — but not the raising of the dead — was being asked to accept four miracles and balk at the fifth, smaller one; and the Qur'an's repeated reply is to place the fifth in the series of the admitted four. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss on verse 5 — that the consideration is addressed to those who deny the Resurrection — confirms that the whole passage is constructed as a single proof: origin observed, power inferred, return asserted.

**The Crushed Bone at the Prophet's Hand**

The exegetical tradition preserves scenes of how concretely this debate was joined. It relates that one of the staunchest deniers, ʿUbayy ibn Khalaf — later to be killed at Badr, the sūrah's final verse foreshadowing the same outcome — came to the Prophet ﷺ carrying a decayed bone, which he crumbled to dust in his hand and flung to the wind, saying: *who will give life to these, when they have decayed?* The reply came down upon him as revelation: *"He strikes out for Us a likeness and forgets his own creation. He says: 'Who will give life to the bones when they are decayed?' Say: 'He will give them life Who created them the first time; and He knows every creation"* (36:78–79) — reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and others in the commentary literature. The gesture was the whole objection in miniature: the denier's case was never an argument but an exhibition — *look how thoroughly gone it is.* The Qur'an's answer is likewise an exhibition, pointed in the opposite direction: *look how thoroughly arrived you were, from nothing.* The crushed bone and the gushing fluid are the two exhibits in the same courtroom; the sūrah simply turns the jury's head from the first to the second.

**Sleep: The Resurrection Rehearsed Every Night**

Beyond argument, the Qur'an offers every human being a nightly personal rehearsal of death and raising: sleep. *"God takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that have not died in their sleep; He retains those on which He has decreed death, and releases the others until an appointed term. In that are signs for a people who reflect"* (39:42). Sleep is described as a partial taking; waking, as a giving back; and the verse names this cycle, with its explicit framing, *signs for a people who reflect*. Everyone who rises from bed has experienced a return from a state in which he was, to all outward appearance, gone — unconscious, unresponsive, his body continued by another Breath than his own will. The Prophet ﷺ institutionalized the lesson in the very liturgy of waking, teaching the believer to say upon rising: *Praise be to God who gave us life after He caused us to die, and to Him is the resurrection* — the formula transmitted in the collections from Abū Hurayrah (al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-Daʿawāt). Thus the doctrine of verse 8 is not an exotic future claim only; it is the meaning assigned to a universal daily experience. The God who returns the sleeper every morning asks, through this sūrah, why the skeptic grants Him the little return and withholds the great one.

**Ibn Taymiyyah: Habit Is Not Reason**

The deeper structure of resurrection-denial received one of its most durable analyses from Ibn Taymiyyah. His argument, pressed across his works, is that the denier's certainty rests on *habit (ʿāda)*, not on reason: the skeptic has seen people die and stay dead countless times, and habit therefore whispers that death is final. But habit, Ibn Taymiyyah insists, is not a logical proof — the non-occurrence of an event in one's experience cannot establish its impossibility; it can only report that one has not seen it. The resurrection's possibility is undamaged by the absence of precedent, for the One whose power is in question is precisely the One who broke precedent at every first beginning — the first human being, the first rain, the first spring. Reason, honestly applied, cannot rule out what the Creator announces; it can only confess that the matter lies within a power whose earlier performances it can inspect but not replicate. On this analysis, verse 8's double-emphasis — *innahu…la-qadīr* — is exactly the sentence the habitual mind needs: not a new datum, but the correction of a category error — the error of measuring the Maker's future by the creature's past.

**Remembering Death to Live Uprightly**

The verse's practical work is the re-ordering of life under the fact of return. The Prophet ﷺ taught the discipline in one compressed counsel:

> *"Remember often the effacer of pleasures."* — meaning death (al-Tirmidhī 2307, from Abū Hurayrah; and others)

Not a morbid fixation, but a frequency: the knowledge of return kept near enough to arbitrate decisions — the unfair gain, the postponed repentance, the word of cruelty that seemed costless. A life lived facing the fact of verse 8 is neither anxious nor grim; it is simply *oriented*, as a traveler is oriented, and therefore both serious about the journey and light-hearted about its trivia. The sūrah has now secured its central claim: He is able to bring him back. Verse 9 will ask the question the claim immediately raises — *and then what?* — and will answer with the one word that explains why the whole proof mattered: a Day.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:9

> **on the Day when secrets are tested,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Balāʾ and Sirr: The Testing of the Hidden**

Verse 9 attaches to verse 8's assertion of return by a single defining word — *yawma*, "on the Day" — and then names what that Day does: *tublá al-sarāʾir*, "when the secrets are tested." The vocabulary is exact. *Sarāʾir* is the plural of *sirr*, a secret — that which is sealed (*s-r-r*, related to the idea of a tightly drawn or hidden thing); it names the innermost contents of the heart and mind, the intentions, motives, and hidden deeds that no other human being has ever witnessed. *Tublá* is the passive of the verb from the root *b-l-w*, the root of *balāʾ* — the test, the trial, the proving. The same root gives the word for testing gold in fire: *ibtilāʾ* is what separates true metal from counterfeit. The Day, in other words, does not merely *reveal* secrets — a weaker verb would have served that purpose — it *tests* them: subjects them to a criterion that establishes what they really were. Al-Zamakhsharī, as the source records, understands the secrets broadly: all that is hidden in the heart and mind, and all of one's deeds — the hidden act as well as the hidden motive. The passive voice is also a sermon in itself: the secrets will *be tested* — the construction spares us the spectacle of God rummaging; there is simply a Day whose nature is assay, as fire's nature is heat.

**The Crucible, Not the Spotlight**

The difference between *testing* and *exposing* is the difference between a court and a gossip column, and the Qur'an chose the court. Exposure merely displays; testing *evaluates*. Consider the image the root supplies: an assayer does not drag the ingot into the town square; he takes it to the crucible, where heat shows what proportion of the metal was gold and what was alloy — not by opinion, but by melting point. That is what the Day does to secrets: the charity that looked like generosity may, under the Day's heat, prove to have been vanity with an accountant's discipline; the quiet, unremarkable kindness no one photographed may prove pure throughout. The same fire that vindicates one secret convicts another. And the Day's assay is comprehensive, because the hidden life is the *substance* of moral life: the visible act is only the last inch of a long interior intention. This is why the Qur'an can judge hearts, not merely deeds — *"whether you disclose what is in your breasts or conceal it, God knows it"* (3:29) — and why the verse's passive construction is so calm: nothing needs to be argued on that Day. The secret, tested, testifies for itself.

**"Your Secrets Are Your Deeds": Muʿādh's Question**

The tradition preserves a remarkable Prophetic dialogue on this very verse, transmitted in the commentary of al-Tustarī and cited in the source. The Companion Muʿādh ibn Jabal — celebrated for his piety and knowledge — asked the Prophet ﷺ what these secrets are, for which God's servants will be tested:

> *"O Messenger of God, what are these secrets for which the servants will be tested on the Day of Resurrection? He said: Your secrets are your deeds: such as prayer, fasting, alms, the minor ablution, the major ablution, and all the obligatory acts. All deeds are hidden secrets. For if a man wishes, he says, 'I have prayed,' though he has not prayed; and if he wishes, he says, 'I performed the ablution,' though he has not performed it."*

The Prophet's answer relocates the secret from the motive *behind* deeds to the deed *itself*: the acts of worship are hidden — the prayer prayed at home, the fast carried silently, the ablution no one witnessed — because their performance is known, in the first instance, only to the one who performed them and to the Guardian of verse 4. A person can lie about them indefinitely; society can audit none of them. Hence they are pre-eminently the *sarāʾir*, and the Day is pre-eminently their audit: on it, the record no court could check is checked. The report's closing logic is quietly devastating: the man who claims a prayer he did not pray has banked on the one assumption this sūrah exists to destroy — that the hidden stays hidden.

**The Witnesses No Court Can Silence**

The testing of secrets does not proceed by interrogation, for the Qur'an describes a Day on which the creation itself testifies. The members of the body will speak: *"That Day We shall seal their mouths, and their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to do"* (36:65); *"…and their tongues, their hands, and their feet will testify against them concerning what they used to do"* (24:24). The skin itself will be called: *"…until, when they reach it, their hearing, their eyes, and their skins will testify against them concerning what they used to do. And they will say to their skins: 'Why did you testify against us?' They will say: 'God, who gives speech to all things, made us speak'"* (41:20–21). Even the ground will publish its memory: *"That Day it will recount its tidings, for your Lord has inspired it"* (99:4–5). The psychological insight is exact and modern: the person is not the sole custodian of his own record; his acts were inscribed in bodies, in places, in others — and on the Day the whole inscription rises. The defendant cannot take the stand in his own defense because he is surrounded by witnesses who were there: his own hands, his own feet, his own skin, his own earth.

**Intention: the Secret Beneath the Secret**

Beneath the hidden deed lies the hidden motive, and the Qur'an's doctrine of the Day probes to that depth. The foundational Prophetic sentence governs the whole field:

> *"Deeds are only by intentions, and every man shall have only what he intended."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907 — the first hadith of the Ṣaḥīḥ of al-Bukhārī, from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb)

Two men may perform outwardly identical prayers, and the assay of the Day assign them opposite values — one accepted, one rejected — because the secret intention differed: one sought God, the other an audience. This is the furnace's true work: nothing else in the universe can grade the interior of an act. The Prophet ﷺ gave the standard in his description of the seven whom God will shade on the Day when there is no shade but His — among them *"a man who gives charity so secretly that his left hand knows not what his right hand spends"* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031). Note the arithmetic of secrecy: not merely hidden from people, but hidden from one's own other hand — intention so purified that even the self's accounting is silenced. Such a secret, tested on the Day, is the one kind that *wants* to be tested: its whole life was a preparation for exactly that fire.

**Terror and Comfort in One Furnace**

The verse is therefore double-edged by design. For the concealed wrong, it is terror: the abused child, the defrauded widow, the whispered slander, the plotted betrayal — all the wrongs that human courts never reached — meet, on this Day, the one Court that has the transcript. The Qur'an's repeated consolation to the wronged rests here: *"And do not think God is heedless of what the wrongdoers do"* (14:42). For the concealed good, it is vindication: the believer whose worship was dismissed as fanaticism, whose decency went uncredited, whose quiet spending was unknown, is crowned in the only hearing that matters. And for the ordinary ambivalent soul — most of humanity — it is the summons to interior honesty *now*, while the record is still being written: to pray as one would pray if the Guardian were seen, to give as one would give in the open court, and thus to convert the Day's assay from dread into appointment. The modern condition adds a poignant accent: an age that curates every visible self has never had more hidden material. The gap between the profile and the person is precisely the *sirr* — and verse 9 announces the Day that closes the gap forever, publishing, at last, the truth of each.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:10

> **whereat man has neither strength nor helper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Quwwa and Nāṣir: The Two Last Refuges**

The verse describing the Day now describes man upon it, in a negation engineered with exacting care: *famā lahu min quwwatin wa-lā nāṣir* — "he has neither strength nor helper." The two nouns are not synonyms filling out a line; they partition the entire human security-system into its two final components. *Quwwa* is strength, power, capacity — the internal resource: the reserves of the body, will, wealth, ability; whatever a person can *do for himself*. *Nāṣir* is the helper, from the root *n-ṣ-r*, to aid, to grant victory — the external resource: allies, patrons, kin, armies, advocates; whatever others can *do for him*. Every strategy of self-preservation ever devised reduces to one of these two: either more capability, or more company. The negation is doubly total: *mā lahu min* — "he has not [so much as] any of" strength; and the emphatic *wa-lā nāṣir* — "nor any helper at all." Arabic possesses no stronger way to say *nothing and no one*. The verse thus stands as the systematic demolition, in advance, of every plan the human being might carry into that Day — as though the Qur'an had enumerated the escape routes beforehand and posted a guard on each: your body will not serve you, and your network will not attend.

**The Day Alliances Dissolve**

The theme is one of the Qur'an's most repeated verdicts about the Day, and the repetition itself is pastoral — a surgeon repeating a diagnosis until the patient stops bargaining. *"And fear a Day when no soul shall avail another at all, and no intercession shall be accepted from it, nor ransom taken from it, and they shall not be helped"* (2:48). *"The Day when no patron shall avail a patron in anything, and they shall not be helped"* (44:41). *"The Day when neither wealth nor children avail, save one who comes to God with a sound heart"* (26:88–89). *"And We shall turn their hearts and their eyes away, as they refused to believe in it the first time"* — no, the sure word is plainer still: *"Close friends on that Day will be enemies to one another, except the reverent"* (43:67). The picture is of an entire social architecture — the architecture on which worldly life is wholly premised — deconstructing in an hour: patronage, friendship, purchase, advocacy, each dissolving at the boundary it cannot cross. The Qur'an even scripts the futile attempts: the wrongdoer offering *"his children as ransom, and his wife and his brother, and his kin who sheltered him, and whoever is in the earth, all of it, to save himself"* (70:11–14) — the verb of the last clause being exactly our root: *in yuftadā* — as though he would buy himself out, and cannot. The verse before us is the summary of all those scenes: strength, helper — none.

**What About Intercession?**

The question arises immediately, for the Qur'an also affirms intercession: *"Who shall intercede with Him except by His permission?"* (2:255); *"Intercession is of no avail with Him, except for one to whom He gives leave"* (34:23). The reconciliation is doctrinally precise, and the classical commentators held it steadily: what is denied is *autonomous* intercession — the helping, in the patronage-economy of this world, that a powerful creature bestows by his own discretion, purchased by kinship, fee, or favor. What is affirmed is *authorized* intercession — granted solely by God's permission, solely to whom He permits, solely for whom He accepts, an act of His mercy wearing the form of advocacy. The Prophet ﷺ was granted the great intercession on that Day — the station of praise he will occupy when all creation, from Adam onward, declines to intercede and points to him — as the long hadith of intercession, transmitted from Anas ibn Mālik, relates in al-Bukhārī and Muslim. The logic of verse 10 is therefore untouched: on that Day no one *has* a helper as of right; if help reaches anyone, it is because the Judge Himself authorized it — which is not a resource the defendant brings, but a mercy the Court extends. The one who spent this life collecting patrons while neglecting the Patron of all patrons arrives to find his rolodex void; the one who spent this life with God arrives to find that God has not waited to be petitioned.

**The Mercy of Losing False Refuges**

Seen from within faith, the verse's demolition is not cruelty but surgery. False refuges are not neutral furniture in the soul; they actively deform it — the man who trusts his strength becomes harsh, and the man who trusts his allies becomes servile, and both forget God. The Qur'an names the forgetting as the very signature of the hypocrite: *"They took their oaths as a shield, and turned away from the way of God; evil is what they used to do"* (58:16) — oaths as *shield*: the manufactured helper. Against this whole economy the sūrah sets one alternative, stated in a single Qur'anic clause: *"And whoever trusts in God, He is sufficient for him"* (65:3). The Prophet ﷺ gave the alternative its most loved form:

> *"If you were to rely upon God as He ought to be relied upon, He would provide for you as He provides for the birds: they go out in the morning hungry, and return in the evening full."* (al-Tirmidhī 2344, from ʿUmar; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

Note what the hadith does not say: that the birds do not go out. Trust is not passivity; it is effort with the heart's weight resting on God rather than on provision. The verse's negation, then — no strength, no helper — is the negative space around the one positive that remains: when every other column falls, one Support stands, and the falling of the others is what finally lets the soul lean on it with its full weight. The Qur'an will say of the dwellers of the Fire, in words that join this verse by its own final word: *and for the wrongdoers there is no helper (wa-mā li-l-ẓālimīna min naṣīr)* (35:37).

**A Civilization Built on Borrowed Strength**

The verse reads with special force over a modern world that has professionalized both refuges. *Quwwa* has become insurance, retirement portfolios, deterrence, health metrics, emergency funds; *nāṣir* has become networks, legal representation, alliances, diplomacy, and the ever-expandable contact list. None of these is condemned in itself — the Qur'an legislates contracts and permits preparedness; Joseph stored Egypt's grain. But the verse asks the standing question of where the *ultimate* trust is parked, and it answers with a thought experiment that no modern instrument survives: transport the entire portfolio to the Day of verse 9. The policy does not underwrite that Day; the network does not attend it; the representation ends at its threshold; and the strength of the body — the most personally owned asset of all — is the very item being reclaimed. A person's entire civilization, in that hour, is worth exactly what verse 10 says it is worth: nothing and no one. The believer performs no nihilism with this knowledge; he simply holds his instruments loosely, knowing their jurisdiction ends at a line that is certain, drawn by the One who described the far side of it first. *He has neither strength nor helper* — and the sūrah, having stripped the human being bare before his Judge, now turns back to the sky, and swears again.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:11

> **By the resurgent sky,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Oath: From Watching to Returning**

With verse 11 the sūrah's ring structure completes its second span: the first oath-cluster (sky and Night Visitant, vv. 1–3) had opened the argument about the soul's origin and judgment (vv. 4–10); now a second oath-cluster returns to the heavens before the argument's final movement (vv. 13–17). The new oath is a single participial phrase: *wa'l-samāʾi dhāti al-rajʿ* — "by the sky possessing return." The grammar itself teaches. The sky is not sworn by as a mere object but as *dhāt* — the possessor, the owner — of a property. In Qur'anic usage *dhāt* names the essential, abiding character of a thing, its what-it-*is*; and here what the sky *is*, is *rajʿ* — return. The heavens are invoked not as scenery but as a system defined by recurrence: whatever else the sky may be, it is the thing that comes back. And the noun is the very noun of verse 8: God's power over man's *rajʿ* — his return — is sworn by the sky's *rajʿ*; the same word knits the doctrine to its witness. The sūrah's editorship of its own vocabulary is deliberate: resurrection has already been defined as return, and now the oath summons the vastest observable instance of return in human experience to stand beside the doctrine.

**What Returns: Rain, Light, and the Cycles**

What exactly does the sky return? The classical commentators, as the source records, gave a cluster of answers around one center. The majority — Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Tustarī among them — read the return as *the rain*: the sky is the possessor of return because the rain returns to it and from it, again and again, in its seasons; the same reading hears in *rajʿ* the clouds that circle back bearing their loads. Al-Ṭabarī transmits as well the reading of the celestial cycles: the sun, the moon, and the stars return — rising after setting, night after day, the whole luminous procession of the heavens cycling back upon itself without fail. The readings interlock rather than compete: rain is the sky's return *to the earth*, the lights are its return *upon itself*, and both are the same property observed at different scales. Behind them all stands the phenomenology that any pre-modern listener possessed: the sky is the most punctually reliable thing in human experience. Springs fail, wells go dry, kingdoms fall — the sky comes back. To swear by the *returning* sky is to swear by the single most persistent recurrence the race has ever observed.

**The Hydrological Sermon**

The rain-reading opens into the Qur'an's most extended natural analogy for resurrection — the revivification of the dead land — which it deploys in passage after passage:

> *"And We sent down from the sky blessed water, and caused to grow thereby gardens and the grain of the harvest, and the tall date-palms with ranged clusters — provision for the servants; and thereby We gave life to a land that was dead. Like that will be the going forth."* (Qur'an 50:9–11)

The final clause — *like that will be the going forth (kadhālika al-khurūj)* — states the analogy outright: your emergence from the graves will be like the land's emergence from drought, because the same Hand performs both. The Qur'an binds the two events together again and again: *"And of His signs is that you see the earth humble, and when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells; He who gives it life will surely give life to the dead; and He is powerful over everything"* (41:39); *"He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living, and revives the earth after its death — and like that will you be brought forth"* (30:19). The argument is empirical in the most generous sense: the resurrection of the world is not a theory but a spectacle, performed annually on every arable acre of the planet, attended by every farmer who ever watched rain fall on cracked ground and waited. The skeptic who has watched a hundred springs is not being asked for a leap; he is being asked to count.

**Rajʿ Twice: the Water's Return and Man's**

The word-economy of the sūrah deserves one more look, because it carries the argument. Verse 8 used *rajʿ* for man: God is able to *return* him. Verse 11 uses *rajʿ* for the sky: the sky is the possessor of *return*. The pairing is a designed rhyme of doctrine and cosmos: the same root, first as promise, then as proof. The logic is exactly parallel to the sūrah's first movement — where the *gushing fluid* of verses 6–7 served as the visible precedent for the invisible return — except that the scale has now been raised from the private body to the public world. Then the evidence was *your own origin*; now the evidence is *everyone's environment*. The skeptic is enclosed: the proof stands both inside his skin and outside his window. And the rain-reading adds a refinement the fluid-argument could not carry: the water that returns in spring had *departed* — evaporated, vanished, written off — before it returned. The earth's resurrection is not merely annual novelty; it is the visible restoration of what had visibly gone away, which is precisely the resurrection-denier's stated impossibility: *gone means gone.* The sky has been refuting that aphorism since the third day of creation, twice a year, over every field on earth.

**A Cosmos That Runs on Return**

The oath's reach extends, without strain, into the modern picture of the universe, for the property it names — recurrence — turns out to be the physical world's fundamental operating principle. Meteorology formalized it as the water cycle: evaporation, transport, condensation, return — the rajʿ of the sky as a measurable planetary process, so constant that the total water of the earth has circled through it uncounted times. Astronomy formalized it further: the cycles of day and year; the recycling of stellar matter itself, in which the heavy elements of which bodies are made were forged inside earlier stars and scattered by their deaths — the very atoms of the listener were, quite literally, returned to the universe by a previous generation of suns. Modern ecology is, at bottom, the study of return: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, each cycling through living things and back, because a world of one-way flows would be a world that had already ended. The sūrah's oath thus names what twelve centuries of subsequent science would confirm as the cosmos's signature: it is a *dhāt al-rajʿ* all the way down. The believer may hear verse 11 with full modern ears and find the oath stronger, not weaker, than at its first sounding: the heavens do not merely witness to return; they are made of it. Verse 12 will now summon the oath's second member — the earth — and show the same property on the ground: not the water that comes back, but the seed that comes out.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:12

> **and by the furrowed earth,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ṣadʿ: The Crack That Lets Life Out**

The oath's second member stands in exact parallel to the first: *wa'l-arḍi dhāti al-ṣadʿ* — "and by the earth possessing the crack." The construction repeats *dhāt* from verse 11: the earth, too, is sworn by as the possessor of a property, and the property it owns is *ṣadʿ* — from the root *ṣ-d-ʿ*, to split, to crack, to cleave open. The noun names the fissure that opens in soil: the split of parched ground after rain swells it; the seam that parts when a shoot forces its way to the surface; the furrow itself, the crack of the ploughed field, which Arabic idiom calls *ṣudūʿ*. The image-system is exact and, like *ṭāriq*, is built from a verb of striking and parting: as the Night Visitant strikes upon the closed door of the sky's darkness, the seed strikes upon the closed surface of the earth — and the earth, faithful to its nature, *splits*. The oath therefore summons not the earth as ground, but the earth as the thing that opens. And the parallelism with verse 11 is a completed argument in two lines: the sky possesses the *return* of the water, the earth possesses the *splitting* for the growth — heaven gives from above, earth opens from below, and between the two operations stands every plant that has ever lived. Rain without splitting runs off; earth without rain cannot split to purpose; the pair is a single machine in two members.

**The Buried Seed's Small Resurrection**

The Qur'an elsewhere makes the seed itself the explicit type of resurrection, using the same root of splitting as an attribute of God: *"God is the Splitter (fāliq) of the grain and the date-stone; He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living — that is God; so how are you perverted?"* (6:95). Note what the splitting is: the seed — alive but finished, sealed in its shell, its story apparently over — is *broken open*, and what emerges is not the seed but the seed's future: a green thing the seed never was. The seed does not merely revive; it is *exceeded*. The same logic governs the sūrah's most intimate parallel passage in Sūrah ʿAbasa, which commands the same look this sūrah commanded in verse 5, and then walks the listener through the proof:

> *"Let man look at his food: We pour down water abundantly, then split the earth in clefts, and cause grain to grow therein, and grapes and herbage, and olives and date-palms, and lush gardens, and fruits and pastures — provision for you and your cattle."* (Qur'an 80:24–32)

The sequence — water poured, earth split, life issued — is the oath-pair of verses 11–12 expanded into a paragraph. And the phenomenology is one every agrarian listener owned: to farm is to bury, and to harvest is to receive back what you buried, transformed, multiplied, and raised. The farmer is, liturgically, the daily practitioner of resurrection-faith: he has personally put things into the ground and watched them come out.

**The Field as a Foreshadowed Graveyard**

Here is where the oath's force becomes unnerving for its first audience, in exactly the way the sūrah intends. The Makkan objection to resurrection was, at bottom, an objection about *burial*: the body goes into the ground, the ground consumes it, and what the ground has taken the ground keeps. The Qur'an's answer, in this oath, is not to argue with the picture but to *invert its ending*: the ground you trust to keep the dead is the same ground you have watched, your whole life, give back everything buried in it alive. Burial is not, in the Qur'an's vision, the signature of ending; it is the signature of sowing. The grave is a furrow. And the oath dares the listener to hold the two burials apart — the seed that comes up and the friend who does not — when the same God is named as the Splitter of both. The Qur'an presses the point precisely: *"And among His signs is that you see the earth humble; and when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells; He who gives it life will surely give life to the dead; and He is powerful over everything"* (41:39). The earth's humility is dry ground lying low, waiting; its stirring is the swelling of wet soil; its swelling breaks — *ṣadʿ* — into green. The scene is annual, universal, free to watch, and, says the Qur'an, signed with the same power that will one day make the cemeteries do what the furrows do.

**Water and Soil: the Completed Pair**

The pairing of verses 11 and 12 also quietly completes the earlier pairing of verses 6 and 7. There, a fluid descended from between the loins and the chest into a hidden lodging; here, a water descends from the sky into the split earth — and the sūrah has thereby built two complete, rhyming cycles of creation: the private cycle (drop, lodging, emergence as a living child) and the public cycle (rain, soil, emergence as living growth). The parallels are not ornamental: in both, life begins as a *descent into darkness*; in both, the place of descent must *open* for life to issue — the womb delivers, the soil splits; in both, what emerges is *greater than what entered* — from the drop, a man; from the seed, a forest. And every human being, by being born, has already been on the inside of the private cycle: each birth is a splitting-open (*ṣadʿ*) of the firm lodging — the womb — and a going forth (*khurūj*, 50:11) of what was hidden within. Man does not merely observe the resurrection-analogy; he has *starred* in it. The first experience of every soul now living was emergence from a dark enclosure into light and breadth — the very transition the skeptic declares impossible at the grave. The oath-pair of verses 11–12 thus restates, at the scale of the world, what verses 6–7 stated at the scale of the body: the God of both cycles is demonstrating, in public and in private, the exact operation He is disbelieved about.

**Furrows Made by Human Hands**

One human detail deepens the sign. The classical gloss on *ṣadʿ* includes the furrows of cultivation — and a furrow, unlike a natural crack, is *made*: the farmer's plough deliberately opens the earth precisely so that the burial-and-return cycle can be run under his management. Agriculture is thus humanity's ancient, conscious partnership with the oath's property: for ten thousand years, on every continent, people have been cutting ṣudūʿ into the ground, dropping seeds into the splits, and banking on the return. No farmer, in all that time, has ever sown in the conviction that the seed will not come forth; the entire vocation rests on faith in the split earth and the returning sky. The sūrah's oath therefore invokes not merely a natural process but the oldest human collaboration with that process — a collaboration so trusted that humanity staked its bread upon it. The denier of resurrection has spent his life eating from the very cycle he refuses to trust; the bread on his table was a seed in a furrow a season ago. *By the returning sky, by the splitting earth* — the witnesses have now been sworn, their testimony is in, and verse 13 is ready to pronounce what they were summoned to certify: a decisive Word.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:13

> **truly it is indeed a decisive Word.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qawl Faṣl: Speech That Cuts**

With verse 13 the oaths reach their object. The Arabic carries a double emphasis — *innahu la-qawlun faṣl* — "truly it is indeed a decisive word." *Faṣl* is a noun of the root *f-ṣ-l*, whose field covers separating, dividing, deciding: a *faṣl* is a division between chapters; a *faṣl* in the body is the articulation of a joint; *faṣl al-khiṭāb* — literally the severing of speech — is the Arabic idiom for the word that settles a dispute, the pronouncement that cuts through entanglement to the decision. The Qur'an honors the idiom in its praise of David: *"…and We strengthened his kingdom and gave him wisdom and decisive speech (faṣl al-khiṭāb)"* (38:20). To call something *qawl faṣl*, then, is to call it speech of the deciding kind: not speech that adds an opinion to the pile, but speech that *divides the pile* — separating true from false, right from wrong, and — as its use here, directly after the oath-pair of heaven and earth, signals — separating those who hear and humble themselves from those who hear and scheme. The word is kin to the Qur'an's great self-designation *Furqān*, the Criterion: *"Blessed is He Who sent down the Criterion upon His servant, that he might be a warner to the worlds"* (25:1); *"…a guidance for mankind, and clear proofs of the guidance, and the Criterion"* (2:185). A criterion is not a new fact so much as a new *edge*: the instrument by which facts already present are told apart.

**The Word or the Day: Two Readings of the Faṣl**

What precisely is this decisive Word? The commentators, as the source records, transmit two principal answers, both classical. The first reading says: it is the Qur'an itself — the Book now being recited, the message of which this very sūrah is a part. On this reading, verse 13 is the disclosure of what all four oaths were sworn about: by the sky, the Night Visitant, the returning heaven, and the splitting earth — that *this* recitation is decisive speech from God, not poetry, not soothsaying, not tales of the ancients. The second reading says: it is the Day of Judgment — the decision itself, the verdict that parts destinies. This reading is anchored in the Qur'an's own fixed epithet for that Day: *yawm al-faṣl*, the Day of Decision — *"This is the Day of Decision; We have gathered you and the ancients"* (37:21); *"…and the Day of Decision between them"* (44:40); *"…the Day of Decision"* (77:13, 77:38); and the sister-oath of Sūrah al-Nabaʾ: *"Surely the Day of Decision is appointed"* (78:17). The two readings do not so much compete as identify cause and effect: the Word decides *now*, in history, each time it is heard and accepted or heard and refused; the Day decides *then*, publicly and finally, everything the Word had already cut. The sūrah's own structure joins them — the oaths about creation and resurrection (vv. 1–12) certify the Word (v. 13), and the Word announces the Day (v. 9); each is decisive, and each derives its decisiveness from the Speaker.

**The Prophet's Description Cited in the Commentaries**

The reading that the decisive Word is the Qur'an carries a Prophetic description preserved in the commentary literature, cited by Ālūsī in the source: the Prophet ﷺ said of the Qur'an, in the phrasing there transmitted, that *"it is a statement that separates right from wrong."* The description states with total simplicity what the term *faṣl* technically means, and it locates the Qur'an's claim exactly where its first audience contested it. The Makkans did not deny that the Prophet spoke; they denied that what he spoke *decided* anything — *"Tales of the ancients, which he has written down"* (25:5), *"confused dreams"*, *"nay, he has invented it"* (21:5). Their theory of the recitation was that it was one more voice in the market of voices — to be enjoyed, mocked, or ignored, but not *obeyed as a verdict*. Verse 13, backed by the sworn testimony of sky, star, rain, and seed, asserts the opposite: this speech does not join the market; it judges the market. Every other utterance in the listener's experience — the poet's ode, the soothsayer's mutter, the elder's counsel — offered positions within the world; this Word speaks as the world's *owner*, sorting the world's inhabitants. That is why it cannot be met with appreciation, the way one meets a fine poem; it can only be met with submission or warfare — which is, precisely, what verse 15 will now report: *they are plotting.*

**The Oaths' Secret Disclosed**

Read in context, verse 13 resolves the sūrah's own architecture, and the classical analysis — found in substance in Ibn Kathīr's survey of the passage — runs as follows. Sūrah al-Ṭāriq opened with two oaths (vv. 1–3), and Arabic oath-grammar expects an oath-object, the thing sworn about; but instead of stating it, the sūrah pivoted to man's creation, origin, and judgment (vv. 4–10). Then a second oath-pair (vv. 11–12) — and again the grammar dangles, until verse 13 supplies the object of *both* clusters at once: *by the sky and the Night Visitant… by the returning sky and the splitting earth — it is indeed a decisive Word.* The delay is the rhetoric: the listener has been made to wait through guardian, drop, loins, Day, and helplessness before being told what all the swearing was for. The technique is that of a verdict read after the evidence, not before it — the courtroom order deliberately re-sequenced so that the sentence, when it comes, lands on a prepared mind. And the sūrah's five witnesses were chosen, one may now see, for their relevance to the verdict: the star that pierces darkness (the Word pierces unbelief); the guardian over every soul (the Word will be read against every soul's record); the gushing fluid and the returning rain (the Word's resurrection-claim is supported by observed resurrection); the splitting earth (the Word itself splits, as the earth splits, to bring forth life). The oaths were not decoration before the doctrine; they were the doctrine, spoken in the world's own language.

**A Generation That Cannot Decide**

The claim of verse 13 lands with particular weight in a culture of suspended judgment. The modern condition is, in one description, decision-fatigue surrounded by engineered indecision: infinite options, endless feeds, both-sides framings, and a general etiquette that treats strong discrimination between truth and falsehood as a failure of sophistication. Into this atmosphere the verse quietly re-asserts that some speech *decides* — that reality itself is not a continuum of positions but a structure with edges, and that a Word exists which locates those edges as a blade locates a joint. This is experienced, by the believer, not as aggression but as relief. A world in which nothing is decidable is a world in which nothing is bearable; the burden of endless arbitration is heavier than the obedience it avoids. The Qur'an's claim to be *faṣl* removes that burden: the decisive separations have been made — belief and denial, right and wrong, the returning and the perishing — and the human being is invited not to adjudicate the cosmos but to choose a side of a cut already made. Verse 14 will immediately add the verse's sternest corollary: this Word, being decisive, is not — was never — a matter for amusement.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:14

> **And it is not for amusement.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Not Hazl: The Refusal of Jest**

Verse 14 is the shortest sentence of the sūrah and among its most final: *wa-mā huwa bi'l-hazl* — "and it is not for amusement." *Hazl* (root *h-z-l*) is jest, play, that which is not meant in earnest — the opposite of *jidd*, seriousness. The construction is a strong negation: the word is *not* — in any part, at any time, under any reading — a thing of play. The verse stands as the negative twin of verse 13's positive: first the Word is *decisive*; then, in case any listener should propose that even decisive-sounding speech might be deployed for entertainment, the possibility is removed. The pairing defines by exhaustion: it is *faṣl* (it decides), and it is not *hazl* (it does not amuse). The brevity is functional — five Arabic words that shut a door. And the door they shut was, in the sūrah's first context, the exact door the Makkans kept trying to reopen: the possibility of receiving the Qur'an aesthetically — as an experience to be sampled, judged, and set down — without receiving it existentially, as a claim upon one's whole life.

**The Play the Qur'an Does Condemn — and the Play It Permits**

To say the Word is not for amusement is not to say amusement is forbidden; the verse's knife cuts along a precise line that the Qur'an elsewhere marks carefully. What the Qur'an says is *not* play is its own Word, and creation itself: *"We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them in play"* (21:16); *"We created not the heavens and the earth and what is between them in sport; We created them only in truth — but most of them know not"* (44:38–39); *"Did you reckon that We created you in vain, and that you would not be returned to Us?"* (23:115). What the Qur'an says *is* play — with the world's own consent — is the unserious portion of worldly life: *"And the life of this world is nothing but play and amusement; and the abode of the Hereafter is better for those who are reverent — do you not understand?"* (6:32); *"The life of this world is but play and diversion; but if you believe and be reverent, He will give you your wages"* (47:36). The distinction, then, is not between laughter and solemnity as such, but between the *axis* of existence and its *furniture*. Play has an address in the good life — rest, festivity, the laughter the Prophet ﷺ himself practiced with his family and Companions; and the lawful joy of God's gifts is defended by the Qur'an: *"Say: Who has forbidden the adornment of God, which He brought forth for His servants?"* (7:32). What has no address is play *at the center*: the treatment of ultimate things — God, death, judgment, the Word that decides — as material for diversion. Verse 14 is the boundary-stone of that center: here is the axis; here nothing is recreational.

**The Labels the Mockers Used**

The historical setting gives the verse its bite. The Makkans did not merely ignore the recitation; they categorized it into the entertainment-economy of their day. Some called it *asāṭīr al-awwalīn* — tales of the ancients: *"And they say: tales of the ancients, which he has written down; and they are dictated to him morning and evening"* (25:5); *"And when Our signs are recited to them, they say: we have heard; if we wished, we could say the like of this — this is nothing but tales of the ancients"* (8:31). Some called him a poet: *"…a poet for whom we await the accident of fate"* (52:30) — to which the Qur'an answers: *"We did not teach him poetry, nor does it behoove him"* (36:69). Some called him a soothsayer, a madman, a sorcerer — each label a way of *filing* the Word among things one may safely spectate. Behind all the labels lay one demand: that the recitation be some kind of performance — and performances have audiences, not servants. Verse 14 refuses the filing system entire. A jest can be reviewed; a decisive Word must be answered. The tragedy the Qur'an notes elsewhere is that the mockers knew this in their better moments: *"And they marvel that a warner has come to them from among themselves; and the disbelievers say: this is a lying sorcerer — has he made the gods one God? This is a wondrous thing!"* (38:4–5). The wonder was real; the filing into jest was a management strategy for a claim too large to face.

**Serious Without Grimness**

A word must be said against the misreading of this verse that would make religion itself grim — for the verse targets the *Word's* nature, not the believer's face. The Prophet ﷺ was the most serious man who ever lived about exactly this Word, and he smiled often, joked gently with children and wives, raced with ʿĀʾishah, and approved festivity in his mosque; his Companions recorded that he was *"the most smiling of people."* The seriousness the verse demands is not the absence of joy but the presence of order: the knowledge of which things are toys and which are not. A civilization can be measured by what it refuses to laugh about; and a person can be measured the same way. The believer's mirth is real precisely because it is bounded — the play of a guest, not the anarchy of a tenant who thinks the house is his. Ibn al-Qayyim, meditating on the hearts of the ancients, observed that what ruined them was not laughter but the migration of jest from the margin of life to its center — the heart that plays with the Reminder the way idlers play with dice. Verse 14 is the standing diagnosis of that migration, wherever it occurs.

**Why the Verse Stands Between Word and Plot**

Within the sūrah's sequence, verse 14 is the hinge on which the last movement turns, and its position is logical rather than ornamental. Verse 13 has just certified: the Word decides. Verse 14 adds: the Word is earnest. Together they yield the premise of verses 15–17 — for if the Word is decisive and earnest, then what is to be made of the people who, far from answering it, are actively *working against it*? Their plotting can no longer be understood as ordinary rivalry — the normal competition of interests that fills every society — because the object of their plot is not a rival interest but the decisive, earnest claim of God. That is why the sūrah can expose the plot (v. 15) and answer it with the divine plot (v. 16) without further argument: the argument is complete. To scheme against a jest would be absurd; to scheme against a decision is rebellion; and rebellion against the Decider has exactly one possible ending, which verse 17 will state in the softest and most terrifying grammar the Arabic language can frame. The Word is not for amusement — and therefore the scheming against it, whatever it feels like to the schemers, is not a game either.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:15

> **Truly they are devising a scheme,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kayd: Cunning with Malice**

The sūrah's final movement opens with a disclosure: *innahum yakīdūna kaydā* — "truly they are devising a scheme." The root *k-y-d* names a particular species of plotting: not open warfare, but covert contrivance — the stratagem that works because its target does not see it; in Arabic usage the word almost always carries malice, the cunning of one who means harm by indirection. The grammar intensifies the noun: *yakīdūna kaydā* is the cognate accusative — "they are plotting a plot" — a construction Arabic reserves for emphasis, so that the sentence performs what it reports: the plot is not casual opposition but plotted plotting, effort stacked upon effort. And the opening *inna* certifies it: this is not the Prophet's suspicion; it is heaven's intelligence report. The verse, in other words, tells the plotted-against something he could not fully verify on his own — the Makkans' hostile intent as seen from above. For the beleaguered few in Makkah, who heard ridicule in the streets and felt pressure in the markets, verse 15 came as validation: what you are experiencing has a name, and it is visible from where your Lord sits.

**The Plots Against the Messenger**

The reference is not abstract. The early Makkan years saw an escalating campaign against the Prophet ﷺ and his followers: character assassination (the labels of poet, sorcerer, madman), economic strangulation (the boycott that starved the Muslims and their clans in the valley for three years), intimidation of converts, the torture and killing of the vulnerable — Bilāl, the family of Yāsir — and, in the sūrah's own register of *kayd*, the closed-council scheming of the Qurashī leadership. The most celebrated of those councils is the one the sīrah of Ibn Isḥāq records at Dār al-Nadwah, the parliament of Quraysh, where the notables met to decide, at last, what to do with the son of ʿAbd Allāh whose movement would not die. Three options were tabled, in the sīrah's account: imprison him in chains; banish him from the land; or kill him — but kill him cleverly, with a young man from every clan striking a single blow, so that his blood would be spread across all the families of Quraysh and his avengers would face the whole tribe at once. The council adopted the third. That deliberation — conducted in secrecy, refined for plausible deniability, aimed at a man they called their kinsman — is *kayd* in the Qur'an's exact sense; and the Qur'an later memorialized the genre in the twin passage of Sūrah al-Anfāl: *"And remember when those who disbelieved plotted against you — to imprison you, or kill you, or expel you; they were plotting, and God was plotting; and God is the best of plotters"* (8:30).

**They Were Not the First**

The source commentary adds the comparative note that the Quraysh here stand in a long line: schemers against the prophets before them — Joseph's brothers plotting to be rid of the dreamer; Pharaoh's council contriving to kill Moses; Hūd's people building their conceits against him. The Qur'an narrates the pattern explicitly: *"Likewise there came no messenger to those before them but they said: sorcerer, or madman. Did they bequeath it to one another? Rather, they are a transgressing people"* (51:52–53) — plotting against God's messengers as a *tradition handed down*, an inheritance of method. And the Qur'an records the pattern's constant ending, so that no reader may mistake the trend: *"Those before them plotted; but all plotting belongs to God. He knows what every soul earns; and the disbelievers will come to know for whom is the sequel of the Abode"* (13:42); *"And they plotted their plot — and their plot is with God, though their plot were such as to move mountains"* (14:46); *"And they plotted a plot, and We plotted a plot while they perceived not. So behold what was the end of their plot: We destroyed them and their people, all together"* (27:50–51). The verse before us is thus not only intelligence about Makkah; it is the re-opening of the oldest file in sacred history — and every prior entry in that file ends the same way.

**The Plotter's Paradox: Scheming Against Oneself**

The Qur'an adds a psychological observation of surgical precision about the kayd of the disbelievers: *"And thus We placed in every city the great ones of its criminals, that they might plot therein; and they plot only against themselves, and they perceive not"* (6:123). The sentence should be read twice, for its second half reverses its first: the powerful scheme for their own advantage and in fact scheme *against themselves* — their devices return upon them, their concealments become their own traps — and the whole process runs *below the threshold of their own perception*. This is the deep law the sūrah's next verse will personify: the schemers are free, real agents — the Qur'an never denies their freedom or the reality of their cunning — yet the very freedom they exercise is encompassed by a Knowledge and a Power that makes their cunning service, in the end, of purposes they never chose. Joseph's brothers meant a cistern; God meant a ministry, a famine, and a reconciliation in Egypt. The cross was meant as an ending; the Quraysh's council at Dār al-Nadwah meant an execution; history, directed by the One who plots, bent both toward beginnings. This is not fatalism — the plotters remain fully accountable, as verse 17's warning shows — but it is a kind of metaphysical jujitsu in which the opponent's momentum becomes the instrument of the opponent's defeat.

**How God "Schemes": the Reciprocity Reading**

The theology of the next verse — *and I too am devising a scheme* — raises at once the question of how such language befits God, and the classical tradition answered with care, in the line of interpretation represented by al-Ṭabarī and developed by later scholars: in Arabic usage, to *kāda* someone can mean to meet his kayd with kayd — to repay the schemer precisely in the coin of his own scheming. God's plotting is not the acquisition of His people's vices; it is the perfect, just mirroring of them: the one who digs a pit finds a pit; the one who spreads a net is caught in a net; and this return is not a second injustice added to the first but the first injustice *completed* — allowed to arrive at its own address. Ibn Taymiyyah's principle governs the vocabulary as a whole: God's attributes as the Qur'an states them are real, befitting His majesty, without resembling the attributes of creatures in their mode — God's kayd is infinitely above ours in power and wisdom, yet it is *toward us* exactly what His word says: the overturning of schemes. The comfort to the oppressed is specific: their enemies' cunning is not uncovenanted chaos; it is being answered, even now, measure for measure, by a Plotter who cannot be out-plotted — *"God makes the scheming of the disbelievers feeble"* (8:18); *"…but God is swifter in scheming"* (10:21).

**Plots Modern and Ancient**

The closing verses have never gone out of date, because kayd has never gone out of practice. The scheming that dogged the Prophet ﷺ has its precise modern analogues: the coordinated character assassination, the disinformation campaign, the quiet economic pressure on the believer, the institutional burrowing of corruption — the same structures of concealed malice at new scales and speeds, now amplified by networks no Qurashí council could have imagined. The believer's temptation under such pressure has also not changed: either despair — the sense that the plot is everywhere and the truth defenseless — or imitation — the conviction that the only answer to cunning is counter-cunning of the same low kind. Verse 15, standing before verse 16, forbids both. It does not say *you* plot back; it says the plotting is *seen* — *innahum*, truly they — and hands the file to the only One with jurisdiction, in the words the Qur'an addresses to the Prophet in the very passage the source cites: *"Grieve not on their account, nor be distressed by what they plot"* (16:127). The believer works, speaks, and remains honest, because the plot is not his to answer. The answer is already drafted — in the first person singular, in the next verse, in five words.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:16

> **and I, too, am devising a scheme.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Pronoun Shift Like Thunder**

The Arabic of verse 16 is among the most starkly effective in the whole Qur'an: *wa-akīdu kaydā* — "and I too am devising a scheme." Two features of the wording deserve to be felt. First, the pronoun: the Qur'an typically speaks of God in the majestic plural — *We created, We sent down* — but here the sūrah drops, without warning, into the first person singular: *I*. The shift is a known Qur'anic instrument, deployed at moments of unmediated majesty or menace — as when God says, concerning the fate of the wrongdoers: *"Leave Me with him whom I created alone"* (74:11), or *"Leave Me with those who cry lies to this discourse"* (68:44). The plural announces the court; the singular arrives when the King Himself stands up. Second, the brevity: the plotters of verse 15 required a cognate accusative and a long council to mount their scheme; God's answer is two words. The disproportion is the theology. Ibn Isḥāq's account of Dār al-Nadwah — the debate of options, the deliberation over clans and blood-money, the hired youths, the careful engineering of shared guilt — is the story of how much effort creatures must spend to accomplish evil; *wa-akīdu kaydā* is the story of how little the Creator needs to spend to accomplish anything. The verse does not even say what the divine scheme *is* — and the withholding is itself the menace: an adversary who must explain his plan is weak; an adversary whose plan needs no explanation has already begun.

**The Waiting Plot: Istidrāj**

What, then, is God's kayd against the schemers? The Qur'an's most consistent answer is: *respite with intent* — the tradition's doctrine of *istidrāj*, the gradual drawing-on. God lets the plot proceed; He lets the plotters prosper; He grants them the very successes they schemed for — and the success itself becomes the lengthening of the rope. The Qur'an states the mechanism plainly: *"And I will respite them; surely My device is firm (inna kaydī matīn)"* (7:183); and in the passage most twinned with this sūrah: *"So leave Me with those who cry lies to this discourse. We shall draw them on little by little, from where they do not know; and I will respite them — surely My plan is firm"* (68:44–45). The same root again — *kaydī*, My scheme — and the same pairing of delay with firmness. The reason for the delay is stated just as plainly: *"And let not those who disbelieve reckon that the respite We grant them is good for them; We grant them respite only that they may increase in sin; and for them is a humiliating punishment"* (3:178). The tradition transmits the warning in the Prophet's own description: that when the servant is seen hastening in disobedience while his blessings are multiplied, that is not honor — that is the drawing-on (istidrāj), reported from ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir in the Musnad of Aḥmad and the collections of the Sunan. The psychology is exact and sobering: impunity does not purify the plotter; it emboldens him, hardens him, and silently converts his gains into evidence. The divine scheme needs no new act; it lets the human scheme run to completion — and completion, for evil, is exposure.

**The Night the Plot Failed in Front of the Plotters**

The sīrah preserves a scene that reads like verse 16 staged. The council of Dār al-Nadwah resolved on the assassins: a youth from every clan, swords ready, at the Prophet's door by night. They took their places around the house and waited for sleep — the plot in its final position. And the Prophet ﷺ, as Ibn Isḥāq relates, came out — through their very ring — reciting the verses of Sūrah Yā-Sīn upon the schemers as they stood:

> *"And We have set before them a barrier, and behind them a barrier, and covered them, so that they do not see."* (Qur'an 36:9)

He cast dust upon their heads as he passed, and they saw nothing; by morning he was gone, and gone also, in the weeks that followed, was the whole future the council had planned — for the flight the plot provoked became the Hijrah, and the Hijrah became Madinah, and Madinah became the polity that returned, at Badr, to face the clans who had sent assassins to a doorstep. Every element of the divine kayd is visible in the episode: the plot was *permitted* to reach its final hour; its moment of apparent victory was made the exact moment of its failure; and the plot itself — the killing plan that forced the emigration — became the *instrument* by which God's scheme advanced. The plotters dug; the digging watered the garden of the man they buried.

**Respite as Entanglement — and as Mercy**

Yet istidrāj is only half the meaning of the divine delay, and the tradition has always guarded the other half: for some, respite is entanglement; for others, it is a window. The Qur'an announces both. The entanglement: *"And when We would destroy a township, We command its affluent ones, and they commit wickedness therein; so the sentence is justly due, and We destroy it utterly"* (17:16) — the delay was the last chance, and it was spent on increase. The window: *"Do they not see that they are tried once or twice in every year? Yet they do not repent, nor do they remember"* (9:126) — the delay was mercy, and it was refused. And the door left open for the returning: *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God's mercy; God forgives all sins; He is the Forgiving, the Merciful"* (39:53). The same divine patience, therefore, is salvation or sentence depending on the heart that receives it — which means that the *meaning of God's respite is being decided, in real time, by what the respite is used for*. The hardened schemer experiences the delay as impunity and digs deeper; the wavering soul experiences it as space and turns. Verse 16, *I too am devising a scheme*, is thus — astonishingly — also an invitation: the verse's dreadful reading is reserved for those who make it dreadful; the respite is still running; the return is still open, until it is not.

**The Asymmetry Inside the Symmetry**

The verse's grammar is a designed symmetry: *they plot (yakīdūna)… and I plot (akīdu)* — the same verb, both parties, as though the contest were even. The content of the verse demolishes the impression the grammar creates. The plotters plot *while breathing a breath they did not give themselves*, *inside a day they did not appoint*, *against a Word they cannot silence*, *before a Guardian they cannot see* — every resource of their scheming is leased from the One they scheme against. The symmetry is that of the fisherman and the fish: the fish strains with all its strength, and the net strains with the fisherman's, and every motion the fish mistakes for progress is a further gathering of the net. Ibn Kathīr, surveying the passage, notes the Qur'an's summary verdicts elsewhere — *"God makes the scheming of the disbelievers feeble"* (8:18); *"the disbelievers' scheme is ever in ruins"* — and the word *feeble* is the technical correction of the grammatical symmetry: they plot with strength that looks like theirs, and it is *ḍaʿīf* — weak — because it is held by Another. The believer learns from this asymmetry not contempt for the schemers but realism: no plot against God's servants is as large as it feels from inside, and no defense of God's truth is as necessary as it appears, for the Truth has a Defender who has already declared, in the first person, that He is handling it.

**Trust in the Darkness Before Dawn**

For the first audience — the insulted, boycotted, hunted community of early Makkah — verse 16 must have arrived like a change of air. They could do nothing about Dār al-Nadwah; they could not attend the council, refute the scheme, or protect their Prophet from the youths with swords. But they now knew two things the council did not: the plot existed (v. 15), and the Plotter of plotters had taken the case (v. 16). That knowledge did not remove the night; it made the night survivable. It still does. Every generation of believers since — under persecution, under propaganda, under the quieter plots of being made irrelevant — has recited this verse and understood that the last word on human scheming is not the schemers'. The contest looks symmetric from below; it is settled from above; and the sequel — what the respite is quietly accumulating toward — is stated in the sūrah's final verse with a softness that is the most severe thing in the whole seventeen.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:17

> **So be gentle with the disbelievers; grant them respite for a while.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mahhil: The Imperative of Letting Time Work**

The sūrah ends on an imperative addressed to the Prophet ﷺ: *fa-mahhili al-kāfirīn* — "so grant the disbelievers respite." The verb is from the root *m-h-l*, to give time, to let pass, to postpone; and the root's whole theology is contained in its use: time itself is here treated as a *thing granted* — an item of wealth the servant neither owns nor distributes. The disbelievers' continued seasons, the interval in which their scheme runs and their repentance remains possible, is issued to them by command. The verse then doubles the verb in a second imperative: *amhilhum ruwaydā* — "leave them a little while." The doubling is deliberate: God first commands the respite, then commands its *manner* — unhurried, unruffled. There is no urgency in the divine grammar because there is no uncertainty in the divine calendar. The verse is simultaneously a command, a consolation, and — as the entire classical tradition hears it — a threat: the speaker who can afford to say *let them be* is the speaker who holds the sequel. The sūrah that opened with a knock upon a door closes with the door of respite held open — but held by a hand that also holds the hour.

**A Diminutive That Carries Both a Caress and a Warning**

The last word of the sūrah is *ruwaydā* — a diminutive formation from the root of *ruwīd*, "to go gently, to allow slowly." Arabic diminutives can be tender — *ruwayyid*: gently, little one — or, in context, quietly terrifying — *ruwaydā*: just a little while… if you can keep it. The word's register has always cut both ways in Arabic idiom: it can soothe, as one says to a child to walk slowly; and it can menace, as the victor says to the cornered: take your time — there is nowhere to go. The Qur'an exploits the full register in a single word. To the Prophet ﷺ and his followers, *ruwaydā* is gentleness: do not despair, do not rage, do not force the ending; walk with the situation slowly, as one walks a loaded animal. To the schemers, over whose heads the verse is publicly recited, the same word is a countdown: their possession of the stage — their councils, their boycotts, their songs of victory — has a diminutive attached to it. *A little while.* The whole doctrine of the verse lives in that suffix of scale: what feels to the plotters like an era of success is, from the vantage of the One speaking, a brief while — and He is telling them so, to their faces, in the word He chose to end His sūrah.

**To Whom Is It Spoken, and What Does It Mean?**

The commentators transmit, as the source records, two complementary understandings. The first takes the verse as a genuine command to the Prophet ﷺ: *do not seek to hasten their punishment; bear their scheming; continue the preaching; leave the timing to God.* This is the reading that makes the verse a charter of prophetic patience — the commandment not to pray down the destruction of the deniers, not to answer plot with force while the message is still being delivered, and to conduct the daʿwah with the unhurried confidence of one whose Lord has the file. Al-Ṭabarī, in this line, notes that the respite forbids haste in the Hour and its punishment, in contrast to the behavior of the disbelievers themselves who demand that it be hastened: *"And they ask you to hasten the punishment; but God will not fail His promise; and a day with your Lord is as a thousand years of what you reckon"* (22:47). The second understanding, transmitted from al-Tustarī and recorded in the source, takes the verse as an announcement *about* them: their term is fixed, their respite is small, and the verse is the last word of the case — with the outcome located either at Badr, in the second year after the Hijrah, when the clans who had plotted at Makkah were broken in a single morning by the very community they had tried to strangle at birth; or at the Day of Resurrection, when every respite expires together. The verse supports both horizons without strain, for the sūrah's warnings — secrets tested, no strength, no helper — stand at their fullest on the Day; while the sūrah's historical shadow — Dār al-Nadwah, the ring of assassins, the flight, the return at Badr — was fulfilled within the lifetime of most of its first hearers.

**The Twins of This Command Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

This closing command is not unique to al-Ṭāriq; it has siblings, and together they map a whole Qur'anic doctrine of patience under persecution. The nearest twin, nearly word for word: *"And leave Me with those who cry lies to this discourse, the men of ease; and respite them a little (wa-mahhilhum qalīlan)"* (73:11). Then the gentler companions: *"So leave them until they meet their Day, in which they shall be thunderstruck"* (52:45); *"Leave them to plunge and to play, until they meet their Day which they were promised"* (70:42); *"And bear patiently with what they say, and depart from them in a handsome departure; and leave Me with the deniers, the men of ease; and respite them a little"* (73:10–11) — patience, courtesy, and respite, in one breath. The threefold pattern — *do not grieve, do not hasten, leave it to Me* — is the Qur'an's standing regimen for its Prophet under fire: *"And be patient for the decision of your Lord, for you are before Our eyes"* (52:48). That last clause is the doctrine of verse 4 returned as comfort at the sūrah's close: the Guardian over every soul is also the Guardian over His Messenger; the watched man needs no other watchman.

**The Patient Preacher and the Merciful Victor**

The verse's instruction proved to be prophecy of the Prophet's entire career. For more than a decade after this sūrah, he neither hastened the punishment nor abandoned the preaching — he bore the ridicule, grieved without despair, and kept reciting. The sūrah's promise overtook its targets at Badr, within its appointed *ruwaydā*; yet even then, and supremely at the conquest of Makkah eight years later, the manner of his victory obeyed the manner of this verse. Standing over the city that had plotted his death, he asked its people: *what do you think I will do with you?* They said: good — you are a noble brother, the son of a noble brother. And he answered them with the words Joseph had spoken to the brothers who had thrown him into the well: *"No reproach shall be on you this day; may God forgive you; and He is the Most Merciful of the merciful"* (12:92, spoken by Joseph) — and then, in the sīrah's account: *Go — you are free.* The man of the respite-command became the man of the respite-mercy: the ending he could have executed, he forgave. In that final scene the two halves of verse 17 — the gentleness toward the disbelievers and the divine handling of their case — fused into a single historical fact: the Messenger who had been told to wait was now the victor able to pardon, and he pardoned as he had waited, without hurry and without vengeance. The verse thus contains, in embryo, both the patience of Makkah and the magnanimity of Makkah's conquest.

**The Knock That Ends the Waiting**

Read as a whole, the sūrah's last verse closes its circle with perfect economy. The sūrah opened with a knock upon the night — the ṭāriq, the one who arrives unannounced upon the closed door. It has sworn by watchers and splittings, traced the soul from a gushing fluid to a Day of tested secrets, declared the Word that decides, and disclosed the duel of plots. And now, at the end, it tells the believer how to live inside that whole architecture: *gently, and for a little while.* Every soul's respite is running; every door will receive its knock; the Guardian over each has never once looked away. For the schemer, the ending of the sūrah is the beginning of dread: his *while* is diminutive, and the plot he trusts is already answered. For the believer, the ending is rest: his Lord has the file, the sky has been sworn as witness, the earth has testified by every seed it ever split, and the only command laid upon him in the meantime is the two sweet burdens of this verse — gentleness, and patience with God's calendar. The Night Visitant is still on the road; the decisive Word still stands open for the returning; and the last word the sūrah leaves in the reciter's mouth is *ruwaydā* — softly — a little while. Sūrah al-Ṭāriq is complete; and all praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds.
