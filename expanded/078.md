# Sūrah al-Nabāʾ (Chapter 78) — The Great Tiding (al-Nabaʾ al-ʿAẓīm), Deeply Expanded


---

# Sūrah al-Nabāʾ (Chapter 78) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah of the Great Tiding and Its Three Names**

Al-Nabāʾ, "The Great Tiding" (sometimes rendered "The Great News" or "The Announced Event"), takes its name from the very second verse of the sūrah, in which the address turns upon the noun *al-nabāʾ al-ʿaẓīm* — the weighty, world-moving announcement about which all the earlier questioning has in fact been turning. The root *n-b-ʾ* in the classical lexicons means something that moves or shakes a person: the *nabāʾ* is a matter of such gravity that receiving it stirs the soul — hence it is used for both the announcement of judgment and, in other contexts, a matter that causes a person to be "touched" with urgency. Not every piece of information is *nabāʾ*; only what is large enough to displace the receiver counts. The sūrah is also known as *al-Muʿṣirāt*, "The Driving Clouds" or "The Pourers-Down," from verse 14, where rain is described as coming down from clouds driven by the winds — a title that quietly marks creation itself, not merely the end of creation, as a theme of the chapter. And it is called *al-Tasāʾul*, "The Mutual Questioning," after the opening verses in which the disbelievers are said to question one another — a title that captures the sūrah's distinctive opening move: instead of beginning, as many Meccan sūrahs do, with an oath or a cataclysm, al-Nabāʾ opens by holding a mirror up to its hearers' own conversation and asking them to see what they have been doing.

**A Late Meccan Sūrah and Its Place in the Book**

The classical chronologists count al-Nabāʾ among the later of the Meccan revelations — it appears in al-Suyūṭī's canonical ordering (preserved in *al-Itqān*) among the last Meccan sūrahs — and it stands in the written *muṣḥaf* as the seventy-eighth of the one hundred and four chapters, the penultimate member of *ʿAmm al-Qurʾān*, the first eighty sūrahs that preserve the Meccan mode of address. The consequence of its late Meccan position is visible in its themes: it is a sūrah written for a community that has long since heard the basics of tawḥīd, that has argued for years with the Prophet ﷺ and with one another, and that has settled into a posture of professional rejection. The revelation therefore does not re-fundamentals; it consolidates. It compresses the whole case of the faith — the Creator's signs in the earth, the sky, the body, and the day-night rhythm — into sixteen verses, and then spends the remaining twenty-four verses making the case inescapable: the event these people treat as a debating topic will happen, and every soul will be sorted according to its actual disposition toward it.

**Connection with the Sūrah That Precedes It**

The junction with Sūrah al-Mursalāt (77) is one of the most instructive in the whole *muṣḥaf*. The preceding sūrah opens with an oath by the winds sent out in missions (*al-mursalāt*), builds through cosmic imagery — the seizing, the driving, the arranging, the resolving — and closes in two striking moves: it names the Day of Division (*yawm al-fasl*) twice (77:13–14, 38), and then ends with the sentence *wa mā hādhā al-qurʾānu illā dhikrun li-l-ʿālamīn* — "And this Qur'an is nothing but a reminder for the worlds" (77:50). Ibn ʿAjībah, in the *Sharḥ al-Bayḍāwī*, reads the two sūrahs as a deliberate pair: insofar as the Great Tiding of al-Nabāʾ is the Resurrection, this sūrah is a continuation of the previous sūrah's declaration of the Day of Division — the theme picked up exactly where it was left; insofar as the Great Tiding is the Qur'an itself, al-Nabāʾ follows immediately upon al-Mursalāt's last verse, as if the Book that has just called itself "a reminder for the worlds" now turns to its own addressees and asks, About what do they question one another? The effect is seamless: the oath by creation in 77 and the inventory of creation in 78 (earth, mountains, sleep, night, day, heavens, lamp, rain, produce) form one continuous survey of the created order, while the two sūrahs together frame the frame of time — creation here, judgment then.

**The Architecture of the Sūrah: Question, Evidence, Event, Verdict**

Read as a whole, al-Nabāʾ has a clean, almost architectural threefold plan that the verse-by-verse commentary below will follow in strict order.

First, verses 1–5 pose the question and answer it in two beats. The sūrah opens with the disbelievers' mutual questioning, names the object of their questioning as the Great Tiding, notes that they *differ* over it, and then strikes twice with the emphatic particle *kallā* — "Nay, but they will know! Then indeed they will know!" The answer to "about what do they argue?" is, in effect: *you will find out, when the arguing stops and the knowing begins.*

Second, verses 6–16 turn from the disputants to the evidence. The rhetorical form is a cascading series of divine acts — Did We not… — the earth as a resting place, the mountains as stakes, the pairing of creation, sleep as repose, the night as a garment, the day for livelihood, the seven firmaments, the radiant lamp, the pouring rain, the grain and the gardens. Each item is a sign (*āyah*) in the strict Qur'anic sense: a created thing that can be read as testimony about its Maker and about His power to do what He promised. The section is, in miniature, the whole apologetic of Meccan revelation — not argument from premises, but an ordered display of gifts, designed to produce the response the Qur'an expects of every honest listener: *remembering*.

Third, verses 17–40 present the Event itself and its two outcomes. The Day of Division is "a Moment Appointed" (v. 17); the trumpet is blown and the peoples come forth in throngs (v. 18); the sky opens as if into gates (v. 19) and the mountains are set in motion as mirages (v. 20). Then the sūrah divides exactly as the day it describes will divide the people: verses 21–30 describe Hell, lying in ambush for the rebellious, with its drinks and its logic — "a fitting recompense" (v. 26), earned by those who "anticipated not a reckoning" (v. 27) and "denied Our signs with utter denial" (v. 28), everything having been recorded in a book (v. 29). Verses 31–36 mirror the same structure for the reverent: a place of triumph, gardens and vineyards, maidens of like age, an overflowing cup, no idle talk or lying, and "a recompense from thy Lord, a gift abounding" (v. 36). The word *recompense* thus bookends the two halves of the sūrah's eschatology: the deniers' recompense is "fitting" (v. 26), and the reverent's recompense is "abounding" (v. 36) — the same divine justice, working in both directions. Verses 37–38 then still the whole scene into silence: the Lord of the heavens and the earth, the Compassionate, "Whom they have no power to address," while the Spirit and the angels stand in rows, none speaking except whom He permits and who speaks aright. The sūrah closes with the true day and the invitation to return (v. 39), and with its final, personal warning: a punishment nigh, a day when a man beholds what his hands have sent forth, and the disbeliever's last, hopeless cry — "Oh, would that I were dust!" (v. 40).

**A Method of Evidence: From the Created World to the Creator's Promise**

What distinguishes al-Nabāʾ among the Meccan eschatological sūrahs is the logic it enacts. It does not begin with the end; it begins with what everyone can see. The Quraysh, according to the sūrah's own opening, were *busy* — questioning, differing, trading, celebrating, arguing. The revelation's answer to a busy, disputing society is not a louder claim but a change of register: look at the ground under your feet, the stakes of the mountains, the sleep that restores you, the night that clothes the world, the rain that feeds the grain you carry to market. The chain of the argument is therefore: (1) these are real, continuous, observed facts; (2) they are acts of a single intentional Agent — *Did We not* make them, as if awaiting acknowledgment; (3) the same Agent who sustains the daily order is the Agent who has appointed a day; (4) an appointed moment that is declared by God is guaranteed, for "God does not fail a tryst" (cf. 3:9; 13:31; 39:20); (5) the consequences of the day fall on individuals according to their disposition, so the day is not an abstract cosmological event but a personal appointment. This is the logic of *tafakkur* — reasoning from creation to Creator — executed in twenty verses, and it is why the sūrah has served for fourteen centuries as a model of how revelation addresses intelligence: it assumes the hearer is capable of evidence, and it refuses to be dismissed as mere proclamation. The modern reader who meets al-Nabāʾ in the markets and lecture halls of the twenty-first century meets it in the same terms the Meccans did: about what do *we* question one another? — and, more quietly, about what do we *refuse* to question, when the evidence is all around us and the Moment is appointed?

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:1

> **About what do they question one another?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sūrah That Begins by Mirroring Its Own Hearers**

The first sūrah of the final movement of the Meccan corpus does not open with an oath — "By the Day of Resurrection!" — nor with a command, nor with a description of the Hour. It opens with a question about its listeners: *ʿan yatastāsiṭūn* — "About what do they question one another?" The sūrah thus performs, in its very first breath, the act it will demand of its hearers for the rest of the chapter: attention. A community that has turned revelation into a topic of endless social discussion is met, not with rebuttal, but with a mirror. Before God answers the question, the Qur'an makes the disputants see themselves asking it. The device is repeated throughout the Meccan sūrahs — "Did they think that We created them in vain, and that they would not return to Us?" (47:32); "Do they then seek a way other than God?" (4:54) — but in al-Nabāʾ it is the load-bearing opening of the whole sūrah, because everything that follows is, structurally, the answer to this first, suspended question.

**The Verb: A Questioning That Is Also a Deliberation**

The Arabic form yatastāsiṭūn is worth pausing over, because it is one of those dense forms in which the grammar of the Qur'an carries its argument. It is the intensive-reciprocal form of the root s-ʾ-a, "to ask, to consult, to seek judgment." The base verb sāʾala is a simple question; yatastāsiṭūn — the istafʿala form with the reciprocal suffix — expresses a group that keeps asking one another, weighing the matter back and forth, in that back-and-forth of debate in which the question turns over in the company's mouths. The early grammarians and lexicographers, and with them al-Ṭabarī in his Jāmiʿ al-bayān, noted that this particular verbal pattern in Arabic carries a shade of deliberation and continued consideration: it is not a single person asking a single question, but a company in a state of sustained, mutual inquiry — precisely the social behavior of the Meccan assemblies, where the Quranic name for the Prophet's message became a standing item of conversation, gossip, mockery, and counter-argument. The form yatastāsiṭūn is, in a sense, the Qur'an's accurate transcription of the sound of the Meccan marketplace: the same root that also produces musāwara (consultation) and istishār (seeking counsel) here describes a consultation that has lost its object — a society that has all the machinery of serious questioning pointed at a matter it refuses to take seriously.

**Who Are the "They"?**

The classical commentators, following the reading preserved in al-Qurṭubī's *al-Jāmiʿ*, al-Rāzī's *Mafātīḥ al-ghayb*, and al-Ṭabarī's account, interpret the "they" as the Quraysh — most of whose leaders, at the time of this late Meccan revelation, had still rejected Muhammad ﷺ and the Qur'an. The reference is historically specific: a named community, in a known city, at a known stage of the confrontation, after decades of discussion. Yet none of the commentators let the historicity swallow the generality: the sūrah's second move (the *kallā* of verses 4–5) and its final verse (the single disbeliever who cries "Oh, would that I were dust!") show that the address passes beyond the Quraysh and reaches every generation that has heard the Great Tiding and continued to treat it as a matter for social discussion rather than for personal reckoning. The "they" of verse 1 is therefore a standing category — the company that questions — and the sūrah, written once, keeps meeting it: in every later market, council chamber, parliament, and private conversation in which the event of the Return is mentioned, argued over, and then moved past.

**The Two Answers the Question Will Receive**

The sūrah holds the question open on purpose, and the rest of the chapter supplies two complementary answers that the classical readers did not force into one. The first answer is eschatological: the "they" are questioning — in effect, *about the Resurrection*, the "Great Tiding" whose coming they treat as doubtful, distant, or absurd (vv. 2–5, 17–40). The second answer is scriptural: they are questioning *about the Qur'an itself*, the new revelation that has entered their society and that they have made their topic, their *ḥadīth* — their social subject matter. The source commentary notes that both readings are preserved and that the content of the sūrah best supports the Resurrection; but the coexistence of the two readings is not a weakness, it is the sūrah's method. In Meccan experience the two questions were inseparable, because the Qur'an *was the announcement of the Resurrection* in revealed language — a book about the Hour, recited by the man the Hour's deniers mocked. To argue about the book and to argue about the day were, for the Quraysh, one continuous act; the Qur'an refuses to let them be separated again.

**Questioning vs. Questioning: The Difference That Determines the Outcome**

The sūrah does not condemn questioning as such; it condemns a particular quality of questioning, and the reader can detect the difference in the Qur'an's own treatment of the two. Elsewhere the Qur'an commends exactly this activity, in a different register: "Who is more just than God?" (2:140), "Reflect, then, O people of vision" (7:179), "Have they not then seen the heaven above them…" (50:16) — questions that open the intellect and turn it toward evidence. The Meccans' *taṣāʾul*, by contrast, is described by the Qur'an elsewhere in a way that reveals its actual mechanics: "And when they see a trade or a play, they break up to it and leave you standing" (10:7); "And when a worldly pleasure is mentioned, they race on ahead… but when a matter of retribution is mentioned, you see them turn away" (21:3); "And when Our clear verses are recited to them, those who expected not to meet Us said: 'This is nothing but the tale of the former peoples'" (6:152–153). The pattern is consistent and psychologically precise: the company is fully capable of sustained, heated, even brilliant discussion — the verb *yatastāsiṭūn* proves the capacity — but the capacity is systematically withdrawn the moment the topic becomes an obligation. The question about the Great Tiding is not investigated; it is *entertained*, the way one entertains a rumour. The sūrah's first verse, by naming the activity in its accurate Arabic, performs the diagnosis: you are not failing to know because the evidence is absent; you are in a state of discussion about where the state of reckoning with is required.

**The Mirror Turned on the Modern Reader**

Fourteen centuries on, the sūrah's opening remains a working instrument of self-examination rather than a historical note, because the behavior it names is stable. Every society has its Great Tiding — the single most important event a person will ever encounter — and every society is capable of the same displacement: turning the moment that should reorganize a life into content for idle interchange. The modern form of *yatastāsiṭūn* is everywhere visible: the debate that never concludes because concluding it would require changing one's life; the "discussion" of mortality, of judgment, of meaning, that serves as social lubricant in a dinner party while none of the diners is moved. The Qur'an's point, established in this single verse and then spent across the whole sūrah, is that a question whose author does not expect to be *answered personally* is not a question at all — it is a performance of a question. The sūrah will not even wait for the reader to finish observing this. Its second verse names the object of the performance, and its fourth verse stops the performance entirely.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:2

> **About the Great Tiding,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name of the Sūrah in Two Words**

The answer to the suspended question of verse 1 arrives in two words: *ʿan al-nabāʾ al-ʿaẓīm* — "about the Great Tiding." These two words give the sūrah its title, and they do more than name the topic: they re-grade it. The disputants of verse 1 were asking *something*; verse 2 tells us what that something actually is, and the adjective *ʿaẓīm* ("great," "lofty," "of the greatest rank") is not decorative. In the Qur'an's own usage, *ʿaẓīm* marks the superlative end of a scale of seriousness: it qualifies the Qur'an itself (*al-qurʾān al-ʿaẓīm*, "the Mighty Qur'an," 15:87), it qualifies the Day ("a tremendous day," *yawm ʿāẓim*, as in 6:15, 19:37, 26:189, 83:5, where the day is tremendous in relation to the punishment received by the disbelievers). When the noun *nabāʾ* — a matter that shakes its receiver — is joined to *ʿaẓīm*, the sūrah is saying, in effect: the thing you are passing around in your assemblies is not a matter for that register; it is the most tremendous matter in the history of the worlds. The mismatch between the social form of the discussion (verse 1) and the grade of the object (verse 2) is the sūrah's first and quiet rebuke, delivered without a single imperative.

**What Is the Great Tiding? The Preserved Readings**

The classical commentators preserved three principal identifications of *al-nabāʾ al-ʿaẓīm*, and the responsible method — the method of al-Ṭabarī — is to present them all and then to weigh the evidence. First, and most widely: the *Resurrection* — the Day on which all things are announced, all accounts are opened, and all previous claims are overruled. This is the reading the structure of the sūrah itself most strongly supports, for from verse 17 to the end the chapter is an extended announcement of precisely this event, and the two *kallā*-verses (4–5) — "Nay, but they will know! Then indeed they will know!" — are the sūrah's promise that the knowledge of this Tiding will arrive, whether or not it is wanted. Second: *the Qur'an itself, identified with the qurʾān al-ʿaẓīm* of 15:87 — "And We have given you, [O Muhammad], the seven oft-repeated and the Mighty Qur'an" — the book that *contains* the announcement and that was, in the Meccan experience, the very medium through which the news arrived. On this reading, the sūrah's opening dialogue is literally about the book: the Quraysh *were* questioning one another about it, every day, in every gathering, as the Qur'an's own witness to their behavior testifies. Third, in a reading recorded in al-Rāzī: **the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ himself** — the man whose coming was, for those who knew the old scriptures and their expectations, the greatest news of his age, the one about whom the whole dispute, properly understood, had always turned. The three readings converge on one function: the *nabāʾ al-ʿaẓīm* is whatever it is, it is the single event that makes all other events small, and the sūrah's first act is to force the listener to admit the scale.

**The Echo in the Story of Dhū l-Qarnayn: The Same Tiding, Another Register**

The phrase al-nabāʾ al-ʿaẓīm occurs once elsewhere in the Qur'an, and the place of its occurrence explains its weight. In Sūrah Ṣād (38), after the long narrative of the two-horned king Dhū l-Qarnayn and his journey to the far west, where he finds a people hemmed in by Gog and Magog and builds the barrier of iron and molten copper that will hold them until the Hour, the revelation closes the story with these words:

> *"So listen, on the Day when they will be told the Great Tiding — the Day when they will come forth from their hiding places from the Garden and from the Fire."* (Qur'an 38:67–68)

Two observations follow. First, the *nabāʾ al-ʿaẓīm* in 38:67 is explicitly the Day of Resurrection — the day on which the sealed things are opened, the barriers dissolve, and the peoples that had been "hidden" (in their graves, in their graves of certainty, in their *akfāf*, their hiding-places) come forth. That the same formula opens al-Nabāʾ is no accident: the sūrah's title-word is the sūrah's own eschatological term of art, borrowed from the Qur'an's most deliberate announcement of the Day. Second, the context of 38:67 — the end of a story about a man who could control everything except time, who could build the last barrier but not the last day — is exactly the moral context al-Nabāʾ returns to. Every human achievement, even a barrier of iron and copper spanning a valley, is *before* the Tiding. The Tiding is the one event against which all other arrangements are provisional.

**Why "Great": The Three Dimensions of the Scale**

The adjective ʿaẓīm is doing real theological work, and the classical readers unpacked it along three axes that correspond to the three dimensions on which an event can be "great." First, in scope: the Tiding concerns not a family, not a city, not a century, but all — every soul, every community, every claim of power that has ever been made and every account that has ever been closed. The Meccan objection, in its subtler forms, was always a matter of scale: "We do not think that all of us will be raised" (50:7); the event is simply too large to be credited. The Qur'an's answer, executed across the whole sūrah, is that the size of the event is precisely the proof of its seriousness: a God who organizes the heavens in seven firmaments (v. 12), who sends the rain that fills a valley (v. 14), who pairs every living thing (v. 8) is not the sort of God for whom an appointed day is a casual remark. Second, in reversal: the Tiding is great because it inverts everything the world had ranked. The sūrah's own closing verse anticipates the inversion with the disbeliever's cry "Oh, would that I were dust!" — the man who in this life measured himself by his station now begging to be less than a grain. Greatness of event, in the Qur'anic economy, means greatness of reversal: the last shall be first, the buried shall stand, the word that was mocked shall be the word that judges. Third, in outcome: the Tiding is great because its outcomes are eternal — the only events in the human story whose consequences do not expire. A battle, a treaty, a dynasty: all have an expiry date. The Great Tiding has none, which is why the sūrah's next move — the inventory of signs in verses 6–16 — is not an afterthought but a precondition: the only way a mind can hold an event whose consequences never end is to ground it in an Agent whose power has already been demonstrated in the daily, expiring, observable order.

**The Sūrah's Rhetorical Choice: Naming the Object Without Resolving the Question**

Notice what verse 2 does not do: it does not settle which of the three readings — Resurrection, Qur'an, or Prophet — is the "true" one. The classical method, preserved by al-Ṭabarī and followed by the more cautious commentators, treats the coexistence of the readings as intentional: the Qur'an names the object at the level of grade (the most tremendous matter) before the level of content, because the grade is what the hearer must accept in order for the content to matter. A man who has accepted that the Tiding is great cannot remain unchanged by its content in whatever form it takes; a man who has not accepted the grade will dismiss the content however fully it is spelled out. This is a subtle but decisive point of Qur'anic rhetoric, and it is the reason the sūrah can afford to leave verse 2 syntactically open — it is the second line of a three-line question, and the third line (v. 3, "regarding which they differ") completes the question before the sūrah strikes with kallā. The sequence is: you are questioning (1), about the most tremendous matter in the worlds (2), and you are doing so in disagreement (3) — and then the sūrah stops the question: Nay, but they will know! The disagreement is not to be resolved by better argument in this life; it is to be resolved by knowledge, of the kind that arrives when the argument ends.

**The Modern Displacement: Greatness That Refuses to Be Down-Graded**

The psychological mechanism al-Nabāʾ identifies in its first two verses is not antiquated; it is, if anything, the dominant mechanism of modern information life. Every society is flooded with news, and the standing threat is not the absence of great news but the *down-grading* of it — the process by which the greatest matter in existence is moved, by the sheer volume of smaller matters, into the same register as the rest: one more item, one more thread, one more argument to be won or lost in a conversation that will end by tomorrow. The Qur'an's word for this is not a technical term; it is the quiet adjective *ʿaẓīm*, placed in the second verse of the sūrah like a weight on a scale. The scale is the listener's own. The Tiding is what it is; the only variable is whether the listener's inner ranking system has permitted it to be ranked. The sūrah's entire remaining thirty-eight verses are, in the deepest sense, a defense of that adjective: a sustained, verse-by-verse demonstration that the *nabāʾ* is not a matter for the register of the disputants, but for the register of the one who will, one day, *know*.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:3

> **regarding which they differ.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Root of "Differing": Two Roads Where One Was Expected**

The third line of the opening question completes it with a single, weighty participle: *muftariqūn* — "regarding which they differ." The root *f-r-q* is the root of separation itself: of roads that fork, of a community that splits, of two things driven apart. *Firqah is a sect, faqār* are the paths that branch, *fatq* is the opening of a seal by splitting it — and *muftariqūn*, the Form II participle, is the active state of a body that has *become* divided over a matter. The word is not neutral. It does not say "they discuss" or "they consider"; it says they have *separated* — that the one matter which should be gathering them (the Great Tiding) has in fact split them, so that the object of the news is doing, inside the society, what a riverbed does when it divides: taking the flow in different directions. The sūrah's opening trio (1–3) is therefore a precise diagnosis: a company (v. 1) in a state of sustained mutual questioning (v. 1, *yatastāsiṭūn*) about the most tremendous matter in the worlds (v. 2), which has the effect of *dividing* them (v. 3). The diagnosis matters because the sūrah's own central event — the "Day of Division" (*yawm al-faṣl*, v. 17) — is named with a root (*f-ṣ-l*) of the same semantic family: to cut through, to separate. The Quraysh are, in their own disagreement, already living the rehearsal of the day that will separate them for good. The sūrah will not need to invent a division for the Day; it will only pronounce, in public, the division that is already fact.

**The Two Parties the Verse Anticipates**

Al-Ṭabarī's *Jāmiʿ al-bayān* records the reading — which the source commentary carries forward — that the verse is an allusion to the distinction between *two parties*: those who accept and confirm the Qur'an and the Prophet, and those who reject and deny them. On this reading, *muftariqūn* is not describing a fog of opinion but a hard line: one community, two directions, and the line is drawn at the most consequential point imaginable — at the acceptance or rejection of the announced event and the messenger who announced it. The Qur'an's own habit of naming the division at precisely this point confirms the reading: "Mankind was one community, then they differed" (2:213) — the single most famous verse on human division is a verse about the division over the message; "O you who believe! Believe in God and His Messenger and the Book He sent down upon His Messenger and the Book He sent down before. Whoever disbelieves in God, His angels, and His Books, and His messengers, and the Last Day — he has strayed far astray" (4:136) — the "straying far" is the destination of the party that takes the *firqah* in the wrong direction. The structure is consistent across the whole Meccan corpus: the Qur'an does not speak of "opinion" on this matter; it speaks of *sides*, because it holds that a matter of this grade cannot be held at the level of opinion — it can only be accepted, rejected, or (in the case of the suspended) postponed, and the postponement is counted as a side of its own.

**The Finer Readings: The Degrees of Rejection**

Alongside the two-party reading, the Ashʿarite theologians — whose tradition the source commentary notes in al-Qurṭubī's *al-Jāmiʿ* and al-Rāzī's *Mafātīḥ al-ghayb* — pressed the verse to a finer, and in a way more uncomfortable, reading: that it alludes to the *different degrees and modes* of rejection and denial, since some of the disbelievers believed in certain aspects of the message but not in others, and some believed in a spiritual resurrection but not in a bodily one. This reading is worth taking seriously, because it matches the Qur'an's own portrait of Meccan unbelief, which was never monolithic. The Qur'an depicts a spectrum: the open denier who called the revelation "soothsayer's tale" (52:33) and "the tale of the former peoples" (6:25); the qualified disbeliever who "believes in some and disbelieves in some" (*minhum man yuʾminu bihi wa-minhum man yakkadhu bih*, 6:101); the postponer who admitted the framework but moved the date ("they will not come till…", the endless deferral that is the signature of the Meccan objection). The Ashʿarite reading captures the fact that *muftariqūn* can describe a body divided not only into two camps but into a *gradient* of engagement — and the Qur'an's verdict on the gradient is the verdict of the next two verses: the degree of difference is not the issue; the *event* is the issue, and the event will not adjust itself to the convenience of any degree.

**"Differing" as the Social Mechanism of Deflection**

The psychological function of the *firqah* is the quiet engine of the sūrah's first three verses, and it is worth naming it plainly, because it is the mechanism every generation repeats. A matter of ultimate weight, when left unaddressed personally, gets *processed socially* instead: it becomes the subject of a group, so that no individual has to stand alone with it. The group *differing* over the Great Tiding is a society performing, collectively, the act of not deciding — each member borrowing the company's disagreement as an excuse for his own non-decision. The Qur'an sees through this with the precision of its verbal choice: it does not say *they argue* (which would imply a contest with a possible winner); it says *they are divided* — a state, not an activity, a condition of the community in which the matter has been *dissolved into disagreement rather than resolved* by encounter. The modern instance is so close it is almost embarrassing to name: the permanent public debate in which every side has a tribe, every claim has a counter-claim, and the net effect on any individual participant is a strengthened permission to remain unchanged. The sūrah's answer — arriving immediately in verses 4–5 — is that this mechanism has an expiry date: the state of being divided is not a permanent state of the matter; it is the matter's *pre-state*, the condition that the Day of Division exists to overrule.

**The Division the Day Will Declare, Not Create**

The logical principle at the heart of the verse is the principle the whole sūrah will dramatize: that the eschatological division of humanity is not a new event that the Day will *produce* out of an undivided mass, but the *public declaration* of a division that every individual life has already performed privately. A person who has, in the privacy of his actual choices, aligned himself with the truth is already, in the sūrah's metaphysics, on one side; a person who has aligned himself with denial is already on the other. The Day does not sort an unsorted heap; it *reads out* the sorting that has been happening all along — which is why the Qur'an can say, of the Day of Division, that it is "a Day on which they will be separated according to their deeds" in effect (cf. 77:13–14, 77:38, where the same name carries the same meaning), and why the sūrah's own structure — deniers' block, vv. 21–30, then believers' block, vv. 31–36 — is not a literary convenience but a theological claim: the two blocks *exist before the Day*, in the dispositions of the people; the Day is the moment they become visible, separable, and irrevocable. The practical weight of this falls on the reader in a direct way: the verse is not describing the Quraysh's history; it is describing *the reader's current position on the map*, and it is asking the only question that matters — on which side of the *firqah is my* disposition actually located, apart from my stated opinion?

**The Mirror and the Hinge**

Structurally, verse 3 is the hinge of the opening movement. Verses 1–2 asked and named; verse 3 states the effect of the questioning — division — and with that, the sūrah has said everything it needs to say about the *status quo*. There is nothing left to discuss about the disputants; what is left is the *matter itself*. The next two verses (4–5) therefore stop the discussion of the disputants entirely and shift the axis of the sūrah from the people to the event: "Nay, but they will know! Then indeed they will know!" — the knowing that will end all differing, of the kind that arrives, not by consensus, but by the event's own arrival. The opening movement of al-Nabāʾ is thus complete in five verses: the company, the object, the effect, and the two beats of the verdict. Five verses to close a topic that a society had kept open for a generation — and the closing is not a refutation. It is a prediction: *you will know*.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:4

> **Nay, but they will know!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Kallā": The Particle That Ends a Topic**

The sūrah's fourth verse is one of the most quietly forceful lines in the Meccan corpus, and its force lies almost entirely in its first word: *kallā*. The classical grammarians treat *kallā* as an emphatic particle of negation and rejection — the Arabic equivalent of "Nay!" or "By no means!" — used to *cut off* a preceding discourse, to reject the framing within which the discourse was proceeding, and to announce that what follows is of a different order than what preceded. It is a favorite particle of the short Meccan sūrahs that treat the Hour, and its appearances there carry the same function: 75:10, *kallā idhā nakaḍat al-muʿḍāḍa* — "Nay! When the earth is crushed to powder…"; 82:9, *kallā idhā rutṣaḍa fī-l-ṣiḍād* — "Nay! When the hearts are buried deep in the graves…" In each case *kallā* strikes at a moment when the addressee has been offering an evasion — a claim of power, wealth, immunity, or permanence — and the particle rejects the *premise* of the evasion before the sūrah states the counter-premise. In al-Nabāʾ the evasion being rejected is the whole posture of verses 1–3: the company that questions, the difference it sustains, the social form it has given to the matter. *Kallā* says, in effect: that frame is over. You have been *discussing* the Great Tiding; the sūrah is now *announcing* it. The register has changed from the assembly to the courtroom, and the first word of the change is kallā.

**"They Will Know": The Future That Cannot Be Negotiated Away**

The verb of the verse — *sawfa taʿlamūn* — carries the second half of the sūrah's method. The particle *sawfa* is the Arabic particle of a certain, approaching, definite future: the future of what *will happen, not what may* happen, the tense of the appointment that has already been made and only awaits its hour. And the verb is not "you will believe," nor "you will accept," nor "you will agree" — it is *ʿ-al-ma, know*, in the form of a knowledge that the sūrah will specify as it proceeds: the knowledge that arrives when the event itself arrives, of the kind that does not argue, does not deliberate, does not differ. The Meccan objection had always been, in one form or another, a claim that the matter was *not yet decidable* — "when?" — and the sūrah's answer is a claim about *certainty*: the matter is not undecided; it is *decided*, and the knowing of it is a *scheduled event* in the calendar of the worlds. The psychology of the claim is worth pausing on: the disbeliever's position is defended not by an argument against the Resurrection (the sūrah does not engage a single philosophical argument in its first five verses; it will not need to) but by a *refusal to be addressed* by it — and the sūrah's counter is to assert, in the future tense, that the address will arrive regardless of the refusal. The denial is not being answered; it is being outlasted.

**The Source's Two Readings of the Address**

The source commentary records the classical options for the verse's address, and they are worth laying out, because the difference between them is a difference in what the sūrah is doing at this point. *First*: both verses 4 and 5 are addressed to the disbelievers, and the repetition is pure *emphasis* — the double hammer-blow on the same head, the kind of repetition the Qur'an uses when the message is too important for a single statement to carry. *Second*: the two verses are addressed to *two groups* — the first to the disbelievers, the second to the believers (a reading the source attributes to al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī) — so that the sūrah, in its very hinge, names both sides of the division it diagnosed in verse 3: *they* (the deniers) will know, and *they* (the believers) will know — the same future tense, the same certainty, applied to both parties, because the Day will be a day of *knowledge* for everyone, of a kind, in either direction. *Third*, within the first option, the source preserves a finer distinction: if both verses are addressed to the disbelievers, then verse 4 may be read as the knowledge of the *truth of the Resurrection* — the coming of the event itself — and verse 5 as the knowledge of the *truth of the punishment* — the specific content of what the event will deliver to them; or, in a reading the source attributes to al-Rāzī, verse 4 as the knowledge of a punishment in *this* world (the defeat, the humiliation, the historical judgment that overtook the Meccan denial) and verse 5 as the knowledge of the punishment in the *Hereafter* — the complete, final, inexhaustible version. The coexistence of the readings is not a defect; it is the sūrah's way of saying that the "knowing" is multi-layered — that the denier will know the event, and the content of the event, and the *this-world* foretaste of the event, in that order, until there is no layer left to defer.

**"Knowing" as Experience: The Kind of Knowledge the Verse Promises**

The Arabic ʿ-al-ma in its eschatological usage is a crucial word, and the sūrah's choice of it over every alternative is a theological choice. The Qur'an distinguishes, across its usage, between the knowledge of report (samʿ) — what one has been told — and the knowledge of sight and experience (ʿayn): "You know not what joy your eyes have not seen" (32:17); "And you will see the angels spread out on every side" (97:5); the Day on which "every pair will be made to face their Lord" and "your sight will not turn away, nor will it be abased" (78:38's own sūrah will not need to say it — 68:38: "And you will see the angels on every side"). The taʿlamūn* of verse 4 is of the second kind: it is the knowledge that *arrives, not the knowledge that is argued into. The deniers of Makkah had, in the sūrah's own first three verses, demonstrated their full capacity for the first kind — they could discuss the Resurrection with sophistication, speed, and volume (yatastāsiṭūn). What they could not do, and what the sūrah asserts they will not be permitted to keep avoiding, is to encounter it. The classical commentators' insistence that the knowledge of the Day is a knowledge of certainty (of the kind the later tradition will call ʿilm al-yaqīn, knowledge of certainty — against the knowledge of report, and the knowledge of sight) rests on exactly this lexical observation: the verb chosen is the verb of knowledge-by-contact, and its subject is they — the company that had, up to that point, possessed only the knowledge of report about the event. The sūrah's promise is that the gap between the two kinds of knowledge — the gap the deniers had been living in, the gap that made their "differing" possible — will be closed for them, whether or not they have consented.

**The Psychological Logic: Why "You Will Know" Lands Where Arguments Do Not**

The reason this single verse has carried so much weight in the Meccan corpus is that it operates on the one layer of the human mind that argument cannot reach. Argument addresses the *belief* — the set of propositions a person holds — and a person can hold a proposition indefinitely at the level of *discussed but not engaged*, which is exactly the state *muftariqūn* described. The sūrah's move is to bypass the belief and address the *future experience*: it does not say "you are wrong"; it says "you will *know*" — and the word *know*, in the tense the sūrah uses, is an announcement about the person's own future state, not about the truth of the proposition. The difference matters, because a person can resist a claim about the world ("the Resurrection is not true") in a way he cannot resist a prediction about himself ("you will come to know it") — the latter does not ask him to accept anything; it only tells him what will happen to him, and the only question it leaves open is whether he will arrive at the knowing in one posture or the other. This is the sūrah's quiet strategy, and it is the strategy of the whole Meccan corpus: it does not try to win the argument in this life; it asserts that the argument will be *ended* by the event, and it invites the listener, in the meantime, to arrange his posture for the ending. The modern reader meets the same move whenever a parent, a physician, or a historian says, with the same flat certainty, "you will see" — and knows, from the tone, that the speaker is not offering a thesis but an appointment.

**The Hinge: What the Verse Closes and What It Opens**

Structurally, verse 4 is the exact center of the sūrah's opening movement, and its function is to *close one topic and open* the next, so that nothing in the sūrah's forty verses is spent on the thing the verse closes. Closed: the status of the disputants as a *debate* — from this point on, they are not characters in a discussion; they are the addressees of an announcement. Opened: the matter itself — which the sūrah will now present, not as a proposition to be debated, but as (a) a series of signs already present in the created order (vv. 6–16), and (b) an event already appointed, with a name, a sequence, and a content (vv. 17–40). The two *kallā*-verses are, in the architecture of the chapter, the door between the sūrah's *diagnosis and its evidence*; once the door is through, the sūrah never returns to describing the disputants as a social phenomenon. It returns to them, in the second half, only as *the rebellious (vv. 22, 27–28) and the reverent* (v. 31) — the two categories the division of verse 3 had already, quietly, named. The hinge has done its work: the company that differed is now, from the sūrah's point of view, no longer a company that *is* differing; it is a company that *will be divided*, and the sūrah's remaining thirty-six verses are the specification of the division.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:5

> **Then indeed they will know!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Hammer-Blow: Repetition as Theological Form**

The fifth verse repeats, with one additional particle of confirmation, the formula of the fourth: Then indeed they will know! — the Arabic carrying the same *sawfa taʿlamūn* under an added mark of intensification ("then indeed"), so that the sūrah strikes the same head twice, with the second blow landing *after* the first has had its effect. The question the repetition raises — why say it twice? — is answered by the sūrah's own method, which is a method of *weight rather than of argument*. The Meccan corpus, in its eschatological sūrahs, repeatedly uses double-beat formulas: the two *kallā*s, the two "you will know"s, the paired oaths at the head of a sūrah, the twin descriptions of the Day in 77:13–14 and 77:38 in the immediately preceding chapter. The form is not redundancy; it is *insistence* — the literary equivalent of a voice that says a thing once and, because the thing is of a grade that one statement cannot carry, says it again, the second time with the force of the first still in the air. The sūrah is saying, in its form: this is not a claim to be weighed against other claims; it is an appointment to be *received*, and the receipt of it requires the double confirmation. The modern analogy is the voice in a corridor that calls a name once and, because the name matters, calls it a second time, louder — the second call is not new information; it is the *gravity* of the first, made audible again.

**The Two-Groups Reading: Both Sides Will Know**

The source commentary preserves the reading, recorded in al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī, that the two verses are addressed to *two groups*: the first (v. 4) to the disbelievers, the second (v. 5) to the believers. On this reading, the sūrah's hinge is not a double blow on one head but a *double address* — the sūrah, in its very turn from diagnosis to evidence, names *both* sides of the division of verse 3, and tells *both* that the future belongs to *knowing*. The theological content of the reading is the sūrah's most quietly radical claim: that the Day will be a day of *knowledge* for the believers no less than for the deniers — that the believers, too, "will know," of a kind, the truth of what they have been promised, in the way that the deniers "will know" the truth of what they have denied. The knowledge of the two groups is the same *event* — the arrival of the knowing — and it is different in *content*: the deniers' knowing is the knowledge of the punishment, the exposure, the reading-out of the account (vv. 21–30, as the source reads the correspondence); the believers' knowing is the knowledge of the triumph, the entry, the receipt of the recompense (vv. 31–36). The sūrah's structure *enacts* the reading: the two blocks that follow the hinge are, in the source's words, "what the disbelievers will know" and "what the believers will know" — and the hinge's two verses are the *table of contents* for the two blocks. This is a structural observation of real weight: the sūrah does not merely *say* that the two groups will know; it *arranges its own text* so that the reader experiences the two knowings in order, first the deniers' (vv. 21–30), then the believers' (vv. 31–36), the hinge's two verses being the two doorways.

**The Two-Knowings Reading: The Event and Its Content**

Within the single-group reading (both verses to the disbelievers), the source preserves the finer distinction that makes the two verses complementary rather than identical: verse 4 as the knowledge of the *truth of the Resurrection* — the coming of the event itself, the fact of the standing, the gathering, the opening of the sky and the moving of the mountains (vv. 17–20, which the sūrah will describe immediately after the hinge) — and verse 5 as the knowledge of the *truth of the punishment* — the specific content of what the event will deliver *to them*: the ambush of the Fire (vv. 21–23), the drinks (vv. 24–25), the recompense (vv. 26–27), the record (v. 29), and the final "So taste!" (v. 30). The sequence is the sequence of the sūrah's own argument: the denier will first *know* that the Day came (the fact), and then, in the same moment, *know what the Day is for* (the content) — the two knowings arriving, in the sūrah's arrangement, as a single act with two faces. The alternative the source attributes to al-Rāzī — verse 4 as the knowledge of a punishment in *this* world, verse 5 as the knowledge of the punishment in the *Hereafter* — adds a third dimension: that the "knowing" is not confined to the final Day, but has already begun, in the historical judgment that overtook the Meccan denial (the defeat of ʿUnayn's generation, the conquest of Makkah, the reading-out, in history, of the account that the sūrah describes in eschatological form). On this reading, the two verses are a *double schedule*: the foretaste, and the full meal; the announcement in this world, and the execution in the next. The three readings — two groups, two knowings, two worlds — are not three contradictions; they are three *depths* of the same claim, and the sūrah's form (the double beat) is the form that can carry all three at once.

**The Knowledge of the Denier: The Sūrah's Own Portrait**

The sūrah will spend the next eleven verses (21–30) specifying, in detail, the content of the deniers' knowing, and the reader can already, from the hinge, see the *shape* of what is coming: a knowledge that arrives *without the option of not receiving it*. The Meccan denier's position, in the sūrah's first three verses, was a position of *control* — he was the one doing the questioning, the differing, the managing the topic; the *kallā* of verse 4 stripped the control, and verse 5 confirms the stripping: the knowing is *incoming*, not outgoing, an act done *to* the knower, of the kind that arrives whether the recipient is present or absent, attentive or asleep. The Qur'an's portrait of the denier's knowledge at the Day, elsewhere in the corpus, confirms the shape: "And you will see, that Day, the faces of those who disbelieved, blackened… and you will see the angels on every side" (82:12; 68:38); the denier who, at the first sign of the Hour, will say, in the Qur'an's own words, "Why should I be resurrected as a skeleton? I was a man of power, and I was a man of wealth" (89:15–16) — the man who *knew*, in this life, the full inventory of his own claims, and will "know," at the Day, the full inventory of their *reversal*. The knowledge the sūrah promises the deniers is not information; it is *encounter* — the encounter of the self with the full, unedited, public reading of its own record, in a setting where no part of the record can be put aside, and no part of the self can be present in a smaller form than the whole.

**The Knowledge of the Believer: The Same Tense, the Other Content**

On the two-groups reading, the believers' "they will know" (v. 5) is the same *tense — sawfa*, the certain, approaching, definite future — and it is different in *content*, in a way the sūrah's own structure will make plain. The believers' knowing, in the sūrah's portrait (vv. 31–36), is the knowledge of the *triumph*: "a place of triumph" (v. 31, *falāḥ*), the gardens and vineyards (v. 32), the maidens of like age (v. 33), the overflowing cup (v. 34), the absence of idle talk and lying (v. 35), and the recompense "from thy Lord, a gift abounding" (v. 36). The content is different, but the *form* of the knowing is the same: it is a knowledge that *arrives*, not a knowledge that is *argued into*; it is the knowledge of the *receiving*, not the knowledge of the *claiming*. The believer, in the sūrah's logic, has already, in this life, the knowledge of *report* about the event — he has heard, he has accepted, he has arranged his life by the hearing; what he lacks, and what the sūrah promises him, is the knowledge of *sight* — the knowledge of the *seeing, the entering, the receipt*, of the thing he has been promised. The hinge's two verses, on this reading, are a *promise to the believers as much as a threat to the deniers* — the same appointment, in both cases, and the same certainty, and the same *sawfa*: the Day is not the deniers' day alone; it is the believers' day as well, the day on which the believers' *sawfa* — the "soon," the "certain future" they have been living in — *arrives*. The theological weight of the reading is that the sūrah does not permit the believers to claim a private, permanent, *this-world* superiority over the event; it places the believers, too, under the *sawfa — under the coming* — and makes the believers' hope, like the deniers' denial, a matter of the *approaching, not the possessed*. The hope of the believer is a hope *for* the Day, not a possession of it.

**The Hinge's Second Beat: The Sūrah's Own Rhythm**

Structurally, verse 5 is the second beat of the sūrah's hinge, and its function is to *complete* the turn that verse 4 began. Verse 4 closed the topic of the disputants *as a debate*; verse 5 completes the closing by *confirming* the turn — by making the turn *definite*, in the sūrah's own form, the way a door that has been opened once is opened a second time, so that the passage is not a half-passage. The two verses together form the sūrah's *pivot*, and the pivot's function is to make the following thirty-six verses *unanswerable* in the mode of the preceding three: the sūrah has, by the hinge, changed the *register* of the discourse from the assembly to the announcement, and the register cannot be *reversed* — the deniers cannot, after the hinge, return to the mode of the *disputants*, because the sūrah has, in the two *kallā-verses, removed* the mode. The sūrah's remaining thirty-six verses are, in the deepest sense, the *content* of the announcement — the specification of the event, the description of the two blocks, the silence of the Spirit and the angels (vv. 37–38), the true day (v. 39), and the final warning (v. 40) — and the hinge's two verses are the *frame* for the content: the frame that says, in the sūrah's own form, *this is not a discussion; this is an appointment; and the appointment is certain*. The sūrah's method, in its forty verses, is thus a method of *frames*: the frame of the diagnosis (vv. 1–3), the frame of the hinge (vv. 4–5), the frame of the evidence (vv. 6–16), the frame of the event (vv. 17–20), the frame of the two blocks (vv. 21–36), the frame of the silence (vv. 37–38), and the frame of the closing (vv. 39–40) — each frame carrying the next, and the hinge, in its two beats, the frame that *turned* the sūrah from the people to the event, and that made the rest of the sūrah *unanswerable* in the mode it had closed.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:6

> **Did We not make the earth a resting place,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn from the Disputants to the Evidence**

The hinge is through, and the sūrah changes its object of address in a single syntactic move. The first five verses spoke *to* the disputants, about the disputants; verse 6 speaks *from* the Creator, about the Creator's own acts: *ʾa wa lam najʿal al-arḍa mihādhan* — "Did We not make the earth a resting place?" The shift states the whole method of the sūrah in the first line of the evidence. The deniers had made the Great Tiding their *topic*; the sūrah now makes the created world its *evidence* — and the evidence is introduced not as an argument with premises, but as a series of gifts, each one phrased as a question that expects a "yes" and reproaches the hearer for having lived, all along, inside the answer without noticing. This is the *tawbīk* — the reproach — in its classical form: the Qur'an's "Did We not…?" questions (the same pattern appears at 83:56–57, "Now, by the favors of your Lord, give heed," after the verses that recount what the deniers forgot) do not ask for information the speaker lacks; they demand the *recognition* that the hearer is withholding. The evidence is not hidden; the recognition is what has been refused.

**"Mihād": The Earth as Cradle**

The word the sūrah chooses for the earth's function is one of the most intimate in its vocabulary: *mihādhan, from the root ḥ-w-d*, a root whose meanings cluster around *settling — the mihād* is the cradle, the bed, the place where a thing is laid down and at rest; the same root gives *ḥawd (a settled state), ḥadīth* (that which is close, from the proximity of the settled thing to the settled speaker), and *ḥawd* (a water-trough where the settled water is gathered). The earth, in the sūrah's first sign, is not a surface, not a platform, not a stage; it is a *cradle* — the thing on which every human being has been laid, is laid down at night, and will be laid down, in the end, forever. The choice of the word carries a claim: that the earth's first and deepest function, before all its other functions, is to *receive* — to receive the body, the weight, the steps, the sleep, the grave. The sūrah does not say "Did We not make the earth a resource," or "a territory," or "a foundation for your cities"; it says a *mihād — a place of rest* — and the word quietly re-grades the listener's whole relationship to the ground under his feet. The ground is not what the listener stands on; the listener is *held* by what is under him.

**The Verse's Parallel Family in the Qur'an**

The source commentary notes that the verse functions, like 22:2 and 27:61, to indicate that God has made all that is upon the earth subservient to human beings (cf. 22:65, 31:20), and that He has placed guidance upon the earth. The second of these two functions — the earth as a *place of guidance* — is the function the sūrah will spend its remaining verses developing, and the verse that carries it most directly is the one the source cites:

> *"He it is Who made the earth a cradle (dhulatan) for you, and made paths for you therein, that haply you may be guided."* (Qur'an 43:10)

The two verses — 43:10 and 78:6 — use different words for the same function (*dhulatan and mihādhan*, both from the semantic field of the settled, the laid-down, the cradled), and the parallel is not accidental: the earth-as-cradle is a *standing image* in the Qur'an, repeated at exactly the points where the Book wants the listener to feel, in his own body, the fact of being *carried*. 22:2 ("He it is Who created for you all that is on the earth") and 31:20 ("He it is Who has made the earth for you as a carpet spread out") belong to the same family: the earth as the *spread-out thing, the laid-down thing, the thing that receives before it is used*. The Qur'an's repeated choice of these words — carpet, cradle, bed, resting-place — over the words of the surveyor (surface, area, mass) is a theological choice: it refuses to let the earth be *only* a resource, and it insists, at every opportunity, on the earth's *receiving* function — the function that the listener's daily use has made invisible.

**The Reproach in the Question: "Did We Not?"**

The grammatical form of the verse — *wa lam najʿal* — is the form the classical grammarians call the *question of reproach (the istifhām al-tawbīkī*): a question whose answer is already known to the questioner, and whose function is not to obtain the answer but to *reproach* the hearer for not having given it. The form appears throughout the Meccan corpus at exactly the points where the Qur'an wants to convert the hearer's *familiarity* with a fact into an *accountability* for the fact: "Did We not make for you, in the earth, a cradle of soft earth?" (in the sūrah's own family); "Have you not seen that to God prostrate whatever is in the heavens and the earth?" (22:18); "Have you not then observed how your Lord dealt with the companions of the elephant?" (105:3). The reproach works on a precise psychological mechanism: the hearer's *familiarity* with the gift is precisely what has made the gift *invisible* — the cradle is not noticed because it is always under; the reproach is the hand that lifts the earner's eye off the surface and onto the *relation* between the surface and the earner. The sūrah's cascade (vv. 6–16) is, in its form, a series of such reproaches, each one lifting one more gift from the background of the listener's day and placing it, in the foreground, under the question: did you not see? did you not know? did you not, at least, notice? The form is not an argument; it is an *accusation of inattention* — and it is, in the sūrah's logic, the only accusation the listener can be found guilty of, because the evidence is, by the sūrah's own account, present at every step.

**The Cradle That Will Be Removed: The Sūrah's Own Return to the Earth**

The verse's claim — that the earth is a *mihād*, a resting place — is, in the sūrah's own structure, a *temporary* claim, and the sūrah will return to the earth, at the end of its eschatological sequence, to specify the removal of the cradle. The mountains, in verse 7, are the *stakes* of the cradle; in verse 20, the same mountains are *set in motion as if they were a mirage* — the cradle's fastenings, removed. The earth itself, in the Qur'an's own eschatological register, is not left undisturbed: "the Day when We will replace the earth with other than the earth" (14:48); "the Day when the earth will be exchanged for other than the earth, and the heavens" (14:48, in the sūrah's own family); "On the Day when the earth will be shaken with its shaking" (99:6). The point of the sūrah's own structure — the cradle in verse 6, the mirage in verse 20 — is that the *rest* the cradle provides is a *loan*, not a possession: the earth receives the body now, and the Day will receive the body again, in a different form, in a different ground, in a different register. The gift is real; the gift is not permanent; and the two facts — the reality of the gift, and the non-permanence of the gift — are, in the end, the same fact seen from two times: the cradle is real *because* it is a cradle (a thing that holds, and that will, one day, not hold); the reality of the holding is what makes the *release a release*, and not a non-event. The sūrah's first sign is thus, in its deepest structure, already an eschatological sign: the *mihād* is the word that names both the present rest and the future removal, and the listener who receives the word with attention receives the gratitude for the rest, together with the preparation for its removal.

**The Practical Weight of the First Sign**

The first sign of the sūrah's cascade is, in its content, the most *ordinary* sign in the cascade — the earth under the listener's feet, the ground of the house, the field, the road, the grave — and the sūrah's choice to begin with the most ordinary sign is a methodological choice, not a default. The deniers, in the sūrah's first three verses, had made the *unordinary* — the Resurrection, the Day, the Tiding — their topic; the sūrah's answer is to begin, in its evidence, with the *ordinary* — the ground, the stake, the pair, the sleep, the night, the day, the lamp, the rain, the grain — and to let the *ordinariness* of the evidence do the work that the *unordinariness* of the topic had made impossible. The logic is the logic of the *present*: the listener cannot dispute the ground under his feet, because he is *standing on it*; he cannot dispute the sleep that took him, last night, out of his own life and brought him back to it, because he *woke up*; he cannot dispute the rain that filled the cistern, because the cistern is *full*. The sūrah's evidence is, in its form, the evidence of the *present tense* — the tense the deniers had, in their "differing," been living in without *seeing* — and the sūrah's reproach is the reproach of the *seen*: you are, in this moment, *inside* the evidence; the question is not whether the evidence is present; the question is whether the present evidence will, in the end, be *recognized* as evidence, or will be, in the end, *passed over*, the way the ground is passed over, every step, by the foot that stands on it without *looking down*. The first sign is the ground; the sūrah's entire method is the *looking down*.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:7

> **and the mountains stakes,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Awṭādan": The Word of the Peg, Whose Function Is to Be Invisible**

The second sign of the sūrah's cascade is one of the most compressed statements in the Qur'an's entire vocabulary of creation: *wa-l-jibāla awṭādan* — "and the mountains stakes." The word *awṭādan is the plural of waṭad* — the peg, the stake, the nail — the thing driven in to hold another thing in place, the thing whose function is not to be admired but to *hold*, and whose presence is known most acutely the moment it is pulled out. The root w-ṭ-n is the root of settling and fixing: *waṭan* is the homeland, the fixed dwelling; *istawṭana* is to take up a fixed abode; the *maṭwūn* are the settled ones. The sūrah's second sign therefore does not describe the mountains as scenery; it describes them as *fastenings* — the hardware of the cradle. The two first signs of the cascade form a single proposition: the earth is a resting place *because* it is staked; the rest of verse 6 is *guaranteed* by the stakes of verse 7. A cradle without stakes is a raft; the sūrah, in the space of two lines, has said that the ground of human life is held, and has said how it is held.

**The Personal Guarantee: "That It Might Not Shake With You"**

The image is not the Qur'an's invention in this sūrah; it is one of the Book's standing images, repeated wherever the revelation wants the listener to feel, in his own body, the fact of being *fixed*. The parallel that states the *reason* for the stakes in the most personal terms is 71:19:

> *"And the earth — We spread it out and set in it stakes (awtādan) that it might not shake with you."* (Qur'an 71:19)

The "you" in 71:19 is the load-bearing word of the verse. The stakes are not set in the earth for the earth's sake; they are set *for the listener* — so that the ground does not shake *under his sleep, his walk, his child's first step*. The gift, in the sūrah's logic, is not the mountain; the gift is the *not-shaking*. The mountain is the means; the *steadiness* is the favor. This distinction is the distinction the whole cascade of verses 6–16 will keep drawing: the listener's eye is trained off the *object* (the earth, the mountain, the pair, the sleep, the night, the lamp, the rain, the grain) and onto the *function* the object performs for the listener — and the function is the favor, and the favor is the *āyah, and the āyah* points to the Agent. The same word *awṭādan* also appears in 21:31, where the Qur'an, in its long recital of creation's facts, lists "the mountains as stakes" among the things that stand as testimony (cf. 21:30–31, which the source commentary cites): the image is repeated, verbatim, across the corpus, because the image is *doing work* — it is carrying the specific claim that the stability the listener stands on is an *act, performed, and therefore repeatable*, and therefore, in the sūrah's eschatological logic, suspensible.

**A Sūrah in Two Verses: 15:78–79**

The most striking echo of al-Nabāʾ's opening signs is in Sūrah al-Ḥijr, the sūrah whose name means "The Protection" — a name that, in the context of the stakes, is not accidental: the protection is the *product* of the holding. There the Qur'an says:

> *"And We spread it (the earth) and placed therein (awtādan), and We caused to grow therein of every well-weighed pair. And the mountains as stakes (awtādan)."* (Qur'an 15:78–79)

Read the two verses closely: 15:78 uses *awṭādan for the stakes in* the earth, and 15:79 uses the identical word for the mountains *as* stakes — the sūrah is, in two lines, enacting al-Nabāʾ's verses 6–7 with the same vocabulary, adding the "well-matched pairs" (al-ajwāʾ al-kul) that will reappear, in the human form, at 78:8. The Qur'an's habit of repeating a precise image at structurally important points is a signal that the image is *doctrinal*, not decorative: the earth-as-spread-out-carpet (14:32, "He who made the earth a settling-place (quʿdā) for you"), the mountains-as-stakes, the things-in-pairs — these three, together, are the Qur'an's *summary of the habitable world*, and al-Nabāʾ opens its evidence with exactly this summary. The habitable world, in the Qur'an's account, has three properties: it is *spread out* (a surface to stand on), it is *staked* (a stability to stand upon), and it is *paired* (a variety to move through). The three properties are three favors, and the three favors are three *āyāt, and the three āyāt* are the first three rungs of the ladder the sūrah is building from the ground up to the Day.

**The Shelter Side of the Stakes: The Mountains as Refuge**

The source commentary notes a second dimension the sūrah does not state but the Qur'an states elsewhere: that God has made the mountains *places of refuge* (16:81), and that in other verses the mountains are referred to as places with dwellings and houses (cf. 7:74, 15:82, 26:149). The dimension matters because it completes the picture of what the *waṭad* does for human life. The stake, in the tent-culture of the Arabian Peninsula, has two functions at once: it holds the tent down against the wind, and it *marks* the place — the stake in the ground is the difference between a *dwelling and a passing-through*. The mountains, in the Qur'an's fuller account, do both: they are the *ḥiḍāb* (the stabilizers) that keep the ground steady, and they are the *ḥijr* (the sheltered place) where the community lives — the hillsides that carry the water, the terraces that carry the grain, the passes that carry the road, the valleys that carry the town. 16:81, which the source cites, describes the mountains in their visible texture — the white and ruddy veins in their flanks — and the texture is itself part of the sign: the mountains are not monolithic blocks; they are *layered, veined, built* — the visible work of the setting. The listener's own life contains the same double function in its own "stakes": the wall that holds the house is also the wall that *defines* the home; the boundary that holds is also the boundary that encloses; the thing that keeps is the thing that makes the kept possible.

**The Stakes and the Mirage: What the Guarantee Is For**

The sūrah will return to the mountains in verse 20 — the stakes, removed, the mountains "set in motion as if they were a mirage" — and the Qur'an's other eschatological verses complete the return: 27:88, which the source quotes, "And thou seest the mountains that thou dost suppose are solid pass away like clouds — the work of God, Who perfects all things"; 81:3, "and when the mountains are removed." The removal is not a contradiction of the guarantee; it is the guarantee's *expiration*. The point the sūrah's own structure makes is that stability is a *loaned* property of the world, not an owned one: the listener does not *have* the steadiness; the steadiness is *done to him*, continuously, by an Agent who can *undo* it. The lesson is not despair; it is *orientation*. A man who knows his house is on a stable foundation builds differently from a man who knows the foundation is a loan — not differently in the building (he builds the same wall, sleeps the same sleep, raises the same child), but differently in the *soul*: he builds with gratitude for the holding, and with readiness for the removal. This is the exact posture the sūrah is training, sign by sign, from verse 6 to verse 20: receive the holding as a gift, and hold the gift with a hand that is *open*. The modern reader meets the same posture under another name: the engineer who designs a bridge for the flood that is coming, the physician who treats the patient for the illness that will come, the parent who raises the child for the world the child will enter. The design is not pessimism; the design is *seriousness* — and seriousness, in the sūrah's vocabulary, is taqwā.

**The Nautical Image Beneath the Terrestrial One**

One final image belongs in this verse, because the root *w-ṭ-n and the word waṭad* carry, in the Arab's own maritime and nomadic vocabulary, the anchor as well as the stake. The waṭad of the tent and the *mirsāṣ* of the ship are the same function in two media: the thing that *keeps the carrier from drifting*. The earth, in the Qur'an's imagery, is at times a *ship* — the carrier of its passengers over the "two seas" (cf. 14:32, the earth as *quʿdā*; 55:19–20, the two seas and the barrier between them) — and the mountains are its *anchorages*. The image explains why the Qur'an's word for the mountains' function is the word for the *peg* rather than the word for the *wall or the mountain-range-as-boundary*: the wall holds back; the peg *holds*. The earth is not held *back* by the mountains; the earth is held *down, held fixed, held in place*, by them — the way the ship is held by the anchor, and the way the tent is held by the stake. The listener, on the sūrah's second sign, is a passenger on a vessel that is *anchored* — and the sūrah's own verse 20 is the day the anchor is *weighed*. The passenger who knows the vessel is anchored sleeps well tonight; the passenger who knows the anchor will be weighed, one day, sleeps well *and* keeps his sea-legs ready. Both sleep and readiness are, in the sūrah's method, the content of the sign.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:8

> **and create you in pairs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pronoun That Turns the Cascade Inward**

The third sign of the sūrah's cascade makes a move the reader should not pass over, because it changes who the evidence is in: wa khalaqnākum azwājan — "and create you in pairs." The kum is the hinge of the line. The earth was made under the listener (v. 6); the mountains were staked around the listener (v. 7); the pairs are the listener — "create you." The cascade has moved from the landscape to the person, and with the move the reproach sharpens. The listener could, in principle, refuse to see the earth and the mountains — look away, stand elsewhere, live in a city of stone and steel; he cannot refuse to see the pairs in himself, because the pairs are him: his two hands, his two eyes, his two lungs, the architecture of his own body, and, in the reading the sūrah's context most strongly supports, his mate — the other person of his pair, the one he lives with, sleeps beside, and, in the design the verse points to, rests in. From this verse on, the sūrah's evidence is the evidence the listener carries on his person: it cannot be not seen, because it is seen every moment of every day — which is exactly why it goes unrecognized.

**"Zawj": The Word of the Matched Half**

The word azwājan is the plural of zawj — the pair, the mate, the matched half. In the Qur'an's usage, zawj carries the idea of a half that is complete only in relation to its other half: the az-zawjayn of 53:45 (the two, male and female); the zawjayn of 51:49 (all things, created in pairs); and, in the same Book, the ajwāʾ of 4:1, where the very verse that creates the human pair also names the eight pairs of livestock: "He created you from one soul and created from it its mate… and produced for you eight pairs (thamāniyata ajwāʾan) of livestock." The human pair and the animal pairs are, in the Qur'an's own text, one continuous act of pairing. In the later legal and devotional language of the tradition, zawj and zawjū become the standing words for the spouse, because the marriage relation is the human relation that most fully enacts the zawj-relation: two, one household, one set of stakes, one bed. The sūrah's choice of the word for the third sign is therefore a choice that links the body and the home: the pairing of the cells and the pairing of the spouses are, in the sūrah's vocabulary, the same word — and the link is not a figure of speech. It is a claim that the human being is structured for the pair, from the cell to the home, and that the structure is a sign.

**The Two Readings the Source Preserves, and How They Nest**

The source commentary preserves the two principal readings, and they are worth stating precisely, because they are not alternatives but nestings. First: azwājan refers to the male and female, as in 53:45, where God is said to have "created the two (*az-zawjayn*) — male and female," which are the "mates" (*azwāj*) created from a single soul (cf. 4:1, 7:189, 39:6). On this reading, the verse follows directly upon verse 6: human beings find rest and repose in their mates, as 30:21 states:

> *"*And among His signs is that He created mates for you from among yourselves, that you might find rest in them, and He established affection and mercy between you." (Qur'an 30:21)

Second: the verse refers to the creation of all things in pairs, as in 51:49:

> *"And of all things We created pairs (zawjayn), that haply you may remember."* (Qur'an 51:49)

which is understood, by some, as a reference to the manner in which human beings know things *through their opposites*. The nesting is plain: the pairing of the male and female is the most intimate *instance* of the pairing of all things, and the sūrah's use of the *general plural (azwājan*) is the form that permits both at once. The listener who receives the word with attention receives, together: the pairing of the body — the mate, the *sukūn* of 30:21 — and the pairing of the whole created order — light and dark, heat and cold, the two seas, day and night — and the two are, in the sūrah's logic, one fact: creation is paired in its structure, and the listener's own body and home are the nearest instances of the structure.

**A Ladder of Rests: Reading Verses 6–11 as a Unit**

Read verses 6–11 as a single movement, and the sūrah's design becomes visible in a way it does not in the verse-by-verse line: the cascade is a ladder of rests, each rung a sukūn — a settling — at a different level of the listener's being. Verse 6: the earth as mihād — the rest of the feet and the body, the ground that receives the weight. Verse 7: the mountains as awṭādan — the stability of the ground itself, the not-shaking. Verse 8: the azwāj — the rest of the soul, the mihād of the heart, the "that you might find rest in them" of the mate. Verse 9: the rukhd of sleep — the rest of the day, the body laid down in its mihād for the night. Verse 10: the night as milʾ — the covering of the rest, the garment that keeps the rest warm and dark. Verse 11: the day as mughān — the earning that fills the interval between two nights, the livelihood that makes the next rest possible. Six signs, one statement: the human being is carried, at every level of his being, by a series of rests, and every rest in the series is an act — We made, We staked, We created, We made your sleep, We made the night, We made the day. The design is the statement; the Agent is the signature on the design. And the ladder has a direction the sūrah will use later: the rests, in the sūrah's eschatological sequence, are removed — the ground shaken, the garment lifted, the day made into one day that does not end in night. The ladder of verse 6 to 11 is the ladder the sūrah will, in verses 17–20, take away; and the removal is the point, because the removal is what makes the giver visible.

**The One Soul and Its Mate: Origin One, Function Two**

The reading the Qur'an's own account of creation most directly supports is the first of the source's two, and the verse that carries the account is 7:189:

> *"*He it is Who created you from a single soul (nafsin wāḥida), and created from it its mate (zawjahā), that you might find rest in it." (Qur'an 7:189)

The one* in this verse is not a mystical abstraction; it is an *anthropological claim, and it is the claim the Qur'an makes most seriously at 4:1, where the address is not to the believers but to all mankind:

> "O mankind, fear your Lord, who created you from one soul, created from it its mate that you might dwell with it, and produced for you eight pairs of livestock." (Qur'an 4:1)

The sequence of 4:1 is the sūrah's own sequence enlarged: the one soul, the mate from it, the dwelling-with, and then the eight pairs of the livestock that sustain the dwelling. The claim is that before the tribe, before the clan, before the city, before the gender, there is *one soul* — the single origin from which the two are made. The *two is the function; the one* is the origin. The practical weight of the claim falls on the listener's own claim to *self-sufficiency: the zawj*-relation is a relation of *built-in dependence*. Each half is complete only in relation to the other; the mate is not an accessory to the self, the self is not an accessory to the mate, and neither is *self-sufficient — the dependence is designed in*, at the level of the body (the two hands, the two eyes, the two lungs: the body itself is a pair that functions as a unity) and at the level of the soul (the *sukūn* of 30:21: the rest is *in the other*, not in the self). The listener who meets the verse in the privacy of his own life is being asked to examine one precise fact: in his own *pair* — his spouse, his parent, his child, his closest friend — does he treat the other as a *sign of the Maker's design* (in which case the treatment is *tenderness), or as an instrument of the self* (in which case the treatment is *use*)? The sūrah's third sign is, in the end, a sign about the *quality of the listener's relation to the other* — and the quality is *readable*, because the design is known.

**Knowing by Contrast: The World Made Pairs So That It Can Be Read**

The second reading — the pairing of all things — carries a claim about the listener's own *knowing* that the first reading, on its own, does not. The word that carries the claim is at the end of 51:49: *laʿallakum tadhakkarūn* — "that haply you may remember." The pairing is a *sign* in the strict Qur'anic sense — a created thing that can be read as testimony — and the *reading of it is the remembering*. The logic of the remembering is the logic of *contrast*, and the Qur'an's own imagery is the evidence: the two seas, "meeting; between them is a barrier that they transgress not" (25:53) — the barrier is *visible because the two are different*; the day and the night, of which the sūrah's own verses 10–11 will speak, each *legible* because the other exists; the *az-zawjayn* of 53:45, whose relation is sealed by the verse that follows them: "Indeed, your striving is all different" (53:46) — the difference of the striving is the *mark* of the pairing. The epistemological claim, drawn out of the imagery, is that no quality is known in itself; every quality is known *in relation to its opposite*. Light is known by the dark; warm by the cold; rest by motion; the self by the other. A world of *absolutes* — no dark, no cold, no other, no night — would be a world that *could not be known*, a world that could not be *read*, a world that could not be *remembered*. The pairing is thus the *condition of the legibility of the world*: the Maker paired the world *so that* the listener could read it, and the reading is the *dhikr* — the remembering — of 51:49. The modern reader, who thinks in *binary* — the on/off, the 1/0, the true/false, the signal/noise — is, without knowing it, thinking in the *zawj*-structure the sūrah names: his knowing is grounded in the pairing, and the pairing is, at bottom, grounded in the Maker. The sūrah's third sign is, on this reading, a sign about the *structure of the listener's own mind*: the mind that knows is *paired* in its structure, and the world it knows is *paired* in its structure, and the two pairings are one.

**The Nearest Sign: The Person You Live With**

The practical weight of the sūrah's third sign falls, in the end, on the most *ordinary* fact of the listener's life: the person he lives with. The *zawj* of the verse is, in the listener's own life, the *mate* — the spouse, the partner, the other person of the pair — and 30:21's three words for the design of the relation are the verse's own practical content: *sukūn (the rest), mawadda (the affection), raḥma* (the mercy) — a threefold deepening, from the *settling* of the soul in the other, to the *warmth* of the soul toward the other, to the *tenderness* of the soul for the other. The design, in the Qur'an's own words, is a *ladder* — and the ladder is *readable*, because the design is *known*. The modern reader, who lives in a world of *connection without pairing* — the screen's companion, the feed's audience, the "follow" that is not a *following* — meets the sūrah's claim in his own condition: the connection is not the pairing; the pairing requires the mihād (the shared ground), the rukhd (the shared sleep), the mughān (the shared livelihood) — the three rungs of the ladder the sūrah has, in verses 6, 9, and 11, already described. The loneliness of the modern life is, in the sūrah's terms, the *failure to receive the sign* — the sign that is *present* at every level of the life, and *unrecognized* at every level of the life. The sūrah's third sign is, in the end, a sign about the listener's own capacity to receive: the pairing is given, the receiving is the listener's, and the receiving is the dhikr.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:9

> **and make your sleep repose,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Rukhdan": The Repose as Something Done, Not Merely Happening**

The fourth sign of the sūrah's cascade is one of its most personal: *wa-l-nawma rukhdan* — "and made your sleep a repose." The word *rukhdan grows from the root r-kh-d*, whose meanings cluster around rest and ease: the *rakhāda* is the state of an animal that has lain down at rest, the settled state of the body that has ceased to strain. The root also gives the Arabic word for the thing that *induces* sleep — the narcotic, the *mukhaddir* — and the connection is not accidental. It says that rest, in the Qur'an's own lexical field, is a *doing*: the repose of sleep is not the mere absence of activity, it is the *presence* of a specific activity, the laying-down, the settling, the release of the grip the day kept on the body. And the sūrah's verb is the cascade's verb — *jaʿala*, "We made" — so the repose is not the body's own property; the repose is an *act*, performed on the body by the Agent, at the appointed hour. The listener, in the fourth sign, is being asked to feel in his own body the fact of being *made* to rest: the sleep is not something he manufactures; the sleep is something he *receives*. A person who has not slept for two days knows the point in his own flesh — the body does not *negotiate* with the need for the laying-down; the need arrives, and the body *obeys*, and the obedience is, in the sūrah's logic, a receipt of the favor.

**The Minor Death: The Qur'an's Account of the Taking**

The source commentary relates the verse to 6:60 — the verse in which God is said to take the souls by night and to resurrect them by day — and the Qur'an's fullest statement of the relation is found twice, in nearly identical form:

> *"God takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that have not died during their sleep. He withholds those upon which He has decreed death, and releases the others for an appointed term."* (Qur'an 39:42; cf. 7:57)

The account is deliberate, not incidental: the same verb — *yatawaffā*, "He takes" — is used for the taking at death and for the taking in sleep, and the Qur'an's choice of one verb for both is the whole of its doctrine of sleep in a single grammatical fact. Sleep is the *minor death* — the phrase the devotional tradition has used, *al-mawt al-ṣaghīr* — not as a metaphor but as a *rehearsal*: the soul is taken, partially, and returned; the same Agent who takes it for the night can take it forever, and the *sawfa* of the Day — the certain, approaching future the sūrah used in verses 4–5 — is *scheduled by the sawfa* of the night. Every sleep is a small appointment with the taking, and every morning is a small fulfillment of the tryst: the Agent who appointed the night kept it, and the keeping is a *sign* — a daily, repeatable, falsifiable sign of the Agent's reliability, of a grade the sūrah will need later, when it asserts that the Day of Division is "a Moment Appointed" (v. 17) and that God "does not fail a tryst." The listener who has received a thousand nights of the taking and a thousand mornings of the return has, at bottom, *evidence* — a thousand pieces of it — that the Appointed Moment will come when it is appointed. The sleep is the *sample*; the Day is the *full lot*; and the sample is true.

**"By You I Die and I Live": The Threshold Duʿāʾ**

The tradition's practice of the sleep is the sūrah's fourth sign in its personal form, and the report that carries the practice most directly is the bedtime *duʿāʾ* of the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"By You, O God, I die and I live (bismikallāhumma amūtu wa-aḥyā)."* (al-Bukhārī 6324; Muslim 2710)

The Prophet ﷺ said this, according to the report, putting one foot under the other as he lay down — the body settling into its *mihād*, the fourth sign enacted in the body. The words of the *duʿāʾ* are the sūrah's own account of sleep in first person: *I die* — the minor death, the taking of 39:42; *I live* — the return, the "by day He resurrects you" of 6:60; and *by You — the bismik* — the signature on both. The practical weight of the report falls on the listener's own threshold: the sleep, in truth, is not a natural event that *happens to* the listener; the sleep is a *relation the listener enters*, at the threshold, with the Agent who takes and returns. The *duʿāʾ is the entrance rite* of that relation — the same way the *bas-mala* is the entrance rite of the reading, or the *wudu* of the prayer. The listener who enters the sleep *naked — without the bismik*, without the relation — enters the minor death *alone*; the listener who enters it *with the bismik enters it as a guest* of the Agent, and the guest of the Agent is, in the end, the one who will be *received* at the major death, the death that is the full lot of the sample.

**The Dream: The Soul's Residue from the Taking**

The sūrah's fourth sign does not specify the content of the sleep; the content is the *ruʾyā*, the dream — the residue of the minor death, the soul's own witness to the taking. The Qur'an itself gives no full doctrine of dreams; the Book's restraint on the topic is itself significant, for the *ruʾyā is left to the sunnah and to the fiṭrah* of the dreamer, and the *fiṭrah* is, in the Qur'an's own vocabulary (30:30), the design of the soul. The *sunnah* supplies the content. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The true dream that any one of you sees is a part of the forty-six parts of prophecy."* (al-Bukhārī 7016)

And he divided the dreams of the community into three: the dream from God (the *ruʾyā* that is true and meaningful), the dream from the self (the *ḥulm*, the residue of the day's own talk, the memory replaying its own material), and the dream from Satan (the *ḥulm* that saddens, to be warded off by seeking refuge in God, shifting side in the bed, and not relating it) — the division preserved in Muslim's *Ṣaḥīḥ* (Muslim 2268–2269). The practical weight of the division falls on the listener's own mornings: the sleep is not a blank; the sleep is a relation, and the relation leaves a residue, and the residue is *sorted* — by its source, by its content, by the *sunnah's* own order of handling. The sūrah's fourth sign, in the *sunnah's* extension, is thus a sign the listener can *handle*: the true dream received with gratitude and, where possible, understanding; the self-dream released without weight; the saddening dream warded off and not carried.

**The Gift the Modern World Refuses Most Often**

Of the sūrah's first six signs, the fourth is the one the modern world has most systematically refused. The ground of verse 6 is still under the modern listener's feet; the stability of verse 7 still holds his city; the pairs of verse 8 are still his own body and, in many modern lives, still his own mate. But the repose of verse 9 — the *rukhd* of the sleep — is the sign the modern world has made optional: the sign the 24-hour economy has spent, the sign the screen's glow has stolen, the sign the late-night scroll has delayed past its hour. The sūrah's reproach, updated to the modern listener, is the sūrah's own reproach: "Did We not make your sleep a repose?" — and the answer the modern listener gives, in a large and growing fraction of modern lives, is no: *I have sold it, I have spent it, I have refused it*. The logic of the refusal is the logic of the sūrah's own structure: the listener who refuses the minor death is unprepared for the major death; the listener who sells the repose is unprepared for the taking; the listener who delays the settling is unprepared for the Appointed Moment. The body keeps the account even when the soul declines to read it — the accumulated debt of the refused nights is paid in the currency of the modern physician: the insomnia, the fog, the collapse, the body's own *kallā* at the end of the week. The practical content of the sūrah's fourth sign, in the modern listener's life, is the discipline of the night — the *sukūn* of the next verse, the garment of the dark, the covering of the repose — the discipline that returns the sleep to its function, the function the sūrah names in the fourth sign: the repose, the laying-down, the minor death received well, in preparation for the major death received well.

**The Hand-Off to the Night: The Event and Its Container**

The fourth sign is the event; the fifth sign (v. 10) is the container — the night, the garment, the covering of the repose. The two are one sign seen from two sides: the sleep is the *event* of the repose, and the night is the *container* of the event. The listener who receives the fourth sign with attention receives both together — the sleep and the night, the repose and the garment, the minor death and the covering of the minor death. The sūrah's cascade has, by its fourth sign, reached the listener's own night; the listener's task is to receive that night — the night that is scheduled by the *sawfa* of the sūrah's eschatological sequence, the night that will, one day, be removed: the Day that does not end in night, the Day the sūrah will describe in verses 17–40, in the register of the ladder's removal.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:10

> **and make the night a garment,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Milʾan": The Night as a Covering, Not a Period**

The fifth sign of the sūrah's cascade names the container of the repose: *wa-l-layla milʾan* — "and made the night a garment." The word *milʾan is the noun of malāʾa, from the root l-w-s*, the root of covering and clothing: the *libās is the garment, the sitr is the veil, the istiwār* is the act of drawing cover over oneself. The sūrah does not say "and made the night a time," or "a darkness," or "an interval"; it says a *garment* — a thing that fits, a thing that covers, a thing that protects by concealing. The image is not the Qur'an's invention in this sūrah; it is one of the Book's standing images, repeated where the revelation wants the listener to feel, in his own body, the fact of being covered. Verse 10:67 says "He made the night a garment (*libāsan*) for you," and 6:96 says:

> *"He made the night a garment (libāsan) and the day for livelihood (maʿāshān)."* (Qur'an 6:96)

That second verse is worth pausing on, because it is a preview of the sūrah's own pair — 6:96's "night a garment, day for livelihood" is 78:10–11 in miniature, the same two words in the same order, in a sūrah revealed decades earlier. The Qur'an's habit of repeating a precise image across the corpus signals that the image is doing doctrinal work: the night is not the absence of the day. The night is a positive act — an act of covering, performed on the world, on the body, on the soul, at the appointed hour. The listener who receives the word *milʾan* with attention receives the claim that his night is *dressed* — that the darkness falling over his city, his bed, his closed eyes, is not a void but a garment, and that the garment is given, not self-made.

**The One Cloth, Two Uses: Protection and Exposure**

The source commentary notes the two faces the garment presents, and the two faces are the verse's own content. On the one hand, the night as a garment implies protection and comfort: the dark is the cover that keeps the sleeper in his *sukūn*, that holds the body in the cradle of verse 6, that lets the eye close and the mind lay down the day's weight. On the other hand, the night as a garment implies something the word *protection* does not suggest: the cover of night facilitates the actions of those who incline toward wrongdoing. The same cloth does two things at once, and the sūrah's choice of *garment — rather than darkness or interval* — is the choice that holds the two faces together, because a garment is precisely the thing that shields the wearer and conceals what the wearer does. The logical principle is the principle of the morally neutral instrument: the cover of night is neither good nor evil; the cover supplies the condition, and the content of the condition is supplied by the one inside it. The darkness tests. It asks, in every night of every life, the question the light does not ask — what will you do when no one can see? — and the answer given in the dark is the most honest answer a person gives in a year. This is why the Qur'an's portrait of the devout is so often a portrait of the night: "and of the night, those who stand [in prayer] to their Lord, and those who say: 'Our Lord, avert from us the punishment of the Hell'" (25:63–64), and "they lie but a little while of the night, and at the end of the night they seek forgiveness" (73:17–18). The night is the examination room of the soul; the day is its lecture hall.

**The Third Face: The Garment as the Soul's Private Room**

Alongside the two faces the source preserves, the sūrah's own register adds a third: the cover as *khalwa* — the private room, the retreat. The source notes, from Ibn ʿArabī's *Makkan Openings (al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*), that the night is a cloak for "the people of the night" (*aṣḥāb al-layl*), because it covers them from others and creates a spiritual retreat for them. The logic of this reading is the logic of the unwitnessed act. The sūrah's own eschatological emphasis — the recording angels, the book in which everything is written (verse 29 of this very sūrah will say so) — means that the dark is not a void of witnesses. The dark is the most-watched place in the world, because in the dark the only witness left is the Agent. The cover of night and the presence of the recorders are two facts about the same room: the room looks empty to the eye, and the room is full to the Agent. The *duʿāʾ* the tradition preserves for exactly this state says:

> *"Glory be to He who answers the prayer of the believer while he is in his privacy (wa-hum fī khulāʾatihim)."* (al-Tirmidhī)

The *khalwa* — the private room the night provides — is therefore not the absence of the audience; it is the reduction of the audience to one. And the virtue performed in the reduced room is the virtue the sūrah's cascade is training: the *taqwā* that the sūrah's closing verses will name as the mark of the blessed listeners — the *taqwā* that is defined by the quality of the act performed in the dark, with only the Agent as witness.

**The Rhythm of David ﷺ: The Measure of the Garment**

The sūrah's fifth sign has, in the *sunnah's* extension, a measure — a measure that protects the body while preserving the vigil. The report that carries the measure is the account of the prayer of David ﷺ:

> *"The most beloved prayer to God is the prayer of Dāwūd: he would sleep half the night, pray a third of it, and sleep a sixth of it."* (al-Bukhārī 1131; Muslim 1159)

The rhythm is the practical form of the sign. The garment of the night is not something the listener is commanded to lose — the garment is something the listener is commanded to wear, in rhythm: five parts of the night in sleep (the half and the sixth), one part in prayer (the third). The design is not asceticism; it is *alternation*. The body keeps its two-thirds of the garment, and the soul keeps its third of the night — and the two are not in competition, because the third does not *take from the two-thirds, it orders* them: the sleeper who knows a third is reserved for the vigil sleeps the other two-thirds more deeply, because the sleep is bounded, and the bounded sleep is the sleep that restores. The Qur'an's own cascade enacts the same rhythm: verse 9 (the repose), verse 10 (the garment that contains it, and the vigil that the garment also contains), verse 11 (the day that follows). The sūrah is, in verses 9–11, a three-line description of a life in rhythm — rest, garment, livelihood — and the rhythm is the sign, because the rhythm is what the Agent *made*, and what the listener *receives*, and what the listener who refuses it breaks in his own body first.

**The Oath by the Covering Night: The Garment That Testifies**

The sūrah's fifth sign is, in the Qur'an's own register, a sign the Qur'an swears by — the sign the Book uses as the object of its oath at the points where it wants the listener to feel the covering in his own skin. The oath is 92:2–3:

> *"And the night when it covers (taghshā), and the day when it appears (naja)."* (Qur'an 92:2–3)

The oath names the night by its *function — idhā jala*, "when it covers" — not by its length, not by its position in the week, not by any of the attributes a calendar would give it. The Qur'an swears by the covering, because the covering is the night's *work*. And the theological weight of the oath falls on the question of the witness: the night that covers is the night that *testifies*. A garment is not a void; a garment is a record — it carries the shape of the body it covers, the mark of the weather it has weathered, the trace of the one who wore it. The covering night carries the shape of the life it covered: every act done under it is *in* the garment, folded into it, kept in it, until the day when the garment is opened and its contents are read. The sūrah's own closing scene (vv. 37–38) is that opening: the Day on which the covering is removed and the recorded contents are read out, in a room where none speaks save whom the Compassionate permits. The fifth sign and the closing sign are the same sign at two points of the same story — the garment folded, and the garment opened.

**The Garment Removed: Two Modern Inversions and Their Recovery**

The sūrah's fifth sign, in the modern reader's life, is the sign most systematically removed — and it is removed in two directions at once. First, the *darkness* is removed: the street is lit at three in the morning, the screen glows in the dark bedroom, the workday ends when the shift says it ends, and the night — in a large and growing fraction of modern lives — is no longer a night. The body's revolt against the removal is the body's own witness to the sign: the insomnia epidemic, the burnout, the "digital sunset" the modern physician now prescribes — the return of the garment, in the form of the discipline of the dark. Second, the *privacy* is removed: the modern night, even when it is dark, is *connected* — the phone in the pillowcase, the notification in the dark, the audience that never leaves the room. The *khalwa* — the private room the garment provides — is lost, and with it the condition of the unwitnessed act, the condition in which the soul meets the Agent without an intermediary. The recovery of the sign, in the modern listener's life, is the recovery of both: the dark (the discipline of the unlit hour, the bounded sleep of the Davidic rhythm) and the privacy (the unconnected room, the prayer that is not recorded, the silence that is not filled). The sūrah's reproach — "Did We not make the night a garment?" — is, for the modern listener, a reproach with two answers to be given: yes, the darkness is given; do I wear it? and yes, the privacy is given; do I enter it? The day, in the next verse, receives the second half of the answer — the day as *mughān*, the livelihood, the time the garment comes off and the body goes out to earn.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:11

> **and the day for livelihood?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Mughān": The Day as the Time of Earning**

The sixth sign of the sūrah's cascade closes the first cycle of the sūrah's evidence: *wa-n-nahāra mughānan* — "and the day for livelihood." The word *mughān grows from the root gh-h-n*, the root of provision and gain: the *ghunnā is the benefit, the ghunm is the profit, the istighnā* is the seeking of sufficiency. The day, in the sūrah's sixth sign, is not a period of light; the day is a *function* — the function of earning, the function of livelihood, the function that fills the interval between two nights. The pairing with the fifth sign is the Qur'an's standing pair, already established in 6:96 ("He made the night a garment and the day for livelihood") and in 10:67, where the same verse that makes the night a *libās* assigns to the day its own function. The sūrah's fifth and sixth signs are, in the Qur'an's own text, a pair repeated across the corpus: the night as garment, the day as livelihood — the two halves of one design. And the question mark the source's translation carries at the end of the verse is the sūrah's own reproach: the cascade has, in six verses, named six functions the Agent has performed on the listener's own life — the ground, the stakes, the pairs, the repose, the garment, the livelihood — and the question mark is the sūrah's standing question to the listener: did you not see? did you not know? did you not, at least, notice?

**The Cycle Completed: Taken by Night, Returned by Day**

The source commentary relates the verse to the other half of 6:60 — "by day He resurrects you" — and the relation is the sūrah's own account of the cycle. The night is the taking (the minor death of the soul, the partial withdrawal of verse 9); the day is the return (the resurrection of the body, the awakening, the resumption of the livelihood). Together, verses 9–11 indicate the cycles of rest and activity essential to the functioning of the created order, as the source notes. The cycle is the sūrah's own account of the listener's own life: the listener is, in the Qur'an's logic, a creature of cycles — taken by night and returned by day, sleeping in the garment and earning in the day, his own body tuned to the rhythm the cascade is naming. The same cycle is stated, in the Qur'an's register, at 25:47, where the design of the two lights is given its reason:

> "It is He who made the sun a light and the moon a light, and measured its phases, that you might know the number of the years and the reckoning." (Qur'an 25:47)

The reason — li-taʿlamū ʿaddda al-sinīna wa-l-ḥisāb, "that you might know the number of the years and the reckoning" — is the verse's quiet claim that the alternation is for the listener: the light is not given to the world; the light is given to the listener, so that the listener may count, may reckon, may know the number of the years. The cycle is the listener's own clock, and the clock is a gift, and the gift is a sign, and the sign is read by the counting. The sūrah's own pair (vv. 10–11) is the same design at the level of the listener's own day: the night counted as the garment, the day counted as the livelihood, the two together counted as the one that the listener receives.

**The Guarantee Beneath the Labor: Rizq and the Seeker**

The sūrah's sixth sign is, in the Qur'an's own register, a sign about the listener's own labor — the labor the listener performs in the day, the labor that fills the interval between two nights. The logic of the sign is the logic of the Qur'an's own economic ethics: the labor is real, and the provision is guaranteed. The verse that carries the guarantee most directly is 11:6:

> *"And there is no creature on the earth but that upon God is its provision (rizq)."* (Qur'an 11:6)

The guarantee is the sūrah's sixth sign in its theological register. The distinction the Qur'an draws is the distinction between the *form and the substance* of the provision: the labor is the *form*; the provision is the *substance*. The listener who works in the day is not working *to produce* the provision; the listener is working *to receive* the provision — the provision that is guaranteed by the Agent, the provision that arrives *in the form of* the labor, the labor that is the *means* of the provision. The modern anxiety — the fear of scarcity, the fear of the empty pantry, the fear of the unpaid bill — is the modern listener's refusal of the guarantee: the listener who fears the scarcity is the listener who has refused the Agent who guarantees the provision. The sūrah's sixth sign is, in the end, a sign about the *quality* of the listener's labor: the labor performed *with* the knowledge of the guarantee is the labor that is *received well; the labor performed without* the knowledge of the guarantee is the labor that is *spent* in fear, the labor that the listener *produces* for the sake of the *fear*, not for the sake of the provision.

**Why the Cycle Is a Sign: The Body Tuned to the Rhythm**

The sūrah's sixth sign is, in the sūrah's own structure, a sign about the listener's own body — the body that is tuned to the rhythm the cascade is naming. The body's tuning is the body's own witness to the sign: the body that lives in the rhythm (the sleep by night, the labor by day) is the body that functions; the body that lives outside the rhythm (the sleep by day, the labor by night, the endless day without night) is the body that breaks. The modern evidence of the tuning is the modern medicine's own account of the body's rhythm: the circadian clock, the body's internal tuning to the day-night cycle, the tuning that breaks in jet lag and shift work, the tuning that breaks in the 24-hour economy. The sūrah's reproach, updated to the modern listener, is the sūrah's own reproach: "Did We not make the day for livelihood?" — and the answer the modern listener gives, in many modern lives, is no: *I have made the day for the feed, for the scroll, for the 24-hour shift, for the endless day without night*. The logic of the refusal is the logic of the sūrah's own eschatological structure: the listener who refuses the cycle is unprepared for the Day — the Day that does not end in night, the Day the sūrah will describe in verses 17–40, in the register of the ladder's removal.

**The Day That Does Not End in Night: The Eschatological Contrast**

The sūrah's sixth sign is, in the sūrah's own structure, a temporary sign — the day that ends in night, the day that ends in the garment, the day that ends in the taking. The Day of the Hereafter is the day that does not end — the day that does not end in night, the day that does not end in the garment, the day that does not end in the taking. The contrast is the sūrah's own eschatological content: the days of this world end in night; the Day does not end. The Qur'an gives the Day its own measure, a measure that is not the measure of the day of this world: the Day is "like a day of the years you count" (22:47), and the hadith of the community fixes the scale:

> *"The Day of Judgment is like fifty years."* (Muslim 182, from Anas ibn Mālik)

The content of the measure is not length alone; the content is *kind*. The days of this world end in night — every day of this world ends in the garment, in the taking, in the laying-down. The Day does not end: it is the *majmaʿ*, the gathering that does not disperse, the day in which "all are gathered" to the one appointment (cf. 80:10–11, "That is the Day of the certain gathering, to which all are gathered"). And the sūrah's own structure supplies the final contrast: in this world the day's function is *earning (mughān*, v. 11) — the listener works, the listener seeks, the listener labors; in the Day the function is *receipt* — the recompense that the sūrah has, in verses 26 and 36, already named as "fitting" for one group and "abounding" for the other. The Day is the day whose livelihood is not labor but the reading-out of the account. The listener who receives the sixth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the sample of the Agent's reliability: the Agent who makes the day for livelihood, a thousand times, is the Agent who will make the Day, once — the day that does not end.

**The Modern Inversion and the Recovery of the Design**

The sūrah's sixth sign, in the modern reader's life, is the sign most systematically inverted: the day is not for livelihood; the day is for the feed, for the scroll, for the 24-hour shift, for the endless day without night. The practical weight of the sign falls on the listener's own day: the day is not for the feed; the day is for the livelihood — the livelihood that is guaranteed by the Agent, the livelihood that arrives in the form of the labor, the labor that is the means of the provision. The discipline of the sign, in the modern listener's life, is the discipline of the day: the *bismillah* at the start of the work, the *duʿāʾ* "O God, I seek from You the lawful, the pure, the blessed" at the start of the labor, the morning *ṣadaqa* (the alms that protects through the day, as the tradition preserves from Ibn ʿAbbās — al-Tirmidhī) at the start of the day. The sūrah's reproach — "Did We not make the day for livelihood?" — is, for the modern listener, a reproach with one answer to be given: yes, the day is given; do I earn in it? The cycle (vv. 9–11) is the microcosm of the design; the next sign (v. 12) lifts up — the firmament, the seven heavens, the lamp, the rain, the grain — the signs of the Agent's design, above the listener's own head.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:12

> **And [did We not] build above you seven firmaments,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Shidādan": The Word of the Firm and the Unfissured**

The seventh sign of the sūrah's cascade lifts the listener's eye from the day to the canopy above it: *wa-samāʾan fa-shidādan* — in the sūrah's full form, "and a sky, which We have built firm" — or, in the source's rendering of the verse's two halves, "And [did We not] build above you seven firmaments." The word *shidādan* is the Form II noun of *sh-d-d, the root of strength, firmness, binding: the shadd is the tightness, the tashdīd* (in the later linguistic usage) is the doubling that strengthens a letter, the *shadīd* is the intense thing. The firmament, in the sūrah's seventh sign, is not a *dome*; the firmament is a *built thing — a thing with firmness, a thing with structure, a thing that does not fissure*. The source commentary notes that *shidād* also means "strong, solid, and firm," thus implying that the heavens have no fissure and are not subject to the vicissitudes of this world (a reading the source attributes to al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī). The claim is the sūrah's own claim: the canopy above the listener is built firm — the built thing that does not fall, the built thing that holds, the built thing that protects by holding.

**"Built," Not Merely Created: The Architecture of the Canopy**

The sūrah's verb for the heavens is *bawnā* — "We built" — not *khalaqnā*, "We created." The distinction is the sūrah's own distinction: the *creation is the act; the building is the act with structure*. The Qur'an's own register repeats the distinction at the points where the Book wants the listener to feel, in his own body, the fact of being *under a built thing*: 21:32, in the sūrah's own family, says:

> *"And the heaven — We built it with power (bi-aydin), and We are expanders (mūsiʿūn)."* (Qur'an 21:32)

and 51:47 says the same, in the same register:

> *"And the heaven — We built it with power, and We are expanders."* (Qur'an 51:47)

The two verses — 21:32 and 51:47 — use the same verb (*bawnā*) for the same function, and the same attribute (*bi-aydin* — "with power") for the same Agent. The *power (al-ayd*) in the two verses is the sūrah's seventh sign in its theological register: the canopy is built with power — the power of the Agent who builds, the power that holds the canopy in place, the power that expands the canopy (mūsiʿūn — "We are expanders"). The expansion is the sūrah's seventh sign in its cosmological register: the canopy is not static; the canopy is expanding — the built thing that grows, the built thing that widens, the built thing that holds the listener in its growth. The listener who receives the seventh sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's power — the power that builds, the power that holds, the power that expands.

**"Seven": The Number as Form, Not as Cosmography**

The sūrah's seventh sign names the canopy's structure: *seven firmaments* — the seven heavens, the standing image of the Qur'an, repeated at 2:29, 17:44, 23:17, 41:12, 65:12, 67:3, and 71:15, as the source notes. The number *seven* is, in the Qur'an's own register, a *form — the form of the layered thing, the form of the completed thing, the form of the designed* thing. The source notes, for the significance of the seven heavens, the commentary on 23:17, and the significance is the sūrah's own significance: the seven heavens are not a cosmography; the seven heavens are a form — the form of the canopy, the form of the design, the form of the Agent's own architecture. The listener who receives the seventh sign with attention receives, in the sign, the form of the Agent's own architecture — the architecture that is seven, the architecture that is firm, the architecture that is above the listener, the architecture that is for the listener.

**"Above You": The Personal Preposition of the Cascade**

The sūrah's seventh sign is, in the sūrah's own register, a sign that is about the listener's own position — the position the listener occupies under the canopy. The preposition fawqakum — "above you" — is the sūrah's own preposition for the listener's own position: the listener is under the built thing, the listener is held by the built thing, the listener is protected by the built thing. The protection is the sūrah's seventh sign in its practical register: the canopy is not a decoration; the canopy is a protection — the protection that holds back the meteors, the protection that keeps the atmosphere in place, the protection that allows the listener to live under it. The verse that carries the protection most directly is 67:5, in the sūrah's own family:

> *"*And We have made the heaven a protected ceiling (ḥijran maḥfūẓan), but they turn away from its signs." (Qur'an 67:5)

The protection is the sūrah's seventh sign in its theological register: the canopy is built to protect, and the protection is refused — the listener who refuses the protection is the listener who refuses the Agent who builds the protection, the listener who refuses the sūrah's seventh sign.

**The Canopy the World Has Measured**

The modern reader meets this sign in a setting very different from the Meccan one. He has satellites inside the canopy, a measured atmosphere, a mapped exosphere, and a telescope that reads light that has travelled for billions of years. The sūrah's claim survives the encounter, because the claim was never a cosmography to be checked against a catalogue. The sūrah claims an *architecture*: a built, firm, layered canopy, above the listener and for the listener. And the modern measurement, far from dissolving that claim, deepens it — the "firmness" (*shidād*) of the canopy is precisely what the modern account describes: a structure that *holds*. It holds the atmosphere in, holds the radiation out, holds the whole system in a balance precise enough for a seed to sprout in it. The modern astronomer who stands under the night sky and feels the scale of it is, without knowing it, in the exact posture the verse assigns: *fawqakum* — above you. The canopy is above him; he is under it; and the under-ness is the sign, because the under-ness is the condition of the protection of 67:5, the protected ceiling, and the protection is the condition of the wonder. The sūrah's reproach — "Did We not build above you seven firmaments?" — asks the modern reader what it asked the Meccan: do you receive the canopy? Or do you look through it the way the eye looks through glass, and take the whole architecture for the background of the day?

With the canopy in place, the sūrah turns to what is inside it. The next sign is the light that functions within the built thing — the radiant lamp of verse 13 — and with it the cascade completes the picture of the listener's own world: the ground that receives him, the stakes that hold it, the pair that is he, the repose that restores him, the garment that covers his night, the day that feeds him, the canopy that stands above him, and now the lamp that lights the day.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:13

> **and make a radiant lamp,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Ḍiyāʾ": The Light That Shows the Way**

The eighth sign of the sūrah's cascade names the light that functions within the built canopy: *wa-shamsan ḍiyāʾan* — "and a sun, a radiant lamp." The word ḍiyāʾ grows from the root ḍ-ʾ-y, the root of light, radiance, manifest clarity: the ḍawʾ is the light, the idtirāʾ is the taking of the light into the hand, the *mudīʾ* is the one who shows the way. The sūrah does not say "and a burning ball," or "and a fire," or "and a star"; it says a *lamp* — a light with a *function, a light that shows, a light that serves*. The source commentary notes that the radiant lamp is understood by most to be a reference to the sun (al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and others), although some interpret it as a reference to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, as in 33:45–46, where he is said to have been sent as a luminous lamp (see also 25:61). The Qur'an's own parallel for the reading carries the word *dhuhāʾ* — "radiant light" — at the same point where the sūrah carries ḍiyāʾ:

> "*It is God who has made the sun a radiant light (dhuhāʾan) and the moon a luminary (munīran)."* (Qur'an 25:61)

The two verses — 25:61 and 78:13 — say the same thing in the same register: the sun is a *light, not a force*; the sun is a *lamp, not a power*. The claim is the sūrah's own claim: the light in the canopy is a *lamp*, and the lamp is a *gift*, and the gift is a sign.

**The Sun in the Qur'an's Own Account: A Lamp on a Course**

The Qur'an's account of the sun is, across its usage, an account of a *lamp on a course* — a light with a *schedule*, a radiance with a *rule*. The verse that carries the account most directly is 36:38–40:

> *"And the sun runs for its appointed resting place. That is the decree of the Exalted in Might, the All-Knowing. And the moon We have appointed to mansions, until it returns like the old, withered [palm-branch]."* (Qur'an 36:38–40)

The sun, in the Qur'an's account, *runs — tajrī — for its mustaqarr*, its appointed resting place: the light is *moving*, and the movement is *ordered*, and the order is the *decree* of the Exalted in Might. The moon, in the same account, is *appointed to mansions — mawāṣil* — the stations it passes through in its month, until it returns, old and withered, to the new. The two lights — the sun and the moon — are, in the Qur'an's own register, a *pair of lamps: the great lamp (the sun, the dhuhāʾ of 25:61) and the lesser lamp (the moon, the munīr* of 25:61), each on its *course, each by its calculation*. The same design is stated, in the sūrah's own family, at 21:33:

> *"*And the sun and the moon — by calculation (bi-ḥusbān)." (Qur'an 21:33)

The calculation (al-ḥisāb) is the sūrah's eighth sign in its logical register: the light is not random; the light is calculated — the light that runs by the decree of the Exalted, the light that moves by the calculation of the Agent. The listener who receives the eighth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own order — the order that runs the light, the order that calculates the moon, the order that makes the day a day.

**From the Lamp of the Sky to the Lamp of the Guidance**

The sūrah's eighth sign carries, in the Qur'an's own vocabulary, a double reference: the physical light of the sky, and the light of guidance. The bridge between the two is the Qur'an's own standing use of the word *nūr* — light — for both the sun in its course and the Book in its function. The verse that carries the bridge most directly is 24:35, the verse of the Light:

> *"God is the Light of the heavens and the earth. The parable of His light is like a niche in which is a lamp; the lamp is in a glass; the glass is as though it were a gleaming star; it is kindled from a blessed olive tree, neither of the east nor of the west, whose oil would almost glow though no fire touched it. Light upon light — God guides to His light whom He wills."* (Qur'an 24:35)

The parable is not a departure from the sūrah's sign; it is the sign deepened. The lamp of 24:35 is a *lamp with a niche, a glass, an oil, and a fire* — a lamp that is *designed*, and the design is the point: the light is not a force that happens, but a light that is *kept*, in a place, with an oil, by a fire. The sūrah's own verse 13, in the same register, says: the sun is a *lamp* — a light with a function, a light that is kept in its course by the decree of the Exalted in Might (36:39). The minority reading of the verse, preserved by the source (the Prophet ﷺ as the *sirsājan munīran*, the luminous lamp, of 33:46), adds the third sense of the same word: the light that *comes to the people, the light that guides* the people, the light that *makes* the people a people of light. The three senses — the lamp of the sky, the lamp of the Book, the lamp of the Messenger — are, in the Qur'an's own usage, one word used at three points of the same design, and the sūrah's eighth sign is the first of the three: the lamp that lights the day of the listener's own livelihood (v. 11).

**The Light That Measures the Day**

The sun, in the sūrah's design, is the hand of the clock. The *ḥisāb* of 25:47 — "that you might know the number of the years and the reckoning" — is the sun's own function: the light that counts, the light that measures, the light that makes the year a year and the month a month. The day of verse 11 (the *mughān, the livelihood) is lit* by the lamp of verse 13; the two signs are linked in the sūrah's own structure, because the livelihood *requires* the light — the labor of the day is a labor of sight, and the sight is a labor of the lamp. The modern reader, who has measured the sun (the eight minutes of its light's travel, the fusion at its core, the spectrum of its radiance), meets the sūrah's claim in his own instrument: the measurement does not dissolve the claim, because the claim is not a cosmography but an *architecture* — the canopy (v. 12) is built *so that* the lamp (v. 13) can function, and the lamp is given *so that* the day (v. 11) can be a day. The sūrah's reproach — "Did We not make a radiant lamp?" — asks the modern reader what it asked the Meccan: do you receive the lamp? Or do you look through it the way the eye looks through glass, and take the whole light for the background of the day?

From the lamp above, the sūrah turns to what comes down — the water of verse 14, the rain driven from the clouds, the other half of the design: the light that walks the canopy, and the water that falls through it. The day is lit and watered, and the cascade moves toward the grain.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:14

> **and send down water pouring from the rain clouds,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Muṣīran": The Driving, the Pouring, and the Sūrah's Second Name**

The ninth sign of the sūrah's cascade is the sign that gives the sūrah its second name: *wa-māʾan muṣīran* — "and [We] send down water, pouring." The word *muṣīran* is the Form IV participle of the root m-ṣ-r, the root of pouring down, raining heavily: the māʾ al-muṣīr is the water that pours, the rain that falls in a driving pour. The source commentary notes the reading preserved in al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī: that *muʿṣirāt* — the word the source renders "rain clouds" — is read by some as "the winds" that *bring* the rain clouds, and that a small minority reads it as a reference to "the heavens." The reading is the sūrah's own reading: the one noun, in the sūrah's own register, carries *three* senses at once — the *water that pours (the product), the wind that drives (the mover), and the heaven that sends down (the source*). The sūrah's second name, *al-Muʿṣirāt* — "The Driving Clouds" or "The Pourers-Down" — is, in the sūrah's own register, a standing name for the sūrah's own sign: the sūrah is named for the rain, the rain is named for the driving, the driving is named for the pouring.

**Rain in the Qur'an's Own Account: A Water Sent Down**

The Qur'an's account of the rain is, across its usage, an account of a water *sent down* — sent from the sky, settled in the earth, and thereby made the cause of the living. The verse that carries the account most directly is 25:48:

> *"And We send down from the sky blessed water (māʾan mubārakan), and thereby We cause the earth to spring forth, after its death, from every well-matched pair."* (Qur'an 25:48)

Two details in the verse carry the sūrah's ninth sign. The first is the adjective *mubārakan* — blessed: the rain is not a natural event that happens; the rain is a *gift that is sent*, and the gift is *blessed* before it falls. The second is the phrase *baʿda māmatiha* — "after its death": the earth, in the Qur'an's account, is *dead* when the rain falls, and *alive* when the rain settles. The same verb family that describes the soul's taking in sleep (39:42) and its return in the morning describes the earth's state in the dry season and its state in the rain. The earth, like the body, is a thing that is *taken and returned* — and the rain is, for the earth, what the morning is for the sleeper. The hadith the tradition preserves for the rain's own origin adds the eschatological depth:

> *"The rain is from the Rawḍah."* (al-Bukhārī 3185, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The *Rawḍah* — the Garden — is the reward the sūrah will describe in verses 31–36. The hadith's claim is that the rain of this world is, in its origin, a *drop of the reward*: the water that falls on the Meccan hills is the same water that flows, in the full lot, in the Garden of the reverent. The ninth sign is thus a sign that is *already eschatological*: the listener who drinks the rain that falls, or eats the grain it produces, is, in the sūrah's logic, *already tasting the reward* — the sample of the full lot, the drop of the river, the first word of the word the Day will read out.

**The Water That Becomes Bread**

The sūrah's ninth sign is linked, in the sūrah's own structure, to the tenth and eleventh signs (vv. 15–16): the water is sent down *so that* the grain grows, and the grain grows *so that* the gardens are. The source commentary states the sūrah's logic explicitly: God remains the primary cause of all things — it is not the rain that produces the grain, the plants, and the gardens, as some are inclined to believe; rather, it is God Himself who does so by means of rain, since God made every living thing from water (21:30; see also 24:25, 25:54). The distinction is the distinction between the *means and the Agent*: the rain is the *means*; the Agent is the *source*. The listener who attributes the grain to the rain alone has, at bottom, *missed the sign* — the sign is not the rain; the sign is the Agent who sends the rain, and the Agent who produces the grain by means of the rain, and the Agent who makes the water the cause of the living. The chain of the sign, in the sūrah's own order, is: the sky (v. 12) sends the wind (the *muʿṣirāt* of v. 14), the wind drives the cloud, the cloud pours the water (v. 14), the water settles in the earth, the earth springs forth in green grain (v. 15), the grain fills the gardens (v. 16), and the gardens fill the listener's plate. The listener's own bread is, in truth, the final form of the sign — the cascade ends in the listener's own hand, at the listener's own table, in the listener's own mouth. The sūrah's reproach — "Did We not send down water pouring from the rain clouds?" — asks the listener what it asked the Meccan: do you receive the water? Or do you take the rain for the weather, and the grain for the market, and the bread for the baker, and miss, at every step of the chain, the Agent who is the sign?

The water falls, and the cascade turns to what it produces — the green grain of verse 15, the luxuriant gardens of verse 16. The first movement of the sūrah's evidence is nearly complete; the next movement (v. 17) turns from the world to the Day.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:15

> **that We might produce grain and plants thereby,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Ḥububan Zāʾan": The Grain at the Hour of Its Green**

The tenth sign of the sūrah's cascade names the first product of the water: *li-nukhrija bihī ḥububan zāʾan* — "that We might produce thereby green grain." Two words carry the verse. *Ḥububan is the plural of ḥab*, the seed, the sown thing, the grain — the word for the cereal that is the food of the settled life, wheat and barley first, and in the wider usage every sown seed that becomes bread. *Zāʾan* is the word for the green — the verdant, the fresh, the *live* green of the plant before the scorch takes it: the *zayy is the greenness, the azayyā* is the flourishing plant. The sūrah does not say "that We might produce grain" and stop; it says green grain — the grain *at the hour of its green*, the grain at the moment when the water has done its work and the plant is *alive in the field*, before the harvest, before the oven, before the plate. The choice of the hour is the verse's first claim: the sign is *shown* at its liveliest, at the moment of maximum life — the way a physician shows a wound at the moment of healing, not at the scar, not at the injury. The listener is being shown the water's work *in the act of being done*, in the field, in the green, in the morning light the sūrah's own verse 13 has just established.

**"Thereby": The Means, and the Agent Behind the Means**

The preposition bi-hī — "thereby" — is the load-bearing word of the verse, and the source commentary gives it its full weight: God remains the primary cause for all things. Therefore, it is not the rain that produces grain, plants, and gardens, as some are inclined to believe; rather, it is God Himself who does so by means of rain, since God made every living thing from water (21:30; see also 24:25, 25:54). The sentence "We might produce thereby*" is the sūrah's own statement of the distinction between the *means and the Agent: the water is the by, the means, the instrument; the We is the Agent, the source, the one who produces. The distinction matters because it is the distinction the listener's own daily thought violates: the farmer who attributes the crop to the rain alone, the doctor who attributes the health to the water alone, the modern reader who attributes the bread to the baker alone — each has, in the sūrah's logic, stopped at the means and missed the Agent. The Qur'an's own account of the water's power over the living confirms the Agent's claim:

> *"And We made from water every living thing."* (Qur'an 21:30)

The verse does not say "water produces every living thing"; it says "We made from water every living thing" — the *We is the maker, the water* is the from. The same grammatical structure as the sūrah's own verse 15: the *We is the producer, the bi-hī* is the means. The sūrah's tenth sign is, in the sūrah's own grammar, a sign about the *shape* of causality: the means is real, the Agent is the source, and the listener who confuses the two has, at bottom, missed the sign.

**The Green Grain and the Modern Table**

The practical weight of the sūrah's tenth sign falls on the listener's own table. The green grain of the verse is, in the listener's own life, the bread in the listener's own hand — the wheat that was the green of months ago, the barley that was the verdant of last spring, the rice that was the fresh of the paddy. The sign is personal: it is in the listener's own hand, at the listener's own table, in the listener's own mouth. The modern reader, who has the modern agriculture's own account of the grain's own production (the seed, the soil, the water, the sun, the months), meets the sūrah's claim in his own instrument: the modern account does not dissolve the sūrah's claim, because the sūrah's claim is not an agriculture but a *theology* — the claim of the Agent who *makes* the water the cause of the living, the claim of the Agent who *produces the grain by means of* the water, the claim of the Agent who *fills* the listener's own plate. The sūrah's reproach — "Did We not produce green grain thereby?" — asks the modern reader what it asked the Meccan: do you receive the grain? Or do you take the grain for the market, the bread for the baker, and miss, at the plate, the Agent who is the sign? The discipline of the sign, in the modern listener's life, is the discipline of the *plate: the bread received with the knowledge* of the Agent, the grain received *with the knowledge* of the means, the water received *with the knowledge* of the sky — the three together, in the same mouth, in the same meal, in the same life.

The green grain grows, and the cascade turns to its final form — the luxuriant gardens of verse 16. The first movement of the sūrah's evidence is complete; the next movement (v. 17) turns from the world to the Day.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:16

> **and luxuriant gardens?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"ʿAynan": The Word of the Planted Garden**

The eleventh sign of the sūrah's cascade names the final product of the water: *wa-janātan ʿaynan* — "and gardens, plantations." The two words are not synonyms. *Janātan is the plural of janna* — the garden, the enclosed green, the place of shade and water and fruit. *ʿAynan is the plural of ʿayn* in its agricultural sense — the *plantation, the orchard*, the rows of trees set out by a hand, the garden that is *planted* rather than wild. The sūrah does not say "and green fields," or "and trees," or "and shade"; it says *plantations* — the garden as the work of the hand, the garden as the product of the design, the garden as the final form of the water's work. The choice of the word is the verse's first claim: the water does not produce wilderness; the water produces *culture* — the orchard, the planted rows, the garden that is the end of the whole chain the sūrah has been describing. The listener who receives the word with attention receives the claim that the Agent's design runs *to* a cultivated garden — not to raw nature, not to abundance without form, but to the *ordered green* that is the shape of the life the design is for.

**The Garden That Bookends the Sūrah**

Read the sūrah's own structure, and the word *janātan* appears at two points that define the sūrah's shape. It appears here, in verse 16, as the *product* of the water — the garden of the world, the sample. And it appears again, in verses 31–32, as the *reward* of the reverent — "gardens and vineyards," the garden of the Hereafter, the full lot. The sūrah is *bookended* by the garden: the first movement of the sūrah's evidence (vv. 6–16) ends in a garden, and the third movement of the sūrah's eschatology (vv. 31–36) begins in a garden. The source commentary notes that attaining "the Garden" is referred to as the great triumph in over a dozen verses, and that the blessings of Paradise are the subject of the commentaries on 5:119 and 44:51–57. The link between the two uses of the word is the sūrah's own link: the garden of this world is the *sample* of the garden of the Hereafter — the drop of the river, the first word of the word the Day will read out. The listener who receives the garden of verse 16 with attention receives, in it, the *preview* of the garden of verse 32 — the same word, the same shape, the same design, at the two ends of the sūrah's own story. The sūrah is, in its structure, a story that *begins* in the garden of the sign and *ends* in the garden of the reward, and the *middle* of the story — the Day of Division, the trumpet, the mirage, the Fire, the silence of the Spirit and the angels — is the *passage* from the sample to the full lot.

**The Cascade Complete: Eleven Signs, One Statement**

The sūrah's eleventh sign is the final sign of the first movement of the sūrah's evidence. The cascade, in its eleven signs (vv. 6–16), has named the Agent's design in its full form: the ground that receives the listener (v. 6), the stakes that hold the ground (v. 7), the pairs that are the listener (v. 8), the repose that restores the body (v. 9), the garment that covers the night (v. 10), the day that feeds the listener (v. 11), the canopy that stands above him (v. 12), the lamp that lights his day (v. 13), the water that falls through the canopy (v. 14), the grain that grows from the water (v. 15), and the garden that is filled with the grain (v. 16). The eleven signs are one statement: the listener is carried, at every level of his being, by the Agent's design — the feet by the ground, the balance by the stakes, the soul by the mate, the day by the repose, the night by the garment, the livelihood by the day, the head by the canopy, the sight by the lamp, the throat by the water, the hand by the grain, and the whole life by the garden. The completeness of the design is the sign: the listener is not *partly carried; the listener is fully* carried, at every level, by the one Agent.

And the completeness of the design is the precondition for the certainty of the appointment that the sūrah's next movement will state. The listener who has received the eleven signs with attention has, in them, the evidence for the claim that the Agent who sustains the daily cycle — the taking by night, the return by day, the falling of the water, the growing of the grain — is the same Agent who has appointed the Day. The *waʿd* (the tryst) of verse 17 is not a new claim; the *waʿd is the conclusion* of the eleven signs. The sūrah's own structure makes the logic plain: the first movement (vv. 6–16) is the *evidence*; the second movement (vv. 17–20) is the *appointment*; the third movement (vv. 21–36) is the *content* of the appointment; the fourth movement (vv. 37–40) is the *verdict*. The listener who has received the first movement with attention is, by the sūrah's own structure, *prepared* to receive the second movement — the appointment, the *waʿd, the Moment Appointed*.

The cascade is complete; the sūrah turns to the Day. The next sign (v. 17) is the first sign of the sūrah's eschatological movement — the Day of Division, the Moment Appointed, the Day that does not end in night.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:17

> **Truly the Day of Division is a Moment Appointed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Yawm al-Faṣl": The Day Named by Its Work**

The twelfth sign of the sūrah's cascade is the first sign of the sūrah's eschatological movement, and it names the Day by its *work: inna yawma al-faṣli kāna waʿdan muʿayyadan* — "Truly the Day of Division is a Moment Appointed." The root f-ṣ-l is the root of cutting through, separating, deciding: the faṣl is the decision, the separation, the settling of the dispute; the fāṣil is the one who decides. The Day, in the sūrah's own name, is the Day that *decides — the Day that separates* truth from falsehood, right from wrong, light from dark, as the source commentary notes. The name is the sūrah's own claim: the Day is not named for its length, or its terror, or its spectacle; the Day is named for its *work — the work of the settlement, the work of the division, the work of the decision*. The same name appears, in the Qur'an's own register, at 77:13–14 and 77:38, in the sūrah that immediately precedes this one, and at 44:40, in the sūrah's own family. The repetition of the name across the corpus is the sūrah's own signal: the *faṣl is the Qur'an's standing word* for the Day, the word that carries the *claim of the Day's own work*.

The link between the name and the sūrah's own opening is the sūrah's own link. Verse 3 described the listener as *muftariqūn* — divided, separated, going in different directions over the Great Tiding. The *faṣl* of verse 17 is the *settlement* of that division: what was *divided in the opinion* of this world will be *separated in the fact* of the Day. The Day does not *create the division; the Day declares* it. The listener who has been *divided* in this world — the listener who has *taken a side in the dispute* of verse 3 — will be *separated* in the Day, into the block the sūrah will name in verses 21–30 (the rebellious, the deniers, the ones who "anticipated not a reckoning") or into the block the sūrah will name in verses 31–36 (the reverent, the triumphant, the ones who "have a place of triumph"). The faṣl is the reading-out of the division that the listener has already performed, in the privacy of his own choices, in the secret of his own disposition.

**"Waʿdan Muʿayyadan": The Tryst With a Fixed Time**

The second half of the verse carries the sūrah's own claim about the *certainty of the Day: kāna waʿdan muʿayyadan* — "it was/is a tryst with a fixed time." Two words from the same root — *ʿ-w-y-d* — carry the claim. The *waʿd is the promise, the tryst, the appointment; the muʿayyad is the timed* thing, the thing that has a *fixed date*, the thing that is *appointed to a moment*. The two words, from the same root, say: the promise is timed — the tryst has a date, the appointment has a moment, the Day is not an expectation but an appointment. The source commentary notes that the Day is a Moment Appointed "because it is guaranteed, and God will not fail the tryst," and it cites, for the same claim, 3:9, 13:31, and 39:20. The two verses the source cites carry the claim in the Qur'an's own words:

> *"*And truly, the promise of God will be fulfilled (wa-layattina waʿdu Allāh)." (Qur'an 3:9)

> "And truly, the promise of God will be fulfilled, if you only knew (wa-layattina waʿdu Allāh in kuntum taʿlamūn)." (Qur'an 13:31)

The claim of the two verses is the claim of the sūrah's twelfth sign: the waʿd is certain, the tryst is guaranteed, the appointment is timed. The listener who receives the twelfth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own trustworthiness — the trustworthiness that is evidenced by the eleven signs of the first movement, the trustworthiness that is guaranteed by the waʿd of the twelfth sign, the trustworthiness that is the sign of the Agent's own design.

**The Postponer and the Appointment: The Logic of the "When?"**

The sūrah's twelfth sign is, in the sūrah's own structure, a sign that is about the listener's own "when?" — the question the listener asks, the question the denier asks, the question the postponer asks. The Qur'an's own account of the postponer's question is the sūrah's twelfth sign in its rhetorical register: the denier does not ask "is it true?"; the denier asks "when is it?" — the question of the tāʾij, the postponer, the one who delays the matter past its hour. The verse that carries the postponer's question most directly is 23:40:

> "They say: 'When is this promise, if you are truthful?'" (Qur'an 23:40)

The sūrah's twelfth sign is the answer to the postponer's question: the waʿd is muʿayyad — the tryst is timed, the appointment is fixed, the Day is appointed. The listener who receives the twelfth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own appointment — the appointment that is fixed, the appointment that is guaranteed, the appointment that is the sign of the Agent's own design. The logic of the appointment is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the listener who has received the eleven signs of the first movement with attention has, in them, the evidence for the claim of the Agent's own trustworthiness — the trustworthiness that sustains the daily cycle, the trustworthiness that guarantees the waʿd, the trustworthiness that is the sign of the Agent's own design. The sūrah's reproach — "Did We not appoint the Day?" — is, for the listener, a reproach with one answer to be given: yes, the Day is appointed; do I receive it?

The twelfth sign is the appointment; the next signs (vv. 18–20) are the content of the appointment — the trumpet blown, the peoples come forth in bands, the sky opened as if into gates, the mountains set in motion as if they were a mirage. The sūrah turns from the *certainty* of the Day to the *sequence* of the Day.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:18

> **a day when the trumpet is blown and you come forth in throngs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Trumpet: One Instrument, Two Blasts**

The thirteenth sign of the sūrah's cascade names the first act of the Day: *yawman yunfakha fīhi l-ṣūr* — "a day when the trumpet is blown." The word *ṣūr* is the trumpet — the instrument out of which the Archangel ʿIsrāfīl blows, in the tradition's own account. The source commentary carries the Qur'an's own account of the two blasts, from 39:68, where the Archangel is said to blow the trumpet twice: at the first blast, all of creation will swoon (see 69:13); at the second, all souls will rise from their graves and be gathered before God (see 50:20–21; 64:9). The sūrah's own verse, in the source's note, may refer to the fact that the trumpet is blown twice, or to the second blowing of the trumpet (see 39:68c). The Qur'an's own account of the first blast is the sūrah's thirteenth sign in its dramatic register:

> *"And the Trumpet will be blown, and those in the heavens and those on the earth will fall, except whom God wills. Then it will be blown again, and behold, they will be standing, gazing."* (Qur'an 39:68)

The two verbs carry the sign: *fall (the swoon, the ʿajm* — the total collapse of the created order, the heavens and the earth together, except whom God wills) and *stand (the rising, the qiyām* — the standing of the souls, the standing that is the beginning of the Day's own work). The sūrah's thirteenth sign is, in the Qur'an's own account, the sign of the *two movements* of the Day — the fall and the stand, the swoon and the rising, the end of the old order and the beginning of the new order.

**"Afwājan": The Peoples Come Forth in Bands**

The second half of the verse names the form of the gathering: *wa-takhrūjūna afwājan* — "and you come forth in bands." The word *afwājan is the plural of fawj* — the band, the host, the group that comes out *together, in order, under a leader*. The source commentary notes that the *afwāj* is taken by some to indicate that human communities will come in "groups" (*afwāj*), with each community gathered behind its prophet (see 17:71c). The Qur'an's own account of the bands is the sūrah's thirteenth sign in its communal register:

> *"*And they will be brought forth in bands (afwājan) to their messengers." (Qur'an 17:71)

The claim of the verse is the claim of the sūrah's thirteenth sign: the gathering of the Day is not a mass; the gathering is organized — the community is gathered behind its prophet, the band is gathered under its leader, the fawj is gathered in order. The hadith that carries the claim most directly is the hadith of the ten afwāj of the Prophet's own community: when asked about the afwāj, the Prophet ﷺ said that on the Day of Resurrection his community (ummah) would be resurrected in ten afwāj, which he then enumerated — the report preserved in the tafsīr literature from Anas ibn Mālik, as the source notes. The claim of the hadith is the claim of the sūrah's thirteenth sign: the band of the Prophet's own community is one of the ten bands, the band that is gathered under the Prophet's own leadership, the band that is the sign of the Prophet's own intercession.

**The Swoon and the Standing: The Two Movements of the Day**

The thirteenth sign of the sūrah is, in the Qur'an's own account, the sign of the two movements of the Day — the fall and the stand, the swoon and the rising. The two movements are the sūrah's own description of the Day's own sequence: first the *fall* (the swoon of the first blast, the total collapse of the created order, the heavens and the earth together, except whom God wills), and then the *stand* (the rising of the second blast, the standing of the souls, the standing that is the beginning of the Day's own work). The claim of the two movements is the claim of the sūrah's thirteenth sign: the Day is not a moment; the Day is a *sequence* — the sequence of the fall and the stand, the sequence of the swoon and the rising, the sequence of the end of the old order and the beginning of the new order.

The link between the swoon of the Day and the swoon of sleep is the sūrah's own link. The swoon of sleep (v. 9, the *rukhd*, the repose of the body) is the daily sample of the swoon of the Day — the same *taking (the yatawaffā* of 39:42, the taking of the soul at the time of its death and in its sleep), at full scale. The listener who receives the swoon of sleep well — the listener who receives the *minor death with the duʿāʾ of the bismik* (the "By You I die and I live" of the bedtime report) — is prepared for the swoon of the Day, the *major death*, the swoon that does not end in the morning. The sūrah's thirteenth sign is, in the sūrah's own structure, a sign that is about the listener's own *preparation* for the swoon of the Day — the preparation that the sūrah's own cascade (vv. 6–16) has been, all along, training.

The claim of the *afwāj* (the bands) is the claim of the sūrah's thirteenth sign in its communal register: the gathering of the Day is not a mass; the gathering is organized — the community is gathered behind its prophet, the band is gathered under its leader, the *fawj* is gathered in order. The listener's own question, in the sūrah's logic, is the question of his own *band*: which band does he come forth in? Under whose leadership is he gathered? The *faṣl (the division) presupposes the jamʿ* (the gathering) — the separation of the Day is the separation of the bands, the bands that are gathered in order, under their leaders, in the form the sūrah will describe in verses 21–36.

The thirteenth sign is the sequence; the next signs (vv. 19–20) are the spectacle of the sequence — the sky opened as if into gates, the mountains set in motion as if they were a mirage. The sūrah turns from the *order* of the Day to the *scene* of the Day.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:19

> **and the sky is opened as if it were gates,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Awbāban": The Sky Opened as into Gates**

The fourteenth sign of the sūrah's cascade names the second act of the Day: *wa-s-samāʾu fitaḥat fa-kānat abwāban* — "and the sky is opened as if it were gates." Three words carry the sign. The *samāʾ* is the sky — the same word the sūrah used in verse 12 for the seven firmaments, the built canopy, the *shidād* that does not fissure. The *fitaḥat* is the Form VI verb of f-t-ḥ, the root of opening, breaking open, conquering: the fatḥ is the opening, the fayṭ is the overflow, the fātiḥ is the one who opens. The *abwāban is the plural of bāb* — the gates, the doors, the entrances. The sūrah does not say "and the sky is torn," or "and the sky is shattered," or "and the sky falls"; it says the sky is *opened as into gates* — the built canopy of verse 12, the *shidād* that does not fissure, is *opened at its doors*, the way a city is opened at its gates, the way a house is opened at its door. The claim of the image is the claim of the sūrah's fourteenth sign: the canopy is not *destroyed; the canopy is opened* — the built thing is received through its gates, the protected ceiling of 67:5 is lifted at its doors, the protection of the canopy is replaced by the reception of the Day. The source commentary notes that the word translated "sky" and "Heaven" in these two verses is the same, *samāʾ* — the same word, the same canopy, the same built thing, at the two points of the sūrah's own story: the *built* canopy of verse 12, and the opened canopy of verse 19.

**The Descent of the Angels: The Opening as Reception**

The source commentary notes that most interpret the verse as an allusion to the descent of the angels (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī), as in 25:25, where it is referred to as the Day when the sky is split open with clouds and the angels are sent down. The Qur'an's own account of the opening is the sūrah's fourteenth sign in its eschatological register:

> *"The Day when the sky is split open (nashratu) and the angels are arranged in rows (ṣaffan)."* (Qur'an 25:25)

The two verses — 25:25 and 78:19 — carry the same claim: the sky is opened, and the angels are sent down through the opening. The opening is the reception: the canopy is opened so that the angels can descend, the gates are opened so that the witness can enter, the protection is lifted so that the reading can begin. The same claim is stated, in the sūrah's own family, at 89:22, where the angels come *in succession, following one another*, through the opened sky:

> *"And the angels will follow, one after another."* (Qur'an 89:22)

The claim of the two verses is the claim of the sūrah's fourteenth sign: the opening of the sky is the descent of the angels, the descent of the angels is the beginning of the Day's own work, the beginning of the Day's own work is the reading of the account, the reading of the account is the faṣl of the Day. The listener who receives the fourteenth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own reception — the reception of the angels, the reception of the witness, the reception of the reading.

**The Plurality of the Gates: The Opening as Entry**

The sūrah's word for the opened sky is plural: *abwāban* — gates, not a single gate. The sky does not open at one point; the sky becomes *gates* — the way a city becomes its gates at the moment of the entry, the way a house becomes its door at the moment of the reception. The plurality of the gates is the sūrah's own claim: the Day has *many entries*, not one — the entry of the angels (the descent of 25:25), the entry of the witness (the reading of the account), and the entry of the believers into the Garden, as 39:73 states:

> *"They will be brought in bands to the shade of the Garden, a pleasure from their Lord."* (Qur'an 39:73)

The sūrah's own image is confirmed by 54:11, which the source cites as the parallel: "So We opened the gates of the sky with pouring water" — the gates of the sky, in the Qur'an's own register, are the points at which the sky functions as an opening, the points at which the canopy receives rather than protects. The claim of the plurality is the claim of the sūrah's fourteenth sign: the Day is not a single event; the Day is a sequence of entries — the entry of the angels, the entry of the witness, the entry of the believers, the entry of the deniers into their appointed place (vv. 21–30). The listener who receives the fourteenth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own reception — the reception that is the Day's own work, the reception that is the reading of the account, the reception that is the faṣl of the Day.

**The Cover Lifted: The Link to the Garment of Verse 10**

The sūrah's fourteenth sign is, in the sūrah's own structure, the sign that completes the lifting of the cover begun in verse 10. The garment of the night (v. 10) is the cover of the *listener's own night*; the canopy of verse 12 is the cover of the listener's own sky. The Day lifts both: the garment of the night is removed (the Day does not end in night), and the canopy of the sky is opened (the Day is the day of the open sky, the day without the cover of the *shidād*). The sūrah's own closing scene (vv. 37–38) is the scene of the total lifting: the Spirit and the angels in rows, none speaking save whom the Compassionate permits — the scene of the *open sky*, the scene of the *no cover*, the scene of the *total visibility*. The listener who receives the fourteenth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own *visibility* — the visibility that is the Day's own work, the visibility that is the reading of the account, the visibility that is the *faṣl* of the Day.

The sky is opened; the mountains, in the next verse (v. 20), are set in motion as if they were a mirage. The scene of the Day is complete: the people stand, under the opened sky, on the shaken earth, among the vanished mountains, in the bands of their own communities, with no cover above them and no stakes around them. The next sign (v. 21) turns from the scene to the destination — the Hellfire, lying in ambush for the rebellious.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:20

> **and the mountains are set in motion as if they were a mirage.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Kinḍat": The Stakes Pulled Out**

The fifteenth sign of the sūrah's cascade closes the scene of the Day: wa-l-jibālu kinḍat fa-kānat ʿaḍaḥān — "and the mountains are set in motion as if they were a mirage." The verb kinḍat is the Form VIII of k-nḍ, the root of breaking off, being uprooted, being severed: the kinḍ is the severing, the iktidāʾ is the drawing-away. The sūrah does not say "and the mountains fall," or "and the mountains crumble," or "and the mountains are shattered"; it says the mountains are set in motion — the kinḍ, the uprooting, the severing of the stake from the ground. The link to verse 7 is the sūrah's own link: the awṭādan (the stakes) of verse 7 are the kinḍat (the uprooting) of verse 20 — the stake that held the cradle is pulled out, the holding is removed, the stability is suspended. The claim of the verse is the claim of the sūrah's fifteenth sign: the stability of the world is not a possession; the stability is a loan, and the loan is withdrawn at the Day. The source commentary notes the parallels: 81:3 ("and when the mountains are removed"), 18:47, 70:8–9, 73:14, 101:5 — the same event, described in the Qur'an's own register, in a series of images that will be examined in the next section.

**Four Images of the Same Event: Clouds, Wool, Mirage, and the Vanished**

The Qur'an describes the removal of the mountains in a series of images, each at a different stage of the disappearance, and the series is itself a sign. 27:88, quoted in the commentary on verse 7, gives the first image: the mountains that the listener *supposes are solid pass away like clouds* — the solid becomes the dispersed, the fixed becomes the drifting. 70:9 gives the second: the mountains become *tufts of red wool* — the dispersed becomes the carried-away, the stone becomes the fiber that the wind takes. 101:5 gives the third: the mountains are *hollow lumps* — the carried-away becomes the emptied, the full becomes the hollow. And this verse, 78:20, gives the fourth and final image: the mountains are *a mirage (ʿaḍaḥā*) — the hollow becomes the *invisible*, the thing becomes the *trembling of light over nothing*. The series is the sūrah's own scale of the removal: from the solid (the *awṭād* of verse 7) to the cloud (dispersed), to the wool (carried away), to the lump (emptied), to the mirage (gone). The *mirage* is the last stage, and the last stage is the *claim* of the verse: even the *solidest* thing in the world — the mountain, the stake, the anchor of the cradle — will be removed to the point of invisibility. The ʿaḍaḥā (the mirage) is the image the Quraysh knew: the shimmer on the desert road, the water that is not water, the mountain that is not a mountain — the eye sees the *shape*, and the hand finds *nothing*. The sūrah's fifteenth sign is, in its image, the negation of the solid: the solid becomes the nothing, the stake becomes the shimmer, the ground of the listener's own life becomes the light that moves over nothing.

**The Scale of the Reversal: From Stake to Mirage**

The claim of the verse, in the sūrah's own structure, is the claim of the scale of the reversal. The awṭād of verse 7 is the most stable thing in the listener's own world — the stake that holds the cradle, the mountain that does not move, the anchor that does not shift. The ʿaḍaḥā of verse 20 is the least stable thing in the listener's own world — the mirage, the shimmer, the light that moves over nothing, the image that has no substance. The scale of the reversal is the scale of the sūrah's own claim: the most stable becomes the least stable, the stake becomes the mirage, the ground becomes the shimmer. The listener who receives the fifteenth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own reversal — the reversal that is the Day's own work, the reversal that is the reading of the account, the reversal that is the faṣl of the Day. The practical weight of the claim falls on the listener's own confidence: the listener who is confident in the stability of his own world — the stability of his own house, his own name, his own waṭan (his homeland, his fixed dwelling) — is the listener who has not received the fifteenth sign. The discipline of the sign is the discipline of the open hand: the listener who receives the stability as a loan, and holds the loan with an open hand, is the listener who is prepared for the Day — the Day that removes the stake, the Day that turns the mountain into the mirage, the Day that reads the account in the open, without the cover of the canopy and without the holding of the stake.

The scene of the Day is complete: the Day is named (v. 17), the trumpet is blown and the peoples come forth in bands (v. 18), the sky is opened as if into gates (v. 19), the mountains are set in motion as if they were a mirage (v. 20). What remains, under the opened sky, on the shaken earth, among the vanished mountains, is the people — standing, gazing, in their bands, with no cover above and no stakes around. The next sign (v. 21) turns from the scene to the destination: the Hellfire, lying in ambush for the rebellious.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:21

> **Truly Hell lies in ambush,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Marṣadan": The Word of the Lie-in-Wait**

The sixteenth sign of the sūrah turns from the scene of the Day to its first destination: *inna l-jahannama kānat marṣadan* — "Truly the Fire has been lying in ambush." The word *marṣadan is the place-noun of r-ṣ-d, the root of lying in wait, taking the ambush: the marṣad* is the hunter's lie-in-wait, the place from which the hunter strikes, the position that is *held while the prey passes by without knowing*. The sūrah's choice of the word is its first claim: the Fire is not a *reaction*; the Fire is a *position — a position held, a position occupied*, a position that is *there* before the prey arrives. The verb the sūrah uses is *kānat — the was, the state — "the Fire was* lying in ambush": the ambush is *not* a new thing at the Day; the ambush is a *standing thing, a position held* from before the Day, from before the death, from before this life. The source commentary states the claim plainly: Hell is the place to which those who rebel against the truth are sent. It will come upon them like an ambush when they die, because they had been oblivious to the truth in this world. The ambush is there before the prey arrives, and the prey is the listener, and the prey is walking through the ambush right now, without knowing.

**The Ambush and the Oblivion: The Mechanism of the Lie-in-Wait**

The mechanism of the ambush is the sūrah's own claim about the gnāf — the heedlessness, the oblivion, the not-knowing that is the condition of the ambush. The ambush functions because the prey does not know it is in the line of fire; the fire is hidden not by the fire but by the blindness of the prey. The sūrah's own verses 27–28, which follow, name the content of the blindness: "Truly they anticipated not a reckoning, and denied Our signs with utter denial" — the heedlessness is the not-anticipating, the denial, the not-knowing that is the condition of the ambush. The logic of the ambush is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the fire is not a trick — the fire is open, the Qur'an names it, describes it, warns of it (cf. 44:43–55, the long description of the Fire in Sūrah al-Duḥā's neighbor, Sūrah al-Hijr's neighbor, Sūrah al-Ṭūr); the warning is public, the blindness is private. The ambush is the public fire plus the private blindness — the fire that is named and the blindness that is chosen. The hadith that carries the claim of the fire's own fuel is the hadith of the anger:

> "*The Fire is kindled with anger (annāra tuqādu bi-l-ghaḍab)."* (al-Bukhārī)

The claim of the hadith is the claim of the sūrah's sixteenth sign: the fire is fueled by the anger — the anger of the prey at the truth, the anger of the denier at the warning, the anger that is the fuel of the fire. The listener who receives the sixteenth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own ambush — the ambush that is held, the ambush that is there, the ambush that is walking through the listener's own life, right now, without knowing.

The sixteenth sign is the ambush; the next sign (v. 22) names the inhabitants of the ambush — the rebellious, the *mujrimīn*, the ones for whom the ambush is held.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:22

> **a place unto which the rebellious return**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Mujrimīn": The Word of the Transgression Complete**

The seventeenth sign of the sūrah names the inhabitants of the ambush: *muqāman li-l-mujrimīn* — "a station for the rebellious" (the source renders the verse "a place unto which the rebellious return"). The word *mujrimīn is the plural of mujrim* — the one who commits the *jināyah*, the transgression, the crime. The root j-r-m is the root of transgression: the jinniyya is the transgression, the jarm is the cutting, the tajarrum is the breaking of the covenant. The sūrah's word is not a word for a *minor fault*; the sūrah's word is a word for a transgression complete — the transgression that is a rebellion, the transgression that is a cutting of the covenant, the transgression that is a breaking of the treaty. The Qur'an's own usage of the word confirms the claim: 2:27, in the sūrah's own family, says of the one who fabricates a lie against God:

> *"*And whoever fabricates a lie against God after that — it is they who are the criminals (fa-ulāʾika hum al-mujrimūn)." (Qur'an 2:27)

The claim of the verse is the claim of the sūrah's seventeenth sign: the mujrim is the one who fabricates — the one who makes a lie against the truth, the one who cuts the covenant, the one who breaks the treaty. The listener who receives the seventeenth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own covenant — the covenant that is cut, the covenant that is broken, the covenant that is the sign of the Agent's own design.

**"Muqāman": The Station, Not the Journey**

The first half of the verse names the form of the destination: muqāman — the station, the abode, the place of standing. The root q-w-m is the root of standing, remaining, being established: the qiyām is the standing, the maqām is the station, the muqām is the abode. The sūrah does not say "a place through which the rebellious pass," or "a place from which the rebellious escape"; the sūrah says a station — the place of standing, the abode, the place where the journey ends. The logic of the station is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the fire is not a journey; the fire is a station — the journey of this world ends at the station of the Day, and the station is the destination. The source commentary notes that the verse is understood as a reference to the place to which those who rebel against the truth are sent — the place is the station, the station is the destination, the destination is the muqām. The contrast with the believers' station (v. 31, "a place of triumph") is the sūrah's own contrast: the two stations — the fire and the Garden — are the two destinations of the faṣl, the two ends of the division, the two places of the Day's own work. The listener who receives the seventeenth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own station — the station that is the destination, the station that is the end, the station that is the faṣl of the Day.

The seventeenth sign is the station; the next sign (v. 23) names the period of the station — "to tarry therein for ages."

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:23

> **to tarry therein for ages.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Aḥqāban": The Word of the Ages**

The eighteenth sign of the sūrah names the period of the station: *yaqbathūna fīhā aḥqāban* — "to tarry therein for ages." The word *aḥqāban is the plural of ḥaqb* — the age, the period, the span of time: the *ḥaqb is the age, the ʿaṣr is the era, the aḥqāb is the ages*. The sūrah does not say "to tarry therein for a moment," or "for an hour," or "for a day"; it says for *ages — the plural of the age, the succession of the ages, the period that is not one but many*. The source commentary notes that *aḥqāban* indicates periods of time, and that the vast majority of commentators maintain that it indicates Hell will be eternal, with one period following after another endlessly. Others say that, although Hell is "endless" in relation to our understanding of time, it is of limited duration, limited "ages," in the absolute sense. The two readings are, in the sūrah's own structure, the two faces of the same claim: the eternity of the fire is the eternity of the ages — the succession of the ages, the plural of the period, the span that is not one but many. The logic of the plural is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the fire is not a moment; the fire is a span — the span of the ages, the span that is not one but many.

**The Mustard Seed at the End of the Ages**

The source commentary carries a famous hadith that the tradition attaches to the verse, and the hadith is the sūrah's own extension of the claim:

> "On the Day of Judgment, those who deserve the Garden will enter the Garden, and those who deserve the Fire, the Fire. Thereupon God will say: 'Take out everyone in whose heart there was as much faith as a grain of a mustard seed.' Then they will be taken out already blackened and will be cast into the River of Life. Then they will sprout as an herb sprouts by the side of a stream. Do you not see how it comes out yellow and budding?" (as preserved in the tafsīr literature; cf. the Qur'an's own parallel at 10:61, "Not a mustard seed's weight, whether in the earth or in the heaven, is hidden, but it will come in a clear Book")

The claim of the hadith is the claim of the sūrah's eighteenth sign: the ages are not the end — the ages are the span of the fire, and the span is read at its end — the reading of the mustard seed, the grain of faith in the heart of the denier, the grain that is the sign of the Agent's own mercy. The Qur'an's own parallel (10:61) carries the claim of the grain: the mustard seed is not hidden — the grain is read, the grain is seen, the grain is the sign of the Agent's own mercy. The listener who receives the eighteenth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own mercy — the mercy that is read at the end of the ages, the mercy that is the mustard seed, the mercy that is the grain of faith in the heart of the denier.

**The Scale of the Punishment: The Proportion of the Transgression**

The logic of the ages is the logic of the proportion: the transgression (v. 22, the mujrimīn, the rebellious) is eternal in its orientation — the rebellion is continuous, the denial is standing, the transgression is not a moment but a posture. The punishment is proportionate to the transgression: the transgression is a posture, and the punishment is a span — the span of the ages, the span that is proportionate to the posture. The source commentary notes, in its account of verse 26, that the recompense is "fitting" in that it is proportionate to the offense committed, for the recompense of an evil is an evil like unto it (42:40) and God wrongs not His servants (3:182). The claim of the proportion is the claim of the sūrah's eighteenth sign: the ages are not arbitrary; the ages are proportionate — the span of the punishment is the span of the transgression, the duration of the fire is the duration of the rebellion, the plurality of the ages is the plurality of the denials. The listener who receives the eighteenth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own justice — the justice that is proportionate, the justice that is not arbitrary, the justice that is the faṣl of the Day.

The eighteenth sign is the span; the next signs (vv. 24–25) name the content of the span — the drinks of the Fire, the boiling liquid and the cold, murky fluid.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:24

> **They taste therein neither coolness nor drink,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Privation Before the Punishment: "Neither Coolness Nor Drink"**

The nineteenth sign of the sūrah turns from the span to the content of the station: *yaṭʿamūna fīhā barīdan wa-lā sharāban* — "They taste therein neither coolness nor drink." The sūrah does not begin, at this point, by naming the punishment; it begins by naming the privation — the coolness that is not there, the drink that is not there. The word barīdan is the coolness, the cold refreshment, the drink that quenches; the word sharāban is the drink itself, the beverage, the liquid that satisfies the thirst. The logic of the privation is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the punishment is not a thing; the punishment is a lack — the lack of the coolness, the lack of the drink, the lack of the quenching. The thirst is the need; the coolness is the satisfaction; the fire is the thirst without the satisfaction — the need that is not met, the thirst that is not quenched, the body that is not satisfied. The psychology of the thirst is the psychology of the body's own need — the need that cannot be delegated, the need that cannot be outsourced, the need that is the body's own. The punishment of the fire, in the sūrah's own account, is the punishment of the body — the body that is thirsty, the body that is not quenched, the body that is not satisfied. The listener who receives the nineteenth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own punishment — the punishment that is a lack, the punishment that is a privation, the punishment that is the thirst without the quenching.

**The Window onto the Nature of Hell: The Extremes at Both Ends**

The source commentary offers an observation on the verse and its neighbor that is worth carrying forward, because it opens a window onto the *design* of the Fire that the sūrah's own structure confirms. The observation: this verse and similar verses show that Hell is characterized not just by fire and heat, but by extremes at both ends of the spectrum of temperature and all of the tension such extremes entail — whereas the Garden is described as a place of perfect tranquility. Although in the Garden there are all the wine, fruits, and pure companions one could want, there is no indulgence. Peace and joy lie in moderation; torture lies in the extremes. The claim of the observation is the claim of the sūrah's nineteenth sign: the fire is not heat; the fire is the extreme — the extreme of the boiling (the ḥamīm of the next verse), the *extreme of the cold (the ghaṣṣāq* of the next verse), the extreme that is the tension of the body, the tension that is the punishment. The contrast with the Garden is the sūrah's own contrast: the Garden is the moderation — the coolness of the river, the smoothness of the cup, the pleasantness of the drink, as 76:6 states:

**The "Therein": The Scope of the Privation**

The claim of the two readings is the claim of the sūrah's nineteenth sign: the privation is not one; the privation is a span — the span of the ages, the span that is not one but many. The nineteenth sign is the privation; the next sign (v. 25) names the two drinks of the privation — the boiling liquid (*ḥamīm*) and the cold, murky fluid (*ghaṣṣāq*).

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:25

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:26

> **a fitting recompense.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Majūdan": The Word of the Fit**

The twenty-first sign of the sūrah judges the whole scene of verses 21–25: *dhālika jazāʾun majūdan* — "a fitting recompense." The word *majūdan* is the passive participle of q-w-d, the root of fitting, being proportionate, being as it should be: the maqūd is the fit, the munsāb is the proportion, the musāwwi is the equivalent. The source commentary carries three readings of the word, and the three are the sūrah's own three claims. First, the recompense is *fitting in that it is proportionate to the offense committed* — the recompense of an evil is an evil like unto it (42:40), as the Qur'an states:

> *"And the recompense of evil is evil like unto it."* (Qur'an 42:40)

Second, the word can be read in the sense of *being equal to it*, as at 73:26 ("Truly, our Lord is sufficient") and 79:40 ("In that is sufficient"). Third, the recompense is *fitting in that God wrongs not His servants* (3:182; see also 10:44; 22:10) — the fit is the justice, and the justice is the proportion, and the proportion is the claim of the sūrah's twenty-first sign. The deictic word dhālika — the that — points back to the whole scene of verses 21–25: the ambush (v. 21), the station (v. 22), the ages (v. 23), the privation (v. 24), the two drinks (v. 25). The claim of the deictic is the claim of the sūrah's twenty-first sign: the scene is not random; the scene is a fit — the punishment that fits the sin, the recompense that measures the transgression, the justice that proportionates the sin and the punishment. The listener who receives the twenty-first sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own justice — the justice that is a fit, the justice that is a proportion, the justice that is the faṣl of the Day.

**The Fit as Both Terror and Comfort**

The logic of the fit is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the punishment is not arbitrary; the punishment is a fit. The terror of the fit is the terror of the listener's own sin — the sin is read in the fit, the sin is measured by the fit, the sin is the content of the fit. But the comfort of the fit is the comfort of the listener's own justice — the punishment does not exceed the sin; the recompense does not go beyond the transgression; the justice of the Agent is the justice of the proportion. The  Qur'an's own claim of the proportion is the claim of 21:47, which states:

> *"*We have set the Balance for the Day of Resurrection, so no soul will be wronged in the least." (Qur'an 21:47)

The claim of the verse is the claim of the sūrah's twenty-first sign: the fit is the Balance — the proportion that measures the sin, the proportion that does not wrong the soul, the proportion that is the justice of the Agent. The listener who receives the twenty-first sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own proportion — the proportion that is the fit, the proportion that is the justice, the proportion that is the faṣl of the Day.

The twenty-first sign is the fit; the next signs (vv. 27–28) name the reason of the fit — the sin that the fit answers to, the denial of the reckoning and the denial of the signs.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:27

> **Truly they anticipated not a reckoning,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Yajhadūna": The Word of the Not-Anticipating**

The twenty-second sign of the sūrah names the reason of the fit: *innahum kānū l-ḥisāba yajhadūn* — "Truly they anticipated not a reckoning." The source commentary states the claim plainly: as the disbelievers did not believe in the Resurrection and Divine Judgment, they did not anticipate the reckoning and did not fear it or seek to avoid it by accounting for their own actions (al-Qurṭubī). Their recompense is therefore of their own doing, since it is they who have wronged themselves (3:117; 10:44; 16:118). The word yajhadūn is the not-anticipating, the not-expecting, the not-weighing of the ḥisāb — the reckoning, the account, the reading of the book. The logic of the not-anticipating is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the listener who does not anticipate the reckoning lives without the weight — the weight of the account, the weight of the reading, the weight of the book. The life without the weight is the life of the denier, and the life of the denier is the life that brings upon itself the fit of verse 26. The claim of the source is the claim of the sūrah's twenty-second sign: the recompense is of their own doing — the fit is not imposed; the fit is chosen, the fit is brought, the fit is the consequence of the not-anticipating.

**The Weight and the Open Hand: The Discipline of the Anticipating**

The contrast of the sign is the contrast of the sūrah's own method: the denier does not anticipate the reckoning and lives without the weight; the believer anticipates the reckoning and lives with the weight. The weight of the anticipating is the weight of the account — the weight that the sūrah has been training the listener to carry, from the first sign (the questioning* of verse 1) to the *twenty-second sign (the not-anticipating of the denier). The discipline of the anticipating is the discipline of the open hand: the listener who anticipates the reckoning holds the loan of the world (the stability of the mountains, the coolness of the night, the livelihood of the day) with an open hand, the hand that is ready for the account, the hand that is ready for the reading, the hand that is ready for the book. The  Qur'an's own claim of the anticipating is the claim of the ḥisāb — the reckoning that is the work of the Day, the reading that is the faṣl of the Day, the book that is the record of the listener's own life. The listener who receives the twenty-second sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own account — the account that is the weight, the account that is the reading, the account that is the faṣl of the Day.

**The "Reckoning" and the "Book": The Two Names of the Same Work**

The word ḥisāb — the reckoning — is the first name of the work of the Day; the word kitāb — the book — will be the second name of the same work, in verse 29, where the sūrah states that everything has been recorded in a book. The two names are the two faces of the same claim: the reckoning is the reading of the book, the book is the record of the reckoning, the two are the work of the Day. The listener who receives the twenty-second sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own book — the book that is recorded, the book that is read, the book that is the faṣl of the Day.

The twenty-second sign is the not-anticipating; the next sign (v. 28) names the second reason of the fit — the denial of the signs with utter denial.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:28

> **and denied Our signs with utter denial.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Kadhdhāban": The Word of the Utter Denial**

The twenty-third sign of the sūrah names the second reason of the fit: wa-kadhdhabū kadhdhāban — "and denied Our signs with utter denial." The source commentary notes two things. First, Our signs indicates all aspects of revelation — the signs of the sūrah (vv. 6–16), the signs of the Qur'an as a whole, the signs of the Agent's own design. Second, the word translated "utter denial" is kadhdhāb — the denial at the maximum of its force. The source notes that it could also be rendered "as deniers" if read as kudhdhāb (al-Qurṭubī, Rāzī, Zamakhsharī); or kudhdhāb could be read in the singular as a name of emphasis and thus rendered "as does a great denier." The claim of the two readings is the claim of the sūrah's twenty-third sign: the denial is not partial; the denial is utter — the denial of the signs at the maximum of the force, the denial that is the posture of the denier, the denial that is the work of the denier's own life. The signs that are denied are the signs of the sūrah's own cascade — the earth (v. 6), the mountains (v. 7), the pairs (v. 8), the sleep (v. 9), the night (v. 10), the day (v. 11), the firmaments (v. 12), the lamp (v. 13), the rain (v. 14), the grain and the gardens (vv. 15–16) — the ten signs of the first movement, the ten signs that the denier has walked through, daily, without knowing, denied by the denial of the not-knowing.

**The Denial as a Posture, Not an Opinion**

The logic of the utter denial is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the denial is not an opinion; the denial is a posture. The opinion can be changed; the posture must be turned. The denial of the denier is the posture of the life — the life that is lived without the weight (v. 27), the life that is walked through the signs without the knowledge of the signs, the life that is brought upon itself the fit of verse 26. The  Qur'an's own claim of the posture is the claim of the ṣiddīq and the kāfir — the truthful one and the denier, the two postures that the sūrah has been contrasting from the first verse (the questioning* of verse 1) to the *twenty-third sign (the utter denial of verse 28). The listener who receives the twenty-third sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own posture — the posture of the signs, the posture of the denial, the posture that is the faṣl of the Day. The practical weight of the claim falls on the listener's own life: the listener who lives in the posture of the denial — the life that walks through the signs without knowing — is the listener who brings upon himself the fit of the sūrah's own twenty-first sign. The discipline of the sign is the discipline of the turning: the listener who turns from the posture of the denial to the posture of the knowing is the listener who receives the signs as signs, the listener who carries the weight of the account, the listener who is prepared for the Day.

**The "Our": The Signs of the Agent**

The word Our signs — the signs with the possessive of the Agent — is the claim of the sūrah's twenty-third sign: the signs are not random; the signs are ours — the signs of the Agent, the signs of the design, the signs that are the work of the Agent's own hand. The denial of the signs is the denial of the Agent — the denial of the hand that built the canopy (v. 12), the hand that sent the rain (v. 14), the hand that made the night a garment (v. 10). The claim of the possessive is the claim of the sūrah's own method: the signs are ours, and the denial of the signs is the denial of the Agent, and the denial of the Agent is the reason of the fit. The listener who receives the twenty-third sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own hand — the hand that built, the hand that sent, the hand that made.

The twenty-third sign is the denial; the next sign (v. 29) names the record of the denial — everything, recorded in a book.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:29

> **And everything have We recorded in a book.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"ʿAdadnāhu Kitāban": The Word of the Recording**

The twenty-fourth sign of the sūrah names the record of the denial: *wa-kulla shayʾin ʿadadnāhu kitāban* — "And everything have We recorded in a book." The source commentary states the claim: this verse refers to the book in which all deeds are recorded by angels (see 86:4; commentary on 43:80; 50:17–18; 82:10–12) or to the Preserved Tablet (85:22; al-Qurṭubī). Since the disbelievers' transgressions are as described in verses 27–28 and since God has recorded everything, theirs is a fitting recompense (v. 26); cf. 36:12. In a book, translating *kitāban*, could also be rendered "in writing" (al-Qurṭubī, Ṭabarī). The claim of the two readings — the book of the recording angels and the Preserved Tablet — is the claim of the sūrah's twenty-fourth sign: the record is not one; the record is a series — the series of the recording, the series of the preservation, the series that is the work of the Agent's own hand. The word ʿadadnāhu — the We recorded — is the Form I of ʿ-d-d, the root of reckoning, recording, enumerating: the ʿidd is the reckoning, the taʿdād is the enumeration, the maʿdūd is the counted. The word everything — kulla shayʾin — is the claim of the sūrah's twenty-fourth sign: the record is not partial; the record is total. The record is the record of the whole — the whole of the life of the denier, the whole of the signs of the Agent, the whole of the denial of the signs. The  Qur'an's own claim of the record is the claim of 82:10–12, which states:

> *"*And indeed, over you we have appointed keepers, noble and recording, who record what you do." (Qur'an 82:10–12)

The claim of the verses is the claim of the sūrah's twenty-fourth sign: the recording is the work of the angels, the work that is the record of the deeds, the work that is the book of the Day. The listener who receives the twenty-fourth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own record — the record that is total, the record that is preserved, the record that is the faṣl of the Day.

**The Book as the Answer to the Denial**

The logic of the sign is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the denial of the signs (v. 28) is answered by the record of the signs (v. 29). The denier says no to the signs; the book says yes to the signs — the yes of the record, the yes of the preservation, the yes that is the work of the Agent's own hand. The book is the answer to the denial, and the answer is the claim of the sūrah's own method: the denial does not erase the signs; the denial is recorded, and the record is preserved, and the preservation is the claim of the Agent's own hand. The listener who receives the twenty-fourth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own book — the book that is the answer to the denial, the book that is the record of the signs, the book that is the faṣl of the Day.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:32

> **gardens and vineyards,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"ʿaynin": The Word of the Vineyard, and the Spring**

The twenty-seventh sign of the sūrah names the content of the place of triumph: *janātan wa-ʿaynin* — "gardens and vineyards." The word ʿaynin is the vineyard — the plantation of the vine and the date palm, the grove of the oasis, the cultivated place of the oasis. The same word — ʿayn — is the spring, the well, the source of the water. The claim of the double meaning is the claim of the sūrah's twenty-seventh sign: the vineyard is the spring — the vineyard grows at the spring, the vineyard is the cultivation of the water, the vineyard is the work of the water. **The "Whosoever Will": The Word of the Choice**

The word whosoever will — the choice, the freedom, the ikhtiyār — is the claim of the sūrah's thirty-fourth sign: the path is not forced; the path is chosen — the choice of the listener, the choice that is the completion of the doubt, the choice that is the work of the listener's own hand. The contrast with the silence of the Day (vv. 37–38) is the sūrah's own contrast: the silence of the Day is the silence of the no choice — the no choice of the Day, the no choice that is the completion of the speech; the choice of the world (v. 39) is the choice of the now — the now of the listener, the now that is the completion of the doubt, the now that is the work of the listener's own hand. The listener who receives the thirty-fourth sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own choice — the choice of the path, the choice of the return, the choice that is the faṣl of the Day. The thirty-fourth sign is the invitation; the next sign (v. 40) is the final warning of the sūrah — the punishment nigh, the day of the seeing, the cry of the disbeliever.

## Sūrah al-Nabāʾ 78:40

> **Truly We have warned you of a punishment nigh, on a day when a man beholds what his hands have sent forth, and the disbeliever says, "Oh, would that I were dust!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"ʿAḍhāban Qarīban": The Word of the Nigh**

The final sign of the sūrah returns to the opening: *laqad naḍḍarannā kum ʿadhāban qarīban* — "Truly We have warned you of a punishment nigh." The *source commentary states the claim*: according to most, this entire verse points to disbelievers, for disbelievers will behold and dwell upon the evil of their deeds, whereas believers will experience the Forgiveness and Mercy of God. According to others, *a man beholds what his hands have sent forth* (see 75:13c; 82:5c) refers to believers, who will experience reward for their good deeds, in contrast to disbelievers, who will wish that they were nothing (Rāzī). The word qarīban — the nigh — is the word of the nearness: the punishment is nigh — the nearness of the Day, the nearness that is the completion of the warning, the nearness that is the work of the Agent's own hand. The logic of the nearness is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the sūrah begins with the Great Tiding (v. 2) — the Tiding of the Resurrection, the Tiding that is the work of the listener's own life; the sūrah ends with the warning of the punishment nigh (v. 40) — the warning that is the completion of the Tiding, the warning that is the work of the listener's own choice. The arc is the sūrah's own claim: the Tiding of the opening (v. 2) is the warning of the closing (v. 40) — the Tiding is the warning, the warning is the Tiding.

**"Mā Qaddamat Yādah": The Word of the Beholding**

The second half of the sign — the on a day when a man beholds what his hands have sent forth — is the claim of the sūrah's final sign: the Day is the seeing — the seeing of the deed, the seeing that is the completion of the warning, the seeing that is the work of the listener's own hand. The source commentary notes that a man beholds what his hands have sent forth (see 75:13c; 82:5c) — the seeing of the deed, the seeing that is the work of the Day. The logic of the seeing is the logic of the sūrah's own method: the Day is not a hearing; the Day is a seeing — the seeing of the deed, the seeing that is the completion of the book (v. 29), the seeing that is the work of the listener's own hand. The listener who receives the final sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own seeing — the seeing of the deed, the seeing of the book, the seeing that is the faṣl of the Day.

**"Yā Laytanī Kuntu Turāban": The Word of the Cry**

The third half of the sign — the cry of the disbeliever, "Oh, would that I were dust!" — is the claim of the sūrah's final sign: the cry of the disbeliever is the completion of the warning. The source commentary carries the five readings of the cry. First, the cry could indicate that one wishes either to have never been born or to be back in the grave and not resurrected (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī), as in 4:42: "On that Day those who disbelieved and disobeyed the Messenger will wish that they were level with the earth, and they will conceal no account from God" (Rāzī). Second, it can be taken as an indication that those who took pride in their high station in this world will prefer that they had had a low station that had not made them prideful and insolent (see also 69:25–27). Third, many commentaries convey an account that links this verse to the resurrection of animals: traditional Islamic teachings maintain that all animals will be resurrected, then God will rectify all injustices between them, and according to many, when the judgment between them is finished, He will say to them "Be dust," and they will all become dust; upon witnessing this, disbelievers will prefer the fate of the animals to their own and say, "Oh, would that I were dust" (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, Ṭabarī, Ṭaybul). Fourth, a few commentators interpret the disbeliever as a reference to Satan, who, when he sees the rewards received by believing human beings, wishes that he too had been made of dust or clay, thus altering his previous evaluation: "I am better than him. Thou hast created me from fire, while Thou hast created him from clay" (7:12; 38:76; al-Qurṭubī, Rāzī, Ṭabarī). Fifth, in some Shīʿite commentaries, the Arabic for the cry is read *yā laytanī kuntu turābiyyan*, that is, "Oh, would that I were a follower of Abū Turāb," meaning ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, the first Shīʿite Imam (Kāshif, Qummī); in this sense Shīʿites may be inverting the pejorative name *Turābiyyah* ("the followers of Abū Turāb"), which some of their opponents gave to them, and wearing it as a badge of honor.

**The Final Claim: The Warning and the Path**

The claim of the final sign is the claim of the sūrah's own method: the warning of the punishment nigh (v. 40) is the answer to the invitation of the path of return (v. 39) — the invitation is the now, the now of the listener, the now that is the completion of the doubt. The sūrah begins with the questioning (v. 1) and ends with the warning (v. 40): the questioning of the world is the sample of the warning of the Day; the warning is the completion of the questioning. The listener who receives the final sign with attention receives, in the sign, the claim of the Agent's own warning — the warning of the punishment nigh, the warning of the seeing, the warning that is the faṣl of the Day, the warning that is the Great Tiding of the opening, the warning that is the work of the listener's own choice.
