# Sūrah al-Māʿūn (Chapter 107) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Seven-Verse Mirror Held Up to Religion Without Heart**

Sūrah al-Māʿūn, The Small Kindness, is one of the shortest chapters of the Qur'an — seven brief verses — yet it contains one of the most searching diagnoses of religious failure in the entire Book. In a few strokes it moves from creed to character, from belief about the Last Day to behavior toward the orphan and the poor, and then from outward worship to the inward sincerity that gives worship its life. It names no tribe, legislates no ritual detail, and tells no extended story. It simply describes a type of person: one who denies the Recompense, repels the orphan, ignores the hungry, prays heedlessly, prays to be seen, and withholds the smallest help. Every reader is then left with the unsettling question with which the sūrah opens: have you seen this one — and could he be you.

The sūrah takes its name from its final word, al-māʿūn, in the last verse. Classical Arabic lexicons and commentaries record a wide range of meanings for this word, from neighborly utensils lent between houses, to zakāh, to water, to every small benefit that passes from hand to hand. That breadth is fitting. A sūrah that begins with the greatest matter — dīn, religion and judgment — ends with the smallest matter — a borrowed pot, a cup of water, a helping hand. The message of the pairing is that in the Qur'anic vision there is no great religion without small kindness. The one who fails in the least visible act of generosity has already revealed something decisive about the state of his faith in the most momentous reality.

**Where and When It Was Revealed**

Most commentators maintain that al-Māʿūn is an early Makkan sūrah, most likely revealed after Sūrah 102, al-Takāthur, although some maintain that verses 4–7 were revealed in Madinah. This divided opinion is recorded in the source commentary and reflects a genuine tension in the text itself. On the one hand, the opening verses speak of denying the dīn and mistreating orphans and the poor — themes that belong unmistakably to the Makkan period, when the Qur'an confronted a wealthy, proud Qurayshī elite that prided itself on lineage while neglecting the weak. On the other hand, verses 4–7 speak of heedlessness and hypocrisy in prayer and of showing off — phenomena that became prominent in Madinah, where Islam held political power and where some therefore prayed for advantage rather than conviction.

The source commentary notes the argument for a fully Madinan dating: given that all of its verses treat heedlessness and hypocrisy in the performance of religious obligations, including specifically prayer in verses 4–5, the entire sūrah may be from the Madinan period, since it was not until political advantages could be gained by following the Prophet that hypocrisy became an issue for the early Muslim community. This reasoning has weight, and it is connected to the treatment of hypocrisy in Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn:

> *"When the hypocrites come to you, they say, 'We bear witness that you are indeed the Messenger of God.' And God knows that you are indeed His Messenger, and God bears witness that the hypocrites are indeed liars."* (Qur'an 63:1)

Yet the majority held to a Makkan origin, and their reasoning also has weight. Hypocrisy in religion was not seen as limited to Muslims alone, nor to Madinah alone. The Makkan idolaters also performed acts of worship — circumambulation, sacrifice, oaths by God — while their hearts denied the Resurrection and their hands oppressed the weak. Their religion was itself a kind of showing off: devotion as tribal display. The Qur'an describes their prayer at the House in terms that already anticipate the charge of heedless, hollow worship:

> *"Their prayer at the House is naught but whistling and clapping. So taste the punishment for having disbelieved!"* (Qur'an 8:35)

Whether Makkan, Madinan, or divided between the two periods, the sūrah's message transcends its occasion. Reports that attach it to named individuals — al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, Abū Sufyān, Abū Jahl, or al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah — are best read the way the classical scholars read them: as instances of a universal pattern, not as limits upon it. The sūrah describes a moral type that appears in every age, including among professing believers. That is why Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī all move swiftly from the question of occasion to the question of meaning: whoever does these things, wherever and whenever, has placed himself under the sūrah's warning.

**Its Place Between Two Neighboring Sūrahs**

In the arrangement of the muṣḥaf, al-Māʿūn stands between Sūrah Quraysh (106) and Sūrah al-Kawthar (108), and the neighborhood is instructive. Sūrah Quraysh reminds the tribe of Quraysh of God's favors — security, sustenance, the shelter of the sacred House — and commands the gratitude those favors require:

> *"So let them worship the Lord of this House, Who fed them against hunger and secured them against fear."* (Qur'an 106:3–4)

Al-Māʿūn then exposes those who received such favors and returned ingratitude: they repel the orphan and refuse to feed the hungry, though they themselves were fed against hunger. And al-Kawthar follows with the positive counterpart — abundance given to the Prophet, answered by prayer and sacrifice offered sincerely to God alone:

> *"So pray unto your Lord and sacrifice."* (Qur'an 108:2)

Read in sequence, the three sūrahs form a complete lesson: blessing acknowledged (106), blessing betrayed (107), blessing answered with sincere devotion (108). The heedless, showy prayer condemned in al-Māʿūn stands in direct contrast to the sincere prayer commanded in al-Kawthar. The sequence teaches that worship is the test of gratitude, and sincerity is the test of worship.

**The Architecture of the Seven Verses**

The sūrah divides naturally into two movements joined by a single logic. Verses 1–3 concern the rights of God's creatures: denial of the Recompense produces hardness toward the orphan and indifference toward the hungry. Verses 4–7 concern the rights of God: the same inner disease produces prayer that is heedless, prayer that is performed for spectators, and a hand closed even to the smallest kindness. The first movement shows faithlessness toward man; the second shows faithlessness toward God. Together they demonstrate that the two cannot be separated. Whoever truly believed he would stand before God tomorrow would not repel the orphan today; and whoever prays only to be admired has already shown that his heart does not expect a morrow in which admiration will be worthless.

This structure also answers a question the modern reader may ask: why does a sūrah about creed spend most of its space on manners? The Qur'anic answer is that manners are creed made visible. Belief in the Last Day is not an abstract opinion stored in the mind; it is a conviction that reorganizes desire, spending, speech, and prayer. Where that reorganization is absent, the sūrah permits the inference to run backward: the cruelty and the hollow worship are evidence about the belief. As the source commentary observes, when verses 2–7 are viewed in relation to verse 1, the implication is that denying dīn, whether it be religion or judgment, is the root of all other sins and transgressions.

**How This Commentary Proceeds**

What follows treats each of the seven verses as a complete, independent discourse, drawing upon the Arabic language, Qur'anic cross-references with their actual texts, authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the views of the classical commentators, and the psychological, social, and practical dimensions of each teaching. Verses that the source commentary treats in pairs — verses 1–2 and verses 4–5 — are here separated and given full individual treatment, as the verse-by-verse method requires. Nothing has been compressed because the chapter is short; if anything, the brevity of the sūrah demands the opposite, since each of its few words was chosen to carry the weight of volumes.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:1

> **Hast thou seen the one who denies religion?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is Really an Exposure**

The sūrah opens with a question addressed to the Prophet, and through him to every reader: a ra'ayta — hast thou seen, hast thou considered, hast thou reflected upon. The verb ra'ā in Arabic means both physical seeing and mental considering, and the Qur'an often uses it in this second sense to summon attention to a reality the listener has observed but not yet understood. The question does not seek information. God knows whom He describes, and the Prophet had certainly seen such men among the chiefs of Quraysh. The question functions instead as an unveiling: look again, it says, at a familiar figure — the respectable denier, the prosperous man of standing — and see him now for what his deeds reveal him to be.

This interrogative opening appears elsewhere in the Qur'an at moments of moral unmasking. The same verb opens Sūrah al-ʿAlaq's rebuke of the one who forbids prayer:

> *"Hast thou seen the one who forbids a servant when he prays?"* (Qur'an 96:9–10)

And it recurs in questions about those who turn away, hoard, and deceive. The pattern teaches something about Qur'anic pedagogy: before the Book legislates, it trains perception. It teaches the believer to see through social surfaces — wealth, eloquence, tribal rank, religious performance — to the moral substance beneath. The man described here may have been honored in Makkan society. The sūrah asks the reader to look past the honor to the hardness.

**The Grammar of Denial**

The one described is alladhī yukadhdhibu bi'l-dīn — he who denies, who belies, who calls the lie upon al-dīn. The verb is in the second derived form, kaddhaba, which carries an intensive and deliberate sense: not a passing doubt or an inherited error, but an active, settled rejection, a treating of the truth as falsehood. It is the same verb used of those who denied the messengers and the signs throughout the Qur'an, as in the description of the end of the deniers:

> *"Then see how was the end of the deniers."* (Qur'an 43:25)

The preposition bi- extends the denial over the whole of its object: he denies by and against the dīn in all its dimensions. And the form yukadhdhibu, in the imperfect tense, suggests persistence — a continuing posture, not a single lapse. This is a settled character, a way of being in the world.

Ibn Kathīr, following the early authorities, reads this denial as comprehensive. It embraces denial of God, denial of the Messenger, and denial of the Resurrection and Recompense. Al-Ṭabarī records the same breadth: the verb covers all who treat the message brought by the Prophet as a lie, in whole or in the part that concerns the Hereafter. The sūrah will then prove the charge not by theology but by biography: watch what this man does to the orphan, the poor, the prayer, and the neighbor — and the denial will no longer need to be asserted, for it will have been displayed.

**What Dīn Means Here**

Religion here translates dīn, one of the richest words in the Qur'anic vocabulary. The classical commentators noted two principal senses, both live in this verse. First, dīn means religion: the complete revealed way — belief, worship, law, and morals — sent through the Prophet. To deny the dīn in this sense is to reject Islam itself, to call the Messenger a liar and his message a fabrication. Second, dīn means judgment, recompense, the settling of accounts: the Day of dīn is the Day of Judgment, as in the opening chapter of the Qur'an:

> *"Master of the Day of Judgment."* (Qur'an 1:4)

To deny the dīn in this second sense is to deny the Resurrection, the Gathering, the Balance, and the Recompense — to live as though actions dissolve into nothing and the grave is the end.

These two senses are not rivals; they are roots of a single tree. Whoever denies the religion necessarily denies the judgment it announces, and whoever denies the judgment has emptied the religion of its nerve. Al-Rāzī, developing a point found in al-Ṭabarī and others, observes that denial of the Hereafter is the practical engine of all the vices the sūrah goes on to list. A man who believed with certainty that he would stand before God, that his repelling of the orphan would be replayed before the Judge, that his hoarded morsel would testify against him — such a man might still sin from weakness, but he could not make cruelty his settled character. It is the removal of the Reckoning that removes the restraint. The sūrah's logic is therefore exact: verse 1 names the root, and verses 2–7 exhibit the branches. Cut the root of accountability, and every branch of mercy withers.

**Why the Qur'an Reasons from Deeds to Creed**

A modern reader may object that conduct does not prove belief — that a cruel man might still sincerely believe in God and the Last Day, and that judging his creed from his manners is unfair. The sūrah anticipates this objection by the precision of its portrait. It does not describe a believer who sins and repents, who wrongs the orphan in a moment of anger and then weeps and makes amends. It describes a settled pattern across every domain: toward the weak (verses 2–3), toward God in prayer (verses 4–5), toward spectators (verse 6), toward neighbors (verse 7). A single sin may coexist with faith; a comprehensive inversion of faith's fruits, maintained without remorse, testifies to the state of the root.

The Qur'an applies this reasoning elsewhere with equal directness. Of those who refuse the poor their due, it records their revealing excuse:

> *"And when it is said to them, 'Spend from what God has provided you,' those who disbelieve say to those who believe, 'Are we to feed those whom, if God willed, He would feed?'"* (Qur'an 36:47)

The verse exposes how unbelief manufactures theology to license miserliness. Similarly, the Qur'an treats the refusal to feed the poor on the Day of Reckoning as the confessed cause of damnation, placed in the mouths of the damned themselves:

> *"They will say, 'We were not of those who prayed, nor did we feed the indigent.'"* (Qur'an 74:42–44)

The sūrah's method is thus consistent with the Book's larger logic: faith is a tree known by its fruits, and where no fruit of any kind appears across seasons, the wise judge questions whether the tree is alive.

**The Respectable Denier in Every Age**

There is a psychological reason the sūrah begins with hast thou seen. The denier it describes is not necessarily a coarse atheist who announces his unbelief. In Makkah he was often a man of substance — a chief who fed pilgrims for reputation, swore by God in business, circumambulated the House, and yet denied the Resurrection and oppressed the weak. His denial wore the clothing of respectability. The Qur'an's question strips away the clothing.

The type persists. In every age there are those whose practical religion is present comfort and social standing, whatever their professed creed. They may attend worship, donate visibly, and speak piously, yet organize their lives around the assumption that no Reckoning will come: wealth is hoarded without fear, the weak are managed without mercy, and prayer is scheduled around convenience rather than the reverse. The sūrah does not ask the reader to hunt such people among others. Its address — hast thou seen — is reflexive before it is accusatory. The first application is inward: have I seen this tendency in myself, in my own postponement of repentance, my own hardness when no one watches, my own assumption that tomorrow's reckoning can be discounted against today's appetite. The exposure is meant to become self-examination, and self-examination is meant to become return.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:2

> **That is the one who drives away the orphan.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Denial to Cruelty in a Single Step**

Verse 2 answers verse 1 with a demonstrative of identification: fa-dhālika — so that, then, is the one. The particle fa- draws the consequence: if you wish to know the denier of the dīn, here is his mark. And the mark is not first a doctrinal statement but a social act: alladhī yaduʿʿu al-yatīm — the one who drives away, repels, thrusts back the orphan. The sūrah thereby teaches that the clearest evidence of what a man believes about the unseen world is what he does to the most visible vulnerable person before him.

The word yatīm, orphan, in Qur'anic usage means a child whose father has died while the child is still young and dependent. The father's death in seventh-century Arabia meant the loss of protection, maintenance, and social standing; the orphan stood exposed to whoever managed his inheritance and whoever controlled access to food and favor. That is why the Qur'an returns to the orphan dozens of times, guarding his property, his dignity, and his heart. The present verse is among the sternest of those passages, because it makes mistreatment of the orphan the diagnostic sign of denial itself.

**The Violence Inside the Verb**

The verb yaduʿʿu, from the root da-ʿa-ʿa, means to push away, to thrust back, to repel with harshness and contempt. It is not the word for merely neglecting or forgetting; it is the word for active, humiliating rejection — driving off with the hand, the voice, the face. The doubling of the middle radical (the shaddah) intensifies the action: repeated, forceful shoving. Al-Ṭabarī glosses it as violent repelling (al-dafʿ bi-ʿunf), and al-Qurṭubī notes that the same root appears in the description of being thrust toward Hell:

> *"The Day they are thrust toward the Fire of Hell with a violent thrusting."* (Qur'an 52:13)

The echo is deliberate in the ears of the classical commentators: as he thrust away the orphan in this world, so shall he be thrust away in the next. The punishment fits the deed with the symmetry the Qur'an often displays between sin and recompense.

Ibn Kathīr emphasizes that the repelling includes every form of humiliation: scolding the orphan who asks, seizing his property, cheating him of his due, closing the door in his face, and treating his presence as a nuisance. The source commentary connects this accusation to the habit of devouring the inheritances of orphans addressed across the Qur'an, including the command:

> *"Give orphans their property, and do not exchange the bad for the good, and do not consume their property with your own. Truly that is a great sin."* (Qur'an 4:2)

And the warning:

> *"Truly those who consume the property of orphans unjustly only consume fire in their bellies, and they shall roast in a Blaze."* (Qur'an 4:10)

And the measured, merciful rule:

> *"And they ask thee concerning orphans. Say, 'To set things right for them is best. And if you intermix with them, they are your brethren.'"* (Qur'an 2:220)

Read against this background, verse 2 is not an isolated rebuke but the tip of a complete orphan-law: the Qur'an guards the orphan's wealth (4:2, 4:10), his upbringing (2:220), his marriage and dower (4:127), and the exact measure of his due (6:152). The denier violates the whole edifice with a single shove.

**The Occasions the Tradition Remembers**

The classical reports attach this verse to remembered faces. Muqātil ibn Sulaymān and al-Kalbī said, as the source commentary records, that this sūrah was revealed about al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil al-Sahmī, a prominent Qurayshī opponent of the Prophet known for his mockery of the Resurrection — the same man who, carrying a decayed bone, asked how God could give life to rotted bones, prompting the closing verses of Sūrah Yā-Sīn. Conversely, Ibn Jurayj reported that Abū Sufyān was in the habit of slaughtering two camels every week, and that on one occasion an orphan came asking for something, and Abū Sufyān drove him off by striking him with a stick — whereupon God revealed these verses. Other reports name Abū Jahl, who is said to have been the guardian of an orphan and to have repelled him empty-handed, or al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah.

Al-Ṭabarī's method with such reports is instructive: he records them, weighs their chains, and then affirms the general sense. Even if the verse descended concerning a particular man, its ruling embraces everyone who does the like. Ibn Kathīr follows the same course: the verse describes the character of whoever repels the orphan, whether Makkan idolater or professing Muslim. This generality is confirmed by the Qur'an's own parallel passage in Sūrah al-Fajr, which charges an entire society — not one man — with the same constellation of neglect:

> *"Nay! You do not honor the orphan, nor do you urge one another to feed the indigent, and you devour inheritance with a consuming greed, and you love wealth with a boundless love."* (Qur'an 89:17–20)

The singular in al-Māʿūn (that is the one) is thus a universal singular: any one who does this, in any century, has stepped into the verse.

**Why the Orphan Stands First**

There is wisdom in the ordering: of all the fruits of denial, why does the sūrah name the orphan first? The commentators offer several converging answers. First, the orphan is the test case of mercy without return. Kindness to the powerful may be investment; kindness to the orphan is pure expenditure, since the orphan cannot repay, promote, or protect his benefactor. How a man treats one from whom he expects nothing reveals what he expects from God. Second, the orphan's vulnerability is total and visible: a child, bereaved, without a defender. Hardness here cannot be disguised as policy or prudence; it stands exposed as hardness. Third, the Prophet himself was an orphan, and the Qur'an reminds him of that history as the ground of his own gentleness:

> *"Did He not find thee an orphan and shelter thee?"* (Qur'an 93:6)

> *"So as for the orphan, do not oppress him."* (Qur'an 93:9)

The command in Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā and the condemnation in al-Māʿūn are two sides of one coin: the orphan sheltered by God must never be the orphan scorned by man.

The Prophet's own teaching then raises care for the orphan to the highest rank of companionship. Al-Bukhārī records:

> *"I and the one who cares for an orphan will be like this in Paradise — and he held his index and middle fingers together."* (al-Bukhārī 5304, from Sahl ibn Saʿd)

And Muslim records the associated promise for care of the widow and the poor:

> *"The one who cares for the widow and the indigent is like the striving warrior in the path of God" — and I think he said — "like the one who stands in prayer without rest and like the one who fasts without breaking."* (Muslim 2982, from Abū Hurayrah)

Set beside these promises, verse 2 displays its full gravity: the distance between Paradise's nearest companionship and the denier's repelling hand is the distance between two opposite orientations of the soul — one that gathers the abandoned child close, and one that thrusts him away.

**The Orphan at the Door Today**

The orphan of seventh-century Makkah has counterparts in every modern street: the bereaved child, the foster child shuttled between homes, the refugee child, the child of the broken household whom institutions process but no one cherishes. The verb yaduʿʿu extends, in the judgment of later scholars, beyond the literal shove to every institutional equivalent: the guardian who consumes the orphan's stipend, the relative who takes the inheritance and abandons the child, the bureaucracy that loses the file, the comfortable household that has room for another possession but no room for another person.

Modern life also supplies subtler forms of repelling: the очереди of charity that humiliates the applicant, the camera that photographs the orphan for the donor's publicity, the gift given with a lecture that wounds more than hunger did. The Qur'anic ethic excludes all of these. The orphan is to be honored (89:17), his property guarded until maturity (6:152), his dignity preserved in the giving. A useful test, drawn from the verse's logic: if the child knocked on my own door — hungry, unwashed, inconvenient — would my first motion be welcome or repulsion. The answer to that imagined knock discloses more about my expectation of the Last Day than any doctrinal formula I can recite.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:3

> **And does not urge feeding the indigent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sin of What He Does Not Do**

If verse 2 names what the denier does — he repels — verse 3 names what he does not do: wa lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿām al-miskīn — and he does not urge, encourage, or prompt the feeding of the indigent. The source commentary captures the pairing precisely: if verse 2 can be seen as a reference to all that one who denies religion does in opposing the proper moral order, verse 3 can be seen as a reference to all that one does not do. Commission and omission together complete the portrait. The denier is violent toward the orphan with his hand and absent toward the hungry with his heart.

The verb yaḥuḍḍu, from ḥaḍḍa, means to urge, to incite, to encourage someone toward an act — whether by one's own example, one's speech, or one's organizing. It is stronger than merely wishing good; it is prompting, moving others to act. Al-Qurṭubī notes the implication: the verse condemns not only the one who does not himself feed the poor, but the one who does not encourage feeding — who neither gives nor gathers, neither opens his own store nor opens others' hearts. Some commentators extend the censure further: the verse admonishes those who actively discourage others, who counsel stinginess, who mock donors, who argue that the poor deserve their hunger. The denier's miserliness is thus contagious; he makes the withholding of food a social norm.

**Whose Food Is It**

The phrase ṭaʿām al-miskīn — the food of the indigent — admits two readings, and the classical scholars preserved both. On the first reading, it means feeding the indigent: providing food to the poor person, whether from one's own table or through organized charity. On the second reading, noted in the source commentary, it means the indigent's own food: the portion of wealth that rightfully belongs to the poor and is wrongfully withheld from them. This second reading rests upon the Qur'an's striking doctrine that the poor hold a right (ḥaqq) in the wealth of the rich — not a favor to be begged, but a due to be rendered:

> *"And in whose wealth is an acknowledged due for the beggar and the deprived."* (Qur'an 70:24–25)

> *"And in their wealth was a due for the beggar and the deprived."* (Qur'an 51:19)

On this understanding, the rich man who does not feed the poor is not merely ungenerous; he is unjust. He withholds what God assigned to another through his hands. His granary contains two portions, and he consumes both. This is why the Qur'an elsewhere classes the refusal to feed the poor among the confessed causes of Hell, in words the damned speak against themselves:

> *"We were not of those who prayed, nor did we feed the indigent. And we used to plunge into falsehood with those who plunged, and we used to deny the Day of Judgment."* (Qur'an 74:42–46)

And why, in the scene of the bound sinner, the charge is stated in terms nearly identical to the present verse:

> *"He did not believe in God, the Tremendous, nor did he urge feeding the indigent."* (Qur'an 69:33–34)

The verbal echo between 69:34 and 107:3 is exact — lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿām al-miskīn — and the commentators treat the two passages as mutual commentary. What Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah presents as the epitaph of the damned, Sūrah al-Māʿūn presents as the diagnosis of the living. The time to hear the epitaph is before it is carved.

**The Pseudo-Theology of Stinginess**

The Qur'an does not merely condemn miserliness; it exposes the arguments miserliness invents to excuse itself. The most revealing is the one recorded in Sūrah Yā-Sīn, where those who refuse the poor offer what the source commentary rightly calls a pseudo-theological excuse:

> *"And when it is said to them, 'Spend from what God has provided you,' those who disbelieve say to those who believe, 'Are we to feed those whom, if God willed, He would feed? You are in naught but manifest error.'"* (Qur'an 36:47)

The logic is impeccable and entirely corrupt: God is able; God has not fed them; therefore God wills their hunger; therefore feeding them opposes God's will. Every premise is true except the conclusion, which inverts the entire moral structure of provision. God feeds the poor through the rich; the surplus in one hand is the trust assigned for another. To cite divine power as a reason for human withholding is to use theology as a lock upon the storehouse. Ibn Taymiyyah's school, commenting on this pattern, observes that genuine trust in divine decree never cancels human agency — the farmer who trusts God's provision still sows, and the rich man who trusts God's mercy still feeds. Predestination is a ground of humility, not an alibi for hardness.

Modern versions of the same excuse are easily recognized: the poor are poor by choice; charity creates dependence; the market will provide; the state will provide; my taxes already provide. Each may contain a fragment of prudential truth, and the Qur'an does not forbid wisdom in giving. But as total excuses for total inaction they reproduce the ancient pattern exactly — reasoning that begins in economics and ends in a closed hand. The verse cuts through every version with a single demand: urge feeding. Speak for the hungry, organize for the hungry, give to the hungry. The one who does none of these has answered the question of verse 1 about himself.

**Feeding as the First Measure of a Community**

The Prophet ﷺ placed feeding the hungry among the very first public commands of his mission. ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām, who embraced Islam upon the Prophet's arrival in Madinah, reports that the first words he heard from him were:

> *"O people! Spread peace, feed food, maintain the ties of kinship, and pray at night while people sleep — you will enter Paradise in peace."* (al-Tirmidhī 2485; Ibn Mājah 1334)

Notice the ordering: peace on the tongue, food in the hand, kinship in the heart, prayer in the night. Feeding stands second, before the prayer of the night vigil. And in the famous ḥadīth of the hungry servant, God Himself will say on the Day of Resurrection:

> *"O son of Adam, I was hungry and you did not feed Me. He will say, 'My Lord, how could I feed You when You are the Lord of the worlds?' He will say, 'Did you not know that My servant so-and-so was hungry and you did not feed him? Did you not know that had you fed him, you would have found that with Me?'"* (Muslim 2569, from Abū Hurayrah)

This report discloses the metaphysics beneath verse 3: the hungry man's need is God's appointed meeting-place with the rich man's mercy. To pass it by is to pass by God; to stop for it is to find Him waiting there with the record of the morsel.

The miskīn of the verse — the indigent, the one whose stillness (sukūn) betrays exhaustion and need — includes every gradation of hunger known to the modern world: the famine victim, the homeless sleeper, the working family that eats less so the children can eat, the indebted neighbor too ashamed to ask. The verse's demand — urge feeding — embraces every corresponding gradation of response: the meal, the food bank, the standing charity, the advocacy that reforms the structures producing hunger. The denier does none of these and encourages none; the believer does what he can and urges what he cannot do alone. Between those two postures the sūrah permits no neutral ground.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:4

> **So woe unto the praying**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Startling Woe in the Qur'an**

Fa-waylun li'l-muṣallīn — so woe unto the praying. Few verses of the Qur'an arrest the reader so abruptly. Woe (wayl) is elsewhere pronounced upon the open wicked: the falsifiers, the defrauders, the slanderers, the deniers of the Hour. Here it is pronounced upon people who pray. The worshippers — the very ones who stand, bow, and prostrate — are threatened with ruin. The verse is designed to shock the complacent believer awake: prayer, the pillar of religion and the first deed examined on the Day of Resurrection, can itself become a cause of woe when severed from its spirit. The form without the heart does not merely fail to save; it testifies against its performer.

The particle fa- links the woe to what preceded it. Because he denies the Recompense, because he repels the orphan and abandons the hungry — so, therefore, woe unto him in his praying too. His worship cannot quarantine his cruelty. The man who imagined that ritual observance compensates for hardness toward God's creatures learns that the ritual itself stands condemned when the hardness persists. This is among the Qur'an's most consistent teachings: worship unaccompanied by mercy is weightless, and in the present passage it is worse than weightless — it draws woe.

**What Wayl Means**

The word wayl has been explained by the commentators in several complementary ways, and the source commentary refers the reader to the discussions at 2:79 and 104:1. The principal opinions are three. First, wayl is a word of threat and lamentation: woe, ruin, destruction, calamity — the cry uttered over one who has fallen into perdition. In this sense it functions like a judicial sentence: ruin has been decreed. Second, wayl is the name of a valley in Hell, or a well of its torment, into which the hypocrites and the heedless will be cast. Reports to this effect are transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and others, describing a valley whose heat the Fire itself seeks refuge from. Third, wayl is a curse-word of the Arabs intensified by revelation: may evil befall him. The three senses converge in practice: whether cry, valley, or curse, wayl announces that the matter is grave and the outcome fearful.

Al-Qurṭubī notes the grammatical force of the indefinite noun with tanwīn — waylun — which in Arabic rhetoric magnifies the referent: a woe, a ruin of terrible magnitude, too great to delimit. It is woe unspecified because no specification could contain it. And the preposition li- (unto, upon) fixes the woe upon its recipients with the directness of a verdict. There is no ambiguity about whom the sentence concerns: li'l-muṣallīn — unto the praying ones, the definite article marking a known class. Which praying ones — the verse that follows at once specifies, lest any sincere worshipper despair.

**Whose Prayer Draws Woe**

The classical commentators distinguished carefully, because the verse without its sequel could terrify every believer who has ever been distracted in prayer — which is to say, every believer. The majority, as the source commentary records, interpret the woe as falling upon those who, because of their heedlessness, receive no benefit from their prayers when they perform them and fear no retribution when they do not, or upon the hypocrites, who pray in public but not in private. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī develop the hypocrite reading: these are men who pray to be seen by people while abandoning prayer when alone — whose worship follows the audience rather than the Lord. Others extend the reference to those who delay prayers beyond their proper times, who omit their bows and prostrations, or who perform the outward form correctly while emptying it of humility, remembrance, and presence of heart.

A minority reading takes the verse as referring to the prayer of the disbelievers — the whistling and clapping at the House condemned in 8:35. But the majority reading is stronger, and more searching: the threatened ones are within the community of prayer, not outside it. They possess the form of Islam's central rite and lack its reality. This is why the verse has been cherished by preachers and feared by the righteous in equal measure: it turns the believer's gaze from the quantity of his worship to its quality, from the prayer others see to the prayer God sees.

It must be stressed, as al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both stress, that ordinary distraction — the stray thought that enters even the sincere worshipper's mind — is not the heedlessness threatened here. The Prophet ﷺ himself described Satan's effort to distract the worshipper and prescribed the remedy of seeking refuge and spitting lightly to the left. What draws woe is not the wandering mind of the struggling believer but the settled heedlessness of one for whom prayer is a burden to be discharged, a performance to be displayed, or a schedule to be postponed until its time has nearly expired. The distinction preserves both the verse's terror and its justice: terror for the heedless heart, and mercy for the striving one.

**Why Prayer Can Become Evidence Against Its Performer**

There is a logic to the paradox that repays reflection. Prayer is the servant's standing before his Lord — the appointment in which he declares, seventeen times daily in the obligatory units alone, that God is greatest, that praise belongs to God, that he worships none but Him. For this declaration to proceed from a heart planning cruelty, a tongue fresh from mocking the orphan, a hand closed to the hungry, is to make the prayer itself a witness of contradiction. The limbs testify God is greatest while the conduct testifies appetite is greatest. On the Day when limbs speak, the prayer offered heedlessly will speak too — not for its performer but about him.

The Prophet ﷺ expressed the positive side of this accounting when he said:

> *"The first deed for which the servant will be called to account on the Day of Resurrection is his prayer. If it is sound, he will have prospered and succeeded; and if it is corrupt, he will have failed and lost."* (al-Tirmidhī 413; al-Nasāʾī 465; Ibn Mājah 1425, from Abū Hurayrah)

This report is the key to the present verse: prayer is the first audit because it is the summary of the soul's orientation. Sound prayer — offered in its time, with its conditions, with presence and humility — signals a soul turned toward God, and such a soul will not repel orphans or starve neighbors. Corrupt prayer — delayed, rushed, displayed — signals a soul turned toward itself and its spectators, and such a soul will do exactly what verses 2–3 describe. The woe of verse 4 is therefore not arbitrary; it is the verdict that the audit has already pronounced in this world, awaiting only its reading in the next.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:5

> **Who are heedless of their prayers,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Preposition That Defines the Disease**

Verse 5 specifies the recipients of the woe: alladhīna hum ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn — those who are, of or from their prayers, heedless, forgetful, inattentive. The classical commentators lavished attention upon the preposition ʿan (from, of, away from), contrasting it with the preposition fī (in, during). To be heedless in prayer (fī al-ṣalāh) is the common lot of believers: thoughts intrude, the mind wanders, and the worshipper recalls himself and returns. To be heedless of prayer (ʿan al-ṣalāh) — away from it, turned aside from it — is something else entirely: neglect of its times, abandonment of its conditions, indifference to whether it is performed well, performed late, or performed at all.

This distinction, transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿAṭāʾ ibn Dīnār, and others, and affirmed by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī, preserves the verse from misapplication. Praise belongs to God, said the early authorities, who said ʿan ṣalātihim (of their prayers) and did not say fī ṣalātihim (in their prayers) — for had He said in, scarcely a worshipper would escape. The heedlessness threatened here is not the flicker of distraction but the settled posture of disregard: the prayer delayed until its time has almost departed, the prayer performed without its bows and prostrations completed, the prayer abandoned when no spectator is present, the prayer whose words pass the lips while the heart grazes elsewhere by habit and without struggle or remorse.

**The Faces of Heedlessness**

The commentators enumerate the forms this heedlessness takes, and the enumeration reads like a catalogue of prayer in every age. First, delaying prayers beyond their proper times: the noon prayer pushed to the edge of the afternoon, the afternoon prayer deferred until the sun yellows toward setting, the dawn prayer surrendered to sleep. Second, omitting or curtailing the prayer's integrals: the bow not completed, the prostration pecked like a bird's strike, the stillness between postures abandoned — the haste that the Prophet ﷺ refused to recognize as prayer at all when he told the hurried worshipper:

> *"Go back and pray, for you have not prayed."* (al-Bukhārī 757; Muslim 397, from Abū Hurayrah)

Third, performing prayer in public while abandoning it in private — the hypocrite's signature, praying for the eye of men rather than the eye of God. Fourth, performing the outward form correctly while emptying it of humility (khushūʿ), contemplation of its meanings, and remembrance of God — the body present, the heart absent, reciting praise without praising.

Al-Qurṭubī adds a further form: the one who prays but neither hopes for its reward nor fears the consequence of its neglect — who receives no benefit when he performs and feels no alarm when he omits. This is heedlessness perfected: worship reduced to a motion the soul neither anticipates nor misses. Against every form stands the Qur'an's portrait of the prayerful believers, constant and mindful:

> *"Those who are constant in their prayers."* (Qur'an 70:23)

> *"And those who are mindful of their prayers."* (Qur'an 70:34)

Constancy (dawām) guards the prayer against abandonment; mindfulness (muḥāfaẓah) guards it against corruption. The heedless fail in one or both, and the sūrah pronounces woe over the failure.

**This Is the Prayer of the Hypocrite**

The most vivid prophetic depiction of the heedlessness condemned here is the report concerning the delayed afternoon prayer. Anas ibn Mālik reports that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"That is the prayer of the hypocrite: he sits watching the sun until, when it is between the two horns of Satan, he stands and pecks out four in which he remembers God but little."* (Muslim 622; also Abū Dāwūd 413; al-Tirmidhī 160; al-Nasāʾī 511)

Every phrase of this report illuminates the verse. He sits watching the sun — the whole afternoon available, yet he waits, occupied with everything except the appointment. Between the two horns of Satan — at the last, disliked moment, when the sun approaches setting and the shadows lengthen. He stands and pecks out four — the verb naqara evokes a bird's jabbing strike: rapid, graceless prostrations without stillness or supplication. In which he remembers God but little — the Qur'anic phrase itself, from the description of the hypocrites:

> *"When they perform the prayer, they perform it idly and to be seen of men, and they remember God but little."* (Qur'an 4:142)

Ibn Kathīr cites this ḥadīth as the verse's direct commentary: the sahw (heedlessness) of al-Māʿūn is the sitting, delaying, pecking, and forgetting of the hypocrite's prayer. The report also supplies the verse's psychology: prayer delayed is prayer diminished, because delay reveals desire. The lover hastens to the appointment; the reluctant postpones it until postponement itself becomes the statement. A prayer offered at the last minute, in haste, with the mind still entangled in the afternoon's business, is a prayer that confesses — whatever the lips recite — that God was the day's lowest priority.

**Neglect and Its End**

The Qur'an elsewhere traces the trajectory on which heedlessness travels when it is not repented. In Sūrah Maryam, after recounting the prophets and their devotion, it warns:

> *"Then there succeeded after them successors who neglected prayer and followed desires; so they will meet ruin."* (Qur'an 19:59)

The sequence is causal: neglected prayer, then followed desires, then ruin (ghayy — loss, deviation, a valley of Hell). Prayer is the dam that holds back appetite; breach the dam, and the flood follows its own course. The commentators on 19:59 debate whether neglected means abandoned entirely or delayed and corrupted — and the present sūrah suggests the debate states a continuum rather than an alternative. Delay hardens into habit, habit into abandonment, abandonment into a life reorganized around desire. The woe of al-Māʿūn stands at the head of that road like a signpost, warning the traveler while return is still easy.

For the striving believer, the remedy is practical and immediate. Guard the prayer's time above its convenience: pray early in the window rather than late, and let the day's schedule bend around the appointment rather than the reverse. Guard the prayer's body: complete each bow and prostration with stillness, as the Prophet ﷺ commanded the hurried man until he learned repose in every posture. Guard the prayer's heart: learn the meanings of what is recited, pause between verses, and when the mind wanders — as it will — return it gently, as one returns a straying child, without despair and without surrender. The difference between the condemned heedlessness and the forgiven struggle is exactly this return: the heedless do not notice or do not care that they are absent, while the believers notice, grieve, and come back. God sees the coming back, and He counts it among the prayer's sincerest moments.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:6

> **Those who strive to be seen,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Worship Turned Into Theater**

Verse 6 unveils the third mark of the condemned: alladhīna hum yurāʾūn — those who make a show, who strive to be seen, who perform acts of devotion for spectators. The verb rāʾā, in the form used here, means to do a deed in order that people may see it and admire the doer: to pray where one will be noticed, to lengthen the prayer when watched and shorten it when alone, to give charity with an announcement, to wear devotion as a costume. The English phrase strive to be seen captures the effort involved: this is not accidental visibility — the believer seen praying in the mosque is not condemned for being seen — but deliberate exhibition, worship staged for human eyes.

The placement of this verse after the condemnation of heedlessness is precise. Verses 4–5 described prayer emptied of God; verse 6 describes prayer filled with spectators. The two are one disease in two phases: the heart vacated by remembrance is occupied by reputation. Nature abhors a vacuum, and so does worship: a prayer not offered to God will be offered to someone — to the employer whose favor is sought, to the community whose respect is coveted, to the mirror of self-admiration. The sūrah names the substitute audience plainly: men. And it implies the terrible exchange: the approval of spectators, which perishes with the evening, purchased at the price of the approval of God, which endures forever.

**The Qur'an's Portrait of Show**

The Qur'an treats showing off (riyāʾ) as a defining trait of hypocrisy and as the nullifier of charity and worship alike. Its central portrait stands in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ:

> *"Truly the hypocrites seek to deceive God, but He outwits them. And when they perform the prayer, they perform it idly and to be seen of men, and they remember God but little."* (Qur'an 4:142)

Three phrases, three diagnoses: idly (kusālā — lazily, heavily, as one dragged to a burden), to be seen of men (the audience named), and remembering God but little (the heart's absence measured). Al-Māʿūn distributes the same three across its own verses: the idleness in the heedlessness of verse 5, the spectators in verse 6, the little remembrance implied throughout. The two passages are one portrait in two frames.

Elsewhere the Qur'an extends the condemnation from prayer to charity. In the great verse of spending in Sūrah al-Baqarah, the one who gives to be seen is likened to a dust-covered rock struck by rain — washed bare, holding nothing:

> *"O you who believe! Do not nullify your charities with reminders of generosity and hurt, like the one who spends his wealth to be seen of men and believes not in God and the Last Day. His likeness is that of a smooth rock upon which is dust; then a heavy rain strikes it and leaves it bare. They have no power over anything they have earned."* (Qur'an 2:264)

The parable's terror lies in its final clause: they have no power over anything they have earned. The showy deed feels like earning — applause now, admiration now — but on the Day when only God's acceptance purchases anything, the earner finds his account empty. Nothing survived the transfer from the currency of spectators to the currency of the Hereafter, because nothing was ever deposited in the latter.

Against this stands the Qur'an's closing command of Sūrah al-Kahf, the charter of sincerity:

> *"So whoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord, let him perform righteous deeds and associate none in the worship of his Lord."* (Qur'an 18:110)

The early commentators, including Ibn ʿAbbās, read associating none here as embracing both the great association (shirk akbar — worshipping another god) and the hidden association (shirk khafī — performing God's worship for another's eye). Whoever hopes for the meeting purifies the deed for the One he hopes to meet.

**What the Prophet Said About Showing Off**

No theme of the Prophet's warnings is more sustained, or more fearful, than riyāʾ — precisely because it corrupts the best deeds of the best people. Three reports, each authenticated, together map the danger.

First, the fear the Prophet named as his greatest fear for his own community. Shaddād ibn Aws reports him saying:

> *"The thing I fear most for you is minor associationism." They said, "O Messenger of God, what is minor associationism?" He said, "Showing off. God will say on the Day when people are recompensed for their deeds: 'Go to those for whom you were showing off in the world, and see if you find any reward with them.'"* (Musnad Aḥmad 23630; graded ḥasan by the scholars)

Second, the divine declaration of independence from shared deeds, in a ḥadīth qudsī — God speaking through the Prophet outside the Qur'an. Abū Hurayrah reports:

> *"God the Exalted says: 'I am the One most free from need of any partner. Whoever performs a deed in which he associates another with Me, I abandon him and his association.'"* (Muslim 2985)

Third, the eschatological scene of the first to be judged — not the open sinners but the celebrated righteous: the martyr who fought to be called brave, the reciter who recited to be called a reciter, the giver who gave to be called generous. Each is confronted, each confesses the audience he sought, and each is dragged upon his face into the Fire. Abū Hurayrah reports the Prophet ﷺ saying, in the long ḥadīth preserved by Muslim:

> *"The first people against whom judgment will be pronounced on the Day of Resurrection will be a man who died a martyr. He will be brought, and God will acquaint him with His favors, and he will acknowledge them. Then He will say, 'What did you do with them?' He will say, 'I fought for You until I was martyred.' He will say, 'You lie. You fought so that it would be said: he is brave — and it was said.' Then it will be commanded concerning him, and he will be dragged upon his face until he is cast into the Fire..."* (Muslim 1905)

The ḥadīth continues with the scholar and the philanthropist to the same end. Ibn Taymiyyah, reflecting on such reports, observes that riyāʾ is the characteristic corruption of the religious — as greed is the corruption of merchants and tyranny the corruption of rulers. The warning therefore belongs most urgently to the devout: the danger grows with the deed, and the longest prayers and largest donations require the most vigilant guarding of intention.

**The Subtlety of the Disease and Its Cure**

The early generations feared riyāʾ with a fear that astonishes comfortable ages. They hid their fasting, their night prayer, their charity, and their tears — not from modesty alone but from knowledge of how swiftly the heart converts worship into display. Al-Fuḍayl ibn ʿIyāḍ, the renowned ascetic of the second century, defined sincerity with a precision the later scholars never improved: abandoning a deed because of people is riyāʾ, and performing it because of people is shirk; sincerity is God's preserving you from both. The believer therefore neither prays to be seen nor abandons prayer for fear of being seen — he prays for God, seen or unseen, and leaves the question of spectators to God.

For the modern believer, the theater of verse 6 has expanded beyond anything the classical commentators imagined: the photographed charity, the broadcast devotion, the timed recitation, the publicized pilgrimage. The ruling does not forbid visibility itself — public worship has its place, and good example guides others — but it interrogates intention with a question only the heart can answer honestly: if no one would ever know of this deed, would I still do it with the same care, the same beauty, the same joy. If the answer is yes, visibility is an accident and the deed is safe. If the answer is no — if the prayer lengthens under eyes and shortens in solitude, if the donation depends upon the announcement — then the verse has named the disease, and naming is the beginning of cure. Renew the intention before the deed, guard it during the deed, and after the deed seek God's forgiveness for whatever of the self intruded into His worship. The scholars add one final mercy: the involuntary pleasure of being seen, when it arrives uninvited and is neither sought nor indulged, does not nullify the deed — the condemnation falls upon the striving, the deliberate staging, not upon the passing thought the believer rejects.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:7

> **Yet refuse small kindnesses.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Smallest Word with the Largest Implication**

The sūrah closes with its namesake: wa yamnaʿūna al-māʿūn — and they refuse, withhold, deny al-māʿūn. After the greatest matter (denial of the dīn), the cruelest act (repelling the orphan), the coldest omission (abandoning the hungry), the hollowest worship (heedless prayer), and the falsest motive (showing off), the Book ends with the smallest thing: the little kindness, the minor assistance, the trifling loan. The descent from greatest to smallest is the sūrah's final stroke of genius. It teaches that the soul's ruin is total: a heart that withholds from God the sincerity of worship will withhold from neighbors the lending of a pot. Nothing is too small to reveal it, because nothing is too small to contain it.

The verb yamnaʿūna (they refuse, prevent, withhold) is in the imperfect, denoting habit: this is their settled practice, not a single refusal. And the object, al-māʿūn, with the definite article, denotes the well-known category of small neighborly goods — the things everyone recognizes as too trivial to refuse and too shameful to withhold. The verse thus describes a miserliness so complete that it cannot rise even to the level of ordinary decency. Great generosity is beyond such people; even petty generosity is beyond them.

**What Al-Māʿūn Means**

Few words of the Qur'an have received so many interpretations, and the abundance itself testifies to the word's richness. The commentaries record the following principal opinions, each with early authority behind it.

First, and most widespread: al-māʿūn means the small utensils and tools that neighbors lend one another — the axe, the pot, the bucket, the rope, the sieve, the needle, the salt, the fire. This is the interpretation of Ibn Masʿūd, Ibn ʿAbbās in one narration, ʿIkrimah, Mujāhid, and a host of the Successors, and it is favored by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī as the most fitting sense. Ibn Masʿūd is reported to have said:

> *"Al-māʿūn is the lending of an axe, a bucket, a pot, and the like."*

Ibn Kathīr, citing these reports, explains the wisdom of the sense: the verse condemns those who perform devotions outwardly yet will not extend to others even the most trivial benefit — who pray at length but will not lend a vessel. Their worship is grand; their neighborliness is nil. The contradiction exposes the worship.

Second: al-māʿūn means zakāh, the obligatory alms. This is reported from Ibn ʿAbbās in another narration, from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, and from al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, and it is recorded by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī. On this reading the verse parallels the Qur'an's portrait of the hypocrites:

> *"They only come to the prayer lazily, and they only spend reluctantly."* (Qur'an 9:54)

Third: al-māʿūn means wealth or property (māl) in general, in the dialect of Quraysh. Fourth: it means water — the lending of water, the most basic of all kindnesses, whose withholding the Prophet ﷺ threatened with particular severity. Fifth: it means obedience and righteous deeds in general, or benefit (manfaʿah) in every form — as al-Qurṭubī summarizes, anything in which there is benefit. Sixth: it means hospitality to guests, or the giving of charity in general.

Al-Qurṭubī's method with this plurality is characteristic of the great commentators: rather than cancelling one opinion by another, he embraces the breadth. The word's root (m-ʿ-n) carries the sense of ease, littleness, and ready availability — maʿn means a small thing, an easy thing, a thing at hand. Al-māʿūn is therefore whatever small, easy benefit passes between people: the utensil, the coin of zakāh, the drink of water, the helping hand, the good word. The hypocrite withholds all of it — great and small alike — because his heart is withheld from God. As the source commentary concludes, according to all of these interpretations, the verse indicates the stinginess exhibited by hypocrites, who only come to the prayer lazily and only spend reluctantly.

**Why the Sūrah Ends Here**

There is a deliberate anticlimax in ending a sūrah about the denial of religion with a borrowed bucket. The anticlimax is the point. The Qur'an teaches throughout that God tests His servants in small things before great ones, and that failure in the small predicts failure in the great. The one who cannot be trusted with a pot cannot be trusted with an orphan's inheritance; the one who will not lend water will not pay zakāh; the one who will not rise from his seat to help a neighbor will not rise from his sleep for the dawn prayer. Small kindnesses are the currency of sincerity: too minor to perform for reputation — no one wins fame lending salt — they can only proceed from genuine goodwill. Their absence therefore proves what grand performances cannot disprove.

The Prophet's teaching confirms this economy of the small. He commanded that no neighborly kindness be despised for its size. Abū Hurayrah reports:

> *"O Muslim women! Let no neighbor belittle even a sheep's foot for her neighbor."* (al-Bukhārī 2566; Muslim 1030)

And he warned that the withholding of the smallest essentials draws the gravest consequence. Of surplus water withheld from the traveler, he said:

> *"There are three to whom God will not speak on the Day of Resurrection, nor look at them, nor purify them — and theirs is a painful punishment: a man who had surplus water in the desert and withheld it from a traveler..."* (al-Bukhārī 2358; Muslim 108, from Abū Hurayrah)

And of the arrière-ban of miserliness in general, the Qur'an warns the hoarders that what they withheld will be hung about their necks:

> *"And let not those who are miserly with what God has given them of His bounty deem it good for them. Nay, it is evil for them. What they withheld shall be hung about their necks on the Day of Resurrection."* (Qur'an 3:180)

The borrowed pot of al-Māʿūn, refused in this world, thus reappears in the next as a collar of fire. The small thing was never small.

**The Closed Hand and the Open Door**

Psychologically, the refusal of small kindnesses reveals a soul organized around ownership rather than trusteeship. The believer understands that all he holds — the pot, the house, the wealth, the time — is a trust from God, deposited with him that it might circulate. Lending costs him nothing: the pot returns, the water is replenished, the kindness is recorded. The hypocrite understands his holdings as his identity and his security; to lend is to lose, to give is to diminish, to open the hand is to open the self to risk. His closed hand is therefore a theological statement: he does not truly believe that God replenishes, that the Balance is real, that the morsel lent returns multiplied. His economics deny the dīn his lips may profess.

Socially, the circulation of small kindnesses is the bloodstream of a neighborhood. The borrowed tool, the shared meal, the watched child, the jump-started car, the covered shift — these trivial exchanges weave the trust without which no community survives its crises. A street where pots circulate is a street that will organize the funeral, shelter the burned-out family, and find the lost child. A street where every door is bolted against every request is a collection of strangers awaiting their separate calamities. The sūrah's final verse is thus a sociology in miniature: withhold the māʿūn, and you dissolve the ummah one refusal at a time; circulate it, and you bind hearts with cords stronger than contracts.

For the believer who would live this verse, the practice is beautifully simple: refuse no small kindness in your power to grant. Lend the tool, share the food, give the ride, answer the question, write the recommendation, mind the child, visit the sick — and do it without announcement, since the verse's context in showing off warns that even small kindnesses can be staged. Let the left hand not know what the right hand lends. And when asked for what you cannot give, refuse with the gentleness the Qur'an commands elsewhere — a kind word is better than a charity followed by hurt — so that even the refusal becomes a small kindness. The sūrah that began with the vastness of the dīn ends with the nearness of the neighbor: religion denied in heaven's court is religion refused at the kitchen door — and religion affirmed before God is religion practiced with the borrowed bucket, for His sake, without witnesses, without delay.
