# Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah (Chapter 101) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah That Strikes Like a Hammer**

Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah, "The Striker" or "The Calamity," is the 101st chapter of the Qur'an in the written muṣḥaf, and one of the shortest complete sūrahs: eleven verses, thirty-six words in the Kufan counting, revealed in Makkah in the early period of the Prophet's mission ﷺ. The source commentary places its revelation just after Sūrah Quraysh (106); other classical orderings list it around the thirtieth revelation, in the company of the short, hammering Makkan chapters that announced the Resurrection to a city that denied it. Whenever its exact number in the sequence, its voice is unmistakably that of the earliest proclamation: brief verses, pounding rhymes, scenes of cosmic collapse, and the stark division of mankind into the saved and the lost.

Its name is taken from its opening word, *al-qāriʿah* — the thing that strikes, knocks, and beats with a deafening sound. The Arabs used the verb *qaraʿa* for knocking at a door, for the striking of one hard thing against another, and by extension for any shattering calamity that "knocks" a people down. As one of the Qur'an's names for the Last Day, it belongs to a family of feminine intensive forms that the revelation reserves for the Hour: *al-Ḥāqqah* (the Inevitable Reality, 69:1), *al-Ṭāmmah* (the Overwhelming Calamity, 79:34), *al-Ṣākhkhah* (the Deafening Blast, 80:33), *al-Ghāshiyah* (the Overwhelmer, 88:1). Each name seizes the ear from a different side; this sūrah's name seizes it by force of impact — the Day that knocks, and keeps knocking, until every door is down.

**A Sūrah in Two Movements, Sealed by a Repeated Question**

The eleven verses fall into two movements of almost perfect balance. The first movement (verses 1–5) is cosmic and outward: a mysterious name is announced, magnified by two awestruck questions, and then unveiled in two images — mankind scattered like moths, mountains carded like wool. The second movement (verses 6–11) is personal and inward: the scales are set up, and mankind divides into two parties — the one whose scales are heavy, enfolded in a contenting life, and the one whose scales are light, whose "mother," whose embrace and dwelling-place is the Abyss. The sūrah then closes the way it opened, with the same awestruck formula — *"and what shall apprise thee?"* — asked now not about the Day but about the Abyss, and answered in three words of fire.

That repeated question, *wa mā adrāka* ("and what shall apprise thee of...?"), appears twice — in verse 3 and verse 10 — forming a frame around the whole sūrah. The classical scholars noticed the Qur'an's consistent habit with this phrase: wherever God says *wa mā adrāka*, He Himself supplies the answer, lifting the veil He first lowered; whereas *wa mā yudrīka* ("what could make thee know?") is never followed by an answer, leaving the matter sealed. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said of the former pattern that God then informed His Prophet of it. Here the pattern holds twice: the question about the Calamity is answered with the scenes of the Day, and the question about the Abyss is answered with the raging Fire. The sūrah is thus a small ring — question, unveiling, division, question, unveiling — and the reader who enters at verse 1 is carried around the full circle and set down before the Fire at verse 11.

**Between Heedlessness and Rivalry: The Sūrah's Neighbours**

In the muṣḥaf Al-Qāriʿah stands between Sūrah Al-ʿĀdiyāt (100) and Sūrah Al-Takāthur (102), and the three read as a single triptych on the disease and its cure. Sūrah 100 diagnoses the disease: man is ungrateful to his Lord, passionately in love with wealth, heedless of the day when graves disgorge their contents. Sūrah 101 presents the cure in the form of the coming reality itself: the Striker is coming, the scales will be set, and every weight of deed will tell. Sūrah 102 then names the particular delusion that makes men forget all this — *takāthur*, rivalry in mere increase, the piling of numbers upon numbers until the graveyard ends the contest. Read in sequence, the three sūrahs move from charge (you are heedless) to warning (the Day is coming) to exposure (this is what distracted you). The commentators who attend to the ordering of the sūrahs, such as al-Biqāʿī and in modern times Sayyid Quṭb, treat this neighbourhood as deliberate: Al-Qāriʿah is the thunderclap between the diagnosis and the autopsy.

**What the Prophet ﷺ and the Early Community Said of It**

Unlike the long Madinan chapters, the short Makkan sūrahs rarely carry chapters of "virtues" (*faḍāʾil*) in the canonical collections, and Al-Qāriʿah is no exception: there is no authentic, specifically-attached promise in al-Bukhārī or Muslim for the recitation of this sūrah alone. A report is found in some late works of exegesis, attributed to Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, to the effect that whoever recites Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah will have his scales made heavy by God on the Day of Resurrection — but the critics of ḥadīth, including Ibn al-Jawzī, judged the whole cycle of such sūrah-by-sūrah virtue-reports to be unsound, and the honest student cites it only to set it aside. The sūrah's authority needs no such prop: its images entered the bloodstream of the community through the prayer itself, for the short sūrahs of the end of the muṣḥaf are the most recited passages in Muslim worship, heard by every congregation at dawn and dusk across fourteen centuries.

What is authentically transmitted is the Prophet's ﷺ general terror before the realities this sūrah describes. Al-Tirmidhī records his words:

> *"How can I enjoy life when the Bearer of the Trumpet has put it to his mouth, bent his forehead, and is listening intently, waiting to be commanded to blow?"* (al-Tirmidhī 2431; graded ḥasan by al-Tirmidhī, and its meaning is supported by other reports)

And al-Bukhārī and Muslim record the verse of weighing that this sūrah's second movement turns upon, in the Prophet's ﷺ most beloved summary of the Scales:

> *"Two words, light upon the tongue, heavy upon the Scale, beloved to the Most Merciful: 'Glory be to God and with His praise' (subḥān Allāh wa bi-ḥamdih), and 'Glory be to God the Tremendous' (subḥān Allāh al-ʿaẓīm)."*(al-Bukhārī 6406; Muslim 2694)

These two reports are the sūrah's authentic frame: dread of the Blast, and hope in the weight of a single sincere word.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the eleven verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order, exactly as mandated: every verse is quoted individually, then expanded through Arabic language, the classical commentators, authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the logic of its teaching, and its bearing upon modern life. Verses that the source commentary treats in groups (1–3, 6–9, 10–11) are here separated and given full individual treatment, as the workflow requires. Nothing has been compressed because the chapter is short — if anything, brevity here demands the opposite, for each of these eleven verses is a compressed charge, and the commentary's task is to let each one detonate fully, from *"The Striker"* to *"a raging Fire."*

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:1

> **The calamity;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Single Word as a Whole Verse**

The sūrah opens with a verse that is a single word: *al-qāriʿah*. No verb, no sentence, no explanation — just a name, announced and left hanging in the air. The Qur'an opens several of its most awesome chapters this way, with a lone announcing word followed by awestruck questions: *"The Inevitable Reality! What is the Inevitable Reality?"* (69:1–2) is the exact twin of this opening, and the resemblance is deliberate, training the returning listener to feel the same shudder twice. Rhetoricians call this *tashwīq* — the art of whetting appetite by withholding: a name is dropped like a stone into still water, and the ripples of curiosity do the preacher's work before a single argument is uttered. The Makkan denier, who claimed there would be no Resurrection at all, is not first argued with; he is first startled. Only a speaker supremely confident of his news can afford to begin with a bare noun and trust the noun to detonate.

The abruptness is also mimetic: the verse *performs* what it names. A striker strikes without preamble, and so does the verse. One moment there is silence; the next, a blow. The classical commentator al-Rāzī, who loved to uncover the fitness between wording and meaning, would have called this a perfect correspondence of vessel and content: the Day that will arrive suddenly is announced suddenly. Modern readers, accustomed to headlines engineered for shock, may recognise the technique — but here it serves not commerce but awakening, and the shock it delivers is mercy, since a soul startled awake in this life need not be startled awake in the next.

**What the Root Q-R-ʿ Carries in Its Sound**

*Qāriʿah* is the feminine active participle of the verb *qaraʿa* (qāf–rāʿ–ʿayn), whose core sense is to strike one thing against another so that a sharp sound results. The Arabs said *qaraʿa al-bāb* — he knocked at the door; *qaraʿa al-ʿaṣā* — he struck with the stick; and they called the severe afflictions that "knock down" tribes and lands *qawāriʿ* (plural), the strikers. The Qur'an itself uses the root in this calamity-sense when warning the disbelievers:

> *"And those who disbelieve shall not cease to be struck by a striker (qāriʿah) for what they wrought, or to have it alight near their dwellings, until God's promise comes."* (Qur'an 13:31)

So embedded was this sense that the pre-Islamic Arabs called any dreadful public disaster *qāriʿah* — a famine, a raid, an epidemic. The sūrah takes that tribal word for local catastrophe and raises it to its absolute degree: not *a* striker among strikers, but *the* Striker, with the definite article of uniqueness — the Calamity beside which every famine, war, and plague of history was a mere tap at the door.

Why the feminine form? The grammarians and exegetes offer two complementary answers. First, the feminine *tāʾ* here is the *tāʾ* of intensification (*mubālaghah*), the same that makes *al-ḥāqqah*, *al-ṣākhkhah*, and *al-ṭāmmah* so resounding: it magnifies, as though the calamity were so great it required the most emphatic mould the language owns. Second, some held that an elided noun is understood — *al-sāʿah al-qāriʿah*, "the Hour, the Striker" — so that the adjective stands in for the whole Day. Either way, the effect is the same: a word shaped by the language's own physics to land heavily. Say it slowly — *al-qā-ri-ʿah* — and the throat must stop and strike at the ʿayn, as if the mouth itself were knocking.

**Why the Last Day Is Called "The Striker"**

The classical exegetes competed, reverently, to explain the fitness of this name, and their explanations layer rather than cancel. Al-Ṭabarī (d. 310/923), gathering the reports of the early generations, records the view that it is called *al-qāriʿah* because it strikes hearts with its terror (*taqraʿu al-qulūb bi-hawlihā*) — the blow lands first inwardly, on the seat of awareness. Qatādah (d. 118/736) said it strikes people with its terrors; Ibn Zayd said it is the Hour that knocks mankind down. Ibn Kathīr (d. 774/1373) summarises the consensus plainly: *al-qāriʿah* is among the names of the Day of Resurrection, so called because it strikes hearts with dread. Al-Qurṭubī (d. 671/1273) adds the physical register: the Blast (*al-ṣayḥah*) and the Trumpet's cry will literally strike ears and horizons, so the name joins the inward blow with the outward one.

Ibn ʿAbbās (d. 68/687), the Prophet's ﷺ cousin and the father of Qur'anic exegesis, is reported to have counted *al-qāriʿah* among the Day's many names — the Day of Resurrection, the Day of Recompense, the Striking Hour — each name unveiling one face of a reality too vast for any single term. The logic of many names for one Day mirrors the logic of God's own many Names: multiplicity of names signals plenitude of the named. Just as the Arabs named the lion a hundred names because of its formidable perfection, the Qur'an names the Last Day a dozen names — the Approaching, the Overwhelmer, the Deafening Blast, the Striker — because no one aspect exhausts it. To memorise its names is thus itself an act of preparation: each name is a different alarm bell hung on the same coming morning.

**The Theology Inside a Name**

A name in the Qur'an is never mere decoration; it is condensed doctrine. That the Last Day should be introduced here as a *striker* teaches three articles of belief at once. First, its certainty: a striker is not a hypothesis but an event — it comes whether or not the sleeper inside believes in knocking. The verse therefore answers the Makkan denial of resurrection ("who shall revive these bones?") before the question is even posed, by speaking of the Day in the grammar of fact. Second, its suddenness: strikers do not send ambassadors; doors are knocked without appointment. The Qur'an stresses elsewhere that the Hour will come *"suddenly"* (*baghtatan* — 7:187; 12:107; 22:55), and the image of the knock is suddenness made audible. Third, its universality: a striker at history's door knocks for everyone in the house. No tribe, technology, or treasury exempts its owner; the blow falls on hearts as such, and every living breast owns one.

There is a further theological precision worth pausing over. The Striker strikes, but it does not strike arbitrarily: as the sūrah's second movement will show, the same Day that terrifies is also the Day of the Scales — of exact, letter-perfect justice. Terror and justice are joined in a single Day the way the knock and the reckoning are joined in a single visit: the blow announces the audit. This pairing refutes two opposite errors at once — the error of those who imagine God as mere wrath without measure, and the error of those who imagine Him as mere indulgence without account. *Al-Qāriʿah* is fearsome, and *al-Qāriʿah* is fair; the sūrah will develop the first half in verses 4–5 and the second in verses 6–9, but the seed of both is already planted in this one word.

**How a Single Word Wakes a Sleeping Heart**

Psychologically, the verse is a masterclass in the rhetoric of awakening. The human mind habituates: warnings repeated in full sentences blur into background noise, while a single strange word, unexplained, pierces the fog of routine. Sleep researchers and preachers alike know that an unfamiliar sound wakes more surely than a loud familiar one — the mother sleeps through traffic but wakes at her infant's whimper. *Al-qāriʿah* is the Qur'an's unfamiliar sound: a word the Makkans knew from tribal disasters, suddenly isolated, magnified, and left undefined, so that every hearer must lean forward and ask — which is exactly what verse 2 voices on his behalf. The sūrah does not lecture the heedless soul; it *knocks* on it, demonstrating on the small scale of recitation what the Day will demonstrate on the scale of the cosmos.

The analogy the tradition most loves for this spiritual function is the dawn knock of a concerned friend. A man asleep while his house fills with smoke does not need, first, a lecture on combustion; he needs a pounding at his door. The Qur'an elsewhere describes its own revelation in precisely these rescue terms — a Book sent *"that thou mayest bring mankind out of darkness into light"* (14:1) — and the short Makkan sūrahs are the pounding. Every time this verse is recited in prayer — and it is recited millions of times daily across the earth — the knock sounds again in another breast, offering the sleeper one more merciful startle before the final one.

**The Knock in Modern Ears**

Translate the image into contemporary experience and its force, far from fading, sharpens. Modern life is a fortress against knocking: noise-cancelling headphones, muted notifications, houses alarmed and gated, hearts anaesthetised by endless content. We have grown expert at not being struck by anything — tragedy in one window, comedy in the next, and the soul scrolls past both. Into this padded chamber *al-qāriʿah* speaks with peculiar relevance: there exists a knock that no insulation can muffle, an event that will not be scrolled past, a notification that cannot be muted, for it arrives not through the senses but beneath them, at the level where the self itself is struck.

Consider the nearest earthly analogues the modern mind already understands. The doctor's phone call with the biopsy result; the midnight ring that brings news of a crash; the market crash, the earthquake alert, the siren that empties a street in seconds — each is a small *qāriʿah*, a striker that in one instant reorders all priorities, reducing yesterday's urgencies to trivia. Everyone who has lived through such a moment knows the sensation the verse names: time splits into before and after; the heart is literally struck, the breath stops, the world tilts. The sūrah's claim is that history as a whole has such a moment ahead of it — a universal biopsy result, a cosmic midnight call — and that wisdom consists in letting the small strikers train the heart for the great one. The believer who trembles rightly at the small knocks — a funeral, an illness, a verse heard in prayer — is rehearsing; the Day will find his heart already acquainted with awe, and awe already converted into readiness.

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:2

> **what is the calamity?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is Not Asking for Information**

*Mā al-qāriʿah* — "What is the Striker?" The sūrah's second verse is a question, but it is not posed in ignorance and it does not expect the listener to answer. The Arab rhetoricians gave this device a precise name: *istifhām li-l-taʿẓīm*, the interrogative of magnification — a question asked not to learn but to declare that the thing asked about is too enormous to be captured in the answer. When a man says of a calamity, "What a calamity it was!" he is not requesting a definition; he is confessing that no definition suffices. The Qur'an lifts this everyday idiom to the level of revelation: God Himself asks, *"What is the Striker?"*, and the asking is itself the teaching — namely, that the Striker exceeds thy measure.

Notice the pronoun-less daring of the move. The speaker is God; the addressee is every ear the recitation reaches, beginning with the Prophet ﷺ and widening to all mankind. By voicing the question on the listener's behalf, the verse does what the greatest teachers do: it asks aloud the very question stirring silently in the pupil's breast. The Makkan hearer, struck by the bare noun of verse 1, was already inwardly asking, "What is this Striker?" — and before his lips can move, the revelation speaks his bewilderment back to him. This is dialogue as pedagogy: the student discovers that his confusion has been anticipated, which both honours him (his question is worth voicing) and humbles him (the answer is not his to supply). Al-Rāzī observed of such passages that the question first empties the vessel — the heart confesses it does not know — so that the subsequent description pours into a vessel prepared to receive it.

**The Qur'an's Habit of Magnifying with "Mā"**

This verse belongs to a recurring Qur'anic drumbeat. The formula *mā* + the awesome thing appears at the head of some of the Book's most overwhelming passages, and each occurrence follows the same rhythm of awe, question, and unveiling:

> *"The Inevitable Reality! What is the Inevitable Reality (mā al-ḥāqqah)? And what shall apprise thee of the Inevitable Reality?"* (Qur'an 69:1–3)

> *"And what shall apprise thee of what the Crusher (mā al-ḥuṭamah) is?"* (Qur'an 104:5, answering the question of 104:4)

> *"By the heaven and the Night-Comer. And what shall apprise thee of what the Night-Comer (mā al-ṭāriq) is? It is the piercing star."* (Qur'an 86:1–3)

> *"And what shall apprise thee of what the Night of Decree (mā laylatu al-qadr) is? The Night of Decree is better than a thousand months."* (Qur'an 97:2–3)

The exegetes noted that this *mā* is never the *mā* of dismissal ("what is it to me?") but the *mā* of *tafwkhīm* — aggrandisement. Al-Zamakhsharī remarks that such questions "cast the thing into the arena of the indescribable," signalling that whatever follows will be description of signs and effects, never exhaustive definition. Significantly, the Qur'an applies the formula to matters of both terror and mercy — Hell's depths (*mā saqar*, 74:27), the Day of Recompense (*mā yawmu al-dīn*, 82:17–18), and the Night of Decree alike — teaching that the method of awe is one, though its objects differ. Here, twinned most closely with 69:2, the question stands at the doorway of eschatology proper: what follows is not information but initiation.

**Why the Answer Is Withheld: Knowledge Behind the Veil**

A careful reader will notice that verse 2 receives no direct answer at all. Verse 3 does not define the Striker; it deepens the question ("And what shall apprise thee of the Striker?"), and verses 4–5 answer only with images — moths, carded wool. The definition, strictly speaking, never comes. The classical commentators saw profound wisdom in this refusal. Al-Qurṭubī explains that the repetition of the question is meant to magnify the affair and to confess the inadequacy of human conception: the true nature (*ḥaqīqah*) of that Day cannot be contained in a sentence, so the Qur'an describes its marks (*ʿalāmāt*) and its effects instead. Ibn Taymiyyah's school would later generalise the principle: the matters of the Unseen (*al-ghayb*) are known to us through revealed description truly but not exhaustively — we know them as they have been named to us, while their full reality awaits witnessing.

This is not obscurity but honesty about the limits of language. No one who has never heard an explosion can be given the sound in words; he can be told of shattered glass, ringing ears, and dust, and from the effects he may approach the cause. So the Qur'an, addressing creatures who have never witnessed the unmaking of the world, declines to define and consents to depict. The withholding is itself merciful: a full unveiling of that Day's terror in this life would, as the Prophet ﷺ once said of the Punishment of the Grave, make life itself unlivable:

> *"Were it not that you would stop burying one another, I would have asked God to let you hear the punishment of the grave which I hear."* (Muslim 2868, from Anas ibn Mālik)

The veil, in other words, is load-bearing. Revelation lifts exactly as much of it as the heart can profitably bear — enough to awaken, not enough to annihilate — and verse 2 marks the veil's edge with a question that both invites and restrains.

**What the Prophet ﷺ Said of That Day's Magnitude**

Though the verse withholds definition, the Prophet's ﷺ authentic descriptions fill the imagination with lawful glimpses of what the question points toward. Of the gathering itself, al-Bukhārī and Muslim record:

> *"You shall be gathered barefoot, naked, and uncircumcised."* Then ʿĀʾishah said, "O Messenger of God, men and women together, looking at one another?" He replied: *"The matter shall be too momentous for them to look at one another."* (al-Bukhārī 6527; Muslim 2859)

Of the press and sweat of that standing, Muslim records:

> *"On the Day of Resurrection the sun shall be brought near to mankind until it is a mile's distance from them, and men shall be submerged in sweat according to their deeds: among them one to whom it reaches his ankles, one to whom it reaches his knees, one to whom it reaches his waist, and one whom it bridles."* (Muslim 2864, from al-Miqdād ibn al-Aswad)

And of the Day's crushing length for those unprepared, the Qur'an itself declares that it shall be *"a day the measure of which is fifty thousand years"* (70:4) — a verse the commentators connect with the varying experience of the Day: long as an age for the wretched, light as a prescribed prayer for the believer, as some reports indicate. Each of these texts is, in effect, an answer to *"What is the Striker?"* — not a definition but a facet, a glint of the enormity the question announces. The wise student gathers such facets the way one gathers shards of a brilliant vessel: no single piece is the whole, but every piece proves the splendour — and the terror — of what was broken open.

**The Question That Shrinks Every Other Question**

Finally, consider what this question does to all the other questions that consume human life. Every soul arrives each morning carrying its small interrogatives: What will the market do? What does my rival intend? What is my status, my salary, my image? These are not illegitimate questions, but they are miniature beside *"What is the Striker?"* — and the sūrah's pedagogy consists precisely in holding the miniature beside the immense until proportion returns. The psychologists of attention speak of "attentional capture": a sufficiently arresting stimulus resets the hierarchy of concerns. Verse 2 is divine attentional capture. For the few seconds it takes to recite, the believer's hierarchy is forcibly reordered: the Striker first, everything else after.

There is a practical discipline hidden here, and the righteous of every age have practised it. When a worldly anxiety swells until it fills the whole horizon — a debt, an insult, a diagnosis — set beside it the question of this verse, deliberately and slowly: *What is the Striker?* The anxiety does not vanish, but it is rescaled; it takes its true size against the one event that dwarfs all events. Conversely, when a worldly ambition inflames — a promotion, a triumph, a pleasure — the same question cools it to its true temperature. This is why the short eschatological sūrahs were the daily bread of the early community's worship: they functioned as a twice-daily recalibration of the soul's instruments. The man who asks *"What is the Striker?"* every morning in prayer will find, by evening, that he has asked all his other questions more wisely.

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:3

> **And what shall apprise thee of the calamity?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Knock Falls Harder Than the First**

*Wa mā adrāka mā al-qāriʿah* — "And what shall apprise thee of what the Striker is?" The question of verse 2 is now repeated, but sharpened: no longer "what is it?" but "what could possibly *make thee know* what it is?" The verb *adrā* (fourth form of *d-r-y*, to know) means to cause to know, to apprise, to convey knowledge across a gap — and the question denies, gently, that the gap can be crossed by ordinary means. Verse 2 asked about the thing; verse 3 asks about the *knowing* of the thing, and finds even the knowing beyond reach. It is the difference between asking "What is the ocean?" and "What could ever make thee know the ocean, thou who hast never left the shore?" The second question does not merely withhold the answer; it confesses the incommensurability of the answer with the questioner.

The particle *wa* ("and") at the opening deserves attention, for it binds the two questions into a rising staircase rather than a mere repetition. The rhetoricians call this *taraqqi* — gradation: each step ascends beyond the last. Verse 1 announced; verse 2 marvelled; verse 3 despairs of encompassing — and only then, with the heart fully emptied of pretension, does revelation begin to depict. Al-Rāzī, commenting on the twin passage in Sūrah 69, explains the wisdom of the double question: the first proclaims the thing's greatness, the second proclaims the hearer's incapacity, and between greatness above and incapacity below, awe is born. Awe is precisely the state in which the images of verses 4–5 can do their work; the questions are the plough, the images are the seed.

**"Mā Adrāka": The Qur'an's Signature of Unveiling**

The formula *wa mā adrāka* ("and what shall apprise thee?") occurs thirteen times in the Qur'an, and the classical scholars uncovered a consistent law governing it, transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and affirmed by al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Suyūṭī: wherever the Qur'an says *mā adrāka*, God Himself goes on to apprise — the veil He lowers, He lifts. Its sister formula, *wa mā yudrīka* ("and what could make thee know?", with the imperfect verb suggesting a still-open, unanswered state), is by contrast never followed by its answer in the text:

> *"And what could make thee know (wa mā yudrīka)? Perhaps the Hour is near."* (Qur'an 42:17) — near, but its moment undisclosed.

> *"And what could make thee know (wa mā yudrīka)? Perhaps he might purify himself."* (Qur'an 80:3) — the outcome left with God.

Sufyān ibn ʿUyaynah (d. 198/814), the great Makkan scholar of ḥadīth and exegesis, is reported to have stated the rule crisply: everything in the Qur'an introduced by *wa mā adrāka*, God has apprised His Prophet of; everything introduced by *wa mā yudrīka*, He has withheld. Here the rule holds in its most beautiful double form, for the sūrah asks *wa mā adrāka* twice — of the Striker in verse 3, and of the Abyss in verse 10 — and answers twice: the Striker is unveiled in the scenes of the Day (verses 4–5), and the Abyss is unveiled as the raging Fire (verse 11). The sūrah is thus a diptych of two questions and two unveilings, each pair demonstrating God's pedagogy: first humble the mind, then honour it with knowledge it could never have reached alone.

The verb's form carries a further tenderness. *Adrāka* — "apprise *thee*" — is addressed in the singular to the Prophet ﷺ, yet through him to every reciter who lends the verse his tongue. Each believer who recites becomes, for a breath, the "thee" whom God addresses, the pupil singled out for private instruction. The commentators note this double audience throughout the Qur'an's singular address: its first horizon is Muḥammad ﷺ, its second is every soul until the Hour. To recite verse 3 slowly is therefore to stand, for a moment, in the Prophet's ﷺ own place before the Revealer — asked, humbled, and then taught.

**What the Early Exegetes Heard in This Verse**

Al-Ṭabarī gathers the earliest glosses with his characteristic sobriety: the verse means, "And what thing, O Muḥammad, shall make thee know what the Striker is?" — i.e., its affair is greater than thy knowledge can compass, until We describe to thee something of it. Mujāhid (d. 104/722), the student of Ibn ʿAbbās, is reported to have glossed such verses as divine magnification (*taʿẓīm*): God magnifies the matter in His servant's eyes so that he prepares for it as befits its magnitude. Qatādah heard in the repetition an emphasis that the Day's true nature is beyond reckoning — *"it magnifies its affair,"* as the reports phrase it.

Ibn Kathīr, following this stream, treats verses 2–3 together as a declaration that the Striker is among the most tremendous and terrible of matters, its full reality known to God alone; what follows is description of its terrors by way of warning. Al-Qurṭubī adds a grammatical jewel: the verse's structure — *mā adrāka mā al-qāriʿah* — places the second *mā* (the "what" of the Striker) as the object of the apprising, so that the sentence literally asks, "What shall apprise thee of *what* the Striker is?" — question nested within question, bewilderment squared. And al-Shawkānī (d. 1250/1834), in *Fatḥ al-Qadīr*, draws the practical conclusion the whole tradition implies: since thou canst not know it of thyself, listen to Him who knows, and prepare.

Read together, the early voices agree on a single point with many facets: this verse is the Qur'an's way of saying that eschatology is received knowledge, not discovered knowledge. No telescope, no philosophy, no near-death anecdote can apprise man of the Striker; only the Striker's own Author can. The verse is thus an epistemological boundary stone, planted at the edge of the Unseen.

**Received Knowledge and the Humility of Reason**

That boundary stone carries a large lesson about reason itself — one the Qur'an teaches everywhere but stages here with unusual drama. Human reason is exalted in Islam: it is the faculty by which man knows his Lord from His signs, distinguishes truth from falsehood, and bears the Trust (*amānah*) of moral responsibility. But reason is also bounded: it cannot, unassisted, report on what lies beyond death, for none of its instruments extends past the grave. The verse honours reason precisely by refusing to flatter it — by telling it, plainly, "this thou canst not reach; receive it."

Ibn Taymiyyah (d. 728/1328) made this relationship between reason and revelation a cornerstone of his thought: sound reason never contradicts authentic revelation, but neither can reason dispense with revelation in matters reason cannot access. The matters of the Last Day are the supreme instance. A philosopher may argue elegantly that justice *requires* a reckoning — and the Qur'an itself commends such reasoning:

> *"Did you suppose that We created you aimlessly, and that you shall not be returned to Us?"* (Qur'an 23:115)

— but what the reckoning *is*, how the Trumpet sounds, how the Scales weigh, what the Fire is: these are particulars of the Unseen, and particulars come only by report (*khabar*). Verse 3 is that principle compressed into a question: thou canst reason *that* it must be; only God can apprise thee *what* it is. The rational response is therefore not scepticism but gratitude — the gratitude of a traveller who, unable to scout the road ahead, is handed a true map by One who made the road.

**The Teacher Who Questions Before He Tells**

There is also a universal pedagogy modelled here, and every parent, teacher, and counsellor may take it as a masterclass. Modern educational psychology confirms what this verse practised fourteen centuries ago: material preceded by a provocative question is retained far better than material merely presented. The question creates an "information gap" — the mind, made aware of what it lacks, leans forward; the subsequent telling lands in soil already opened. The Qur'an, the unsurpassed teacher of mankind, uses the device relentlessly: *"Shall I guide you to a commerce that shall deliver you from a painful punishment?"* (61:10); *"And what is the matter with you, that you fight not in the way of God?"* (4:75); and here, the double question before the double image.

The application is immediate and practical. The father who wishes his child to grasp a danger does not begin with the lecture but with the question: "Do you know what fire does?" The caller to God who wishes a heedless friend to consider the Hereafter does not open with the argument but with the wonder: "Have you ever imagined what that Day will be like?" And the believer's own soul — the hardest pupil of all — is best taught the same way: before reciting verses 4–5, pause upon verse 3 and let it interrogate thee. *What shall apprise thee of the Striker?* Ask it in the watches of the night, ask it beside a fresh grave, ask it when the world glitters most convincingly — and then, humbled and leaning forward, receive the answer the Knower Himself supplies.

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:4

> **A day wherein mankind shall be like scattered moths,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Answer: The Veil Lifts on Mankind**

After three verses of announcement and awestruck questioning, the unveiling promised by *wa mā adrāka* begins — and its first scene is mankind itself: *yawma yakūnu al-nāsu ka-l-farāshi al-mabthūth* — "a Day wherein mankind shall be like scattered moths." The grammar, as the source commentary notes, opens the verse in the accusative (*yawma*, "a Day wherein..."), implying an elided governing word: most exegetes supply "Mention" (*udhkur*) — "Mention a Day wherein..." — while others, such as al-Zamakhsharī, attach it to the Striker itself: "It shall strike on a Day wherein...." Either construction preserves the essential drama: the abstract Calamity of verses 1–3 suddenly acquires a date, a populace, and a picture. The Striker is no longer a name; it is a Day, and on that Day there are people — people like moths.

Note the order of the unveiling. The sūrah could have begun its answer with the heavens splitting or the stars falling; instead it begins with *al-nās* — mankind, the ordinary, collective noun for us. The Qur'an's eschatology is never means cosmic spectacle for its own sake; the cosmos convulses, but the camera, so to speak, stays on the human face. This is the same instinct that makes Sūrah Al-Zalzalah (99) — this sūrah's closest thematic sister — pivot its earthquake within six verses to *"that Day mankind shall issue forth in groups to be shown their deeds"* (99:6). The mountains matter, and they will have their verse next; but the moths come first, because the Day is ultimately about them. Every hearer is thereby refused the seat of the spectator: thou art not watching the Striker, thou art in it.

**Why Moths: The Entomology of Bewilderment**

*Al-farāsh* is the winged insect that flutters heedlessly toward light — the moth, the gnat-like night-flyer — and *al-mabthūth* is that which is strewn, scattered broadcast, dispersed in all directions (from *baththa*, to scatter abroad, the same verb by which God "scattered" living creatures through the earth in 2:164 and 42:29). The early exegetes unfolded the simile's precision with evident delight. Mujāhid and Qatādah are reported to have said that men on that Day will be like the *farāsh* in their fluttering confusion, darting to and fro with no direction — the perfect image of a crowd whose every landmark has vanished. Al-Ṭabarī records the gloss that they rush into the Fire as moths rush into flame, heedless and irresistible; الفراش يتهافت في النار — the moth plunges recklessly into fire — being proverbial among the Arabs.

The simile operates on at least four levels at once. First, *multitude*: moths around a lamp on a summer night are numberless, layered, indistinguishable — so too the risen generations, "from the first of them to the last of them," in a throng no eye has ever surveyed. Second, *agitation*: the moth never rests, never flies straight, but flutters, collides, and wheels — the very physics of panic, when purposeful locomotion collapses into vibration. Third, *frailty*: the moth is among the feeblest of flying things, crushed between two fingers, singed by the very light it loves — and so the proud structures of human power, armies and empires and egos, are shown in their true eschatological size. Fourth, and most piercing, *attraction to ruin*: the moth's fatal love of the flame mirrors man's fatal love of what destroys him — the sins he flutters toward though warned, the Fire he approaches though informed. The Prophet ﷺ himself drew exactly this parallel in one of his most tender parables, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"My parable and the parable of the people is that of a man who kindled a fire, and when it lit up what was around him, the moths and insects began to fall into it — and he began to push them back, but they overcame him and plunged in. So I am grasping your waist-wrappers to hold you back from the Fire, but you slip from my hands."* (al-Bukhārī 6483; Muslim 2284, from Abū Hurayrah)

The sūrah's moth is thus already, proleptically, the sinner's moth: the Day only makes visible the fluttering that was always there.

**Moths Here, Locusts There: The Qur'an's Two Crowd-Similes**

The Qur'an describes the risen multitudes with two insect-similes, and the classical scholars pondered why each stands where it does. Here, at the convulsion itself, men are *like scattered moths*; in Sūrah Al-Qamar, at the emergence from the graves, they are *like scattered locusts*:

> *"With their eyes humbled they emerge from the graves as if they were scattered locusts (ka-annahum jarādun muntashir), hastening toward the Caller."* (Qur'an 54:7–8)

Al-Rāzī and others reconciled the two scenes as successive phases of one Day: first the scattering bewilderment of the Blast — moths, directionless, whirled by terror — then the summoned march to the Reckoning — locusts, still numberless and quivering, but now streaming in one direction toward the Caller (*muhṭiʿīna ilā al-dāʿ*, 54:8). The source commentary notes the same distinction from a different angle: the present verse depicts the end of the world (the world's unmaking), while 54:7 depicts the Resurrection (the world's remaking). Both insights cohere: bewilderment first, mustering after; the moth-phase and the locust-phase of mankind's last transformation.

There is also a contrast of sound and gait worth savouring. The moth is silent, erratic, nocturnal — terror in its confused dawn. The locust is drumming, wave-like, daylight-devouring — terror in its ordered march. Between them they exhaust the phenomenology of mass panic as modern disaster studies describe it: first the milling phase, when each individual darts without information; then the herding phase, when the crowd locks onto a single vector — a caller, an exit, a voice. Fourteen centuries before crowd science, the Qur'an staged both phases in two verses revealed years apart, each simile exact to its moment. The denier of Makkah, who had seen locust swarms strip an orchard in an afternoon and moths storm his lamp every night, needed no commentary to feel both.

**Drunk Without Wine: The Qur'an's Full Portrait of That Bewilderment**

The moth-simile does not stand alone in the Book; it is one panel of a larger portrait of eschatological bewilderment, and the panels illuminate one another. The fullest is the opening of Sūrah Al-Ḥajj:

> *"O mankind, reverence your Lord! Truly the earthquake of the Hour is a tremendous thing. On the Day you see it, every nursing mother shall forget what she suckles, and every pregnant female shall lay down her burden, and thou shalt see men as though drunk, though drunk they shall not be — but God's punishment is severe."* (Qur'an 22:1–2)

Every nursing mother forgetting her infant: the Qur'an reaches for the strongest bond in nature — the lactating mother, whose body itself remembers the child — and snaps it, to measure the terror. Pregnant women miscarrying from sheer fright. Men *sukārā*, drunk — staggering, slurring, reeling — yet *wa mā hum bi-sukārā*, "though drunk they shall not be": no wine, only horror, producing drunkenness more total than wine. Add to this the sūrah's moths, and Sūrah ʿAbasa's tableau of the man fleeing his brother, his mother, his father, his wife, and his sons (80:34–36), and the portrait is complete: bonds dissolved, senses unseated, purposeful humanity reduced to fluttering biomass — awaiting only the Caller who will reconstitute it for judgment.

The Prophet ﷺ added the inward dimension to these outward scenes when he described hearts on that Day. Muslim records from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr:

> *"The hearts of the children of Adam are all between two fingers of the Most Merciful, as one heart, and He turns them as He wills."* (Muslim 2654)

If hearts are so turned in this life's small terrors — the interview, the diagnosis, the battlefield — what turning shall they undergo when the Trumpet sounds? The moth's wings beat two hundred times a second; the human heart at full panic approaches two hundred beats a minute; the simile, pursued physiologically, suggests that on that Day every breast shall become a winged thing, hammering against its cage of ribs. Terror will not be an emotion among others; it will be the element in which all else swims.

**The Scattering We Already Practise**

But the verse is not only about the future; like all eschatology, it holds a mirror to the present. *Mabthūth* — scattered broadcast, strewn without order — is an uncomfortably exact description of contemporary mankind's inner state. Consider the modern attention, fluttering from screen to screen, notification to notification, outrage to outrage, never alighting, never resting, drawn irresistibly toward whatever glows brightest regardless of whether it nourishes or burns. The moth's fatal phototaxis — its hardwired plunge toward light — has become, through the engineering of the attention economy, the business model of the age: billions of human moths, eyes locked on lamps designed by others, wings beating, lives scattering. The similitude the sūrah projects onto the Last Day is already visible, in miniature, in every subway carriage of bowed glowing faces.

The moral is not despair but diagnosis and direction. The scatter of the Day is involuntary and universal; the scatter of today is voluntary and curable. Every act of collectedness — a prayer performed with presence, a page of Qur'an read with reflection, an hour given wholly to one's child, a deed done sincerely for God alone — is a gathering of the moth's wings into intention (*niyyah*), a rehearsal of the mustering toward the Caller. The early Muslims understood worship as *jamʿiyyah*, collectedness: five times a day the scattered self is called — *"Come to prayer, come to success"* — and re-gathered facing one Qiblah, shoulder to shoulder with brethren, fluttering stilled into rows. The man who answers the small caller five times daily need not fear the Great Caller; his wings have learned the way home, and on the Day of Scattering he shall be among the gathered.

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:5

> **and the mountains shall be like carded wool.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Answer: The Veil Lifts on the Mountains**

*Wa takūnu al-jibālu ka-l-ʿihni al-manfūsh* — "and the mountains shall be like carded wool." The unveiling's second scene turns from the frailest of things to the firmest: if verse 4 showed mankind, the supposedly stable, reduced to fluttering moths, verse 5 shows the mountains, the supposedly eternal, reduced to teased wool. The pairing is deliberate and devastating. Man and mountain are the Qur'an's two poles of earthly solidity — man the proudest of the living, mountains the firmest of the lifeless — and the Striker unmakes both in two verses of eleven words. What flutters and what stands, what breathes and what anchors: nothing in the frame of the world survives the frame's breaking.

The conjunction *wa* ("and") binds the two scenes into a single Day, a single sentence of unmaking. The rhetoricians note the ascending shock of the sequence: that men should panic is terrible but conceivable — men panic daily. That *mountains* should come undone is inconceivable, and therefore the stronger proof that the Day is unlike all days. The Qur'an reasons from the greater to the less throughout its eschatology: if the mountains, which thou didst take for eternity's own pillars, become as wool, then what, O son of Adam, shall become of thee? The verse does not pose this question aloud; it trusts the moth of verse 4, still fluttering in the listener's imagination, to hear it.

**ʿIhn and Manfūsh: Wool, Colour, and the Carder's Art**

Two words carry the whole image, and both repay close attention. *Al-ʿihn* is dyed wool — wool prepared for weaving, coloured in reds, yellows, and varied hues. Several early exegetes, including Qatādah and al-Ḍaḥḥāk, seized on this detail: the mountains on that Day shall be like wool *of diverse colours*, because mountains themselves are of diverse colours — red sandstone, black basalt, white limestone, green with forest — and the simile preserves their variety even as it dissolves their substance. The Qur'an elsewhere draws attention to the mountains' chromatic splendour as a sign:

> *"And among the mountains are streaks, white and red, of diverse colours, and raven black."* (Qur'an 35:27)

The end of the world does not grey this palette but unbinds it: the colours remain, the coherence goes. It is as though a woven carpet — the earth's mountain-ranges seen from afar are exactly like the figured bands of a carpet — were picked apart into its dyed fibres, hues intact, pattern destroyed.

*Al-manfūsh* is wool that has been carded — teased apart with the carder's bow and comb until its fibres separate and fluff, weightless and wind-borne. The image is domestic, feminine, and precisely observed: every household of Arabia knew the carder's heap, the cloud of wool that a breath could scatter. Al-Ṭabarī records from the early generations that *manfūsh* is wool "which has been teased apart until its parts separate," and Ibn Kathīr glosses the verse as the mountains becoming like carded wool in their lightness, fragmentation, and scattering before the winds. The heaviest things known to man become the lightest things known to woman's hand: the semantic distance between *jibāl* and *ʿihn manfūsh* is the measure of the Day's power. What geologists now call orogeny — the slow violence that raised the Himalayas grain by grain over fifty million years — the Striker reverses between two blasts of a trumpet.

**The Qur'an's Seven-Stage Unmaking of the Mountains**

No cosmic object receives a more detailed eschatology in the Qur'an than the mountains, and the scattered verses, gathered, read as a sequence of unmaking in stages — as though the Book returned to the mountains again and again, each time advancing their dissolution one degree. The classical commentators, and modern thematic studies after them, arrange the stations roughly as follows. First, the violent shock: *"and the mountains are shattered"* (56:5), *"the earth and the mountains are lifted and crushed with a single crushing"* (69:14). Second, the setting in motion: *"and the mountains are set in motion"* (81:3), *"thou shalt see the mountains, which thou supposest firm, passing as the passing of clouds"* (27:88) — the firmest things in experience gliding like vapour. Third, the uprooting and scattering: *"and the mountains are scattered"* (77:10). Fourth, the pulverising: they become *"like heaps of shifting sand"* (73:14), *"dust, scattered"* (56:6). Fifth — our verse — the woolliness: *"like carded wool"* (101:5; cf. 70:9). Sixth, the levelling: *"they ask thee of the mountains — say: My Lord shall blast them into scattered dust, and leave the earth a level plain wherein thou shalt see neither crookedness nor curve"* (20:105–107). Seventh, the vanishing: *"and We shall make what is upon the earth barren dust"* (18:8).

Whether these are strictly successive phases or varied aspects of one convulsion, the exegetes differed — al-Rāzī favoured a real sequence across the two Trumpet-blasts (the swoon and the rising), while others read them as complementary perspectives — but all agreed on the theological point: the mountains, which the Qur'an elsewhere presents as pegs and anchors (*awtād*, 78:7), ballast against the earth's convulsion (*rawāsī*, 16:15; 21:31), and reverent worshippers that glorify God with David (34:10; 38:18), must themselves be unmade, because even the most obedient and most firmly-rooted of creatures is still *creature* — contingent, dependent, and terminable. *"All that is upon it passes away, and there remains the Face of thy Lord, Possessed of Majesty and Honour"* (55:26–27). The mountains' dissolution is the Qur'an's most vivid proof-text for that verse: look upon what thou thought eternal, and learn what alone is eternal.

**What the Prophet ﷺ Said of the Earth's Last Travail**

The Prophetic traditions clothe these Qur'anic stations in further sensory detail, always with authenticated chains the student can trust. Of the earth's flattening, al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from Sahl ibn Saʿd:

> *"On the Day of Resurrection mankind shall be gathered upon a white, dust-coloured land, like a flat loaf of bread — having no landmarks for anyone."* (al-Bukhārī 6521; Muslim 2790)

No mountain, no building, no tree, no banner: a loaf's smooth surface, that no soul may hide behind anything and no eye may orient by anything. Of the earth's testimony, al-Tirmidhī records:

> *"Do you know what its tidings are? Its tidings are that it shall testify concerning every servant, male and female, what he wrought upon its back: 'He did such-and-such on such-and-such a day.' Those are its tidings."* (al-Tirmidhī 3353, commenting on 99:4; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The mountains that crumble to wool and the earth that flattens to a loaf are thus not merely destroyed but *deposed as scenery and promoted as witnesses*: creation's last act is testimony. And of the general terror, ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar reported the Prophet ﷺ as saying:

> *"Whoever wishes to look upon the Day of Resurrection as though seeing it with his own eyes, let him recite: 'When the sun is folded up' (Sūrah 81), 'When the heaven is cleft' (Sūrah 82), and 'When the heaven is split open' (Sūrah 84)."* (al-Tirmidhī 3333; graded ḥasan)

Our sūrah belongs to that same gallery of see-it-with-thine-eyes chapters — shorter than its sisters, but admitting the viewer through the same door: the unmade mountain.

**When the Immovable Moves: The Lesson for Anchored Hearts**

Mountains in the Qur'an are never only geology; they are the standing metaphor for whatever man takes as unshakeable. Men build their inner lives around private mountain-ranges: the fortune that cannot fail, the empire that cannot fall, the health that cannot break, the reputation that cannot crack, the ideology that explains everything. The Qur'an honours the wholesome form of this instinct — faith itself is likened to *"a goodly word, as a goodly tree, its root firm and its branches in heaven"* (14:24), and the believer's heart is to be mountain-like in patience — but it wages relentless war on the idolatrous form: taking the contingent for the necessary, the propped for the self-subsisting.

Hence the verse's psychological surgery. Every soul harbours at least one inner mountain — the thing it leans on besides God, the pillar whose removal it cannot imagine surviving. The Striker's announcement performs a merciful demolition in advance: *imagine it as carded wool*. The fortune? Wool before the wind of a single market morning — as the crashes of living memory proved to millionaires in a weekend. The empire? Wool — as the twentieth century proved to flags that had flown for centuries. The body? Wool — as every hospital corridor quietly testifies. Only one mountain is not wool: *"God — there is no god but He, the Living, the Self-Subsisting"* (2:255), the Root that needs no soil, the Peg that needs no earth. The believer who transfers his leaning from the wool-mountains to the Living Mountain before the Day arrives shall find, on the Day the ranges drift like thistledown, that his footing never moved — for it was never planted in them.

There is a final tenderness in the simile the hard-pressed should not miss. Wool is soft; carded wool is softness perfected — the gentlest substance the Arabs knew, the cradle-lining, the wound-dressing. That the mountains of terror should end as softness suggests, by the Qur'an's own logic of images, that the Day's violence is not the last word even over the inanimate creation: after crushing comes quiet, after levelling comes the Plain of Gathering, and after the Plain — for the heavy-scaled — comes *"a life contenting."* The wool is terrible as dissolution but gentle as touch; and so the sūrah pivots, in its very next verse, from the unmade cosmos to the weighed soul — from what becomes of mountains to what becomes of thee.

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:6

> **As for one whose scales are heavy,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pivot of the Sūrah: From Cosmos to Conscience**

*Fa-ammā man thaqulat mawāzīnuh* — "As for one whose scales are heavy...." With the particle *fa-ammā* ("as for..."), the sūrah pivots on its heel. Five verses have shown the Day from the outside — its name, its terror, its moths, its wool-mountains; now the camera turns inward, from the unmade world to the weighed soul. The rest of the sūrah (verses 6–11) concerns nothing but the human outcome: two parties, two weights, two dwellings. The classical commentators recognised in *ammā... wa ammā* ("as for... and as for...," verses 6 and 8) the Qur'an's great sorting construction — the same that divides mankind at the end of Sūrah Al-Layl (92:5–10), in the seating of the companions of right and left (56:8–9, 90–94), and behind the parable of the two gardens. Grammar itself performs the Separation (*al-faṣl*) that is the Day's innermost meaning: sentences fork, and so does eternity.

Note what the pivot assumes without arguing: that deeds *can* be weighed, that a life *has* a weight, that the scattered moth of verse 4 will be gathered, placed, and measured. Between verse 5 and verse 6 lies the entire middle of the Last Day — the standing, the reckoning, the unfurling of the records — compressed into a conjunction. The sūrah's brevity is not omission but velocity: it hurries past the procedures to the verdicts, because its purpose is decision, not documentation. The Makkan hearer, still trembling from the wool-mountains, is given no time to recover as a spectator; before his awe can cool into curiosity, the question becomes personal: *thy* scales — heavy or light? Every reciter since stands in that same draught between the unmade cosmos and the weighed self, and the sūrah offers exactly two shelters. This verse names the first.

**Mawāzīn: Why "Scales" in the Plural**

The word *mawāzīn* is the plural of *mīzān* (balance, scale), and its plural form provoked one of the sūrah's most discussed questions: shall there be many scales, or one? The majority of the classical exegetes — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī among them — held that the Balance is in reality one, and the plural honours either the multiplicity of the weighed (each soul experiences its own weighing) or the multiplicity of the deeds weighed (many acts upon one Scale), or magnifies the affair itself, as kings say "we." Al-Qurṭubī records the alternative view of some — that each person, or each category of deed, has its own balance — but the weight of the tradition favours one tremendous Balance with two pans, vast enough for heaven and earth, as several traditions describe.

More important than the arithmetic is the distinction the Qur'an maintains between *mīzān* (singular) and *mawāzīn* (plural), which the source commentary rightly highlights. The singular *mīzān* denotes the universal principle of just measure — revelation itself as the criterion, the moral order of the cosmos:

> *"Assuredly We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and sent down with them the Book and the Balance (al-mīzān), that mankind may uphold justice."* (Qur'an 57:25)

> *"And the heaven — He raised it, and set the Balance (al-mīzān), that you transgress not in the Balance."* (Qur'an 55:7–8)

The plural *mawāzīn*, by contrast, appears only for the eschatological weighing of individual deeds:

> *"And We shall set up the just scales (mawāzīna al-qisṭ) for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul shall be wronged in aught."* (Qur'an 21:47)

The theology is elegant: the one universal Balance of justice, revealed in this life as criterion, becomes on the Last Day the many weighings of persons. The standard thou didst live by — or against — becomes the standard thou art weighed upon. No soul is measured by a rule it never received; the Scale is the Sharíaʿah made visible, mercy and justice calibrated to the ounce.

**What Makes Scales Heavy: Deeds, Character, and Intention**

What, then, fills the pans? The Qur'an's first answer is deeds themselves — *"heavy with good deeds,"* as the source commentary phrases it — and the arithmetic of divine generosity is staggeringly tilted toward the doer:

> *"Whosoever brings a good deed shall have ten times the like thereof; but whosoever brings an evil deed shall be recompensed only with the like thereof, and they shall not be wronged."* (Qur'an 6:160)

> *"Truly good deeds remove those that are evil."* (Qur'an 11:114)

> *"And the good deed and the evil deed are not equal."* (Qur'an 41:34)

Tenfold multiplication on the credit side, single counting on the debit side, and erasure of debits by credits: no earthly bank offers such terms. The commentators marvel that with such arithmetic any scales could come out light at all — and conclude that lightness, when it occurs, testifies to a bankruptcy so total, a refusal so persistent, that even tenfold mercy could not fill the pan. The heavy scales are thus less an achievement wrested from a miserly cosmos than a harvest gathered from a lavish one; the wonder is not that some are heavy but that any are light.

But the traditions refine "deeds" into something subtler than mere motions. Regarding character, al-Tirmidhī records the Prophet's ﷺ astonishing ranking:

> *"There is nothing placed in the Balance heavier than goodness of character (ḥusn al-khuluq)."* (al-Tirmidhī 2003; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; also Abū Dāwūd 4799)

Not length of prayer, not volume of alms, but the texture of one's dealings — patience with the difficult, gentleness with the weak, honesty when lying would pay. Regarding intention, the most famous of all traditions, opening both al-Bukhārī and Muslim's rival's collections and the Forty of al-Nawawī:

> *"Deeds are only in accord with their intentions, and every person has only what he intended."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907, from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb)

And regarding the weight of the lightest acts, the two paradisal words already cited in this commentary's introduction — *"light upon the tongue, heavy upon the Scale"* (al-Bukhārī 6406; Muslim 2694). Taken together, the law of heaviness reads: a deed's weight equals its substance, multiplied by the character that performed it, multiplied by the sincerity that aimed it, multiplied tenfold by divine generosity — with the tongue's least motion capable, sincerely uttered, of outweighing mountains. The moth of verse 4, weightless in terror, may yet prove heavier than the mountains of verse 5: such are the Scales' reversals.

**Mercy Above the Scales: The Verdict Behind the Weighing**

Yet the sūrah's logic, pressed by the theologians, opens onto a deeper truth the traditions state explicitly: the Scales weigh truly, but salvation itself flows from mercy, not from arithmetic. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record the Prophet's ﷺ solemn disclaimer:

> *"None of you will enter Paradise by his deeds."* They said, "Not even thou, O Messenger of God?" He said: *"Not even me, save that God shelter me with His Mercy."* (al-Bukhārī 5673; Muslim 2816, from Abū Hurayrah)

And again, with the image of divine covering:

> *"No one will be saved by his deeds."* They said, "Not even thou?" He said: *"Not even me, save that God enfold me with mercy from Himself."* (Muslim 2817)

The theologians, from al-Ashʿarī to Ibn Taymiyyah, harmonised weighing and mercy without diminishing either: deeds are the *sabab* (the occasion and the God-appointed path), mercy is the *ʿillah* (the true bestowing cause); God binds Himself by promise to reward, never by compulsion to be benefited — *"Thy Lord wrongs no one"* (18:49), yet *"He is not questioned concerning what He does"* (21:23). The Scales are thus perfectly just and perfectly gracious at once: just, because every atom is counted — *"whosoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it"* (99:7); gracious, because the counting itself is mercy — ten for one, erasure by repentance, and admission, finally, by the sheltering hand the Prophet ﷺ himself needs. The heavy scales are the form mercy's verdict takes, not a rival to it. This preserves both halves of the believer's heart: diligence, because atoms count; and hope, because mercy outweighs.

**The Audit Every Soul Can Begin Today**

The verse's practical power lies in a single transferable image: life as an accumulation of weight. Modern souls already live by covert scales — the bank balance, the follower count, the curriculum vitae, the bathroom scale — each assigning heaviness to what the market, the crowd, or vanity values. Verse 6 proposes the one Scale that outlasts all markets: deeds weighed by the Knower of atoms, in pans that register intentions the actor himself forgot. The believer who internalises this Scale undergoes a quiet revolution in valuation. The unnoticed kindness to a stranger — weightless on every earthly scale — lands heavily on the heavenly one. The secret prayer in the night's last third — invisible to all biographers — tips a pan. Conversely, the celebrated achievement performed for applause may arrive at the Scales as chaff: *"and We shall turn to what they wrought of deeds and make them scattered dust"* (25:23).

Hence the daily discipline the righteous derived from this verse and its sisters: the evening *muḥāsabah*, the self-audit before sleep. Ḥasan al-Baṣrī (d. 110/728) counselled, in words the tradition has preserved as a maxim:

> *"Call yourselves to account before you are called to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed."*

The practice is simple and takes minutes: review the day's three heaviest deposits and three heaviest withdrawals; repent the withdrawals specifically; resolve one deposit for tomorrow; sleep with scales balanced by contrition. The man who weighs himself nightly in this life, the early Muslims said, will find the Weighing of the Day familiar rather than fearful — he has met these Scales before, in the quiet of his own room, and made his peace with them one evening at a time.

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:7

> **he shall enjoy a life contenting.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words for Eternity: The Verse's Whole Reward**

*Fa-huwa fī ʿīshatin rāḍiyah* — "he shall be in a life contenting." The reward of the heavy scales is pronounced in three Arabic words, and the brevity is eloquent: after the terror of five verses and the suspense of the sixth, bliss arrives without fanfare, as though joy needed no advertising. The particle *fa* ("so, then") binds reward to weight with the tightness of cause and effect — heaviness *yields* contentment as directly as a seed yields its plant — while the pronoun *huwa* ("he") singles out the successful soul for individual address: this bliss is not a collective statistic but a personal hosting. Every heavy-scaled soul hears its own name in that *huwa*. The Qur'an's longest descriptions of Paradise run to pages; here, in the shortest of sūrahs, Paradise is compressed to a preposition, a noun, and an adjective — and the compression teaches that all of Paradise's rivers, couches, and companions are, in essence, one thing: a life that contents.

The preposition *fī* ("in, within") is the verse's quiet marvel. The successful one is not merely *given* a pleasant life as an object handed over; he is *in* it, immersed, enfolded, surrounded on every side as a swimmer is in water or an embryo in warmth. The life is not a possession but an element — an atmosphere of contentment breathed rather than a prize displayed. Al-Rāzī, savouring such prepositions, notes that *fī* here denotes total envelopment (*iḥāṭah*): no edge of the life protrudes into discontent, no corner of the soul remains outside the bliss. Contrast the drowning man's element in this life's sorrows — "in" grief, "in" debt, "in" pain — and the verse promises the exact inversion: a soul *in* contentment as bodies were once *in* affliction, the preposition of immersion transferred from suffering to joy.

**Rāḍiyah: The Grammar of Mutual Contentment**

The adjective *rāḍiyah* (feminine of *rāḍin*, from *r-ḍ-y*, to be pleased, content) is, strictly, an active participle: a "contenting" or "well-pleased" life. The grammarians delighted in its construction, for a life is not literally pleased — its *liver* is. They called this *isnād majāzī*, figurative attribution: contentment so total that it overflows from the soul onto the life itself, until the life is described as pleased on its owner's behalf. It is the idiom of fulfilled love applied to eschatology — as one says "a happy home" meaning the dwellers' happiness has soaked into the walls. The life of Paradise, the verse suggests, is not merely a container of pleasures but a *pleased* thing, a rejoicing ambience that shares its master's joy.

Beneath the grammar lies the root's theology. *Riḍā* — contentment, good-pleasure — is the Qur'an's supreme term for the mutual delight of God and soul. Its highest occurrence crowns the righteous:

> *"God is pleased with them, and they are pleased with Him (raḍiya Allāhu ʿanhum wa raḍū ʿanh). That is the great triumph."* (Qur'an 98:8; cf. 5:119; 9:100; 58:22)

The commentators observed that this mutuality is Paradise's true substance, above rivers and fruits: God's pleasure *with* the soul, and the soul's pleasure *with* God — with His decree, His portion, His neighbourhood. Ibn Taymiyyah's students transmitted his master's hierarchy: Paradise has degrees of delight, but the delight of *riḍwān* — God's good-pleasure — outranks them all, for it is the delight of the Person rather than of the gifts. Our verse's *rāḍiyah* thus smuggles the whole of that theology into a single adjective: the heavy-scaled one enters not merely comfort but *the state of mutual pleasure*, the life in which the ancient tension between what God decrees and what the soul desires is dissolved forever. Every "why?" the soul ever groaned beneath in this life — the child's death, the unanswered prayer, the inexplicable pain — finds its resolution not in an explanation but in a presence: pleased with Him, as He is pleased with thee.

**The Twin Verse: Reading 69:21 Beside 101:7**

The phrase *fī ʿīshatin rāḍiyah* occurs exactly twice in the Qur'an — here, and in the sūrah that is this sūrah's twin:

> *"As for one who is given his book in his right hand, he shall say: 'Here — read my book! Truly I knew that I would meet my reckoning.' So he shall be in a life contenting (fa-huwa fī ʿīshatin rāḍiyah), in a lofty garden, its clusters near at hand. 'Eat and drink with wholesome enjoyment, for what you sent forth in days gone by.'"* (Qur'an 69:19–24)

The longer passage unpacks what our verse compresses: the right hand's book, the cry of vindication (*hāʾumu iqrāʾū kitābiyah* — "Here! Read my book!"), the lofty Garden, the lowered clusters, the festal command to eat and drink. Reading the two together, the exegetes treat 69:19–24 as the commentary and 101:7 as the text — or rather, as two magnifications of one reality. What Al-Ḥāqqah narrates across six verses, Al-Qāriʿah seals in one: the traditions of mutual interpretation (*tafsīr al-Qurʾān bi-l-Qurʾān*) encourage exactly this binocular reading, letting each twin correct the flattening of the other. From 69 we learn the *furnishings* of the contenting life; from 101 we learn its *essence* — that amidst gardens and clusters, the operative fact is contentment itself, the *riḍā* that makes gardens paradisal rather than merely botanical.

Al-Qurṭubī, commenting on the twin, adds a question the thoughtful soul cannot avoid: why should *these* — the right-handed, the heavy-scaled — be content? His answer gathers the Qur'an's scattered promises: because nothing they love shall ever be taken, nothing they hate shall ever approach, nothing they desire shall ever be delayed, and nothing they are shall ever decay. *"No fatigue shall touch them therein, nor shall they ever be expelled therefrom"* (15:48). *"They shall abide therein eternally"* (98:8). Contentment in this life is always mortgaged to time — every joy carries its expiry date, every union its farewell. The *ʿīshah rāḍiyah* is contentment with the mortgage cancelled: joy without farewell, union without parting, life without the small print of death. That, and not the multiplication of pleasures, is what "contenting" means.

**What the Prophet ﷺ Reported of That Contentment**

The authenticated traditions give the contenting life faces and voices the heart can love. Of God's own address to its dwellers, al-Bukhārī and Muslim record the dialogue of *riḍwān* itself:

> *"God shall say to the people of Paradise: 'O people of Paradise!' They shall say: 'At Thy service, our Lord, and at Thy delight!' He shall say: 'Are you content?' They shall say: 'And why should we not be content, our Lord, when Thou hast given us what Thou hast given no one of creation?' He shall say: 'Shall I not give you better than that?' They shall say: 'O Lord — and what is better than that?' He shall say: 'I shall confer upon you My good-pleasure (riḍwānī), and shall never be wrathful with you thereafter.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6549; Muslim 2829, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

Of the life's least portion, Muslim records that the lowest in rank among the people of Paradise shall have *"the like of the world and ten times the like thereof"* (Muslim 186, from Ibn Masʿūd) — so that the minimum of contentment exceeds the maximum of earthly empire. And of its supreme delight, beyond all furnishings, Muslim records:

> *"When the people of Paradise enter Paradise, God shall say: 'Do you desire anything more?' They shall say: 'Hast Thou not whitened our faces? Hast Thou not admitted us to Paradise and delivered us from the Fire?' Then the Veil shall be lifted, and they shall look upon His Face — and nothing they were given shall be dearer to them than looking upon their Lord."* (Muslim 181, from Ṣuhayb)

*ʿĪshah rāḍiyah*, read beside these reports, expands without losing its centre: the life contenting is the life crowned by the Beatific Vision (*ruʾyat Allāh*), the seeing of the Lord that the Sunnī creed affirms *"as thou seest the full moon, without any obscurity"* (al-Bukhārī 554; Muslim 633). Contentment's final object is not a thing but a Face.

**Contentment Rehearsed: The Discipline of Riḍā in This Life**

If the contenting life is the destination, *riḍā* — contentment with God — is the road, and the verse invites the believer to begin walking it immediately. The early community understood *riḍā bi-qaḍāʾ Allāh* (contentment with God's decree) as the highest station of the soul's journey: above patience (*ṣabr*), which endures the bitter, stands contentment, which tastes it as medicine from a beloved Physician. The Prophet ﷺ modelled the station in his own supplications, as Muslim records:

> *"I am pleased with God as Lord, with Islam as religion, and with Muḥammad as Messenger."* (Muslim, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī — the morning and evening formula of riḍā)

And he defined richness itself as contentment's possession:

> *"Richness is not abundance of goods; richness is the richness of the soul."* (al-Bukhārī 6446; Muslim 1051, from Abū Hurayrah)

The practical translation is direct. The soul that practises contentment now — with its portion of provision, its measure of health, its unanswered prayers, its closed doors — is not merely coping; it is *rehearsing eternity*, acquiring in the theatre of this life the exact disposition that constitutes bliss in the next. Every "al-ḥamdu lillāh" uttered sincerely over a diminished portion is a brick of the contenting life laid in advance. Conversely, the soul that nurses chronic discontent — with God, with destiny, with its lot — is rehearsing the opposite state, for Hell's innermost torment, the exegetes say, is precisely discontent made eternal: wrath received and wrath returned, forever. Verse 7 thus quietly reframes the whole spiritual life: thy task is not to accumulate pleasures but to become *the kind of soul that is pleased* — pleased with God here, that thou mayest be pleased with Him there, where He is pleased with thee.

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:8

> **And as for one whose scales are light,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fork in the Sentence: Grammar as Judgment**

*Wa ammā man khaffat mawāzīnuh* — "And as for one whose scales are light...." The sorting construction opened in verse 6 now completes its second arm, and the symmetry is terrible in its simplicity: *thaqulat* (were heavy) against *khaffat* (were light) — two verbs, two destinies, no third. The Qur'an elsewhere acknowledges intermediate states and merciful nuances — the People of the Heights (*al-Aʿrāf*, 7:46–49) detained between Paradise and Fire, the intercession that raises souls, the mercy that rescues *"whoever has an atom's weight of faith"* — but this sūrah, in its Makkan starkness, presents the fork unsoftened. Its purpose at this hour of the proclamation is not to map every eschatological subtlety but to force the only decision that ultimately matters: heavy or light. The grammarians' term for *ammā* is *ḥarf tafṣīl* — the particle of detailing, of sorting the undifferentiated into kinds — and here the detailing is the Day's own: mankind arrives as one fluttering mass of moths and departs as two companies.

The conjunction *wa* ("and") linking the two sortings carries a sombre equality: the same Scales, the same Weighing, the same Lord — divergent outcomes from a single justice. No one is weighed on a harsher instrument than another; the lightness is in the pans, not in the mechanism. This is the Qur'an's consistent theodicy of the Last Day, stated as statute in the verse already cited:

> *"And We shall set up the just scales for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul shall be wronged in aught; though it be the weight of a grain of mustard-seed, We shall bring it forth — and sufficient are We as reckoners."* (Qur'an 21:47)

The verse now before us is that statute's shadow-side actualised: the mustard-seed is sought, the atoms are fetched — and still the pan floats. Lightness, the sūrah insists by its very parallelism, is never the Scale's failure; it is the life's.

**Khaffat: The Physics and Morality of Lightness**

The verb *khaffa* (khā-fā-fā) means to be light, slight, nimble — and, by extension, to be frivolous, flighty, unserious. Its moral colouring runs deep in the Qur'anic idiom. Pharaoh's people are condemned because he *"made light of them"* (*istakhaffa qawmah* — 43:54), deeming them feather-brained enough to follow him into ruin. The hypocrites' oaths are *"light"* because unbacked by resolve. And the human soul itself, in its unredeemed state, is diagnosed as loving the light and fleeing the weighty: amusement over purpose, whim over principle, the immediate over the ultimate. Verse 8 gathers this whole moral physics into a single eschatological fact: the soul that chose lightness in life — the weightless pleasures, the unconsidered words, the uncommitted allegiances — arrives at the Weighing and finds its life *literally* what it *morally* was: light.

The early exegetes pressed the image with characteristic concreteness. Al-Ṭabarī records the gloss that lightness means fewness of good deeds, or rather the preponderance of evil over good until the pan of righteousness floats; Ibn Kathīr summarises that such a one's good deeds are outweighed by his evil deeds, or that he comes with no good deeds at all that avail him — the latter being the state of those who meet God with persistent, unrepented disbelief, whose deeds the Qur'an elsewhere reduces to mirage and ash:

> *"And as for those who disbelieve, their deeds are as a mirage in a desert, which the thirsty man supposes to be water, until when he comes to it he finds it nothing."* (Qur'an 24:39)

> *"The parable of those who disbelieve in their Lord: their deeds are as ashes upon which the wind blows fiercely on a stormy day."* (Qur'an 14:18)

Mirage and ash: the two substances of eschatological weightlessness — one that deceives at a distance and vanishes at arrival, one that scatters at the first breath of wind. Between them they describe every form of weightless living: the achievements that impress crowds but register nothing with God, the virtues performed for applause that the wind of the Last Day disperses like cinders. The moth of verse 4, fluttering and hollow-boned, was already the emblem of this lightness; verse 8 merely weighs it.

**The Twin Verdicts: Reading 7:9 and 23:103 Beside 101:8**

The Qur'an pronounces the sentence of lightness in three places, and the three verdicts, laid side by side, compose a complete doctrine. Our verse states the condition (*scales are light*) and leaves the consequence to verse 9. The other two state condition and consequence together:

> *"And those whose scales are heavy — they are the successful. And those whose scales are light — they are the ones who have lost their souls, in Hell abiding eternally."* (Qur'an 23:102–103)

> *"And those whose scales are light — they are the ones who have lost their souls, because they wronged Our signs."* (Qur'an 7:9)

Two phrases in these twins demand lingering. First, *"they have lost their souls"* (*alladhīna khasirū anfusahum*): the loss is reflexive — not wealth, not status, but the *self* — and the verb *khasira* is the merchants' verb for a venture that consumes its capital. Every soul, the Qur'an implies, is a capital entrusted for trade in the market of this life; the light-scaled trader returns with the capital itself dissipated. Second, *"because they wronged Our signs"* (7:9): lightness has a cause, and the cause is *ẓulm* — wrongdoing — specifically against God's signs: the verses denied, the messengers mocked, the warnings shelved. The Scales do not lighten arbitrarily; they register a life's settled policy toward revelation. The theologians draw the line the twins imply: no soul is light by accident of birth, incapacity, or unreached ignorance — *"We never punish until We have sent a messenger"* (17:15) — but by the culpable refusal the signs document.

Read together, the three passages also display the Qur'an's graduated rhetoric. Sūrah 7 and 23 pronounce the verdict within extended legal and narrative contexts, surrounded by argument. Sūrah 101 pronounces it in the starkness of the Striker's dawn, with no argument at all — because here the argument *is* the terror, and the terror has already been staged in verses 1–5. The same doctrine wears robes in Madinah and a shroud in Makkah; the wise student learns to recognise it in both wardrobes.

**How Scales Grow Light: The Traditions of Dissipation**

If verse 6's traditions taught how pans grow heavy, the Prophet's ﷺ warnings teach, with equal concreteness, how they grow light — and the mechanisms are disturbingly ordinary. The most comprehensive is the parable of the bankrupt (*al-muflis*), recorded by Muslim from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"Do you know who the bankrupt is?" They said: "The bankrupt among us is he who has neither coin nor goods." He said: "The bankrupt of my community is he who comes on the Day of Resurrection with prayer, fasting, and alms — yet comes having insulted this one, slandered that one, consumed the wealth of this one, shed the blood of that one, and struck that one. So this one is given of his good deeds and that one of his good deeds; and if his good deeds run out before what he owes is settled, there is taken of their sins and cast upon him — then he is cast into the Fire."* (Muslim 2581)

Here is lightness manufactured mid-Weighing: the pan arrives heavy and is *emptied* by creditors — every backbitten neighbour, every cheated partner, every struck subordinate withdrawing his due in the currency of deeds. The commentators call these *"the rights of servants"* (*ḥuqūq al-ʿibād*), and they stress what the parable implies: God may forgive what is owed to Him, but the debts between servants are settled to the atom — *"though it be the weight of a grain of mustard-seed, We shall bring it forth"* (21:47).

A second mechanism is subtler: the dissipation of deeds through ostentation (*riyāʾ*). Muslim records the dreadful procession of the first three judged on the Last Day — the martyr, the scholar-reciter, the philanthropist — each confessing under questioning that his splendid deed was performed *"that it be said"* (*li-yuqāl*) — that it be said he was brave, learned, generous — and hearing the verdict: *"Thou hast lied; thou didst it that it be said — and it was said"* (Muslim 1905, from Abū Hurayrah). Deeds done for human "saying" receive exactly their wage — human saying — and arrive at the Scales as hollow shells. The early ascetics summarised the physics in a maxim: *sincerity gives deeds weight; ostentation gives them wings* — and wings, on the Day of Weighing, are precisely the problem. The moth flies because it is light; the sincere deed sinks because it is heavy. Verse 8 weighs the difference.

**Lightness Diagnosed Early: The Mercy of the Warning**

The verse's terror is real, but its function is merciful — and the distinction matters for every anxious heart. The Qur'an's warnings about light scales are addressed, in this life, exclusively to souls whose scales are still open: every reciter of verse 8 is, by definition, still accumulating, still able to deposit, still capable of the repentance that the Prophet ﷺ promised *"wipes out what came before it"* (Aḥmad; its meaning supported by 25:70 — *"God shall change their evil deeds into good deeds"*). The warning is thus not a sentence but a statement of account — the bank alerting the customer to a draining balance while deposits are still possible. Despair (*yaʾs*) upon hearing it is not piety but a second error, explicitly forbidden:

> *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, despair not of God's mercy; truly God forgives all sins. Truly He is the Forgiving, the Merciful."* (Qur'an 39:53)

The practical counsel the righteous drew from this verse mirrors the evening audit of verse 6, but turned toward repair. Examine the pan's three great leaks: rights owed to servants (debts, slanders, injuries — repayable now in cheap currency, exacted later in deeds); deeds hollowed by ostentation (re-sealable now by renewing intention and hiding the deed); and the slow evaporation of heedlessness (recondensable now by remembrance — *dhikr* — the sūrah's own remedy, since two light words are heavy on the Scale). The believer who patches these leaks need not know his final weight — that knowledge belongs to God alone, and the Prophet ﷺ forbade certain judgment of any soul's end — but he can know his direction: pan sinking or pan floating. Verse 8 asks every soul, nightly, the only question about weight that this life permits: *heavier than yesterday, or lighter?*

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:9

> **an abyss shall be his mother.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's Strangest Sentence**

*Fa-ummuhu hāwiyah* — "then his mother is an abyss." Of all the Qur'an's sentences about the Last Day, few have arrested readers like this one. The word for Hell's pit is yoked, impossibly, to the word for mother — the warmest noun in every language — and the shock of the yoking is the teaching. Where verse 7 enfolded the successful soul *in* a contenting life, verse 9 enfolds the failed soul in an embrace of another order: a motherhood of the Pit. The early exegetes felt the verse's strangeness keenly, and their explanations, preserved by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī, unfold its layers without dulling its edge.

The majority gloss turns on the Arabic root of *umm* (mother): *amma–yaummu*, to betake oneself to, to direct one's course toward, to seek as refuge. The "mother" of a thing, in this idiom, is its resort, its home-base, its returning-place — the encompassing origin to which it belongs. Al-Ṭabarī records from Qatādah and others that *fa-ummuhu hāwiyah* means: his resort, his dwelling, his destination to which he returns is the Abyss. Ibn Kathīr concurs: the Fire shall be his abode and his destination, as the child returns to its mother. The Qur'an speaks elsewhere in exactly this register of dreadful belonging:

> *"Their refuge (maʾwāhum) is Hell — and evil is the resting-place."* (Qur'an 3:197; cf. 3:162; 9:73; 13:18)

But no other verse dares the word *mother*. *Maʾwā* (refuge) is cold architecture; *umm* is warm flesh. The sūrah chooses the warm word for the coldest of facts — and in that choice lies a rhetoric of horror the commentators never tired of unfolding: the lost soul shall be *mothered* by ruin, nursed by fire, held to a breast of flame. What the mother is to the infant — encompassing, inescapable, definitive — the Abyss shall be to him: not a visit but a belonging, not a cell but a womb of torment from which there is no second birth.

**Hāwiyah: Anatomy of an Abyss**

The word *hāwiyah* (from *hawā–yahwī*, to fall, plunge, drop from a height) is the active participle in the feminine form: that which yawns to receive the falling, the Plummeter, the Depth. The Arabs called any deep pit *hāwiyah*, and the Qur'an adopts the word as a proper name of Hell — one of its seven canonical names alongside *Jahannam*, *Saʿīr*, *Saqar*, *Jaḥīm*, *Laẓā*, and *Ḥuṭamah*. Al-Qurṭubī, gathering the reports, notes that *al-Hāwiyah* is said to be the lowest of the Fire's depths, the pit beneath the pits, so called because its dwellers fall (*yahwūna*) into it from above and never reach a bottom. A tradition in Muslim lends the name vertiginous scale: the Prophet ﷺ, hearing a crashing sound, said to his Companions:

> *"Do you know what this is?" They said: "God and His Messenger know best." He said: "This is a stone cast into Hell seventy years ago; it has now reached its bottom."* (Muslim 2844, from Abū Hurayrah)

Seventy years of falling: the Abyss is not a place one enters but a fall one never completes — depth as destiny. Other traditions deepen the topography: Hell has seventy thousand valleys, seventy thousand serpents, seventy thousand scorpions (al-Tirmidhī, with various gradings); its least punishment is the ember-sandals that boil the brain (al-Bukhārī 6561; Muslim 213). But the sūrah, in its Makkan economy, names only the falling and the mothering, trusting two words to convey what volumes elaborate: a depth that receives thee as a mother receives her child — wholly, and forever.

The feminine forms deserve a final grammatical pause: *umm* and *hāwiyah* are both feminine, and the verse reads as an equation of two feminines — Mother = Abyss. The rhetoricians call such an equation *istiʿārah* raised to identity: not "his abode is *like* a mother" but "his mother *is* an abyss." The simile's safety-catch — the *ka* ("like") that cushioned verses 4 and 5 — is removed. Moths and wool were likenesses; this is identification. The soul does not merely resemble one falling; it *belongs* to the falling-place as a child belongs to its mother. Grammar performs damnation's innermost logic: the correspondence between the life lived and the eternity inherited is not analogical but ontological — thou becomest what thou choosest, and thy choosing mothers thy becoming.

**Why a Mother: The Commentators' Four Unfoldings**

The classical exegesis of this astonishing metaphor branches into four complementary unfoldings, each attributed and each deepening the wound. First, the *refuge* reading (Qatādah, al-Ṭabarī's preferred sense): as the child flees to its mother in every fright, so the lost soul, with nowhere else to flee, flees — horribly — into the only embrace left open to it. The image inverts the Day's universal flight (*yawma yafirru al-marʾu min akhīh* — 80:34): men flee *from* every mothered bond, only for the motherless to find one mother waiting — the Pit.

Second, the *destination* reading (Ibn Zayd and others): *umm* as the head, the chief end, the thing toward which one's course was always directed. In this sense the verse quietly accuses: the Abyss did not ambush him; he *aimed* at it, step by step, choice by choice — *"directing his course"* (*yaummu*) toward it through a lifetime of turned shoulders. This matches the Qur'an's persistent doctrine that damnation is self-inflicted direction:

> *"God wrongs them not, but themselves they wrong."* (Qur'an 3:117; cf. 7:177; 9:70; 10:44; 16:33)

Third, the *nursing* reading, favoured by the rhetoricians and the gnostics: as the mother feeds the infant from her own substance, so the Abyss shall "feed" its child from its substance — the *zaqqūm*-tree, the scalding water, the pus — a nursing of torment. The Qur'an's descriptions of Hell's "hospitality" (*nuzul*, 56:56 — "this is their hospitality on the Day of Recompense") drip with the same inverted nurture: a mother's welcome extended by ruin.

Fourth, the *belonging* reading, which modern thematic exegetes emphasise: the mother is the one to whom the child *belongs* by nature, and the verse declares a nature accomplished. A lifetime of preferring appetite to command, whim to worship, the low (*hāwī* — note the shared root: *hawā*, caprice, is what *falls*) to the high, gradually mothers the soul into the Pit's likeness — until the Pit is not its punishment only but its kin. Hell, on this reading, is the outside of an inside: the soul's caprices (*ahwāʾ*) objectified as an Abyss (*hāwiyah*), the fall within completed by the fall without. The root unites them: *hawā* falls, and falls, and finds its mother.

**The Abyss Beside the Contenting Life: The Sūrah's Antithesis**

Verses 7 and 9 are the sūrah's two poles, and their antithesis is constructed with jeweller's precision. The heavy-scaled one is *fī ʿīshatin rāḍiyah* — *in* a life, immersed in contentment; the light-scaled one has *ummuhu hāwiyah* — a mother who is an abyss, immersed in falling. The first dwells within a *life* (the most positive of nouns); the second is dwelt-with by a *falling-place* (the most negative). The first is characterised by *riḍā* (mutual pleasure); the second, by implication, by its opposite — *sakhaṭ* (wrath), the state the Prophet ﷺ described as God's dealings with the damned, who hear, in place of *"I am pleased,"* the sentence of exclusion. The symmetry extends to the verbs: *fa-huwa* (so *he*...) against *fa-ummuhu* (so *his mother*...) — the saved soul stands in its own personhood, named and addressed; the lost soul is grammatically displaced by its Abyss-mother, as though personhood itself had been reabsorbed into ruin.

This antithetical architecture is the Qur'an's standard eschatological diptych — companions of the right against companions of the left (56:8–9; 90:18–19), the garden against the fire, the laughing faces against the dust-covered (80:38–42) — but nowhere is it compressed so fiercely as here: two verses, two eternities, eleven words between the moths and the Fire. The compression serves urgency. The sūrah does not argue the reader into virtue; it *shows* him the two mothers awaiting every soul — the contenting Life that nurses with pleasure, the yawning Abyss that nurses with flame — and leaves him to choose his nursing. Every subsequent moral choice in the reader's life is, by the sūrah's logic, a choice of maternity: each sincere deed crawls toward the first breast; each unrepented evil toward the second.

**Choosing One's Mother While Choice Remains**

The verse's practical sting lies in its tense: *fa-ummuhu hāwiyah* is a nominal sentence, timeless and verdict-like — "his mother *is* an abyss." For the soul still in this life, that timelessness is both warning and reprieve: warning, because trajectories harden — the man long accustomed to aiming at the Pit (*yaummu*) finds his course increasingly difficult to alter, as habits groove into character and character into destiny; reprieve, because the sentence is pronounced over the *end* of a course, and no living soul has reached its end. The door of re-mothering stands open until the soul's last breath — *"God accepts the repentance of the servant so long as the death-rattle has not begun"* (al-Tirmidhī 3537, from Ibn ʿUmar; graded ḥasan) — and the Qur'an's roll of the re-mothered is glorious: sorcerers become martyrs in a morning (7:120–126), a murderer of a hundred is taken by mercy (al-Bukhārī 3470; Muslim 2766), the tormentors of the Prophet's ﷺ own household become his Companions and commanders.

The discipline the verse prescribes is therefore the discipline of direction: audit, regularly, *toward what* thou art aiming (*tayaummum*). The five daily prayers are, among their other mercies, five daily re-aimings — each opening *Allāhu akbar* a turning of the face from every abyss toward the Face that mothers with mercy. The Prophet ﷺ gave the re-aimed soul its charter of hope in the divine words of ḥadīth qudsī:

> *"I am as My servant thinks of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me. If he draws near to Me a handspan, I draw near to him a cubit; if he draws near to Me a cubit, I draw near to him a fathom; and if he comes to Me walking, I come to him running."* (al-Bukhārī 7405; Muslim 2675, from Abū Hurayrah)

The Abyss-mother waits passively, a pit that only receives; the Merciful Lord advances actively, running toward the walker. Verse 9 names the one maternity; the tradition names the other, arms open. Between them every living soul still walks — and may still turn.

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:10

> **And what shall apprise thee of her?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Returns: The Sūrah's Ring Closes**

*Wa mā adrāka mā hiyah* — "And what shall apprise thee of what she is?" The sūrah's second *wa mā adrāka* sounds, and the attentive listener recognises it instantly: it is the question of verse 3, returned. There it asked of the Striker; here it asks of the Abyss — *"her"* (*hiyah*), the feminine pronoun referring to *al-Hāwiyah*. The ring composition (*nazm*) that the introduction announced now stands complete: question–unveiling–division–question–unveiling, the sūrah's two halves hinged on the same awestruck formula. The scholars of the Qur'an's coherence (*munāsabah*), from al-Biqāʿī to al-Farāhī's school, present this verse as Exhibit A of the short sūrahs' architectural perfection: eleven verses, not one of them ornamental, each load-bearing in a circle that begins in mystery and ends in fire.

The return of the question performs several functions at once. First, it *re-humbles*: the listener who has grown almost accustomed to the sūrah's terrors — moths, wool, scales, abyss — is stopped short and told that even now, nine verses in, he does not know. Familiarity, the great anaesthetic, is stripped away; the Abyss is made strange again. Second, it *re-magnifies*: whatever the imagination has constructed of *hāwiyah* from verse 9 falls short, and the verse confesses the shortfall on the imagination's behalf. Third, it *re-suspends*: the sentence begun in verse 8 (*"as for one whose scales are light..."*) and continued in verse 9 (*"his mother is an abyss"*) is now held in mid-air, its final term delayed by a question — so that when verse 11 at last supplies the answer (*"a raging Fire"*), it lands with the force of a twice-postponed blow. Delay, in rhetoric as in music, is emphasis saved and compounded.

Note also the pronoun's delicacy. The verse does not say *"what shall apprise thee of the Abyss?"* but *"what shall apprise thee of her?"* — as though the Abyss, once named, were now too dreadful to name again, and must be gestured at pronominally, the way mourners say "it" of a death or soldiers "it" of a battle. The Arabic rhetoricians call this *iḍmār al-tahwīl* — pronominalisation for horror: the thing is so terrible that the tongue retreats from its noun. Al-Zamakhsharī, with his dramatist's ear, would have us hear the reciter's voice drop on *mā hiyah*: *what — she — is*. Three syllables, and between them an abyss of silence into which the imagination peers and recoils.

**Her: The Feminine That Haunts the Sūrah**

The pronoun *hiyah* ("she/her") gathers into itself one of the sūrah's subtlest threads: the feminine that runs through all eleven verses like a dark seam. *Al-Qāriʿah* is feminine; *mā... mā* frames her; *ʿīshah rāḍiyah* is feminine; *umm* is feminine; *hāwiyah* is feminine; and now *hiyah* seals the chain. The sūrah is, grammatically, a procession of feminines — terror and bliss alike wearing the *tāʾ marbūṭah*. The classical grammarians explain each instance locally (intensive forms, natural gender, verbal nouns), but the literary exegetes, ancient and modern, hear a cumulative music: the Last Day speaking in the voice of encompassing powers — the Striker that enfolds, the Life that embraces, the Mother that receives. Encompassment (*iḥāṭah*) is the sūrah's master-metaphor — moths within terror, souls within lives, the lost within the Abyss — and the feminine forms are its grammatical echo, the language of that which surrounds, contains, and births outcomes.

There is a further precision in *mā hiyah*. The *mā* here, like the *mā* of verse 2, is the *mā* of magnification — but now applied not to a noun but to a pronoun, not to the Abyss announced but to the Abyss *presupposed*. The difference is the difference between meeting a stranger and being asked about an acquaintance: "What shall apprise thee of *her*?" assumes thou hast already met her — in verse 9's dreadful motherhood — and declares thy acquaintance nothing. It is the Qur'an's gentle rebuke to every imagination that supposes, nine verses in, that it has domesticated the subject. Thou hast heard the name; thou hast parsed the metaphor; thou hast shuddered appropriately — and still: *what shall apprise thee of her?* The Unseen does not yield to literacy. The scholar and the shepherd stand equal before this question, and the scholar, knowing more names for his ignorance, perhaps stands lower.

**The Thirteen Apprisings: This Verse in the Qur'anic Series**

Set beside its twelve sister-occurrences, this tenth verse reveals the *mā adrāka* series as a deliberate curriculum of the Unseen. The series apprises of: the Inevitable Reality (69:3), Saqar (74:27), the Day of Separation (77:14), the Day of Recompense — twice, for redoubled emphasis (82:17–18), the two registers Sijjīn and ʿIlliyyūn (83:8, 83:19), the Night-Comer (86:2), the Steep Ascent (90:12), the Night of Decree (97:2), the Striker (101:3), the Abyss (101:10), and the Crusher (104:5). Surveying them together, al-Suyūṭī in *al-Itqān* and the exegetes generally confirm Sufyān ibn ʿUyaynah's rule: every *mā adrāka* is followed by its answer — the Night of Decree *is better than a thousand months*, the Crusher *is God's kindled Fire*, the Night-Comer *is the piercing star* — and here, the Abyss *is a raging Fire*.

But the series teaches a second, subtler lesson: the answers never exhaust the questions. To be told that the Night of Decree outranks a thousand months is to be given a measure, not the Night itself; to be told that the Crusher is kindled Fire is to be given the genus, not the burning. Each *adrāka*-answer is an act of merciful accommodation (*tanzīl* to human capacity), true as far as it goes, inexhaustive by design — the map, never the territory. Verse 10, standing second-to-last in its sūrah and among the last of the series in the muṣḥaf's order, thus carries the series' mature pathos: God apprises, and apprises truly, and still the apprised one stands before the Fire's reality as a child stands before the sea with a thimbleful in his hand. Revelation gives all that can be carried; the rest awaits witnessing — *"No soul knows what is hidden for them of comfort for the eyes"* (32:17), and, by the same token, no soul knows what is hidden of terror.

**Why Ask Twice: The Pedagogy of the Repeated Awe**

Why, a student might ask, does so short a sūrah spend two of its eleven verses — nearly a fifth — on the same unanswered-then-answered question? The classical answer is that repetition in the Qur'an is never redundancy but *taʾkīd* (emphasis) calibrated to importance and *tafṣīl* (distribution) calibrated to structure. The Striker needed its question because it names the Day whole; the Abyss needs hers because she names the losers' eternity — and the two terrors, though nested, are distinct. A soul might tremble at the cosmic Striker yet smuggle a secret exemption for itself ("the Day is terrible, but I shall be fine"); verse 10 closes that loophole by dragging the awe from the cosmos down to the cell, from the Day's grandeur to the loser's *her*. The first question awes; the second *personalises* the awe. Between them no complacency survives: neither "it will not be" (refuted by verses 1–5) nor "it will not be *me*" (refuted by verses 8–11).

Modern teachers of rhetoric would add an observation about *spacing*: the two questions divide the sūrah into the golden rhythm of suspense–release–suspense–release, each release partial, each suspense deeper. The first release (moths and mountains) satisfies the eye but not the conscience; the conscience is then weighed (verses 6–9); the second suspense (verse 10) reopens the wound of not-knowing precisely where knowledge matters most — the Fire's nature; and the final release (verse 11) answers in three words that answer everything and nothing. The sūrah is thus engineered, verse by verse, as an experience of approaching dread — each step closer, each view clearer, each clarity more terrible. To recite it slowly, pausing where the questions pause, is to undergo in eleven verses the education the heedless soul otherwise requires a lifetime — or an eternity — to acquire.

**Standing Before "Her": The Contemplative Practice**

The verse prescribes, by its very form, a contemplative practice the early Muslims knew well: *tawaqquf* — pausing. The Prophet ﷺ, ʿĀʾishah reported, would recite the Qur'an *"pausing at its verses"* (*yuqaṭṭiʿu qirāʾatahu āyatan āyah* — Abū Dāwūd 1466; al-Tirmidhī 2927), giving each verse its own breath and weight; and his night prayers, Ḥudhayfah reported, lingered upon verses of warning with supplication for refuge (*istiʿādhah*) before moving on (Muslim 772). Verse 10 is such a verse: its question mark is a monastery. The believer who pauses upon it — *wa mā adrāka mā hiyah* — and refuses to hurry to verse 11, who lets the pronoun *her* hang in the dark before him, who asks it of himself (*what dost thou know of her, O soul?*) and waits for the honest answer (*nothing, save what my Lord shall tell*), has performed the verse's essential devotion. The answer will come — it is one verse away, it is always one verse away — but the pause is where the profit lies. For in that pause the soul confesses the creed of the warned: *I do not know; I cannot bear; I take refuge.* And the One who asked the question hears the confession — and counts it, on the Scales of verse 6, among the heavy things.

## Sūrah Al-Qāriʿah 101:11

> **It is a raging fire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words That Answer Everything**

*Nārun ḥāmiyah* — "a raging Fire." The sūrah's last verse is its shortest answer: two Arabic words (three in English) unveiling the Abyss of verse 9 and settling the question of verse 10. The grammar is a nominal sentence with an elided subject: *(hiya) nārun ḥāmiyah* — "*She* is a raging Fire" — so that verse 10's pronoun (*mā hiyah*, "what is *she*?") finds its predicate here, and the two verses lock together like question and seal. The indefiniteness (*nārun*, "*a* fire" rather than "*the* fire") is, as the grammarians and al-Rāzī note, the *tanwīn* of magnification: not a fire among fires, but a Fire beyond fires — *"a Fire,"* tremendous beyond description, awful beyond comparison. The Qur'an uses this same indefinite thunder elsewhere for the same blaze:

> *"But he who is given his book behind his back shall call for destruction, and shall roast in a blazing Fire (saʿīran)."*(Qur'an 84:11–12)

> *"And I have warned you of a leaping Fire (nāran talaẓẓā)."*(Qur'an 92:14)

Each indefinite names a Fire too vast for the definite article's familiarity. And with these two words the sūrah ends — no epilogue, no softening postscript, no return to the contenting life. The Striker knocked in verse 1; the Fire rages in verse 11; between them the whole of eschatology has passed in review, and the last sound in the reader's ear is flame.

The placement is itself exegesis. The sūrah could have ended with verse 7's bliss, leaving the soul soothed; instead it ends with verse 11's blaze, leaving the soul *warned*. The classical rhetoricians call this *ḥusn al-maqṭaʿ* — the excellence of the closing cut: a discourse should end where its purpose ends, and this sūrah's purpose is awakening. A lullaby ends softly because its purpose is sleep; a warning ends sharply because its purpose is vigilance. The Makkan denier, who laughed at resurrection, is not crooned but confronted; the last word answers his laughter with a noun that does not laugh. Yet the severity is itself a mercy, as every subsequent section of this commentary must keep insisting: a Fire announced in time is a Fire escapable in time, and the verse that terrifies the living is kinder than the silence that would damn them.

**Ḥāmiyah: The Grammar of Rage**

The adjective *ḥāmiyah* (feminine active participle of *ḥ-m-y*, ḥā–mīm–yā) denotes intense, fierce heat — heat at its raging pitch. The root's range is instructive: *ḥamiya* means to burn fiercely, to glow with heat; *ḥamiyyah* is zeal, the heat of protective honour; *al-ḥimā* is the guarded reserve, defended with heat. Fire's rage and the zealous heart's rage share a root because they share a nature: energy at white intensity, consuming what it touches. The Qur'an applies *ḥāmiyah* to the eschatological Fire here and once more, in the sūrah of the Overwhelmer:

> *"Faces on that Day shall be humbled, labouring, weary — roasting in a scorching Fire (nāran ḥāmiyah), given to drink from a boiling spring."* (Qur'an 88:2–5)

The two occurrences frame the Fire's work identically: faces and mothers, persons engulfed. Other verses add further facets to the same blaze — *al-mūqadah*, the kindled (104:6); *talaẓẓā*, the leaping (92:14); *saʿīr*, the blazing (84:12); *laẓā*, the flame proper (70:15) — each participle a different behaviour of one inferno: it is kindled, it leaps, it blazes, it rages. The exegetes, gathering these names as physicians gather symptoms, describe a single syndrome of insatiable combustion:

> *"On the Day We shall say to Hell: 'Art thou filled?' And it shall say: 'Is there any more?'"* (Qur'an 50:30)

A fire that asks for more fuel after swallowing generations is no earthly fire; it is appetite made element, *ḥāmiyah* perfected — heat that does not exhaust itself by burning but renews itself, *"whose fuel is men and stones"* (2:24; 66:6), burning what earthly fires cannot burn and undiminished by what it burns.

**What the Early Exegetes Said of This Fire**

The source commentary preserves two classical glosses on this verse that a fuller commentary must savour. The first, attributed in the source to al-Sharīf (Sh) among others, holds that the verse means the Fire has reached the greatest heat possible — heat's absolute degree, beyond which intensity cannot climb. The second, attributed to R (al-Rāzī in the source's sigla), holds that when viewed in relation to this Fire, no other fire is truly raging — every earthly flame is, by comparison, a coolness. The two glosses are one doctrine seen from opposite sides: the Fire's heat is maximal in itself, and relativising of all else. Ibn Kathīr, commenting on this verse, gathers the traditions that quantify what the verse leaves qualitative, and al-Qurṭubī adds the theologians' reflection: that God's power to create degrees of torment beyond earthly experience follows necessarily from His power to create degrees of bliss beyond it — *"no soul knows what is hidden for them"* (32:17) cuts both ways.

The quantifying traditions are awesome and authentically anchored. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"Your fire which the children of Adam kindle is one part of seventy parts of the heat of Hell."* They said: "O Messenger of God, it would have sufficed as it is!" He said: *"It is favoured over it by sixty-nine parts, each like its heat."* (al-Bukhārī 3265; Muslim 2843)

One seventieth: the forge that melts iron, the wildfire that outruns horses, the napalm of men's wars — a single seventieth of *ḥāmiyah*. Al-Tirmidhī transmits a further tradition, that the Fire was kindled for a thousand years until it whitened, then a thousand until it reddened, then a thousand until it blackened, *"so it is black and dark"* (al-Tirmidhī 2591, from Abū Hurayrah) — a report whose chain the critics debated, but whose meaning the Qur'an's own *"pitch-black"* shadows of smoke (56:43) support. And of the Fire's least touch, the Two Ṣaḥīḥs agree:

> *"The lightest of the people of Hell in punishment on the Day of Resurrection is a man under the soles of whose feet two embers are placed, by which his brain boils."* (al-Bukhārī 6561; Muslim 213, from al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr)

If the minimum is boiling brains from an ember's proximity, the maximum — the *ḥāmiyah* in full rage, the lot of those the sūrah calls light — staggers every attempt at imagination. Which is exactly why verse 10 asked, and verse 11 answered with two words and silence.

**The Fire in the Prophet's ﷺ Warnings**

No one warned of this Fire more vividly, more tenderly, or more persistently than the Prophet ﷺ, and his authenticated warnings are this verse's living commentary. Of the Fire's own sentience before its Lord, al-Bukhārī and Muslim record:

> *"The Fire complained to its Lord, saying: 'My Lord, parts of me have consumed other parts.' So He permitted it two breaths — a breath in winter and a breath in summer. That is the fiercest heat you experience and the fiercest cold."* (al-Bukhārī 3260; Muslim 617, from Abū Hurayrah)

The summer heatwave and the winter freeze — the two breaths of *ḥāmiyah*, exhaled across the seasons as reminders. Of the road that leads to it, Muslim records the Prophet's ﷺ most quoted topography of temptation:

> *"Paradise is surrounded by hardships, and the Fire is surrounded by desires."* (Muslim 2822, from Anas ibn Mālik; cf. al-Bukhārī 6487)

Every *shahwah* indulged against God's command is a step through the Fire's surrounding hedge; every *makrah* endured for His sake — dawn prayer in winter, honesty at a cost, chastity in a flood — is a step through Paradise's. And of the strangeness of men's response to both destinations, al-Tirmidhī records his wondering rebuke:

> *"I have seen nothing like the Fire, whose flee-er sleeps; nor anything like Paradise, whose seeker sleeps."* (al-Tirmidhī 2601, from Abū Hurayrah; graded ḥasan)

The flee-er sleeps: men convinced of *ḥāmiyah* who slumber through the flight. The seeker sleeps: men convinced of *ʿīshah rāḍiyah* who slumber through the seeking. The sūrah's final verse is the alarm bell for both sleepers — and its ring, *"a raging Fire,"* is calibrated to penetrate exactly the slumber the Prophet ﷺ marvelled at.

**Why the Sūrah Ends Here: Severity as Mercy**

The mind that has travelled all eleven verses may now ask the closing question: why should a sūrah that opened *In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful* — for the basmalah crowns this sūrah as it crowns all but one — end upon unrelieved flame? The classical commentators answer on three levels, each a facet of one wisdom. First, the level of *audience*: the sūrah addressed deniers who mocked the Resurrection, and mockery is not cured by comfort but by confrontation; the Fire's finality answers laughter's finality. The Qur'an elsewhere states the pastoral principle explicitly — *"warn by it those who fear to be gathered to their Lord"* (6:51) — and warning requires the warned-thing present, vivid, and last.

Second, the level of *memory*: last words linger. The reciter who closes the muṣḥaf upon *nārun ḥāmiyah* carries the blaze into his day; the worshipper who bows after it carries it into his prostration; the child who memorises it carries it into his dreams. Had the sūrah ended with bliss, the soul might have floated out of it soothed and unchanged; ending with flame, it dispatches the soul *commissioned* — to weigh itself, to lighten its sins, to heavy its scales before the Weighing. The severity is pharmacological: bitter last mouthfuls are remembered, and remembered medicine is taken.

Third, and deepest, the level of *hope's architecture*: the Fire-last sūrah is framed, in the believer's complete recitation, by mercy on both sides — the basmalah's *Compassionate, Merciful* before it, and the Qur'an's ocean of promises after it. The very next sūrahs sing of rivalry's exposure (102), of the age's loss except for believers (103), of the Elephant's Lord who feeds against hunger and secures against fear (105–106). The intelligent reader holds verse 11 stereoscopically with 39:53 — *"despair not of God's mercy; truly God forgives all sins"* — and with the Scales' own ten-for-one arithmetic, and understands: the Fire is last in the sūrah so that fleeing it may be first in life. Ibn Taymiyyah's tradition insists on this binocularity as orthodoxy itself: fear (*khawf*) and hope (*rajāʾ*) are the believer's two wings, and the sūrah that clips neither — terror in verses 1–5 and 8–11, bliss blazing at verse 7's centre like a lamp in a dark corridor — is the complete flying-lesson. The moth need not end in flame; the wool-mountains need not be its only future; the Scales stand ready; the contenting Life waits; and the Fire rages — announced, described, and escapable — only until the soul turns. That turning is the sūrah's unwritten twelfth verse, and every reciter is its author.
