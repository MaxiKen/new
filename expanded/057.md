# Sūrah al-Ḥadīd (Chapter 57) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Named for a Metal**

Sūrah al-Ḥadīd, "Iron," takes its name from a single striking phrase near its center: *And We sent down iron, wherein are great might and benefits for mankind* (v. 25). It is characteristic of the Qur'an's chapter names that they rarely summarize the whole chapter; rather, they lift out one distinctive word or image that occurs nowhere else, or nowhere so powerfully. No other verse of the Qur'an names iron, and none frames it the way this sūrah does — as a substance "sent down" by God, standing at the crossroads of civilization's two powers: the *might* by which justice is defended and the *benefits* by which life is built. In a chapter consumed with the contrast between outward appearance and inward reality — believers whose hidden light will blaze on the Day of Judgment, hypocrites whose outward profession conceals hollow hearts, a world that glitters on its surface and withers beneath — iron is the fitting emblem: outwardly hard, inwardly destined either to become a sword of justice or a ploughshare of provision, depending on the hand that shapes it. The heart of man, the sūrah will say in v. 16, can grow hard like this metal; but iron's hardness is precisely what makes it useful when it is mastered, and a heart mastered by the remembrance of God becomes an instrument of the Balance.

**Makkan or Madinan: The Debate and What It Teaches**

The scholars of revelation's chronology have differed over whether al-Ḥadīd is Makkan or Madinan. Some count it Makkan, but the majority — including the authorities behind the great commentaries of al-Ālūsī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Shaʿrānī — class it as Madinan, and its internal evidence largely supports them: it addresses a standing community of believers with laws and counsels about spending, it speaks of "the victory" (v. 10) in connection with the conquest of Makkah, and it confronts the hypocrites, a constituency that only became politically significant in Madinah, where Islam had power enough to make lip-service profitable. The sixth/twelfth-century Andalusian commentator Ibn ʿAṭiyyah offered a wise reconciliation: the sūrah has Madinan verses, but its middle portion — the lyrical passage on hearts softening and the world withering — carries the tone and compactness of Makkan revelation. The layered composition, if that is what it is, only deepens the chapter's coherence: the expansive theology of its opening belongs to the universal address of the Makkan preaching, while its social exhortations belong to the Madinan community's daily ledger. Either way, the reader should notice that the sūrah unites what later ages so often divide: pure contemplation of God's attributes and the very un-contemplative matter of what one does with one's money.

**First of the Glorifiers**

Al-Ḥadīd stands first in rank of the five sūrahs known in the tradition as *al-Musabbiḥāt*, "the Glorifiers" — the chapters that open with a declaration that all creation glorifies God: al-Ḥadīd (57), al-Ḥashr (59), al-Ṣaff (61), al-Jumuʿah (62), and al-Taghābun (64). Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Shaʿrānī record the report transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ concerning this company:

> *"Within [the Glorifiers] there is a verse that is better than a thousand verses."*

The commentators candidly note that the chain of this report is not free of weakness — al-Ḥusayn ibn al-Faḍl's route of transmission has been questioned — but the scholars discussed its meaning seriously, and two candidates were proposed for the verse in question: some said it is verse 3 of this very sūrah — *He is the First, and the Last, and the Outward, and the Inward; and He is Knower of all things* — while others located it at the end of Sūrah al-Ḥashr, among the cascade of Divine Names with which that chapter closes. The dispute itself is instructive: in both candidates the "better than a thousand" verse is a constellation of God's Names, which tells us how the tradition weighed value — a single verse that gathers the attributes of the Divine outweighs a thousand verses of other matter, because everything in religion is finally ordered to knowing God. Al-Ḥadīd opens its doors, then, with doxology: before a single command is given, the whole universe is shown already praying.

**The Movement of the Sūrah**

The chapter unfolds in five great movements, and the commentators have traced them with general agreement. The six opening verses lay the foundation of everything else: God's all-embracing glorification by creation, His absolute sovereignty, His four great Names of comprehending reality — First, Last, Outward, Inward — His knowledge that penetrates the earth and the breast alike, and His "with-ness" (*maʿiyyah*) toward every creature wherever it is. The second movement (vv. 7–11) builds directly upon the first: *because* all sovereignty is His, believe in Him; *because* all wealth reverts to Him, spend of what He has merely entrusted to you. The third movement (vv. 12–15) swings the reader onto the plain of Judgment, where the light the believers carried hidden in their chests runs visibly before them while the hypocrites beg to borrow a light that cannot be lent, and a wall with a single gate descends between mercy and punishment. The fourth and longest movement (vv. 16–24) is the sūrah's spiritual heart, beginning with one of the most searching questions in the whole Book — *Has not the time come for those who believe for their hearts to be humbled to the remembrance of God?* — then diagnosing the hardening of hearts that overtook earlier communities, reviving hope with the image of earth resurrected after death, and closing with the celebrated anatomy of worldliness (v. 20) and the twin therapies of divine decree for both despair and boasting (vv. 22–23). The final movement (vv. 25–29) widens the lens to sacred history: God sent the messengers with the Book and the Balance so that justice might stand, sent down iron with its might and its benefits, carried prophethood through the progeny of Noah and Abraham, and sent Jesus son of Mary with the Gospel — and then addresses the People of the Book directly, inviting them to the double reward of believing in their own prophets and in the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, ending on the great word that rings like a bell through the chapter's close: the Bounty belongs to God, and He gives it unto whomsoever He will.

**The Chapter's Signature Themes**

Several threads run under the whole fabric and will surface again and again in the verse-by-verse commentary. First, *the outward and the inward*: the sūrah is positively structured around the theological tension announced in v. 3 — God as the Outward who fills every horizon and the Inward who hides behind every veil — and then replayed morally in the hypocrites whose outward and inward do not match, in the believers whose inward faith becomes outward light, in charity which must leave the hand to be real yet remain hidden from men's eyes to be pure, and in the world itself, outwardly sweet, inwardly perishing. Second, *light*: the word or its imagery recurs across vv. 9, 12, 13, 19, and 28, gathering into a single theology of light — revelation bringing man out of darkness, faith stored as light, light running before the saved across the traverse over Hell, light denied to the outwardly clever, light doubled for the People of the Book who complete their belief. Third, *wealth as trust*: no sūrah argues the stewardship of property more economically — man is a trustee (v. 7), the heavens and the earth have God as their ultimate heir (v. 10), spending is framed as lending to God (v. 11, v. 18), and miserliness as a self-mutilation, since God is the Self-Sufficient who loses nothing by a man's closed fist (v. 24). Fourth, *the heart's chronology*: hearts have a life-cycle in this sūrah — soft by nature, hardening over time (v. 16), revivable like dead earth (v. 17) — and the sūrah's most pressing question is about timing: has not the time come? And fifth, *divine bounty over human ledger-keeping*: from the promise of multiplication (vv. 11, 18) to the vastness of a garden as wide as everything (v. 21) to the doubled portion (v. 28) to the final declaration that bounty is God's to give (v. 29), the sūrah steadily dismantles the arithmetic by which men keep score against God.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the twenty-nine verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse order: each verse is quoted individually from the sūrah's translation, then expanded through its Arabic vocabulary and roots, the classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, the Jalālayn, Ibn ʿAjībah, al-Ālūsī, and others — authentic Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the occasions of revelation where they are recorded, the logical and psychological anatomy of each teaching, and its bearing upon the life of a believer today. Nothing is compressed on account of the sūrah's unity of theme; on the contrary, the chapter's coherence means each verse commentary can be read alone, while together they trace the complete arc — from the glorification of all things, through the softening of one human heart, to the gate of the Garden opened by a light that must be earned while it can still be earned.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:1

> **Whatsoever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies God, and He is the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Whatsoever" Rather Than "Whosoever": The Grammar of All-Embracing Praise**

The verse opens with *subbaḥa* as its engine, but its first lesson is in the pronoun: God says *mā* — *whatsoever* — and not *man* — *whosoever*. In Arabic usage, *man* is the relative pronoun for persons, for beings endowed with rational choice; *mā* covers everything else — the animate without reason, the inanimate, the abstract, the unknown. Al-Jalālayn, the joint commentary of Jalāl al-Dīn al-Maḥallī and Jalāl al-Dīn al-Suyūṭī, explains the choice in a single luminous stroke: *mā* is used, though the verse's address will soon turn to men, because the glorification of God by the non-rational creation so predominates in quantity and constancy over the praise of rational beings that the whole chorus is best named by the larger pronoun. Consider what that implies. The rational worshippers of the earth — every prayer mat occupied at dawn, every tongue reciting — are a minority within creation's worship. For every human voice that rises in dawn prayer, billions of creatures at that same moment are glorifying without pause and without lapse of attention: the migrating bird that never misses its way, the leaf whose chemistry turns light to bread, the galaxy holding its silent circuit. *Whatsoever* gathers them all and places man's praise where it belongs — inside an ocean of worship that never began with him and does not depend on his participation.

**The Root of Tasbīḥ: Declaring God Beyond**

The verb *sabbaḥa* carries a history in its root, s-b-ḥ. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nīsābūrī, and Ibn ʿAjībah all trace the connection the lexicographers preserve: *sabaḥa* in its primary meaning is to swim, to move swiftly through a medium, to go far out beyond the near shore — the same root gives *sabḥ*, swimming, and *musbiḥ*, the far-ranging horse. From this, *tasbīḥ* came to mean the act of declaring God to be far beyond — beyond defect, beyond resemblance to the created, beyond every limit the imagination draws around Him. When the believer says *subḥān Allāh*, he is not merely saying "glory be to God" as a cheer; he is exercising a kind of intellectual hygiene, pushing back every encroaching comparison: God is beyond fatigue, beyond need, beyond oblivion, beyond injustice, beyond dying. It is worth pausing on how intelligent this arrangement is. The human mind cannot conceive God directly as He is — *Sight comprehends Him not* (6:103) — but it can do something almost as powerful: it can systematically negate every deficiency from Him. Tasbīḥ is the discipline of transcendence compressed into a single word, which is why the tradition made it the heartbeat of prayer — in bowing and prostration, *subḥāna rabbī* — and the staple of remembrance outside it.

Ibn ʿAjībah and others note a further grammatical subtlety: the verse says *sabbaḥa lillāh*, with the particle *lām* — "for God," "to God." The *lām* can simply mark the object of glorification, but it adds a fragrance the commentators did not want lost: creation glorifies *for His sake*, in pure orientation toward Him. No rock seeks an audience; no star performs. This stands as a silent rebuke prepared for the hypocrites who appear in vv. 13–15, whose religion was exactly the opposite — entirely for the audience, nothing for the Sake. The particle teaches what purity of worship is before the sūrah ever names its opposite.

**Praise by Tongue and Praise by State of Being**

How does a stone glorify? The classical commentators answer with a distinction that unlocks the verse: creatures glorify God either by actual speech (*lisān al-maqāl*), as angels and believing men and jinn do, or by the speech of their state (*lisān al-ḥāl*) — every created thing, by the very way it exists, proclaims truths about its Maker. The regularity of the heavens declares His order; the intricacy of the cell declares His knowledge; the provision that reaches every nest and burrow declares His sustaining care; the seamless fit of each thing to its purpose declares His wisdom. The creation is not silent decoration; it is articulate evidence. This is why contemplation of the world counts in Islam as worship rather than distraction from it — the world is not a rival text to the Book but its visible twin, and God says elsewhere not merely that things praise Him but that their praise is a language real enough to be incomprehensible only to us:

> *"The seven heavens and the earth and all that is in them glorify Him. There is no thing, save that it hymns His praise, though you do not understand their praise. Truly He is Clement, Forgiving."* (Qur'an 17:44)

Two mercies hide in that verse's ending. First, the deafness is ours — the static of the senses and the noise of regard for ourselves — not a silence in the world; the believer striving to hear creation's praise is retuning a receiver, not imagining a sound. Second, the fit of the Names *Clement, Forgiving* at the close of a verse about universal praise: He is clement toward the very creatures too distracted to hear the chorus, and forgiving toward those who fail to join it. The commentators noted also the grammatical spread of the root across the Qur'an — past tense here (*sabbaḥa*), present elsewhere (*yusabbiḥu*), the imperative (*sabbiḥ*), the verbal noun (*subḥān*) — as though the Book were conjugating time itself around the act: glorification was, is, commanded, and eternal.

**The Universe as a Mosque, and Man as Its Imam**

The image that flows from the verse — all the heavens and earth in worship — is not ornamental cosmology; it is a summons posted on the sky. The Prophet ﷺ described the whole earth as a place of prayer for this community (*juʿilat lī al-arḍu masjidan wa ṭahūran*, al-Bukhārī 335, Muslim 521), and the first verse of al-Ḥadīd reveals why that grant fits: the earth was already a mosque before the first man prayed on it. Man's worship is therefore never a lonely innovation but an act of joining — the way a solitary voice slipping into a great choir changes nothing about the song and everything about the singer. Within this lies a profound anthropology. The animals worship by unfailing instinct; they cannot do otherwise, and so their glorification, though real, carries no choice. Man and jinn alone can refuse — can stand inside the universal mosque and close their mouths. And precisely because refusal is possible for him, man's *yes* has a weight that no mountain's obedience can match: he is the only creature whose tasbīḥ is also a moral victory. The entire drama of the Qur'an's call to man lives in that difference.

**"The Mighty, the Wise": Why These Names Seal the Verse**

Every Qur'anic verse that closes with Divine Names chooses them with exactness, and this is the first of many such seals in the sūrah (it ends, significantly, with the near-pair *Strong, Mighty* at v. 25). Al-ʿAzīz, the Mighty, is the One whom nothing can overpower, thwart, or escape; al-Ḥakīm, the Wise, is the One whose every act proceeds from perfect knowledge and purpose. Joined to this first verse, the pair answers the two questions a thinking reader might raise against universal glorification. If all things glorify God, why is the world full of disorder and rebellion? *The Mighty*: the rebels glorify Him despite themselves — their very existence, sustenance, and final destination are in His hand, and their defiance cannot subtract an atom from His kingdom. And why does the worshipping universe contain suffering, catastrophe, decay? *The Wise*: the chorus is not naive; the wisdom that arranged it comprehends what the singers do not. Might without wisdom would be tyranny; wisdom without might would be beautiful impotence. The seal at the end of the sūrah's first verse guarantees both — and thereby teaches the reader the pattern for all thirty of the chapter's signatures: every closing pair of Names is a key cut precisely for the lock of the verse above it.

**Doxology as the Doorway of Religion**

A religion that began this sūrah with a command would have put duty first; a religion beginning with a threat would have put fear first. It begins with the universe praying. The order matters spiritually. Before man is asked to believe (v. 7), to spend (v. 7), to soften his heart (v. 16), to race (v. 21), he is shown that worship is not an artificial burden bolted onto his nature but the native activity of absolutely everything that exists — the default state of reality, interrupted only in the chest of the rational rebel. The worshipper at dawn is not doing something strange; the man who sleeps through the dawn in a cosmos bowing around him is the anomaly, a single flat note in a tuned creation. The modern reader, trained to picture the universe as dead machinery indifferent to meaning, receives here the opposite vision: a universe saturated with orientation toward its Maker, in which the scientific description of things and the doxological description of things are not rivals but two registers of one speech — the physicist measures the orbit, the verse tells him what the orbit is *doing*. And so the chapter named for iron, the hardest common thing men handle, opens with the most immaterial activity there is: praise rising from everything, to the Mighty, the Wise.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:2

> **Unto Him belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth. He gives life and causes death, and He is Powerful over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Universal Praise to Universal Ownership**

The first verse showed everything in the heavens and the earth engaged in God's glorification; the second verse states the ground of that worship: *lahu mulku al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ* — unto Him belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth. The word *mulk* is stronger than the English "ownership." It denotes kingship, dominion, the authority to dispose absolutely of what is possessed — the whole apparatus of rule, not merely the legal fact of title. The Qur'an repeats this declaration across its pages in a drumbeat the commentators could list from memory (cf. 2:107; 3:189; 5:17–18, 40, 120; 7:158; 9:116; 24:42; 25:2), and the repetition is itself the lesson: human beings are chronically prone to act as though sovereignty were distributed — a little to the state, a little to the market, a little to the self — while the Book insists that all of it, from the rotation of galaxies to the next heartbeat of a sparrow, is one undivided dominion. Verse 1 described creation's activity; verse 2 announces creation's status. Together they erect the frame for the entire sūrah: because the sovereignty is His, the wealth you hold is a trust (v. 7), your life is on loan (v. 20), your record is with Him (v. 22), and your end is a return to Him (v. 5).

**The Definition of True Sovereignty**

It is worth thinking logically about what complete sovereignty would have to include. A king owns his land but not the breathing of his subjects; a wealthy man owns his wealth but not the market that prices it; a scientist may describe nature's laws but cannot alter one of them by decree. Human sovereignty is always partial, delegated, and hemmed in by what the sovereign did not make and cannot control — which is to say, it is not sovereignty in the true sense at all, only administration. The verse corrects our political intuitions with a metaphysical fact: sovereignty in its true sense belongs only to the One who is indebted to nothing outside Himself, who created the very constituents of His kingdom and sustains every atom of it at every instant. Al-Rāzī and the theologians develop from this the doctrine men find so hard to hold steadily: there is no true rival power anywhere in existence — not fortune, not fate, not nature, not the self. Everything other than God is a subject, including the things that seem most to rule us. When the believer recites this verse he is performing an act of intellectual liberation, stripping false sovereignties of their crowns: fear of men shrinks when their dominion is seen to be borrowed; anxiety over provision loosens when the Storehouse is seen to have one Keeper.

**Life and Death: The Two Movements No One Else Can Perform**

The verse then narrows from the totality of dominion to its most intimate proof: *yuḥyī wa-yumīt* — He gives life and causes death. The class of things only God can do is small, and these two stand at its head. No human hand has ever assembled a living cell from dead matter, though laboratories now hold every ingredient; no human power, when the appointed moment arrives, has ever held a departing soul one minute past its term. The Qur'an presses this signature of God across its text (cf. 2:258; 3:156; 7:158; 9:116; 10:56; 22:6; 23:80; 40:68; 44:8; 53:44), and it does so because life and death are the two boundaries of every human plan. All our building happens between them; both of them are exclusively His. There is a decisive consequence for how a believer holds his days. If my giving of life is not in my hand, my existence is a gift I did not negotiate; if my death is not in my hand, my end is an appointment I did not schedule. Gratitude on one side, readiness on the other — and between these two the whole of piety stretches like a road between its two gates.

The commentators note that the order matters: giving life is mentioned before causing death, mercy before termination — the pattern of a Lord who creates before He reclaims. Some see in the pairing also the two resurrections and the two deaths: He quickened us when we were nothing, He will lay us down at the term, and He will raise us againfor the Reckoning — as He says: *How can you disbelieve in God, when you were dead and He gave you life; then He causes you to die; then He gives you life; then unto Him you shall be returned* (2:28). The sūrah will itself pick the thread up at v. 17 — *God revives the earth after its death* — turning the doctrine outward to the fields and inward to the heart.

**Al-Muḥyī and al-Mumīt: Names the Qur'an Builds Without Naming**

The traditional lists of God's Ninety-Nine Names include the Giver of Life (al-Muḥyī) and the Causer of Death (al-Mumīt), though neither nominal form occurs in the Qur'an in precisely these shapes. This raises a quiet question the scholars answered carefully: on what authority are Names assigned to God, when His Names are a matter of revelation, not invention (*tawqīf*)? The answer: the many verses that predicate the acts of giving life and causing death of God — including this one — serve as the revealed source from which the Names are derived, exactly as the verbal statement "God creates all things" grounds the Name the Creator. The principle preserves both reverence and reason: we may only name God as He has spoken Himself, but when His speech consistently ascribes an act to Him, the corresponding Name is no speculation — it is listening closely. Behind the technicality lies something pastoral: a believer who learns to call on God as al-Muḥyī in the spring of his fortunes and to know Him as al-Mumīt at the graveside has located, inside the two Names, the entire emotional geography of being a creature.

**"Powerful Over All Things": The Seal That Releases Fear**

The verse closes *wa-huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr* — He is Powerful over all things. This formula appears dozens of times in the Qur'an because it is the premise of every other consolation: a promise is only as good as the power behind it. God will promise the spenders a great reward (v. 7) and a multiplied loan (v. 11); He will threaten a wall and a Fire (vv. 13–15); He will announce a garden as wide as all existence (v. 21). Every one of those claims rides on this one. The seal is also the quiet death of fatalistic despair: whatever a man faces — a diagnosis, a debt, an enemy, a suffocating circumstance — he can hold one fixed point: the Lord of the heavens and the earth is not obstructed by his obstacle. *Over all things* includes the thing in front of me. And the seal disciplines hope as much as fear: no fortune I build exempts me from the two movements named in the middle of the verse, for the Powerful over all things gives life and causes death, and my wealth, my plans, and my arguments are not among the powers that can overrule Him.

**The Creed Compressed Into One Verse**

Stepping back, v. 2 contains a complete miniature of creed: the sovereignty of God (His uniqueness as Lord), the giving of life (creation and the world's dependence), the causing of death (mortality and accountability), and omnipotence (the certainty that His decrees stand). It is no accident that the sūrah plants this creed in its second breath, because everything the chapter goes on to demand — belief, spending, softening of hearts, racing to forgiveness — rests upon it. A man who believes all sovereignty is God's will find the reasoning of v. 7 irresistible: spend from what was never yours, held only in trust from its Owner. A man who believes God alone gives life and death will find the reasoning of v. 20 plain: do not stake your heart on what perishes. The chapter is built as a logical edifice, and this verse is part of its load-bearing wall — the cheerful, terrible, stabilizing truth that the whole estate, and the breath of every tenant in it, belongs to One.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:3

> **He is the First, and the Last, and the Outward, and the Inward; and He is Knower of all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Said to Outweigh a Thousand**

This is the verse that many scholars identified as the candidate for the report about the Glorifier sūrahs — that within them lies a verse better than a thousand verses; some, as Ibn Kathīr and al-Shaʿrānī record, fixed upon this very line, while others gave the honor to the close of al-Ḥashr. Whether or not the report's chain bears the weight placed on it, the instinct that pointed here is sound to the bone: in ten Arabic words the verse gathers the totality of the Divine relation to everything that exists — before everything, after everything, above and beyond everything, beneath and within everything, and knowing everything. There is no empty space in the formula. Try to locate any thing, any moment, any thought outside its sweep and the exercise fails: what precedes time is His precedence, what follows extinction is His remaining, what fills the horizons is His outwardness, what hides behind the veil is His inwardness, and what ticks in the secret of the atoms and the conscience is His knowledge. The early Muslims felt the comprehensiveness physically; the Prophet ﷺ built one of his most famous supplications upon all four of these Names together, as we shall see — which suggests he heard this verse not as metaphysics filed in a book but as a handhold for the drowning.

**The Four Names, One at a Time**

*The First* (al-Awwal): God is the First not in the way a first line-item precedes a second, but in the way Being precedes all beings — before anything was, and there was nothing other than He. Everything else that claims priority — the first cause of an argument, the first principle of a philosophy — arrives late to a hall where He was already present. *The Last* (al-Ākhir): when the whole series of created things is exhausted and every flame of contingent existence gutters out, He remains — as the Book says, *All upon it passes away. And there remains the Face of thy Lord, Possessed of Majesty and Bounty* (55:26–27). *The Outward* (al-Ẓāhir): God is manifest over all things — not that He is a thing among things, spreading across their surfaces, but that every trace, sign, and ordered splendor of the world points upward to Him so insistently that, as the Book puts it, *Wheresoever you turn, there is the Face of God* (2:115); His signs so dominate outward reality that a mind awake cannot walk ten steps without tripping on testimony of Him. *The Inward* (al-Bāṭin): and yet He is nearer than everything — hidden within, veiled by the very splendor that declares Him, beyond the reach of eyes even at their sharpest: *Sight comprehends Him not, but He comprehends all sight* (6:103); and nearer to man than his own lifeline: *We are nearer to him than his jugular vein* (50:16).

Al-Zamakhsharī offered one of the most instructive grammatical observations on the verse: the four Names should not be read as a flat list but as two welded pairs — "He is the First and the Last" (governing time), "and He is the Outward and the Inward" (governing being). At every moment — past, present, future — He is manifestly the First and the Last, and at every point of every thing He is outwardly declared and inwardly concealed. Other commentators, as al-Zamakhsharī also records, read the pair spatially: the Outward as the One above all things, predominating over them; the Inward as the One within all things, knowing their hidden dimensions. The two readings converge: whether one walks outward into time or inward into the soul, there is no exit from Him. The mystics of the tradition, Ibn ʿAjībah among them, saw in *the Outward* the deepest doctrine of all — that everything which exists is in some manner a Self-Disclosure of God, a theophany; not because things are divine, but because every existent is a word spoken by the Speaker, and speech discloses the Speaker. The sober among the scholars always held this reading on a tight leash — the creation declares the Creator without being confused with Him — and the verse itself supplies the leash: *He is Knower of all things*; the disclosure never ceases to be known, owned, and transcended by the One it declares.

**The Prophet's Prayer in the Language of This Verse**

The most beloved commentary on this verse is not a book but a supplication. Muslim records in his Ṣaḥīḥ (no. 2713) that Abū Hurayrah used to spend the night with the Prophet ﷺ, and that the Prophet taught him this prayer, whose central section runs:

> *"O God, Lord of the seven heavens and Lord of the Magnificent Throne! Our Lord, and Lord of everything, Revealer of the Torah, the Gospel, and the Criterion, Splitter of the grain of corn and the date stone! I seek refuge with Thee from the evil of everything over whose forehead Thou hast control. O God! Thou art the First, for nothing is before Thee; the Last, for nothing is after Thee; the Outward, for nothing is above Thee; and the Inward, for nothing is below Thee. Remove the burden of debt from us and free us from poverty."* (Muslim 2713)

Every detail rewards attention. The four Names are each glossed by a negation: nothing before Thee, after Thee, above Thee, below Thee — the mind cannot compass the Names directly, so the prayer defines them by emptying every rival position, exactly as the tasbīḥ of v. 1 worked by negation. And then, after ascending through the highest metaphysics the language can hold, the supplication lands without transition on the two most commonplace miseries of human life: debt and poverty. The Prophet ﷺ understood what the verse is *for*. A man crushed under a debt he cannot pay has lost his future — the Last, who remains when all else passes, is his hope; a man in poverty has lost his means — the First, who needed nothing and gave everything, is his supplier; a man cornered by powers visibly greater than himself — the Outward, above Whom nothing stands, outranks them; a man whose distress is so private no creature perceives it — the Inward, beneath Whom nothing hides, has seen it already. The loftiest verse of theology becomes, in the Prophet's usage, the precise medicine of the indebted night-worker. That is the sūrah's own method: the highest Names of vv. 1–6 will end, in vv. 7 and 11, in the prosaic act of reaching into one's purse.

**The Cure for the Two Chronic Diseases of Religion**

The pair *Outward* and *Inward* functions theologically as a double cure against the two errors between which the religious imagination perpetually swings. One error is crude immanence — God collapsed into the world, worshipped as an idol, a force, a tribe's mascot, a projection of human wishes. Against this stands *the Outward* in His transcendence: nothing is above Him, nothing shares His rank, nothing can be placed beside Him. The opposite error is despairing transcendence — God so remote that He neither knows nor cares, a distant architect who wound the clock and withdrew. Against this stands *the Inward*: nearer than the jugular vein, intimate with every secret, *Knower of all things*. Held one at a time, each half-truth deforms religion; held together as the verse holds them, they produce the distinctive balance of Islam — a God infinitely exalted above human resemblance yet closer to each creature than that creature is to himself. No image survives; no distance estranges. Ibn Taymiyyah would later formulate the principle for the whole Sunni creed in this vein: the proof of God's lordship is that He is exalted above the creation in essence and yet with it in knowledge, power, and care — the so-called combining of the evidence of transcendence and the evidence of nearness, which he traced to precisely these verses.

**Living Inside the Four Names**

What does this verse do to a person who believes it? Psychologically, it abolishes the experience of being truly alone — the Inward is with him in every solitary hour, the Knower of all things registering what no witness saw: the hidden kindness, the swallowed insult, the quiet prayer. It reorders grief: whatever he loses was preceded by the First and will be outlasted by the Last, so no created loss is ultimate. It tames ambition: the Outward is above him, so no summit of status is the summit. And it steadies moral life in crowds: since the Inward knows him, no performance for the crowd can pad his account, and no failure of the crowd to notice can empty it. The verse plants, in four strokes, the *iḥsān* the Prophet ﷺ defined in the hadith of Gabriel — *to worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, He sees you* (al-Bukhārī 50, Muslim 8). The one who internalizes that He is the First surrenders his origins; the Last, his end; the Outward, his surroundings; the Inward, his secrets; and the Knower of all things, his deeds. Nothing is left unheld — and that, the sūrah will argue next verse, is not a sentence of surveillance but the shape of perfect safety.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:4

> **He it is Who created the heavens and the earth in six days. Then He mounted the Throne. He knows that which enters the earth and that which issues therefrom, that which descends from Heaven and that which ascends thereto. He is with you wheresoever you are, and God sees whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Six Days and a Throne: The Shape Churchless Time Takes**

The verse opens with one of the Qur'an's refrains: the heavens and the earth created *in six days*, followed by God's *istiwāʾ* — His mounting, establishing Himself — upon the Throne (the same sequence appears at 7:54, 10:3, 11:7, 25:59, and 32:4). Two perennial puzzles meet here, and the tradition addressed both with characteristic sobriety. First, the "days": since the alternation of day and night is itself created within these events, the commentators hold from the earliest period that these are "days" of the Lord's measure, not of our clocks — *a day whose measure is a thousand years by your reckoning* (32:5), or fifty thousand (70:4). The point of the number is not chronometry but instruction: the One for whom *Be!* suffices (36:82) deliberately paced creation in stages, teaching His creatures the dignity of orderly process. The Maker of the universe worked, so to speak, with the patience of a craftsman when He could have worked with the flash of a decree — a standing rebuke to the haste,carelessness, and contempt for process that ruin human work. Second, the Throne: the Creed of the early community — transmitted through Mālik, al-Awzāʿī, and the Imams, and preserved in the commentaries of al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr — is that the *istiwāʾ* is real, "in a manner befitting His Majesty," without any asking of "how" (*bilā kayf*), without resemblance to how a body sits upon a chair and without rationalizing it away to nothing. Al-Rāzī and later theologians added the point of reverent logic: the Throne is the greatest of created things, the ceiling of the cosmos; describing the Creator as established above it is the Book's way of declaring His supremacy over the whole of existence while keeping Him utterly distinct from it. The plain believer recites it fully believing it, and the philosopher who reshapes it into mere metaphor has been warned by the same verse's ending — God sees whatsoever you do, including what commentators do to His words.

**The Knowledge That Patrols the Two Doors of the Earth**

The middle of the verse strides into astounding imagery: God knows *that which enters the earth* — rain sinking into soil, seeds buried, the dead lowered into graves, water settling into the depths — *and that which issues therefrom* — vegetation splitting the crust, springs welling up, minerals mined, the dead raised on the Last Day; and *that which descends from Heaven* — rain again, angels, decrees revealed to the lower heaven, provision allotted, revelation itself — *and that which ascends thereto* — deeds carried up by the recording angels, the souls of the dead, supplications, the night ascensions of mercy and command. Al-Jalālayn glosses the pairs concisely with rain and corpses going down, vegetation and minerals coming up. But the architecture of the four phrases matters more than their catalogue: the verse erects four gates — two for the earth, two for the heaven — and declares a Ledger kept at all four. Nothing passes anywhere in the universe without a record. The image is total legibility: reality is not a wilderness of accidents but a realm under complete administration, where even the fall of a seed into a furrow is a witnessed event. This is the positive meaning of divine omniscience, so often felt by men only as exposure; here it is announced as *order* — the guarantee that nothing real is ever lost, forgotten, or uncounted, which the sūrah will need when it promises the charitable their multiplied repayment (v. 18) and warns the hypocrites that their secrets are already booked (v. 6).

**"He Is With You Wheresoever You Are": The With-ness of God**

Then comes the phrase around which centuries of theology turned: *wa-huwa maʿakum aynamā kuntum* — He is with you wheresoever you are. The classical commentators divide on its modality while agreeing on its reality. The majority — al-Jalālayn, al-Qurṭubī, and the theologians — explain the with-ness as God's encompassing *knowledge* (and, al-Qurṭubī adds, power and hearing and sight): He is with you by an awareness so complete that no place and no inner state escapes it, exactly as those addressed at 58:7 are told there is no secret converse of three but He is the fourth of them. Others, like Ibn ʿAjībah, were willing to affirm a with-ness of God's very Essence, and al-Rāzī a with-ness of Being — provided, all hasten to add, that no mixing, indwelling, or confinement in place is conceived; God is not *in* the market or the cell but transcends place while nothing is absent from Him. Two pastoral anchors keep the doctrine safe and warm. First, this with-ness can only be affirmed in one direction: God is with man, but man is not thereby "with" God in equality or deserving — the verse joins it immediately to His seeing all we do, and another verse balances it from man's side: *You are needful of God; and He is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised* (35:15). The with-ness is grace downward, never a human achievement upward. Second, the special with-ness of aid belongs to the godly — *God is with the reverent and those who are good-doers* (16:128) — a with-ness of victory and comfort, as when the Prophet ﷺ steadied Abū Bakr in the cave of Thawr during the Hijrah, the two of them hunted and hidden, with the words:

> *"What do you think of two, the third of whom is God?"* — and then, as the verse itself records, *"Grieve not; truly God is with us"* (9:40; the exchange narrated in al-Bukhārī 3653).

**The Psychology of Being Seen**

The verse's seal, *God sees whatsoever you do*, is one of the sūrah's recurring refrains (cf. 2:233; 3:156; 33:9; 48:24; 49:18; 60:3; 64:2), and it is the engine of the chapter's moral psychology. Modern life has run a fascinating experiment on this verse: we have built a society of cameras, dashboards, and analytics — surveillance by the imperfect — and discovered that being watched changes behavior powerfully but coarsely: people perform for the camera, game the metric, and do in the unmonitored dark what they would not do in the monitored light. Divine sight differs at the two decisive points. There is no dark: *He knows what lies within breasts* (v. 6), so the monitored zone includes intention, motive, and fantasy — the very roots of action that no camera reaches. And there is no gaming: the Watcher cannot be deceived by the behavior He inspects. The result of truly internalizing *God sees whatsoever you do* is therefore not the brittle performativeness of the watched but integrity — the wholeness of a person whose private self and public self converge because both stand before one undeceivable Eye. This is what the Prophet ﷺ compressed into the definition of *iḥsān* in the hadith of Gabriel, when asked about spiritual excellence:

> *"It is that you worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, [know that] He sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8)

**An Exile's Comfort Built Into Cosmology**

Notice the audience the phrase addresses: *wheresoever you are* — plural, general, without condition of place. The history of Islam is, to a remarkable degree, a history of people driven from place to place — emigrants stripped of Makkah, prisoners in cells, refugees, the exiled, the isolated believer in a hostile office or an indifferent city. To them all this clause says: locale cannot exile you from oversight or from companionship. The believer in the most godless room on earth is in attended space; the smallest deed in the obscurity of exile is as present to God as a deed at the Kaʿbah. Conversely, no relocation — no new passport, no anonymity of a great city, no closed door — removes a man from the jurisdiction of the Seeing. Comfort and accountability ride the same clause, which is exactly the sūrah's method from its first verse: the truths that humble the proud are the truths that console the crushed, in a single formulation, because both console and humble by reference to the same God.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:5

> **Unto Him belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth, and unto God are all matters returned.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Bookends of Dominion**

Five short verses have now repeated the declaration of God's sovereignty twice — *Unto Him belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth* closes verse 2 and opens verse 5, word for word. When the Qur'an repeats, the tradition's first question is never "why the redundancy" but "why the repetition," and here the commentators saw an act of enclosure. Between the two declarations the sūrah has displayed the four great Names of v. 3 and the total knowledge of v. 4; the repeated sovereignty formula now frames that display as its warrant. God's dominion is not an arbitrary title stamped on the cosmos; it rests on His priority, His outlasting, His manifestness, His hiddenness, and His knowledge of every transaction between heaven and earth. Sovereignty is claimed, then demonstrated, then claimed again — the way a judge announces the verdict, reads the evidence into the record, and pronounces sentence. The believer who recites the opening of this sūrah has thus been walked through a courtroom: dominion asserted, credentials entered, dominion confirmed.

**All Matters Home: The Eschatology of "Returned"**

The second half adds what the first half implies: *wa-ilā Allāhi turjaʿu al-umūr* — unto God are all matters returned. The verb *turjaʿu* (from r-j-ʿ, to come back) frames existence as a round trip: matters originate with God, unfold in the interval of creation, and are repatriated to Him for auditing and settlement. The formula recurs across the Qur'an in both threatening and consoling contexts (cf. 2:210; 3:109; 8:44; 11:123; 22:76; 35:4; 42:53), and its deliberateness is worth catching. The word is *matters* (al-umūr), not only *persons* or *souls*: affairs, disputes, plans, secrets, unfinished business, the question you shelved, the injustice never litigated, the debt privately forgiven, the intention never enacted — all of it is couriered, in the end, to a single desk. Three consequences follow. First, justice: any matter mishandled here is not dropped; it is rerouted upward, which is why the oppressed of the earth can sleep and the unpunished oppressor should not. Second, completeness: history is not a trail that peters out in dust but a file that closes on a desk where nothing is missing — *Not an atom's weight in the heavens or the earth escapes Him, nor is there aught smaller or greater, but that it is in a clear Book* (34:3). Third, proprietorship: since matters return to Him, they were His all along — the return only makes visible what was always the case.

**No Idle Spectator Throne**

In the architecture of these opening verses, v. 5 performs a precise function: it converts the static claims of vv. 2–3 (sovereignty, the four Names) into motion and destination. A sovereignty that merely possesses would be a collector's hoard; a sovereignty that receives all matters back is a government in session with a day of audit on its calendar. The sūrah is quietly preparing its readers for vv. 12–15 — the light running, the wall, the gate — by establishing now that *everything is en route there*. This also answers, in advance, the psychological question every morally serious person eventually asks: is the universe's moral traffic observed from the top, or does it all evaporate? The believer of this verse lives inside a different felt world than the materialist: outrages are filed, kindnesses are filed, and the filing clerk never sleeps and never shreds. Rightly received, the verse does not make a man passive — *leave it all to God* in the lazy sense — but steady: work faithfully in the interval, because the interval ends in a settlement where nothing faithful will be lost.

**Living Between Grant and Return**

The pairing of sovereignty and return also quietly annihilates the illusion of *maverick* existence — the fantasy, so natural to modern man, of a self-directed zone of life with no reporting line. There is none. To hold property is to manage someone else's estate pending audit (v. 7 will make this explicit: *that over which He has appointed you as trustees*). To make a plan is to draft a proposal into an administration whose final sign-off belongs elsewhere — hence the etiquettes of *in shāʾ Allāh* (18:23–24) are not superstition but accurate bookkeeping of authority. To suffer a wrong is to witness evidence being couriered to the one court with perfect records. And to die is simply the local branch closing while the ledger transfers upstairs. The sūrah's whole moral program will keep invoking this shipping metaphor: racing to forgiveness (v. 21) is sending freight ahead; lending God a goodly loan (v. 11) is exporting capital to the only bank that cannot fail; despairing over what passed or exulting over what came (v. 23) are both bookkeeping errors in a branch office that imagines itself head office. Unto Him the sovereignty; unto Him the return; in between, the brief, supervised liberty of the creatures — that is the entire human situation in nine Arabic words, and the sūrah will spend its remaining verses drawing out its implications.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:6

> **He makes the night pass into the day and makes the day pass into the night, and He knows what lies within breasts.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Interfusions: A Signature Repeating on the Horizon**

The verse joins the visible cosmos to the invisible heart with a single stroke: *yūliju al-layla fī al-nahāri wa-yūliju al-nahāra fī al-layl* — He makes the night pass into the day and the day into the night — *wa-huwa ʿalīmun bi-dhāti al-ṣudūr* — and He knows what lies within breasts. The verb *yūliju* (from w-l-j, to enter, to thread one thing into another) is intimate and physical: night is threaded into day the way a needle carries thread through cloth, each disappearing into the other in a long, graded interfusion — there is no wall between dusk and dawn, only a slow weaving. The commentators saw two complementary references in the alternation: the daily exchange of light and darkness, as Ibn ʿAjībah reads it, and the seasonal lengthening and shortening of the days, as al-Jalālayn suggests — the pattern recurs at 22:61, 31:29, and 35:13, and in one of those parallels the very next clause folds the sun and moon into the same grammar of pressing: *He created the heavens and the earth in truth. He wraps (yukawwiru) the night over the day and wraps the day over the night* (39:5). Every man on earth has watched ten thousand dusks and photographs few of them, so accustomed is the eye to the miracle; the Qur'an's method is to re-name the familiar until the wonder leaks back into it. The dark does not merely *end*; it is actively drawn into the light by a Hand; and day does not *fail*; it is folded deliberately into rest. Neither is an accident, and both are appointments — which is precisely why the Book elsewhere turns this rhythm into a covenant of gratitude:

> *"Say: Have you considered, if God made the night continuous over you until the Day of Resurrection, what god other than God would bring you light? Will you not listen? Say: Have you considered, if God made the day continuous over you until the Day of Resurrection, what god other than God would bring you night, in which you might rest? Will you not see?"* (Qur'an 28:71–72)

**Mercy Hidden Inside Astronomy**

The alternation is not only proof but provision — and the sūrah's readers, who have just been told that all matters return to God, are now shown the most mundane evidence that their Lord's administration is *kind*. Night is the cessation of labor, the cooling of the earth, the hush in which bodies repair and the sleepless find a different commerce — *the night, when it enshrouds* (92:1), *and We made the night a garment* (78:10); day is the season of livelihood and gathering — *and We made the day a livelihood* (78:11). A stationary heaven with either pole permanent would break agriculture, sleep, trade, and sanity within weeks. The Maker who threads one into the other has thereby dosed existence with both ambition and rest in alternation — and the believer is invited to read his own fortunes by the same sign: the night of hardship is threaded into a coming day, no darkness is the final state, and even the longest polar night on this earth has its sunrise scheduled in the Book of decrees. The verse is cosmology with a pastoral spine: the same hand on the sky is on your circumstances, and it is a hand that has never once left the night un-ended.

**From the Two Horizons to the Hidden Chest: Why the Join?**

Then comes the seam that makes the verse famous among the commentators: light-and-darkness governance joined immediately to knowledge of the breast's contents. The junction is not arbitrary. This verse and the ones before it (vv. 3–6) coordinate the doctrine of v. 3 — the Outward and the Inward — into the domain of knowledge. Verse 3 declared the Names; v. 4 attached the Outward side, knowledge of all that enters earth, exits earth, descends, ascends, and of *whatsoever you do*; v. 6 now attaches the Inward side: not only acts but the unlit interior — intention beyond reach of any witness, motive invisible even to one's intimates, envy kept off the face, sincerity never displayed, hatred nursed, hope clutched, doubt privately entertained. As the commentators observe, the sūrah is thereby quietly preparing its stage for the hypocrites of vv. 13–15: people whose entire strategy depended on an unpatrolled gap between outward appearance and inner reality. This verse closes the gap in principle before the narrative ever arrives — the interior is not a blind spot of the divine attention but its deepest field of view. As the Book says elsewhere in the same grammar:

> *"And We created man, and We know what his soul whispers to him; and We are nearer to him than his jugular vein."* (Qur'an 50:16)

**The Comfort and the Warning of a Chest Already Read**

The knowledge of hearts cuts morally in two directions, and a balanced believer lets both edges work. As warning: nothing about a person's religious accounting rides on presentation, because the decisive exhibits were never in his visible conduct but in the folded layers of his intention, already fully known — so the entire economy of religious image-management is revealed as a ponzi scheme whose auditor saw through it before it began. As comfort — and the commentators note the poor in spirit have always found more here than the proud — the same knowledge means *no hidden devotion is ever wasted*. The secret charity given so quietly the left hand did not know (per the Prophet's teaching, Muslim 1031, from the seven shaded by God), the swallowed rude answer that no one heard choke in the throat, the night-vigil eyes in a house of sleepers, the intention to do good that circumstances forbade, credited as done per the hadith: *Whoever intends a good deed and does not do it, God records it for him as a complete good deed* (al-Bukhārī 6491, Muslim 131) — all of it was already on record *before* manifestation. The hypocrite finds this doctrine a nightmare and the quiet believer finds it the end of loneliness: every interior labor had exactly one witness, and it was the only Witness whose testimony decides the case.

**Life Application: An Interior With a Window**

Applied plainly, the verse restructures the modern disciplines of selfhood. The prestige economy of public life — résumés, feeds, personal brands — runs entirely on managing what others perceive; the believer's counter-discipline runs on managing what *God* already knows, which frees the former of its tyranny: if the inward is known, curating the outward beyond truth is wasted motion. In therapy-language, the verse describes the only relationship of absolute, non-distorting disclosure; in police-language, the only incorruptible surveillance; in familial language, the presence that never misreads the stammering thing we meant. Because He knows what lies within breasts, prayer becomes the one conversation in which we never need to find the words — the chest is already open before the Knower, and the movement toward Him in secret is the most public thing a soul ever does. The day folded into night of the first clause becomes the emblem of this interior: the periods of spiritual light and darkness threaded through a believer's life are also under management, neither permanent nor unwitnessed — He threads them into one another with the same fidelity, and He knows throughout what the breast carries in both.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:7

> **Believe in God and His Messenger and spend from that over which He has appointed you as trustees. For those of you who believe and spend, theirs shall be a great reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Theology of Verses 1–6 Draws Its Sword**

After six verses of pure doctrine — God's sovereignty, life and death, the four Names, the total knowledge, the alternating sky — the sūrah suddenly issues its first commands, and they are two: *āminū* — *believe!* — and *anfiqū* — *spend!* The pairing is the chapter's logic made visible. The theology of vv. 1–6 was never ornamental; it was the construction of the premises from which these two imperatives follow as conclusions. Because all dominion is His (v. 2), belief in Him and in the Messenger He actually sent — rather than in imaginary sovereignties or self-appointed authorities — is simple realism. And because you are a tenant whose estate reverts to its Owner (v. 5), spending from it is not generosity out of your own pocket but the proper accounting of someone else's money in your hand. The commentators especially al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī dwell on the architecture: this is a sūrah that argues. The demands on the purse arrive only after the mind has been shown that the purse was never its own.

**"That Over Which He Has Appointed You as Trustees": The Word That Refounds Property**

The crux of the verse is a single word: *mustakhlafīn*, you who have been made *khulafāʾ* — trustees, successors, vicegerents — over what you possess. The root is kh-l-f, "to come after, to succeed," and from it comes *khalīfah*, the title of man's very commission on earth: *When thy Lord said unto the angels, "I am setting a vicegerent (khalīfah) upon the earth"* (2:30), and *He it is Who made you vicegerents (khalāʾif) of the earth* (6:165). The commentators unfold the word in two complementary directions, each devastating to the ordinary instincts of ownership. The first, pressed by al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī: nothing you call property is actually your property — it is God's, entrusted into your agency; you are the steward, not the lord, of the estate. From which follows the commentators' quietly radical piece of reasoning: spending from it should be *easy* — easier, indeed, than spending from another person's wealth in the ordinary sense, for here the true Owner has commanded the disbursement and guaranteed the recompense. The second direction, the dynastic sense of the root, adds time to the picture: you are a *successor* to those who held this wealth before you — parents and peoples now buried — and those after you will succeed you in holding it. You clasp today what yesterday's dead clasped and tomorrow's heirs will clasp; the gold flows through the generations like water through a chain of cupped hands, and each hand is judged not by how long it held the water but by where it poured. The Prophet ﷺ pressed this transience into one of his most famous sayings, muslim records it in his Ṣaḥīḥ:

> *"The son of Adam says, 'My wealth! My wealth!' But what of your wealth is yours, save what you eat and consume, or wear and wear out, or give in charity and send ahead?"* (Muslim 2958)

Possession, in the audited sense, exists only in three forms: what passed your lips, what perished on your back, and what you exported to the Hereafter. Everything else was inventory in transit — held in the name of the Estate, answerable at the Return of all matters (v. 5).

**Belief and Spending: Why the Two Commands Are Welded**

It is not incidental that the sūrah's first command pairs faith with expenditure. In the Qur'an's moral psychology, the purse is the most sensitive seismograph of conviction — what a man *does with money* registers his creed more honestly than what he says with his tongue, for money is crystallized option: it stands for every future pleasure, security, and vanity it could buy, so that parting with it is a measurable sacrifice of imagined futures. Hence someone can sincerely *think* he believes and be exposed by his wallet; the wallet does not have moods or eloquence. This is why the hypocrites of this very sūrah (vv. 13–15) failed at exactly this checkpoint — outwardly present, inwardly unpersuaded — and why the chapter keeps returning to spending (vv. 7, 10, 11, 18, 24) as though it were the sūrah's appointed litmus. The modern reader should not miss the structural clarity: God does not ask here for the greatest act of spending — no amount is fixed in this verse — but He welds the command to believe directly to the command to disburse, making generosity the breath of faith rather than a philanthropy occasionally indulged. And the close seals the promise in terms picked up all the way to v. 29: *theirs shall be a great reward* — the believer spends from a trust and is paid for the disbursement as though he had contributed his own, because before God, intention converts the stewardship into personal merit.

**The Stewardship Mindset in the Twenty-First Century**

The applications write themselves, but they cut deep. At the personal level: the believer runs his finances as an agency relationship — income is a delegated account, and every outflow is either consumption (permitted, within limits), preservation, or the one upright investment that the Owner prices above all: spending in His way — feeding, relieving, endowing, supporting knowledge, forgiving debts. The internal conversation changes at the till and at the transfer screen: not *"how much of mine can I bear to lose?"* but *"what share of the trust has the Owner signaled should move?"* At the social level: a community formed on mustakhlafīn doctrine cannot generate the pathologies that flow from absolute-ownership doctrine — hoarding as a right, waste as a prerogative, and the poor as strangers with no claim — because the Book elsewhere teaches that the poor possess a *stipulated share* (*ḥaqq maʿlūm*) in the wealth (51:19, 70:24–25), something the trustee was holding for them before he ever met them. At the level of the heart: the doctrine dismantles both greed and guilt in one motion — greed, because the One who truly owns the store will multiply what is moved (v. 11); guilt, because keeping and reasonable enjoying of the trust is not theft from God but part of the estate's permitted administration — *forget not your portion in this world* (28:77). One hand opens upward in gratitude, the other outward in giving, and the steward walks between the two gates of v. 2's life-and-death the way the Owner intends: trusting, generous, unburdened, and rich in the only currency that crosses the grave.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:8

> **How is it that you believe not in God when the Messenger calls you to believe in your Lord—and He has indeed made a covenant with you—if you are believers?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rhetorical Astonishment That Argues With Man's Own Nature**

The verse is a question of wonder, one of the Qur'an's great rebuking questions — *wa-mā lakum lā tuʾminūna bi-Allāh* — "What is with you, that you do not believe?" The form (*mā lakum*) expresses astonishment at a thing that should not be, the way one stares at a man pouring water onto his own burning house: belief is so native, so owed, and so corroborated that its absence requires explanation. The verse then stacks, in rapid succession, the three witnesses arrayed against unbelief. First, the external witness: *the Messenger calls you to believe in your Lord* — the claimant is not a rumor but a man present among you whose truthfulness his own enemies conceded and whose message has the self-evidencing texture the sūrah's first verses displayed. Second, the primordial witness: *He has indeed made a covenant with you* — which the commentators, following al-Ṭabarī and Ibn ʿAjībah and Ibn Kathīr's chain of interpretation, identify with the covenant God took from all humanity before the creation of the world, when the progeny of Adam were brought forth from his loins like specks and testified to His lordship:

> *"And when thy Lord took from the Children of Adam, from their loins, their progeny, and made them bear witness concerning themselves — 'Am I not your Lord?' — they said, 'Yes, we bear witness. Lest you should say on the Day of Resurrection, "Truly we were heedless of this."'"* (Qur'an 7:172)

Third, folded into the astonishment itself, the internal witness: the constitution of the listener, which the verse presumes to be so constructed as to recognize its Maker's call the way a struck chord hums in sympathy. The grammar thus lines up, as al-Nīsābūrī and al-Rāzī note, the outside voice (Messenger), the pre-historic voice (covenant), and the inside voice (the innate aptitude to assent) — and asks man what excuse he still has when all three agree.

A grammatical note the commentators record (al-Baghawī, Ibn ʿAjībah): the clause *wa-qad akhadha mīthāqakum* — "and He has indeed taken your covenant" — is also read in a passive form, "and your covenant has indeed been taken," with the same meaning; both readings invite the listener to remember a binding acknowledgment that precedes his earliest memory as an adult and precedes even his birth as a soul.

**The Fiṭrah: The Covenant Built Into the Self**

Behind the doctrine of the covenant stands the broader Qur'anic doctrine of the *fiṭrah* — the created disposition, the original constitution of the human being, which the Book sets beside religion itself: *So set thy face toward the religion, as one with primordial faith — the primordial nature (fiṭrah) of God upon which He formed mankind; there is no altering the creation of God* (30:30). The Prophet ﷺ sharpened the doctrine to a saying every Muslim child learns:

> *"Every child is born upon the fiṭrah; it is his parents who make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Zoroastrian."* (al-Bukhārī 1385; Muslim 2658)

The logic of the verse then becomes exact: unbelief is not the natural default that faith must labor uphill to construct; it is an *accrual* — something overlaid, by upbringing, appetite, ideology, or negligence, upon a prior constitution that already assents. The Messenger's call does not install a foreign operating system; it speaks the self's own native tongue back to it. This is why the call to belief in the Qur'an so often takes the form of *reminder* (dhikr, tadhkīr) rather than information: the addressee is being recalled to what he is, not introduced to what he is not. The practical consequence is enormous for how one thinks about doubt, about one's scattered acquaintances, about one's own cold seasons: the ground of the soul remains, under whatever drift of debris, struck and signed with the covenant; calling people to God — including oneself at 4 a.m. in crisis — is never shouting into a foreign country but calling someone's own deep name at the door of the house they presently wander.

**"If You Are Believers": A Conditional That Closes the Last Loophole**

The verse's ending has puzzled inattentive readers and delighted the commentators: *in kuntum muʾminīn* — "if you are believers." How can the unbelief of the addressee be reproached, and then the sentence close as though they might already believe? The resolved sense, as the commentators lay it out: the astonishment addresses anyone whose profession or constitution already implies faith — the one who claims belief in *some* lordship, some creator, some right and wrong, while stopping short of its consequence in the revealed religion. Such a man's position is self-devouring: he concedes by his constitution and his lips the very premise on which the Messenger's claim rides. Either he means his belief — then let him follow it consistently to the Messenger who calls him to his *actual* Lord, not the one of his preference — or he does not mean it, and the title has been costume. The conditional is thus a courteous cornering: *if you are believers* — as you claim, as your nature claims, as your covenant claims — then this astonishment applies to you: *what is with you* that you hold back? The rhetorical gentleness of it belongs to the sūrah's larger mercy: the reproach never expels the hearer from the company of believers; it insists he belong to it in fact.

**The Reading Modern Unbelief Would Rather Skip**

Contemporary unbelief often arrives dressed as sophistication — as though the default of serious minds were disbelief and faith were the residual mood of the unexamined. This verse inverts the tribunal. It treats *unbelief* as the position needing explanation, because unbelief must answer three exhibits it cannot honestly answer: the Messenger whose character and message stand under the historical light overhead; the covenant still palpable in the human longing for the Absolute, in the flash of conscience, in the universal grammar of praise and blame that no materialism has ever cashed out; and the interior resonance that attends the revelation itself when heard unarmored. The verse does not mock the doubter — the sūrah is too merciful for that, and its Author knows what lies within breasts (v. 6) — but it relocates the burden of proof onto the habitual assumption of autonomy, and quietly invites the listener to the experiment his own nature is built for: *hear the call, and note what assents*. Believers debating an unbelieving friend, or wrestling with their own recurring clouds, would do well to remember the verse's structure: one does not argue a man into an alien conclusion; one removes, layer by layer, whatever is throttling a conclusion his own chest has already signed.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:9

> **He it is Who sends down clear signs upon His servant to bring you out of darkness into light, and truly God is Kind and Merciful unto you.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Signs Sent Down Upon a Servant**

The verse names its subject in the third person — *Huwa alladhī yunazzilu ʿalā ʿabdihi āyātin bayyināt* — He it is Who sends down clear signs upon *His servant* — and the honor-word must be felt: at the summit of prophethood, the title chosen for Muhammad ﷺ is *His servant* (ʿabdihi). The Qur'an does this at the highest moments: the opening of the Night Journey — *Glory be to Him Who carried His servant by night* (17:1); the sending of the Criterion — *Blessed is He Who sent down the Criterion upon His servant* (25:1); the encounter at the Lote-Tree — *He revealed to His servant what He revealed* (53:10). The lesson folded into the style is central to Islamic prophetology: prophethood is not man climbing up to divinity's ledge but God sending down to a man who remains, at his highest, exactly what everyman should be — a servant. As for the *clear signs* (āyāt bayyināt), the commentators give the phrase breadth: the revelation's verses themselves, whose clarity evidences their origin, and — as when the phrase is used of Moses (17:101; 28:36) — the accompanying miracles and demonstrations that vindicate the messenger. The plural bays all ambiguity to rest: not one sign but a surging sequence, each *bayyinah*, self-clarifying, needing no interpreter to establish that a light is a light.

**From Darkness Into Light: The Rescue Grammar of Revelation**

Then the verse states the entire purpose of revelation in a single directional clause: *li-yukhrijakum mina al-ẓulumāti ilā al-nūr* — to bring you out of darkness into light. The same clause frames the sending of the Book at 14:1 and 14:5 and the angelic ministry of God at 33:43, and its pattern deserves a close reading. The darknesses are plural — *ẓulumāt*: not one darkness but strata of them, an outer dark and inner darks beneath it — while the light is singular — *al-nūr*: with the definite article, the one Light, God's own. Unrevelation is a plural condition with many rooms — the darkness of ignorance, of appetite, of idols, of despair, of tribal blindness, of being unable to locate the door — and revelation is the single luminous exit from the whole labyrinth. The verb *yukhrijakum* — *brings you out* — must also be weighed: God does not say "to inform you about the light" nor "to describe the exit" but to *bring*, that is, to effect a transfer of persons — revelation is transport, not just information; the Book is less a map than a hand. And the movement is emigration: from a homeland (al-ẓulumāt is where the soul is *residing*) into a different country of being. The commentators trace the paired symbols across the whole Book: light against darkness as guidance against misguidance (2:257: *God is the Protector of those who believe; He brings them out of darkness into light*), as life against death (6:122), as sight against blindness (13:16, 35:19–22), until the great Verse of Light gathers the whole symbol to its point:

> *"God is the Light of the heavens and the earth. The parable of His Light is as a niche wherein is a lamp — the lamp in a glass, the glass as it were a glittering star — kindled from a blessed olive tree, neither of East nor West, whose oil would well nigh shine, though no fire touched it. Light upon light. God guides to His Light whomsoever He will."* (Qur'an 24:35)

**Ibn ʿAjībah's Five Darknesses: The Interior Cartography**

The North African commentator Ibn ʿAjībah (d. 1224/1809), whose tafsīr carries the contemplative tradition, maps the clause inward in a sentence that has become a classic of spiritual psychology; it deserves quotation nearly entire:

> *Out of the darkness of sin into the light of repentance and righteousness; out of the darkness of heedlessness into the light of wakefulness; out of the darkness of desire and selfishness into the light of renunciation and purity; out of the darkness of the sensual into the light of the spiritual; out of the darkness of ignorance into the light of knowledge through God.*

Notice the ladder: it begins exactly where every returning sinner begins — with sin and repentance — and it rises through heedlessness (the disease of the un-sinning religious, whose danger is numbness rather than crime), then to desire (the disease of the awake — wanting what owns you), then to the sensual orientation itself (the disease of the disciplined — the cage of the five senses construed as the whole of evidence), and ends not at information but at *knowledge through God* — ʿilm billāh, knowledge whose object is God and whose instrument is His illumination. The ladder thus runs from the jail to the throne room, and its rungs are arranged to prevent the believer from stopping at any intermediate floor and calling the climb finished.

**"Kind and Merciful Unto You": The Reason the Light Was Sent**

The verse closes with the justification of the whole transaction: *inna Allāha bikum la-raʾūfun raḥīm* — truly God is toward you Kind, Merciful. Two Names, each doing distinct work. *Raʾūf* (from raʾfah, a tenderness finer than ordinary mercy — the gentleness that anticipates and removes distress before it hardens) and *Raḥīm* (the steady, covenant-attached mercy that embraces the believers, as in *He is Merciful unto the believers*, 33:43, the verse that pairs *Raʾūf* with the emigration from darkness exactly as here). The closing clause is the sūrah's preemptive answer to every suspicion that revelation is a prosecution: the sending of signs was not surveillance, not an exam designed for failure, not cosmic bureaucracy. It was tenderness — as though the verse insisted the sending of the Book be filed in the heart under the same heading as rain to a drought. The psychology this builds in a believer is the linchpin of healthy religion: the rule of God over me is the rule of *raʾfah* and *raḥmah* — the commands are the light sent down out of kindness, the prohibitions are darkness-blocked doors. A law loved as mercy transforms the one it binds; a law resented as intrusion generates exactly the outward compliance and inner mutiny that vv. 13–15 will dissect in the hypocrites.

**Why People Stay in Darkness: And How the Light Actually Takes Hold**

The verse also quietly poses a question every honest reader must face: if the signs are *clear* and the Agent is *Kind and Merciful*, why does anyone remain in darkness? The sūrah has already supplied the instrument of the answer — the covenant could be muffled (v. 8) and the breast can hold against knowledge (v. 6) — and the rest of the chapter will answer it in case studies. But the verse itself hints at the positive dynamics of exit: light is *sent down upon His servant* and then *brings you out* — the climb begins with a descent, guidance arrives before it is achieved, and no one climbs out of the strata of darkness by climbing; one is *brought* out by answering a hand already extended. Practically, this orders a believer's hope in two directions at once: downward in humility — my exits from my own darknesses were gifts of kindness, not trophies of acumen — and forward in confidence — since the Light still descends upon the servant's Book, no one I love is beyond rescue while breath and reminder remain. And the last mercy folded into the grammar is tense: *yunazzilu*, present-future — He *sends down*, habitually, continuingly: the descent of light is not a finished event of the seventh century but the sky's standing habit, falling fresh on every reader who opens the Book with the darknesses he actually lives in, asking the Kind and Merciful to do again what the verse says He does — *bring you out*, plurally, institutionally, personally, into the singular Light.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:10

> **And how is it that you do not spend in the way of God when unto God belongs the inheritance of the heavens and the earth. Not equal among you are those who spent and fought before the victory. They are greater in rank than those who spend and fight afterwards; yet God has promised unto each that which is most beautiful, and God is Aware of whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inheritance Argument: A Daunting Arithmetic for the Closed Fist**

The verse begins with the same astonished rebuke as v. 8 — *wa-mā lakum* — but now turned from belief upon the purse: "What is with you, that you do not spend in the way of God?" And its argument is a single devastating link of chain: *wa-lillāhi mīrāthu al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ* — unto God belongs the *inheritance* of the heavens and the earth. The commentators as one explain the image: an inheritance is what passes to an heir when the owners are gone; the heavens and earth, with every treasury and title in them, pass back at last to God when every holder is finished holding — as the Book says, *Surely it is We Who give life and cause death, and We are the Inheritor* (15:23), whence the Divine Name al-Wārith. Now observe what this does to miserliness at the purely logical level, which al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī spell out with relish. If a man spends in God's way, the wealth returns to God *through his own hand*, credited to him as meritorious charity, multiplied in his account (v. 11), and transmuted into reward. If he does not spend, the wealth still returns to God — through probate, through the grave, through the universal reversion of all things — and the man is left with nothing but the omission. Either way the money goes home. The only choice is whether it goes home *for* him or *without* him. Generosity, the verse thus argues, is not a luxury of the soft-hearted; it is arithmetic intelligible to a banker: the only spendable capital is capital already under recall notice, and the only question is whether it will generate a return. Elsewhere the Qur'an states the identical schedule from the side of the owners: *You will never attain piety till you spend from that which you love* (3:92) — because loved capital is the only capital whose release proves anything about what owns a man.

**"Not Equal": The Divinely Certified Ranking of Sacrifice**

Then comes the principle that made the verse famous in the community's memory: *lā yastawī minkum man anfaqa min qabli al-fatḥi wa-qātal* — Not equal among you are those who spent and fought before the victory — *ulāʾika aʿẓamu darajatan min alladhīna anfaqū min baʿdu wa-qātalū* — those are greater in rank than those who spend and fight afterwards — *wa-kullan waʿada Allāhu al-ḥusnā* — yet God has promised unto each that which is most beautiful. The "victory" (al-fatḥ, literally "the opening" — the same word as the sūrah of the conquest's name) the commentators overwhelmingly identify with the conquest of Makkah in 8/630, the watershed after which Islam's fortunes in Arabia, and then beyond, were decided and secure. Before it, to spend for Islam or to fight for it was to back a beleaguered minority at mortal cost, with every worldly sign arguing against; after it, the same actions were increasingly underwritten by success — still meritorious, still required, and still rewarded, but no longer possible under the old conditions of pure risk, and therefore not *the same act* in the eyes of divine valuation. The distinction is not invidious tribal nostalgia; it is an exact moral measurement — the Book measures deeds by the honesty of the moment in which they were done, not merely by outward form. The Prophet ﷺ fixed this hierarchy in the most quoted hadith on the Companions' rank, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, in the aftermath of a slur:

> *"Do not revile my Companions! For by Him in Whose Hand is my soul, were one of you to spend the like of Mount Uḥud in gold, it would not equal a mudd of one of them, nor even half a mudd."* (al-Bukhārī 3673; Muslim 2540)

A mudd is a dry measure — two cupped hands, a farmer's double handful of grain. Against a mountain of gold. The hyperbole is precisely the teaching: raw quantity does not price a deed; *the moment of truth does*. A handful dates in a famine year of a hunted faith outranks, in the scales of the Aware, an era's fortune donated when giving has become safe, fashionable, and tax-advantaged. And the commentators then preserved a famous incident at the verse's own occasion of revelation: several report the verse to have descended after friction between Khālid ibn al-Walīd and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, early pillars both — and the Prophet's response, in effect, was the verse's own: the ranking is established, cease comparing across what God has already graded.

**The Abū Bakr Precedent: Spending Everything at the Bottom of the Market**

The commentators — al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr among them — connect this verse with the career of Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq above all men, whose spending was entire and whose timing was at Islam's darkest haul. The most moving report attached to the verse tells of Gabriel descending while Abū Bakr sat with the Prophet ﷺ shrouded in patched poverty, having put the whole of his fortune behind the cause. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī recount the exchange: Gabriel asks the Prophet about Abū Bakr's condition, is told he has spent all his wealth before the victory, and is charged with returning God's greeting of peace and the very question this verse prepares us to understand — *Are you pleased with Me in this state of poverty, or displeased?* The Prophet conveys it; Abū Bakr weeps and answers, with the entire doctrine of contentment compressed into a sentence:

> *"How can I be displeased with my Lord? I am pleased with my Lord! I am pleased with my Lord!"*

The story's weight is not sentimental but doctrinal. The God of v. 10, to Whom the inheritance of the heavens returns, personally asks the man who liquidated his estate for Him whether the exchange grieves him — and the question, so far from suggesting loss, is the proof that the purchase was mutual. The man left with rags is asked if he is *pleased* — and the pleasure recorded in his reply is the exact profit this sūrah calls, at its end, *the Bounty of God, which He gives to whomsoever He will* (v. 21, v. 29).

**"Yet God Has Promised Unto Each": Equality Beneath the Hierarchy**

The verse's last movement is as important as the ranking itself: *wa-kullan waʿada Allāhu al-ḥusnā* — yet *each*, the earlier and the later, has been promised the Most Beautiful — Paradise itself, and beyond it the beatific good the word al-ḥusnā carries (as in *For those who do good is the most beautiful [reward], and more*, 10:26). The same principle appears in gradation form at 4:95: *God favors those who strive with their goods and their lives a degree above those who stay behind; yet unto both God has promised that which is most beautiful.* Classes differ in rank; none of the sincere is refused. The balance is a masterpiece of social wisdom, and it matches the reported prophetic verdict: *The strong believer is better and more beloved to God than the weak believer, and in each there is good* (Muslim 2664). A religion of pure hierarchy would dishearten the late and the ordinary; a religion of pure egalitarianism in outcomes would dissolve the moral meaning of risk. The verse does neither: it certifies rank — keeping history honest about who carried the weight when it was crushing — while flinging the door as wide as sincerity reaches. In practical terms: the believer who came to God after the era of heroes is not told to despair at the closed ranks but to spend and strive *in his present*, trusting the same Aware One who noticed the mudd to notice his own. And that seal — *God is Aware of whatsoever you do* (khabīr, the Expert in fine-grained knowledge) — is precisely the seal the doctrine requires: ranking by risk is only possible for a Judge who perceives, in each era and each chest, exactly what each act cost the one who did it.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:11

> **Who is it that will lend unto God a goodly loan? He will multiply it for him, and his shall be a generous reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Astonishing Metaphor in the Language of Finance**

After commanding belief and spending (v. 7) and rebuking the withheld hand (v. 10), the sūrah now rises to the most daring figure in all its teaching on wealth: *man dhā alladhī yuqriḍu Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan* — "Who is it that will lend unto God a goodly loan?" The figure recurs across the Book as a signature motif (cf. 2:245; 5:12; 57:18; 64:17; 73:20), and its audacity is meant to arrest. The Self-Sufficient, to Whom belongs the inheritance of the heavens and the earth (v. 10), Who *owns* the very dirham in the lender's hand — this God frames the believer's charity as a *loan made to Him*. The commentators linger over the graciousness of the construction. Linguistically, *man dhā* ("who is it that...?") is the form an offer takes when a crier in a marketplace announces an opportunity no one is expected to refuse; the verse is styled as an open invitation, not a command — an auctioneer's call addressed to everyone within earshot. Theological care: God of course borrows nothing (as any borrowing entails need), and the scholars made plain, as al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr do, that the loan-language is *divine condescension to human measure* — taḥabbub, God clothing a gift His creature gives His creature in the vocabulary of a transaction the creature can trust. Men understand lending: capital leaves the hand but is not lost; it exists as an asset on the strongest possible counterparty. When God puts charity in that vocabulary, He is translating the unseen economy into the one bourgeois instrument every man grasps: a secured, interest-bearing credit. And the security is absolute — the Borrower cannot default, devalue, disappear, or die, and the partial verse already quoted guarantees enforcement: *unto God are all matters returned* (v. 5).

**"Goodly": The Ten Conditions on a Loan God Accepts**

The adjective *ḥasan* — goodly, beautiful, wholesome — is the hinge. Not every dirham that leaves a hand enters this account; the Book and the commentators record exacting conditions, the most complete catalogue being al-Rāzī's, which the commentary tradition has transmitted and refined. Condensed, the goodly loan is: (1) from wealth lawfully earned — for *God is good and accepts only what is good*, as the Prophet ﷺ said (Muslim 1015); (2) from the best of one's property, not from its refuse — *spend of the good things you have earned, and do not seek out the bad of it to spend* (2:267); (3) given from what one loves and would rather keep — *You will never attain piety till you spend from that which you love* (3:92), and the Bedouins of the desert brought their camels they loved; (4) directed to the genuinely needy and deserving rather than scattered for effect; (5) concealed where possible — *if you conceal the alms and give them to the poor, it is better for you* (2:271) — protecting both giver from vanity and receiver from shame; (6) un-followed by reminders and injury — the Prophet's phrase in the verse's shadow: charity unharmed, for God says *annul not your acts of charity with reproach and harm* (2:264); (7) intended for God's face, not men's eyes — unlike those *who spend their wealth to be seen of men* (4:38); (8) regarded by the giver afterward as little, however large — for it was only ever worldly money; (9) chosen from what is dearest and most prized; and (10) given with no condescension toward the receiver, who does the giver the service of accepting what purifies the giver's wealth and soul. One more reported saying perfectly catches the spirit of the whole list: the Prophet ﷺ said, paraphrased in its sense in the two Ṣaḥīḥ collections, that God takes the charity of the honest giver — *even a half-date* earned lawfully — in His right hand, and nurtures it for its owner as a man rears a foal, until it grows like a mountain (al-Bukhārī 1410, Muslim 1014). The foal image is chosen with stunning precision: the deposit does not sit in vaults; it is *alive*, and it is reared.

**The Multiplication: Economics From the Other Side of the Sky**

Then the yield: *fa-yuḍāʿifahu lahu* — He will multiply it for him. The rates are posted elsewhere in the Book, and they defy every market on earth. The floor: *Whoever brings a good deed shall have ten times the like thereof* (6:160). The standing offer: the famous parable —

> *"The parable of those who spend their wealth in the way of God is that of a grain that grows seven ears, in every ear a hundred grains. And God multiplies for whomsoever He will. And God is All-Encompassing, Knowing."* (Qur'an 2:261)

— one to seven hundred, then *beyond*, open-ended, *for whomsoever He will*. And the duplicate of this very promise at the sūrah's sister verse: *If you lend unto God a goodly loan, He will multiply it for you and forgive you; and God is Thankful (al-Shakūr), Clement* (64:17) — where the borrower-side Name is al-Shakūr, the Most Thankful: God *thanks* His servant for the loan, the greatest of all condescensions, repaying with thanks what was itself only His own bestowed wealth. The remaining promise in our verse — *and his shall be a generous reward (ajrun karīm)* — adds, say the commentators, the note of honor: karīm, generous and noble, of the kind that gives without calculation or stinginess in return, exactly the character the loaner showed at the telling moment. The two clauses together cover quantity (multiplication) and quality (generosity of the bestowal): he will receive *more than* the amount and *better than* the measure.

**The Psychology the Loan-Teaching Rewires**

Beneath the vocabulary runs a rewiring of two deep human reflexes. First, the reflex of *deprivation*: the heart experiences giving as loss because it watches the hand, not the ledger. The loan metaphor relocates the asset — the wealth has not vanished, it has been *transferred to an account with the only perfectly solvent Bank*, where it grows at seven-hundred-plus and will be honored at the precise moment (the plain where, per 3:14 and v. 20, no earthly currency trades) the holder needs it most. The tradition dramatized the safety of the account in a famous concrete image: the Prophet ﷺ had a camel given, at his death, as charity (or a debt disputed — the stories run close), and the Companions knew his teaching that the settlement of loans is exact beyond the grave — the whole community learned Muhammad's own verdict: *the martyr is forgiven everything except debt* (Muslim 1886) — because obligations remain obligations even toward men, and how much more a credit standing *toward God* on the believer's side is safe. Second, the reflex of *condescension*: much human giving is a performance of superiority — the pose of the well-supplied dropping alms upon the less. Loan framing inverts the dignity: the giver is a *creditor soliciting* the humblest borrower-facing transaction in history, and like any man offering a loan, his posture is supplicant, not grandee. The beggar at the door recasts as an opportunity-broker; the giver is the one in need — of someone to accept his transfer. Who is it, then — *man dhā* — that will take up this offer? The interrogative is designed to make every reader answer from his own chest before he reads the next verse, where he will see the day the loan pays out.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:12

> **On the Day when you see the believing men and the believing women with their light spreading before them and on their right, "Glad tidings unto you this Day: Gardens with rivers running below, therein to abide. That is the great triumph."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scene Changes to the Plain of Judgment**

With *yawma* — *on the Day when* — the sūrah lifts its readers out of the marketplace of giving and sets them down on the vast plain of the Resurrection, where the loans of v. 11 come due. The commentators (al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn ʿAjībah) note the two grammatical avenues: the verse can read as the tail of the previous promise — *his shall be a generous reward, on the Day when you see...* — or as a new sentence with an implied imperative of remembrance: *Remember the Day* — the Prophetic way of summoning a scene before the eyes. Either reading gathers the previous verses' teaching about wealth into a single panoramic answer: *this* is what the goodly loan yields. The staging deserves attention. The day is one of universal assembly, terror, and blinding exposure — the plain spread flat for the congregation of all souls, as at 18:47 (*We shall leave them, that Day, billowing over one another*) and 79:14 (*and behold, they are awake upon the earth's surface*). In that crush and glare, the reader is instructed to *look* — *tarā* — you see — and the scene the verse commands the imagination to hold is not the Fire but the procession of the saved: believing men and believing women, each carrying light, moving.

**Their Light: What It Is and Why It Runs**

*Nūruhum yasʿā bayna aydīhim wa-bi-aymānihim* — their light runs before them and on their right. The verb *yasʿā* means it *runs, hastens, strides* — not a lamp held at the hip but a radiance that precedes the believer like a guide eager to lead him home. What is the light? The commentators offer complementary answers, no one of which excludes the others. It is, say Ibn ʿAjībah and others, the light of faith itself — hidden in the breast through the whole of life (the very "what lies within breasts" God knew at v. 6) — now externalized: the inward made outward at last, which is this sūrah's deepest pattern arriving at its consummation. It is equally, as Ibn Kathīr transmits from the early exegetes, the light of *deeds* — prayer, fasting, charity (this sūrah's own obsession), Qur'an, repentance — each act in the world laying down, unbeknownst to the doer, lumen for the road out of that Day's darkness. And it is the light of the countenance, the radiance the Prophet ﷺ promised specifically to the people of ablution in a hadith recorded by Muslim from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"Verily my community will be summoned on the Day of Resurrection with radiant faces, hands, and feet from the traces of ablution; whoever among you can extend his radiance, let him do so."* (al-Bukhārī 136; Muslim 246)

Al-Rāzī adds the metaphysical capstone that reconciles all these readings: *the real light is God* — the Light of the heavens and the earth (24:35) — and the light granted the believers is the light of knowledge and insight, the Participated glow of that unborrowed Light; every glow on the plain is derivative, a reflected shine from the one Source. Hence no hypocrite can counterfeit it: borrowed theology shines in the world; on that Day only light that was *grown* burns. And the direction — before them and on their right — is read by Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī and al-Shaʿrānī as the fit of the moment: the scroll of each believer's deeds will be put into his *right hand* (*as for him who is given his book in his right hand*, 69:19), and the light thus shines from the side of acquittal — before them the road, and beside them at the right the evidence of their acquittal glowing like a lantern held out by a beloved.

**The Crossing of the Ṣirāṭ: Why Light Is the Only Vehicle**

The destination given in the glad tidings is Gardens beneath which rivers flow; between the plain and the Gardens the tradition knows a traverse — the *ṣirāṭ*, the bridge over Hellfire, which the Prophet ﷺ described in the two Ṣaḥīḥs in the terrifying physiognomy that generations of believers have carried in memory:

> *"Then the bridge (al-jisr) is set over Hell... a bridge thinner than a hair and sharper than a sword"* — as the assembled hadith of the Crossing run (see Muslim 182–183, the long hadith of intercession; and the description in al-Tirmidhī and others) — *"and upon it are hooks and thorns... the believers cross it like the blink of an eye, like lightning, like wind, like fine horses and camels; some are saved unharmed, some are scratched, some are cast into Hell."*

Against that traverse the light is function itself: it is *that by which they cross* (as the commentators on this verse and on 66:8 explain in detail); according to the famous report on the authority of ʿAbdallāh ibn Masʿūd that al-Ḥākim transmits, the people are given light in measure with their deeds — the light of one like a mountain before him, of another like a palm-tree, of another less, down through graded measures, to the last man whose light is upon the big toe of his foot, flickering on and off as he goes — one step in light, one in darkness — until God delivers him. The gradation is urgent practical morality, not mere spectacle: *the lumen of that Day is variable and econometric* — it can be stocked in this one, and only in this one. Once the Day arrives the ledger of photons is closed. That is precisely the discovery the next verse reports the hypocrites making — *Wait for us, that we may borrow from your light* — and precisely why this verse must precede it: we are shown light in full possession *before* we are shown the scene of its intransferability.

**"Believing Men and Believing Women": The Full Inclusion of That Day**

No one should slide past the verse's double vocative: *al-muʾminīna wa-l-muʾmināt* — the believing men *and the believing women*. On earth, and especially in the marketplace of display, spiritual rank is often narrated through public male offices; on the plain of Resurrection the light runs before the women of faith equally and individually, with no husband's light and no father's profile to share or diminish. Islam's Book insists on this even-handedness at the moments of ultimate consequence — *Indeed the men who submit and the women who submit... God has prepared for them forgiveness and a great reward* (33:35) — and it does so here at the very crest of the human story. The Prophet ﷺ pressed the equality into a saying Muslim records from ʿĀʾishah's report of the comforting words to a bereaved mother and in many another tradition — that the reward attaches to endurance and belief irrespective of the world's podiums. Every woman who ever spent quietly from v. 7's trust while history's floodlights played elsewhere is vindicated in this verse's grammar: the only light that finally matters is the one she carries herself, and on that Day it runs before her own feet.

**"Glad Tidings Unto You This Day": The Voice That Greets the Saved**

Then the procession is addressed: *bushrākum al-yawma jannātun tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru khālidīna fīhā; dhālika huwa al-fawzu al-ʿaẓīm* — Glad tidings unto you this Day: Gardens with rivers running below, therein to abide. That is the great triumph. Several notes. The word *bushrā* — from the same root as *bashīr*, "bringer of good news" — is the exact word the Qur'an uses for the best announcements humans ever hear (the birth of a son to Abraham or Zachariah); to receive it as an address on that Day means the news exceeds all former news, and the commentators note the news is declared by announcement — some say by the angels, some by the requiting Word itself — before the saved even reach the Garden gates, as though consolation must catch them on the road, not wait for arrival. The Gardens are described in the Book's signature line — *rivers running below* — the eternal undoing of thirst and drought that every desert-hearer felt in the soles of his feet. And *khālidīn*, therein to abide: the word erases the one grief all earthly joys carry, even the holiest — their ending. The sealing clause names the whole ensemble *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm*, the great triumph — in the Qur'an's repeated litany (4:13; 5:119; 9:89, 100; 61:12; 64:9; 85:11) — and "triumph" is chosen after thirty verses of commerce and loan imagery with exact intention: the ultimate accounting term, the one in which every smaller balance sheet of life is finally denominated, is winning. The believer has been invited, all sūrah long, to think of his life as capital management; here at the first sight of payout, the Book lets him call it what it feels like: *victory* — and not his own achievement alone but the garden-proclamation arriving like a herald: the great triumph, objective, official, and his.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:13

> **On the Day when the hypocrites, men and women, will say to those who believe, "Wait for us that we may borrow from your light," it will be said, "Turn back and seek a light!" Thereupon a wall with a gate will be set down between them, the inner side of which contains mercy, and on the outer side of which lies punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counterparty Scene: Those Who Assumed They'd Belong**

Where v. 12 showed the believing men and women moving with their light, this verse holds the camera on the second crowd — *al-munāfiqūna wa-l-munāfiqāt*, the hypocrites, men and women — and the pairing with v. 12 is a deliberate mirror: the same two words (*men and women*) applied to both groups announce that hypocrisy, like faith, is a perfectly gender-equal human possibility; piety has no demographic, and neither does its counterfeit. Hypocrisy in the Qur'an's technical sense — inward unbelief wearing the outward signs of Islam for advantage — had been, as the sūrah's introduction in the Madinan census showed, a political-religious survival strategy in Madinah, where a man could attend the mosque, marry, inherit, and campaign with the believers while privately waiting for Islam to collapse (as 4:141 depicts: *those who wait upon you, and if a victory comes to you from God they say, "Were we not with you?"*). Spiritual logic requires only one premise for such a life to seem sensible: *that the appearances are the whole of religion* — that if the outward matches, nothing further can ever be audited. The sūrah has already closed that premise at v. 6 (He knows what lies within breasts). This verse now dramatizes the closing: the moment the hypocrite discovers that of the three currencies visible that Day — light, direction, and gate-access — he possesses none, and that the one he planned to draw upon, the believers', is subject to a law of no-transfer.

**"Wait for Us": The Tragedy of the Loan That Cannot Be Made**

Their cry — *unẓurūnā naqtabis min nūrikum* — carries a deft ambiguity the commentators untangle. *Unẓurūnā* comes from the verb of looking and of waiting alike: "Wait for us" (as al-Jalālayn's alternate reading *anẓirūnā*, grant us respite, makes plain) or "Look toward us," since, as al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, and Ibn ʿAjībah explain, the believers' light shines *ahead* of them, and to catch any glimmer of it the hypocrites would need the believers at least to glance back. The verb *naqtabis* is exquisitely chosen: from qabasa, *to take a live coal or struck flame from another's fire* — the exact verb for a man arriving at a neighbor's with a cold hearth asking to borrow embers. The image is built on long familiarity: in the desert night, fire is life, and fire *can* be shared — a coal passed from one nook to another lights both without diminishing the first. And the Qur'an itself had used this very verb positively, when the family in the dark says *wait, I perceive a fire; perhaps I will bring you from it a burning coal (qabas)* (20:10). Everything about the request is reasonable in the world of hearths — *and precisely therefore* the answer arrives as a devastation: *qīla irjiʿū warāʾakum fa-ltamisū nūran* — it was said, "Turn back and seek a light." Turn back to where? The commentators record the somber possibilities: back to the place where the believers first received their light — the site of the standing, the accounting, now closed; or, read with full finality, back to the *world* — the only jurisdiction in which light is for sale — which no longer exists. A responder on the plain offers a direction like a bystander's conventional "go back and get one" — except the store has been demolished with the world. The instruction is not mockery; it is the plainest statement of an economy's closing hours. Every tolerated postponement of repentance in life — *after this project, after this season, when things settle* — is revealed here in its true form: a man postponing his coal-borrowing to after the fire is gone.

**The Wall With a Gate: The Most Haunting Architecture in the Book**

Then the verse performs its famous separation: *fa-ḍuriba baynahum bi-sūrin lahu bāb; bāṭinuhu fīhi al-raḥmatu wa-ẓāhiruhu min qibalihi al-ʿadhāb* — thereupon a wall with a gate is set down between them, its inner side containing mercy, its outer side, fronting them, punishment. The wall (*sūr*) — the great barrier Mujāhid and the earliest commentators identified with the veil of the Heights: *Between them shall be a veil (ḥijāb), and on the Heights (al-Aʿrāf) shall be men who know each by their mark* (7:46) — is no mere partition but the final geometry of justice: mercy and punishment now occupy adjoining jurisdictions, separated by a single structure, with a *gate* in it. Note the details the Book insists on. The wall *has* a gate — the mercy-side is not a conceptual elsewhere but a walled garden-city with a visible door; the hypocrites can *see* through or toward it, which al-Bayḍāwī takes as a refinement of their anguish: perpetual visibilty of the felicity they forfeited. Mercy is on the *bāṭin*, the inward side; punishment on the *ẓāhir*, the outward side, *from their direction* — the hypocrites stand on the outside of the inward, faces to the wall. One cannot, the sūrah teaching this scene rightly noted, fail to hear in bāṭin/ẓāhir the entire moral grammar the chapter has been building since v. 3: God is the Outward and the Inward; faith is an inward made outward; hypocrisy was an outward holding no inward at all — and now, in the architecture of eternity, the hypocrite is stationed forever on the outside of things, staring at the surface of a mercy whose interior he never entered. The punishment fits the crime with a terrible aptness: the man of surfaces is left with one surface — a wall — between him and everything real.

**The Light, the Wall, and the Long Hadith of Intercession**

The gate in the wall keeps, however, one door of theology open, and the commentators (al-Qurṭubī records the reasoning, and the tradition's books of the Last Day lay it out) connect it with the vast hadith literature of the *shafāʿah*, the Prophet's intercession for the sinners of his community — for the wall separates hypocrites from believers, but many a believing monotheist arrives at the Fire's edge with light dim and deeds mixed, and for *them* a gate in the side of mercy is precisely what the promise requires. In the long hadith of intercession — the great narrative assembled in al-Bukhārī (6565, 7510) and Muslim (182, 183, 193) from Abū Hurayrah and Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī — mankind seeks intercession through Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, until they reach the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, who is given leave to intercede, and the sequence of divine commands then lifts people from the Fire in descending measures of faith:

> *"Bring out of the Fire whoever has in his heart a dinar's weight of faith... then whoever has half a dinar's weight... then an atom's weight of faith"* — until God says: *"Remove from it whoever said 'There is no god but God' and has in his heart a barley-corn's weight of good"* (cf. al-Bukhārī 22, 7510; Muslim 193).

And in the hadith Muslim transmits from Jābir (183), intercession's reach is stated for the whole community: *people will be brought out of the Fire after they have been burned in it*, cast on the river of life and made to grow as a sprout grows in the flood's debris — entering the Garden scraped and healed, the *jahannamiyyūn* (the "Hell-dwellers," so-named among the people of Paradise) — as the hadith that al-Bukhārī and Muslim report of their entry conveys. The wall of 57:13 is thus not the end of God's outreach to the monotheist wrongdoer; it is the end of the *hypocrite's*. Between the counterfeit Muslim and the sinning Muslim stands a difference that no sociology of religion can detect, and that Day's wall will draw it in stone.

**The Dead Lesson for the Living: Light Must Be Carried In the First Person**

For the living reader, the verse converts into a single comprehensive warning: *build light, not proximity*. The hypocrites' calamity was not lack of information or lack of society — they had attended every gathering, knew every slogan, could quote the community's idiom better than some of the sincere. What they never possessed was the object that transfers only by creation, not contact: light kindled in the chest. Their world taught them — as ours teaches us with far greater sophistication — that association is a currency: the right circles, the right affiliations, the publicly identifiable markers of belonging; and the Day of the wall reveals the terminal worthlessness of all of it. The believer takes from this neither smugness nor paranoia but a discipline: every act done in secret, every meter of private remembrance, every dirham spent in the darkness of v. 11's goodly loan, every forgiven slight and swallowed retort, is light struck in one's own hearth — the only energy that will matter when all coals must be one's own. And where the hypocrite hears *turn back and seek*, the living hear it as the sweetest possible present-tense instruction: while the world stands and the store of light is open and the first grease of deeds still flames — go back now, and seek.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:14

> **They will call unto them, "Were we not with you?" They reply, "Indeed! But you tempted yourselves, bided your time, and doubted; and false hopes deluded you till the Command of God came, and the Deluder deluded you concerning God."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Were We Not With You?": The Last Argument of the Outward Life**

The scene continues: after the wall falls, the hypocrites call across to the believers with the only claim they ever had — *a-lam nakun maʿakum?* — "Were we not with you?" It is the entire defense of the outwardly affiliated, compressed into four words: we prayed in your rows, spoke your formulas, bore your labels, stood in your assemblies, shared your functions; our location was among the believers, so our lot should be theirs. The defense treats religion as a matter of juristic address — as if the question at Judgment would be *where were you standing?* The believers' reply, however, is one of the most considered speeches in all revelation: they concede the externality — *balā* — "Indeed! You *were* with us, outwardly, in the mosques, in the markets, in the ranks" — and then they deliver the autopsy of a counterfeit life in four clauses, each naming a separate and progressive disease of the heart. The listeners of the earliest generation read the verse and trembled not at the hypocrites only but at the sheer applicability of the four diseases, because the four can afflict, in lesser degrees, persons inside faith itself — as the commentators read v. 14 through the light of v. 16, where the believers themselves are reproached and warned against the subtler versions of these same corruptions.

**The First Disease: You Tempted Yourselves**

*Fa-tantum anfusakum* — wait: the text says *fatantum anfusakum* — *you tempted yourselves*, or: you brought trial, testing, *fitnah* upon your own souls. Fitnah is trial in both senses — ordeal and seduction — and the reflexive form is the scandal: no external persecutor did this to them; they *themselves* generated the ordeal, by living so long in double vision that the double vision became their sight. Al-Ṭabarī explains: they threw themselves into the trial of hypocrisy; Ibn ʿAbbās's school takes it as *you tested your own souls with doubt*; and the deeper commentators add: every time a man's actions deny what his tongue affirms, his soul suffers a small fitnah — an erosion of the instrument by which truth is recognized — until, one day, the instrument no longer works and the man can no longer even *see* what faith requires. The sinner who knows he sins retains the possibility of repentance because his fitnah-detector still registers distortion; the hypocrite has corrupted the detector itself. This is why the tradition treats sincerity — *ikhlāṣ* — as the immune system of religion: not the absence of falls, but the maintenance of the instrument that notices falls. The first clause of the believers' reply therefore names not an act but a *wrong relationship with one's own soul*: they turned their hearts into laboratories of self-deception, and ran the experiment for years.

**The Second Disease: You Bided Your Time**

*Wa-tarabbaṣtum* — you lingered, watching and waiting, *biding your time* — from rabasa, to lie in wait for an outcome. The Qur'an had already exposed this posture in Madinah's politics: the hypocrites were the hedgers, holding positions on both sides of history, *who wait upon you, and if a victory comes to you from God they say, "Were we not with you?" But if the disbelievers have some success, they say, "Did we not overwhelm you?"* (4:141). Spiritually, the disease is larger than seventh-century politics; it is the permanent temptation to treat God as one plausible power among others, and truth as a portfolio position. The tarabbuṣ-diseased man *calculates* the faith rather than commits to it: he watches which way the wind of advantage blows, maintains his mosque-seat as an option, keeps his unbelief warm as an exit, and never lets either loyalty become total — because totality forecloses the other side's spoils. The Book elsewhere names this straddling *the wavering* — *wavering in between, belonging neither to these nor to those* (4:143) — and the psychology is recognizable in every age: the man whose religion is real but always *on trial*, provisionally adopted pending further data about its worldly payoff; the man whose prayer is contingent, whose loyalty lasts exactly as long as the community remains ascendant. Al-Ṭabarī notes the second shade: *tarabbaṣtum* also conveys that they waited for misfortune to befall the believers — the hedger's secret hope becoming the hedger's secret wish. Biding time thus decays, if persisted in, into wishing ill; indifference to God's cause matures quietly into hostility toward it. The waiting-room of the heart has no neutral furniture in it; whoever lingers there eventually rearranges it against the believers.

**The Third Disease: You Doubted**

*Wa-rtabtum* — you fell into *rayb*, doubt — the inner corrosion of certainty. The Qur'an always tracks doubt in hypocrisy's company: *In their hearts is a sickness... in their hearts is doubt, so they waver in their doubt* (9:45). Doubt here is not the honest questioning of the seeker — which the Book honors, since it built a sūrah's answer upon *Have you considered...? will you not see?* — but the manufactured hesitation of the man who does not wish to reach a conclusion, because conclusions obligate. So he keeps matters in suspension, feeding on ambiguity as though it were humility. The fourth disease follows: *wa-gharratkum al-amāniyy* — and false hopes deluded you — *amānī*, vain longings, wishful prospects: *plenty of time yet; repentance is a door always open; God is Merciful — He will surely forgive such as us; the end is far away; the comfortable arrangement of the present will keep*. These amānī are the anesthetic the sinner applies to his own mortality, and the sūrah is about to name their pharmacology: they work — they really do pacify the conscience — *until the Command of God comes*: the moment of death, in the first instance, when amānī expire mid-thought and no time remains to repent (which is the Book's rule: *repentance is not for those who do evil deeds until, when death approaches one of them, he says, "Now I repent"* — 4:17–18), and the Day of Judgment, in the second, when the period of all amendments is closed.

**"The Deluder Deluded You Concerning God"**

The last clause gathers all four diseases under their chief agent: *wa-gharrakum bi-Allāhi al-gharūr* — and the Deluder deluded you concerning God. Al-gharūr, on the intensive form, is Satan — the Book's name for him in exactly this role: *O mankind! Let not the life of this world delude you, and let not the Deluder delude you concerning God* (31:33); *indeed Satan is a deluder of men* (35:5–6). The delusion is always *bi-Allāh* — *concerning God* — the special shape of satanic fraud in matters of religion: not immediately that there is no God (a denial too crude for the religiously formed), but that God *is such as will not mind*: God is merciful — endlessly — therefore the postponed repentance will be accepted; God is forgiving — therefore deliberate sin carries no risk; God is generous — therefore accounting is a formality. Satan's last and subtlest theology, in other words, is a *misuse of mercy* — building a presumption upon the names of compassion (which Name-pairs like *Kind and Merciful* in v. 9 describe truthfully) until mercy itself becomes the solvent of obedience. The scholars read the alternate word-form too: read as *al-gharūr* it can equally denote delusion itself — self-generated, the sinner's own wishful presumption — and al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, and al-Baghawī preserve both readings, because both run in fact: Satan whispers, and the self wills to be whispered to. Ibn Taymiyyah's generation of scholarship formulated the doctrine in its mature precision when parsing *presumption* (tamannī) against *hope* (rajāʾ): hope resembles presumption exactly, except in one member — *hope works, presumption idles*; hope is planting in prepared soil, presumption is standing over untilled land expecting the harvest anyway. The four diseases of the hypocrite are thus, in the anatomy this verse leaves the believer: manufacture no fitnah for your soul (keep your detector uncorrupted), bide no time (decision is worship), doubt not the plain (deliberate vapidity is not humility), and take no sweet promise as a substitute for planting (God's mercy greets the runner, not the lingerer). Then the Command of God will find you mid-race, rather than mid-postponement — and the voice across the wall will have nothing to reply to you, for you will be on the light side of it.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:15

> **So this day no ransom shall be taken from you, or from those who disbelieved. Your refuge shall be the Fire; it shall be your master. What an evil journey's end!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No Ransom: The Collapse of the Last Strategy**

The verse is the spoken verdict that lands on the hypocrites after the believers' four-clause autopsy: *fa-l-yawma lā yuʾkhadhu minkum fidyatun wa-lā min alladhīna kafarū* — So this day no ransom shall be taken from you, nor from those who disbelieved. Ransom — *fidyah* — is the Book's word for the price by which one buys oneself out of a fate: the compensatory payment, the substitute offered, the fine for exemption. The Qur'an had announced this market's closure on the general public of the Last Day from its earliest sūrahs: *Fear a Day when no soul shall requite another soul in anything, and no intercession shall be accepted from it, and no compensation (ʿadl) taken from it, nor shall they be helped* (2:48); and its twin, *no ransom (fidyah) shall be taken from it* (2:123; cf. 5:36). The hypocrite's whole life-strategy was, at bottom, a confidence in *ransom-in-principle* — the belief that whatever the reckoning turned out to be, something on hand would settle it: affiliation, family, wealth, eloquence, excuses, the borrowed light of v. 13. The verse's reply completes the demolition that began there: there is *nothing on hand*, because Day has no market, no negotiators, and no substitute goods. Economically, it announces the terminal condition every earthly defendant dreams the law would never reach: a court that hears no settlement offers.

Psychologically, the closure of ransom is the last comfort-removing wall of accountability — the removal, one by one, of the cushions with which men pad the future. Throughout life, safety-nets abound: mistakes are fixable, debts renegotiable, bridges rebuildable, injuries compensable; the moral intuition that *everything can eventually be bought back* sinks into the soul as a supposedly merciful axiom. The Qur'an pulls that axiom out by the root at its own appointed hour — only at *this* Day, mark — because the mercy of the present world is precisely that ransom *still functions here*: repentance, restitution, apology, reform, charity, *good deeds effacing evil deeds* (11:114) are the ransom-currencies of the interval, all freely exchangeable now, all to be demonetized at the arrival of the Command of God. The verse therefore legislates with the world's most urgent time-sensitivity: *act while ransom exists*. In the Prophet's teaching this urgency took the plainest retail form ever uttered about the Hereafter:

> *"Save yourselves from the Fire, even with half of a date; and whoever does not find even that, then with a good word."* (al-Bukhārī 6540; Muslim 1016)

Half a date — the smallest spendable unit of the seventh-century charity market — is accepted today as ransom; no mountain holds value tomorrow. The whole of prudence is compressed in the asymmetry.

**"Your Refuge Shall Be the Fire": The Grammar of Inescapability**

Then the sentence of place: *maʾwākumu al-nār* — your refuge is the Fire. *Maʾwā* is the word for a home where one repairs, a shelter, the place one returns to at evening; its use for the Fire is the Book's terrible irony at its most distilled — the same word describes Paradise elsewhere (*the Garden is the refuge, maʾwā*, 79:41; 32:19), and the Quran makes the two meanings stare at each other. Shelter is where a man is *safe*; the Fire is the only address left them, and so it is *called* their shelter — the home that fits what they built. The commentators parse the next clause — *hiya mawlākum* — it shall be *mawlā* of you — with the double valence the word carries in Arabic: master/patron *and* nearest kin, closest associate; the word that elsewhere means the protecting guardian (*God is your Mawlā — how excellent a Mawlā and how excellent a Helper*, 8:40) is here applied to the Fire. Ibn ʿAjībah and al-Ṭabarī unfold the two blades: the Fire is their *master* — it owns them, rules them, permits them nothing — and their *nearest one*, ūlā bihim, *most fit for them* — the companion their inward state was always courting. And note the furthest reach of justice in that second sense: God does not sentence souls to an alien place; He delivers each to its *mawlā*, its nearest affair: the soul that lived for the world dissolves with the world perishing; the soul that lived in the Fire's fuels — spite, pretense, greed — is handed to the fire as one returns a thing to what it belongs to. Which is why the cry the Book elsewhere records from that side has the self-indictment in it — *Our Lord, remove us from it, and if we return, then we are indeed wrongdoers* (23:107): by then even the punished utter the true predicate of the sentence.

**"What an Evil Journey's End"**

And the seal — *wa-biʾsa al-maṣīr* — what an evil journey's end, what a wretched destination — is grammar the sūrah's readers can now fully hear: *maṣīr*, from ṣāra, *to become, to arrive*, the word for the endpoint of all becoming. This sūrah has been speaking about arrival and return from its fifth verse (*unto God are all matters returned*), and v. 15 is simply the announcement, on the far side of the wall, of the concept's dark application: there is a *maṣīr* for every path, and this one is declared *biʾsa* — evil, wretched — in the divine court's own diction. All of Islam's ethics are, in a sense, a schooling of the imagination about destinations: *the wicked have an evil maṣīr* is repeated as refrain (see 5:60 and this sūrah's adjacent images), and the believer is trained to measure options not by their opening but by their *maṣīr* — a habit that rewires every greed, grievance, and shortcut at onset. The Prophet's ﷺ guidance to the dying was to help them say *lā ilāha illā Allāh* (Muslim 917): the last arrival-provision. And to the living, his daily worship kept the destination in hourly view: *Glory to Him in whose hand is the kingdom, and unto Him you shall be returned* — the verse the community recites every morning and evening in al-Mulk (67), so that no day ends un-closed by the thought of the end. Verse 15 stands at the exact middle of Sūrah al-Ḥadīd as its valley of shadow: it is the last word on the hypocrites, and the silence that follows it — the pivot at v. 16 — is where the Book turns from warning the *seemers* to healing the *sincere*: those who still have time to ensure their arrival differs, because their ransom-currencies are still legal tender, the gate in the wall still operates from this side, and the journey has not yet been walked out to its end.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:16

> **Has not the time come for those who believe for their hearts to be humbled to the remembrance of God and the truth that has come down, and to be not like those who were given the Book aforetime? But the span of time was too long for them, such that their hearts hardened and many of them are iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Searches Every Believer**

After the closed door of v. 15, the sūrah turns — with a suddenness that the first audiences felt in their chests — away from the hypocrites and directly at the believers: *a-lam yaʾni li-lladhīna āmanū an takhshaʿa qulūbuhum li-dhikri Allāh* — *Has not the time come* for those who believe that their hearts should humble themselves to the remembrance of God? The verb *yaʾni* (from a-n-y, the arrival of a thing's *appointed moment*, its full ripeness) is not mere chronological asking; it is the grammar of a thing overdue, ripe, knocking — "has not the hour struck?" The earliest generations of Islam received this as a direct address, as a word *for them*, first of all men. Ibn Masʿūd — among the very first believers — said, in the report the commentators al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Ālūsī, Ibn ʿAjībah and al-Zamakhsharī all transmit:

> *"There were but four years between the time we entered Islam and the time this verse came to reproach us."*

Four years — barely long enough to learn to pray with understanding — and the believers are already being reproached for the hardness that had begun to crust; and others among the early authorities (Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report cited by Ibn Kathīr and Ibn ʿAjībah) placed the address at Madinah, about the thirteenth year of revelation, when the hearts *had grown tepid* amid the comforts and politics of the established state. The two reports are not in contradiction but in sequence: the disease returns with every stage of ease, and the remedy must be re-administered in each. The report is among the most disquieting in the whole literature for every comfortable century of Muslims since: if the Companions — the first and finest of believers — could need this question after four years, no one after them is safe from needing it sooner.

**"Humbled": The Word the Prophet Dreaded Losing**

The verb *takhshaʿa* is drawn from *khushūʿ* — the lowliness, soft intentness, and submission of the heart — and the tradition's estimation of this quality stands very nearly at the head of all the virtues. The Prophet ﷺ made its absence one of the four things he sought refuge from *by name* — the supplication Muslim records in his Ṣaḥīḥ from Zayd ibn Arqam:

> *"O God, I seek refuge in You from knowledge that does not benefit, from a heart that is not humbled (qalbin lā yakhshaʿu), from a soul that is not satisfied, and from a supplication that is not answered."* (Muslim 2722)

Notice the quartet: unbenefiting knowledge, humbleless heart, insatiable soul, unanswered prayer — the first and third are diseases of *gathering* (knowledge and appetite), and the heart's non-submission stands between them as the root from which both grow. And the tradition contains the celebrated forecast about the community's latter days, reported by al-Ṭabarānī and graded acceptable by the hadith critics, whose place in this commentary al-Shaʿrānī's mention has made famous:

> *"The first thing to be lifted from this religion is khushūʿ — humility of heart — until you scarcely see a single humble-hearted person."*

*The first* thing — before knowledge is lifted, the Prophet said in its paired hadith (Bukhārī 100: knowledge will be taken away, and ignorance will settle) — humility of heart goes first, the canary of the religion: long before doctrine is forgotten in a community, the soft heart that doctrine was for has already departed, which is why men continue to recite with precision long after the recitation has ceased to pierce them.

**"The Remembrance of God and the Truth That Has Come Down": Heart, Tongue, and Book**

To what should the hearts humble themselves? The verse names two recipients of the softening: *li-dhikri Allāh* — the remembrance of God — *wa-mā nazala mina al-ḥaqq* — and that which has descended of the truth. The commentators take the pair in its natural reading, with the two objects overlapping and reinforcing: the remembrance (dhikr) is God Himself mentioned — as the Book says, *Only they are believers whose hearts quake when God is mentioned* (8:2) — and also the Qur'an itself, whose own revealed names include *the Remembrance* (al-Dhikr, 15:9; 21:50); and al-Rāzī notes that "what has come down of the truth" in this address is, preeminently, the Quran. The joining teaches the believer the correct use of both instruments of softening: dhikr of the tongue-and-heart, and the recited Book — the one free, unscripted, available in every heartbeat; the other a fixed text delivered from above — and the heart is to go soft under *both*: under the unbidden remembrance and under the deliberate reading. The tradition's greatest symptom-test of health runs on exactly these points: hearts at peace in remembrance — *Verily in the remembrance of God do hearts find peace* (13:28) — and skins and hearts opening to the recitation —

> *"God has sent down the most beautiful discourse, a Book consistent, oft-repeated, at which the skins of those who fear their Lord crawl, then their skins and their hearts soften unto the remembrance of God."* (Qur'an 39:23)

**Earlier Communities as Mirrors: The Hardening of the Long Span**

The verse then gives the believers the case-file of their predecessors as a warning: those given the Book aforetime — the Jews and the Christians — for whom *the span was too long* (ṭāla ʿalayhim al-amad): the ages stretched between them and their prophets, and *their hearts hardened, and many of them are iniquitous (fāsiqūn)*. The mechanism matters, and the Quran diagnoses it clinically elsewhere: hearts harden first by *time* — the long interregnum in which revelation recedes from living voice to inherited archive — and then by everything time imports: habituation (the books and prayers become furniture), custodianship without discipleship (the clergy becomes a guild, the text becomes property), negotiation with appetite (the law is adapted to the comfortable), and the settling in of *fasād* — the iniquity named as the hardened heart's fruit. The other verses in the syndrome are exact: *Then your hearts hardened thereafter — like stones, or harder still* (2:74); *for their breaking their covenant We cursed them and made their hearts hard; they distort the word from its context* (5:13). The Prophet ﷺ framed the same law communally, as the commentators record the saying: the righteous depart one by one until what remains is the sediment of people — the prophecy standing forever over the communities that inherit revelation for a living.

And then there is the single most important application: this was said to *Muslims about their own risk* — the verse's whole weight is *lā yakūnū ka-lladhīna* — **be not like them**. The pathologies described are *transportable*: had they been ethnically or covenantally exclusive, the warning would be dead content. A Muslim community can have the last revelation and the hardest hearts; can multiply the copies of the Book faster than it manufactures soft hearts to receive them; can pass years — *four years*, and Ibn Masʿūd wept — in formal practice while the inner instrument corrodes.

**The Diagnosis and Therapy of Hardness, With God's Own Prescription**

The Book's cardiological vocabulary is rich — hearts blind (22:46), diseased (2:10), rusted (83:14: *Nay, but that which they earned has rusted upon their hearts*), scaled over (2:7), locked (47:24) — and its pharmacy is equally articulated, all of it converging on this verse's two prescriptions: the remembrance of God, and the truth that has descended. The Prophet's ﷺ teaching adds the therapies of action: visiting the sick, attending funerals, remembering death — *Visit the graves, for they remind you of the Hereafter* (Muslim 977) — and his reported counsel: much remembrance of *the destroyer of pleasures* — death (al-Tirmidhī 2307, from Abū Hurayrah) — because hardness cannot survive the steady remembrance of the grave. And then, subtlest mercy of all: the very question *has not the time come?* is itself an act of divine *raʾfah* — the same Kindness of v. 9 — because a God willing to reproach is a God still at the door: the hardest heart is not the rebuked one but the one left alone. The verse ends *wa-kathīrun minhum fāsiqūn* — *many* of them iniquitous, not all — leaving the proportion open to every age of believers to decide, with its own heart, how high the number will run this time.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:17

> **Know that God revives the earth after its death. We have indeed made the signs clear for you, that haply you may understand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Hardened Heart to the Dead Field**

The placement of this verse is a lesson in divine pedagogy. Verse 16 ended with the most frightening diagnosis in the sūrah — hearts hardened like stone after the long span, many iniquitous — which the honest reader cannot hear without the question closing in from behind it: *can hardness be reversed?* Verse 17 answers not with a proposition but with a sight every reader has seen ten thousand times: *iʿlamū anna Allāha yuḥyī al-arḍa baʿda mawtihā* — *Know* that God revives the earth after its death. The imperative *know* (iʿlamū, plural, addressed to all) commandeers attention the way a teacher taps the board: stop theorizing about resurrection of the heart and *look at the field*. Every autumn the visible world dies by stages — the canopy thins, the meadow browns, the ground hardens to barrenness, and the whole apparatus of green withdraws so completely that a visitor from another season could not guess it ever lived; every spring the same ground that gave no sign of containing anything now splits with blades and blossoms beyond counting. The Qur'an uses this cycle as its standing analogy for the resurrection of bodies: *And among His signs is that you see the earth still and lowly; then when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells. Indeed He Who revives it shall revive the dead* (41:39; likewise 22:5–7, 35:9). Here the sūrah turns the same key inward: the commentators Ibn Kathīr, al-Jalālayn, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī all state explicitly that the verse reaches beyond agronomy — *so too does He revive hearts*, restoring them to humility after their hardness, exactly as He revives the fields after their death. The One whose ordinary, repeated, publicly observable practice is the resurrection of dead ground cannot be thought incapable of — or unwilling to perform — the resurrection of dead hearts.

**The Living and the Dead: The Prophet's Definition of Remembrance**

The tradition sealed this reading with one of the most reportable similes the Prophet ﷺ ever gave, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī (and with close parallels from Abū Hurayrah):

> *"The likeness of one who remembers his Lord and one who does not remember his Lord is the likeness of the living and the dead."* (al-Bukhārī 6407; cf. Muslim 779)

That is the verse translated into biography. Remembrance — the very dhikr to which v. 16 ordered the hearts to soften — is *life*, and its absence is *death*, and the difference between two men passing on the street with identical pulses and identical prospects is therefore, in God's scales, the difference between a man alive and a man not. The analogy runs into detail precisely as the verse's field does: the unremembering heart is not a bad heart or a defiant heart — rebellion at least implies a vital energy directed wrongly — it is *dead* ground: unresponsive, un-growing, neither softening under the rain of recitation nor stirring at the approach of death. And the converse is equally agricultural: no matter how long the field has lain hard, winter is not its essence. Death is a *state* of the earth, not its identity; the ground that has been dead longest is one rain away from grass. Every believer who has watched his own heart cool through years of heedlessness — and every parent, friend, and caller to God who has watched another's — is handed this as the working principle of hope: the duration of the death establishes nothing about the possibility of the life.

**"Made the Signs Clear": The Method of Evidence and the Offer of Understanding**

The verse closes: *qad bayyannā lakum al-āyāti laʿallakum taʿqilūn* — We have made the signs clear for you, that haply you may understand. Two notes deserve weighing. First, *we have made clear* (bayyannā): divine pedagogy claims that the evidences required have already been delivered with full disambiguation — the signs are not hidden, the proofs are not withheld pending someone's discovery; the preacher bears no responsibility to manufacture evidence, only to point. The Qur'an uses this formula throughout to assert the sufficiency of what has been shown (cf. 2:242; 3:118; 24:58–59), and here it comes after the single most unobscurable sign in the natural world — the seasons. Second, the purpose clause selects its faculty precisely: *laʿallakum taʿqilūn* — that you may exercise *ʿaql*, reasoned understanding. The Qur'an never asks for unexamined assent; its consistent appeal is to the intelligence of the addressee — *do you not understand?* is the Book's standing challenge to men (2:44; 3:65; 6:32; 36:68, and passim) — and the choice of the verb here quietly commissions the whole rational weight of the comparison just drawn: the God experienced reviving the visible earth in public annually is not less capable of reviving the invisible heart He already knows (v. 6: *He knows what lies within breasts*). Inference from the seen to the unseen is not a leap of faith in this Book; it is what *reason* does with adequate clarity: you have *already* watched Him resurrect, ten thousand times, in the ground under your feet; be consistent.

**Resurrection as Standing Policy, Not Exception**

What the verse contributes to the sūrah's argument as a whole can now be seen whole. The chapter's middle movement (vv. 16–24) is a treatment course for the believers' hearts, and it risks, at its beginning, the danger of all honest diagnosis — that the patient hears *hardened heart* as a fixed sentence. Verse 17 is placed exactly where the diagnosis could turn to despair, and it converts despair into prognosis: hardness is curable, as a matter of the Ordinary Process of the Lord who revives earths. The effect on practice is direct. Seasons of spiritual deadness must be read as *winters*, not as verdicts — and the response to winter is not to declare the field barren but to submit it to whatever rain is offered: dhikr, Quran, good company, the funeral, the poor, repentance — the moisture the tradition repeatedly calls the heart's revival. The believer waits in the winter of himself the way the Muslim world waits in June for the monsoon: not pretending the ground is green, but knowing with a knowledge ratified by every previous year that the hand that rains is already moving. And one last mercy before the verse hands over to v. 18: notice the verse orders nothing; it commands only to *know*. Between the reproach of v. 16 and the resumed economics of v. 18, the chapter inserts pure hope — the doctrine of an endlessly reviving God — because a religion that reproaches without reviving produces despair, and the sūrah's Lord is Kind and Merciful (v. 9) even in rebuke. The field and the heart have this in common: both belong to the heavens' schedule, not to the despair of their last season.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:18

> **Truly men who give in charity and women who give in charity and lend unto God a goodly loan, it will be multiplied for them, and theirs shall be a generous reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Hides Two Religions Into One**

The verse opens with a word on which a recitational and theological mine of meaning rests: *inna al-muṣṣaddiqīna wa-l-muṣṣaddiqāti* — truly the men who give charity and the women who give charity. The standard reading spells it with the doubled ṣād of the form V participle — *mutaṣaddiqīn*, those who practice *ṣadaqah*, voluntary almsgiving — but another transmitted reading gives a single ṣād, which the commentators record alongside it: *al-muṣaddiqīn / al-muṣaddiqāt*, *the men who confirm the truth and the women who confirm it* — from al-taṣdīq, declaring truth, affirming sincerely — the very quality of the *ṣiddīq* whose plural appears two verses below in v. 19. The two readings turn out to be in profound harmony, and the commentators treat them as layers rather than rivals: the one who gives charity is *proving* the truth of his faith in coin — ṣadaqah *is* taṣdīq externalized — and the one who confirms the truth sincerely will in time show it as spending (see 2:177: true piety includes *giving wealth, despite love of it*). Every watering of the wallet is a profession of the creed, and the Prophet ﷺ made the equation explicit in the hadith recorded by Muslim from Abū Mālik al-Ashʿarī:

> *"And charity is a proof (burhān)"* — i.e., of faith (Muslim 223) — and in another narration: *"ṣadaqah is an evidence (dalīl)."*

The theologians immediately noted the logic: a man parts with the perceptibly real wealth of this world for the imperceptible reward of the next only when his conviction of the next is functioning as present fact. Coins are testified with the hand what tongues testify with the mouth, and the recruiter of witnesses on the plain of Judgment knows no stronger attestor than the record of one's own past giving.

**The Repetition of the Double Gender: An Honor Twice Conferred**

The verse names *men who give charity and women who give charity* with full and separate mention — the same double vocative the sūrah used at the procession of light in v. 12 — and the pattern across those two verses deserves the commentator's pause. In Madinah, the primary donors of the era include names the tradition has enshrined precisely as *women's* giving: al-Bukhārī records that the women of the Anṣār and the Muhājirūn brought their jewelry for the cause (reports around the campaigns when the call for spending went out, women giving earrings, necklaces, and bangles — the reports centering on the expeditions of Tabūk and similar calls), and the Prophet ﷺ stationed the alms-advice at the two ʿĪd gatherings, saying to the gathered women, in the report al-Bukhārī and Muslim preserve from Ibn ʿAbbās at the ʿĪd prayer:

> *"Give charity! For I have seen that you are the majority of the people of the Fire"* — and, says the narration, the women began throwing their earrings and rings into the garment of Bilāl, spread for the collection (al-Bukhārī 98, 324; Muslim 884).

The lesson the verse encodes for every age is structural: the economy of God is not an affair of the male public sphere devolving small change upon the dependents; believing women stand *in the first person* as holders of wealth, stewards under v. 7's mustakhlafīn covenant, and addressees of the loan-contract of v. 11 — the actual phrase of which this verse borrows — the ones who *lend God a goodly loan* and collect the multiplication in person at v. 12's light. And their scale of credit is the same as the men's: the feminine plural sits inside the same *yuḍāʿaf lahum* — *it will be multiplied for them all*, no measure diminished.

**The Loan Extended and the Multiplication Repeated**

The middle clause of the verse repeats, almost exactly, the language of v. 11 — *wa-aqraḍū Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan yuḍāʿaf lahum* — and the deliberate return demands the question of why the Book repeats: in three verses, we have had the astonishing loan-offer twice and shall have its framing again as generous reward twice. The first occurrence (v. 11) addressed the whole community in the second-person *man dhā* crier's-call; this second occurrence *appoints the functionaries* — the men and women who answer that call — and folds into the promise the double reward-structure: *yuḍāʿaf* (it will be multiplied — the passive leaving the Multiplier unnamed out of reverence and all-embracing grace, since every agent of God's recompense participates in it: the angels recording, the Prophet interceding, the poor praying for the giver) *wa-lahum ajrun karīm* — and theirs a generous, noble reward. The parable of multiplication stands at 2:261 already quoted — the grain growing seven ears, each with a hundred grains — but the commentary tradition has always drawn attention to the parable's *limits precisely located*: the stalk, the soil, the seven ears are all *natural* figures — God then closes *And God multiplies for whomsoever He will* beyond the seven-hundred ceiling, in the passive beyond all quantification. Some commentators record the hadith of the man who gave a she-camel in charity and was promised seven hundred on the Day of Judgment; and the famous narration from Abū Masʿūd and Abū Hurayrah fix the image for the whole ummah — God takes the lawful half-date in His right hand and *rears it* until it outvies the mountain (al-Bukhārī 1410; Muslim 1014) — rears it as an owner rears a foal, with attentiveness, pride, and outgrowth beyond the investor's timescale. The believers of v. 18 — the charitable men and charitable women — are the enrolled beneficiaries of that rearing; the multiplication is not merely quantitative but *husbanded*, attended, raised.

**Ibn ʿAjībah's Inward Reading: Lending the Self**

The mystical tradition heard a deeper register in the verse, and Ibn ʿAjībah's commentary gives it one of its classic statements: those who lend God the goodly loan in the fullest sense are those who strive in His way until they have removed the love of all that is other than God from their hearts — until what is "lent" is no longer things *but the self itself*, from which point the multiplication is no longer money but *Divine lights and knowledge of the divine mysteries*: He multiplies for them illuminations upon illuminations. The sober scholar keeps both readings alive simultaneously, exactly as the tradition did from the beginning: the outbound coins of the supporters of Madinah — feeding armies, digging the Trench, ransoming the captives, funding the mosque of Qubā' and the pulpit — are the letter of the text, and the self ultimately yielded is its traversed end: the Sufi's language simply names where the trajectory of all the small loans is heading. The ḥadīth qudsī in al-Bukhārī about the servant's approach to God maps the same ascent onto devotional life:

> *"...My servant does not draw near to Me with anything dearer to Me than what I have made obligatory upon him; and My servant continues to draw near to Me with supererogatory deeds until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing with which he hears, his sight with which he sees..."* (al-Bukhārī 6502)

The economy of v. 18 and the economy of this hadith are one: from the giving of things to the giving of the hearing and the sight — the loan beginning with a coin, ending in the whole posture of the servant.

**Generous (Karīm): The Quality of God's Payback**

The verse seals on *ajrun karīm* — a generous, noble, munificent reward — repeating the signature promised at v. 11 and to be repeated again at v. 19, and the word repays a final attention. A *karīm* payment in Arabic usage is not the payment that merely meets the ledger; it is payment the way generous kings pay — beyond arithmetic, without haggling, with honor added and no grudge in the giving hand. Its use here tells the charitable man and woman something about the *manner* in which they will receive what the multiplication has grown: not a bureaucratic transfer but a *gift-giving* at which the giver will be honored and the Receiver-of-the-loan rejoicing — *God is Thankful (Shakūr), Knowing* is how 64:17 seals the identical promise. The sūrah's teaching on wealth now stands complete in three movements: God's sole ownership (v. 7's trust; v. 10's inheritance), the irresistible invitation (v. 11), the enrollment of the charitable — named, men and women — into the multiplication (v. 18). The reader who has passed through all three knows exactly where his money finally lives, and what the Qur'an supposes to be the simplest rational decision available to a mortal with capital and a fixed departure date.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:19

> **And those who believe in God and His messengers—it is they who are truthful and are witnesses before their Lord. They have their reward and their light. And those who disbelieve and deny Our signs, they are the inhabitants of Hellfire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faith Makes Truthful Ones and Witnesses: The Two Crowns**

Following directly upon the men and women of charity, the verse now names the destiny-investiture of all believers: *wa-lladhīna āmanū bi-Allāhi wa-rusulihi ulāʾika humu al-ṣiddīqūna wa-l-shuhadāʾu ʿinda rabbihim* — those who believe in God and His messengers, they are the ṣiddīqūn, and they are the shuhadāʾ, before their Lord. The early commentary tradition records two beautiful weighings of the scope. Mujāhid — the student of Ibn ʿAbbās and pillar of the first generation of tafsīr — made the boldest claim, transmitted by al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī:

> *"Every believer is a ṣiddīq and a shahīd,"* — and he recited this verse as his proof.

Others restricted the crown: the verse designates a select rank of believers who exceed the rest — those whose faith has purified their inward the way ṣadaqah outwardly proves it (Ibn ʿAjībah and others). The two scopes do not quarrel in the end: every believer holds the *qualification* of the titles (his faith is authentic, his shahādah over the nations stands), and the ranks open their full pour in those in whom the inward has caught up with the outward — in the manner the sūrah itself distinguishes the *believers* at v. 12 from the *hypocrites* at v. 13 by exactly that inward-outward alignment.

The *ṣiddīq* is the emphatic noun of *ṣidq*, truthfulness — the intensive form marking those who are steeped in truthfulness the way a *ṣalīm* is steeped in soundness; and the commentators define it with a formula that cuts exactly across this sūrah's whole concern with appearance: the ṣiddīq is *the one whose inward and outward are the same*. Abū Bakr received this very word as his title — al-Ṣiddīq — because, when the Prophet ﷺ told Quraysh he had been carried by night to Jerusalem and ascended through the heavens, and they hurried to Abū Bakr to bait him — *"Do you hear what your companion says?"* — he answered with the sentence the ummah has memorized as the definition of the quality: *"If he said it, then he has told the truth"* — adding *"I believe him concerning what is more amazing than that: the news from heaven"* (the report transmitted in the biographies and mustadrak literature, in the famous account of the Isrāʾ; al-Bayhaqī and others). The whole Qur'an's company-order of the afterlife assigns the ṣiddīqūn the station just beneath the prophets:

> *"Whosoever obeys God and the Messenger, they are with those whom God has blessed: the prophets, the truthful (al-ṣiddīqīn), the witnesses (al-shuhadāʾ), and the righteous (al-ṣāliḥīn) — and an excellent company are those!"* (Qur'an 4:69)

**Shuhadāʾ: Witnesses, and Who Gets to Be One**

And *shuhadāʾ* — witnesses — carries the tradition's explicit double range. One wing glosses it as *martyrs* — those fallen in God's path, called shuhadāʾ because God and His angels witness their entering Paradise, or because their spirits witness the felicity to which they went, in the glosses al-Ṭabarī compiles (and 3:140's *that God may know those who believe, and take witnesses (shuhadāʾ) from among you* confirms the martial sense as scriptural); under this wing of meaning, the levels of martyrdom in the Prophet's teaching are broad in shockingly merciful ways — the full rigor of the hadith at Muslim 1905–1906 on the ranks and categories, and the sweeping one in the two collections that runs: *the one killed in God's path is a martyr; and the one who dies of plague is a martyr; and the one who dies of abdominal illness is a martyr...* (Muslim; al-Bukhārī 653) — where the battlefield widens until dying defenseless under epidemic can enter the title. The other wing — and the one most exact for *this* verse, since the verse predicates witness-status of all who believe — is the witness over humankind: the believers as God's documentary evidence against the nations on the Day of Judgment — *that you may be witnesses over mankind and the Messenger a witness over you* (2:143; cf. 22:78) — the ummah of the middle standing as the standard-bearer: God's proof-carrying archive against every people to whom the message came. The Prophet ﷺ made plain that this title belonged to this community as a whole in the scene embedded in al-Bukhārī's account of the Judgment: Noah will be summoned and will say he delivered; his nation will deny; his witnesses will be called — *and they are this community* (the ummah of Muhammad ﷺ) — and they will be brought to testify (al-Bukhārī 3339, and parallels). Witness-status, then, belongs to the believer as such; martyrdom is its most literal corporeal seal.

**"Their Reward and Their Light": The Closing Pair That Ties the Sūrah's Threads**

*Lahum ajruhum wa-nūruhum* — theirs shall be their reward *and their light*. The juxtaposition is a deliberate gathering-in: the reward — ajr, the wage account, the multiplication of v. 18 — and the light — nūr, the radiant equipment of v. 12 — are now explicitly declared the two currencies of the saved. The whole sūrah has been systematically building these two accounts side by side; vv. 12–13 staged the light's visibility and intransferability; vv. 7, 10, 11, 18 grew the reward from giving; and v. 19 seals them into a single personal possessive: *theirs*. The late reader of 2026 cannot fail to notice that economics and light are precisely the two assets this life trains men to hoard separately — capital and reputation, the balance-sheet and the glow — and the sūrah announces that in the Hereafter exactly these two arrive purified and made one's own in the only form in which they outlast their possessor: *reward* (all that one gave, matured) and *light* (all that one became, made visible). And against this crowned pair the verse sets the counterparty in the economy of the place: *those who disbelieved and denied Our signs — those are the companions (aṣḥāb) of the Blaze (jaḥīm)* — no dual endowment, only *aṣḥāb*: companions, intimates, the dwellers, of the fire — the “nearest one” of v. 15 now named by its resting physiognomy. The symmetry of the verse is the sūrah's whole doctrine of the inward in miniature: those whose inward lined up with God's messengers receive an inward treasure made outward (reward and light); those whose outward denials served an inward refusal receive an outside they must forever be *inside* of.

**Truthfulness as the Technology of Belief**

For the living, the verse's practical demand is named in its operative word: al-ṣiddīq gives us the essence-level vocation — *become one whose inward and outward match* — and the verse implies the path, since the quality is predicated *of those who believe in God and His messengers* completely: belief itself, un-pared, un-balanced, un-hedged, is what makes truthfulness constitutional rather than occasional. Hence the Prophet ﷺ taught his community the working definition of its practice in the hadith from Ibn Masʿūd recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"Truthfulness (al-ṣidq) leads to righteousness, and righteousness leads to Paradise; a man keeps telling the truth until he is recorded with God as a ṣiddīq. And falsehood leads to wickedness, and wickedness leads to the Fire; a man keeps lying until he is recorded with God as a liar."* (al-Bukhārī 6094; Muslim 2607)

Note what the hadith and the verse each build: *vocations become names*. The man who practices truth "until" is *recorded* a ṣiddīq — at some point, in the ledger's authority, the adjective hardens into his identity. Verse 57:19 announces the moment on the other shore at which the recorded names are read out — the ṣiddīqūn and shuhadāʾ crowned in public before their Lord, reward and light delivered — and it tells the living exactly how those names get written: believe in God and His messengers as one thing, without the tariff-wall the hypocrites built between profession and heart, and give until giving has made testimony of you.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:20

> **Know that the life of this world is but play, diversion, ornament, mutual boasting among you, and vying for increase in property and children—the likeness of a rain whose vegetation impresses the farmers; then it withers such that you see it turn yellow; then it becomes chaff. And in the Hereafter there shall be severe punishment, forgiveness from God, and contentment, and the life of this world is naught but the enjoyment of delusion.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Five Rooms of the House of This World**

The verse begins with the imperative of v. 17 repeated — *iʿlamū* — *Know!* — as though to say: what follows must not merely be heard but metabolized. The commentators have always attended closely to the fivefold dissection that follows, for the five terms name the five descending chambers of worldliness as the Quran sees them, each a fuller maturity of the disease: *laʿib* — play (the world as recreation, the adult's game with no stakes admitted); *lahw* — diversion (that which pulls attention from assembly of the Hereafter — the Word is elsewhere set against the remembrance of God explicitly, as at 63:9 and 31:6); *zīnah* — ornament (the world as display-case, adornment for the eye: *We made whatever is upon the earth an adornment for it, that We may try them* — 18:7); *tafākhur* — mutual boasting (the world as status-tournament, in which one matches one's house, title, lineage, and portfolio against others — the Book's word for the same drive elsewhere is *fakhūr*, which God precisely does not love, 31:18 and the end of v. 23, just ahead); and *takāthur fī al-amwāli wa-l-awlād* — vying for increase in wealth and children (the world as accumulation-race, whose title verse elsewhere is a whole sūrah: *Vying for increase has distracted you, till you visit the graves* — 102:1–2). The critics of the heart in the commentary tradition read the five as a *descending staircase of seriousness*: play is the least invested and knows itself to be play; diversion is the abdication of attention; ornament begins the investment of meaning; boasting begins the investment of self; takāthur is the total claim — *my* property, *my* progeny, the world not as theater but as empire. And note the objects selected for the summit of the staircase: wealth and children — the two loves over which a man is readiest to sell his Hereafter, because they are the two things in this life that most perfectly counterfeit *survival beyond death*: the fortune that outlives the hand and the offspring that outlives the name.

The Quran elsewhere assigns these five their adult seriousness under the category that governs them all — enjoyment of the world — and locates the same whole physiology in one pronouncement at 3:14: *Made to seem fair unto mankind is the love of passions: women and children, and heaped-up heaps of gold and silver, horses of mark, cattle, and tillage. That is the enjoyment of the life of this world, and God — with Him is the most beautiful place of return.* The diagnosis is not that these five are vile — each is a created good and each is put to righteous use daily — but that they *are the world* in the exact sense in which the world, taken as the whole, is a treadmill: movement within a closed frame, recursive, without destination.

**The Parable of the Rain: The Withering Curve of Every Green Thing**

Then the Book draws its celebrated parable — one of the most perfect natural images in all literature: *ka-mathali ghaythin aʿjaba al-kuffāra nabātuh* — like a rain whose vegetation impresses the farmers — *thumma yahīju fa-tarāhu muṣfarran* — then it withers, and you see it turn yellow — *thumma yakūnu ḥuṭāman* — then it becomes chaff. Each word is a stage. *Ghayth*: the rain that comes as relief after withholding — the word chosen (against *maṭar*, mere rain) is precisely the rain of deliverance, the rain meant for gratitude. *Aʿjaba al-kuffār nabātuh*: the plants that rise from it *amaze the tillers* — and the commentators, al-Ṭabarī and the Jalālayn first, note the exquisite severity in the Book's word for the admirers: *kuffār* is literally the disbelievers, and the wording cannot be accident — the same Book that warns *do not exult in what He gives you* in the next verses chooses as the stand-in for the field-admirer the very man least schooled in gratitude for rains. The sprout rises green and richly full — *impresses*, the astonishment of early yield — then *yahīj*, withers, at the moment of its fullness — and *you see it* — *fa-tarāhu*: the verb of *witnessing* forces the reader to imagine himself standing at the field's edge watching his own green turn — yellowing in patches, *muṣfarran*, pale-gold where the life retreats — and then *ḥuṭām*: brokenness — dry shattered stubble crushed under passing feet, indistinguishable from the dust that was never planted. Four acts — rain, green, yellow, chaff — and the whole of worldly glory passes between the first and the last, in a span the observer watches from one standing place, as 10:24 runs the parable in its twin: *the likeness of the life of this world is only as water We send down from the sky; the plants of the earth mingle with it... till the earth takes on its finery and is adorned, and its people think they have power over it — Our command comes to it by night or by day, and We make it a stubble, as though yesterday it flourished not.* The modern city-dweller who has never farmed watches the same parable at one remove in every portfolio and every headline, the same four acts: boom, glow, fade, chaff — and the sūrah is about to tell him what to do with the seeing.

**Not World-Hatred but Calibration: The Quran's Two Hands**

If the verse stopped at the parable, it could be (and has sometimes been) misread as declaring this world an evil — the premise of every unhealthy asceticism. The sūrah's own measure requires the correction to be named with full force. The same Quran that calls the world's life play and diversion commands, in the same breath-register, engagement with it: *But seek, in what God has given you, the Abode of the Hereafter, and forget not your portion in this world* (28:77); the spouses' enjoyment of one another is a *sign of God* (30:21); the etiquettes of the market and the plough are revealed legislation; the Prophet ﷺ — whose own comportment is the canon of the balance — said of this very world, in the hadith recorded by Muslim from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī:

> *"The world is sweet and green, and God has appointed you as stewards in it, to see how you act. So fear the world and fear women..."* (Muslim 2742)

— sweet and green: the parable's own colors; and *to see how you act*: the whole creation-assignment of the world is trial-through-beauty. So the verse's declaration *the life of this world is naught but the enjoyment of delusion — matāʿu al-ghurūr* — condemns not the world's use but its *exchange-rate error*: treating temporary drink-merriment as the estate. Ibn ʿAjībah's commentary runs the psychological autopsy that generations of teachers after him have requoted — let it stand here in its essence, for it is the classical diagnosis of the modern condition:

> *The world appears beautiful on the outside and conceals what is ugly within; like one who tricks people and then cheats them, it appears to those who pursue it as sweetness and infatuation, overtaking them little by little, until they become totally entangled in it and neglect all preparation for the Hereafter; life passes from their hands in vanity, and the world continues to delude and betray them until it brings them bankrupt to the moment of their death.*

**Punishment, Forgiveness, Contentment: The Three Real Alternatives**

The verse's end sets the Hereafter's market openly beside the world's: *wa-fī-l-ākhirati ʿadhābun shadīd, wa-maghfiratun mina Allāh, wa-riḍwān* — and in the Hereafter is severe punishment — *and* forgiveness from God — *and* good pleasure/contentment. The commentators draw attention to the three-term presentation: two sides meet in a single list, but with what a difference of shape — punishment comes as one flat severe thing, while the alternative is given *doubly* and from two directions: forgiveness *from God* (sins effaced at the Source) and *riḍwān* (His good pleasure descending upon His servants — the word elsewhere declares itself the supreme good: *God has promised the believers... good pleasure from God is greater* — 9:72). And between these two genuine estates, the world's name is spoken for the third and last time *as it truly is*: *wa-mā al-ḥayātu al-dunyā illā matāʿu al-ghurūr* — what is the life of this world but the enjoyment of delusion (the same seal at 3:185). Every man on earth holds, in effect, exactly three tradable instruments at death: the doomed one (a life taken as the whole) which converts to severe punishment; the pardoned ledger; and the Pleasure. The sūrah began by showing that everything glorifies God and that all matters return to Him; v. 20 has now priced the intervening interval honestly for whoever will *know*. The Banquet is real; the delusion is real; the parable of the field stands in every crevice of it — and the next verse, as we shall see, gives the one command that answers the whole diagnosis: *Race.*

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:21

> **Race unto forgiveness from your Lord and to a garden whose breadth is as the breadth of Heaven and earth, prepared for those who believe in God and His messengers. That is the Bounty of God, which He gives to whomsoever He will, and God is Possessed of Tremendous Bounty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Race!": The Only Imperative That Fits a Perishing World**

Directly after the verse that stripped worldliness down to its yellowing, the sūrah issues a single verb: *sābiqū* — *race!* — from sabaqa, to precede, to outrun, to get ahead of; the form III imperative — *sābiqū* — carries the sense of *racing against others*, of contest and forestalling: not merely journey, not merely hasten, but *compete to be first*. It is the same verb the Book raised to the Company of Companies — *the forerunners, the forerunners: those are the ones brought near* (56:10–11), where al-sābiqūn is the title of the highest rank of the saved; and it stands everywhere the Book wants urgency without panic: *vie with one another in good deeds* (2:148, 5:48), *race one another in forgiveness* (3:133). The psychology compressed into the verb deserves the commentator's pause: a race differs from a duty precisely in that it enlists *ardor*. Obligation can be performed cold, at the point of necessity, over a lifetime's habit; racing implies eagerness, watchfulness of rivals, refusal to dawdle, a attending to the track. God could have said *do* forgiveness-worthy deeds, or *seek* forgiveness; He said *race* — because inside the human animal there is a drive that only competition awakens, and revelation, which knows man's frame as only the Maker knows it, does not merely command the good: it *inaugurates it as a tournament*. The administrative genius is visible from the sūrah's own preceding material: v. 20 showed men's energies already racing — after play, diversion, ornament, boasting, and accumulation. Verse 21 does not condemn the racing instinct; it *reroutes* it. The race is kept; the finish is changed.

**The Double Prize: Forgiveness First, Then Garden**

And the object of the race is grammatically doubled: *ilā maghfiratin min rabbikum* — unto forgiveness from your Lord — *wa-jannatin* — and a garden. The order is instruction itself: forgiveness is named *before* Paradise, because it is its gate and condition; no one walks into the Garden carrying his ledger open. The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī) note how this calibrates the runner's attention: the one racing may think he is racing for the greenery; he is racing, in the first instance, to be *cleared* — and the clearance is declared to be *from your Lord*, personal, near, and anticipated — in contrast with the hypocrite of vv. 13–15 whose seeking ends at a wall. Then the Garden is measured: *ʿarḍuhā ka-ʿarḍi al-samāʾi wa-l-arḍ* — its breadth is as the breadth of heaven and earth. The comparison defies visualization exactly as it should: take the widest thing known — the heavens spread over the earth — and set that as the *width* of a single reward. Nearly word-for-word this phrase constitutes the praise at 3:133 — *a garden whose breadth is the heavens and the earth, prepared for the reverent* — and our verse makes one deliberate variation the commentators attend to with care: there it is prepared for *al-muttaqīn*, the God-wary; here for *alladhīna āmanū bi-Allāhi wa-rusulihi*, those who believe in God and *His messengers* — in the plural, embracing every prophet ever sent — because the sūrah stands on the eve of addressing the People of the Book (vv. 26–29) and the door must be framed wide enough for every sincere follower of every true prophet to enter through belief in all of them, capped, per v. 28, by belief in the last. And the Prophet's ﷺ traditions catch up and complete the measurements the verse leaves hanging in immensity — most startling in the report in the two Ṣaḥīḥs from Abū Hurayrah about the least of Paradise's people in station:

> *"...And verily the least of the people of Paradise in rank is the one for whom there is the like of the world and ten times over"* — the report running through the two collections (cf. Muslim 186, the long account of the last man to enter Paradise; al-Bukhārī 6571 and parallels).

When the leasehold of *the least of them* begins at ten worlds, no unit of measurement on this earth applies to the breadth-claim of the verse.

**"Prepared": The Garden as Already-Existing Appointment**

Small grammatical load-bearing clauses carry this sūrah's consolation: the Garden is *uʿiddat* — prepared, readied — in the past-tense passive: *already* prepared, before the race begins, standing made. This matches the Book's consistent insistence that Paradise exists now, created and appointed: *prepared for the reverent* (3:133), *the Garden presented to the reverent, not far off* (50:31), and the Prophet's ﷺ vision during the Night Journey describing his being shown it — the narratives in the two Ṣaḥīḥs of his entering it and seeing its palaces and rivers (Muslim 162–168; al-Bukhārī 3207 ff. and 7417). Doctrine exacts from this a tenderness: for the believer racing, the destination is not the contingent outcome of the race — something to be built by his finishing — but a completed city awaiting its appointed citizens; salvation is therefore *occupation of one's own premade home*, which the hadith of the two abodes dramatizes exactly: the Prophet ﷺ said, in the report al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from ʿUbādah and Abū Hurayrah's circle of transmission:

> *"There is no soul among you but has its seat written in Paradise or in the Fire"* — then, when the Companions asked, "Shall we not then rely, O Messenger of God?" — *"Work; for each is eased to what he was created for"* (al-Bukhārī 4945, 6605, 7217ff.; Muslim 2647).

— the freedom in *work* belonging to the next and final clause of our verse, which now comes to secure the whole.

**"That Is the Bounty of God": Grace Above Wage**

Here the verse lands its theological capstone — and the sūrah's chief thesis about salvation: *dhālika faḍlu Allāhi yuʾtīhi man yashāʾ; wa-Allāhu dhū al-faḍli al-ʿaẓīm* — *That* is God's Bounty, which He gives to whomsoever He will; and God is Possessed of Tremendous Bounty. *Faḍl* is the decisive word: the surplus, the overflowing bounty, the unearned increment — the exact antonym of wage. The Prophet ﷺ defined the principle with unmatched plainness in the hadith that the two Ṣaḥīḥs transmit from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"None of you shall enter Paradise by his deeds."* They said: "Not even you, O Messenger of God?" He said: *"Not even I — unless God wraps me in mercy from Him."* — and in its fuller parallel: *"...do good deeds and keep to the straight and moderate course, and know that none of you shall be saved by his deeds..."* (al-Bukhārī 5673, 6463–6464; Muslim 2816).

The two clauses of the verse now work of one piece: because Paradise is *Bounty* and not a payroll, the race of v. 21 is not an earning but a *reaching-toward the hand that gives*; and because it is bounty *to whomsoever He will*, no man may invoice God, and no man may despair — the Same Hand that holds the bounty dispenses to the willing-for-it, and the willingness is itself the track. This is the sūrah's studio-precision answer to the oldest anxiety of religion: deeds without grace would never add up; grace without deeds would never call; verse 21 fuses them into *race toward the Bounty*, and v. 29 immediately after the sūrah's last audience will be told that the Bounty is *in God's Hand*, where ledger-keeping ends. The mind can then rest across a double certainty: run, for the prize is real; and rest, for the prize is gift.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:22

> **No misfortune befalls the earth nor yourselves, save that it is in a Book before We bring it forth—truly that is easy for God—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ledger That Precedes the Event**

Having commanded the race, the sūrah now administers the medicine every racer needs against the track's hardest feature — its losses. *Mā aṣāba min muṣībatin fī-l-arḍi wa-lā fī anfusikum illā fī kitābin min qabli an nabraʾahā* — no misfortune strikes in the earth, nor in yourselves, but it is in a Book before We bring it into being. The scope is total in both directions: the macro scale (*in the earth* — droughts, earthquakes, floods, the withering of v. 20's field) and the intimate scale (*nor in yourselves* — illness, bereavement, failure, the private wound no camera ever sees). And the word chosen for these things is carefully generic: *muṣībah*, a "striking," a calamity-that-hits — the Book does not call them punishments or accidents, leaving each to be read in its own moral light. For the believer not every *muṣībah* is a chastisement; some are trial, some expiation, some elevation of rank — the Prophet ﷺ laid the doctrine in a manner the ummah has never ceased to quote at every sorrow, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"Nothing afflicts the believer — no fatigue, no illness, no anxiety, no grief, no harm, no distress — even the prick of a thorn — but that God expiates some of his sins by it."* (al-Bukhārī 5641–5642; Muslim 2573)

The burden of the verse, however, is located in the phrase *fī kitābin* — *in a Book* — and the clause of priority: *before We bring it forth*. The commentators, al-Rāzī most explicitly, identify this Book with the *Preserved Tablet*, the Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ — *Nay, but it is a glorious Qur'an, upon a Preserved Tablet* (85:21–22) — on which the entirety of decree down to the Day was written before creation's first act; the supporting verses make the record exhaustive: *My Lord neglects nothing in the Book* — Moses's words to Pharaoh at 20:52; *nor anything fresh nor withered but is in a clear Book* (6:59); and the verse's own content is confessed again nearly verbatim at 35:11: *none who grows old grows old, nor has aught lessened of his life, but that it is in a Book; truly that is easy for God.* The striking of the thorn in your foot this morning, then, was older than the sky.

**"Before We Bring It Forth": Foreknowledge Without Finger-Wagging**

The phrasing *before We bring it into being (nabraʾahā)* — *before We create it* — is chosen so that the relation between the decree and the event is unmistakable: the Book is not a diary being written as history unfolds, nor a record assembled after the fact; the writing *precedes* the creation of the written-about thing itself. This is the doctrinal nerve — *qadar*, the divine decree — and the sūrah is fully aware that it has put its finger on the joint where human reason most strains, which is why the apology of the verse is arranged to be *pastoral* rather than philosophical, as v. 23 immediately shows. Ibn Taymiyyah, whose centuries-later summation became the soundest guide through this thicket for the Sunni mind, held the matter to the Qur'an's own two rails with invariable discipline: God's knowledge and will encompass every event — this rail — and human acts are real acts with real doers, commanded and forbidden, prized and blameworthy — that rail; whoever denies the first rail falls outside the Scriptural portrait of God, and whoever denies the second makes prayer, command, and blame absurd. The great analogy the teachers have always used is itself purely logical: when a teacher who knows her pupils predicts — even writes down — what each will choose, the writing has not *compelled* the choice; it has *anticipated* what the chooser will freely choose. Human temporal foreknowledge at the level of educated guess already poses no threat to freedom; divine eternal knowledge, which sees all moments in one present, poses it less, for God does not *press* the event into happening — He *knows* the happening, having willed the creation of a world containing real agents whose acts are really theirs. What God wills in decree co-exists, in the Quran's own pairing, with *you do not will unless God wills* (76:30, 81:28–29) — held together, as Ibn Taymiyyah insisted, without forcing either rail curve.

**"That Is Easy for God": The Seal Against All Ceiling-Effects**

The verse inserts — mid-flow, as a parenthesis of reassurance — *inna dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīr* — truly that is easy for God. Registering every leaf-fall and thorn-prick of an unfolding cosmos on a Preserved Tablet in the twinkling of an unbegun eternity is no strain upon the One *Powerful over all things* (v. 2). The parenthesis serves two devotions at once. Against the thought that multiplicity must overwhelm the Registrator — *How could every misfortune in every soul across the whole earth be individually authored?* — it testifies that to God detail adds no labor: the small and the great are written with the same absence of effort, *not an atom's weight... escapes Him* (34:3). And against the thought that any of it was reluctant — that misfortune represents some resistance in reality against God's preference — it testifies that nothing that befalls was a defeat of His governance: it was *brought forth* by Him, easily. Sickness then is not proof of God's inattention; famine is not proof of breakdown in the high court; your worst year is not the year the ledger fell off the shelf. Nothing about evil is denied — the Book gazes at suffering with unflinching eyes throughout — but the frame around it is complete before it arrives.

**The Consolation Built Into Decree**

The tradition cemented what the verse was *for* in the most-quoted sentence of Ṣuhayb al-Rūmī's report, preserved by Muslim, in which the Prophet ﷺ says of the believer whose affairs are thus bound in the Book:

> *"Amazing is the affair of the believer! Truly all of his affair is good — and that is not the case for anyone except the believer. If prosperity touches him, he is grateful, and that is good for him; and if adversity afflicts him, he is patient, and that is good for him."* (Muslim 2999)

The verse's register is perfectly matched: the doctrine of prior decree does what no philosophy of randomness can do at the sickbed — it makes the blow *meaningful*: not a noise but a sentence in a letter addressed to me, written before my birth in the hand of the Lord who is Kind and Merciful (v. 9). The believer can therefore grieve — for grief belongs to being human, and the Prophet wept at the death of his son Ibrāhīm and said *the eye weeps and the heart grieves, and we say only what pleases our Lord; and by your departure, Ibrāhīm, we are sorrowed* (al-Bukhārī 1303; Muslim 2315) — but he grieves within the Book, not outside it, and so his grief is held grief, a river within banks, never despair. As the next verse will say: that is exactly what the recordedness was given *for*.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:23

> **that you not despair over what has passed you by, nor exult in that which He has given unto you. And God loves not any vainglorious boaster,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Clause of the Ledger: Emotional Jurisprudence**

The verse opens with the purpose for which v. 22's decree-doctrine was given: *li-kaylā taʾsaw ʿalā mā fātakum wa-lā tafraḥū bimā ātākum* — so that you do not grieve to despair over what has escaped you, nor exult over what He has given you. The commentators dwell on the architecture: the divine foreknowledge and pre-recording of every misfortune was *given as therapy* — declared to us in advance specifically to protect the soul at the two points where calamity and blessing each carry lethal dosage. The two forbidden poles map the soul's two hinges. First is *asā* — the grief that has gone beyond mourning into desolation, the sorrow of *loss-as-final-verdict*, in which the missed thing (the lost investment, the failed venture, the collapsed plan, the departed dear one) is experienced as *deserved withheld good*, evoking complaint against the Dispenser. Second is *faraḥ* in its ungoverned register — the bounding joy that swells into self-congratulation: the recipient stands inside the blessing taking credit and forgetting tenure, exactly the state in which the Book's language pairs this verb-family with ruin — *You multiplied yourselves... and boasted* — *exult not, for God loves not the exultant* is Qārūn's rebuke (28:76), the companions of the Garden fell upon *removal of the blessing through attribution to themselves* (68:17 ff.), and the prophet's remark to his wardstands at the door of every fifteen centuries: the world is sweet-and-green, and God watches how *you act* (Muslim 2742, cited at v. 20). The Qur'an itself confirms the exact pairing in mirrored terms elsewhere: *Whatever calamity strikes you is on account of what your own hands have earned — and He pardons much* (42:30) for the first pole; and *whatever good you have is from God* (4:79) for the second. Against the one stands the Book: my loss was seen, written, known, and never escapes Him; against the other stands the same Book: my gain was granted, loaned, fore-registered, and never of my own making.

**"What Has Passed You By": The Missed and the Unhad**

The precision of the first clause's object — *mā fātakum*, what *passed you by* — deserves exactness: not only what you possessed and lost, but what you sought and *never reached*, the opportunity foreclosed, the marriage not given, the rank another attained, the child not granted. Despair over losses one has suffered is hard enough; despair over goods *never possessed* — the life unlived, the road untaken — is the chronic toxicity of minds that compare alternatives in the dark. The verse's address to both as the same forbidden *despair* is of great use: the ontological standing of the unattained is here explicitly revealed as *outside my ownership*, from eternity — not torn from my hand, but never destined for my hand at all, which means its omission carries no indictment and requires no recovery. To see the unhad as *written not-for-me* is the surgery that separates aspiration — which remains good, since *Whoever strives toward good...* (2:148) — from the bitterness that devours strivers who lose. Al-Ghazālī's century of spiritual psychologists put the diagnosis into its mature form, and the sūrah's own sequence realizes the therapy: work still goes on at full tilt (v. 21's *race*), but the *emotional charge* is discharged from outcomes by Their Owner's knowledge: striving belongs to the hand's zone; outcome belongs to the Book's zone; confusing the zones is the seed of both despair and vainglory.

**"Vainglorious Boaster": The Face the Verse Is Watching**

The second half of the verse names the man of the second pole in full profile: *Allāhu lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūr* — God loves not any *mukhtāl fakhūr*: *mukhtāl*, the self-admiring swaggerer — one inflated with himself at the credit of his gifts, and *fakhūr*, the boaster — one unable to hold his blessing without broadcasting it into others' faces. The Qur'an builds this two-word portrait with care everywhere it appears (cf. 4:36; 31:18's *God loves not any swaggering boaster* — in Luqmān's counsel right beside *do not turn your cheek from men, nor walk in the earth exultantly*; and 16:23): *mukhtāl* is the inner chamber — self-admiring attribution, the blessing read as autobiography — and *fakhūr* is its outer door — the translation of self-inflation into public claim upon rank. Between them the entire psychopathology of pride is cornered: the opiner who conceives himself *worth his fortune* at its root — as Qārūn says in the Book's play-back, at 28:78: *He said, "I was only given it on account of knowledge I have"* — and the displayer who must wound others with it to feel it afterward. The Book's counter-ethic stands next door in al-Furqān: the servants of the Merciful *walk upon the earth with humility* (25:63) — the gait itself, *hawran*, tranquil and unstrutting, because the ground under a trustee is always borrowed ground. The love-negated grammar (*God loves not...*) is its own pastoral pressure: not the crude threat of punishment but the quietly devastating picture of a door of divine love closed against the swaggerer — the What has passed arrives in some lives precisely as the last schooling of this very love.

**Gratitude and Patience: The Two Sacrifices That Replace the Two Disorders**

The verse's positive program stands implied by its twin prohibitions, and the Prophet ﷺ stated it in the dictum at Muslim 2999 already cited: for the believer every affair is good — in ease he is *grateful*, in calamity he is *patient*. The Ishāq-circle of teachers compressed the total sūrah-psychology into the saying attributed to the Prophet and the early pious that patience and gratitude are the two halves of faith — the two poles of response corresponding one-to-one to the sūrah's two forbidden poles. Gratitude (*shukr*) is the blessing spent right: named as God's (*It is He who gave you this*), circulated outward in generosity (thanksgiving's obligatory hands are the visible feedings and zakāh, in the Book's accounting of 76:8–9), and terminating openly in *fear of the Giver*: *If you are grateful I shall increase you* (14:7) — where, note, *increase* attaches to gratitude, never to the mere fact of possession. Patience (*ṣabr*) is the loss spent right: complaint channeled not into despair but upward — *I only complain of my grief and sorrow to God* (12:86) is Jacob's charter of wept patience — and continuing performance of duty in the interval until the threading of night into day (v. 6) arrives. The practical litmus the verse installs into a believing household's ordinary disasters is beautifully operationable: lost job, reject letter, closed door — one asks first, *asā or ṣabr?* — the forbidden and commanded affect within the same sorrow — and promotion, windfall, applause — *faraḥ or shukr?* — the forbidden and commanded affect within the same gladness. The two prohibitions do not abolish feeling; they *convert* it — grief into patient hope, joy into grateful fear. And the verse's address remains plural — *you*, collectively — because communities also despair and exult together: nations in depression years and nations in boom years are the same verse read at scale.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:24

> **those who are miserly and enjoin people to be miserly. Yet whosoever turns away, truly God, He is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Anatomy of Double Miserliness**

The verse carries forward the description of the vainglorious boaster of v. 23, adding the social face of the man's disease: *alladhīna yabkhalūna wa-yaʾmurūna al-nāsa bi-l-bukhl* — those who are miserly *and enjoin miserliness upon people*. The behavior classified here is more than the private vice of the closed fist; it is the evangelism of stinginess, its export as doctrine. The exact phrase occurs at 4:37 amid the great inventory of social arrogance — *those who are miserly, and enjoin miserliness upon people, and conceal what God has given them of His Bounty* — and the commentators note that the Quran's eye discerns here the natural progression of the vice: the miser cannot rest as a solitary deviant. Man is not permitted, by his own conscience's shape, to be the only practitioner of his deviation — there is a social reflex in vice by which the sinner, unable to be virtuous, consoles himself by multiplying his like: *misery loves company* carries a deep Qur'anic warrant, because the closed-handed man in a generous community is condemned by every open hand around him, and so he preaches prudence, teaches frugality, sneers at softness, and dresses avarice in the vocabulary of wisdom until the neighborhood's giving slows. The circle of his safety is precisely the circle of corruption. Historically the commentators saw in the verse two audiences that map onto sūrah's entire frame: the Madinan hypocrites, whose giving was transactional publicity — as in 4:38, *those who spend to be seen of men* — and a general diagnosis of the miserliness that afflicts not only wealth but, Ibn ʿAjībah adds, *knowledge*: the learned who hoard instruction, the elders who bar the young from the keys, signaling that in every good under human guard there is a withholdable form of it and a so-called stewardship that is really hoarding in vestments.

**Turning Away: The Indifference of Heaven to the Closed Fist**

The verse's second clause is a pivot of immense theological comfort: *wa-man yatawalla fa-inna Allāha huwa al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd* — yet whosoever turns away — from belief, from spending, from the whole covenant of vv. 7–11 — *truly God, He is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised*. The construction has the air of a door slammed gently in the face of bargaining. To the man calculating what his stinginess wins him against heaven, the Book answers: *nothing, and against no one*. Al-Ghanī: the One free of all need — whose ownership is so complete that no creature's contribution adds to it and no creature's withholding subtracts from it. And al-Ḥamīd: the Praised — the One to whom all gratitude flows and is due regardless, the Owner who remains worthy of praise whether or not this or that servant renders it. The pairing disposes in one stroke of every imagined leverage. Where a human patron faced with a withholding subordinate is weakened — soliciting, punishing, threatening replacement — against God the refusal to spend is simply recategorized: you have not declined *to benefit Him* (that was never the mechanics of the case); you have declined *to benefit yourself* — for the estate still returns to Him (v. 10), the multiplication was yours to collect (v. 11, v. 18), and the light was yours to carry (v. 12, v. 19). The loss, in other words, is entirely yours, and the economy of God's kingdom is untouched: as He says at 47:38 on exactly this same battlefield, *If you turn away, He will substitute for you a people other than yourselves; then they will not be the likes of you* — with the companion grammar of 35:15 already mentioned at v. 4: *O mankind! You are the ones needful of God; and God, He is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised.*

**The Self-Sufficient and the Praised: The Two Names' Fit**

Why this particular Name-pair as the seal — and why does the sūrah repeat it in its verses' fabric? The commentators' instincts, and the placement logic developed throughout the sūrah, suggest the double seal works as the answer to the miser's two inner voices. The miser's first voice whispers to him that his withholding *matters* in the scale of powers — that his dirhams are real force, that God-who-asks needs takers, that his closed hand is a move. Against him al-Ghanī: complete independence. The miser's second, quieter voice whispers that by withholding he has at least avoided *something owed in gratitude* — that praise and thanks require wealth paid out, that generosity is the rent on praise. Against him al-Ḥamīd: praise is due Him and given Him at every level of reality anyway — the heavens, the earth, the glorifiers of v. 1 — so your hoard did not even reduce the world's thanksgiving; only your share in it vanished. The pairing recalls the deepest motive in economic piety: men give because praise and gratitude are *heavier than gold*, and men withhold because their estimate of them has sunk below the weight of the coinage. The Names re-set the weights.

**The Disease at the Level of the City and the Century**

The verse, though personal in wording, legislates also for communities and ages, exactly as vv. 22–23 did. A society whose teachers, preachers, and pundits systematically commend withholding — and every age produces the voices: the scoffer at charity as naïveté, the philosopher of egoism as the motor of all, the economist of zero-sum who declares every open hand a fool's — is the *yaʾmurūna al-nāsa bi-l-bukhl* of its century, and the Quran's diagnosis predicts its interior weather: distrust normalizing into institutions, the disappearance of the volunteer, the rise of the transactional in every room, and — in the traditions' own forecast — the appropriation of everything into the strong hand, as the Prophet ﷺ warned of the last days in the language of the belongings: *the trust (amānah) will be taken away*, so that people trade amid treachery (al-Bukhārī 6497's chain of signs) — and the hadith-record of miserliness's career-version: *no two wolves loose among sheep*, as the saying runs in al-Māwardī's and the dictionaries' preservation of the proverb-voice of the Arabs the Prophet cited approvingly in the closest form — ghulūl and bukhl devouring as predation does. The verse's counsel to the believer caught in such a century is compressed into the sūrah's own alternation: your with-ness (v. 4) stands fast; your race (v. 21) is private and open simultaneously; your wealth's Owner has no need of your age's permission; and the praising of the Praised continues overhead eternally even if your neighbors close their shops and their hearts. The miser's city can be lived in with open hands — because the hands close upon nothing in the end, and the Estate (v. 10) is inherited, in both the world's senses, by its Owner.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:25

> **We have indeed sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and We sent down the Book and the Balance with them, that the people would uphold justice. And We sent down iron, wherein are great might and benefits for mankind, and so that God may know those who will help Him and His messengers unseen. Truly God is Strong, Mighty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Three Furnishings of Every Messenger: Signs, Scripture, Scales**

The sūrah's last movement begins by stepping back to the whole institution of prophecy: *laqad arsalnā rusulanā bi-l-bayyināti* — We sent Our messengers with clear proofs — the word *bayyināt* that 98's sūrah takes its name from, covering the miracles, the manifest evidences of truth, and the self-clarifying content of the message (as at v. 9). With them were sent down two instruments: *al-kitāb wa-l-mīzān* — the Book and the Balance. The Book, as al-Qurṭubī notes, gathers all scriptures generically — Torah, Psalms, Gospel, Qur'an — revelation in its textual form. But the second instrument is the shock: *al-mīzān*, the scales, the instrument of exact weighing. The Book elsewhere places the same instrument among the great cosmic fixtures — *The heaven He raised high, and He set up the Balance, that you transgress not in the balance; and uphold the weight with justice, and stint not the balance* (55:7–9) — and declares it the very architecture of Judgment: *We shall set up the scales of justice for the Day of Resurrection, so no soul shall be wronged aught* (21:47); and 42:17 joins the two instruments in the single phrase: *God it is Who sent down the Book in truth, and the Balance — and what shall make you know? Perhaps the Hour is nigh.* Read together, the dispatch of the Balance alongside the scripture announces that revelation is not a stream of sermons but an *apparatus of measure*: a god-given standard by which right and wrong, justice and its violation, truth and illusion, can be *weighed objectively* — outside the cravings of the powerful, outside the drift of custom. Al-Qurṭubī and the mainstream gloss it precisely here as the Law (Sharīʿah) itself: the balance is the legal-moral criterion by which holdings are measured, and Ibn ʿAjībah and al-Nīsābūrī widen it to all those instruments by which mankind upholds the justice the scripture enjoins — courts, measures, tests, reasoning by precedent — anything that weighs things truly. The purpose clause then states *the telos of all revelation* in four words: *li-yaqūma al-nāsu bi-l-qisṭ* — *that the people would uphold justice* — humanity's standing up-straight (qāma: stand, establish, be upright) *in* justice: revelation's final product is not mystery but scales held level by human hands. It is impossible to overstate what that clause teaches: the prophets are sent, the books come down, and the Balance is set, all to one public end: *that mankind stands by justice*. Theology exists, in this verse, for the sake of a fair world.

**"And We Sent Down Iron": The Metal as Revelation**

Then the verse — from which the sūrah is named — performs its own turn of astonishment: *wa-anzalnā al-ḥadīd* — and We *sent down* iron — the same verb (*anzalnā*) used for the rain from the sky and for the Books themselves. The commentators were awake to the force of the expression from the earliest times: some read it with complete literalness — iron came down from above with Adam from Paradise (a report transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās in the tafsīr tradition), so that the first man's earthly craft began with heaven-sent material — while the mainstream, al-Qurṭubī among them, resolve the usage elegantly through the Book's own grammar: God says *He sent down for you of cattle eight pairs* (39:6), and cattle are plainly created *in* the earth, so *sending down* here bears its settled Qur'anic sense of *bestowal from above* — every provision descends from its Allotted decision on high (cf. 15:21: *there is no thing whose treasuries are not with Us; and We send it down only in a known measure*). The modern reader will not fail to notice — and many sincere commentators of our age have written whole essays around it — one detail of the created world that rhymes strangely with the verse: the iron of the earth's accessible crust is, in contemporary astrophysics, understood to be forged in dying stars and, in significant part, delivered to planets through accretion and meteoritic delivery in the earliest aeons; the verse's ancient wording, *sent down*, sits quietly atop that picture like a seal on an envelope. The classical meaning stands by itself without the science; the rhyme is left to the reader's reflection, not pressed into proof — for the Book operates on the heart's evidence long before a spectrometer can weigh it.

What follows is iron's own résumé in two lines — the two-fold contents of the metal — *fīhi baʾsun shadīdun wa-manāfiʿu li-l-nās*: in it is *great might* — the sword, the lance, the armor, the tool of war and defense, the state's capacity to enforce the Balance against those who would shatter it — *and benefits for mankind*: plough and scythe, nail and axe, needle and hinge — the entire instrumentation of agriculture, dwelling, and craft. The commentators' reading of the verse as the chapter's metal-logic is exact: the Scale of justice (al-Mīzān) requires iron, because justice unenforced and uncultivated is parchment; al-Qurṭubī articulates the complete nexus — the Balance declares the just, iron defends the just against the transgressor and feeds the just from the earth — the Balance, the sword, and the ploughshare are made, in this ordering, messengers' dowry to civilization. And the double content of iron matches exactly the two hands of human power held in one moral body: force (which a created order inhabited by transgressors requires to stand aright) and fruitfulness (which a created order inhabited by the hungry requires to live) — both of them under one verdict: *sent down*, bestowed, charged to trustees.

**"...That God May Know Those Who Help Him and His Messengers Unseen"**

Then the verse gives the purpose of iron's might, and it is a purpose of *knowledge*: *wa-li-yaʿlama Allāhu man yanṣuruhu wa-rusulahu bi-l-ghayb* — and so that God may know who helps Him and His messengers, *in the Unseen.* The clause has exercised the commentators in the noblest way, because God's knowing of the thing already lies beyond all question (vv. 3, 4, 6) — what then does "so that God may know" mean? The answer runs through the Book's own usage: God's knowledge *of what will be* precedes all events, but Scripture frequently speaks of God's *knowing what happens* at its occurrence — His knowledge *as the event comes into actuality and is witnessed and requited*, as in 2:178's *so that God may know those who fear Him in the Unseen*? No — precisely: 3:142 (*did you suppose you would enter the Garden without God having known those who strove among you and known the patient?*), 3:166–167, 9:16, 47:31 — where the *knowing* stands at the moment the striving actually enters existence, separating the helper from the pretender by demonstration, the way a test does not teach the Teacher but exhibits the truth *in act*. Iron is the forge on which the helpers are made known: *the sword in a man's hand under tyranny reveals what his tongue had concealed.* And the word *bi-l-ghayb* — *unseen* — receives two beautiful glosses the commentators record from al-Qurṭubī and al-Zamakhsharī: helping God and His messengers *unseen to men* — not for display (rebuking forever the hypocrite's whole economy) — and helping them *believing in the Unseen* — serving a victory not yet in sight and a Lord not seen at all: the very first credential of *the reverent* at the opening of the Book — *who believe in the Unseen* (2:3).

**Strong, Mighty: The Iron-Verse's Seal and the Government of Force**

The verse seals on the Name-pair the sūrah reserved for its namesake verse rather than its first: *inna Allāha qawiyyun ʿazīz* — Truly God is *Strong, Mighty*. Not *Mighty, Wise* (v. 1) but *Strength* first, *Might* after — in the verse of iron and war. The seal's instruction is the last lesson the sūrah gives on power: strength exists, unequivocally, in the divine order — He is Strong — and sovereignty belongs only to His strength — He is Mighty — therefore the might iron gives men is neither demonized (the pietism that pretends meekness is the whole of religion) nor deified (the politics that pretends force is its own justification): it is *subordinated to God*, held by trustees, governed by the Balance, and appearing on the books only under the account *helping God and His messengers*. In the hands of the ummah this theology produced its most remarkable historical fruit: a civilization in which carrying arms was charged with the ethics of the scale — wars its Prophet ﷺ was ordered to fight being hedged by revealed law from first to last — *fight in the way of God those who fight you, but transgress not; God loves not the transgressors* (2:190) — no killing of noncombatants in the Prophet's standing instructions on expeditions (Muslim 1731 from Buraydah: advance, fight, don't exceed, don't mutilate, don't kill children), treaties sacrosanct, booty law-governed, and — in the tradition's own celebrated reversal of Roman pattern — an empire whose chroniclers were accused by posterity of being blamed for conquest too *slow*, too burdened with scruple about the peoples' consent. Whatever one makes of each era's practice — and Muslims, being men, have both risen to the standard and fallen below it — the *norm* the iron-verse fixes is immovable: iron's purpose inside the covenant is justice served, the measure guarded, the weak defended, the help of God extended *unseen* — and the test of the sword is always the same, from Badr to any boardroom or battlefield: *was it weighed on the Balance, or only swung by the self?*

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:26

> **And indeed We sent Noah and Abraham and established prophethood and the Book among their progeny. And among them is he who is rightly guided; yet many of them are iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Fathers of the Prophetic House**

The verse begins its survey of sacred history with the two patriarchs whom the Qur'an consistently pairs at the head of the prophetic succession: *We sent Noah and Abraham*. The pairing is no accident, and the Book explains its own choice elsewhere: *Those were some of the prophets upon whom God bestowed favor... from the progeny of Adam, and of those We carried with Noah; and from the progeny of Abraham and Israel* (19:58), and *We bestowed upon Abraham his rectitude aforetime, and We knew him well* (21:51) — for Noah is the second father of all living mankind after the Flood (as the Book singles his seed out: *We made his progeny the ones remaining*, 37:77), and Abraham is the single human being from whom — through Isaac and through Ishmael — the entire open chain of later prophecy runs. Clause by clause the Mystery deepens: *We established prophethood and the Book among their progeny (fī dhurriyyatihimā)* — neither prophethood nor scripture would henceforth be scattered randomly among the earth's clans, but would follow a *line*: from Abraham, through Isaac — the prophets of Israel: Jacob, Joseph, Moses, Aaron, David, Solomon, and the long succession — and through Ishmael, in the fullness of time, the Seal of the prophets, Muhammad ﷺ, whose arrival the verse is quietly preparing its People-of-the-Book addressees to recognize. The Book details the same grant at 29:27 — *We bestowed upon him Isaac and Jacob, and We established prophethood and the Book among his progeny* — and 4:54, *We gave the House of Abraham the Book and wisdom, and We gave them a great kingdom*. The whole institution described in v. 25 — the Book and the Balance and the clear proofs — now receives its genealogy: it descended through two fathers' houses.

**"Among Them Is He Who Is Guided": The Honest Racial Arithmetic**

Then comes the clause that protects the inheritance from becoming an idol: *wa-minhum muhtad; wa-kathīrun minhum fāsiqūn* — among their progeny is he who is rightly guided; yet *many* of them are iniquitous. The construction governs the whole of the Quran's doctrine of covenantal peoplehood. Prophethood and scripture in a lineage are an *entrustment*, not a nobility: presence in the house of the prophets confers no automatic guidance, and descent from Noah and Abraham saves no one, precisely as Noah's own son perished in the flood despite his father's prophethood and plea — the crushing verse that stands as the eternal charter of this clause: *God strikes a parable for those who disbelieve: the wife of Noah and the wife of Lot...* (66:10) — and above all the exchange on the mountain of judgment: *Noah called to his Lord and said: "My Lord, verily my son is of my household, and Thy promise is true, and Thou art the most just of judges." He said: "O Noah! He is not of thy household — his is a deed not righteous..."* (11:45–46). Progeny does not transmit righteousness by blood; the line carries the *Word*, and each generation of the line must answer for itself. The verse's proportion-word is chosen with the same measured frankness: *many* (kathīr) — not all; the guided among the prophets' descendants are real and were crowned — the Messiah's apostles, the faithful men and women of the two houses, the Israelites who kept faith with the Torah before distortion, and the People of the Book in every era who answered the call — but the clause of the many stands as the verdict of history: many are *fāsiqūn*, those who have gone out beyond the bounds of obedience.

As the source commentary adds with truthfulness that belongs to the sūrah's own fearless self-examination: this is not a verdict against the *scriptures* or the prophets of earlier peoples, whom the Book honors and vindicates; it is a verdict about the humanum — the fissile instability of transmitted religion in time, the same hardening over the long span that v. 16 warned the Muslims of: the verse is therefore *Muslim-facing* before it is anyone else's. The Prophet ﷺ fastened the warning to his own community in the famous hadith the source cites and al-Bukhārī transmits in wording of unforgiving clarity, from Mirdās al-Aslamī and its companion narrations:

> *"The righteous will keep departing, one after another, until there remains but the sediment (huthālah) of people — like the sediment of barley or of bad dates — and God will hold them in no esteem at all."* (al-Bukhārī 6437, with related wordings in the collections)

**Chosen-ness Converted to Accountability: The Lesson for the Heirs of the Prophet**

Read in the sūrah's own rhetorical placement, v. 26 does two pieces of work for the final verses that follow. First, it levels the People of the Book's central boast — *we are the seed of prophets* — not by denying the lineage but by redefining its meaning: the seed bore the *Book* and was found, many of them, iniquitous; the gift was never a shield. Second, it silently installs the identical mirror before the Muslim reader, who stands in a line from Abraham through Muhammad ﷺ, and holds the last Book and the last prophethood's completion: the verse warns every century of Islam's heirs that the sūrah's own hardening (v. 16), hoarding (v. 24), and delusion (v. 20) are transportable diseases, and that the hadith about righteous departures is spoken inside *this* community. The Prophet ﷺ completed the address in the matched hadith on chosenness's true basis recorded by Muslim — recited to the people in the Farewell-era teaching against every lineal arrogance, and fixed in the community's memory: nobility is by taqwā (*Verily the noblest of you before God is the most reverent of you*, 49:13), none being carried by his father's stride — *whoever is slowed by his works, his lineage will not speed him* is the saying the tradition preserves (Muslim 2699, from Abū Hurayrah, in the exquisitely sweeping generation-by-generation list the Prophet pronounced, ending with his request to his own beloved daughter: *Ask of me whatever you will of my wealth, but I can avail you nothing against God*). The house of prophethood is a house of responsibility; its keys open the treasury of the Book and the Balance — or fall, unused, from the hands of the iniquitous, to a people who will honor what was laid down. That is the door the sūrah now opens at v. 27 upon the Lord Jesus and his followers.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:27

> **Then We sent Our messengers to follow in their footsteps, and We sent Jesus son of Mary, and We gave him the Gospel and placed kindness and mercy in the hearts of those who follow him. And monasticism they invented—We did not ordain it for them—only to seek God's Contentment. Yet they did not observe it with proper observance. So We gave those of them who believed their reward, yet many of them are iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Patriarchs to the Messiah: The Succession Completed**

The verse continues the thread of v. 26 with sovereign rhythm: *thumma qaffaynā* — then We sent successively, each at the heels of the last (the verb's root means to follow closely, heel-to-heel — an image of the prophets as one unbroken relay through history) — until the succession arrives at the climactic signatory before Muhammad ﷺ: *ʿĪsā bna Maryam* — Jesus son of Mary — and We gave him *al-injīl*, the Gospel. Every element of the phrasing is load-bearing. The title *son of Mary* is the Quran's chosen honor for him — persistent, distinctive, and doctrinal: fixing his human genealogy to his mother and so preserving both his miraculous creation (without father) and his pure humanity, in a time when some would deify him — *They have taken their rabbis and monks as lords apart from God, and the Messiah son of Mary — yet they were only commanded to worship One God* (9:31). The gift of the Gospel, al-Injīl, marks in this sūrah's succession-account the completion of the Book-line through Abraham's seed: Torah, Psalms, then Gospel (as the Prophet's prayer at v. 3 gathered them: *Revealer of the Torah, the Gospel, and the Criterion*). And then a description of the character of Jesus's community that deserves far more attention than it usually receives: *We placed in the hearts of those who follow him kindness and mercy* — *raʾfah and raḥmah*, the same two words al-Ḥadīd used at v. 9 to describe God Himself toward the believers — the divine tenderness of the Sender deposited, by the Sender's own hand, into the hearts of the followers. Every reader of the Gospel tradition recognizes the fulfillment: the Sermon on the Mount's meekness and mercy, the washing of feet, the Good Samaritan, the tenderness toward lepers, sinners, and children. The Quran describes the original Christian community as a people *formed from inside* by gentleness — not as a concession to Christian claims but as its own testimony: when Christianity itself was true, the Book of Islam says, its heart's furniture was *raʾfah* and *raḥmah*. One hears the deep honor hidden in the phrase: the followers of Jesus were, constitutionally, *the soft-hearted among the nations*.

**"Monasticism They Invented": The Delicate Verdict on Self-Made Religion**

Then the sūrah turns to the strange and debated clause: *wa-rahbāniyyatan ibtadaʿūhā* — and monasticism — they invented it — *mā katabnāhā ʿalayhim* — We did not prescribe it for them — *illā ibtighāʾa riḍwāni Allāh* — except (in) seeking God's contentment — *fa-mā raʿawhā ḥaqqa riʿāyatihā* — yet they did not observe it with the observance due to it. The commentators worked over this clause for centuries, and the mature consensus of the tradition (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, al-Ālūsī) resolves it along two readings which must be heard together to keep both the verse's fairness and its warning intact.

The first reading: monasticism as such — the desert, the cloister, celibacy, the abandonment of the world's legitimate participation — was *not* ordained by God in Christian revelation; it was a self-imposed institution the followers generated *out of a genuine motive*: *only seeking God's contentment*. Their motive was not perversion but hunger — they found the religion so much on the side of the soul, and their world so much against it, that they built extra chambers to flee into. The verse explicitly credits this: they invented it *only* (illā) seeking God's riḍwān — as though to say the inventing was, at heart, devotion reaching for more devotion. But — and here the verdict sounds — *they did not observe it with due observance*: the institution they built themselves, they then failed to sustain *even by their own rule*: history and the Quran's gaze see the cloisters filled, in time, with the very worldliness the cloister was built to exclude — property, power, appetite, hypocrisy — until the towering irony stands: a self-made piety became self-defeating, because man does not well bear burdens his own zeal invented: *what God has not laid upon the self, the self will either perform in pride or break under in collapse*.

The second reading — held by many, and carefully recorded by al-Qurṭubī — takes the syntax so that kindness, mercy, *and* monasticism were all placed in their hearts by God, and the *inventing* (ibtadaʿūhā) refers to their innovations *within* it, distortions introduced into what was given; the clause *We did not ordain it except seeking His contentment* then means God ordained of them only sincere aim. On either reading, the two fixed truths the whole tradition extracts are unshaken: monastic life as an institution was not a revealed commandment; and whatever was not commanded yet sincerely attempted still demands of its practitioners *faithful observance* — *with due observance* — which their generations went on to fail.

The Prophet ﷺ, when asked about monasticism, sealed the principle for his own community in the hadith Muslim records from Abū Umāmah and which Ibn Kathīr quotes beneath this verse:

> *"Every community has its rahbāniyyah (monasticism), and the monasticism of this community is striving (jihād) in the way of God"* (Muslim 1402) — and in a well-known companion-report from ʿAwf ibn Mālik's circle: the Prophet was asked about those who sought the solitary life, and he directed the worshipper back into the ranks of the ummah.

And the hadith of ʿĀʾishah about the three men — one vowing perpetual fasting, one perpetual night-prayer, one abstinence from women — seals the balance forever:

> *The Prophet ﷺ came (and anger rose in his face) and said: "What is the matter with people who say such-and-such? By God, I am the most God-fearing and most pious among you — yet I fast and I break my fast, I pray and I sleep, and I marry women. So whoever turns away from my Sunnah is not of me."* (al-Bukhārī 5063; Muslim 1401)

**The Psychology of Self-Made Religion and the Islam of the Middle**

There is enormous benefit in this verse for religious psychology, beyond its historical curtain-call on Byzantine monasticism. The Quran's own revealed religion is precisely *the middle nation* — *Wa-kadhālika jaʿalnākum ummatan wasaṭan* (2:143) — and the first lesson of al-Ḥadīd's verse to Muslims is not smugness at medieval monks but vigilance over the same temptation within: the zeal-driven invention of disciplines God never commanded, the piling of burdens on the self and the community beyond the revealed form. Ibn Taymiyyah spent whole treatises (notably his *al-Istiqāmah* and writings on bidʿah) distinguishing the two errors between which religious life perpetually tacks: *tafṣīl* — leaving the commanded — and *ibtidāʿ* — inventing the uncommanded — and this verse is the charter verse of the second error's exact profile: driven by (real) desire for God's contentment, incapable of being thus burdened, ending in false profession, hypocrisy, or collapse. The modern believer should test against it every project of perfection generated in himself — the man who vows practices beyond his years of strength, the community that manufactures new ladders of rank and piety-tiering, the movements whose performance of rite crowds out the heart's softness: this verse observes the whole phenomenon, diagnoses its origin even through a veil of genuine sincerity, and names both the necessary mercy (their aim was God's contentment) and the inevitable outcome (they did not observe it with due observance). And Ibn ʿAbbās's famous gloss transmitted in the classical commentaries on the end of the verse — *So We gave those of them who believed their reward* — completes the grace of the entire construction: *even amid the failure of their self-made discipline, those who believed received their wage from the Lord of the Balance* — God's justice and bounty taking from the sincere even what their sincerity wrongly built. That sentence is the sūrah's door into v. 28: *O you who believe...*

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:28

> **O you who believe! Reverence God and believe in His Messenger; He will give you a twofold portion of His Mercy, make a light for you by which you may walk, and forgive you—and God is Forgiving, Merciful—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"O You Who Believe": A Call to the People of the Earlier Books**

After tracing the prophetic succession from Noah and Abraham to Jesus son of Mary (vv. 26–27), the sūrah now issues a direct summons — *yā ayyuhā lladhīna āmanū* — *O you who believe!* — but with an address upon which the classical commentators made a decisive observation: in the context of this final section, the call is understood by the most part (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī) to be directed to *the People of the Book* — the Jews and Christians who believe in their own prophets — now called to complete their belief in the Messenger who has come after Jesus: *Reverence God and believe in His Messenger*. The remainder of the Quran witnesses precisely this address-pattern: *Say: O People of the Book! You stand upon nothing until you uphold the Torah and the Gospel and what has been sent down to you from your Lord* (5:68) — their own scriptures their ground, the final revelation their call upward. Others among the commentators (Ibn ʿAjībah, al-Qurṭubī also records) allow the address to reach every believer, summoning even the already-Muslim forward into a deeper faith — the Book elsewhere uses the same vocative to call believers *into* belief: *O you who believe, believe in God and His Messenger* (4:136) — where the object is not conversion but deepening, the call of the believer to become *more* believer: in which case the verse reproduces for all ages the sūrah's mid-course summons of v. 16. The two readings join in one highway: whether the addressee is a Christian of Najrān hearing of the Prophet for the first time, or a Muslim of the tenth generation stepping forward from nominal to devoted, the prescription is identical — *reverence God; believe in His Messenger* — with taqwā placed, as always in the Book, as the *ground* of the movement: the God-fearing lens first, then belief as the walking-course upon it.

**A Twofold Portion: The Double Mercy of Believing Twice**

The promised recompense comes in three escalating gifts. First: *yuʾtikum kiflayni min raḥmatihi* — He will give you a *twofold* portion of His mercy — a double share, two allotments: one share for the true belief they held in their own prophet and scripture, and the second for their belief, atop it, in Muhammad ﷺ — and the Prophet ﷺ identified this reward's exact beneficiary in the celebrated hadith recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī (himself formerly of the People of the Book's widening circle of converts):

> *"Three shall be given their reward twice: a man from the People of the Book who believed in his prophet and then believed in me; a slave who fulfills the right of God and the right of his masters; and a man who owns a slave-girl, educates her well, teaches her beautiful manners, then frees her and marries her."* (al-Bukhārī 97, 3011; Muslim 154)

Notice the double-layered grace: belief upon belief receives mercy upon mercy — no erstwhile history is discounted, no former covenant unremunerated. The man who served Moses truthfully and then serves Muhammad ﷺ loses nothing of the former service; it is credited and doubled, not erased. The clause is a standing refutation of every suspicion among the People of the Book that conversion would be an amnesty from blank — God asks them to bring their old faithfulness with them, and pays it at par, with interest.

**"A Light By Which You May Walk": The Third Gift of Guidance**

Second: *wa-yajʿal lakum nūran tamshūna bihi* — and He will appoint for you a light by which you may walk. The image gathers the whole sūrah's light-motif into a personal gift: the light the believers carried hidden in the breast (v. 6, v. 12), the light that runs before and to the right on the Day (v. 12), the light over the traverse whose steps differ by deeds (al-Ḥākim's report from Ibn Masʿūd). The commentators read the light at two levels which the tradition has never needed to choose between, since the one conducts to the other: the light of *guidance in this life* — the Quran's own name for itself (*a light and clear Book*, 15:1; *a light by which We guide whom We will*, 42:52) — the decisive faculty of discerning truth from confusion in the thickets of family, commerce, and history; and the light of *the Hereafter's passage* — the lumen by which the believer walks over the ṣirāṭ against the Fire's dark, as muslim's long hadith of the intercession makes the plain the dominant image of the crossing. The Book's conditioner applies to both levels with rigorous simplicity:

> *"...Or is one who was dead, and We gave him life and appointed for him a light by which he walks among mankind, like one whose likeness is in the darknesses, never to emerge from them?"* (Qur'an 6:122)

— the revived-from-death of v. 17 and the walking-light of v. 28 united in a single verse; and the antipole stands one sūrah earlier in the Quran's own verdict upon those from whom light is withheld:

> *"...Had God willed, He would have taken away their hearing and sight..." — and in the Sūrah of Light: "...he for whom God has not appointed any light has no light"* (Qur'an 24:40).

A man may see by candle, streetlamp, and screen all his life and walk, at every step where it decides, in un-learned dark: the walking-light of this verse is the faculty whose absence no wattage replaces.

**"And Forgive You": The Third Gift That Closes All Accounts**

Third: *wa-yaghfir lakum* — and He will forgive you. The last of the three gifts looks backward over the addressee's entire previous life: past denials (the People of the Book's rejection of the Prophet while it held), past mis-observances (v. 16's hardening recalled, v. 27's monastic failure), past sins of any label — *all folded* into one pardon; and the verse seals on the Names that guarantee the pardon is no concession but God's standing disposition: *wa-Allāhu ghafūrun raḥīm* — and God is Forgiving, Merciful. The pair closes the second half of the sūrah as the pair of v. 9 — *Kind, Merciful* — closed the first: mercy announced over the call at its announcement, mercy closing the covenant at its sealing. The tradition is full of the actualized form of this transition: when Heraclius of Byzantium interrogated Abū Sufyān about the Prophet (al-Bukhārī 7), the exchange ends with the emperor tracing each of the Prophet's names and commands and concluding *this is the pool of the prophets — what I knew of him* — not long after which the Prophet's letters went out to the kings of the earth — Heraclius, the Negus, Khosrow, the Muqawqis — precisely the published bidva of this verse, "O you who believe" reaching each throne in writing: *As-salāmu ʿalā man ittabaʿa al-hudā...* — Peace upon whoever follows the guidance — then the Prophet's own fiat of invitation to Islam in the letter's heart (al-Bukhārī 2941). The sūrah has thus walked the reader to the exact station of its audience: the people of the earlier scriptures standing before the final Messenger, offered double mercy, a walk-light, and open forgiveness. One last word remains — the reason behind the whole arrangement — and v. 29 delivers it.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:29

> **such that the People of the Book may know that they have no power over any of God's Bounty, and that the Bounty is in God's Hand; He gives it unto whomsoever He will; and God is Possessed of Tremendous Bounty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose of the Double Reward: So That They Know**

The final verse of Sūrah al-Ḥadīd opens with a purpose clause tied to v. 28: God promised a double portion of mercy, a walking light, and forgiveness *li-allā yaʿlama ahlu al-kitābi* — *so that the People of the Book may know* — *allā yaqdirūna ʿalā shayʾin min faḍli Allāh* — that they have no power over anything of God's Bounty — *wa-anna al-faḍla bi-yadi Allāh* — and that the Bounty is in God's Hand — *yuʾtīhi man yashāʾ* — He gives it to whom He will — *wa-Allāhu dhū al-faḍli al-ʿaẓīm* — and God is Possessed of Tremendous Bounty. The wording weaves promise and pedagogy into one: the very grant of a twofold reward to those of the People of the Book who followed both their prophet and the last Prophet (v. 28) functions as the *demonstration* of the eternal principle — the Bounty belongs to God; no people owns it, administers it, or can withhold it from another. The commentators (al-Zamakhsharī with particular clarity) describe the occasion and spiritual cause: segments of the People of the Book had objected, in rooted pride, that prophetic and religious merit — *faḍl* — was a trust of *their* house, stemming from prophethood among their line (which God Himself established there, v. 26!), and so the latecoming community — even believing — could not over-stride them in God's queue. The Quran had already recorded the gripes of this posture: their saying of the Prophet that *If only this Quran had been sent down to some great man of the two cities!* (43:31), their persistent account that prophets come only from Isaac's seed (2:90: *reserving to themselves that God should send down of His Bounty upon whomsoever He will among His servants* — the same root-word: *faḍl*). Verse 29 is the answering sovereign-decree over the whole dispute: the Bounty is not an inheritance carried in genealogies; it is in God's *hand*. And the double rewards given to whoever of the earlier covenant believe in the last Messenger is its showcase: the latecomer receives more than the old tenant — because the paymaster pays for *believing*, not for seniority.

**The Hadith of the Three Wages: God's Own Parable Commentary**

The Prophet ﷺ himself furnished the verse's most famous commentary — the parable of the laborers, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim in its full narration from Abū Hurayrah (and its near-companion from Ibn ʿUmar), which Ibn Kathīr cites immediate beneath this verse:

> *"The likeness of you and the Jews and the Christians is that of a man who employed laborers and said, 'Who will work for me from morning until midday for one qīrāt each?' The Jews worked. Then he said, 'Who will work for me from midday until the time of the afternoon prayer for one qīrāt each?' The Christians worked. Then he said, 'Who will work for me from the afternoon until sunset for two qīrāts each?' — and you are those who worked it. Then the Jews and the Christians were angered and said, 'We had more labor and less wage!' He — God — said: 'Did I wrong you of anything of your wage?' They said, 'No.' He said: 'Then that is My Bounty; I give it to whomsoever I will.'"* (al-Bukhārī 557, 2271; Muslim 1627 – wordings vary across the routes by detail, but the teaching is one).

Few parables in scripture cut deeper into this sūrah's whole subject. The structure is exact: the work is the same kind (believing, worshipping, working in God's field); the durations differ (the communities' spans as bearers of the covenant differ); the wages differ — the double going to the latest shift — and the question raised is the question in every human heart upon seeing such an account: *is that fair?* The parable's genius is the last exchange: the employer first *proves his justice* — each contracted wage delivered undiminished; no one deprived of his qīrāt — and only then deploys the doctrine of the verse: what exceeds the contract is *bounty* (faḍl), intrinsically the employer's to bestow. Justice governs the wage; grace governs the gift. Whoever was paid what was agreed can make no claim against the employer's gifts to others. And the sūrah's reader immediately feels the parable's second blade, turned toward Muslims themselves: the one-qīrāt shifts were borne by men holding *true* scriptures — the Torah and the Gospel before distortion — borne under Roman spears, exile, and centuries of stewardship; the Muslim's double qīrāt is *pure gift* — the Book will later say in another sūrah, in words of the same rank: *He has chosen you... the creed of your father Abraham — He named you Muslims aforetime* (22:78) — and the hour from afternoon to sunset is the shortest shift of all. The believer who receives the double wage is thereby positioned in the parable as himself a beneficiary of the principle: his portion is bounty too — never entitlement — which protects him from precisely the vanity the parable condemns in its earlier holders.

**The Closed Arc: From the First Glorifier to the Hand of Bounty**

And now the sūrah completes — and the commentators have always marveled at — its final architecture. The chapter opened with all existence praising God and ended with the declaration of His Bounty; began with His Names of Might and Wisdom, closes on *dhū al-faḍli al-ʿaẓīm* — the Possessor of the Tremendous Bounty. The word *al-ʿaẓīm* (Tremendous, Magnificent) is the adjective that throughout the Quran guards the ceaseless gap between God and every measure: the Throne is Tremendous (9:129), the Reward Tremendous, the *fawz* the Triumph Tremendous (v. 12) — and here it qualifies bounty so that the verse's arithmetic be driven home to the last: the Bounty's *magnitude* and the Bounty's *sovereignty* both belong to Him alone. The whole teaching of twenty-nine verses now appears as one counsel taken together: *nothing* of whatever real estate men administer — revelation's line, religious honor, the books and tombs of the patriarchs, wealth and its multiplication, light and its sight, rank and admission — is in any human hand: the hand is One. The People of the Book are thereby comforted as much as corrected: the doctrine that dethrones their seniority also promises them, upon belief in the last Messenger, *more* than their seniority ever paid — twofold mercy (v. 28). And the Muslim, heir of evening-shift and double wage, finishes the sūrah dethroned from every parsimony of v. 24 and every boasting of v. 23: his Islam itself was *faḍl* given when he was late; his continuing in it is *faḍl* moment by moment; and his race (v. 21) is run on a track wholly owned by the Bounty-Giver.

**The Last Word of the Sūrah in the Life of the Ummah**

This verse's final phrase — *Wa-Allāhu dhū al-faḍli al-ʿaẓīm* — is a seal the Quran used before at its other great unveiling of the same subject (2:105, at the very context of the People of the Book's envy), and it left in the early community a settled teaching the hadith-literature records under many doors. Envy of God's distribution was forbidden outright: *Do not envy one another* (al-Bukhārī 6064; Muslim 2559), for envy objects — at root — to the division of the Tremendous Bounty. The believer who sees a brother outstripped in knowledge, wealth, or acceptance with men is to remember the laborers' parable: the bounty was the Giver's; the wage was rendered to each of us complete. And when catastrophe erases what a man held, or the inheritance of the heavens and the earth (v. 10) reverts to its Lord, the same seal operates in the other direction: *the Bounty was God's before He gave it, and His after He took it*. The sūrah's first command after its throne-doctrine — *spend of that over which He has appointed you trustees* (v. 7) — and its last word — *the Bounty is in God's Hand* — are the same sentence pronounced once at each end of the chapter, so that whoever walks its twenty-nine verses from glorification to grace returns to his purse, his heart, his light, and his people knowing whose Hand held all four: the hand that glorifies in the heavens (v. 1), writes the ledger (v. 22), plants from dead earth (v. 17), balances justice (v. 25), doubles the latecomer's wage (v. 28), and — after every distribution — remains Possessor of Tremendous Bounty, giving unto whomsoever He will.

*Thus ends Sūrah al-Ḥadīd: and praise belongs to God, the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward; and He is, of all things, Knower.*
