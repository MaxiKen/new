# Sūrah al-Muzzammil (Chapter 73) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sūrah from the First Days of Revelation**

Al-Muzzammil, "The Enwrapped One," belongs to the earliest dawn of the Qur'anic revelation. Most commentators place its first nineteen verses immediately after the opening verses of Sūrah 74, al-Muddaththir, in chronological order — that is, within days or weeks of the Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ first encounter with the angel Gabriel in the cave of Ḥirāʾ. This dating matters enormously for understanding the chapter. These are not instructions given to an established community with mosques, judges, and armies. They are instructions given to one frightened man, wrapped in his cloak, and to a handful of believers meeting in secret — yet they lay down a program of spiritual formation so complete that fourteen centuries of Muslim devotional life have never moved beyond it: rise at night, recite slowly, remember God, trust Him alone, endure opposition with dignity, and warn of a Day that will unmake the world.

**The Two-Stage Revelation and the Easing in Verse 20**

The classical sources preserve a striking detail about this sūrah's composition: verse 20 was revealed about one year after verses 1–19, according to many commentators cited by al-Ālūsī and Ibn Kathīr, while others hold that verse 20 belongs to the Madinan period. Either way, the final verse looks back on the first section from a later vantage point and modifies it: the night vigil, first imposed as an obligation, is eased into a voluntary devotion. This makes al-Muzzammil one of the clearest windows in the Qur'an into the divine pedagogy of gradual legislation (tadarruj). God first commands the hardest form of the devotion, lets the believers practice it until their feet swell, and only then lightens the load — so that the community learns both the height of the ideal and the mercy of the Lawgiver. The same pattern appears in the rulings on fasting, on wine, and on the number of daily prayers during the Ascension: the believer is shown the summit before being settled on a sustainable path.

**Night Vigil Before the Five Prayers**

One of the most remarkable facts about this sūrah is that it made the night prayer obligatory before the five daily prayers had been established. The five prayers were enjoined at the Ascension (al-Miʿrāj), roughly a year before the Hijrah; the night vigil of al-Muzzammil belongs to the first year of revelation, more than a decade earlier. The order is theologically eloquent. Before God gave His servants the public, communal, scheduled worship of the daytime, He gave them the private, solitary, unscheduled worship of the night. The sequence teaches that public religion must be built on private devotion, not the reverse. A community whose members have learned to stand before God when no one is watching can be trusted with mosques, pulpits, and treasuries; a community that has only public religion is one scandal away from collapse. The modern parallel is exact: institutions vet their leaders' private conduct precisely because the night reveals what the day conceals.

**The Architecture of the Chapter**

The sūrah unfolds in four movements. Verses 1–9 address the Prophet ﷺ directly, commanding the night vigil, measured recitation, remembrance, and trust — the inner formation of the Messenger. Verses 10–14 counsel patience toward opponents and describe, in escalating images, the requital awaiting arrogant denial: respite first, then fetters, choking food, and an earthquake that melts mountains into sand. Verses 15–19 turn the address outward to the deniers themselves, likening Muhammad to Moses and the Quraysh to Pharaoh's people, and sketching the Day of Judgment through apocalyptic inversions — children gray-haired with terror, the sky split open. Verse 20 then returns, a year or more later, with the easing: God knows the vigil was heavy, so He lightens it, while anchoring the believers in the three permanent pillars — prayer, alms, and voluntary charity — and closing the sūrah on the note of forgiveness. Command, consolation, warning, mercy: the whole Qur'anic method in twenty verses.

**The Weighty Word and Its Bearers**

At the center of the opening section stands verse 5 — "Truly We shall soon cast upon thee a weighty Word" — which explains everything around it. The night vigil is not an arbitrary hardship; it is training for weight-bearing. Revelation, the sūrah teaches, is heavy: heavy in the obligations it imposes, heavy with reward in the scales of the Last Day, and heavy in a literal, physical sense that the Prophet's ﷺ body felt when it descended. No one carries such weight on unprepared shoulders. The sūrah is thus, among other things, the Qur'an's earliest statement of a universal principle of leadership and moral formation: great trusts require great preparation, and the preparation happens in solitude before it is tested in public. Every subsequent verse of the chapter — the patience, the trust, the warning, the easing — flows from this center.

What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary. Each of the twenty verses is treated as a complete, independent discourse, drawing upon the Arabic language, the classical commentators, the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, and the reasoning that connects this earliest of texts to the conditions of the modern human being.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:1

> **O thou enwrapped!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Name That Is a Snapshot**

The sūrah opens with a single word of address — *yā ayyuhā al-muzzammil* — "O thou enwrapped one." The Arabic *muzzammil* is the active participle of the fifth verbal form (*tazzammala*), from the root *z-m-l*, meaning to wrap or enfold oneself in a garment. The fifth form carries a reflexive sense: he wrapped *himself*. This is not a title the Prophet ﷺ chose, nor a description of his office like "messenger" or "prophet." It is a snapshot of his physical state at the moment of revelation: a man enfolded in his cloak. God addresses him not by rank but by condition — as a father entering a dark room might call his child not by name but by what he sees: "O you hiding under the blanket." The tenderness of the address is the first teaching of the sūrah. Before any command is given, the Commander shows that He sees His servant exactly as he is, and speaks to him from within that state rather than demanding he emerge from it first.

**The Night That Produced This Verse**

The classical commentators connect this address to the aftermath of the first revelation in the cave of Ḥirāʾ. When the angel Gabriel seized the Prophet ﷺ three times and commanded him to "Read" — the opening of Sūrah 96 — the Prophet returned to his wife Khadījah trembling, his heart pounding, saying, "Cover me, cover me" (*zammilūnī, zammilūnī*). Al-Bukhārī and Muslim both record this account from ʿĀʾishah in the Book of the Beginning of Revelation:

> *"The angel came to him and said, 'Read.' He said, 'I am not one who reads.' The Prophet said, 'Then he seized me and pressed me until I was exhausted, then released me and said, "Read."... Then the Messenger of God ﷺ returned with it, his heart trembling, and entered upon Khadījah bint Khuwaylid, saying, "Cover me, cover me." So they covered him until the fear left him."* (al-Bukhārī 3; Muslim 160)

This is the scene the verse names. The man wrapped in his cloak, shaking from an encounter with the unseen world, is called by God with the very word his own lips had gasped. There is a profound psychology here: God does not rebuke the fear. He honors it by making it the doorway of address. The sūrah that will command the Prophet to stand half the night in prayer begins by acknowledging that he is, at this moment, a frightened man under a blanket — and that this is a perfectly acceptable starting point for prophethood.

**One of Seven Qur'anic Names**

According to a report attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, preserved by Ibn ʿAṭiyyah and al-Qurṭubī, *al-Muzzammil* is one of seven names by which the Prophet said he is called in the Qur'an. The others are Muḥammad (3:144; 33:40; 47:2; 48:29), Aḥmad (61:6), Ṭā Hā (20:1), Yā Sīn (36:1), *al-Muddaththir*, "thou who art covered" (74:1), and ʿAbd Allāh, "servant of God" (72:19). Two of the seven — *Muzzammil* and *Muddaththir* — are not names at all in the ordinary sense but descriptions of the wrapped, covered state, and they head the two sūrahs revealed at the very beginning of the mission. The pairing is deliberate and instructive. In Sūrah 73 the enwrapped one is told to rise for *himself* — night prayer, recitation, devotion; in Sūrah 74 the covered one is told to rise for *others* — "Arise and warn." Inner formation precedes outer mission. A man who has not stood before God in the night cannot stand before men in the day. The two wrappings are thus the two halves of prophetic preparation, and their order — first the vigil, then the warning — is the order of all sound religious leadership ever since.

**An Injunction Disguised as an Address**

Ibn Kathīr notes a second layer in the verse: in context, "O thou enwrapped" functions as an implicit command — "O thou who art sleeping, throw off thy bedding and stand." The proof is the verse that follows immediately: "Stand vigil at night." The address is therefore not mere description; it is a gentle summons. But observe the gentleness of the method. God does not open the mission with "Stand!" He opens with the man's name-state, establishes recognition and intimacy, and *then* commands. This is the Qur'anic pedagogy of command in miniature: address before injunction, acknowledgment before obligation. The same pattern recurs throughout the Qur'an — "O you who believe" precedes nearly every major female and communal ruling — because a command received inside a relationship is obeyed differently from a command shouted from a distance. Modern studies of motivation confirm what this verse enacted: people comply with directives from those who have first demonstrated that they see and value them; bare orders breed bare compliance or quiet rebellion.

**Why Fear Is the Right Beginning**

There is a final wisdom in the fact that prophethood begins under a blanket. The Prophet's ﷺ trembling is the Qur'an's standing refutation of two opposite errors about religious experience. Against the cynic who says revelation is self-induced ecstasy, the trembling answers: no one frightens himself into this state deliberately; the Prophet's first response to revelation was terror and retreat, not triumph. Against the fanatic who imagines closeness to God means swaggering certainty, the blanket answers: the greatest of mankind began his mission shaking. Awe is the authentic first response to the Holy, as it was for Moses, who fell senseless when the mountain crumbled (7:143), and as the Qur'an describes the righteous generally:

> *"It is only those of His servants possessed of knowledge who fear God."* (Qur'an 35:28)

Fear, in this sense, is not the opposite of faith but its evidence — the soul's correct measurement of the distance between itself and its Lord, registered in the body before the mind can articulate it. The sūrah honors that measurement by building the entire mission on top of it rather than asking the Prophet to pretend it away.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:2

> **Stand vigil at night, save a little,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Obligation Was the Night**

The first command of the Prophet's ﷺ mission, after the bare summons to read, is this: *qum al-layl* — "stand the night." The verb *qāma* means to rise, to stand upright; in worship it denotes the standing posture of prayer, and by extension the entire prayer itself. Before the five daily prayers existed, before fasting, before alms, before pilgrimage — before the Muslim community numbered more than a handful of souls meeting in secret — God made obligatory upon His Prophet and his Companions the standing of the night in prayer. The order is startling and deserves slow reflection. One might have expected the first duty to be public proclamation, or organizing the believers, or confronting the idolaters. Instead it is solitary prayer in the dark, when no one can see, applaud, or join. The mission begins not with a campaign but with a vigil. The logic will become explicit in verse 5: a weighty Word is coming, and only night-trained shoulders can carry it. Every great public trust in human life follows the same law — the surgeon trains for years unseen before touching a patient, the athlete drills in empty gyms before the stadium — and God applies it to the greatest trust of all.

**Feet That Swelled in the Dark**

The early community obeyed this command with a literalism that left marks on their bodies. The Prophet ﷺ and his Companions would stand so long that their feet swelled, as verse 20 will recall and as the two Ṣaḥīḥs record from al-Mughīrah ibn Shuʿbah:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ stood in prayer until his feet became swollen. It was said to him, 'Has God not forgiven you your past and future sins?' He said, 'Should I not then be a grateful servant?'"* (al-Bukhārī 1130; Muslim 2819)

Consider what this report reveals. The Prophet ﷺ — whose sins were forgiven in advance — stood longest of all, and his reason was gratitude, not fear of punishment. The night vigil was never, even at its most strenuous, a transaction of dread. It was thanksgiving made physical: the body standing because the heart could not sit still under the weight of blessing. Al-Ghazālī later distilled the principle: the worship of the fearful is good, the worship of the hopeful is good, but the worship of the grateful is the worship of the free — *ʿibādat al-aḥrār* — because it expects nothing and therefore cannot be disappointed. That the very first obligation of Islam was built on this motive tells the believer what kind of religion he has entered: one whose hardest duties are framed as gratitude, not groveling.

**"Save a Little": Mercy Inside the Command**

Even in its strictest form, the command carries its own softening: *illā qalīlā* — "save a little." A portion of the night is excepted from the standing: the believer must sleep some of it. The exception is small but theologically large. From the first obligation onward, Islam refuses the logic of total self-destruction in worship. The body has rights; sleep is not the enemy of devotion but its appointed partner. Years later, when three men came to the Prophet's ﷺ household asking about his worship and then vowed extremes — one to pray all night without sleeping, one to fast without breaking, one to abandon marriage — the Prophet ﷺ corrected them publicly, as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Anas:

> *"As for me, I fast and I break my fast, I pray and I sleep, and I marry women. Whoever turns away from my practice is not of me."* (al-Bukhārī 5063; Muslim 1401)

And when he learned that ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr was praying all night and fasting every day, he instructed him to moderate, saying:

> *"Your body has a right over you."* (al-Bukhārī 1153; Muslim 1159)

The "little" of this verse is the seed of that entire ethic. God commands the night but protects the sleep; He wants servants who endure, not candles that flare once and gutter out. The spiritual life is measured in decades, and only a sustainable practice survives them.

**Why the Night, and Not the Day**

The Qur'an returns to the special status of the night again and again — *"And keep vigil in prayer for part of the night, as a supererogatory act for thee"* (17:79); *"They used to sleep but little of the night"* (51:17); *"And in the night's watches, they would seek forgiveness"* (51:18) — and the reasons form a coherent psychology. At night the world withdraws its claims: no commerce, no spectators, no interruptions. The prayer offered then is the least contaminated by ostentation (*riyāʾ*), the disease of worship performed for eyes. At night the soul is also closest to its honest state: the day's roles — merchant, father, notable — have been laid aside with the clothes, and the one who stands is simply a servant. And at night, as the famous ḥadīth qudsī recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim teaches, God Himself draws near in a manner befitting His majesty:

> *"Every night, during the last third of the night, our Lord descends to the heaven of this world and says, 'Who calls upon Me that I might answer him? Who asks of Me that I might give to him? Who seeks My forgiveness that I might forgive him?'"* (al-Bukhārī 1145; Muslim 758)

The night vigil is thus an appointment the Lord Himself keeps. The believer who rises is not knocking on a closed door; he is answering a call already issued. Modern life, with its lit screens and severed rhythms, has made this appointment harder to keep — but also more precious. The hours before dawn remain the only hours that belong wholly to the soul, and this verse, fourteen centuries old, still marks them as sacred time.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:3

> **half of it or reduce it a little,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Precision in Devotion**

Having commanded the standing of the night, God now quantifies it: *niṣfahū* — half of it. The pronoun refers to the night, measured from sunset to dawn. The believer is to stand approximately half the night in prayer. Then comes a first adjustment: *aw unquṣ minhu qalīlā* — "or reduce it a little," that is, pray somewhat less than half, such as a third. The next verse will complete the range by permitting somewhat more than half, up to two-thirds. What strikes the reader first is the sheer precision. The Qur'an, which often speaks in sweeping moral terms, here speaks like a physician writing a prescription: half, a little less, a little more. The precision dignifies the devotion. It tells the believer that his night has a measured value in God's sight, that the hours are counted rather than vaguely approved. Worship in Islam is never left to mood alone; even its most intimate form arrives with quantities attached, so that the soul can plan, commit, and persevere rather than drift on the tides of feeling.

**A Sliding Scale, Not a Fixed Bar**

The deeper wisdom lies in the range itself. God does not fix a single duration but offers a spectrum — a little less than half, half, a little more — within which the believer finds his own measure. The classical commentators understood this as divine accommodation of human variety: nights differ in length between summer and winter, bodies differ in strength, and the same believer differs between health and sickness, youth and age. A fixed bar would have broken the weak or bored the strong; a spectrum holds both. This is the Qur'anic principle of capacity-legislation in its earliest form, later stated universally:

> *"God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity."* (Qur'an 2:286)

And in the Prophet's ﷺ own instruction on voluntary worship, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from ʿĀʾishah:

> *"Take on such deeds as you are capable of, for God does not tire until you tire. And the most beloved deed to God is the one done most regularly, even if it is small."* (al-Bukhārī 43; Muslim 782)

The "little less" of this verse is therefore not a concession to laziness but a strategy for permanence. A man who prays a sustainable third of the night every night for forty years has done more — and become more — than one who prays two-thirds for a month and abandons the practice forever. The spectrum teaches the believer to calibrate, honestly and without shame, and then to hold his calibration with the stubbornness of habit.

**The Pedagogy of Starting Hard**

Yet there is a deliberate severity here that must not be explained away. Half the night in prayer is, by any measure, an immense obligation — and it was imposed on a community still counting its members in dozens, many of them poor, persecuted, and laboring by day. Why begin with the hardest form? The commentators, reflecting on verse 20's later easing, discern a pedagogy: God shows the summit first so that the subsequent settlement feels like mercy rather than demand. Had the vigil begun as a light recommendation, few would have tasted its depths; beginning as a heavy obligation, it forged a generation that knew what the night contained, so that when the easing came they continued voluntarily what they had begun obligatorily. Human institutions stumble upon the same principle: military training, medical residency, athletic conditioning all front-load hardship, knowing that capacities built under pressure persist when pressure lifts. The first Muslims were the residency class of the new faith, and the night was their hospital shift. What they learned standing — patience, concentration, intimacy with the Word — they carried into every later trial, from the boycott to Badr.

**Time as the First Thing God Regulates**

There is a final observation worth making: the first thing God legislates for the new community is not money, not marriage, not warfare, but *time* — specifically, the hours of darkness. Before any social law, there is a schedule. The implication is that the reformation of the soul begins with the reformation of the clock. A life whose nights belong to God will produce days that can be trusted; worship that captures the most private hours has captured the person. The modern believer, whose nights are more likely to belong to glowing screens than to prayer, should feel the verse's quiet challenge. The question it poses is not "Are you pious?" but something more concrete and harder to evade: *what do your nights contain?* For the Qur'an's answer, from its earliest pages, is that the night is not leftover time. It is the first time God claimed.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:4

> **or add to it; and recite the Qur'an in a measured pace.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Completing the Range: Permission to Exceed**

The verse opens by completing the spectrum begun in verse 3: *aw zid ʿalayh* — "or add to it," that is, pray more than half the night, up to the two-thirds that verse 20 will mention. The three options together — a little less than half, half, a little more — form a complete devotional ladder. Notice the direction of completion: the range ends on the side of *increase*. The last word on quantity is permission to do more, not license to do less. This asymmetry is characteristic of Qur'anic legislation on voluntary devotion: the floor is set mercifully low, but the ceiling is left open to aspiration. The same structure appears in the laws of charity — the obligatory alms fixed, voluntary giving unlimited — and in fasting — Ramaḍān fixed, voluntary fasts open-ended. The message is consistent: God guarantees that the minimum suffices, then invites the soul to discover how much more it is capable of loving. The believer who began at a third may find, after a year of nights, that two-thirds has become his natural portion — not through grim effort but through the quiet growth of appetite that all sustained devotion produces.

**The First Command to Recite the Qur'an as Worship**

The verse's second clause is historically momentous: *wa rattil al-qurʾāna tartīlā* — "and recite the Qur'an in a measured pace." These are the first words in the chronological order of revelation to command the recitation of the Qur'an as an act of worship. The Qur'an had been *descending* for only days or weeks; already God commands that it be *recited* — slowly, deliberately, beautifully. The imperative establishes recitation (*tilāwah*) as the central devotional act of Islam from the very beginning, before any other ritual existed. Later verses will repeat the command in many forms — *"And recite what has been revealed to thee of the Book"* (29:45); *"And I have been commanded to be of those who submit, and to recite the Qur'an"* (27:91–92) — but this is the fountainhead. The religion's first ritual act, performed in the dark watches of the night by a handful of believers, was to take the few verses they had been given and turn them over slowly on the tongue. Everything that followed — the memorization of millions, the sciences of recitation, the night prayers of Ramaḍān resounding across continents — descends from this clause.

**What Tartīl Means**

The key term is *tartīl*, from the root *r-t-l*, which in classical Arabic denotes evenness, arrangement, and measured order — teeth are called *ratl* when they are evenly spaced and well set. To recite with *tartīl* is to render the Qur'an distinctly, word by word, giving each letter its articulation, each verse its pause, each meaning its moment to arrive. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib defined it with famous concision as *"tajwīd al-ḥurūf wa maʿrifat al-wuqūf"* — pronouncing the letters correctly and knowing the places of pausing. The Prophet's ﷺ own practice embodied it: his wife Umm Salamah reported, as transmitted in the collections of al-Tirmidhī and Abū Dāwūd and cited by Ibn Kathīr, that he would recite verse by verse, pausing at the end of each verse — *"He used to divide his recitation verse by verse."* Anas ibn Mālik, asked about the Prophet's ﷺ recitation, described it as *"an elongation"* (*madd*), stretching the sounds with unhurried clarity (al-Bukhārī 5045). The opposite of *tartīl* is *hadhramah* — racing through the words as though fleeing them — which the Prophet ﷺ forbade in spirit when he warned against reciting the whole Qur'an in less than three days, saying of such haste:

> *"He who recites the Qur'an in less than three days has not understood it."* (al-Tirmidhī 2949; Abū Dāwūd 1390)

The logic is plain: the Qur'an was revealed to be *understood* and *weighed*, and speed is the enemy of both. A measured pace is not aesthetic decoration; it is the velocity of comprehension.

**Beauty of Voice as a Commanded Good**

The command to measured recitation carries a second dimension: beauty. The Prophet ﷺ commanded his followers, as Ibn Kathīr records, *"Adorn the Qur'an with your voices"* (*zayyinū al-qurʾāna bi-aṣwātikum*, Abū Dāwūd 1468; al-Nasāʾī 1015), and warned, *"Whosoever does not chant the Qur'an melodiously is not one of us"* (*man lam yataghanna bi'l-qurʾān fa-laysa minnā*, al-Bukhārī 7527). The Arabic *yataghannā* implies rich, resonant, melodious rendering — not singing as entertainment but the natural music of a heart moved by what it reads. The wisdom is psychological as much as devotional: beauty opens the gates that argument cannot force. A melody carries meaning past the guards of habit and heedlessness into chambers of the soul that prose never reaches. This is why the Qur'an's effect on its first hearers was so often immediate and overwhelming — grown men weeping at verses whose legal content they had not yet studied — and why, to this day, listeners who understand not a word of Arabic find themselves arrested by a master reciter's *tartīl*. God commands the beauty because He made the soul to be opened by it.

**The Fruits of Recitation: Four Parables in One Ḥadīth**

The Prophet ﷺ illustrated the transformative power of recitation with one of his most beloved parables, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī:

> *"The believer who recites the Qur'an is like a citrus fruit: its fragrance is pleasing and its taste is sweet. The believer who does not recite the Qur'an is like a dried date: it has no fragrance, but its taste is sweet. The hypocrite who recites the Qur'an is like basil: its fragrance is sweet, but its taste is bitter. The hypocrite who does not recite the Qur'an is like the colocynth: it has no fragrance and its taste is bitter."* (al-Bukhārī 5059; Muslim 797)

Four human types, sorted by two axes — inward faith (taste) and outward recitation (fragrance). The parable honors the silent believer — his taste is sweet; faith alone saves. But it crowns the reciting believer, whose goodness, like citrus perfume, benefits everyone in the room. Recitation is thus not private hoarding but public fragrance: the one who recites slowly at night becomes, by day, a presence others breathe more easily around. And the parable's warning edge is equally sharp: outward beauty of recitation without inward faith is basil — sweet to the ear, bitter to God. *Tartīl* must rise from the heart it claims to express, or it is perfume on a corpse.

**Gatherings of Recitation and Their Promised Gifts**

The communal dimension of this command was sealed in the famous ḥadīth recorded by Muslim from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"Never does a group gather in one of the houses of God, reciting the Book of God and studying it among themselves, but that tranquility descends upon them, mercy covers them, the angels surround them, and God mentions them among those with Him."* (Muslim 2699)

Four gifts for one gathering: *sakīnah* (tranquility) within, mercy above, angels around, and divine mention on high. The verse's command, given to solitary believers in the night, thus flowers into the study circle, the memorization school, the Ramaḍān recitation — every setting where Muslims gather around the Book. And for the struggling individual reciter, the Prophet ﷺ left a promise that removes all shame from slow or broken reading, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from ʿĀʾishah:

> *"Whosoever recites the Qur'an fluently is with the noble, obedient scribes [the angels], and whosoever recites it with difficulty, stammering through it, shall receive a double reward."* (al-Bukhārī 4937; Muslim 798)

Difficulty doubles the wage. The stammerer out-earns the fluent — a divine economics in which effort, not polish, is the currency. No believer, however unlettered, is excluded from this verse's command.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:5

> **Truly We shall soon cast upon thee a weighty Word.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Explains Everything Around It**

This short verse is the keystone of the sūrah's opening section. Everything before it — the night vigil, the measured recitation — and everything after it — the remembrance, the trust, the patience — is preparation for, or consequence of, what it announces: *innā sa-nulqī ʿalayka qawlan thaqīlā* — "Truly We shall cast upon thee a weighty Word." The particle *sa-* denotes the near future: the casting is imminent. The verb *ulqī* (We shall cast, hurl, lay upon) suggests a burden placed onto shoulders from above — the same verb used when God "cast" tranquility upon the believers (48:26) and when Moses' staff was "cast" down (7:115). The Word (*qawl*) is the Qur'an itself, the revelation that had just begun and would continue for twenty-three years. And its defining attribute here is *thaqīl* — heavy, weighty, grave. The mission is introduced not as an honor to be celebrated but as a weight to be carried. Before the Prophet ﷺ tastes a single triumph, he is told the truth about the trust: it will be heavy. The honesty of the announcement is itself a mercy. No one who was warned can later claim betrayal, and no weight carried knowingly can crush the way an unexpected one does.

**Heavy in Obligation**

The classical commentators, including Ibn Kathīr and al-Suyūṭī, explain the weight first in terms of obligation. The Qur'an is weighty in what it *requires*: the duties it imposes on the Prophet and his followers — prayer, almsgiving, fasting, honesty in trade, courage in battle, forgiveness of enemies, purity of heart — are not light matters. Each command is a weight laid on the appetites; each prohibition a weight laid on the passions. The Prophet ﷺ felt this weight most of all, for upon him fell not only obedience but delivery: he must live the Word perfectly *and* convey it completely, to friends who would reject him and enemies who would seek his life. When the Qur'an later describes what would happen if it descended on a mountain — *"Had We sent down this Qur'an upon a mountain, thou wouldst have seen it humbled, rent asunder out of fear of God"* (59:21) — it is this same weight, measured against the hardest thing in creation and found heavier. Mountains would crack; the Prophet's ﷺ heart was asked to hold. The night vigil of verses 2–4 is now revealed as weight-training: the standing, the recitation, the slow hours are how a human heart is conditioned to bear what would break a mountain.

**Heavy in the Scales**

A second meaning, emphasized by al-Suyūṭī, turns the weight from burden to treasure: the Word is weighty in the *good* it places in the believer's scales on the Day of Judgment. Every letter recited, every command obeyed, every verse pondered becomes mass on the side of salvation. The Prophet ﷺ described this economy of weight in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"Two words are light on the tongue, heavy in the Balance, beloved to the Merciful: 'Glory be to God and with His praise; glory be to God the Magnificent.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6406; Muslim 2694)

If two short phrases weigh heavily, what of the entire Qur'an carried for a lifetime? The same revelation that presses down on the shoulders in this world presses down on the scales in the next — but there, heaviness is salvation, for *"as for him whose scales are heavy, he shall have a pleasing life"* (101:6–7). The verse thus contains a complete theology of religious effort: every weight borne for God's sake in the dark is a weight transferred to the Balance in the light. The believer groaning under an obligation should picture it leaving his back and settling onto his scales. Nothing carried for God is ever truly heavy; it is only heavy *now*.

**Heavy on the Body: The Physical Weight of Revelation**

According to several accounts preserved by Ibn Kathīr, the weight was not merely figurative. The Prophet's ﷺ body physically registered the descent of revelation. His wife ʿĀʾishah reported that revelation would come upon him even on an intensely cold day, and when it lifted, his forehead would be dripping with sweat (al-Bukhārī 2; Muslim 2333). On another occasion she said that when revelation descended while he was mounted on his camel, the beast's neck would buckle and it would kneel under a weight it could not name — "it was like a blow to the camel's neck." ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit similarly reported that when revelation came, the Prophet's ﷺ face would darken and he would lower his head while the Companions lowered theirs (Muslim 2334). These reports answer the skeptic who would reduce revelation to private meditation: meditation does not make camels kneel or foreheads sweat in winter. The weight was an objective event with witnesses, as real as weather. And it explains the tenderness of verse 1: a man upon whom such weight falls needs, first, a blanket, and then, night by night, the training to stand beneath it.

**Why the Bearer Must Be Told**

There remains the question of *why* God announces the weight in advance. The answer touches the logic of all divine commands that involve hardship. A trust imposed without warning breeds resentment when the hardship arrives; a trust announced beforehand recruits the will. By saying "weighty" before the weight fully descends, God obtains the Prophet's ﷺ informed consent — and obtains it, remarkably, without any recorded hesitation on the Prophet's part. The silence that follows this verse in the sīrah is eloquent: the Prophet ﷺ simply rose, night after night, and carried it. For the believer today, the verse reframes every heavy obedience — the prayer that interrupts sleep, the honesty that costs money, the forgiveness that costs pride — as a share in the weighty Word. The Word is still weighty; it has not slimmed down in fourteen centuries. But neither has the promise changed: what is heavy on the back today is heavy on the scales tomorrow, and the shoulders that train in the night will not buckle in the day.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:6

> **Truly the vigil of the night is firmest in tread and most upright for speech.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rising in the Night: What the Key Phrase Means**

The verse opens with the phrase *nāshiʾat al-layl* — "the vigil of the night," literally "the rising of the night" or "that which rises in the night." The word *nāshiʾah* comes from the root *n-sh-ʾ*, meaning to rise, originate, or come into being — the same root as *inshāʾ* (origination) and *nashʾah* (growth, as in "the second growth," the resurrection, in 29:20). Al-Ṭabarī explains it as rising from sleep for prayer after a portion of sleep — the getting-up itself. In this light the phrase carries a beautiful double sense: the believer *rises* in the night, and something *rises* within him — his spirit, his heart, his aspiration ascend while the world lies flat in sleep. Some commentators extend the meaning to the hours of the night themselves, which "rise" one after another in succession. Whichever reading is taken, the verse names the night-rising as a distinct spiritual species, different in kind from daytime worship, and then assigns it two superlatives found nowhere else in the Qur'an: it is *ashaddu waṭʾan* — firmest in tread — and *aqwamu qīlā* — most upright in speech.

**Firmest in Tread: The Step That Leaves a Mark**

The first superlative, *ashaddu waṭʾan*, repays close attention. *Waṭʾ* means treading, stepping, pressing the foot into the ground. To say the night vigil is "firmest in tread" is to say it leaves the deepest footprint — and the footprint is on the heart. Al-Ṭabarī and others explain that worship performed at night impresses itself upon the soul more powerfully than anything done by day: the meanings penetrate, the awe settles, the resolutions take root. The image is agricultural as much as martial: soft night-soil receives the seed that hard daytime ground, compacted by traffic and noise, would let blow away. There is also a second, widely transmitted reading of the word — *wiṭāʾ* instead of *waṭʾ* — meaning agreement, harmony, or congruence. On this reading, preserved by al-Ṭabarī, the verse means the night is best for realizing *congruence between the heart, the ear, and the tongue*: what the tongue recites, the ear hears in silence, and the heart receives without competition. Anyone who has prayed in a quiet house at 3 a.m. knows the difference experientially: verses one has recited a thousand times by day suddenly *arrive* — the heart hears them as if for the first time. That arrival is the *wiṭāʾ*, the inner agreement, that this verse promises.

**Most Upright for Speech: When Words Stand Straight**

The second superlative, *aqwamu qīlā* — "most upright for speech" (*qīl* meaning speech, utterance) — declares the night the best time for the recitation of the Qur'an (al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī). The adjective *aqwam* (straightest, most upright, most correct) suggests that speech itself stands straighter at night: recitation is clearer, contemplation deeper, supplication more sincere. The reasons are partly physiological — the rested voice, the rested mind after first sleep — and partly circumstantial: no audience to impress, no hurry to finish, no noise to compete with. Words spoken to God in the dark tend to be honest words; the tongue, freed from performance, says what the heart actually means. This is why the Qur'an elsewhere links the night with seeking forgiveness — *"and in the watches before dawn, they would seek forgiveness"* (51:18) — and why the Prophet ﷺ made his longest, most weeping supplications in night prayer. Speech by day is often crooked — bent by flattery, fear, or haste. Speech by night, with none to hear but God, stands upright.

**The Science of Silence Confirms the Verse**

Modern research into attention and cognition has, without intending to, written a commentary on this verse. Studies of deep work and memory consolidation consistently show that new learning encodes most durably in conditions of quiet focus, free from interruption and multitasking — precisely the conditions of the pre-dawn hours in a sleeping household. Sleep scientists add that the brain, after its first cycles of sleep, enters states unusually favorable to insight and emotional processing; therapists note that clients speak more honestly late at night, when the day's defenses have tired. The Qur'an knew none of these vocabularies and all of their substance: *the night leaves the strongest impression and straightens speech*. The believer need not choose between revelation and reason here; they point at the same dark window. The practical conclusion is the same in both idioms: guard a portion of the night for the Book, and the Book will do in months what years of distracted daytime reading cannot.

**Sincerity Grows Where No One Watches**

There is a final, ethical dimension to the verse's praise of the night. Worship performed unseen is worship purified of the deadliest pollutant of devotion: ostentation. The Prophet ﷺ warned that hidden ostentation is *"more hidden than a black ant on a black stone in the darkness of the night"* (Aḥmad 3:30) — and the cure he prescribed is deeds done in actual darkness, where no eye can corrupt the intention. The night vigil is thus the great sincerity-machine of Islamic devotion: one cannot show off to a sleeping house. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, the great early ascetic, is reported to have said that the mark of the sincere is a secret between him and God that no one knows — and for most believers, the night prayer is that secret. The verse promises that such secrecy is not sterile hiding but fertile ground: tread firm, speech upright, heart opened. What is planted in the dark, God brings to fruit in the light.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:7

> **For truly by day you have lengthy affairs.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Swimming Through the Day**

The Arabic of this verse is more vivid than any translation captures: *inna laka fi'l-nahāri sabḥan ṭawīlā* — "truly for thee in the daytime is a long *sabḥ*." The word *sabḥ* comes from the root *s-b-ḥ*, whose basic meaning is to swim, to move freely through a medium — the same root as *sibāḥah* (swimming) and, remarkably, *tasbīḥ* (glorification of God, as though the tongue swims through praise). The day's affairs are thus pictured as water through which the believer must swim, stroke after stroke, for long hours: earning, providing, meeting, mending, answering, going and coming. The image is neither condemnation nor celebration — it is recognition. God does not pretend the day is empty. He acknowledges, in His own speech, that daylight is full of legitimate, demanding, exhausting business, and He gives that business a name that honors its effort: a long swim. The verse is the Qur'an's earliest charter for the working believer: your daytime labor is seen, named, and taken into account by God Himself.

**Why the Night Is Commanded: Because the Day Is Full**

The particle *inna* (truly) and the context make the verse's function clear: it explains *why* the night vigil was commanded. The reasoning runs: by day you are submerged in affairs that leave no room for extended devotion, *therefore* devote the night to prayer. Two complementary readings emerge from the commentators. On the first (al-Jalālayn), the verse concedes that daytime responsibilities *prevent* extensive worship, so the night must carry what the day cannot. On the second (Ibn Kathīr), the verse notes that God has granted a wide expanse of time for worldly matters by day, *therefore* justice requires returning a portion of the night to Him. Either way, the logic is that of balance, not escape. Islam does not solve the problem of busy days by abolishing them — no monastic withdrawal, no abandonment of trade or family — but by fencing off the night as devotion's protected reserve. The day belongs to affairs; the night belongs to prayer; and each, guarded in its bounds, enriches the other. The swimmer who rests at night swims better by day, and the night's prayer is sweetened by a day honestly spent.

**Work Sanctified, Not Sacralized**

This short verse quietly establishes Islam's entire theology of work. The daytime affairs — *sabḥ* — are treated as a given of the prophetic life itself: even the Messenger of God ﷺ, the verse implies, has lengthy business by day. Work is therefore not a fall from spirituality but its daytime form. The Prophet ﷺ lived this synthesis: he mended his own sandals, milked his own goat, did business in the market, judged disputes, received delegations, and played with his grandchildren — and then stood half the night in prayer. When the Qur'an later commands the believers to scatter after Friday prayer — *"then disperse through the land and seek of God's bounty"* (62:10) — it is this verse's logic extended to the whole community: mosque, then market; prayer, then provision. The Prophet ﷺ sealed the ethic in his famous assurance, recorded by al-Ṭabarānī and others, that the honest, trustworthy merchant shall be with the prophets, the truthful, and the martyrs. Yet the verse also sets work's limit: the swim is *by day*. When night falls, the swimmer comes ashore. A civilization that lets its affairs flood the night — emails at midnight, markets never closing, the mind swimming even in bed — has broken the boundary this verse drew, and pays for it in the exact coin the night vigil was meant to provide: peace, perspective, and presence before God.

**The Rhythm That Protects Both Worlds**

Step back, and verses 2–7 reveal a complete daily rhythm: long prayer by night, long affairs by day, each protected from the other. The rhythm guards against the two perennial deformations of the religious life. Against the worldly, who would let affairs devour everything, it sets the non-negotiable night. Against the otherworldly, who would let devotion devour everything, it dignifies the day's swim as divinely acknowledged business. The Prophet's ﷺ practice embodied the balance to the end: ʿĀʾishah was asked what he did at home, and she answered, as recorded by al-Bukhārī, that he was *"in the service of his family, and when prayer time came, he would go out to prayer"* (al-Bukhārī 676). Service, then prayer; swim, then stand. The modern believer drowning in "lengthy affairs" longer than any seventh-century merchant's — the commute, the inbox, the second job — should hear this verse as both comfort and command. Comfort: God knows the day is a long swim; He named it so. Command: precisely because the day is a swim, the night must be a standing. The rhythm is not optional equipment for the spiritual elite; in this sūrah, it is the first thing God ever asked of His Prophet.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:8

> **So remember the Name of thy Lord and devote thyself to Him with complete devotion—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**After Affairs, Remembrance**

The verse opens with a connective logic: *wa'dhkur isma rabbik* — "and remember the Name of thy Lord." The command follows the long swim of the day (verse 7) and the long standing of the night (verses 2–4) as their synthesis and seal. Ibn Kathīr links it to the later verse *"So when thou art free, exert thyself"* (94:7): once the believer has finished his worldly affairs, he turns to devotion. The "remembrance" (*dhikr*) commanded here is comprehensive. Some commentators (al-Jalālayn) read it narrowly as reciting the basmalah — "In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful" — at the opening of Qur'anic recitation, fitting the sūrah's theme of night recitation. Others (al-Ajwibah tradition, al-Alūsī) read it broadly as remembering God at every moment of every day — with the tongue in glorification, with the heart in awareness, with the limbs in obedience. The breadth is supported by the Qur'an's own superlative for remembrance:

> *"And the remembrance of God is surely greater."* (Qur'an 29:45)

Greater than what? The commentators answer: greater than every other act of worship, for it is the soul of them all. Prayer without remembrance is calisthenics; charity without remembrance is public relations. This verse, revealed in the first weeks of the mission, thus plants the seed of what would become the vast Islamic science and practice of *dhikr* — the morning and evening litanies, the remembrances after prayer, the glorifications on beads and fingertips — all of it unfolding from this clause.

**Severing for God: The Meaning of Tabattul**

The verse's second command carries the sharper edge: *wa tabattal ilayhi tabtīlā* — "and devote thyself to Him with complete devotion." The verb *tabattal* comes from the root *b-t-l*, meaning to sever, to cut off. The fifth-form reflexive means to cut *oneself* off — from everything, *unto Him*. The commentators (al-Ajwibah, al-Qurṭubī) explain: sever the heart's attachments to all besides God and attach it wholly to Him. The emphatic verbal noun *tabtīlā* ("with complete devotion") intensifies the command: not partial detachment but total dedication. The same root gives *al-Batūl*, "the Severed One" — the title of Mary, mother of Jesus, who was severed from the world unto God, and by extension a title of honor for Fāṭimah, the Prophet's ﷺ daughter. The imagery is surgical: devotion as amputation. Whatever competes with God for the heart's ultimate allegiance — wealth, status, persons, even one's own ego — must be cut away, cleanly and deliberately, so that the heart faces one direction only. This is the inner meaning of the very first pillar: *lā ilāha illa'llāh* is itself a severing, a no to every god before the yes to God.

**Detachment Without Monasticism**

A crucial qualification must be made, for the Prophet ﷺ himself made it. The severing commanded here is of the *heart*, not of society. When some Companions, hearing verses like this, proposed extreme renunciation — celibacy, withdrawal from the world, perpetual fasting — the Prophet ﷺ forbade it. The tradition is explicit: *"There is no monasticism in Islam"* (*lā rahbāniyyata fi'l-islām*, reported by al-Bayhaqī and others), and the Qur'an later notes that monasticism was something the Christians *"invented; We did not prescribe it for them"* (57:27). The Prophet's ﷺ own *tabattul* was practiced in the midst of life: he detached his heart while trading in markets, judging in mosques, and nursing his children. The model is the diver, not the deserter: the heart descends to God while the hands remain in the world. Al-Qushayrī, the great early Sufi commentator, captured the balance: true detachment (*zuhd*) is not that you own nothing, but that nothing owns you. The merchant whose wealth cannot buy his conscience is more detached than the beggar whose poverty consumes his thoughts. This verse commands the inner severing that makes outer engagement safe — the heart cut free from the world precisely so the hands can serve it without being enslaved by it.

**The Psychology of Single-Heartedness**

Why does God command such totality — "complete devotion," with emphasis? Because the divided heart is the suffering heart. Modern psychology has mapped, in its own terms, what this verse legislates: the person pulled between incompatible ultimate loyalties — God and greed, conscience and career, prayer and prestige — lives in chronic inner friction, and friction generates heat. Anxiety, indecision, and the peculiar modern exhaustion of "having everything and enjoying nothing" are the clinical faces of a heart that has not severed. The Qur'an diagnoses the condition directly:

> *"God has not made for any man two hearts in his breast."* (Qur'an 33:4)

One breast, one heart, one ultimate direction. The command of *tabattul* is therefore not divine jealousy but divine therapy: the heart was made single-chambered in its allegiance, and only single-hearted devotion lets it rest. The Prophet ﷺ described the result in the famous ḥadīth of intention:

> *"Actions are but by intentions, and every man shall have only what he intended."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907)

The wholly devoted heart transforms even eating, sleeping, and earning into worship — because all are done *unto Him* — while the severed-from-God heart transforms even prayer into theater. Totality of direction is thus not the luxury of saints; it is the minimum condition of peace.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:9

> **Lord of the East and the West, there is no god but He, so take Him as a guardian.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Lord of the Two Horizons**

The verse opens with a cosmic title: *rabbu'l-mashriqi wa'l-maghrib* — "Lord of the East and the West." *Mashriq* is the place of sunrise, *maghrib* the place of sunset — the two horizons that bound the human day and, in ancient idiom, the entire inhabited world. To call God their Lord (*rabb* — cherisher, sustainer, sovereign) is to declare that everything between dawn and dusk, everything the sun touches in its course, belongs to His dominion and care. The same title recurs — *"Lord of the East and the West and what is between them, if you would but understand"* (26:28) — and widens elsewhere into the plural *"Lord of the easts and Lord of the wests"* (55:17), embracing every point of rising and setting through the seasons. The Qur'an's geography of lordship leaves no gap: *"And to God belong the East and the West; so wherever you turn, there is the Face of God"* (2:115). The title is placed here, immediately after the command to total devotion (verse 8), as devotion's reason. Why sever the heart unto Him alone? Because there is nowhere else to turn that is not already His. The East is His, the West is His; the heart that flees to any other guardian flees from one corner of His kingdom to another.

**No God but He: The Creed Inside the Command**

The middle clause — *lā ilāha illā hū* — "there is no god but He" — is the second half of the Islamic testimony of faith, embedded directly into the verse. Its placement is precise and logical. The verse moves in three steps: universal lordship (He owns all), exclusive divinity (none deserves worship but He), practical consequence (so take Him as guardian). Each step entails the next. If He alone is Lord of all horizons, then none besides Him can ultimately benefit or harm; if none besides Him can benefit or harm, then trust placed anywhere else is misplaced; therefore — *fa'ttakhidh'hu wakīlā* — "so take Him as a guardian." The Qur'an's monotheism is never abstract metaphysics; it always lands in a practical imperative. Theology here is not studied but *used*: because God is One, the believer reorganizes his dependencies. Every other verse in the Qur'an that commands taking God as guardian follows the same logic — *"and God suffices as a Guardian"* (4:81, 132, 171; 33:3, 48); *"and thy Lord suffices as a Guardian"* (17:65) — for sufficiency is the whole point. A guardian who suffices needs no supplement.

**What Wakīl Means: The Trustee of All Affairs**

The word *wakīl* deserves careful unpacking, for it is richer than "guardian." A *wakīl* in Arabic law and life is one entrusted with another's affairs — a trustee, a deputy, an agent, a defender. When a man appoints a *wakīl*, he hands over his case, his property, or his interests, and rests: the agent acts, the principal trusts. To take God as *wakīl* is therefore to appoint Him, formally and consciously, as the manager of one's affairs — provision, protection, outcomes, enemies, future — and then to rest in the appointment. The Qur'an gives the archetype of this appointment in the words of the believers when threatened by a gathering enemy:

> *"Those to whom the people said, 'The people have gathered against you, so fear them' — but it increased them in faith, and they said, 'God suffices us, and He is the best of guardians (niʿma'l-wakīl).'"* (Qur'an 3:173)

And the Prophet ﷺ taught the individual form of the appointment in his counsel to the young Ibn ʿAbbās, recorded by al-Tirmidhī:

> *"Be mindful of God and He will protect thee. Be mindful of God and thou wilt find Him before thee. When thou askest, ask of God; and when thou seekest help, seek help from God. And know that if the entire nation gathered to benefit thee with anything, they could benefit thee only with what God has already decreed for thee; and if they gathered to harm thee, they could harm thee only with what God has already decreed against thee. The pens have been lifted and the pages have dried."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516)

This is *tawakkul* — reliance — in its complete form: human effort exerted, human outcome entrusted. The farmer plants, then appoints God over the harvest. The student studies, then appoints God over the result. Effort without trust is anxiety; trust without effort is delusion. The verse commands the trust that completes the effort.

**The Psychology of Entrustment**

Why does the human heart resist this appointment, even when it believes the theology? Because handing over control feels like losing it. The anxious mind would rather carry its affairs badly than entrust them perfectly — at least the carrying *feels* like doing something. This verse exposes the fallacy with an analogy from everyday life. When a traveler hires the most skilled pilot available and then spends the flight gripping the armrests and shouting instructions to the cockpit, his interference adds nothing to safety and everything to misery. The rational act, once the pilot is chosen, is to sit back. Taking God as *wakīl* is sitting back after choosing the only Pilot who never sleeps, never errs, and never abandons the aircraft. The Qur'an promises the psychological fruit explicitly:

> *"And whosoever relies upon God — He suffices him."* (Qur'an 65:3)

Sufficiency is not merely a fact about God; it is an experience granted to the one who relies — the unmistakable inner quiet of affairs handed over. The Prophet ﷺ, facing the might of Quraysh with a handful of followers, was given this verse before he was given any army: the Guardian first, the soldiers years later. The order teaches every overwhelmed believer the same sequence. Before the means arrive, appoint the Trustee. The entrustment itself is the first victory.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:10

> **Bear patiently that which they say and take leave of them in a beautiful manner.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Patience as the First Social Command**

With this verse the sūrah turns from the Prophet's ﷺ private devotion to his public trial: *wa'ṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūn* — "and bear patiently that which they say." The command is *ṣabr* — patience, endurance, steadfast holding — applied specifically to *speech*: the mockery, accusations, and insults of those who reject the message. The commentators note that at this earliest stage the Prophet had likely not yet begun public preaching; the "they" were the few individuals approached privately, whose ridicule already stung. Yet God legislates the response before the opposition has fully formed: patience, announced in advance, as standing policy. The same counsel recurs throughout the Qur'an — *"And bear patiently, and thy patience is only through God"* (16:127); *"So bear patiently that which they say"* (20:130; 50:39); *"And bear patiently a beautiful patience"* (70:5) — until patience becomes the single most commanded virtue in the Book, mentioned in some ninety verses. The reason is structural: every truth that challenges entrenched power will be mocked before it is debated and debated before it is accepted. A messenger who cannot absorb mockery cannot deliver anything. Patience is therefore not passive resignation here but mission equipment — the shield without which the missionary does not survive his first week.

**What Patience Toward Words Requires**

The verse specifies patience toward *what they say* — not toward swords, which had not yet been drawn, but toward words. This precision honors a truth every bearer of an unpopular message learns: words wound. Mockery is designed to make the mocked abandon his cause in order to recover his dignity; the insult says, in effect, "Withdraw your claim and we will restore your honor." Patience toward speech means refusing that bargain — holding the claim and absorbing the dishonor, trusting God to settle accounts of honor in His own time. The Prophet's ﷺ life became the supreme exhibit: he was called poet, soothsayer, madman, and liar to his face, and answered none of it with retaliation. When ʿĀʾishah once returned the insults of some Jews who had greeted him with a curse disguised as greeting, the Prophet ﷺ corrected her, as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"O ʿĀʾishah, God is Gentle and loves gentleness in all things."* (al-Bukhārī 6927; Muslim 2165)

Gentleness under insult is not weakness; it is strength with its hand deliberately stayed — and the staying of the hand is precisely what proves the strength. Anyone can retaliate; only the God-anchored can absorb. The verse thus sets the Prophetic standard for all later bearers of the message, including every believer who speaks truth in a hostile workplace, family, or forum: the words will come; let them pass through patience, not bounce back as revenge.

**Leaving Beautifully: The Art of Hajr Jamīl**

The verse's second command is subtler: *wa'hjur'hum hajran jamīlā* — "and take leave of them in a beautiful manner." The verb *hajara* means to leave, withdraw from, abandon — and the verbal noun is qualified by *jamīl*, beautiful, gracious, becoming. Al-Ṭabarī explains that this is leaving *for the sake of God*: a withdrawal motivated by devotion rather than spite, executed without ugliness. The qualification matters enormously, for leaving can be done in many ways — in rage, with slammed doors and parting curses; in cold contempt, with silence designed to wound; or beautifully, with dignity intact on both sides. God commands the third. The beautiful leaving has marks the rest of the Qur'an spells out: no retaliation (*"and when they pass by idle talk, they pass by with dignity,"* 25:72); no lingering hatred (*"repel evil with that which is better,"* 41:34); and a door left open for return, as the Prophet ﷺ left every door open — the doors of Ṭāʾif, which stoned him, and of Makkah, which exiled him, until both cities entered Islam. Beautiful leaving is thus not the end of the relationship but its suspension in grace: I withdraw from your mockery, not from your humanity; from your words, not from your possible future.

**Disengagement Before Confrontation**

There is strategic wisdom here as well. At this stage of the mission, the Muslims were powerless: no state, no arms, no tribal protection for most. Confrontation would have meant annihilation; capitulation would have meant extinction of the message. The verse charts the third way: *disengage with dignity*. Withdraw from futile argument, conserve energy for worship and preparation, and let time and truth do their work. The principle generalizes far beyond seventh-century Makkah. Every wise counselor knows that some arguments cannot be won because the other party is not arguing but mocking — and mockery, engaged, only multiplies. The beautiful leaving starves it of fuel. Modern discourse, with its arenas designed to reward outrage, desperately needs this verse's ethic: the disciplined refusal to perform anger for an audience, the quiet exit from conversations whose purpose is humiliation rather than truth. Silence, the Prophet ﷺ taught, can itself be wisdom — *"Whosoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent"* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47) — and this verse shows silence's noblest form: not sullen, not scheming, but beautiful, leaving even opponents with nothing to report but grace.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:11

> **Leave to Me the deniers living in luxury, and be gentle with them for a while.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Leave Them to Me": God Assumes the Case**

The verse opens with a striking divine utterance: *wa dharnī wa'l-mukadhdhibīn* — "and leave to Me the deniers." The verb *dharnī* ("leave Me," i.e., leave them to Me, let Me deal with them) is God's assumption of jurisdiction. The Prophet ﷺ is told, in effect: this case is no longer yours; hand over the file. The deniers (*mukadhdhibīn* — those who call the truth a lie) are now God's direct defendants. The same formula appears at the same early stage elsewhere — *"So leave Me with whosoever denies this discourse"* (68:44); *"Leave Me with him whom I created alone"* (74:11) — always carrying the same double meaning: *relief* for the Prophet, who is discharged from anxiety over results, and *terror* for the deniers, who now face not a powerless preacher but the Lord of the East and the West. There is a precise human analogy: when a harassed citizen's complaint is taken up personally by the highest authority — "leave this to me" — the citizen rests and the accused trembles, for the case has moved to a court from which there is no appeal. Every verse of this form re-enacts that transfer. The missionary's duty is delivery; requital belongs to Another.

**Luxury as a Spiritual Condition**

The deniers are further described: *ulī al-naʿmah* — "those possessed of luxury," the affluent, the comfortable. The qualification is not incidental. The Qur'an consistently observes that material ease, unaccompanied by gratitude, hardens the heart against truth. Luxury whispers that the present order is self-sustaining, that its beneficiaries need nothing — least of all a prophet telling them they will be judged. The archetype is established here and repeated across the Book: it is the *mutrafūn* — the luxury-steeped — who lead the rejection of every messenger (*"And We sent no warner to a town but that its luxury-livers said, 'We disbelieve in what you have been sent with,'"* 34:34). The mechanism is psychological before it is moral. Comfort narrows sympathy: the well-fed find it hard to credit warnings about hunger; the secure find judgment an abstraction. Wealth also purchases the illusion of control — lawyers, guards, physicians, influence — which mimics divine attributes just closely enough to make the real Divine seem superfluous. This is why the Prophet ﷺ warned, in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī:

> *"By God, it is not poverty that I fear for you. Rather I fear that the world will be spread out for you as it was spread out for those before you, and you will compete for it as they competed for it, and it will destroy you as it destroyed them."* (al-Bukhārī 3158; Muslim 2961)

The verse names luxury not to condemn wealth itself — the Qur'an honors lawful provision — but to mark its specific spiritual peril: ease that forgets the Ease-Giver becomes a veil thicker than poverty ever wove.

**Gentleness for a While: The Meaning of Respite**

The verse closes with a command that puzzles first readers: *wa mahhil'hum qalīlā* — "and grant them respite for a little," be gentle with them for a while. Why should deniers luxury-drunk and hostile be granted gentleness and time? The commentators, led by Ibn Kathīr, discern three layered wisdoms. First, respite is *opportunity*: the time granted may be the time in which they repent — and many of the luxury-class deniers of Makkah, including Khālid ibn al-Walīd and ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, did exactly that, becoming pillars of Islam. To destroy them immediately would have been to destroy future believers. Second, respite is *evidence*: the longer truth is displayed before them and refused, the more just the eventual requital — excuses evaporate with time. Third, respite is itself, for the obstinate, the beginning of punishment: the Qur'an calls it *istidrāj*, gradual leading-on — *"We shall lead them on step by step, whence they know not"* (68:44) — in which continued ease while persisting in wrongdoing deepens guilt and multiplies the final account, as in *"We grant them enjoyment a little, then We compel them toward a grave punishment"* (31:24). Gentleness for a while is thus simultaneously mercy for the redeemable, justice for the record, and a tightening noose for the hardened. Only divine wisdom can grant one respite that serves all three ends at once.

**The Relief of Surrendered Outcomes**

For the believer laboring for any good cause — a parent with a rebellious child, a teacher with a heedless student, a caller with a mocking audience — this verse offers one of the Qur'an's great psychological liberations: *outcomes are not your jurisdiction*. The Prophet ﷺ, the most eloquent warner in history, was told to hand the file over. How much more the ordinary striver? The verse does not permit abandoning effort — the Prophet continued preaching for thirteen Makkan years after it — but it forbids the specific torment of *responsibility for results*. Do the work beautifully; leave the deniers to God; be gentle meanwhile. The gentleness is possible only because the outcome is surrendered: one can afford to be gracious toward opponents when one is not desperately trying to force their conversion by Friday. Modern burnout, including religious burnout, so often comes from carrying what this verse commands us to set down — the crushing belief that if others do not change, we have failed. God assumes the case. The servant's task is delivery with excellence; the results were never his to produce, and laying them down is not defeat but obedience.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:12

> **Truly with Us are fetters and Hellfire,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Respite to Reality**

This verse answers the question verse 11 deliberately leaves hanging: the deniers are left to God — but what awaits them *with* God? The answer is immediate and stark: *inna ladaynā ankālan wa jaḥīmā* — "truly with Us are fetters and Hellfire." The particle of emphasis (*inna*) and the presentation (*ladaynā* — "with Us," already prepared, already in divine custody) strip away any sense that punishment is hypothetical or distant. The fetters exist *now*, in God's keeping, awaiting their occupants as a prepared prison awaits sentenced criminals. The verse's brevity is part of its force: no argument, no elaboration, just inventory. Two nouns — shackles, Blaze — placed before the luxury-livers of verse 11 like exhibits before a court. The Qur'an often pairs the announcement of respite with the announcement of what respite defers, lest gentleness be mistaken for indifference: time is granted, but the destination is fixed, and every day of respite misused is a day walked closer to chains already forged.

**Fetters: Bondage as the Shape of Requital**

The first item, *ankāl* (singular *nikl*), means heavy fetters, shackles, chains that bind the neck, hands, or feet — restraints so weighty they prevent all movement. The Qur'an employs the image elsewhere for the same population: *"Truly We have made fetters for the necks of the disbelievers"* — *"We placed shackles upon their necks, up to the chins, so they are stiff-necked"* (36:8). The symbolism is exact and retributive in the deepest sense. These are people who refused all restraint in the world — no curb on appetite, no check on arrogance, no submission to law — and their requital is absolute restraint: bound hand, foot, and neck, unable to move an inch. The freedom they worshipped becomes the bondage they wear. There is also a present-tense truth folded into the image that the Sufis and moralists drew out: the denier is *already* fettered. The miser is chained to his vault, the arrogant to his image, the addict to his craving — each drags through life a weight he cannot set down. The eschatological fetters only make visible and permanent the bondage voluntarily worn in the world. Hell, in this reading, does not invent new tortures so much as unveil old ones: the chains appear because they were always there.

**Hellfire Named Jaḥīm**

The second item is *jaḥīm* — the Blaze, one of the Qur'an's proper names for Hell, denoting a fiercely blazing, all-consuming fire. The Qur'an distinguishes the Fire's aspects with a vocabulary of precision: *jahannam* (the deep Pit), *saʿīr* (the raging Flame), *saqar* (the scorching Fire), *ḥuṭamah* (the crushing Fire), *hāwiyah* (the Abyss), *laẓā* (the flaming Fire) — each name a facet of one reality. *Jaḥīm* emphasizes furious, roaring combustion, the fire at its most violent. Its pairing with fetters completes the scene: bound so they cannot flee, encircled by flame so there is nowhere to flee to. The verse thus compresses into five Arabic words the two dimensions of requital — confinement and torment — that later, longer passages will unfold at length (e.g., 14:49–50; 69:30–37). The compression suits the sūrah's early date and urgent purpose: these verses are not yet legislating the details of eschatology but sounding its alarm. A fire alarm does not describe combustion chemistry; it names the fire and demands movement. This verse is the alarm.

**Why the Tender Sūrah Turns Severe**

A reader may feel the shock of transition: the sūrah that opened with the tender "O thou enwrapped" and the intimate night vigil now speaks of shackles and blazes. The shock is instructive, for it mirrors the Qur'an's complete moral vision. Tenderness toward the Prophet and severity toward obstinate denial are not contradictions but two faces of one justice — as a physician is gentle with the compliant patient and grave with the one tearing out his stitches. The severity, moreover, is itself a mercy to three audiences. To the deniers, it is a final warning while respite still runs — the alarm rung *because* escape remains possible. To the persecuted believers, it is consolation: your tormentors' power is temporary and borrowed; the real fetters are being kept for them. And to all later readers, it establishes the moral seriousness of the universe: choices have destinations, and the Qur'an loves its readers too much to hide the map's darkest region. A revelation that promised only comfort would be a lullaby, not guidance. This verse keeps the Book honest — and honesty, even about Hell, is a form of divine compassion.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:13

> **food that chokes and a painful punishment—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**When Nourishment Becomes Torment**

The inventory of requital continues: *wa ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣah* — "and food that chokes," food possessed of *ghuṣṣah*, the strangling catch in the throat that can neither be swallowed nor spat out. The Qur'an elsewhere specifies the menu of Hell in horrifying detail: the tree of Zaqqūm — *"Truly the tree of Zaqqūm is the food of the sinful, like molten brass, boiling in bellies"* (44:43–45); the bitter thorn-plant *ḍarīʿ* — *"They shall have no food but bitter thorn, which neither nourishes nor avails against hunger"* (88:6–7); and filth — *"And no food but filth, which none eat but the iniquitous"* (69:36–37). Al-Jalālayn adds to the list thorns of fire that can be neither vomited nor ingested. This verse gathers all of them under one experience: the choking. The damned, tormented by hunger, grasp at food — and the food itself becomes a second tormentor, lodging in the throat, prolonging agony at the very moment of hoped relief. The cruelty of the image is precise: it is not starvation but *betrayed eating* — need reaching for satisfaction and finding strangulation.

**The Moral Logic of Inverted Food**

Why should food — the gentlest of God's gifts, the daily proof of His mercy — become an instrument of punishment? The Qur'an's answer is the logic of inversion: in Hell, every blessing misused in the world returns in perverted form. The luxury-livers of verse 11 feasted while prophets went hungry; they consumed the unlawful sweetly and swallowed the rights of the weak without choking. Their throats, which took everything easily in the world, find in the next world that nothing goes down. The pattern holds across the Qur'an's eschatology: those who hoarded wealth find it heated and branded upon their flesh (9:35); those who devoured orphans' property find they have devoured only fire into their bellies (4:10); those who refused water to the thirsty find themselves begging for it (7:50). Hell does not import foreign tortures; it *returns* the sinner his own deeds in their true form. The choking food is thus the eschatological truth of every unlawful morsel: it always *was* a thorn; the throat simply could not feel it yet. The verse invites every reader to examine what goes down easily now — the dubious income, the backbiting relished like flavor — and to recognize it before it recognizes him.

**"A Painful Punishment": The Comprehensive Seal**

The verse closes with *wa ʿadhāban alīmā* — "and a painful punishment." After the specific images — fetters, Blaze, choking food — comes the general, all-embracing term: *ʿadhāb alīm*, a punishment that is *painful* in the fullest sense. The adjective *alīm* (from *alam*, pain) in Qur'anic usage covers more than physical hurt; it denotes suffering that penetrates — the pain of regret, of shame, of hope finally extinguished. Its generality is deliberate. The specific torments are specimens; the *ʿadhāb alīm* is the ocean from which they are drawn, exceeding every inventory. The Qur'an uses the phrase dozens of times, for offenses ranging from idolatry to slander to breaking treaties, as though to say: whatever the sin's shape, its requital shares one quality — it *hurts*, truly and deservedly. And the dash at the verse's end in this translation points forward: the punishment is *on a Day when...* — the sentence completes in verse 14, binding the torments to the Day that frames them. Pain has an address and a date. The Qur'an gives both.

**Warning as an Act of Love**

It is worth pausing to ask why the All-Merciful describes such horrors at all — and to a small, fragile community in the first weeks of revelation. The answer is that warning is proportionate to peril, and silence about peril is not kindness. A father who sees his child wandering toward a cliff does not whisper; he shouts, and his shouting is the measure of his love, not his anger. The Qur'an states this logic openly about its own warnings: *"And thy Lord would never destroy the towns unjustly while their people were reforming"* (11:117) — destruction comes only after warning, and warning comes *so that* destruction need never come. Every verse of threat in this sūrah is thus a verse of invitation wearing stern dress: the fetters are shown so they may be avoided, the choking food described so that lawful food may be chosen, the painful punishment announced while the painful deeds can still be abandoned. The luxury-livers who heard these verses in Makkah were being offered, in the very act of being threatened, the only escape that has ever existed: repentance. Many took it. The horror of the description is the width of the door.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:14

> **on a day when the earth and the mountains shake, and the mountains will be like heaps of shifting sand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day That Completes the Sentence**

This verse completes the sentence begun in verse 13: the fetters, the Blaze, the choking food, the painful punishment — all of it arrives *yawma tarjufu'l-arḍu wa'l-jibāl* — "on a Day when the earth and the mountains quake." The verb *rajafa* means to shake, tremble, convulse — the violent shudder of earthquake. The Day of Judgment in the Qur'an is never a quiet courtroom scene; it is a cosmic upheaval in which the solid world itself testifies. The earth — humanity's floor, the one thing every sane person trusts to stay still — shakes. And with it the mountains — the proverbial symbols of permanence in every human language ("firm as a mountain," "old as the hills") — shake too. The verse selects the two most trusted objects in creation and untrusts them both. If the floor moves and the mountains tremble, what remains for the denier to lean on? The Day answers: nothing but deeds. The entire Qur'anic eschatology is contained in that transfer of trust — from the solid that will shake to the God who will not.

**Mountains Like Shifting Sand**

The verse's second image is among the Qur'an's most haunting: *wa kānat al-jibālu kathīban mahīlā* — "and the mountains will be like a heap of shifting sand." *Kathīb* is a dune, a heaped mound of sand; *mahīl* (from *h-y-l*) describes sand so fine and loose that it slips through fingers and collapses underfoot — shifting, pouring, unable to hold shape. The mountain — granite massif, rooted deep, crowned with snow, the very emblem of the immovable — becomes a sandpile collapsing into itself. The Qur'an stages this transformation across many verses, each adding a facet: the mountains are *"borne away and ground to dust in a single grinding"* (69:14); they become *"like carded wool"* (70:9; 101:5) — tufts of fluff drifting on wind; they are *"set in motion"* (18:47; 52:10), walking like clouds. Read together, the images form a sequence of dissolution: shaking, uprooting, crumbling, scattering, vanishing. Modern geology, which has watched mountains rise and erode over eons and seen solid rock flow like liquid under sufficient force, finds nothing absurd in the physics of it; what staggers is the timescale — eons of erosion compressed into a Day. The God who built the mountains grain by grain unbuilds them grain by grain, only faster. Permanence was always a loan, and the Day is its recall.

**Apocalyptic Inversion: The World Turned Inside Out**

The commentators identify this verse as an instance of the Qur'an's *apocalyptic inversions* — the systematic overturning, on the Last Day, of every order of this world. The sūrahs devoted to the Hour (81, 82, 84, 99, 101) catalog the inversions: the sun folded up, the stars falling, the seas set boiling, the pregnant camels abandoned, the wild beasts gathered, the heaven stripped away. Here the inversion strikes the *geological* order: the lowest (earth) convulses, the highest (mountains) collapse. The next verses will extend the inversion to the *human* order (children gray-haired, verse 17) and the *celestial* order (the sky rent asunder, verse 18). The pattern's meaning is theological before it is spectacular. Each inversion unmakes an object of false trust: those who trusted the sun's regularity, the sea's bounty, the mountain's permanence, the sky's shelter discover that all were temporary arrangements, held in place by a Hand that can withdraw. The inversions are thus not destruction for destruction's sake but *disclosure*: the scaffolding removed so the Building may be seen. What abides when mountains flow like sand is stated elsewhere without metaphor:

> *"All that is upon it passes away, and there abides the Face of thy Lord, full of Majesty and Bounty."* (Qur'an 55:26–27)

**The Permanence Illusion and Its Cure**

Why does the Qur'an return so obsessively to the shaking of the solid? Because the human heart builds its idols out of permanence. Every age has its "mountains" — the things assumed too big to fall: empires, currencies, institutions, reputations, the body itself in youth. The Quraysh had theirs: their ancestry, their sanctuary, their trade, their gods of stone. This verse, revealed when Islam was a whisper and Quraysh a mountain, announces the mountain's geology: all massifs are future sand. History has vindicated the pattern with monotonous regularity — every "eternal" empire now a ruin visited by tourists — yet each generation rebuilds the illusion with fresh materials. The verse's cure is not despair but transfer: lean on what does not shake. The Prophet ﷺ embodied the transfer in his supplication, recorded by Muslim, when the wind rose and the sky darkened — moments that reminded him of peoples destroyed by cosmic signs:

> *"O God, I ask Thee of its good, the good that is in it, and the good with which it was sent; and I seek refuge in Thee from its evil, the evil that is in it, and the evil with which it was sent."* (Muslim 899)

Even weather reminded him of the Day. The believer who reads this verse rightly carries a portable earthquake within: a standing awareness that floors shake and mountains pour, and that only the deeds sent forth — prayer, alms, forgiveness sought (verse 20) — arrive intact on the other side of the shaking.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:15

> **We have indeed sent you a messenger as a witness concerning you, just as We sent unto Pharaoh a messenger.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Turns: From Prophet to People**

With this verse the sūrah pivots. Verses 1–9 addressed the Prophet ﷺ; verses 10–14 spoke *about* the deniers in the third person; now the address turns to face them directly: *innā arsalnā ilaykum rasūlan* — "We have indeed sent *to you* a messenger." The pronoun "you" is read by the commentators (al-Ālūsī, al-Jalālayn) as the disbelievers of Makkah — or, more broadly, humanity at large, since the Prophet's ﷺ mission is universal (*"And We sent thee not but as a mercy to the worlds,"* 21:107). The emphatic *innā* ("We have indeed") carries the full weight of divine authority: this sending is fact, not claim; history, not hypothesis. After nine verses of forming the Messenger and five of warning his opponents, God now presents the Messenger *to* the opponents as evidence against them. The structure mirrors a trial: the witness is prepared (verses 1–9), the charges are read (verses 11–14), and now the witness is called to the stand before the accused. No one addressed by this verse can later plead that no messenger came. The sending itself is the proof.

**A Witness Concerning You**

The messenger is described as *shāhidan ʿalaykum* — "a witness concerning you" (*ʿalā* here carrying the sense of "over" or "against": bearing witness over their response). Al-Ṭabarī explains that the Prophet ﷺ will testify on the Day of Judgment concerning what his people did with the message — for the believers, against the deniers. Other commentators (al-Ajwibah, al-Ālūsī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Zamakhsharī) sharpen it to "a witness *against* you": he will testify to their disbelief and opposition. The Qur'an confirms the role universally: every community faces its messenger-witness — *"So how shall it be when We bring a witness from every community, and bring thee as a witness over these?"* (4:41) — a verse that made the Prophet ﷺ weep when Ibn Masʿūd recited it to him (al-Bukhārī 4582; Muslim 800). His tears reveal what the office cost him: to love one's people and to know one must testify against those who refused. The witness-concept also transforms the missionary encounter itself. Every preaching is a deposition; every rejection is entered into record — not by a hostile spy but by the gentlest of witnesses, one who weeps over the testimony he must give. This is why the Prophet ﷺ grieved so deeply over unbelief that God had to console him: *"So do not let thy soul waste away in sorrow for them"* (35:8). The witness loves the accused. That is what makes the testimony terrible.

**Just as We Sent unto Pharaoh a Messenger**

The verse's second half draws the sūrah's master parallel: *kamā arsalnā ilā firʿawna rasūlā* — "just as We sent unto Pharaoh a messenger." Muhammad is likened to Moses; the Quraysh, by implication, to Pharaoh and his people (Ibn Kathīr). The parallel is historically precise. Both prophets were sent to arrogant powers that claimed divinity or its privileges — Pharaoh declaring *"I am your lord most high"* (79:24), the Quraysh declaring their idols and ancestry sufficient. Both powers answered signs with mockery and threats. Both persecuted the believers. And both, the parallel warns, face the same outcome — which verse 16 will state. What is remarkable is the *earliness* of the reference: this sūrah belongs to the first weeks of revelation, yet it assumes its Makkan audience already knows the story of Moses and Pharaoh, at least in outline. The pre-Islamic Arabs, through Jewish and Christian neighbors and Arabian tradition, carried the Exodus in cultural memory — and God builds His warning on ground already laid. The Qur'an's later, extended narrations of Moses (in Sūrahs 7, 10, 20, 26, 28) will fill the outline; here a single clause suffices, because the audience already knows how Pharaoh's story ends. The verse needs only to place the Quraysh inside it.

**History as Warning: The Pattern That Never Breaks**

The Pharaoh-parallel establishes one of the Qur'an's great laws of history: *the pattern of arrogant power confronting divine warning never breaks*. Pharaoh is not merely a man but a *type* — every entrenched power that answers truth with persecution re-enacts him, and inherits his ending. The Qur'an makes the typology explicit elsewhere: *"Have you received the story of Pharaoh? ... So God seized him as an example for the latter and the former"* (79:15, 25) — Pharaoh preserved as *exhibit*, a standing precedent cited against every successor. The verse thus does more than threaten; it *interprets* the Quraysh to themselves. They imagine themselves defenders of ancestral honor against an upstart; God re-describes them as Pharaoh's re-enactors facing Moses' successor. Worldly conflicts always look, from inside, like unprecedented emergencies; revelation reveals them as reruns. The comfort for believers is immense: if the present trial is a rerun, its ending is already known — the sea always closes over the pursuers, in one form or another. Badr, the boycott's end, the conquest of Makkah — the early Muslims watched the pattern hold within a single generation. And the warning to every later Pharaoh, in whatever boardroom or capital he sits, stands as fresh as the day it descended: the messenger has been sent; the witness is recording; the sea is waiting.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:16

> **But Pharaoh disobeyed the messenger, so We seized him with a torrential seizing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Disobedience in a Single Word**

The verse recounts Pharaoh's response in one verb: *fa-ʿaṣā firʿawnu al-rasūl* — "but Pharaoh disobeyed the messenger." *ʿAṣā* (from *ʿ-ṣ-y*) means to rebel, defy, refuse obedience to a rightful command. The Qur'an uses the same verb for humanity's archetypal lapses — Adam *"disobeyed his Lord"* (20:121), Satan was commanded and refused — placing Pharaoh in the oldest lineage of defiance. Note what the verse does *not* say: it does not say Pharaoh disbelieved arguments or rejected evidence. It says he *disobeyed*. The framing is moral before it is intellectual. Pharaoh's problem was never lack of proof — Moses showed him sign after sign, "nine clear signs" (17:101), until even his own magicians surrendered. His problem was that obedience would have cost him his throne, his god-claim, his ordering of the world. So he called the signs sorcery, the messenger a madman, and marched his army into the sea. The verse's single verb thus diagnoses every powerful denier since, including the Quraysh: the barrier is not the mind's confusion but the will's rebellion. Arguments do not convert Pharaohs; only the sea does — and the sea converts them too late.

**A Seizing Described as Wabīl**

The consequence follows with divine terseness: *fa-akhadhnāhu akhdhan wabīlā* — "so We seized him with a *wabīl* seizing." The noun *wabīl* (from *w-b-l*, heavy rain, torrential downpour) denotes what is severe, grievous, evil in outcome — a punishment that falls like a deluge, inescapable and overwhelming. Al-Ṭabarī glosses it as severe, grievous. The Qur'an describes the same seizing elsewhere with matching gravity: God *"seized them with a devastating blow"* (69:10), and states the universal law — *"Such is the seizing of thy Lord when He seizes the towns while they are doing wrong. Surely His seizing is painful, severe"* (11:102). The historical referent is the drowning: the parted sea collapsing upon Pharaoh and his hosts, the mightiest army of its age destroyed by water — the softest of elements, the very substance of life. The instrument of destruction is part of the message. Pharaoh, who boasted *"and these rivers flowing beneath me"* (43:51), mastering water as proof of divinity, was mastered *by* water. The Qur'an's requitals so often turn the boast into the bane: the instrument of pride becomes the instrument of ruin. Every tyranny drowns, in the end, in the river beneath its own balcony.

**"We Seized Him": The Grammar of Divine Agency**

Observe the subject of the seizing: *We* — God Himself. Not Moses, not the Israelites, not natural forces, not historical accident. The Exodus was not a slave revolt that succeeded; it was a divine act witnessed by slaves. This grammar runs through the Qur'an's entire philosophy of history: God seizes peoples — *"And each We seized for his sin"* (29:40) — while human agents are at most instruments. The theology protects believers from two opposite errors. Against triumphalism, it teaches that victories are God's doing, not the movement's brilliance — the Israelites at the sea did nothing but walk through it, terrified. Against despair, it teaches that the outcome never depends on the believers' strength: a handful of unarmed slaves watched the superpower drown because the superpower's quarrel was not with them but with God. The early Muslims, hearing this verse while powerless in Makkah, were being taught to read their own future: your Pharaohs will be seized, not by your swords — you have none — but by the Seizer. Badr would vindicate the grammar within thirteen years: three hundred ill-equipped believers routing a thousand, with the Qur'an commenting, *"You did not slay them, but God slew them; and thou didst not throw when thou threwest, but God threw"* (8:17). The subject of history's decisive verbs is always He.

**The Warning Inside the History**

The verse's final function is prospective: it tells the Quraysh — and every audience after them — that Pharaoh's story is not past but *pending*. The structure "he disobeyed, so We seized" is presented as a completed precedent precisely so the current generation can complete the parallel themselves: *we are disobeying; therefore...* The conclusion is left unstated, which makes it more terrible than any explicit threat. Rhetoric calls this *enthymeme* — the argument whose final step the listener supplies, and therefore cannot evade, since it arrives in his own voice. Each Quraysh notable hearing this verse had to finish it inwardly. Some, like ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb and Ḥamzah, finished it by surrendering to Islam. Others finished it at Badr, in the wells of the battlefield, where the Prophet ﷺ stood over their corpses and addressed them by name — "Have you found what your Lord promised to be true?" — as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim (al-Bukhārī 3976; Muslim 2875). History, for the Qur'an, is never mere record. It is always address: the past deployed as the future's warning, with the present moment as the only court in which the warning can still be answered.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:17

> **So if you disbelieve, how will you guard against a day that would make children gray-haired?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is an Argument**

The verse poses a question that functions as a proof: *fa-kayfa tattaqūna in kafartum yawman yajʿalu'l-wildāna shībā* — "So if you disbelieve, how will you guard yourselves against a Day that makes children gray-haired?" The verb *tattaqūna* (from *w-q-y*, the root of *taqwā*) means to shield, guard, protect oneself — the same root as the "reverent" (*muttaqīn*) whom the Qur'an guides (2:2). The question's logic is airtight: *taqwā* — guarding — is your only shield against that Day; disbelief is the discarding of the shield; so disbelieve, and tell Me — *how* will you guard? The question leaves no answer because there is none, and the absence of an answer *is* the answer. This is the Qur'an's characteristic use of the rhetorical question (*istifhām inkārī*): not seeking information but forcing recognition. The denier is invited to produce his plan for the Day — his wealth, his sons, his idols, his excuses — and to watch each evaporate in the asking. Some readers (al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī) note an alternative shading: "How will you show reverence (*taqwā*) on a Day..." — meaning the disbelievers will find no path from disobedience to reverence once the Day has dawned. Either way the point stands: the Day admits no unguarded entrants, and guards can only be fashioned *before* it.

**Children Gray-Haired: Terror Made Visible**

The Day is described through a single, unforgettable image: *yawman yajʿalu'l-wildāna shībā* — "a Day that makes children (*wildān*, newborns, the youngest) gray-haired (*shīb*, white with age)." Al-Ṭabarī explains: their hair turns white from fear and awe. The image works by violating the most basic law of human biology: gray hair is theslow script of decades; here a single Day writes it on infants. The Qur'an strikes the same note elsewhere with matching horror: *"O mankind, fear your Lord. Truly the quaking of the Hour is a tremendous thing. On the Day you see it, every nursing mother will forget what she suckles, and every pregnant female will lay down her burden, and thou wilt see men drunken, though they are not drunken"* (22:1–2). The nursing mother forgetting her infant, the child gray by evening — these are not tortures inflicted but *reactions* described, and reactions reveal magnitude more honestly than any measurement. We gauge earthquakes by their effects on buildings; God gauges the Day by its effects on the most resilient things in creation — maternal instinct, youthful vitality. When those break, the Day's magnitude needs no further witness.

**Inversion of the Human Order**

The commentators classify this as an *apocalyptic inversion* of the human order, companion to the geological inversion of verse 14 (mountains into sand) and the celestial inversion of verse 18 (the sky rent open). In the world, time moves one way: children grow, the young strengthen, hair grays slowly as strength ebbs. The Day reverses the current: the young age in hours, strength and vitality drain without the dignity of years. The inversion's meaning is moral. Youth in this world is the great excuse — "I will repent when I am old," "judgment is far from me" — and the Qur'an answers by collapsing the distance: on that Day, youth itself grows old. No one can plead the resilience of the young, for the young will be the Day's most vivid victims. The image also inverts the natural shelter-structure of life: in the world, adults shield children from terror; on that Day, adults cannot shield themselves, let alone their young — *"a Day when no soul shall avail another soul aught"* (2:48). Every inversion strips another layer of false refuge, until nothing stands between the soul and its record.

**The Only Guard Is Packed Today**

The verse's practical conclusion is the one the question demands: since no guard avails *on* the Day, the guard must be prepared *before* it — and the sūrah has already named its materials. The night vigil (verses 2–6), the measured recitation (verse 4), the remembrance and devotion (verse 8), the trust in God alone (verse 9), the patient endurance (verse 10) — these are the shield-plates, forged night by night, that the believer wears into the Day. The Qur'an elsewhere names the currency the Day accepts: *"The Day when neither wealth nor sons shall avail, save him who comes to God with a sound heart"* (26:88–89). Wealth cannot bribe terror; sons cannot intercede against quaking; only the *qalb salīm* — the heart made sound through years of vigilance, remembrance, and trust — passes through. The gray-haired children are thus, paradoxically, a mercy to the reader: a terror described in time is a terror that can still be prepared for. The verse frightens in order to equip. The one who trembles at it today, and rises tonight because of it, is already fashioning the guard the question demands — and will find, on the Day when children gray, that his own heart was kept young by the nights he gave to God.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:18

> **The sky shall be rent asunder thereon. His Promise shall be fulfilled.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Roof Torn Open**

The verse continues the Day's portrait: *al-samāʾu munfaṭirun bih* — "the heaven/sky shall be rent asunder thereon" (on that Day). The participle *munfaṭir* (from *f-ṭ-r*, to split, cleave, crack open) depicts the sky splitting apart — the same root as *īnfiṭār*, the Cleaving that names Sūrah 82 (*"When the heaven is cleft asunder"*), and *fatara*, to originate creation by a first splitting. The sky that splits is the same sky the Qur'an elsewhere presents as humanity's most trusted shelter: *"And We made the sky a guarded roof"* (21:32) — a canopy held without pillars (13:2), adorned with stars (37:6), sending down rain in measure. Every human civilization has lived beneath it as under a father's tent. On that Day the tent tears. The Qur'an repeats the rending across many verses with slightly different verbs — the heaven *"split open"* (25:25), *"rent asunder"* (55:37; 69:16), *"split"* (84:1), *"stripped away"* (81:11) — as though circling one unspeakable sight from many angles, each verb catching a different facet of the fracture. The repetition is itself meaningful: of all the Day's inversions, the tearing of the sky most directly unmakes the human sense of *home*. Earth may quake beneath, but the sky was supposed to hold above. When it too gives way, shelter ends — and the soul learns, finally and totally, what verse 9 taught in words: there never was any guardian but Him.

**Inversion of the Created Order**

Where verse 14 inverted the geological order (earth and mountains) and verse 17 the human order (children graying), this verse inverts the *celestial* order — completing the sūrah's triptych of unmaking: below, within, above. Together the three verses dismantle the three tiers of the ancient cosmos: the underfoot, the human, the overhead. Nothing in the created frame survives intact. The systematic completeness is the point. The Qur'an's eschatology does not merely punish individuals within a stable world; it *retires the world itself*, folding the entire stage on which the drama played. The later sūrahs will state the folding explicitly — *"On a Day when We shall roll up the heaven as the rolling up of scrolls for writing"* (21:104) — but this early verse already contains the whole: the roof torn, the show of the world struck, the audience of souls standing in the open before their Lord. Modern cosmology, contemplating the heat-death of the universe or its collapse, has learned to speak of cosmic endings in sober prose; the Qur'an spoke of them in poetry fourteen centuries ago, and added what physics cannot: the ending is not accident but *appointment* — which is exactly what the verse's second clause declares.

**His Promise Shall Be Fulfilled**

The verse closes with certainty's own sentence: *kāna waʿduhu mafʿūlā* — "His Promise has ever been [a thing] done," i.e., His promise *shall* be fulfilled — stated in the past tense (*kāna*, "was") to express absolute inevitability, as though already accomplished. The "Promise" (*waʿd*) encompasses everything God has pledged about that Day: the resurrection, the gathering, the reckoning, the requital — every threat and every hope the sūrah has uttered. The past-tense grammar is the Qur'an's signature for divine certainty: what God has promised is so sure it may be spoken of as done — *"And the command of God is a decree decreed"* (33:38). The clause answers the skepticism the Makkan deniers voiced openly — *"When is this promise, if you are truthful?"* (10:48; 21:38; 36:48) — by relocating the promise from the calendar to the nature of God. The question "when" assumes the promise might fail; the verse answers that failure is impossible, for the Promiser is God, *"and who is truer to his covenant than God?"* (9:111). Delay is not doubt. The sky still holds because the term has not come; when it comes, the verse's first clause and second clause will coincide — the tearing and the truth, together, in a single Hour.

**Living Under a Temporary Roof**

For the believer, the verse sets the whole of life in correct proportion. Every roof under which he sleeps — concrete, tile, or tent — is a small copy of the great roof, and shares its temporariness. Houses crack, careers collapse, empires fall, and finally the sky itself tears: the verse universalizes impermanence until only one permanence remains, the Promise and its Maker. The practical fruit is detachment without despair. The believer builds, plants, marries, and founds — Islam forbids the world-abandonment that impermanence might suggest; the Prophet ﷺ commanded that even if the Hour arrives while one holds a seedling, he should plant it (Aḥmad 3:191) — but he builds *lightly*, holding every structure loosely because he has read its end in advance. And the Promise-keeper's certainty becomes his own stability: while skies may tear, *"God does not break His promise"* (3:9; 30:6). The verse thus leaves the soul in the exact posture the sūrah has cultivated from its opening: unpropped by the world, propped wholly by God — awake in the night beneath a roof he knows will tear, reciting the Word he knows will stand.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:19

> **Truly this is a reminder; so let him who will, take a way unto his Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Message Named: A Reminder**

After the terrors of verses 11–18 — fetters, choking food, quaking earth, gray-haired children, the torn sky — the sūrah pauses to name what all of it *is*: *inna hādhihi tadhkirah* — "truly this is a *tadhkirah*, a reminder, an admonition." The demonstrative "this" is read by the commentators as the Qur'an as a whole (al-Ṭabarī) or this sūrah in particular (Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī) — and both readings converge, for the sūrah is the Qur'an in miniature. The word *tadhkirah* (from *dh-k-r*, to remember) carries the Qur'an's entire theory of revelation in a single noun. Revelation does not primarily *inform* — bringing news of things never known — but *reminds*: it recalls to the soul what it already knows and has forgotten. The Qur'an grounds this claim in the primordial covenant, when God drew forth humanity's seed and asked, *"Am I not your Lord?"* and they answered, *"Yea, we bear witness"* (7:172). Every soul arrives in the world having already testified; life under the world's noise buries the testimony; revelation digs it back up. This is why the Qur'an's dominant verb for heedlessness is *ghaflah* — forgetfulness, drowsiness — and why its proof-texts so often appeal to what the listener *already* senses: the design in creation, the voice of conscience, the certainty of death. The sūrah's terrors are thus not alien impositions but *memoranda*: the Day described is the Day the soul always knew was coming. The identical verse recurs as 76:29 — *"Truly this is a reminder"* — sealing two sūrahs of warning with the same self-description, as though God signs His threats with the same word: *remember*.

**The Will Addressed: Let Him Who Will**

The verse's second clause turns to human freedom: *fa-man shāʾa'ttakhadha ilā rabbihi sabīlā* — "so let him who wills, take a way unto his Lord." After reminder comes choice. The verse places the will (*shāʾa* — whoever wills, wishes, chooses) at the center of response: the way is shown, the walking is yours. Al-Ṭabarī explains "him who will" as whoever among human beings chooses the path of faith and obedience. Read in this sūrah's context, "a way unto his Lord" points back to everything the chapter has enjoined — the night vigil, the measured recitation, the remembrance, the devotion, the trust: these *are* the way, paved night by night toward God. The clause dignifies the human being as the Qur'an consistently does: man is not dragged to salvation nor herded from damnation but *invited* — shown the road, warned of the cliff, and left to his feet. The moral weight of the Last Day depends entirely on this freedom; judgment would be theater if the will were fiction. And the invitation's openness — *whoever* wills, without qualification of tribe, rank, past, or present — keeps the door this sūrah opened in verse 11's respite standing open still. Pharaoh's re-enactors may yet become Moses' followers. The will is the hinge, and it still turns.

**Whose Will? The Classical Debate**

The classical commentators record a profound second reading. The verb "wills" could take God, not man, as its subject: "so let *him whom He wills* take a way" — i.e., guidance follows the divine will, since *"you do not will but that God wills"* (76:30; cf. 81:29). Ibn Kathīr notes this reading alongside the first, and the tension between them — human choice and divine will — became one of Islamic theology's central contemplations. The mainstream resolution, articulated by al-Ṭabarī, al-Ashʿarī, and Ibn Taymiyyah alike, holds both truths without dissolving either: man genuinely chooses, and God's will encompasses all choosing. Ibn Taymiyyah illustrated the harmony through the Qur'an's own pattern: God commands the will (*"let him who will"*), then reveals that all willing occurs within His will — the command establishes responsibility, the encompassing establishes sovereignty, and neither cancels the other. The believer's practical posture follows: *act* as though all depended on your choosing — rise, recite, repent — and *trust* as though all depended on God — for it does. The verse gives the first half of that posture ("let him who will"), and its twin in Sūrah 76 supplies the second half in the very next verse (76:30). Read together, they form the complete engine of the spiritual life: human striving inside divine encompassing, like rowing a boat on a river that itself flows toward God.

**The Way Unto the Lord: What It Looks Like**

"Take a way (*sabīl*) unto his Lord" — the Qur'an's most frequent image for the religious life is the *road*: the straight path (*ṣirāṭ mustaqīm*) of the Fātiḥah, the ways of peace (*subul al-salām*, 5:16), the path of those who turn to God (31:15). The road-image teaches what abstractions cannot: that salvation is *directional* and *progressive* — a matter of facing rightly and walking steadily, not of arriving all at once. In this sūrah the road's first miles are mapped concretely: tonight's vigil is a step, this verse's slow recitation a step, the morning's trust a step. The Prophet ﷺ described the road's divine accompaniment in the beloved ḥadīth qudsī recorded by al-Bukhārī:

> *"Whosoever draws near to Me by a hand's span, I draw near to him by a cubit; and whosoever draws near to Me by a cubit, I draw near to him by a fathom; and whosoever comes to Me walking, I come to him running."* (al-Bukhārī 7405; Muslim 2675)

The road unto the Lord is thus unlike every other road: here the Destination moves toward the traveler, multiplying each step. The verse asks only the first movement of the will — *let him who will, take* — and the ḥadīth promises what the will cannot achieve alone. Between the human step and the divine running, no sincere traveler is lost. The sūrah's warnings showed what lies off the road; this verse shows the road itself, open, lit by night-vigils, ending at the Lord — and leaves the reader, as all revelation leaves him, at the road's head, with the will in his hand and the night before him.

## Sūrah al-Muzzammil 73:20

> **Truly thy Lord knows that thou dost stand vigil well-nigh two-thirds of the night, or a half of it, or a third of it, as do a group of those who are with thee; and God measures out the night and the day. He knows that you will not keep count of it and has relented unto you; so recite that which is easy for you of the Qur'an. He knows that some of you will be sick, while others travel upon the earth, seeking God's Bounty, and others fight in the way of God. So recite that which is easy of it, perform the prayer, give the alms, and lend unto God a goodly loan — whatever good you send forth for your souls, you will find it with God better and greater in reward. And seek God's Forgiveness. Truly God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse Revealed a Year Later**

This final verse stands apart from the sūrah in time. Most commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Ālūsī) hold that it was revealed some twelve months after verses 1–19, while others place it in the Madinan period. Its length alone signals the distance: it is among the longest single verses of the early revelation, looking back on the night vigil from a vantage point of experience. Between the command and this verse lies a year of practice — a year in which the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions stood as commanded, in halves and thirds and two-thirds, until their feet swelled and their nights were transformed. The verse is thus God's *review* of that year: He reports what He saw, acknowledges what it cost, and then — in one of the Qur'an's most tender legislative acts — lightens what He had imposed. The sūrah that began with a man under a blanket ends with God bending over a community of standing servants and saying, in effect: I saw; I measured; it was heavy; let Me lighten it. No verse in the Qur'an displays more completely the divine pedagogy — command, practice, acknowledgment, easing — and none repays slower reading.

**"Thy Lord Knows": The Vigil Seen and Recorded**

The verse opens: *inna rabbaka yaʿlamu annaka taqūmu adnā min thuluthay al-layl wa niṣfahū wa thuluthah* — "Truly thy Lord knows that thou standest vigil near two-thirds of the night, or half of it, or a third of it." The first word of the easing is *knowledge*: before God lightens the burden, He testifies that He *saw* it borne. The quantities — two-thirds, half, third — mirror the spectrum of verses 3–4, confirming that the Prophet ﷺ lived inside the commanded range, varying with strength and season. The detail that God *knows the fractions* carries the verse's first consolation: no standing went unrecorded, no tired verse unheard. Worship performed in darkness, unseen by any human eye, was measured to the fraction by the only Eye that matters. The Qur'an generalizes the comfort: *"And thy Lord suffices as Knower of the sins of His servants, Seer"* (17:17) — and, by the same seeing, Knower of their standings. For every believer whose devotion goes unwitnessed — the night prayer no one knows, the charity no one counts, the forgiveness no one applauds — this clause is God's affidavit: *I know*. The Fractions Glory records what the world's cameras miss, and its records are the only ones read aloud on the Day (17:13–14).

**A Group Who Stood With Him**

The verse adds: *wa ṭāʾifatun min alladhīna maʿak* — "as does a group of those who are with thee." The Prophet ﷺ did not stand alone. A band of Companions — the sources describe them standing until their feet swelled (al-Jalālayn, al-Ṭabarī) — emulated him night after night through that first hard year. Consider what this means historically: in the sūrah's first year, Islam's entire devotional life consisted of this — a few dozen believers rising in scattered Makkan houses, reciting the handful of verses revealed so far, standing halves and thirds of Arabian nights. No mosque, no pulpit, no call to prayer yet — only standing servants in dark rooms, bound to one another by a shared secret of worship. That "group" (*ṭāʾifah*) became the seed of everything: from their night-standing came the steadfastness that endured the boycott, the Hijrah, Badr, and beyond. Movements are built in public, but their *members* are built in private; the verse preserves the earliest evidence that Islam's first generation was night-forged. And God's mention of them — unnamed, counted only as "a group with thee" — honors anonymous faithfulness as the Qur'an always does: heaven keeps the names earth never learned.

**God Measures the Night and the Day**

The verse continues: *wa'llāhu yuqaddiru al-layl wa'l-nahār* — "and God measures out the night and the day." *Yuqaddir* (from *q-d-r*, measure, decree, apportion) means He determines their extents, alternates their lengths through the seasons, and knows their exact quantities. The clause serves the easing directly: *God* knows the night's true measure — its shifting length, its watches and fractions — even when *you* cannot track it. The early community had no clocks; "half the night" in summer differs from winter's; clouds obscure the stars; sleep overtakes the watcher. Precise compliance with the commanded fractions was, for ordinary believers, effectively impossible to guarantee — which is exactly what the next clause states. But the sentence also carries a wider theology of *qadar* (divine measure): the One who apportions night and day apportions all things — provision, lifespan, strength, trial — in exact measures. The believers' inability to measure the night is a small instance of humanity's universal inability to measure anything perfectly; God's perfect measuring covers every human mismeasurement. The verse thus moves the ground of acceptance from human precision to divine knowledge: you cannot count the night exactly, but the Measurer counts it for you.

**The Relenting: "You Will Not Keep Count"**

Then the easing itself: *ʿalima an lan tuḥṣūhu fa-tāba ʿalaykum* — "He knows that you will not keep count of it, so He has relented unto you." Note the shift the commentators mark: the verse began addressing the Prophet ﷺ in the singular (*rabbaka*, *annaka taqūmu*) and now turns to the community in the plural (*lan tuḥṣūhu*, *ʿalaykum*). The Prophet's standing is commended; the community's capacity is assessed — and the assessment is merciful realism. *Lan tuḥṣūhu*: you will never fully encompass it, count it exactly, perform it perfectly. The verb *aḥṣā* means to encircle by counting, to master by enumeration — and God declares, from eternity, that this particular obligation exceeds the community's counting. So *tāba ʿalaykum* — literally "He turned toward you," i.e., He relented, He returned to you with mercy, He lifted the strictness. Most commentators (al-Ṭabarī) judge that this clause *abrogated* (*nasakha*) the obligatory status of the night vigil, rendering it voluntary — explicitly confirmed later: *"And keep vigil in prayer for part of the night, as a supererogatory act for thee"* (17:79), where the word *nāfilah* (supererogatory) names its new station. The abrogation here is of burden, not of value: the vigil remains the crown of voluntary worship, but its omission is no longer sin. God showed the summit, let them climb it for a year, then moved the camp to sustainable ground — while leaving the summit visible for all who would still ascend.

**"Recite What Is Easy": The New Rule of the Night**

In place of measured fractions, God sets a new rule of beautiful simplicity: *fa'qraʾū mā tayassara min al-qurʾān* — "so recite what is easy for you of the Qur'an." *Mā tayassara* — whatever comes easily, whatever is facilitated, whatever your memory, strength, and night allow. The command is repeated twice in the verse for emphasis, bracketing the excuses. Some commentators (al-Ṭabarī) record early opinions fixing minimums — fifty verses, a hundred, even five (Ibn Kathīr) — but the verse's own wording refuses fixation: *ease* is the measure, and ease varies by person and night. The Prophet's ﷺ practice after the easing illustrated the new rule: ʿĀʾishah reported that he customarily prayed eleven units (*rakʿahs*) in the night, as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"He would pray four, and do not ask about their beauty and length; then he would pray four, and do not ask about their beauty and length; then he would pray three."* (al-Bukhārī 1147; Muslim 738)

Eleven units of long, beautiful standing — far less than two-thirds of the night, yet sustained for a lifetime. The new rule teaches the master-principle of all voluntary devotion: *regular ease outranks occasional heroism*. God prefers the small steady flame to the great sporadic blaze — *"the most beloved deed is the most regular, even if small"* (al-Bukhārī 43) — and this verse is that preference's first legislation. The insomniac's two verses, the worker's brief standing, the mother's quiet chapter while the house sleeps — all of it is *mā tayassar*, all of it commanded, all of it seen.

**Three Excuses That Honor Three Lives**

The verse then names, with divine specificity, who finds the vigil difficult: *ʿalima an sayakūnu minkum marḍā wa ākharūna yaḍribūna fi'l-arḍ yabtaghūna min faḍli'llāh wa ākharūna yuqātilūna fī sabīli'llāh* — "He knows that some of you will be sick, while others travel the earth seeking God's bounty, and others fight in God's way." Three conditions, three dignities. The *sick* — their excuse needs no argument; Islam's law lifts burdens from the ill as a matter of course. The *travelers seeking God's bounty* — merchants, workers, seekers of provision on the road — whose labor is itself honored as "seeking God's bounty" (*faḍl*, the same word used for divine grace): earning a lawful living is re-described as seeking grace, sanctifying the marketplace. And the *fighters in God's way* — those straining in struggle, whose nights belong to watchfulness and whose days to peril. The mention of fighting has led some to date the verse Madinan, since armed struggle began after the Hijrah; others (Ibn Kathīr) see it as foreshadowing, revealed in Makkah as prophecy of travails to come. Either way, the trio covers the human conditions that break routines: illness breaks the body, travel breaks the schedule, struggle breaks all normalcy. God's law bends for all three — not by abolishing devotion but by *easing* it: recite what is easy. The Lawgiver who knows the sickbed, the caravan, and the battlefield legislates for their occupants by name. No believer in any of these states need feel abandoned by a religion of night-standers; the verse carries his exemption signed by God.

**Three Pillars Reaffirmed: Prayer, Alms, Charity**

After easing the night, the verse anchors the day: *wa aqīmū al-ṣalāh wa ātū al-zakāh wa aqriḍū'llāha qarḍan ḥasanā* — "and perform the prayer, give the alms, and lend unto God a goodly loan." Three institutions, stated in the order of their permanence. *Perform the prayer* (*aqīmū al-ṣalāh* — establish it, erect it upright, as in 2:3) refers to the obligatory prayers (al-Ṭabarī) — the five daily prayers that would soon be enjoined at the Ascension and would carry what the night vigil had begun: regular, communal, scheduled worship. *Give the alms* (*al-zakāh*) is, remarkably, the first mention of *zakāh* in chronological revelation order. Since the specific rates and rules of *zakāh* were institutionalized only in Madinah, the commentators (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī) read it here as charity in general or as the early establishment of the duty whose details came later — the seed of the alms-system planted in the first year. *Lend unto God a goodly loan* (*qarḍan ḥasanā* — a beautiful, generous, willing loan) extends giving beyond obligation into voluntary charity, named a "loan" to God that He will repay multiplied (cf. 2:245; 5:12; 57:11, 18; 64:17). Here as in 5:12, the goodly loan stands beside prayer and alms as the third leg of devotion: duty to God upward (prayer), duty to society outward (alms), generosity beyond duty (the loan). The night vigil was eased; these three are not. They are the load-bearing walls of the religious life, and the verse sets them in place before the sūrah closes.

**The Economics of Eternity: Better and Greater**

The verse then states the divine economics that fund all three: *wa mā tuqaddimū li-anfusikum min khayr tajidūhu ʿinda'llāhi huwa khayran wa aʿẓama ajrā* — "whatever good you send forth for your souls, you will find it with God, better and greater in reward." Every phrase labors. *Tuqaddimū* — "you send forth, advance": good deeds are *forwarded*, dispatched ahead like luggage sent before a journey; the believer travels light through the world because his wealth has gone on before him. *Li-anfusikum* — "for your souls": the beneficiary of charity is the giver himself, a truth the Qur'an insists upon — *"Whatever good you spend is for yourselves"* (2:272) — dissolving the illusion that giving diminishes. *Tajidūhu ʿinda'llāh* — "you will find it with God": nothing forwarded is lost; God is the warehouse that neither burns, floods, nor bankrupts. *Huwa khayran* — "it is better": what returns exceeds what was sent in *quality* — purer, nobler, unmixed with the world's dross. *Wa aʿẓama ajrā* — "and greater in reward": and exceeds it in *quantity*, multiplied by the divine arithmetic the Qur'an states elsewhere:

> *"The parable of those who spend their wealth in God's way is that of a grain that grows seven ears, in every ear a hundred grains. And God multiplies for whomsoever He will."* (Qur'an 2:261)

One grain becomes seven hundred, and seven hundred a floor, not a ceiling — *"God multiplies for whomsoever He will."* Ibn Kathīr draws the verse's practical moral: what you spend in God's way is *better* than what you keep for yourself or leave as inheritance. The kept decays; the inherited divides and disputes; the forwarded multiplies. Every miser keeps his grain in the barn where weevils find it; every giver plants his where God grows it. The arithmetic of eternity inverts the arithmetic of earth: here, keeping is losing and sending is finding.

**Seeking Forgiveness: The Seal on Every Deed**

The sūrah's final imperative: *wa'staghfirū'llāh* — "and seek God's forgiveness." After the year's standings, after prayer, alms, and loans — *seek forgiveness*. The placement is the Qur'an's standing lesson in the etiquette of devotion: no deed, however strenuous, arrives perfect, and the wise servant seals every obedience with repentance for its defects. The Prophet ﷺ, whose night standings this very verse commended, sought forgiveness seventy to a hundred times *daily*, as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"By God, I seek God's forgiveness and repent to Him more than seventy times a day."* (al-Bukhārī 6307)

> *"O people, repent to God, for I repent to Him a hundred times a day."* (Muslim 2702)

If the commended Prophet repents a hundred times, what of the negligent? The command also universalizes the verse's mercy: the same God who eased the vigil opens the door of forgiveness beside the eased command, so that failures under the old burden and negligence under the new ease both find remedy. *Istighfār* is the solvent of every shortfall — and the verse closes by naming the Guarantor twice: *inna'llāha ghafūrun raḥīm* — "Truly God is Forgiving, Merciful." *Ghafūr* (from *gh-f-r*, to cover, as a helmet covers the head): He covers sins so they neither shame nor destroy. *Raḥīm*: He adds mercy beyond covering — blessing, nearness, reward. The sūrah that opened with a trembling man under a blanket ends with the two names that shelter every trembling soul since. Between the blanket and the forgiveness lies the whole path: stand, recite, remember, trust, endure, warn, give — and when all is done, seek forgiveness from the Forgiving, the Merciful.
