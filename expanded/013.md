# Sūrah al-Raʿd (Chapter 13) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Named After the Thunder**

Sūrah al-Raʿd takes its name from the thirteenth verse, in which the thunder is described as hymning God's praise:

> *"The thunder hymns His praise, as do the angels, in awe of Him."* (Qur'an 13:13)

Naming a chapter after a natural phenomenon is itself a statement. The thunder — a sound that ancient peoples trembled at and sometimes worshiped — is here recruited into the service of divine praise. What frightens is not a rival god; it is one more voice in the cosmic chorus of glorification. The sūrah thus opens its account of the natural world by reinterpreting it: every power in nature, from the heavens to the lightning to the thunder to the shadows on the ground, exists in a posture of submission to the one Creator. This is the theological spine of the chapter, and it is struck again and again.

**The Opening Letters and the Six Sūrahs**

Al-Raʿd belongs to a series of six sūrahs that begin with the detached letters *alif, lām, rāʾ* — the series running through chapters 10, 11, 12, 14, and 15 — and it is the only one of the six to add a fourth letter, *mīm*, producing *alif, lām, mīm, rāʾ*. The detached letters are among the Qur'an's celebrated mysteries, and their presence at the head of this sūrah announces that what follows is no ordinary speech. As will be discussed under verse 1, the letters function at once as a summons to attention, as a seal marking divine authorship, and as the raw material of the very challenge the Qur'an lays down: these are the letters you know and speak daily, yet from them has come a book you cannot imitate.

**Makkan or Madinan: A Question the Text Itself Answers**

The scholars of the earliest generations differed over where the sūrah was revealed. Some held it to be wholly Makkan; others held it to be Madinan; still others judged it Makkan in substance with certain verses revealed in Madinah. Al-Zamakhsharī, with characteristic caution, simply records that its time of revelation is uncertain. The contents of the sūrah, however, read unmistakably like a Makkan argument. Its audience is a community that rejects the Prophet's message (v. 1), doubts the Resurrection (v. 5), asks mockingly for tangible signs (vv. 7, 27, 31), and refuses to acknowledge that Muhammad ﷺ is a messenger at all (v. 43). Its weapons are the weapons of the Makkan phase: appeals to the visible order of nature, parables drawn from water and fire, warnings of past nations destroyed, and a portrait of Paradise and Hell. The verse that reports the disbelievers' refusal to recognize the name "the Compassionate" (v. 30) fits the historical moment when the Makkan idolaters, hearing *al-Raḥmān* used as a divine name, retorted, "And who is the Compassionate?"

**The Architecture of the Chapter: Nature as Proof, Truth as Endurance**

The sūrah builds its case in a clear sequence. Verses 1–4 establish the source of revelation and point to the cosmos — the heavens raised without visible support, the sun and moon running to an appointed term, the spread earth, the mountains, the paired fruits, the variegated produce of a single water — as evidence for the Creator and for the meeting with Him. Verses 5–7 turn to the disbelievers' objections: their astonishment at bodily resurrection, their reckless haste for punishment, their demand for miracles. Verses 8–15 assert the reach of divine knowledge and power, from the womb to the secret word, to the guardian angels, to the lightning and the thunder. Verses 16–17 deliver two of the Qur'an's most powerful arguments — the argument from Lordship and the parable of water and froth distinguishing truth from falsehood. Verses 18–29 draw the portrait of the two communities and their two destinies, culminating in the celebrated verse on the remembrance of God and the peace of hearts. Verses 30–43 close the chapter by answering the remaining objections about prophethood, signs, and the universality of revelation, and by assuring the Prophet ﷺ that his task is only to convey, while the reckoning belongs to God.

**Two Parables, Two Destinies, One Center of Gravity**

Two images dominate the chapter. The first is the parable of water and froth (v. 17): rain fills the riverbeds, a torrent carries a swelling scum that soon vanishes, while the water that benefits mankind remains; likewise the ore refined in fire casts off its dross while the pure metal endures. This is the Qur'an's most complete illustration of the fate of truth and falsehood. The second is the image of the heart at peace (v. 28): *"Are not hearts at peace in the remembrance of God?"* Between them the sūrah moves from the cosmic to the intimate — from thunder and lightning to the still point within the human breast. It is this double movement, outward to the signs of nature and inward to the repose of the heart, that gives al-Raʿd its distinctive power. The same God whose thunder hymns His praise is the God in whose remembrance hearts find rest.

What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary. Each verse is treated as a complete, independent discourse — as the verse-by-verse method demands — drawing upon the Arabic language, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, and others), the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, and the living reasoning that connects this fourteen-hundred-year-old text to the conditions of the modern human being.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:1

> **Alif. Lām. Mīm. Rā. These are the signs of the Book; and that which has been sent down unto thee from thy Lord is the truth, but most of mankind believe not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Four Letters That Guard the Threshold**

The sūrah opens with four detached letters: *alif, lām, mīm, rāʾ*. Twenty-nine chapters of the Qur'an begin this way, each with one of a fixed set of single letters or letter combinations — *alif-lām-mīm*, *alif-lām-rāʾ*, *ḥā-mīm*, *kāf-hā-yā-ʿayn-ṣād*, *yā-sīn*, *ṣād*, *qāf*, and *nūn*, among others. These are the *ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*, "the cut-off letters," so called because they are pronounced as separate letters rather than blended into words. Al-Raʿd is the only chapter in the entire Qur'an to open with the combination *alif-lām-mīm-rāʾ*: it stands as a unique marker among the six sūrahs of the *alif-lām-rāʾ* family, as though the mīm had been added to distinguish this particular door from its five sisters.

The very fact that these letters resist easy explanation is part of their message. A book that could be exhaustively paraphrased would be a book of man's making; the presence of an irreducible opening announces from the first line that this is a text whose depths will not be mastered in one reading.

**What the Letters Mean: The Scholars' Positions**

The classical commentators preserved several coherent positions on the letters, and the range itself is instructive. The first and most cautious, associated with the early generations and recorded by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, is that the letters belong to the *mutashābihāt* — the verses whose full meaning is known only to God — and that the believer says "I believe in it; all of it is from our Lord" (cf. 3:7) and passes on. A second position, transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās (d. 68/687) and reported widely in the commentaries, reads the letters as abbreviations of the divine names and attributes: the *alif* of Allāh, the *lām* of al-Laṭīf (the Subtle), the *mīm* of al-Majīd (the Glorious), and the *rāʾ* of al-Raḥmān (the Compassionate), each letter standing as a title of the One about to speak. A third position, associated with al-Kisāʾī and others, held that the letters function as an oath, or as devices of *tanbīh* — a summons to attention, the way a speaker clears his throat and raps the table before the serious words begin. Yet a fourth position, favored by al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī, located their function in the very challenge they enable.

**The Miracle Hidden in Familiar Letters**

The fourth position deserves careful reflection because it is the most logically satisfying. The letters that open these sūrahs are, quite deliberately, the ordinary letters of the Arabic alphabet — the same letters from which the disbelievers' own speech is composed. Al-Zamakhsharī observed that God begins these chapters with the very material of human language and then challenges the Arabs to produce the like of what He has made from it, as in:

> *"Or do they say, 'He has fabricated it?' Say, 'Then bring a sūrah like it, and call upon whomsoever you can apart from God, if you are truthful.'"* (Qur'an 10:38)

The point is devastating in its simplicity. No new alphabet, no unfamiliar instrument, is required to compose the Qur'an — only the same letters the Makkans used every day in their markets and their poetry. Yet from those same letters no one could produce a single chapter of its like. The letters thus stand at the head of the sūrah as both the raw material and the proof of the challenge. It is as if a master builder, challenged to show that his craft surpasses others, were to hand his rivals the same bricks and the same tools and say: build. The identity of the material throws the difference of the result into the sharpest possible relief.

**The Book and Its Signs**

The letters are then followed by the declaration: *"These are the signs of the Book."* Here a question arises that the commentators answered in two complementary ways. Some understood "the Book" to mean this specific sūrah, so that "the signs" are its verses — the āyāt of al-Raʿd itself, which the reader is about to enter. Others understood "the Book" more broadly as the Qur'an as a whole, so that the opening asserts: what stands before you, letter by letter and verse by verse, are the signs of the Book of God. Both readings converge on the same reality, for the sūrah is a portion of the Book and the Book is present in every sūrah. The word *āyāt*, "signs," is itself worth pausing over, because it deliberately names the verses by the same word used for the visible signs in nature — the heavens, the mountains, the alternation of night and day that the very next verses will invoke. The Qur'an is not a collection of assertions to be believed on bare authority; it is a tissue of signs, each verse a marker pointing to a reality beyond itself, just as the natural world is a tissue of signs pointing to its Maker.

**The Truth Is Not Decided by a Headcount**

The verse then makes its central claim and immediately acknowledges its reception:

> *"And that which has been sent down unto thee from thy Lord is the truth, but most of mankind believe not."*

Notice the structure. First, the affirmation: what has come to the Prophet is *al-ḥaqq*, the truth — not merely useful, not merely beautiful, but true, in the sense of corresponding to reality and issuing from the Real. Then, without flinching, the qualification: most people do not believe it. The order matters. The truth is stated first and the disbelief second, so that the second can never be mistaken for evidence against the first. This is a permanent lesson in the logic of truth. A proposition is not made false by the number of people who reject it, any more than it is made true by the number who accept it. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same point in almost the same words:

> *"And most of mankind, though thou strive, are not believers."* (Qur'an 12:103)

> *"The truth is from thy Lord; so be not among the doubters."* (Qur'an 10:94)

And again, with a clarity that reads like a direct answer to the modern confusion of popularity with validity:

> *"Most of them follow naught but conjecture; truly conjecture avails nothing against the truth."* (Qur'an 10:36)

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr understood the phrase "most of mankind believe not" as a statement of observed fact — the Prophet ﷺ was not being told that rejection was surprising, but that it was expected, and that his task was therefore to convey with patience rather than to compel with despair. The majority's unbelief is here not a scandal but a confirmation of the very human freedom the message presupposes.

**Why the Majority Does Not Believe: A Psychological Reading**

The verse invites a further question that classical commentary answers with precision: why should most people reject what is true? Al-Qurṭubī and others locate the answer in the nature of belief itself. Faith is not the automatic result of seeing evidence; it is a commitment that carries demands — accountability, restraint, humility before a Lord, the surrender of one's ego and its appetites. Evidence is abundant, but the will to follow evidence to its conclusion is rarer, because the conclusion costs something. The verse thus diagnoses the human condition without condemning it: the truth stands plain, and yet "most" turn away, not because the truth is hidden, but because its acceptance requires a change that many decline to make. This is why the Qur'an so often pairs its calls to reflect with its calls to humble oneself. The intellect can see, but the heart must consent.

**A Modern Mirror**

The verse's logic travels well into the present. In every age there are truths — about God, about the soul, about justice, about the consequences of conduct — that stand fully evidenced and yet are refused by the many because accepting them would disturb comfort or dethrone desire. The believer learns from this verse to stop measuring the truth of a matter by the crowd that follows it, and to stop being shaken when his own convictions are a minority possession. The verse is, in effect, an immunization against the loneliness of conviction: it tells the reader in advance that to hold the truth he may have to hold it against the current. That is not a sign that something is wrong with the truth; it is the sign the truth itself predicted.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:2

> **God it is Who raised the heavens without pillars that you see, then mounted the Throne; and He made the sun and the moon subservient, each running for a term appointed. He directs the affair, expounding the signs, so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument from the Sky Above**

Having declared the truth of revelation in verse 1, the sūrah turns immediately to the visible world as its witness. The sequence is not accidental. The disbelievers "believe not" (v. 1); the reply is not a bare assertion but an invitation to look up. The verse assembles four facts of nature — the raised heavens, the movement of the sun and moon, their running to an appointed term, and the governing of all affairs — and presents them as signs arranged to produce a single conviction: *"so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord."* Al-Rāzī captures the underlying argument in a sentence: the one who reflects on the order, harmony, and dependence of the universe will be led inevitably to the knowledge of the One who established it, and from there to the certainty that the same One who set this order will set the appointment of return.

**Heavens Without Visible Pillars**

The Arabic phrase *bi-ghayri ʿamadin tarawnahā* — "without pillars that you see" — generated two classical readings, both recorded by al-Ṭabarī. The first reads it to mean that the heavens have no pillars at all: they are raised by God's power alone, held in place by a command that needs no material support. The second reads it to mean that they have pillars, but pillars you do not see. Both readings converge on a single theological point, and the Qur'an itself endorses the first reading most explicitly elsewhere:

> *"God maintains the sky lest it fall upon the earth, save by His Leave."* (Qur'an 22:65)

The sky — by which the verse means the entire celestial canopy, the vast architecture of the cosmos — hangs above the earth with nothing visible holding it. The point is not that ancient listeners needed to be told about gravity, but that the stability of the cosmos is a standing miracle disguised as an ordinary fact. A child can see that a roof needs columns; no one has ever seen the columns of the sky. The verse presses this observation into service: whatever it is that holds the universe in its order, it is not something you can point to among created things. It is God.

**The Word Raised: Power Without Tool**

The verb *rafaʿa*, "He raised," deserves attention for what it implies. The Qur'an consistently attributes the raising of the heavens to God directly, without any intermediary instrument. In 79:27–28 it says:

> *"Are you harder to create, or the heaven He built? He raised its roof and ordered it."* (Qur'an 79:27–28)

The choice of vocabulary throughout is that of a builder — but a builder who needs neither scaffolding nor columns nor crew. Classical commentators noted the deliberate contrast between human construction, which always depends on visible supports, and divine construction, which depends on nothing at all. The lesson is a general one in how to read the Qur'an's nature verses: the emphasis falls not on mechanics but on the sheer fact of dependence. The heavens do not hold themselves up; nothing visible holds them up; therefore something invisible and uncreated must be their support.

**Mounting the Throne: A Statement About Kingship, Not Space**

The verse then says that God *"mounted the Throne"* (*istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh*). This is one of the Qur'an's most discussed expressions, and the classical tradition handled it with a precision that is itself a lesson in theological method. The verb *istawā* in Arabic carries the sense of settling, ascending, or establishing oneself; combined with the Throne it conveys the image of a king taking his seat after completing his work — the work, here, being creation. The early authorities, including Mālik ibn Anas (d. 179/795), when asked how God mounted the Throne, replied with the formula that became the standard of the tradition:

> *"The mounting is known, the manner of it is unknown, belief in it is obligatory, and asking about it is an innovation."*

The scholars of the salaf thus affirmed the text without trying to imagine the modality, rejecting both the claim that God occupies a physical seat in space and the claim that the verse is a meaningless metaphor to be discarded. Ibn Kathīr summarizes the sound position: the Throne is real, the mounting is real, and the manner is beyond created comprehension, because God is *"not like unto anything"*:

> *"Naught is like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing."* (Qur'an 42:11)

The theological point beneath the imagery is what matters for the sūrah's argument: after raising the heavens, God did not retire. He remains upon the Throne — that is, in unceasing command of His kingdom, managing its affairs moment by moment. The Throne-verses throughout the Qur'an are therefore statements of active sovereignty, not of spatial location.

**The Sun and the Moon: Subservient, Not Sovereign**

Next the verse presents the sun and the moon as *musakhkharayn*, "subservient" — made to serve, subjected to a command. The word is the passive form of *sakhkhara*, "to subject, to press into service," and its repetition throughout the Qur'an carries a pointed message against the star-worship of the Prophet's own environment. The same sun and moon that some of the Arabs venerated are here declared to be laborers in God's service, each *"running for a term appointed"* (*ajal musammā*). The word *ajal* is the Qur'an's term for a fixed, measured span, and it recurs across the whole book as the boundary set on everything created:

> *"And He it is Who created the heavens and the earth in truth; and the day He says 'Be,' it is."* (Qur'an 6:73)

The mention of a term appointed means that the celestial bodies are not eternal: they had a beginning and will have an end, and their motion is a journey with a destination. The Qur'an states their end explicitly:

> *"When the sun is enshrouded, and when the stars are dimmed..."* (Qur'an 81:1–2)

> *"And they have not measured God in His true measure, when the whole earth shall be but a handful to Him on the Day of Resurrection, and the heavens shall be enfolded in His right Hand."* (Qur'an 39:67)

The logic is precise and worth spelling out, as al-Rāzī does: if even the sun — the most magnificent and enduring thing the human eye can see — runs on a schedule and moves toward a conclusion, then nothing in the created order is exempt from finitude. And if everything finite is being managed toward an end, the question of a final accounting for human beings, who are themselves finite and journeying, becomes not an oddity but an expectation.

**Directing the Affair**

The phrase *yudabbiru al-amr*, "He directs the affair," condenses the sūrah's whole doctrine of providence. The root *d-b-r* gives the sense of following through to the end of a matter, managing its outcome, attending to its consequence. The noun *al-amr* — "the affair, the command" — is used in the Qur'an for the totality of what God administers. The verse thus claims not only that God created the cosmos but that He is, at every moment, running it — assigning the sun its course, the womb its fruit, the soul its span. This is the positive counterpart to the previous point about finitude: the universe is not a wound-up machine left to itself, nor a chaos, but an ongoing act of direction. And the very next sentence tells us why the direction is described as "expounding the signs" (*yufaṣṣilu al-āyāt*): God administers the world in a way that is also a teaching, spelling out evidence "so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord."

**Why Certainty of the Meeting Is the Conclusion**

The verse's last clause reveals its true destination. The heavens, the Throne, the sun and moon, the term appointed, the directing of affairs — all of it is arranged, the verse says, for one purpose: *"so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord"* (*liqāʾ Rabbikum*). The reasoning runs like this. You observe a world that is ordered, supported without visible support, set in motion with measured limits, and governed continuously. Order implies an orderer; support implies a sustainer; measure implies a measurer; governance implies a governor. And a governor who has set a term for the sun and the moon has likewise set a term for man; a Creator who arranged this life with such precision will not leave the moral destinies of His creatures — the very beings capable of recognizing this order — unfinished. Certainty of the meeting with God is thus not a leap beyond the evidence but the rational completion of the evidence. The cosmos, read correctly, is a promise of the Hereafter.

**From Cosmic Order to Personal Assurance**

Finally, the verse's method is as important as its content. It does not argue for God in the abstract; it points to particular, universal, daily experiences — the sky overhead, the sun's rising, the moon's phases — and reasons from them. This is the Qur'an's characteristic pedagogy: begin with what every human being already sees, and lead the mind step by step to what it has not yet fully accepted. The modern reader is in no worse a position than the seventh-century listener; the sky has not stopped being a sign, and the sun still runs its measured course. What has changed is only familiarity, which the Qur'an treats as the great enemy of wonder — and which this verse, like a lens wiped clean, is designed to remove.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:3

> **He it is Who spread out the earth and placed therein firm mountains and streams, and of every kind of fruit He placed therein two kinds. He causes the night to cover the day. Truly in that are signs for a people who reflect.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Sky to the Ground**

The sūrah's argument now descends, as it were, from the heavens of verse 2 to the earth beneath our feet. The movement is deliberate and complete: verse 2 asked us to look up, this verse asks us to look down. The Qur'an's case for God is built from both directions at once, and this verse gathers the earth's own testimony — its spread surface, its fixed mountains, its flowing streams, its paired fruits, and the ordered alternation of night and day. Each item is a sign; each sign is introduced by the same verb of creation; and the verse ends by declaring that all of this speaks only to *"a people who reflect"* — *qawmin yatafakkarūn*.

**The Earth Spread Out: A Question of Perspective**

The verb *madda*, "He spread out, He stretched forth," is the first word to consider. Al-Ṭabarī and other commentators gloss it as "He laid it out, He extended it," and the image is of a surface made ready for habitation — a carpet unrolled for living things. But the verse immediately raised a question that the commentators took seriously, and their answer is a small masterpiece of how classical scholarship handled the relationship between scripture and observation. Ibn Juzayy (d. 741/1340) records the apparent difficulty: the phrase "He spread out the earth" might seem to suggest a flat earth, yet the earth was known to be round. His resolution is that the verse speaks of the earth as experienced: every portion of land, wherever one stands, lies spread out before the viewer — flat from the local perspective — and yet these countless flat portions together constitute a single spherical surface. The Qur'an, in other words, describes the world from the standpoint of the human observer, which is exactly how human beings actually encounter it. There is no conflict between "spread out for us" and "round in itself," because the verse is not a treatise in geometry but a description of experience.

**Mountains as Stakes and the Grammar of Firmness**

Next come the *rawāsī* — "firm mountains" — a word from the root *r-s-w*, which carries the sense of being fixed, anchored, heavy and settled. The Qur'an uses this same term repeatedly to describe mountains as stabilizing the earth, and in 78:7 it calls them *awtād*, "stakes" or "pegs":

> *"Have We not made the earth a resting place, and the mountains stakes?"* (Qur'an 78:6–7)

The image of the peg is precise: a tent peg disappears into the ground, only a fraction visible above, while the bulk of it grips below. Classical and later commentators alike noted that the mountains, with their deep roots extending far beneath the surface, play exactly this stabilizing role in the earth's crust. The verse's point, however, is not primarily geological; it is perceptual. Mountains are the most massive, immovable things the human eye can see. They stand while everything else changes. The Qur'an takes the most apparently permanent feature of the landscape and says: even this is something God *placed* (*jaʿala*) — set down, fixed, and held. What seems eternal is, in fact, installed.

**Streams: The Gift of Running Water**

The mountains are followed by *anhār*, "streams" or "rivers." The pairing of mountains and rivers is not incidental. Rivers begin in high ground, fed by rain and meltwater, and flow down through the land; the mountain is the source and the river is the distribution. Water, as the Qur'an says elsewhere, is the basis of all life:

> *"And We made from water every living thing. Will they not, then, believe?"* (Qur'an 21:30)

The stream is thus placed between the mountain and the fruit as the artery of the earth's fertility — the very next verse will take up the theme of water in detail. For now the verse simply registers the streams as one more sign: the water that makes the land productive runs in channels God cut through it.

**Fruits in Pairs: The Signature of Pairing**

The phrase *"and of every kind of fruit He placed therein two kinds"* (*min kulli al-thamarāti jaʿala fīhā zawjayni ithnayn*) has been read in two ways that the commentators, including al-Bayḍāwī and others, record side by side. The first reads "two kinds" as referring to the duality of color — light and dark — or of taste — sweet and sour; that is, every fruit comes in contrasting varieties. The second, more sweeping reading sees "two kinds" as a reference to the created order's most pervasive law, the law of pairing: male and female, the Qur'an's *zawjayn* (cf. 36:36, "Glory be to Him Who created all the pairs"). In this reading the fruit of the earth is one more instance of the pairing that structures all creation. Either way, the verse is pointing to a fact of experience that no one denies and that few stop to weigh: the earth does not produce a monotone; it produces variety organized into complementary pairs, and this organized variety is precisely what a blind, purposeless process would not be expected to yield.

**The Night Covering the Day**

The verse then turns from the ground to time: *"He causes the night to cover the day"* (*yughshī al-layla al-nahār*). The verb *ghashiya* means to cover, to envelop, to wrap over — and the Qur'an's choice of imagery is exact. The night does not destroy the day; it covers it, the way a garment covers the body, and then uncovers it again. The same verb appears in 7:54, where God is said to cause the night to "pursue" and "cover" the day, and the whole of 92:1–2 swears by this alternation:

> *"By the night when it covers, and by the day when it appears."* (Qur'an 92:1–2)

The alternation of night and day is, in the Qur'an's estimation, one of the most eloquent of all signs, because it is at once utterly reliable and utterly outside human control. No empire, no technology, no will has ever slowed it by a minute. It is order incarnate, and it is order that no creature produces. The verse quietly asks: if the day and night keep their appointment so faithfully, each in its season, who appointed them?

**Reflection as the Proper Instrument**

The verse closes by identifying its audience: *"Truly in that are signs for a people who reflect."* The word is *yatafakkarūn*, from *fikr* — thought, reflection, the turning of the mind over a matter. This is the verse's most important single word, because it names the faculty the whole passage is addressed to. The signs are present for everyone; only those who reflect profit from them. The Qur'an's method is here laid bare: it does not demand belief without thought; it supplies evidence and commands thought. The final word of the verse is thus not a restriction but an invitation and a diagnosis — the reason so many pass by the signs of earth and sky is not that the signs are hidden, but that reflection is rare. As verse 1 said, "most of mankind believe not"; the reason, as this verse explains, is not any defect in the evidence but the dormancy of the instrument meant to receive it.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:4

> **Upon the earth are neighboring tracts, vineyards, sown fields, and date palms of a shared root and not of a shared root, watered by one water. And We have favored some above others in bounty. Truly in that are signs for a people who understand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Water, a Thousand Different Harvests**

This verse presents what may be the most philosophically concentrated nature argument in the whole chapter. Its claim can be stated in a single sentence: the same water feeds plots of land that stand side by side, yet those plots produce fruits, crops, and flavors that differ from one another in kind and in quality. From that one observation the verse draws a conclusion that al-Bayḍāwī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī all develop in their commentaries: the difference cannot be explained by the water, because the water is one. Therefore the difference must be traced to the One who appointed to each plot its particular nature. The verse is, in effect, a controlled experiment in which the single constant — the water — is shown to produce a riot of different results, and the only adequate explanation is a wise and purposeful Creator.

**The Vocabulary of Neighboring Difference**

The Arabic opens with *qiṭaʿ mutajāwirāt* — "neighboring tracts," plots of land lying adjacent to one another. The proximity is the point. Two fields share the same soil beneath, the same sky above, the same rain, the same sun, and often the same irrigation channel; and yet one yields sweet fruit and the other sour, one flourishes and the other fails, one is suited to grain and the other to vine. The verse then lists its examples with care: *jannāt min aʿnāb*, vineyards; *zarʿ*, sown fields of grain; and *nakhīl ṣinwān wa-ghayr ṣinwān*, date palms growing from a single root and palms growing from separate roots. The phrase *ṣinwān* (plural of *ṣinw*) denotes palm shoots springing from one trunk — brothers of one root — while *ghayr ṣinwān* denotes palms each with its own root. Even these, the verse notes, are all *yusqā bi-māʾin wāḥid* — watered by one water. The catalogue of examples is chosen to cover the spectrum: creeping vine, standing grain, and towering palm; single-rooted and separate-rooted; and yet one water for all.

**The Argument Against Mere Naturalism**

The commentators were explicit about what this verse is arguing against. Al-Qurṭubī reports the debate in his age and earlier: some might say the differences in produce are fully explained by the differences in seeds — the water merely actualizes what the seed already contains. But this, the commentators reply, only pushes the question back one step, and the question remains standing: where did the seeds and their differing natures come from in the first place? If one water, one soil, and one climate can be met by such different outcomes, then something other than the material elements must be responsible for assigning to each its character. That something, the verse teaches, is God. Al-Rāzī sharpens the point into a formal argument: the specific natures of things — sweetness here, sourness there, nourishment in one fruit and poison in another — are not produced by the uniform causes of water and soil; therefore they must proceed from an intelligent disposer who chose these natures and placed them in their seeds. Nature, the verse implies, is not an explanation but a description; behind the regularities of nature stands a disposer of nature.

**"And We Have Favored Some Above Others in Bounty"**

The verse then adds a clause that extends the argument from botany to economics and ethics: *"And We have favored some above others in bounty"* (*wa-nufaḍḍilu baʿḍahā ʿalā baʿḍin fī al-ukul*). The verb *faḍḍala* — "to prefer, to give excellence to" — introduces the theme of graded excellence. Some of these neighboring plots and fruits are superior to others in what they yield, though they drink the same water. The clause has been read on two levels. On the first, it simply completes the natural observation: God has given excellence to some produce over others — some sweeter, some more nourishing, some more prized — all from the same water, which is itself further evidence of the wise apportionment of qualities. On the second level, the clause opens the door to a broader principle the Qur'an states in full elsewhere: that the differentiation of provisions, abilities, and stations among people is also God's doing, a test and a mercy rather than an accident:

> *"And He it is Who made you vicegerents upon the earth and raised some of you above others in degrees, that He may try you in what He has given you."* (Qur'an 6:165)

The verse thus quietly plants a seed that the sūrah will harvest later, in verse 26, where it says that God outspreads and straitens provision for whomsoever He will. Difference — whether in fields or in people — is not an argument against the Creator but an occasion for recognizing His apportioning wisdom and for the gratitude and humility that difference should awaken.

**Understanding Versus Reflection: The Two Faculties**

The verse closes with *"Truly in that are signs for a people who understand"* — *li-qawmin yaʿqilūn*, from *ʿaql*, the faculty of binding, grasping, and holding a thing together. It is worth noticing the careful distinction between this verse and the one before it. Verse 3 ended with "a people who reflect" (*yatafakkarūn*); verse 4 ends with "a people who understand" (*yaʿqilūn*). The classical commentators noted that the two terms name two moments of a single mental act: reflection turns the mind over the evidence, gathering and considering it; understanding then binds the evidence together into a conclusion and holds it fast. The natural signs of verse 3 call for the patient turning of reflection; the logical argument of verse 4 — one water, many outcomes, therefore a disposer — calls for the firm grasp of understanding. The Qur'an's pedagogy is thus shown to be graduated, addressing each verse to the faculty its argument actually requires.

**A Parable in a Garden**

For the modern reader, this verse is among the most portable in the chapter, because it can be verified in any garden or market on any day of the year. The same soil, sun, and water produce a strawberry and a chili pepper; the same rain falls on the wheat field and the vineyard; the same irrigation reaches a sweet palm and a bitter one. None of this is controversial, and none of it is explained by the water. The verse's question is as alive now as it was fourteen centuries ago: if the causes are the same and the outcomes so different, then the differences point to something that set the outcomes in the first place. The believer sees in this the hand of the Apportioner (*al-Qāsim*), and the verse's closing words tell him what faculty to bring: not just passive wonder but active understanding, the mind's steady grip on the conclusion the evidence supports.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:5

> **And if thou dost wonder, then wondrous is their saying, "When we are dust, shall we indeed be [raised] in a new creation?" It is they who disbelieve in their Lord; it is they who will have shackles upon their necks; and it is they who will be the inhabitants of the Fire; they shall abide therein.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Astonishment Turned Back Upon the Astonished**

The verse opens with a rhetorical move of great force, addressed to the Prophet ﷺ: *"And if thou dost wonder, then wondrous is their saying."* Al-Qurṭubī explains the sense precisely: if you are astonished at anything — if anything in the stubbornness of these people should amaze you — then the thing most deserving of your astonishment is this saying of theirs, their denial of the Resurrection. The construction sets one wonder against another and declares the second greater. The Prophet's possible wonder at their rejection of him is answered by pointing to something still more astonishing: their rejection of the most self-evident of all truths about the fate of the human being.

**The Recurring Objection: Dust and Bones**

The objection itself is quoted verbatim, and it is one the Qur'an records many times, always with the same ingredients — dust, bones, decay, and the demand for a "new creation":

> *"And he strikes for Us a parable and forgets his own creation. He says, 'Who gives life to the bones when they are decayed?'"* (Qur'an 36:78)

> *"They say, 'When we are bones and dust, shall we indeed be resurrected as a new creation?'"* (Qur'an 17:49)

> *"And they say, 'What, when we have become bones and dust, shall we indeed be raised in a new creation?'"* (Qur'an 17:98)

The objection is not a careless quip; it is the central denial around which disbelief in the Qur'an's portrait organizes itself. Its logic runs: the body decomposes; decomposition is final; therefore a new creation is impossible. The verse, and the Qur'an generally, does not dismiss this reasoning so much as expose its hidden premise. The premise is that the power which assembled the human being in the first place is bound by the decay it now witnesses — as though the one who made the first creation from nothing were unequal to making it again. The Qur'an's standing reply is that the first creation is the harder case, not the second:

> *"And he strikes for Us a parable and forgets his own creation... Say, 'He will give them life Who originated them the first time.'"* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

The denial of the Hereafter is thus diagnosed as a failure of proportion: the objector weighs dust against power and forgets that the dust itself was once brought to life by that same power.

**Why the Resurrection Seemed Impossible to Them**

The classical commentators add an important layer of historical and psychological explanation. Al-Rāzī and others note that the Makkan idolaters were not denying that God created them; their difficulty was specifically with the restoration of the body after its total dissolution. Their minds were fixed on the familiar: in their experience, the dead do not return, and decay is the last word. The Qur'an's response is to break that fixation by pointing to the larger scale of creation — the heavens and the earth themselves, brought into being from nothing, stand as witnesses that God is not limited by the categories of human experience. The very next verses of this sūrah will develop that reply by turning to the punishment of past nations, but the principle is already present here: wonder at the power displayed in creation should make the resurrection easy to accept, not hard.

**The Grammar of Consequences**

Having quoted the objection, the verse pronounces upon its authors in three parallel clauses, each beginning with *ulāʾika* — "it is they" — a grammatical device that gathers and isolates the group, fixing the verdict upon them:

> *"It is they who disbelieve in their Lord; it is they who will have shackles upon their necks; and it is they who will be the inhabitants of the Fire; they shall abide therein."*

The three clauses form a chain of cause and consequence. The denial of the Lord is the root; the shackles are the first fruit; the Fire is the final lodging. Notice that the verse does not say the disbelievers *will be given* shackles as an arbitrary punishment imposed from outside; it says the shackles *will be upon* them, the way a condition already latent in their denial becomes visible. The Qur'an's picture of punishment is consistently this: the Hereafter does not so much add something foreign to a person as reveal what his choices have already made him. The one who denied the Lord and denied his own return is, in that very denial, already bound — bound by the false premise, bound by the lie, bound to the conclusion that awaits him.

**Shackles on Necks: The Image Examined**

The *aghlāl* — "shackles" or "collars" — are described as being "upon their necks" (*fī aʿnāqihim*), and the image recurs throughout the Qur'an, most fully in 36:8:

> *"Truly We have placed shackles upon their necks, up to their chins, so their heads are forced up."* (Qur'an 36:8)

The commentators explain the force of the image on two levels. On the physical level, it depicts the binding of the disbeliever on the Day of Judgment, chained and unable to move — the Qur'an elsewhere describes the chains and the collars with which the people of the Fire are bound (69:30–32; 76:4). On the psychological level, the shackle is a figure for the state of the denier in this very life: the one who refuses to see is, as it were, collared, his head held up so that he cannot look down at the truth beneath his feet. The two levels are continuous, not separate: the blindness chosen in this world becomes the bond endured in the next.

**Abiding Therein: The Finality of the Choice**

The verse ends with *hum fīhā khālidūn* — "they shall abide therein." The word *khālidūn*, from *khulūd*, means permanence without exit. Classical theology, represented by Ibn Taymiyyah and the mainstream tradition, affirms this permanence for those who die in disbelief: the Fire is their eternal abode, and the verse states it without mitigation. But the verse should also be read in its full context within the sūrah, where the severity of this verdict is always paired with the reminder of mercy. Verse 6, immediately following, will say that God is "Possessed of Forgiveness for mankind despite their wrongdoing." The Qur'an never presents the eternal abode as a surprise; it presents it as the endpoint of a choice, fully disclosed in advance, with the door of return left open until the very end. The verse's function is therefore not to delight in doom but to state, with complete clarity, the stakes of the denial it has just quoted — so that no one who enters that end can claim he was not told.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:6

> **Yet they would have thee hasten evil before good, though examples have passed before them. Truly thy Lord is Possessed of Forgiveness for mankind despite their wrongdoing, and truly thy Lord is severe in retribution.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demand for Punishment as a Mockery**

This verse introduces a theme that runs through the whole sūrah: the disbelievers' taunt that if punishment is real, let it come now. The phrase *"they would have thee hasten evil before good"* (*yastaʿjilūnaka bi-al-sayyiʾati qabla al-ḥasanati*) describes the Makkans demanding that the Prophet ﷺ bring down the threatened chastisement at once, rather than the mercy and forbearance he was actually offering them. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī explain the two terms precisely: "evil" here is the punishment, and "good" is mercy — the respite, the forgiveness, the opportunity to repent. The disbelievers, in their mockery, inverted the order. They wanted the threat executed before the offer accepted; they preferred the risk of ruin to the patience of grace.

**Precedents That Should Have Taught Them**

The verse's central clause — *"though examples have passed before them"* — supplies the rational answer to the taunt. The word *al-mathulāt* means "examples, exemplary punishments," and it refers to the destroyed nations of the past: the people of Noah, of ʿĀd, of Thamūd, of Lot, of Pharaoh. Their histories were not legends to the Makkans; they were local knowledge, inscribed in the ruins that lined the caravan routes. Al-Qurṭubī and others gloss the clause as: the punishments that befell those who disbelieved in their messengers are before you, present and known — so why do you ask to be added to their number? The demand to hasten punishment is absurd precisely because the precedents are visible; the one who has seen the ruins of the destroyed and still asks to join them has not learned what the ruins were meant to teach.

**The Qur'anic Pattern of Haste and Its Correction**

The theme of hastening punishment is treated many times in the Qur'an, and always the response is the same: the timing is God's, not the mockers':

> *"And they say, 'When is this promise, if you are truthful?' Say, 'I have no power over what harm or benefit may come to myself, save as God wills. For every community there is a term; when their term comes, they shall not delay it by an hour, nor shall they advance it.'"* (Qur'an 10:49)

> *"And they ask thee to hasten the punishment. Truly God will not fail His tryst."* (Qur'an 29:53)

The Qur'an's logic is consistent: punishment is not withheld because it will never come, but because it has an appointed time, and the interval between warning and execution is itself mercy. The mockers convert God's patience into an argument against Him — "if He were real, He would have struck already" — and the verse answers that this very patience, this open door of reprieve, is the clearest evidence of the mercy they are refusing to recognize.

**Forgiveness Despite Wrongdoing: The Divine Pattern of Respite**

The verse then balances the warning with one of the Qur'an's most hope-filled affirmations:

> *"Truly thy Lord is Possessed of Forgiveness for mankind despite their wrongdoing."* (*inna Rabbaka la-dhū maghfiratin li-al-nāsi ʿalā ẓulmihim*)

The preposition *ʿalā* here carries the weight. It means "despite," "notwithstanding" — God's forgiveness surrounds mankind even while they are in the midst of their wrongdoing. Al-Ṭabarī explains: God does not hasten punishment for their wrongdoing but covers them with forbearance, granting them respite in this world so that they may return. The commentator ʿIkrimah and others understood the verse's practical application to be exactly the point just made: the delay of punishment is not negligence but forgiveness. This is the divine pattern the mockers had mistaken for weakness.

**The Two Names Held in Balance**

The verse's final clause — *"and truly thy Lord is severe in retribution"* (*shadīd al-ʿiqāb*) — completes the balance. The structure of the verse as a whole is a lesson in how the Qur'an presents God's attributes. It never lets mercy be imagined as indifference, nor severity as cruelty. The same Lord who is possessed of forgiveness is severe in retribution; the two descriptions stand side by side, each guarding the other from distortion. Al-Rāzī explains the relationship: God's forgiveness is for those who turn back, and His severity is for those who persist. If one repents, the forgiveness is ready; if one does not, then the severity should be known. The verse is thus not a contradiction but a condition — the two attributes address two possible futures, and the human being chooses which of the two he will meet.

**The Psychological Structure of Warning and Hope**

This verse is a study in the psychology of warning, and its structure deserves explicit attention. A warning that contains only threat produces despair or defiance; a warning that contains only comfort produces complacency. The Qur'an's method, exemplified here in a single verse, is to place the two side by side so that each does its proper work: the severity stirs fear, and the forgiveness stirs hope, and the human heart, caught between the two, is moved to act — to repent while the door of forgiveness is open. The classical scholars called this pairing *al-khawf wa-al-rajāʾ*, fear and hope, and held it to be the correct posture of the believer throughout life. The verse demonstrates the principle in miniature: the mockers are shown the retribution so that they will fear; they are shown the forgiveness so that they will not despair of returning. Both truths are true at once, and the person who holds only one of them has not heard the verse.

**A Modern Application: The Patience That Invites Scorn**

The verse's portrait of the mocker — the one who sees patience and calls it absence, who sees respite and calls it nonexistence — has not aged. In every age there are those who take the continued operation of the world as evidence against judgment, who mistake the delay of consequences for the cancellation of consequences. The verse corrects this in two directions at once. To the skeptic it says: the delay is not absence; it is a term, and the term will arrive. To the believer it says: do not be provoked by the mockery into wishing the punishment hastened; your Lord's pattern is forbearance, and His severity is real but not for you to schedule. The mature response to mockery, the verse implies, is to leave the timing to God and to keep the two attributes — forgiveness and severity — held together in the heart exactly as the verse holds them in the text.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:7

> **Those who disbelieve say, "Why has not some sign been sent down upon him from his Lord?" Thou art but a warner; and for every people there is a guide.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Endless Demand for a Sign**

The disbelievers' question — *"Why has not some sign been sent down upon him from his Lord?"* — is one of the most frequently recorded objections in the Qur'an, and this verse is one of its clearest answers. The request is recorded in nearly identical words in several places:

> *"And they say, 'Why has not a sign been sent down upon him from his Lord?' Say, 'The Unseen belongs to God alone. So wait; I too am waiting with you.'"* (Qur'an 10:20)

> *"And those who disbelieve say, 'Why has not a sign been sent down upon him from his Lord?' Thou art but a warner, and for every people a guide."* (Qur'an 13:7)

The question sounds reasonable in isolation, but the Qur'an's treatment exposes what lies beneath it. The Makkans had already been given the greatest sign any people ever received — the Qur'an itself, a book of unmatched eloquence delivered through an unlettered man — and yet they asked for more. Their request was not the hunger of a genuine seeker but the evasion of one who has decided in advance not to believe and is merely negotiating the terms of his disbelief. The Qur'an elsewhere says plainly that no sign would suffice for those who have already closed the matter:

> *"Even if We opened unto them a gate to Heaven and they ascended through it all the while, they would say, 'Our eyes were merely dazzled. Nay, we are a people bewitched.'"* (Qur'an 15:14–15)

The demand for signs, in other words, was never about evidence. It was about setting conditions that no prophet could meet, so that the refusal to believe could be dressed up as a grievance.

**The Prophet's Task Reduced to Its Essence**

The answer God gives is not an argument but a definition: *"Thou art but a warner"* (*innamā anta mundhir*). The particle *innamā* — "only, merely" — confines the Prophet's role to its boundary. A warner is one who conveys the warning; he does not manufacture signs on demand, he does not compel belief, and he is not answerable for the outcome. This is the recurring comfort and commission of the Qur'an's treatment of prophethood, stated with maximum force elsewhere:

> *"And We have not sent thee, save as a warner and a bearer of glad tidings."* (Qur'an 25:56)

The verse's answer is thus a quiet but complete dismissal of the demand. You ask why a sign has not been sent down; but the sending of signs is not the prophet's office. His office is to warn. The signs exist — the Qur'an, the cosmos, the ruins of the destroyed — but the warning is his specific charge, and he fulfills it whether or not the listener responds. This reframing is psychologically important for anyone who conveys truth in a resistant environment: the task is measured by faithful delivery, not by the acceptance of the audience. The Prophet ﷺ was being released, in this verse, from the illusion that his success depended on the conversion of those who had chosen not to be converted.

**"For Every People There Is a Guide"**

The second half of the verse — *"and for every people there is a guide"* (*wa-li-kulli qawmin hād*) — is one of the most commented-upon phrases in the sūrah, because of both its general meaning and its specific interpretation. On the general level, it states a principle of divine justice and mercy: God has never left a people without guidance. The Qur'an affirms this universality repeatedly:

> *"For every community there is a warner."* (Qur'an 35:24)

> *"And there is no community, but that a warner has passed among them."* (Qur'an 35:24)

The guide may be a prophet, or the inherited call of a prophet, but the principle holds: no people is punished for ignorance before guidance has reached it, because God does not condemn a people until the message has been made clear to them. The verse thus pairs the two offices — warner and guide — as two faces of one mission: the warning that alarms and the guidance that leads.

**The Hadith: "I Am the Warner and ʿAlī Is the Guide"**

The specific interpretation is a matter of well-known disagreement, and it must be presented with the care it deserves. Ibn ʿAjībah and other commentators record a report that when this verse was revealed, the Prophet ﷺ placed his hand on his chest and said, "I am the warner," and then placed his hand on ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and said, "You, O ʿAlī, are the guide." The Shiʿi tradition, represented here by al-Qummī and al-Ṭūsī, built upon this and similar reports an identification of "the guide" with the Imams of the Prophet's household generally and with ʿAlī specifically. The Sunni tradition, represented by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, reads the verse in its general sense: the Prophet is the warner to his own people, and the guide is either the Prophet himself in his guiding capacity, or the guidance of God that attends every people through its messengers. Ibn Kathīr transmits the interpretation of Ibn ʿAbbās that the guide is the Prophet ﷺ — the one who warns and guides — while others understood "every people" to mean every past nation that received its own guide. The verse's general principle is agreed upon by all; the specific application to ʿAlī is a matter of well-attested difference between the traditions, and both readings can be stated without distortion.

**The Logic of Warning and Guidance as a Pair**

Setting the historical question aside, the verse's pairing of *mundhir* and *hād* contains a subtle psychology that deserves its own attention. A warning without guidance produces only terror; guidance without warning produces only information. The two belong together. The warner alerts the traveler to the precipice; the guide shows him the safe road. This is why the Qur'an so often describes the Prophet ﷺ with both terms in one breath, and why the office of prophethood is incomplete without both. The verse, read this way, is not merely a reply to the Makkans but a permanent description of how divine truth reaches human beings: it always arrives as a double gift — the alarm that shakes us from the dangerous path, and the hand that leads us to the safe one.

**The Modern Reader and the Refusal of Evidence**

Finally, the verse holds up a mirror to a tendency that has not disappeared: the habit of demanding from truth conditions it was never obliged to meet. The Makkans asked for a sign on their terms — a physical marvel, produced on command, conforming to their specifications — when the decisive sign was already before them. The modern analogue is the person who says he will believe only if God proves Himself according to a checklist he has drawn up, while declining to weigh the evidence that actually stands before him: the order of the cosmos, the coherence of revelation, the testimony of history, the witness of his own conscience. The verse's answer remains the answer: the truth has already delivered its warning and its guidance; the question is not whether more evidence will be supplied, but whether the evidence already supplied will be received.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:8

> **God knows that which every female bears, how wombs diminish and how they increase. Everything with Him is according to a measure—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Depths of Knowledge That No Eye Reaches**

With this verse the sūrah turns from the visible world to the hidden one, and the transition is theologically exact. Verses 2–4 argued from what can be seen; verse 8 argues from what cannot — the hidden content of the womb, the secret processes of gestation, the measured decrees that govern all things. The argument reaches its summit in the claim that God's knowledge penetrates where no creature's knowledge can go, and that this knowledge is not vague or general but precise to the last measure.

**What Every Female Bears**

The first clause — *"God knows that which every female bears"* (*Allāhu yaʿlamu mā taḥmilu kullu unthā*) — is absolute in its scope. The word *kull* ("every") leaves no exception: every female of every species, at every moment, in every womb. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī spell out the contents of this knowledge. God knows whether the child is male or female, sound or deficient, beautiful or plain, tall or short; He knows its disposition, its lifespan, its provision, and its fate. The Qur'an repeats this claim in contexts that emphasize the same totality:

> *"And He knows what is in the wombs."* (Qur'an 31:34)

> *"God knows that which every female bears, how wombs diminish and how they increase."* (Qur'an 35:11)

The significance of choosing the womb as the example deserves notice. The womb is the Qur'an's standing symbol of the hidden — the place where a new human being comes into existence out of sight, beyond the reach of even the mother who carries it. In 22:5 the stages of development within the womb are described in detail precisely because they are invisible to the human observer, and the invisible is God's domain. To say that God knows the womb is to say that there is no hidden place in all creation, no depth of darkness, that His knowledge does not fill.

**"How Wombs Diminish and How They Increase"**

The second clause — *"how wombs diminish and how they increase"* (*wa-mā taghīḍu al-arḥāmu wa-mā tazdād*) — has been read at two levels by the commentators. On the literal, biological level, it refers to the stages of pregnancy: the womb diminishes and increases as the child grows, the gestation lengthening or shortening, the fetus developing through its measured stages. Al-Ṭabarī transmits this reading along with another, more expansive one, from Ibn ʿAbbās and others: the wombs diminish in what they carry and increase in what they carry; and some read it as a reference to the menstrual cycle itself — the womb's blood decreasing and increasing — for all of which God alone keeps the exact account. On the spiritual level, the clause extends to the inner life of every soul: the knowledge of God encompasses the growth and diminution of the heart, the waxing and waning of faith, the increase and decrease of good and evil in a person — nothing of it escapes His count.

**"Everything with Him Is According to a Measure"**

The verse closes the sentence with a clause that is the keystone of the Qur'an's doctrine of divine decree: *"Everything with Him is according to a measure"* (*wa-kullu shayʾin ʿindahu bi-miqdār*). The word *miqdār* is the noun of measure — a fixed, determined quantity — and it answers the question the first two clauses implicitly raise: how can God's knowledge of the womb be so precise? Because all things exist by measure. Nothing is random, nothing is approximate, nothing slips out of the account. The same idea is stated in its most famous form:

> *"Truly We have created everything according to a measure."* (Qur'an 54:49)

And the moon's phases are elsewhere given as the visible demonstration of the same principle:

> *"And the moon, We have determined for it mansions, until it returns like an old date stalk."* (Qur'an 36:39)

The doctrine of *qadar* — that God has measured and decreed all things — is the theological frame within which this verse sits, and the commentators were careful to state it without collapsing human responsibility into it. The measure is God's knowledge and decree; the human being remains accountable for his choices because the measure encompasses, rather than cancels, his willing and acting. The verse's emphasis is on the completeness of divine knowledge, not on any denial of human agency.

**The Comfort of Being Known**

The verse's immediate purpose in the sūrah's argument is to establish the completeness of God's knowledge as the ground of His authority to warn and to judge. But its effect on the listener is also comfort, and this deserves to be said plainly. The clause "God knows what every female bears" is addressed to every human being who has ever felt unseen — the hidden sorrow, the concealed fear, the secret hope that no other person knows. The verse says that the deepest and most private realities of a person's existence are not private from God. For the believer this is not surveillance but assurance: the One who sees the unseen is the One who is Compassionate and Merciful, and being fully known by Him is the beginning of being fully understood and fully cared for. The same knowledge that grounds the warning grounds the comfort — the Lord who will hold each soul to account is the Lord who has never once lost sight of it.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:9

> **Knower of the Unseen and the seen, the Great, the Exalted.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Completion of the Divine Description**

This verse completes the sentence begun in verse 8, and the two must be read together as a single movement of thought. Verse 8 established the precision of God's knowledge — the womb, the measure, the decree; verse 9 now names the One in whom that knowledge resides, in four titles that each bear their own weight: *Knower of the Unseen and the seen, the Great, the Exalted* (*ʿālim al-ghayb wa-al-shahādah, al-kabīr, al-mutaʿāl*). The sūrah's method is visible here in miniature: it builds from the particular evidence to the general attribute, from the fact of God's knowledge to the nature of the Knower.

**The Unseen and the Seen: The Full Span of Knowledge**

The phrase *ʿālim al-ghayb wa-al-shahādah* — "Knower of the Unseen and the seen" — is one of the Qur'an's most important descriptions of God, and it recurs at strategic points:

> *"Knower of the Unseen and the seen, and He is exalted above the partners they ascribe."* (Qur'an 23:92)

> *"He is God, other than whom there is no god, Knower of the Unseen and the seen."* (Qur'an 59:22)

The two terms divide reality into its two fundamental regions. *Al-ghayb* is the hidden — that which is absent from the senses: the angels, the jinn, Paradise, Hell, the content of the heart, the events of the future, the decrees of the Preserved Tablet. *Al-shahādah* is the witnessed — the visible world present to the senses. The verse's point is that this division, which is absolute for creatures, does not exist for God. What is hidden from us is open to Him; what is present to us is no more present to Him than the hidden, because He is the ground of both. The Qur'an states this negation of the division with perfect clarity:

> *"With Him are the keys of the Unseen; none knows them but He."* (Qur'an 6:59)

The pairing of the two terms also carries an implicit rebuke to the Makkans, who acknowledged God as Creator of the seen world yet worshiped idols. The verse implies: He who knows the Unseen and the seen is the only fitting object of worship, for only such complete knowledge can ground complete sovereignty.

**"The Great": Greatness That Admits No Rival**

The title *al-Kabīr*, "the Great," is examined by the commentators with attention to its scope. Al-Ghazzālī, in his treatise on the Divine Names, explains that greatness here is not of size but of rank and perfection: the Great is the One whose essence, attributes, and acts are beyond anything comparable, before whom all else is small. The title does not mean God occupies a large space; it means He possesses the fullness of greatness that creatures can only approximate in fragments. Ibn Kathīr notes that the pairing of al-Kabīr with al-Mutaʿāl in this verse is deliberate: greatness and exaltation belong together, because the One who is truly great is thereby above every partner and every rival. The title is a direct answer to the idolaters' association of partners with God — partners whom the Qur'an reduces to nothing beside His greatness:

> *"That is because God is the Truth, and what they call upon apart from Him is false, and because God is the Exalted, the Great."* (Qur'an 22:62)

**"The Exalted": The Transcendence That Grounds Everything**

The final title, *al-Mutaʿāl*, "the Exalted," is the intensive-reflexive form of the root *ʿ-l-w*, meaning height and elevation. The form conveys self-exaltation: God is exalted by His very essence, not by the praise of creatures. The commentators connect this title to the doctrine of divine transcendence (*tanzīh*): God is above all deficiency, above all likeness, above all that the imagination can reach. This is the attribute that answers every attempt to confine God within a category:

> *"Naught is like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing."* (Qur'an 42:11)

The placement of this title at the end of the verse is significant. The sūrah has just spent several verses describing God's creation and His knowledge in terms drawn from human experience — He raised the heavens, He mounted the Throne, He directs the affair. The closing title *al-Mutaʿāl* then guards all of that description from anthropomorphism: whatever these verses affirm, they do not reduce God to the level of His creation, because He is the Exalted, above all that they say of Him in the manner in which creatures say such things. Al-Rāzī makes the point with his usual precision: the descriptions of God in the Qur'an are true, but they are true of a being who transcends the categories in which creatures live, and the title *al-Mutaʿāl* is the seal that protects this truth.

**The Verse as a Model of Balanced Theology**

Taken together, verses 8 and 9 form a compact model of the Qur'an's theology of the divine attributes. Knowledge is affirmed — precise, comprehensive, reaching the unseen. Greatness is affirmed. Exaltation is affirmed. But each affirmation is balanced by the others, so that no single attribute can be isolated and distorted. The Knower is also the Great, so His knowledge is not the passive observation of a spectator but the knowledge of a sovereign. The Great is also the Exalted, so His greatness is not the competitive bigness of a rival but the transcendence of the One above all rivals. The verse teaches the believer to hold the attributes together, to affirm without comparing, and to let each name of God correct the others — a discipline that remains the heart of sound theology.

**A Practical Reflection: The Unseen in Daily Life**

For the modern reader, the phrase "Knower of the Unseen and the seen" holds a practical application that the sūrah's first audience would have felt immediately. Human life is lived mostly in the dark: we do not know the future, the outcome of our choices, the content of other hearts, or even fully the content of our own. The verse offers a fixed point in that darkness — the assurance that the whole of reality, hidden and visible, is known to God and measured by Him. The believer's response is not passivity but trust: to act with diligence in the sphere of the seen, and to entrust the sphere of the unseen to the One who alone knows it. This is the disposition the Qur'an calls *tawakkul*, reliance upon God, and this verse is one of its firm foundations.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:10

> **Alike among you are those who speak secretly and those who do so openly, those who lurk by night, and those who go forth by day.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Equality of All Conditions Before Divine Knowledge**

This verse is the immediate consequence of what came before, and the sūrah makes the connection explicit by its grammar. Verse 9 declared God the Knower of the Unseen and the seen; verse 10 now draws the conclusion for human conduct: because God's knowledge spans the hidden and the visible, the distinction between secret and open, between night and day, collapses entirely before Him. The word *sawāʾ* — "alike, equal" — is the hinge of the verse. It declares that the four conditions listed — speaking secretly, speaking openly, hiding by night, moving by day — are all one and the same to God. None is more hidden from Him, none more exposed. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Qurṭubī both state the principle plainly: whatever a person's state, God's knowledge of his actions is complete.

**The Four Conditions as a Survey of Human Concealment**

The verse's four phrases are chosen with precision to exhaust the ways a person tries to escape observation. *"Those who speak secretly"* — the whispered word, the private conversation, the thought given voice only in a closed room. *"Those who do so openly"* — the public speech, which needs no concealment because it seeks none. *"Those who lurk by night"* — the one who moves under darkness, when the cover of night seems to promise invisibility. *"Those who go forth by day"* — the one who moves in daylight, fully visible. The two pairs are structured as contrasts — secret against open, night against day — and the verse's claim is that the contrast is real only between human beings, never before God. To one observer, the secret is hidden and the open is seen; to the other observer, both are equally plain.

**The Roots: Secrecy, Openness, Lurking, and Going Forth**

The Arabic vocabulary rewards a closer look. *Asarra* (to speak secretly) and *jahara* (to speak openly) come from roots that the Qur'an uses as a fixed pair for the hidden and the public — the same pair appears in the divine names and in the many verses that assert God's knowledge of both. *Sāraba*, translated "lurks," is from the root *s-r-b*, and carries the sense of slipping away, moving in concealment — the classical lexicographers gloss it as walking by night, disappearing into the dark. *Sāraḥa*, "goes forth," is its opposite — going out openly, in the broad day. The verse's artistry is in the symmetry: it names the two dimensions of concealment (the word and the movement) and the two poles of each (hidden and open), covering the whole field of human secrecy in four strokes.

**The Purpose: Vigilance and Self-Scrutiny**

What is the verse for? Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn ʿAjībah answer this question with an insight that deserves to be stated at length. The verse is not merely a statement of fact about God's knowledge; it is a device for producing a specific state in the human heart — *murāqabah*, watchfulness, the constant awareness of being observed. If the secret and the open are alike to God, then the person who has internalized this verse has no private arena left in which he permits himself what he would not permit in public. The effect is not fear of exposure but a steady, inward vigilance: the same standard applied to the hidden deed as to the visible one, because the audience before whom both are performed is one and the same.

Ibn ʿAjībah adds a further depth: the verse summons the believer to scrutinize not only his actions but his thoughts and the states of his heart, since these too are among the things that are "alike" to God. The one who polices only his public conduct while leaving his inner life unexamined has not yet understood the verse, for the verse's whole point is that the inner life is as open to God as the outer.

**The Teaching of ʿUmar: Accounting Before the Accounting**

The classical tradition attaches to this theme one of its most famous ethical sayings, attributed to the second Caliph, ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (d. 23/644), and transmitted in the sources of Muslim ethics and Sufism:

> *"Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed for you."*

The saying is the practical application of the verse. The accounting on the Day of Judgment is certain; the wise person anticipates it by conducting it now, in this life, when correction is still possible. The verse and the saying together establish the discipline of *muḥāsabah* — self-reckoning — which the scholars of the spiritual path, from al-Ḥārith al-Muḥāsibī (d. 243/857), who took his very name from this practice, to al-Ghazzālī after him, developed into a systematic science. The logic is simple and unanswerable: if you are going to be judged anyway, judge yourself first, while the verdict can still be changed.

**The Modern Mirror: The Private Screen and the Public Face**

The verse's diagnosis travels directly into the conditions of modern life, where the division between public and private has been reshaped by technology but not abolished. The person whose public conduct is upright and whose private conduct — behind the screen, in the anonymous message, in the hidden transaction — is another matter is living precisely the contradiction this verse dissolves. The Qur'an's claim is that the screen conceals from people, never from God; that the anonymous act has an observer; that the deleted message is still on record. The verse is not, in the end, a threat but a liberation: the person who understands that the secret and the open are alike before God is freed from the exhausting double life, because he no longer has to maintain two standards — one for the audience and one for himself. He can live as one person, because he lives before one audience.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:11

> **For him there are attendant angels to his front and to his rear, guarding him by God's Command. Truly God alters not what is in a people until they alter what is in themselves. And when God desires evil for a people, there is no repelling it; and apart from Him they have no protector.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guardians at the Front and the Rear**

The verse opens with a statement about the unseen protection that surrounds every human being: *"For him there are attendant angels to his front and to his rear, guarding him by God's Command"* (*lahu muʿaqqibātun min bayni yadayhi wa-min khalfihi yaḥfaẓūnahu min amri Allāh*). The word *muʿaqqibāt* is the intensive plural of *muʿaqqib*, "one who follows in succession" — angels who come in relays, one shift succeeding another, so that the guard is never broken. The phrase "to his front and to his rear" describes the completeness of the coverage: nothing approaches from any direction without passing through the appointed protection. The Qur'an elsewhere names these guardians explicitly as the *kirām kātibīn*, the noble recorders:

> *"Yet truly over you are guardians, noble, writing — they know whatsoever you do."* (Qur'an 82:10–12)

The classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, explain the function of these angels on two levels. On the first, they guard the person physically by God's command — preserving him from harms that would otherwise reach him, each hazard averted or permitted only as God decrees. On the second, they are the recording angels, and their "guarding" includes the guarding of the record: every deed, word, and movement is preserved by them with exactness, so that nothing is lost that will be needed at the accounting. The two functions meet in the word *yaḥfaẓūnahu* — "they guard him" — which means both that they shield him and that they keep his deeds.

**The Provision of Protection Is Conditional**

But the verse does not leave the protection unconditional, and this is the hinge of its argument. The angels guard *min amri Allāh* — "by God's Command." The protection is not an independent power possessed by the angels; it is a commission they carry out, and the commission can be changed. This prepares the listener for the verse's central declaration, which follows immediately and which is among the most quoted sentences in the entire Qur'an:

> *"Truly God alters not what is in a people until they alter what is in themselves."* (*inna Allāha lā yughayyiru mā bi-qawmin ḥattā yughayyirū mā bi-anfusihim*)

**The Law of Change: No Destiny Alters Without a Change Within**

This sentence has been called a verse of the greatest importance for understanding the Qur'an's doctrine of human responsibility, and the commentators give it the full treatment it deserves. The phrase *"what is in a people"* refers, on the reading of al-Māwardī and others, to the blessings and favors of this life — health, security, prosperity, wellbeing — the good states that God has placed among a people. *"Until they alter what is in themselves"* refers to their conduct: the misuse of those blessings in disobedience. The law stated is therefore: God does not withdraw His favor from a people until that people first changes its own inward state and outward behavior from obedience to disobedience, from gratitude to ingratitude. Al-Rāzī states the principle in its general form: the alteration of the divine decree in either direction — from good to bad or from bad to good — follows upon the alteration of the human condition. God does not change a people's condition of prosperity into adversity unless they first change; and by the same law, He does not change a people's adversity into prosperity unless they first change themselves.

The same principle appears elsewhere in the Qur'an in an even more explicit form:

> *"That is because God changes not a blessing He has bestowed upon a people until they change what is in themselves."* (Qur'an 8:53)

**A Law of Justice, Not a Denial of Decree**

This verse is the Qur'an's clearest statement of the relationship between divine decree and human freedom, and it must be stated precisely to avoid the two opposite errors. On one side stands the error of fatalism — the belief that human action is irrelevant because all is predestined. This verse demolishes that error in its first half: the change in a people's condition is explicitly tied to their own change, which would be meaningless if their action were nothing. On the other side stands the error of self-sufficiency — the belief that human beings control their own destiny independently of God. The verse's second half demolishes that error: *"And when God desires evil for a people, there is no repelling it."* The two halves together state the full truth: human beings are genuinely responsible for what they change in themselves, and the outcome remains genuinely in God's hand. The change within is ours; the verdict upon the change is His. Ibn Taymiyyah and his school built a large part of their discussion of *qadar* around exactly this balance: the verse affirms real human agency without ever transferring sovereignty away from God.

**"When God Desires Evil for a People"**

The clause *"when God desires evil for a people, there is no repelling it"* requires careful interpretation, and the commentators supply it. The word "evil" here is not moral evil in God — God's decree is never unjust — but the punishment and affliction that follow upon a people's persistent wrongdoing. Al-Qurṭubī explains: God "desires evil" for a people in the sense that He decrees for them the consequence their own conduct has earned — the withdrawal of protection, the arrival of the punishment. The phrase is the Qur'an's idiom for the execution of deserved retribution, and it is always presented as the response to a prior human choice, never as an arbitrary divine whim. The verse's logic is thus complete: protection is given by command; the command is withdrawn when the people change themselves for the worse; and once withdrawn, no one can restore it, because *"apart from Him they have no protector"* (*wa-mā lahum min dūnihi min wāl*). The *wāl* is the guardian, the patron, the one who takes responsibility — and the verse's closing point is that no such guardian exists for them except God, whom they have abandoned.

**The Psychological and Social Depth of the Law**

This verse is one of the Qur'an's deepest contributions to the understanding of social change, and its implications extend far beyond the theological context. The law it states — no change from without without a change from within — is verifiable in every sphere of life. A nation's political condition does not improve while the character of its people remains unimproved. An individual's circumstances do not change while his habits remain unchanged. The verse teaches that the locus of change is internal first, and that those who wait for their condition to change while leaving themselves untouched are waiting for what the law of God has not promised them. The classical scholars drew from this verse a whole discipline of self-reform: the one who wishes God to alter his state must begin by altering the state of his own soul. This is the verse that undergirds every movement of personal and communal renewal in the Islamic tradition — the certainty that reform is real, that it must begin within, and that its outcome is in the hand of the One who honors the effort.

**The Guardians and the Change: The Verse's Complete Picture**

Read as a whole, the verse presents a complete picture of the human being under divine care. He is surrounded by guardian angels, appointed in relays, preserving him by God's command. That preservation is a trust, and the trust is conditional upon the preservation of his own inward state. When he changes for the worse, the protective command changes; when the protective command is withdrawn, no created power can restore it. The picture is neither comforting at the expense of responsibility nor alarming at the expense of hope — it is exactly proportioned, as the Qur'an's doctrine always is, between the reality of divine care and the reality of human answerability.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:12

> **He it is Who shows you lightning, arousing fear and hope, and Who produces heavy-laden clouds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lightning as a Display of Divine Power**

The sūrah now returns from the unseen world of the angels to the visible world of the sky, and the transition is again deliberate. Verse 11 spoke of unseen guardians; verse 12 speaks of lightning — the most startling of the sky's visible phenomena. The two are placed side by side to make a single point: God's management of the world is total, whether through the hidden agency of angels or through the visible drama of the storm. The verse's opening, *"He it is Who shows you lightning"* (*huwa alladhī yurīkum al-barqa*), attributes the lightning directly to God as something He *shows* — a display, a sign, presented to the viewer rather than a mere physical event. The Qur'an returns to this image in 30:24 with almost the same words:

> *"And of His signs is that He shows you lightning, arousing fear and hope, and sends down water from the sky, reviving thereby the earth after its death. Truly in that are signs for a people who understand."* (Qur'an 30:24)

**Fear and Hope in a Single Flash**

The verse's most important phrase is *khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan* — "arousing fear and hope." Al-Rāzī explains the two emotions with precision: the flash of lightning arouses fear of the thunderbolt, the storm, the flood that may follow; and it arouses hope of the rain that will revive the land. The single phenomenon produces both responses at once, and this duality is the verse's theological point. The God who shows the lightning is the God of mercy and of severity at once — the same flash that promises the rain also warns of the strike. The believer who watches a storm is thus given, in nature, a miniature of the divine attributes: hope in God's provision, fear of God's power, held together in a single glance at the sky.

This is why the Qur'an so often pairs fear and hope when describing the believer's relationship to God. The storm is the natural parable of the spiritual life: the believer lives between *khawf* and *rajāʾ*, fearing God's displeasure and hoping for His mercy, and the lightning is the sky's way of teaching that the two belong together.

**The Heavy-Laden Clouds**

The verse closes with *"and Who produces heavy-laden clouds"* (*wa-yunshiʾu al-saḥāba al-thiqāl*). The word *yunshiʾu* is from the root *n-sh-ʾ*, meaning to bring into being, to originate, to raise up — the clouds are not merely blown about but *produced*, brought into existence by God and formed into shape. The adjective *al-thiqāl*, "heavy-laden," describes the clouds weighed down with the water they carry — the rain stored up in them before it falls. The image is of a burden borne across the sky and then released. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the full cycle with wonder:

> *"Hast thou not seen that God drives the clouds, then joins them together, then makes them into a heap, and thou seest the rain come forth from within them?"* (Qur'an 24:43)

The heavy cloud is thus the storehouse of mercy, and the lightning that flashes within it is the announcement of what it carries. The verse's sequence — lightning, fear and hope, heavy clouds — traces the storm in the order a human being experiences it: first the flash, then the double emotion, then the recognition of the cloud's burden and its promise of rain.

**The Spiritual Reading: Flashes of Divine Illumination**

The verse also became, in the tradition of Sufi commentary, a parable of the spiritual life, and this reading deserves mention alongside the literal. Al-Suhrawardī (d. 587/1191), the philosopher of Illumination, read the lightning as the sudden flashes of divine illumination that descend upon the soul walking the spiritual path — moments of clarity and presence that come without warning, bringing joy, and that leave in their wake the fear that they will vanish and the hope that they will return. The heavy clouds, in this reading, are the accumulated states of the heart that these flashes water. This symbolic reading does not replace the literal; it rests upon it, for the Qur'an's nature imagery is always available at both levels at once — the physical sign and the spiritual lesson drawn from it.

**The Storm as a Sign for the Modern Viewer**

For the modern reader, the verse's power is undiminished because the storm has not ceased to be overwhelming. Technology has explained the lightning's physics but has not removed the instinctive awe it produces. The Qur'an's point was never that lightning is unexplained, but that it is a sign — that the human response of fear and hope before it is itself a testimony to the One who arranged both the flash and the emotion. The verse invites the viewer to do what the storm naturally prompts: to feel the fear and the hope, and to trace both upward to their source. The believer who watches a storm with this verse in mind is not merely enduring weather; he is reading a sign, and the sign is addressed to him personally.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:13

> **The thunder hymns His praise, as do the angels, in awe of Him. He sends forth the thunderbolts and strikes therewith whomsoever He will. Yet they dispute concerning God, and He is severe in wrath.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Thunder Joins the Chorus of Praise**

This is the verse from which the sūrah takes its name, and it is the theological climax of the chapter's nature imagery. The thunder — the loudest, most terrifying sound in the ancient world's experience — is declared to be *yusabbiḥu bi-ḥamdihi*: hymning His praise. The verb *sabbaḥa* means to declare transcendence, to glorify, to proclaim God far above all deficiency; *bi-ḥamdihi* adds praise to the declaration, so that the thunder both exalts God and gives thanks to Him. The sūrah has been building to this claim since verse 2: the heavens, the sun, the moon, the mountains, the streams, the fruit — all were signs; now the thunder is named outright as a voice of worship. And the Qur'an states the general principle from which this verse is drawn in 17:44:

> *"The seven heavens and the earth and whosoever is in them glorify Him. And there is no thing, save that it hymns His praise, though you do not understand their praise. Truly He is Clement, Forgiving."* (Qur'an 17:44)

The clause "though you do not understand their praise" is the key that unlocks this verse. The thunder's praise is real, but its language is not ours; the created thing glorifies its Maker by its very existence, by its obedience to the command that set it in motion. The thunder glorifies God by thundering — by being exactly what it was made to be, in exactly the place and manner appointed.

**The Angel Named Thunder: The Tradition and Its Limits**

The commentators record a further tradition that adds depth to the verse's imagery. Several early authorities transmit that *al-raʿd* — "the thunder" — is the name of an angel charged with the clouds, and that his voice is the thunder we hear, just as the lightning is sometimes described as his whip or his flash. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī all transmit this interpretation from the early generations, and it is also recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās. On this reading, the verse's first clause becomes perfectly literal: the thunder hymns His praise because the thunder is an angel, and the angel's glorification is the sound of the storm. The tradition is not unanimous — other commentators read the thunder simply as the sound itself, which praises God in the manner of all created things — but the two readings converge on the same truth: the thunder is not an independent power but a servant, and its voice is worship.

**The Angels in Awe**

The verse then adds: *"as do the angels, in awe of Him"* (*wa-al-malāʾikatu min khīfatihi*). The angels, too, glorify God — but here the emphasis is on the motive: *min khīfatihi*, "out of fear of Him, out of awe before Him." The word *khīfah* is fear born of reverence — not the cowering of a creature before a tyrant, but the awe of a servant before the majesty of his Lord. The pairing is significant: the thunder glorifies, and the angels glorify out of awe. The natural world and the angelic world are here shown doing the same thing for converging reasons — the thunder by its created nature, the angels by their reverent knowledge. The one God receives both forms of praise, and neither is redundant.

**The Thunderbolts: Power That Strikes Where It Will**

The verse then turns from praise to power: *"He sends forth the thunderbolts and strikes therewith whomsoever He will"* (*wa-yursilu al-ṣawāʿiqa fa-yuṣību bihā man yashāʾ*). The *ṣawāʿiq* are the thunderbolts — the destructive lightning that strikes the earth. The verse's point is the totality of divine power: the same God whose thunder praises Him is the God whose thunderbolt can strike whom He wills. No refuge, no height, no technology can place a person beyond the reach of that strike; and conversely, the strike never falls without His will.

The commentators record a specific occasion connected to this clause. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Zamakhsharī transmit that the thunderbolt here refers, in one famous instance, to the mortal bolt with which God struck Arbad ibn Rabīʿah, who had conspired with ʿĀmir ibn al-Ṭufayl against the Prophet ﷺ. The two had come to Madinah plotting to kill him; when they left, God struck Arbad with a thunderbolt and he died on the spot, while ʿĀmir died of a plague. Other commentators, including al-Qurṭubī, caution that the wording of the verse is general and is not confined to this single incident — the verse states a standing reality, of which the death of Arbad is one illustration among many. Both positions are recorded, and both are true: the verse's claim is universal, and the tradition supplies a historical instance of it.

**"Yet They Dispute Concerning God"**

The verse's final movement is its sharpest: after all this — after the thunder's praise and the angels' awe and the bolt that strikes whom God wills — *"yet they dispute concerning God"* (*wa-hum yujādilūna fī Allāh*). The verb *jādala*, to dispute, to argue, to wrangle, describes the Makkans' quarreling about God: whether He is one or many, whether He has partners, whether He can raise the dead. The verse sets the dispute against the thunder as its crushing answer: while they argue about God in their assemblies, the thunder is hymning His praise above their heads and His bolt is in His hand. The absurdity of the dispute is exposed not by argument but by juxtaposition — the whole universe is singing the answer they are still debating.

**"Severe in Wrath": The Meaning of Miḥāl**

The verse closes with *wa-huwa shadīd al-miḥāl* — "and He is severe in wrath." The word *miḥāl* is the verse's most interesting single term, and the commentators examine it closely. It derives from *ḥīlah*, meaning stratagem, plot, or subtle device, and in this intensive form it denotes the perfection of God's scheming — His power to out-plot every plotter, to overtake every opponent by a route the opponent never anticipated. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both gloss the clause as "intense in plotting, severe in stratagem" — but with the crucial clarification that God's *miḥāl* is never deceit, since His stratagem is always just and always deserved by its target. The same theme runs through the Qur'an's treatment of divine "plotting":

> *"And they plotted, and God plotted; and God is the best of plotters."* (Qur'an 3:54)

> *"Truly My scheme is firm."* (Qur'an 7:183)

The theological point is precise: God is not deceived, cannot be outmaneuvered, and is never taken by surprise; every human scheme against His truth is already encompassed by His, and His prevails. The verse thus ends where the sūrah will later return (v. 42): *"unto God belongs plotting altogether."*

**The Verse's Single Lesson**

Taken whole, the verse teaches one lesson in three movements: the thunder praises God, the thunderbolt serves God, and yet people dispute about God. The lesson is the poverty of the dispute and the majesty of its object. The believer who reads this verse during a storm is given the full frame: to hear the thunder as praise, to fear the bolt as power, and to feel the smallness of every human quarrel about God beside the universe's unanimous testimony to Him. The name of the sūrah is thus a summary of its argument: the thunder is not a god to fear in place of God, but a servant to fear God through.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:14

> **Unto Him is the supplication of truth; and those whom they supplicate apart from Him answer them not in the least, save as one who stretches forth his palms toward water that it may reach his mouth, though it never reaches him. And the supplication of the disbelievers is naught but astray.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Only Prayer That Is True**

The verse begins with a compact declaration that the commentators unpack into a full theology of prayer: *"Unto Him is the supplication of truth"* (*lahu daʿwat al-ḥaqq*). The phrase has been read on two levels that converge. On the specific level, transmitted by Ibn ʿAbbās and others, the "supplication of truth" is the *shahādah* itself — *lā ilāha illā Allāh*, "There is no god but God" — the testimony of God's oneness, which is the truest thing any tongue can say and the truest call any heart can make. On the general level, it is any supplication that is true — any call addressed to the One who actually hears and actually answers — as opposed to the supplications addressed to what cannot hear. God Himself issues the standing invitation:

> *"Call upon Me, and I shall respond to you."* (Qur'an 40:60)

The point of the phrase is therefore an argument about the nature of prayer. Prayer is not a magical formula that works regardless of its object; prayer is an address, and its entire value depends on the reality of the one addressed. A prayer to a real, living, hearing, answering God is the supplication of truth; a prayer to an idol, a dead saint, a star, or a force of nature is an address to nothing, and its truth-value is nil. The verse's opening clause quietly redefines worship as correspondence with reality.

**The Idols That Cannot Answer**

The verse then turns to the contrast: *"and those whom they supplicate apart from Him answer them not in the least"* (*wa-alladhīna yadʿūna min dūnihi lā yastajībūna lahum bi-shayʾ*). The word *shayʾ* — "in the least, not a thing" — is emphatic: the idols and false gods do not answer with anything at all, because they possess nothing with which to answer. The Qur'an makes the same point with relentless clarity in verse after verse:

> *"As for those upon whom you call apart from Him, they do not possess so much as the husk of a date stone."* (Qur'an 35:13)

> *"Do they have feet wherewith they walk, or hands wherewith they grasp, or eyes wherewith they see, or ears wherewith they hear?"* (Qur'an 7:195)

The logic is a simple inventory of the capacities that answering requires. To answer a prayer one must at least hear it; to benefit the one who prays one must possess some power; the idols possess neither hearing nor power. Therefore they cannot answer. The verse's brilliance is that it does not argue against idolatry by insult but by description: it simply asks what the idol can do, and the honest answer is nothing.

**The Parable of the Stretched Palms**

Then comes the verse's central image, one of the most vivid in the Qur'an:

> *"...save as one who stretches forth his palms toward water that it may reach his mouth, though it never reaches him."*

The picture is exact. A thirsty man stretches out his open palms toward a body of water, as though the water could perceive his gesture and leap into his mouth. The water, of course, does not move; it does not know he is there; his outstretched hands remain empty. Al-Zamakhsharī explains the correspondence point by point: just as the water cannot perceive the man's stretched palms or respond to them, so the false gods cannot perceive the supplicant's call or respond to it. The gesture is real, the thirst is real, the reach is real — and the result is nothing, because the object of the reach has no capacity to reach back.

The parable does a subtle thing worth naming: it locates the problem not in the sincerity of the worshipper but in the nature of the worshiped. The thirsty man in the image is not insincere; he stretches his palms with real desire. His tragedy is that he has addressed his need to something that cannot meet it. Idolatry, the verse implies, is not always cold or hypocritical; it is often earnest — and it fails not because the worshiper does not try, but because the object is wrong.

**The Prayer That Is Astray**

The verse closes with the verdict: *"And the supplication of the disbelievers is naught but astray"* (*wa-mā duʿāʾ al-kāfirīna illā fī ḍalāl*). The word *ḍalāl* is the Qur'an's term for going astray — wandering, missing the mark, losing the way. The supplication of the disbelievers is not answered and not merely neutral; it is itself a going astray, a wasted motion, an address sent down a road that leads nowhere. The same phrase appears in 40:50, confirming that this is a fixed formula of the Qur'an's teaching on false prayer.

But the verdict should be read with the verse's own mercy in view. The supplication is astray because its object is astray; redirect the same supplication to its true object, and the same words, the same tears, the same outstretched hands become the supplication of truth. The Qur'an never asks the human being to stop praying; it asks him to pray to the One who answers. The desire to call upon something greater than oneself is not the error — it is the deepest and truest instinct in the human being; the error lies only in misdirecting it.

**The Modern Relevance: What We Actually Call Upon**

The verse's diagnosis extends beyond temples and idols into the ordinary texture of life. Whatever a human being turns to in his need with the hope of being answered — wealth, power, reputation, a person, an institution, luck, fate, his own effort — is, in the verse's terms, an object of supplication. The Qur'an's question is the same for all of them: do they hear? do they answer? can they benefit or harm themselves, let alone you? The verse's modern reader is invited to perform the same inventory on his own objects of reliance that the verse performs on the idols of Makkah, and to ask the one question that decides the matter: when I stretch out my palms, does anything reach back? The verse's answer is already given: only God does, and therefore only His is the supplication of truth.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:15

> **And unto God prostrates whosoever is in the heavens and on the earth, willingly or unwillingly, as do their shadows in the morning and the evening.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prostration That No One Can Refuse**

This verse is one of the Qur'an's most sweeping statements about the relationship between creation and Creator, and it deserves to be read slowly. Its claim is that everything in the heavens and the earth — every being, without exception — prostrates to God, either willingly or unwillingly. The verb *yasjudu*, "prostrates," is here understood by the commentators in the sense of submission, lowliness, and the state of being under a power one cannot refuse. Al-Rāzī explains the choice of the word precisely: prostration is the bodily posture of the lowest point — the forehead to the ground — and the verse uses it to say that every created thing is, by its very existence, at the lowest point of dependence before God. The verse's twin is found in 19:93:

> *"There is none in the heavens and on the earth, but that it comes unto the Compassionate as a servant."* (Qur'an 19:93)

The two verses state the same truth from two angles: the servant comes, and the servant prostrates — whether he knows it or not.

**Willingly or Unwillingly: The Two Prostrations**

The verse's central distinction is between *ṭawʿan* and *karhan* — willingly and unwillingly. The commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, explain the two terms as describing the two possible attitudes of a creature toward its own state of dependence. Some beings understand their state and prostrate voluntarily — the angels, the believers, the things that worship God in knowledge and love. Others do not understand and yet prostrate involuntarily — the disbeliever, the idol, the whole order of nature — because the very structure of their existence is submission, whether or not they consent. The idol-worshiper who refuses to bow to God is himself, in his very refusal, still sustained by God, still subject to God's decree, still prostrate in the existential sense. His refusal is an attitude, not an escape.

This is why the verse functions as a quiet but devastating answer to the idolatry the sūrah has been dismantling. The pagans prostrated to idols — carved stones — while those same idols, and the pagans themselves, were prostrate before God. The verse reverses the entire hierarchy the idolaters had built: what they bowed to was itself bowed, and the One they refused to bow to was the One before whom everything, including their refusal, was laid low.

**The Spiritual Reading: Two Kinds of Voluntary Prostration**

Al-Maybudī, in his celebrated commentary *Kashf al-Asrār*, adds a spiritual depth to the distinction between willing and unwilling prostration that is worth preserving. The willing prostration, he explains, is the prostration of the one who submits to God's command and majesty for no ulterior motive — not for reward and not from fear of harm, but for God alone, out of pure love. The unwilling prostration is the prostration of the one who bows for a purpose — to elicit a blessing, to avert a harm, to obtain something for the self. The distinction is not between the worshiper and the disbeliever only, but between two grades of the worshiper: the one who bows because he wants something, and the one who bows because God deserves the bowing. The verse thus opens, for the spiritual tradition, onto the highest question of worship — not merely whether one prostrates, but why.

**The Shadows: Even the Silhouette Submits**

The verse's final image is its most striking: *"as do their shadows in the morning and the evening"* (*wa-ẓilāluhum bi-al-ghuduwwi wa-al-āṣāl*). The shadows of all things prostrate to God at dawn and at dusk. The image is drawn from direct observation: at the two ends of the day, when the sun is low, every object casts a long shadow that stretches out along the ground, bending and lengthening as though bowing. The verse takes this daily sight and reads it as worship — the shadows themselves, the most insubstantial part of any thing, are in a posture of prostration. The Qur'an develops the same image elsewhere:

> *"Have they not considered that whatsoever God has created casts its shadow to the right and to the left, prostrating to God while in a state of abject humility?"* (Qur'an 16:48)

Al-Nasafī explains the mention of morning and evening as indicating perpetuity — the shadows prostrate at both ends of every day, meaning always. The point is total: even the shadow of a thing, the part of it with no substance at all, is not outside the prostration. If the shadow submits, how much more the thing itself.

**The Verse as a Foundation for the Prostration of Recitation**

This verse is one of the verses of the Qur'an at which the reader performs *sujūd al-tilāwah*, the prostration of recitation. The practice is significant in light of the verse's own content: the reader, hearing the claim that everything prostrates, does not merely agree with it in words but enacts it with his body. He joins the chorus the verse describes, placing his forehead on the ground in voluntary prostration, aligning his own act with the universal prostration of the cosmos. The prostration of recitation is thus the verse's own meaning made physical — the worshiper steps out of the category of those who prostrate unwillingly and into the category of those who prostrate willingly.

**A Reflection on Universality**

For the modern reader, the verse offers a correction to a common modern assumption — the assumption that worship is a private, optional, human activity, an add-on to existence rather than its ground. The verse's claim is the opposite: submission to God is the fundamental condition of everything that exists; the only question is whether each being acknowledges the condition or lives in denial of it. The believer's voluntary prostration is not the creation of a new fact but the recognition of an old one. The verse thus grounds worship in the structure of reality itself: to bow to God is to be true to what one is; to refuse is to fight a battle against one's own existence — a battle that, the verse calmly notes, is already lost, because even the shadow of the one who refuses is prostrate.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:16

> **Say, "Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?" Say, "God." Say, "Then have you taken, apart from Him, protectors who have no power over what benefit or harm may come to themselves?" Say, "Are the blind and the seer equal, or are darkness and light equal?" Or have they ascribed unto God partners who have created the like of His creation, such that that creation seems alike to them? Say, "God is the Creator of all things, and He is the One, the Paramount."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument That Ends Its Own Debate**

This verse is a complete argument against idolatry, delivered in a sequence of commands to the Prophet ﷺ — each beginning with "Say" (*qul*) — that builds from the opponent's own admission to the conclusion he cannot escape. The commentators, led by al-Bayḍāwī and Ibn Kathīr, reconstruct the logic of the verse as a series of steps, and it is worth laying them out in order, because the verse's power lies in the order.

**Step One: The Answer They Cannot Refuse**

The first command is a question: *"Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?"* And the second command supplies the answer before any opponent can speak: *"Say, 'God.'"* Why does the Prophet answer his own question? Because, as al-Bayḍāwī explains, the Makkan idolaters could not answer otherwise. Ask any of them who created the heavens and the earth, who sends the rain, who gives life and death, and they would reply, "God" — for the Makkan pagans, in their own belief, acknowledged Allāh as the supreme Creator while associating lesser deities with Him in worship. The Qur'an records this admission repeatedly:

> *"And if you asked them, 'Who created the heavens and the earth?' they would surely say, 'God.'"* (Qur'an 31:25)

> *"And if you asked them, 'Who created you?' they would surely say, 'God.'"* (Qur'an 43:87)

The argument therefore begins from common ground. The sūrah does not have to prove God's existence to the idolaters; they already admit it. What it must prove is that the Creator they admit has no partners — and the proof proceeds from that very admission.

**Step Two: The Protectors Who Cannot Help Themselves**

The second question exposes the contradiction: *"Then have you taken, apart from Him, protectors who have no power over what benefit or harm may come to themselves?"* The word *awliyāʾ* — "protectors, patrons, guardians" — is precisely what the idolaters claimed their deities were: they worshiped them, they said, so that the deities might intercede and protect. The verse's reply is an inventory of capacity: do these protectors control their own benefit and harm? They do not. An idol cannot bring itself good or ward evil from itself; it cannot even defend itself against the worshiper who carves it. Al-Bayḍāwī draws the conclusion: if the deity cannot benefit or harm itself, it cannot, by a still stronger logic, benefit or harm the one who worships it. The reliance on such protectors is therefore not a lesser form of prudence but an outright irrationality — a trust placed in what is, by the worshiper's own admission, powerless.

**Step Three: The Blind and the Seer**

The third command introduces the verse's central metaphor: *"Are the blind and the seer equal, or are darkness and light equal?"* The blind and the seer, darkness and light — these are fixed pairs in the Qur'an's moral vocabulary. The commentators, including al-Qurṭubī, explain the correspondences: the blind is the disbeliever, the seer the believer; darkness is disbelief, light is faith; and the two pairs converge on the same distinction — the difference between the one who perceives reality and the one who does not. The question is rhetorical, and its answer is obvious to every hearer: they are not equal. The verse's point in posing it here is to say that the difference between worshiping God and worshiping idols is not a difference of taste or culture; it is the difference between sight and blindness, light and darkness. One cannot say "I prefer my path and you prefer yours" as though the two were parallel; one is vision and the other is not.

**Step Four: The Partners Who Create Nothing**

The fourth command is the verse's deepest thrust: *"Or have they ascribed unto God partners who have created the like of His creation, such that that creation seems alike to them?"* The question proposes the only condition under which idolatry would make sense: if the partners had created something comparable to God's creation, so that the two creations could be confused. But the premise is false, and the idolaters know it. Their partners create nothing at all. Al-Ṭūsī and others explain the argument's full force: the idolaters admit that God created everything; their idols created nothing; therefore there is no likeness between the two, no confusion between the works, and no ground whatever for giving the idols a share of worship. The verse's phrasing is a masterpiece of irony — it states the impossible condition ("have created the like of His creation") precisely in order to reveal its impossibility.

**Step Five: The Conclusion**

The verse then closes the argument with the declaration that follows from all four steps: *"Say, 'God is the Creator of all things, and He is the One, the Paramount.'"* The title *al-Wāḥid*, "the One," states God's unity — He is one in His essence, without division or partner. The title *al-Qahhār*, "the Paramount, the Subduer," states His supremacy — He prevails over all things, and all things are subdued before Him. The two titles together are the conclusion of the whole argument: because God is the Creator of everything, He is One; because He is One, He prevails over everything; and because He prevails over everything, the idols — which neither create nor prevail — are nothing at all.

**The Structure of the Verse as a Teaching in Method**

The verse deserves study not only for its conclusion but for its method, which is a model of how the Qur'an reasons with an opponent. It begins from the opponent's own admitted premise; it proceeds by questions whose answers the opponent himself must supply; it uses analogies drawn from common experience; and it ends in a conclusion that follows with necessity. There is no insult, no threat, no dismissal — only the patient unfolding of a contradiction. The verse is, in effect, a complete Socratic dialogue compressed into five commands, and its tone teaches the believer how to argue: with the opponent's own premises, not against his person.

**The Modern Reader and the Argument from Lordship**

The argument of this verse — known to theologians as the argument from *rubūbiyyah*, from God's lordship — remains fully alive. Its modern form is simple: whatever a person treats as his ultimate reliance — and everyone has an ultimate reliance — must possess the attributes that ultimate reliance requires: the power to benefit and harm, the knowledge to hear and answer, the permanence to outlast the one who relies on it. The verse's inventory can be applied to any candidate. Does it control its own benefit and harm? Did it create anything comparable to creation? The verse's conclusion follows for every false candidate exactly as it followed for the idols: nothing qualifies except the Creator of all things, the One, the Paramount. The argument from Lordship is thus not an artifact of a bygone dispute; it is a standing test, and the Qur'an has already written out both the questions and the answer.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:17

> **He sends down water from the sky, so that the riverbeds flow according to their measure and the torrent carries a swelling froth; and from that which they kindle in the fire, seeking ornament or pleasure, is a froth like unto it. Thus does God set forth truth and falsehood. As for the froth, it passes away as dross. And as for that which benefits mankind, it remains on the earth. Thus does God set forth parables.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Parable of Water and Froth**

This verse contains what is arguably the sūrah's central parable — the image by which the Qur'an most fully illustrates the fate of truth and falsehood. It presents not one but two similitudes, and the commentators, including al-Baghawī and al-Rāzī, treat them as a pair deliberately yoked: the torrent's froth and the furnace's dross. Both are scum that rises and vanishes; both are contrasted with a substance that remains and benefits. The verse then states its meaning explicitly, so that no listener can miss it: *"Thus does God set forth truth and falsehood."*

**The First Similitude: The Torrent's Froth**

The first image is drawn from the desert rain that the sūrah's first audience knew intimately. God sends water from the sky; the water flows into the *wādīs*, the riverbeds, each carrying *"according to its measure"* (*bi-qadarihā*) — each valley receiving and channeling what its size and shape allow. When the water swells into a torrent, it churns up the dirt, the twigs, the debris of the land, and carries on its surface a *zabad rābiyan* — a swelling froth, a bulging scum. Al-Baghawī describes the scene: the froth rides high on the flood, catching the eye, looking for a moment like the substance of the water itself, while the water — the real substance — runs beneath it. Then the torrent subsides. The froth breaks, dries, and disappears, blown away or left stranded on the banks, while the water soaks into the earth and remains, becoming the source of life.

**The Second Similitude: The Furnace's Dross**

The second image shifts from water to fire. The verse speaks of *"that which they kindle in the fire, seeking ornament or pleasure"* — the ores, the gold and silver and copper, that people heat over flames to refine. The purpose is *ḥilyah*, ornament — jewelry and adornment — or *matāʿ*, utility and enjoyment — tools and vessels. In the refining, the impurities separate from the metal and float to the surface of the melt as froth, *"a froth like unto it"* — like the torrent's scum. The refiner skims it off and discards it. The metal remains — pure, valuable, enduring.

**What the Two Similitudes Have in Common**

The verse's artistry lies in the parallel it constructs, and al-Rāzī spells out the points of contact. In both images, a valuable substance — water, metal — rises from the earth or sky; in both, a worthless scum rises with it and for a moment appears on top; in both, the scum is cast off and the substance remains. The froth is loud, buoyant, and briefly visible; the water and metal are quiet, heavy, and enduring. The verse then makes the application explicit: *"Thus does God set forth truth and falsehood."* The truth is the water and the metal; falsehood is the froth and the dross.

**The Application: Truth Remains, Falsehood Vanishes**

The verse's verdict is stated twice, once as image and once as interpretation. As image: *"As for the froth, it passes away as dross."* As interpretation: *"And as for that which benefits mankind, it remains on the earth."* The conclusion is the Qur'an's fixed law of history and of ideas:

> *"Truth has come, and falsehood has vanished. Truly falsehood is ever vanishing."* (Qur'an 17:81)

The application works on every scale. Falsehood may rise high — a false ideology, a false religion, a false claim, a false ruler — and for a season it may dominate the surface, catching the eye, swelling above everything around it, while the truth runs quietly beneath. But the scum is buoyant precisely because it is empty; its height is the height of nothing. The truth is heavy precisely because it is real; its depth is the depth of substance. The froth passes; the water remains; and every observer of history can verify the pattern. Empires built on falsehood have swelled and broken; truths have been buried, mocked, and persecuted, and have remained.

**Why the Parable Is Framed as Water and Fire**

A further point deserves attention: the verse chooses water and fire as its two elements — the two elements on which all human life and industry depend. The choice is not accidental. Al-Qurṭubī notes that water and fire are the two great agents of purification in human experience: water cleanses, fire refines. Both, in the verse, produce the same lesson — that purification casts off a worthless scum and leaves a valuable remainder. The parable thus links the physical world's law of purification to the moral world's law of truth: what is pure endures, and what is impure is separated and discarded. The verse is saying that the world itself, in its water and its fire, is a standing demonstration of the fate of truth and falsehood.

**The Modern Reading: The Buoyancy of the False and the Patience of the True**

For the modern reader, this verse is a remedy for a specific form of despair — the despair of seeing falsehood apparently triumph. The verse's answer is not that falsehood never rises, but that its rising is precisely what the parable predicts. The froth is on top; that is its nature and its doom. The believer who understands the parable is freed from two errors at once: he does not mistake the froth's temporary height for permanence, and he does not mistake the truth's temporary depth for defeat. The parable teaches a kind of patience that is not passive but discerning — the ability to watch a torrent of falsehood and know, with certainty, what the end of a torrent is. And it teaches a corresponding confidence in the truth's own weight: the water does not need to shout; it needs only to remain water.

**"Thus Does God Set Forth Parables"**

The verse closes with the formula that frames the whole Qur'anic method of teaching: *"Thus does God set forth parables."* The word *amthāl* — parables, similitudes — names the Qur'an's characteristic way of making the invisible visible, of clothing abstract truths in the imagery of daily experience. The formula is itself a teaching: the parables are not ornaments but instruments, chosen because the human mind grasps the concrete before the abstract, the seen before the unseen. A listener who remembers nothing else from this sūrah will remember the torrent and the froth — and the verse's structure guarantees that the memory of the image carries the truth inside it, the way the water carries the life that remains.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:18

> **Those who answer their Lord shall have that which is most beautiful. But those who answer Him not—were they to possess all that is on the earth and the like of it besides, they would seek to ransom themselves thereby. For them there shall be an evil reckoning. Their refuge is Hell. What an evil resting place!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Answers and Their Two Outcomes**

The parable of the froth and the water has just shown the fate of truth and falsehood in the abstract; this verse now applies the lesson to human beings, dividing mankind into two groups according to a single criterion: the answer they gave to God's call. The structure is a clean contrast. *"Those who answer their Lord"* (*alladhīna istajābū li-Rabbihim*) receive *"that which is most beautiful"* (*al-ḥusnā*). *"Those who answer Him not"* (*wa-alladhīna lam yastajībū lahu*) receive the ransom that fails, the reckoning that is evil, and the refuge that is Hell. The verse's message is that the whole of human destiny turns on one thing: whether the call was answered.

**What It Means to Answer the Lord**

The word *istajāba* — "answered, responded" — deserves attention, because it contains more than belief. To answer a call is to hear it and to move. The call here is God's call to faith and to obedience — the call delivered by the prophets, inscribed in the fitrah, echoed in the signs of nature. Al-Qurṭubī explains that those who answer their Lord are those who respond to His call to believe in Him and to accept His messengers. The word thus gathers together hearing, accepting, and acting: the one who answers is not the one who merely acknowledges the message but the one who lets it change his direction. The Qur'an elsewhere gives the fullest picture of what this answering means in practice:

> *"The response of the believers, when they are called unto God and His Messenger that he may judge between them, is only to say, 'We hear and we obey.'"* (Qur'an 24:51)

**"That Which Is Most Beautiful": The Reward Named Before the Punishment**

The reward of the answerers is named first, and it is named by the superlative *al-ḥusnā* — "that which is most beautiful." The commentators read it on two levels that are not alternatives but expansions. Al-Qurṭubī transmits the reading that it refers to Paradise — the most beautiful of all outcomes, the Garden whose description fills the closing verses of the sūrah. Others, including the reading preserved by al-Baghawī, understand it more broadly as the beautiful recompense in both worlds — divine assistance in this life and Paradise in the next, matching the pattern of 10:26:

> *"Unto those who are virtuous, that which is most beautiful, and more besides."* (Qur'an 10:26)

The important structural point is that the reward is stated before the punishment, the hope before the warning. The verse's first clause holds open the door before the second clause describes what lies beyond it. This ordering is characteristic of the Qur'an's mercy: the glad tiding is always given first, so that the warning, when it comes, falls on an ear that has already heard the alternative.

**The Ransom That Cannot Be Paid**

The verse then turns to those who did not answer, and its picture is built on a single, devastating image: *"were they to possess all that is on the earth and the like of it besides, they would seek to ransom themselves thereby."* The word *iftadā* — "to ransom oneself" — is drawn from the language of captivity and war: a prisoner buys his release; a captive pays his price. The verse's point is that on the Day of Judgment the non-answerers would gladly give everything — the whole earth and its double — to buy release from what they face, and the offer would not be accepted. The Qur'an repeats this image in several places, each time expanding the quantity of the rejected ransom:

> *"Even if those who disbelieved possessed all that is on the earth and the like of it besides, to ransom themselves thereby from the punishment of the Day of Resurrection, it would not be accepted from them."* (Qur'an 5:36)

The image's logic is worth pausing over. The value of a ransom is relative to what it buys; the verse is saying that no quantity of the world can equal the weight of a single soul's reckoning. The man who spent his life accumulating the world discovers, in that final moment, that the entire world is not enough to buy back what he sold his soul for. The time for exchange has passed; the only currency that ever had value in that transaction — the answered call — was left unspent.

**The Evil Reckoning**

The verse then names what awaits: *"For them there shall be an evil reckoning"* (*sūʾ al-ḥisāb*). The reckoning (*ḥisāb*) is the Day of Judgment's great accounting, when each soul is confronted with the full record of its deeds. The word *sūʾ*, "evil," modifies the reckoning itself: it is not merely a reckoning with an unfavorable outcome but a reckoning that is, in its very conduct, severe — the Qur'an elsewhere calls it a reckoning in which there is no leniency, no reduction, no overlooking:

> *"And those who disbelieve in the signs of God, for them is a severe punishment."* (Qur'an 3:4)

The commentators contrast this with the *ḥisāb yasīr* — the "easy reckoning" — promised to the believers, in which the account is settled with mercy. The difference between the two reckonings is the difference between the two answers: the one who answered God's call in this life finds the accounting gentle; the one who ignored it finds it exacting in full.

**"Their Refuge Is Hell. What an Evil Resting Place!"**

The verse closes with the sūrah's characteristic exclamation: *"Their refuge is Hell. What an evil resting place!"* (*maʾwāhum jahannam wa-biʾsa al-mihād*). The word *maʾwā* — "refuge, shelter, resting place" — is bitterly ironic: the word that normally promises safety here names its opposite. *Al-mihād* is the spread bed, the cradle — the word used in the Qur'an for the earth itself as a resting place prepared by God (cf. 78:6). The verse says that what the non-answerers have prepared for themselves is the worst possible version of a resting place — a bed of fire in place of the spread earth. The exclamation *biʾsa* — "how evil!" — is the Qur'an's formula of condemnation, and it closes the verse with the full weight of the divine verdict: the refusal to answer a call was, in the end, the refusal of a refuge.

**The Verse's Single Lesson**

The verse reduces the whole question of human destiny to one variable, and it is worth stating the reduction plainly because it is the verse's entire force. The two groups are not distinguished by wealth, lineage, intelligence, or any accident of birth; they are distinguished by the answer. Those who answered receive the beautiful; those who did not receive the ransom that fails. Everything else the verse says — the earth and its double, the reckoning, the refuge — is elaboration of that one fact. The lesson for the reader is immediate and personal: the call is still being delivered, the answer is still possible, and the verse's whole contrast is assembled for no other purpose than to move the reader from the second group into the first.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:19

> **Is one who knows that what has been sent down unto thee from thy Lord is the truth like one who is blind? Only those who possess intellect reflect,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Divides Mankind**

This verse opens a long sentence that will continue through verse 22, and it poses the sūrah's most fundamental question in its most pointed form. The comparison is between two human beings: one who *knows* that what has been revealed to the Prophet is the truth, and one who is *blind*. The question — *"Are they equal?"* — is left formally unspoken but is present in the very construction, and its answer is the most obvious answer in the world: no. Al-Ṭūsī and others explain the verse's force: the one who knows is the believer, the one who is blind is the disbeliever, and between them there is a difference not of degree but of kind, the difference between sight and blindness.

**The Knowledge That Is Contrasted with Blindness**

The verse's first phrase deserves precision: *"one who knows that what has been sent down unto thee from thy Lord is the truth"* (*man yaʿlamu annamā unzila ilayka min Rabbika al-ḥaqq*). The knowledge here is not a vague religious sentiment but a specific conviction with a specific content: that the revelation is *al-ḥaqq* — the truth — and that its source is *min Rabbika* — from your Lord. The two elements are inseparable: the believer knows both *what* the revelation is (truth) and *whence* it came (from God). Al-Bayḍāwī and Ibn ʿAjībah note that this knowledge is not a claim the believer merely asserts; it is a state that transforms the one who holds it, because to know that a message is from God is to stand in a relationship of obligation to it. The knowing one accepts the message and acts upon it; the blind one does not.

**The Blindness of the Heart**

The word *aʿmā* — "blind" — is the Qur'an's fixed term for spiritual blindness, and it must be distinguished from physical blindness, which the Qur'an nowhere condemns. The blindness here is of the heart, not the eye, as the Qur'an states with perfect clarity:

> *"Truly it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts within the breasts go blind."* (Qur'an 22:46)

The blind man of this verse is one who possesses sight and yet does not see — whose eyes register the signs but whose heart does not perceive them. Al-Bayḍāwī explains the mechanics: the disbeliever's blindness is not a deprivation he was born with but a condition he has acquired through turning away. The Qur'an's teaching is that spiritual blindness is, in the final analysis, chosen — the heart that will not look, the eye that will not be lifted, the truth that is kept at a distance. This is why the verse can pose the comparison at all: if blindness were an innocent misfortune, the comparison would be cruel rather than just; but because it is a refusal, the comparison is the verdict.

**The Comparison Extended**

This verse's comparison is part of a fixed pattern in the Qur'an, and the sūrah itself has already used it. Verse 16 asked whether the blind and the seer are equal, and verse 19 now returns to the pair in its personal form. The same contrast recurs across the Qur'an:

> *"The parable of the two parties is as the blind and the deaf, and the seer and the hearer. Are they equal in comparison?"* (Qur'an 11:24)

> *"And not equal are the blind and the seer, nor are darkness and light."* (Qur'an 35:19–20)

The recurrence is not repetition but insistence: the Qur'an wants the reader to feel the starkness of the division, to see that between belief and disbelief there is no middle position of "equal but different." The question "are they equal?" always has the same answer, and the answer is never in doubt.

**"Only Those Who Possess Intellect Reflect"**

The verse closes with the phrase that will govern the whole passage to come: *"Only those who possess intellect reflect"* (*innamā yatadhakkaru ulū al-albāb*). The word *albāb* is the plural of *lubb*, which the lexicographers gloss as the core, the kernel, the innermost essence of a thing — the purest and most concentrated part, as the kernel is the purest part of the nut. *Ulū al-albāb* — "those possessed of kernels, of pure minds" — is the Qur'an's title for the truly intelligent, the ones whose intellect has not been buried under the husks of appetite and distraction. The verb *yatadhakkaru* — "reflect, remember, take heed" — comes from *dhikr*, remembrance, and it carries the sense of calling a forgotten truth back to mind. The verse is saying: only those with pure minds call the truth back to mind; the rest let it be buried.

**The Nature of the Pure Mind**

What makes a mind "pure," in the verse's terms? The classical commentators answer by describing the *lubb* as the intellect freed from the domination of passion and distraction — the mind that can see a truth and follow it, regardless of what following it costs. Al-Rāzī and others note the connection between this verse and the passage that follows: the very next verses will describe what those with pure minds actually do — fulfill the pact, join what is joined, fear their Lord, practice patience, prayer, and charity. The verse thus defines its elite not by what they claim to know but by what they do; the proof of the pure mind is the conduct that follows from it. The intellect that merely knows and does not act is not the *lubb*; it is still husk.

**A Word for the Modern Reader**

The verse's question travels intact into every age because the division it names is permanent. In every generation there are those who know — who have weighed the evidence and accepted the truth and let it reorganize their lives — and those who are blind — who have the same evidence before them and do not see. The verse's contribution is to state the division without apology and without relativism: the two are not equal, and no amount of polite pluralism can make them so. But its second half softens the starkness into an invitation: the quality that makes the difference is *albāb* — the pure mind — and the pure mind is not a birthright but a cultivation. The verse thus ends by pointing the reader toward what he can become: not toward a verdict on his present blindness but toward the possibility of the mind that remembers and reflects.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:20

> **who fulfill the pact with God and break not the covenant,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Mark of the Pure Mind**

The previous verse declared that only those possessed of pure minds take heed; the sentence now continues by describing, one quality at a time, who these people actually are. The first mark is given in this verse: they *"fulfill the pact with God and break not the covenant"* (*yūfūna bi-ʿahdi Allāhi wa-lā yanquḍūna al-mīthāq*). The verse thus opens the Qur'an's portrait of the perfected believer with fidelity — with the keeping of promises. That fidelity should come first is itself significant: before prayer, before charity, before patience, the pure mind is defined by its reliability, its trustworthiness, its refusal to break what it has pledged.

**The Pact and the Covenant: Two Names for One Bond**

The verse uses two words that the commentators examine with care: *ʿahd* (pact) and *mīthāq* (covenant). Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī explain the relationship between them. The two terms point to the same underlying reality — the bond between the human being and his Lord — but they approach it from different angles. *ʿAhd* is the pledge itself, the commitment undertaken; *mīthāq* is the binding and strengthening of that pledge — the word comes from *wathāqa*, to tie firmly, and denotes a covenant drawn tight so that it cannot slip. The verse therefore says: they fulfill the pledge, and they do not loosen the knot. The double expression is the Qur'an's way of closing every possible loophole, of describing fidelity at both the level of the original promise and the level of its daily maintenance.

**The Primordial Covenant**

What is the pact that these people fulfill? The commentators trace it to the primordial covenant recorded in 7:172, the event that stands at the beginning of the Qur'an's account of the human condition:

> *"And when thy Lord took from the Children of Adam, from their loins, their progeny, and made them bear witness concerning themselves: 'Am I not your Lord?' They said, 'Yea, we bear witness.'"* (Qur'an 7:172)

This is the *mīthāq* in its original form — the covenant in which every human soul, before entering the world, bore witness that God is its Lord. The commentators explain that the verse's two terms map onto this reality in a complementary way: the *ʿahd* is the specific pretemporal pledge of acknowledging God's lordship, and the *mīthāq* is the covenant in its general, encompassing form — covering the whole of what the servant owes his Lord, from belief to obedience to the rights owed to other creatures. Some commentators, including al-Rāzī, offer an alternative reading in which the *ʿahd* is the burden of *taklīf* — the moral and legal responsibility with which God charged the human being — and the *mīthāq* is the specific content of that responsibility as accepted through the exercise of free will: the promise to obey and to act righteously. Both readings converge: the pure-minded are those who keep the bond.

**Fidelity as the Root of All Virtue**

Why does fidelity come first in the portrait? The answer lies in what fidelity is. A promise is the soul's own word given binding force; to keep a promise is to keep faith with one's own word, and every other virtue is, in a sense, a promise kept — a commitment to truth, to justice, to prayer, to charity, each of them a pledge that the soul has made and must honor. The one who breaks the covenant with God has broken the root, and every branch of virtue withers with it. The Qur'an presents fidelity as the foundation precisely because it is the most general of the virtues: it is the virtue that binds the person to the good across time, against the drift of mood and circumstance. A person who does only what he feels like doing is not yet moral; morality begins when the promise holds even when the feeling fails. The pure-minded are defined by this: their bond does not depend on their mood.

**The Corrupting Effect of Broken Pledges**

The verse's negative half — *"and break not the covenant"* (*wa-lā yanquḍūna al-mīthāq*) — points to what fidelity is guarding against, and the Qur'an's wider teaching explains why the breaking of the covenant is so grave. The one who breaks his pledge to God does not merely fail in one obligation; he unravels the bond on which all obligations rest. The Qur'an describes those who break the covenant as the ones who "work corruption upon the earth" (cf. 2:27; 13:25), because a broken bond is a severed connection, and a person severed from his word becomes a source of disorder wherever he acts. The verse's portrait of the believer is therefore, in its negative half, a portrait of what the believer refuses to become: the covenant-breaker, the one whose promises are weather, who can be counted on only as long as it suits him.

**A Modern Application: The Character That Underlies Religion**

This verse corrects a persistent modern misreading of religion — the idea that religious devotion is an inward, emotional affair separable from character. The Qur'an's first description of the perfected believer is not about ecstasy or sentiment; it is about fidelity, the most characterological of all the virtues. The pure-minded believer is, first of all, the kind of person who keeps his word. A person whose mystical states are high but whose promises are broken has, in the verse's terms, not yet taken the first step. The verse thus locates religion in the most practical and verifiable place: the keeping of promises, with God and with people. Anyone can test himself by this verse, because everyone knows what he has pledged and whether he has kept it.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:21

> **who join what God has commanded be joined, fear their Lord, and dread an evil reckoning,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second and Third Marks of the Pure Mind**

The description of the pure-minded continues with two further qualities joined in a single verse: they *"join what God has commanded be joined"* (*yaṣilūna mā amara Allāhu bihi an yūṣal*), and they *"fear their Lord and dread an evil reckoning"* (*wa-yakhshawna Rabbahum wa-yakhāfūna sūʾ al-ḥisāb*). The three elements — joining, fearing, dreading — form a tight progression: outward conduct, inward reverence, and future accountability. Together they complete the picture begun in verse 20, moving from the bond with God to the bonds among people, and from the bonds among people to the awe that governs them all.

**What God Has Commanded Be Joined**

The phrase *"what God has commanded be joined"* is one of the Qur'an's most capacious expressions, and the commentators deliberately read it broadly. Ibn Kathīr transmits the interpretation that it refers, first and foremost, to the ties of kinship (*ṣilat al-raḥim*) — the maintaining of family bonds, the kind and correct treatment of blood relatives, and kindness to the poor and the needy. The word *ṣila* — "joining, connecting" — is the exact antonym of *qaṭīʿah* — "severing" — and the Qur'an's moral vocabulary is built around the pair: the righteous join, the corrupt sever. But Ibn Kathīr and others also transmit the wider reading: *"what God has commanded be joined"* encompasses the fulfillment of all acts of obedience — everything that God has ordered to be connected, whether the ties of kinship, the bonds of faith among the believers, the obligations of the Law, or the connection of the heart to its Lord. The phrase is deliberately general because the principle is general: the believer is a joiner, a connector, a maintainer of bonds; the disbeliever, as verse 25 will show, is a severer.

**The Social Logic of Joining**

Why does the Qur'an make the joining of bonds a defining mark of the believer? The answer lies in the structure of human flourishing. Human beings live in a web of relationships — family, community, the bonds of faith and of common humanity — and the health of every society depends on the maintenance of these bonds. A bond that is maintained conducts good between people — help, mercy, knowledge, support, love; a bond that is severed cuts off that flow and leaves people isolated. The verse's image of joining is thus an image of circulation: the believer is one through whom the good continues to flow between persons, because he keeps the channels open. The severer, by contrast, is a blockage. This is why the Qur'an so consistently links the severing of bonds with "corruption upon the earth" — the severed bond is, in social terms, a broken circuit, and a community of broken circuits is a community collapsing into mutual harm.

**The Primacy of Kinship**

Within the general command to join, the commentators give kinship the first place, and the Qur'an's treatment of *ṣilat al-raḥim* explains why. The family is the first society, the first school of every moral quality, and the bond of kinship is the first bond a human being ever experiences. The one who maintains his kin has learned, in the most intimate setting, the discipline that all other bonds require: patience with difficulty, generosity without accounting, forgiveness of injury, constancy over time. The Prophet's ﷺ teachings on kinship are emphatic, and the tradition records them in strong terms:

> *"The one who severs the ties of kinship will not enter Paradise."* (al-Bukhārī 5984; Muslim 2556)

> *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him maintain the ties of kinship."* (al-Bukhārī 6138; Muslim 47)

The commentators read these teachings as the Prophet's own commentary on verses such as this one: the joining of what God commanded be joined begins, concretely, at the family table and the family door.

**Fearing the Lord: The Inward Root**

The verse then turns inward: *"fear their Lord"* (*yakhshawna Rabbahum*). The verb *khashiya* is not the ordinary word for fear; it is a fear composed of knowledge and awe — the reverence of one who understands the majesty of the one he stands before. Al-Rāzī distinguishes it from other kinds of fear: *khashyah* is fear grounded in the knowledge of God's greatness, not the animal fear of a threat. The pure-minded fear their Lord because they know Him; their awe is the shadow cast by His majesty upon a heart that perceives it. The commentators note that the fear here is attached to the joining just described: they maintain the bonds and obey the commands *while fearing their Lord* — that is, their obedience is not performed for show or for advantage but in a state of reverence toward the One who commanded it. The fear is thus the quality-control of the obedience: it guarantees that the joining is done for God and under God.

**Dreading an Evil Reckoning: The Future That Governs the Present**

The verse's final element is *"and dread an evil reckoning"* (*wa-yakhāfūna sūʾ al-ḥisāb*). Here a second word for fear appears — *khāfa*, the more general term for dread — and it is attached to a specific object: the evil reckoning, the severe accounting of the Day of Judgment that verse 18 described awaiting those who did not answer their Lord. The verse's logic is now complete and visible: the pure-minded join the bonds because they fear their Lord, and they fear their Lord because they dread the reckoning. The chain runs from the future backward — the anticipation of the accounting governs the conduct of the present. This is the Qur'an's characteristic orientation of the believer toward time: the person who lives with the reckoning in view lives differently from the person who has forgotten it. The dread is not paralyzing; it is protective, the way the awareness of a cliff's edge makes a walker careful, not immobile.

**The Verse's Complete Portrait**

Read together with verse 20, this verse now gives the full first half of the believer's portrait: fidelity to the covenant, the joining of all commanded bonds, the fear of the Lord, and the dread of the evil reckoning. The four qualities interlock. The covenant is kept, the bonds are joined, and both are done in a state of awe before the Lord, sustained by the awareness of the accounting to come. What emerges is not a list but an organism — a complete human being, reliable in promise, generous in relationship, reverent in heart, and sober about his own end. The sūrah will add the remaining qualities in the next verse, but the foundation is already laid here: the pure-minded are, above all, people of bonds — kept, joined, and honored in the sight of the Lord they fear.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:22

> **and who are patient, seeking the Face of their Lord, perform the prayer, and spend from that which We have provided them, secretly and openly, and who repel evil with good. For them there shall be the reward of the Abode—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Completion of the Believer's Portrait**

This verse completes the long description of the pure-minded that began at verse 19, and it gathers the remaining qualities into a single sweep: patience, prayer, spending, and the repelling of evil with good. Each quality has been prepared for by what came before — the fidelity of verse 20, the joining and fear of verse 21 — and together they assemble the full portrait of the believer whom the sūrah has been describing. The verse closes the sentence with the reward: *"For them there shall be the reward of the Abode"* (*ulāʾika lahum ʿuqbā al-dār*).

**Patience Seeking the Face of the Lord**

The first quality is patience — *ṣabr* — but it is immediately qualified in a way that transforms its meaning: *"seeking the Face of their Lord"* (*ibtighāʾa wajhi Rabbihim*). Patience alone, the verse implies, is not yet virtue; endurance can be stoic pride, suppressed rage, or the calculation of one who waits to strike later. The patience that counts is the patience whose motive is the Face of God — endurance undertaken for His sake, in pursuit of His good pleasure, not for any earthly return or any display of fortitude. The phrase "seeking the Face of their Lord" recurs throughout the Qur'an as the standard of sincerity:

> *"And feed, for love of Him, the needy, the orphan, and the captive."* (Qur'an 76:8)

> *"Those who spend their wealth seeking the Face of God."* (Qur'an 2:272)

Al-ʿAjamī and other commentators on this verse explain that the qualification governs not only the patience but everything else in the verse: the prayer, the spending, the repelling of evil — all of it is done seeking God's Face, for no selfish reason, from no pride, and with no desire for recognition. The Face of God is here the Qur'an's name for God Himself in His aspect of being sought — the goal of the believer's whole life, the one thing he is looking toward. The entire verse is thus held together by this single phrase: every act it names is an act of seeking.

**Prayer as the Standing Obligation**

Next comes *"perform the prayer"* (*aqāmū al-ṣalāh*). The verb *aqāma* — "to establish, to make stand" — is the Qur'an's fixed term for the performance of prayer, and the commentators note its precision: one does not merely "do" the prayer but *establishes* it, making it stand as a permanent pillar of the life rather than an occasional gesture. The Qur'an pairs patience and prayer together so often that the pairing has the force of a formula:

> *"Seek help in patience and prayer."* (Qur'an 2:45)

The logic of the pairing is that patience and prayer are the two great resources of the believer: patience for the endurance of what comes, prayer for the strength to endure it. Prayer is here listed not as an afterthought but as the central act around which the other qualities orbit — the regular, standing renewal of the bond with God that makes fidelity, joining, fear, and patience all possible.

**Spending Secretly and Openly**

The verse then names charity in both of its modes: *"and spend from that which We have provided them, secretly and openly"* (*wa-anfaqū mimmā razaqnāhum sirran wa-ʿalāniyah*). The phrase "from that which We have provided them" is itself a quiet theology: the wealth is not theirs but God's provision entrusted to them, and the spending is therefore not a favor but a rendering. The two adverbs — secretly and openly — cover the two situations in which charity is given, and the commentators explain the wisdom of each. Secret charity protects the recipient's dignity and purifies the giver's intention, removing every trace of display; open charity serves the social good, encouraging others and establishing the public norm of generosity. The Qur'an elsewhere praises both and explains their respective virtues:

> *"If you disclose your charity, that is good; but if you conceal it and give it to the poor, that is better for you."* (Qur'an 2:271)

The ideal believer, the verse suggests, practices both modes as occasion requires — sometimes hidden, sometimes open, always from the provision of God, always seeking His Face.

**Repelling Evil with Good**

The verse's most distinctive quality comes last: *"and who repel evil with good"* (*wa-yadraʾūna bi-al-ḥasanati al-sayyiʾah*). The verb *daraʾa* means to push back, to ward off, to repel — and the verse's claim is that the believer repels evil not with more evil but with good. Al-Ṭabarī explains the meaning: they repel the evil done to them by doing good in return. The wrong is answered by kindness, the insult by patience, the injury by benefit. This is not a counsel of passivity but a strategy of transformation — the deliberate reversal of the cycle of harm. The Qur'an develops the same teaching at length elsewhere:

> *"Repel by that which is better, and behold, the one between whom and thee there was enmity becomes as though he were a warm friend."* (Qur'an 41:34)

The logic of the verse is psychological and social at once. Evil answered by evil doubles the evil; evil answered by good can break the cycle, because the one who strikes expects a strike in return, and the unexpected good disarms the expectation. The believer's repelling of evil with good is therefore not weakness but a superior force — the force that operates on the adversary's conscience rather than his body, and that wins, when it wins, a whole person rather than a temporary advantage.

**"The Reward of the Abode"**

The verse closes the long sentence with the reward: *"For them there shall be the reward of the Abode"* — *ʿuqbā al-dār*, the final outcome of the Abode, the praiseworthy end of the Hereafter. The word *ʿuqbā* is the consequence, the sequel, the end to which a course of action leads — and the verse's point is that the qualities just listed are the course of action, and the Abode is where that course arrives. Ibn Juzayy and others explain that the Abode is Paradise, and that the "reward of the Abode" is the whole of what the next verses will describe: the Gardens of Eden, the greeting of peace, the company of the righteous. The reward is thus named before it is pictured — the sentence points forward to the description that is about to unfold.

**The Shape of the Whole**

Taken together, verses 20–22 present a complete and carefully ordered portrait of the believer. Fidelity to the covenant is the root; the joining of bonds and the fear of the Lord are the trunk; patience, prayer, charity, and the repelling of evil with good are the branches; and the reward of the Abode is the fruit. Every quality is qualified by sincerity — the seeking of the Face of the Lord — so that the whole portrait is governed by a single motive. The sūrah has spent several verses answering the disbelievers' objections; it now pauses to show, in concrete and lovable form, what the alternative to disbelief actually looks like — not an abstract "faith" but a complete human being, recognizable in any age, whose life is a quiet, steady movement toward the Abode.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:23

> **Gardens of Eden that they shall enter along with those who were righteous from among their fathers, their spouses, and their progeny; and angels shall enter upon them from every gate.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reward Named and Then Shown**

The previous verse promised "the reward of the Abode"; this verse now shows it. The reward is *jannātu ʿadn* — the Gardens of Eden, the gardens of perpetual residence — and the verse's first and most important point is the company in which the believer will enter them. The reward is not a solitary estate but a reunion: the believer enters the Gardens *"along with those who were righteous from among their fathers, their spouses, and their progeny"* (*wa-man ṣalaḥa min ābāʾihim wa-azwājihim wa-dhurriyyātihim*). Al-Ṭabarī explains the condition precisely: the fathers, spouses, and children who enter with them are those who believed and were righteous in this world — the family is not gathered by blood alone but by blood joined to faith.

**The Reunion: A Mercy for the Human Heart**

The mention of the family is one of the Qur'an's most tender provisions, and it deserves to be weighed. Throughout the sūrah, the believer has been described as one who joins what God commanded be joined — who maintains the bonds of kinship and refuses to sever them. Now, in the reward, the joining is repaid with joining: the bonds maintained in this world are not dissolved at its end but perfected. The verse answers a fear that lives in every believing heart — the fear of a Paradise that would be shadowed by the absence of loved ones who were left behind. The Qur'an's answer is that the righteous believer's family is not lost but gathered, provided the family too was righteous. The commentators note the tenderness of the provision: God, who knows the human heart's need for its own, has arranged that the reward includes the dearest of its companions.

**The Condition: Righteousness, Not Blood Alone**

The verse's precision must not be softened. The phrase *"those who were righteous from among"* — *man ṣalaḥa min* — is a partition: some of the fathers, spouses, and progeny enter, namely those who were righteous. The verse does not promise automatic salvation to the family of a believer regardless of their own state; it promises the reunion of those who shared not only a household but a way of life. The commentators are careful here, because the verse has sometimes been misread as a guarantee of salvation by family membership. Al-Ṭabarī states it plainly: the fathers, spouses, and offspring who enter are those who were righteous, for kinship alone saves no one. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same point with the story of Noah, whose own son was not saved by his father's ark:

> *"O Noah, truly he is not of thy family; truly it is a deed not righteous."* (Qur'an 11:46)

The mercy of the reunion is thus perfectly balanced by the justice of the condition: the family bond is honored, and the individual's responsibility is not dissolved in it.

**The Elevation of Rank: Joined Together in the High Places**

A further depth is added by a promise recorded in the Qur'an elsewhere and developed by the commentators: God will join the believing family members together in rank, elevating the lower to the higher so that the reunion is complete. The promise is given in 52:21:

> *"And those who believe and whose progeny follow them in faith, We shall join their progeny with them, and We shall not diminish anything of their deeds."* (Qur'an 52:21)

The commentators read this verse together with 13:23 as the Qur'an's fuller statement of the provision: the family is joined, the lesser rank is raised toward the greater, and no one's reward is diminished in the raising. The provision reveals the structure of divine generosity — God's mercy in the Hereafter is not a fixed quantity to be divided among the saved, but an abundance that increases to include, and the inclusion of a loved one costs no one else anything.

**The Angels Entering from Every Gate**

The verse's second image completes the first: *"and angels shall enter upon them from every gate"* (*wa-al-malāʾikatu yadkhulūna ʿalayhim min kulli bāb*). The angels — the same angels who were described in verse 13 as hymning God's praise in awe — now appear as the greeters of the Garden's inhabitants. The commentators explain the image's force. The angels enter from every gate, not from one direction, which signifies that honor and glad tidings arrive from every side at once; the believer in Paradise is surrounded by welcome, with no direction from which good does not approach. Al-Qurṭubī adds that the angels come bearing gifts and salutations from God, commissioned to honor the believers as hosts honor the guests they most esteem. The same image appears throughout the Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise:

> *"And those who feared their Lord shall be driven unto the Garden in companies, till, when they reach it and its gates are opened, its keepers shall say unto them, 'Peace be upon you; you have done well, so enter, to abide therein.'"* (Qur'an 39:73)

**The Gates as a Figure of Security**

The mention of gates deserves a further note, because the gate in the Qur'an's imagery is the symbol of security — the city that can be closed against its enemies, the house that admits only its own. The believer in the Garden is behind gates, and the angels pass through those gates freely, which is the image of perfect safety joined to perfect honor. The contrast with the disbeliever is implicit: where the believer is within the gates, welcomed, the disbeliever is outside, excluded. The verse's picture of Paradise is thus complete in two strokes — the family gathered within, the angels arriving from every gate — and the whole is a portrait of what the "reward of the Abode" actually feels like: belonging, reunion, honor, and safety, all at once and from every side.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:24

> **"Peace be upon you because you were patient." How excellent is the Ultimate Abode!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Greeting That Names the Cause**

The verse opens with the angels' salutation as they enter upon the believers: *"Peace be upon you because you were patient"* (*salāmun ʿalaykum bi-mā ṣabartum*). The greeting is the Qur'an's own *salām* — peace — but it is immediately attached to its cause: *bi-mā ṣabartum*, "because of what you patiently endured." The particle *bi-mā* makes the connection explicit and causal: the peace is the recompense of the patience. This is the verse's central teaching, and it gathers the whole sūrah's earlier description of the believer — verse 22 listed patience as the first quality of the pure-minded — into a single line. The patience practiced in the world is the price whose purchase is the peace of Paradise.

**The Patience That Is Named Here**

What patience is the verse referring to? The commentators answer by recalling the full span of what the believer endures. It is the patience in obeying God against the pull of desire — the daily discipline of prayer, fasting, restraint, and charity when the self would rather not. It is the patience in avoiding what God has forbidden, when the forbidden is near and easy. It is the patience under affliction — loss, illness, poverty, and the trials of the road. And it is, for the first believers especially, the patience under persecution: the mockery, the boycott, the injury, the temptation to abandon the faith in exchange for relief. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr read the verse's "patience" as covering all of these: the believer's whole life is a tissue of small and large endurances, and the greeting of Paradise names them all at once.

**Why Patience Is the Key That Fits the Lock**

The verse's singling out of patience deserves a moment of reflection, because it raises a question: why patience, of all the virtues? The answer is that patience is the virtue of time. Faith, hope, love, and courage all have their moments, but patience is the quality that holds the other virtues in place across the long stretch between the beginning of the road and its end. It is the virtue that keeps the believer praying when prayer is dry, keeps him truthful when the truth costs, keeps him on the road when the road is long. Without patience, every other virtue collapses at its first test; with it, every other virtue is preserved to its reward. The greeting of Paradise therefore names the one virtue that carried the believer through all the rest — the endurance that delivered the rest of his virtues safely to the door.

**The Patience of the Prophet's Own Household**

The commentators connect this verse to a concrete model of patience — the patience of the Prophet's ﷺ own household and Companions, who endured poverty, hunger, and hostility in the Makkan years and did not waver. The classical tradition preserves the memory of that endurance in vivid reports: the stones tied to the stomach against hunger, the years of the boycott, the insults borne in the streets. The verse's greeting, read against that history, is not an abstract promise but a personal address to people who had actually been patient — people for whom the peace of Paradise was the exact opposite of everything they had just endured. The verse thus teaches that the reward is shaped to the suffering: what they endured, the peace repays; the patience is not forgotten but named in the very greeting that welcomes them.

**"Peace Be Upon You": The Word That Undoes the World**

The word *salām* itself carries a depth that the verse's placement makes audible. *Salām* is peace, safety, soundness — the absence of harm and the presence of wellbeing. It is also one of the names of God, as the Qur'an states:

> *"He is God, other than whom there is no god, the King, the Holy, the Peace."* (Qur'an 59:23)

The greeting of Paradise is therefore the bestowal of the divine name upon the believer — the peace of God Himself settling upon those who enter. The word undoes everything the believer endured in the world: the conflict, the fear, the insecurity, the grief — all of it is answered by the one word that names their opposite. And the greeting is mutual: the Qur'an tells us that the believers themselves will also greet one another with peace, and that their final word in the Garden will be praise:

> *"Their greeting therein shall be 'Peace,' and the end of their supplication shall be 'Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds.'"* (Qur'an 10:10)

**"How Excellent Is the Ultimate Abode!"**

The verse closes with God's own exclamation: *"How excellent is the Ultimate Abode!"* (*fa-niʿma ʿuqbā al-dār*). The particle *fa-* — "so, then" — marks the exclamation as the conclusion of everything described: given the gardens, the reunion, the angels, the peace — how excellent, then, is this final home! The word *ʿuqbā al-dār* returns to the phrase that closed verse 22, "the reward of the Abode," so that the two verses bracket the description: verse 22 promised the reward, and verse 24 declares it excellent. The exclamation is the divine seal on the whole passage — God Himself pronouncing the verdict on the home He has prepared. The verse's structure is thus a complete arc: the promise, the picture, the greeting, and the verdict — and the reader, having followed the arc, is left with the exclamation ringing in his ears as the summary of everything Paradise means.

**The Verse's Practical Gift**

For the believer still on the road, this verse is a gift of perspective. It names in advance the exact exchange that will take place at the journey's end: patience now, peace then. The verse thus transforms the experience of endurance itself — the hardship that seemed meaningless is revealed to be the very material of the future greeting, the specific patience that the specific peace will repay. The one who endures with this verse in mind is not merely suffering; he is accumulating, in the Qur'an's own arithmetic, the currency that Paradise will recognize and name. And the verse's closing exclamation lets him hear, even now, the verdict that will be pronounced then — so that the road, however long, is walked with the sound of the destination already in the ears.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:25

> **As for those who break God's pact after accepting His covenant, and sever what God has commanded be joined, and work corruption upon the earth, it is they who shall have the curse, and theirs shall be the evil abode.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Portrait Reversed**

The sūrah has just drawn the portrait of the pure-minded believer in verses 20–24; this verse now draws its exact negative, point for point. Where the believer fulfills the pact, these break it; where the believer joins what God commanded joined, these sever it; where the believer repels evil with good, these work corruption upon the earth; where the believer's end is the Gardens of Eden and the greeting of peace, their end is the curse and the evil abode. The verse is constructed as a mirror, and the mirror's function is to make the choice unmistakable: the two portraits are not two cultures or two temperaments but two directions, and the sūrah has now shown both to the end.

**Breaking the Pact After Its Acceptance**

The verse's first clause is precise in its wording: *"those who break God's pact after accepting His covenant"* (*alladhīna yanquḍūna ʿahda Allāhi min baʿdi mīthāqihi*). The phrase "after accepting His covenant" — *min baʿdi mīthāqihi* — is the crucial addition. These are not people who never knew the bond; they are people who entered it and then broke it. The verb *naqaḍa* means to untie, to unravel, to break a thread — and the commentators note that it is the exact opposite of the *īfāʾ* (fulfillment) with which verse 20 described the believer. The verse is thus describing a specific kind of corruption: the corruption of the one who knew and betrayed, whose guilt is measured not by ignorance but by the covenant he accepted and then dissolved. The Qur'an's treatment of covenant-breaking is consistent: the gravity lies not in never having pledged but in pledging and then unpledging, in knowing the truth and then untying the knot that bound one to it.

**The Clause That Links This Verse to Its Twin**

The first part of this verse is repeated almost verbatim in 2:27:

> *"Those who break God's pact after accepting His covenant, and sever what God has commanded be joined, and work corruption upon the earth — it is they who are the losers."* (Qur'an 2:27)

The two verses are twins, and their repetition across the two chapters tells the reader that this is a fixed formula of the Qur'an's moral teaching. Both link the breaking of the covenant to the severing of bonds and both link the severing of bonds to corruption upon the earth. The chain is theologically exact: the covenant with God is the root, the bonds among people are the branches, and the corruption of the earth is the fruit. Break the root, and the branches fall; let the branches fall, and the earth is corrupted. The verse is the Qur'an's most compact account of how moral ruin propagates.

**Working Corruption Upon the Earth**

The phrase *"work corruption upon the earth"* (*yufsidūna fī al-arḍ*) is one of the Qur'an's great moral categories, and it deserves a fuller treatment than a passing mention. The root *f-s-d* means to spoil, to ruin, to disorder — the opposite of *ṣ-l-ḥ*, to set right. The Qur'an uses "corruption upon the earth" to name the entire spectrum of human wrongdoing in its social dimension: the severing of kinship, the shedding of blood, the oppression of the weak, the violation of rights, the spreading of injustice, and the destruction of the order God established. The commentators note that the Qur'an often links corruption specifically to violence and to the disruption of the social fabric:

> *"And when he turns away, he strives upon the earth to work corruption therein, and to destroy tillage and offspring. And God loves not corruption."* (Qur'an 2:205)

The category is thus not merely "sin" in the private sense but the outward, public damage that sin does when it is institutionalized. The covenant-breaker is a corruptor not only of himself but of the world, because his untied knot becomes a frayed edge in the whole fabric of society.

**"It Is They Who Shall Have the Curse"**

The verse then pronounces the verdict: *"it is they who shall have the curse"* (*ulāʾika lahum al-laʿnah*). The word *laʿnah* — curse — is the Qur'an's term for expulsion from God's mercy, and the commentators are careful to explain it. Al-Ṭūsī and others explain that the curse in the Qur'an's usage is distance from God's mercy — the state of being cut off from the mercy that the believer lives within. It is the exact opposite of the *salām* that greeted the believers in the previous verse: where the believers received peace and nearness, the covenant-breakers receive the curse and distance. Ibn ʿAjībah adds the spiritual dimension: the curse is distance from God's presence, and the one who is cursed is veiled from his Lord.

**"The Evil Abode"**

The verse closes: *"and theirs shall be the evil abode"* (*wa-lahum sūʾ al-dār*). The phrase balances the believer's "reward of the Abode" and "Ultimate Abode" from verses 22 and 24. The *dār* — the abode, the home — is the same word, but the outcomes are opposite: the believer's abode is the Garden and the greeting of peace; the covenant-breaker's abode is evil. Al-Zamakhsharī records two readings of "the evil abode": it can refer to the evil outcome in this world — the ruin and affliction that corruption brings upon itself — or to the punishment of Hell in the Hereafter. Ibn ʿAjībah adds the third, spiritual reading already noted: the evil abode is the sorrow of being veiled from God, the grief of remaining behind the door of His presence while others enter. All three readings are true at their own level, and the verse's structure holds them together: the covenant-breaker's home, in this world and the next, is the ruin he himself built.

**The Mirror's Final Lesson**

Read against the verses that preceded it, this verse completes the sūrah's great antithesis, and its lesson is the lesson of the whole passage: the difference between the believer and the covenant-breaker is not a difference in knowledge but a difference in fidelity. Both know the pact; one keeps it and one breaks it. Both know the bonds; one joins them and one severs them. Both know the world; one sets it right and one corrupts it. The verse strips away every excuse — these people accepted the covenant, and then they broke it — and leaves the reader facing the one question the whole portrait has been assembling: which of the two mirrors is his own?

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:26

> **God outspreads and straitens provision for whomsoever He will. They rejoice in the life of the world; yet compared to the Hereafter, the life of the world is but [fleeting] enjoyment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Economy of Provision**

The verse opens with a declaration that the Qur'an repeats in many forms: *"God outspreads and straitens provision for whomsoever He will"* (*Allāhu yabsuṭu al-rizqa li-man yashāʾu wa-yaqdiru*). The two verbs form the Qur'an's standard pair for the divine management of wealth: *basaṭa*, to spread out, to extend generously; and *qadara*, to measure out narrowly, to straiten. Between them they cover the whole range of human economic conditions, and the verse's claim is that both conditions are in God's hand. The same formula recurs throughout the Qur'an:

> *"God outspreads provision for whomsoever He will, and straitens."* (Qur'an 30:37)

> *"God outspreads provision for whomsoever He will of His servants, and straitens it for him. Truly God is Knower of all things."* (Qur'an 29:62)

**The Wisdom Behind the Widening and the Narrowing**

Why does God widen provision for some and narrow it for others? The commentators answer that the difference is not arbitrary but purposeful, and they draw the purposes from the Qur'an's own teaching. Wealth is a test of gratitude and of the use to which abundance is put; poverty is a test of patience and of trust. The one who is given much is being tested by the much; the one who is given little is being tested by the little. The Qur'an states the principle directly:

> *"And He it is Who made you vicegerents upon the earth and raised some of you above others in degrees, that He may try you in what He has given you."* (Qur'an 6:165)

Provision is thus not a measure of God's favor in any simple sense, nor is poverty a measure of His displeasure; both are distributions within a test, and the test is passed or failed not by the amount received but by the response to it. Al-Rāzī develops this at length: the widening and straitening are both acts of wisdom, because each human soul is best tested — and best perfected — by the particular measure assigned to it.

**The Correction of a Persistent Error**

This verse, and the Qur'an's treatment of provision generally, corrects an error that has accompanied wealth in every age: the assumption that material abundance is the sign of divine approval and material scarcity the sign of divine rejection. The Makkans themselves held some version of this error, measuring the truth of a claim by the wealth of the claimant, and the Qur'an dismantles it repeatedly. The error survives into the modern world in the form of the prosperity gospel — the teaching, foreign to the Qur'an, that faith is rewarded with riches — and the verse answers it in a single line: provision is widened and narrowed *for whomsoever He will*, according to wisdom, not according to the recipient's virtue. The believer is thus freed from measuring himself or others by the size of a provision, and from the despair that such measuring produces.

**"They Rejoice in the Life of the World"**

The verse then turns to the disbelievers' response to provision: *"They rejoice in the life of the world"* (*wa-fariḥū bi-al-ḥayāt al-dunyā*). The verb *fariḥa* — to rejoice, to exult — is here used with a note of rebuke, and the commentators explain why. The rejoicing is not in the Giver but in the gift; it is the exultation of the one who takes the provision as his own achievement and the present world as his final home. The Qur'an contrasts this rejoicing with the rejoicing of the believers, whose joy is in God's bounty and in the Hereafter, and it elsewhere records the disbelievers' characteristic boast in the same terms:

> *"And they exult in the life of this world, but the life of this world, compared to the Hereafter, is naught but a passing enjoyment."* (Qur'an 13:26; cf. 40:4; 28:76–82, the story of Korah)

**The Arithmetic of the Two Worlds**

The verse's final clause is its most quoted: *"yet compared to the Hereafter, the life of the world is but [fleeting] enjoyment"* (*wa-mā al-ḥayāt al-dunyā fī al-ākhirati illā matāʿ*). The word *matāʿ* is the key: it means enjoyment, utility, provision for the road — the small equipment a traveler carries for a journey, as distinct from the home at the journey's end. The verse is saying that the entire life of this world, compared to the Hereafter, is of the same order as a traveler's kit — useful, even necessary, but not the destination, and absurd to mistake for it. The Qur'an makes the comparison explicit in 29:64:

> *"The life of this world is naught but diversion and play; and surely the Abode of the Hereafter is life indeed, if they but knew."* (Qur'an 29:64)

The comparison is not a denigration of the world — the world is God's creation and the believer's workplace — but a correction of proportion. The error is not in using the *matāʿ*; it is in exulting in it as though the road were the home.

**The Saying of Abu al-Dardāʾ**

The classical tradition attaches to this theme a saying of the Companion Abu al-Dardāʾ (d. 32/652), preserved by al-Ghazzālī in his *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*:

> *"When you detest this world, you will come to love the next world; and you will abstain from this world according to the measure of your love for the next world."*

The saying captures the psychology of the verse with precision: the two loves are inversely proportioned, because they compete for the same heart. The one who loves the Hereafter detaches from the world; the one who loves the world cannot, by the same heart, love the Hereafter. The verse's remedy for the exultation it rebukes is therefore not contempt of the world but love of what is greater — the heart is not emptied but redirected.

**A Modern Application: The Giver and the Gift**

The verse's teaching remains the standing cure for a specific modern ailment: the habit of measuring a life by its provision. Its first clause insists that provision is apportioned by God, which kills both the pride of the wealthy (the provision is not his achievement) and the shame of the poor (the straitening is not his disgrace). Its second clause rebukes the exultation in the gift rather than the Giver. And its final clause restores the proportion: the whole of this life is a traveler's provision, and the traveler who exults in his kit has forgotten the journey and the home. The believer who holds these three clauses together can possess wealth without being possessed by it, and can lack wealth without being diminished by the lack — because his joy is attached to what the provision serves, not to the provision itself.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:27

> **Those who disbelieve say, "Why has some sign not been sent down upon him from his Lord?" Say, "Truly God leads astray whomsoever He will and guides to Himself whosoever turns in repentance—"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demand Repeated and Reframed**

The disbelievers' demand for a sign, already answered in verse 7, now returns — and this time the answer is different. In verse 7 the Prophet was told, "Thou art but a warner"; here he is told to answer with a statement about God's guidance and misguidance: *"Truly God leads astray whomsoever He will and guides to Himself whosoever turns in repentance."* The shift is significant. The first answer defined the Prophet's role; the second answer defines the real cause of belief and disbelief. The demand for a sign is, in the end, a distraction from the actual issue — and the actual issue is the direction of the heart.

**The Question of Guidance and Misguidance**

The verse's central claim — that God leads astray whom He will and guides whom He will — is one of the most discussed topics in Islamic theology, and it must be stated with the precision the tradition gives it. The Qur'an attributes both guidance and misguidance to God, but never in a way that cancels human responsibility; rather, the two are the divine response to the human being's own direction. The commentators, led by al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr, explain the relationship in a single principle: God guides the one who turns toward Him, and He leaves astray the one who turns away. The "whomsoever He will" of the verse is not an arbitrary selection but the will that answers the prior choice of the creature — God's guidance is His response to the seeker, and His leaving astray is His response to the refuser. The very next clause of the verse makes this explicit, for it describes the guided not as "whomsoever He will" in the abstract but as *"whosoever turns in repentance"* (*man anāba*) — the one who turns, returns, and seeks is the one whom God guides to Himself.

**The Grammar of Return**

The word *anāba* — "turns in repentance" — is from the root *n-w-b*, which carries the sense of returning, of coming back to a place one has left. The commentators, including al-Māwardī, explain its full meaning: the one who *anāba* is the one who believes, repents of his sins, and returns to God with his heart — the one who, having drifted, turns back. The word is the key that unlocks the verse, because it names the human side of the divine guidance: God guides to Himself the one who returns to Him. The two movements — the creature's return and the Creator's guidance — are here bound into one, and this is the Qur'an's consistent teaching on the matter:

> *"And whosoever believes in God, He guides his heart."* (Qur'an 64:11)

The believer is not passive in his own guidance; his turning is real, and God's guidance is the response that makes the turning succeed.

**The Two Directions of the Heart**

The verse's logic can be stated plainly by distinguishing the two directions a heart can take. A heart that turns toward God — that seeks, that asks, that repents, that wants the truth — is met by God's guidance, which strengthens the turning and brings it home. A heart that turns away — that refuses, that mocks, that demands signs as a way of not committing — is met by God's leaving it astray, which is not an added evil but the withdrawal of the guidance that was refused. The Qur'an states both sides with perfect symmetry, and the symmetry is the answer to the demand for signs. The disbelievers asked for a sign as though belief depended on the arrival of more evidence; the verse replies that belief and disbelief depend on the direction of the heart, and that the heart which has turned away would not be moved by a thousand signs, while the heart that turns is guided without a single additional miracle.

**The Deeper Point About Signs**

This verse, read together with verse 7, completes the Qur'an's teaching on signs. The disbelievers asked why no sign had been sent down; the answer is twofold. First, the signs already sent — the Qur'an, the cosmos, the prophets — are sufficient, and the demand for more is evasion. Second, and more profoundly, signs do not produce faith in a heart that has chosen disbelief, because faith is not forced by evidence but accepted by a heart that turns. The Qur'an makes this second point in its starkest form:

> *"Even if We sent down the angels unto them, and the dead spoke to them, and We gathered all things in front of them, they would still not believe, unless God wills."* (Qur'an 6:111)

The verse thus relocates the entire question of belief from the external to the internal: the decisive factor is not the sign outside but the turning within. This is not to disparage evidence — the Qur'an is full of evidence — but to locate the moment of belief where it actually occurs, in the will's consent to the truth that the intellect has already seen.

**The Theological Balance**

The verse is a standing lesson in theological balance, and it repays careful statement. On one side, it must never be read as making God the author of misguidance in a way that excuses the misguided, for the verse itself ties the guidance to "whosoever turns in repentance," which is a human act. On the other side, it must never be read as making the human being independent of God, for the verse begins by attributing both guidance and misguidance to God. The tradition's synthesis, held by the mainstream from al-Ṭabarī through Ibn Taymiyyah, is that both are true at once: the turning is genuinely the creature's, and the guidance is genuinely God's; the refusal is genuinely the creature's, and the leaving astray is genuinely God's. The two truths are not contradictions but two descriptions of the same relationship from its two sides — the relationship between a Lord who guides and a servant who turns.

**A Practical Reading**

For the reader, the verse's practical application is immediate and hopeful. It locates the beginning of guidance not in a special sign but in the first motion of the heart toward God — the first honest question, the first repentance, the first turning. The verse promises that God guides to Himself the one who turns, which means that no sincere turning is ever wasted, and no seeker is ever refused the guidance he has begun to seek. The demand for signs is answered, in the end, not by a new miracle but by an invitation: turn, and the guidance will come — because the One to whom you turn is the One who guides.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:28

> **those who believe and whose hearts are at peace in the remembrance of God. Are not hearts at peace in the remembrance of God?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse of the Heart at Peace**

This verse — the continuation of verse 27's description of those who turn in repentance — is one of the most beloved verses of the entire Qur'an, and it has nourished the Islamic spiritual tradition from its earliest days to the present. Its claim is simple and total: the hearts of the believers are at peace in the remembrance of God, and this peace is found nowhere else. The verse is constructed as a statement followed by a rhetorical question that repeats and seals the statement: *"Are not hearts at peace in the remembrance of God?"* (*alā bi-dhikri Allāhi taṭmaʾinnu al-qulūb*). The particle *alā* — "is it not so? behold!" — is the Qur'an's summons to assent, the form it uses when the truth being stated is so certain that the listener is expected to affirm it himself.

**The Word Taṭmaʾinnu: The Vocabulary of Stillness**

The verb *taṭmaʾinnu* is the heart of the verse, and its etymology rewards attention. It comes from the root *ṭ-m-ʾ-n*, which the lexicographers gloss as quietness, stillness, and settledness after movement — the state of the water when its surface has gone still, of the ground when it has become firm underfoot, of the person whose agitation has subsided into rest. The word describes not a loud joy but a deep quiet — the peace of arrival, the calm of the one who has stopped searching because he has found. The verse thus claims for the remembrance of God the exact experience that human beings spend their lives pursuing in a thousand other places: the settling of the restless heart.

**The Stages of the Soul**

The commentators locate this verse within the Qur'an's own map of the soul's development, a map drawn across three verses. The soul begins as the *al-nafs al-ammārah bi-al-sūʾ*, "the soul that commands to evil" (12:53); it progresses to the *al-nafs al-lawwāmah*, "the blaming soul" (75:2), the soul that has begun to reproach itself; and it reaches its perfection as the *al-nafs al-muṭmaʾinnah*, "the soul at peace" (89:27):

> *"O thou soul at peace, return unto thy Lord, content, contenting."* (Qur'an 89:27–28)

The adjective *muṭmaʾinnah* in 89:27 is the exact cognate of the verb in 13:28, and the two verses together form the spiritual tradition's most cited pair: the soul reaches its peace through the remembrance of God, and the soul at peace is the soul that is called home. The heart at peace in this verse is therefore not a mood but a station — the highest stage of the soul's journey, reached when the remembrance of God has stilled every inner agitation.

**What Remembrance Is: Dhikr as the Verse's Subject**

The word *dhikr* — "remembrance" — names the practice that this verse made central to Islamic spirituality: *dhikr Allāh*, the invocation and remembrance of God. The commentators explain that *dhikr* includes the tongue's repetition of God's names and praises, the heart's constant awareness of God, and the mind's reflection upon His signs — all three, together, constituting the "remembrance" that stills the heart. Al-Bayḍāwī explains the causal link: the heart is agitated by the world's anxieties and by its own fears and desires, and the remembrance of God is the one object of thought that is greater than all of them, so that a heart filled with the remembrance of God has no room left for the agitation. The peace is not the absence of thought but the presence of a greater one.

**The Mirror of the Heart**

The tradition developed around this verse a rich body of imagery, of which the most famous is the mirror. The heart, the Sufis teach, is like a mirror: it reflects the truth when it is polished, and it fails to reflect when it is tarnished. The tarnish is the forgetfulness of God — the rust of heedlessness that accumulates over a life of distraction — and the remembrance of God is the polish. The Qur'an speaks of this rust directly:

> *"Nay! But that which they used to earn has covered their hearts with rust."* (Qur'an 83:14)

The Prophet ﷺ supplied the complementary image in a saying transmitted in the collections:

> *"For everything there is a polish, and the polish of the heart is the remembrance of God."*

The mirror analogy explains the verse's psychology with precision: a grimy mirror reflects a distorted image, a polished one reflects truly; and the heart, polished by remembrance, reflects the truth — reflects, the spiritual tradition adds, the Reality itself, so that a heart fully burnished becomes, in the famous ḥadīth qudsī, the place that can contain what the heavens and earth cannot:

> *"The heavens and the earth cannot contain Me, but the heart of My faithful servant can contain Me."*

**The Prophet's Words on Remembrance**

The verse's place in the tradition is sealed by the sayings of the Prophet ﷺ that the commentators and the spiritual masters gathered around it. Among the most famous is the exchange in which a Companion asked for something to hold onto among the many duties of the religion:

> *"O Messenger of God, the laws of Islam are many for me; tell me something I can hold fast to." He replied, "Let thy tongue remain moist with the remembrance of God."* (al-Tirmidhī 3375; Ibn Mājah 3793)

And the saying in which the Prophet ﷺ ranked remembrance above even the most visible acts of virtue:

> *"Shall I not inform you of the best of your deeds, the purest of them in the sight of your Lord, the highest of them in rank, and better for you than spending gold and silver and better than meeting your enemy and striking their necks and them striking yours?" They said, "Of course." He said, "The remembrance of God."* (al-Tirmidhī 3377; Ibn Mājah 3790)

These sayings are the Prophet's own commentary on this verse: the remembrance of God is not one duty among many but the duty that nourishes all the rest, the constant that makes the others possible.

**The Two Remembrances**

The verse has also been read, in the spiritual tradition, at a second and deeper level. The obvious reading is that the heart is at peace because the servant remembers God. But al-Maybudī records a further gloss: there are hearts at peace because of *their* remembrance of God, and there are hearts at peace because of *God's* remembrance of them — and the latter is the greater, for the Qur'an says:

> *"So remember Me, and I shall remember you."* (Qur'an 2:152)

> *"And the remembrance of God is surely greater."* (Qur'an 29:45)

The two remembrances are the two movements of one relationship: the servant's remembrance ascends, and God's remembrance — His attention, His care, His nearness — descends upon the heart. The verse's peace is the peace of this mutual remembrance, the rest of the one who is both remembering and remembered.

**The Elixir of the Soul**

Al-Rāzī preserves a final image from the tradition that gathers the whole teaching into one: the remembrance of God is an elixir that turns the base metal of the soul into gold. The image is drawn from alchemy, the ancient art of transmutation, and its point is that remembrance does not merely comfort the soul but transforms it — gradually, through constancy, changing a dark and turbulent heart into one that is luminous and still. The soul is not abandoned in its base state; it is transmuted. The verse's question — *"Are not hearts at peace in the remembrance of God?"* — is therefore not only a description but an invitation and a promise: the peace is real, the means is known, and the one who practices the remembrance will, by the verse's own guarantee, find the stillness it describes.

**The Verse in Modern Life**

Finally, the verse speaks with unusual directness to the modern condition, because the modern condition is, in large part, a condition of agitation. The restless heart — anxious, distracted, never still, pursued by noise — is the verse's own subject, and its diagnosis is as precise as its remedy. The heart's agitation is not cured by more stimulation but by the one object that is greater than the agitation; the peace that modern life pursues through distraction is, the verse says, available only through remembrance. The verse's claim is testable, and it invites the test: the one who actually practices the remembrance of God — not as a theory but as a discipline — will find, at the level of experience, what the verse promises. This is the verse's final gift: it does not merely describe the peace of the heart; it hands the reader the means, names the object, and then asks, with the Qur'an's most tender insistence, whether the heart is not, indeed, at peace in the remembrance of God.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:29

> **Those who believe and perform righteous deeds, theirs is blessedness and a beautiful return.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Promise Attached to Faith and Deeds**

This verse gathers the sūrah's description of the believers into a single promise, using the Qur'an's most characteristic formula: *"those who believe and perform righteous deeds"* (*alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt*). The formula appears throughout the Qur'an, always as the condition of every good outcome, and its structure is itself a teaching. Faith and deeds are named together, never separated: belief is the root, and righteous action is its fruit, and neither is complete without the other. The commentators, including Ibn Kathīr, note that this verse's believers are the same ones described in verse 27 as "whosoever turns in repentance" and in verse 28 as those "whose hearts are at peace in the remembrance of God" — the verse is the conclusion of the chain that began with the turning and passed through the stillness.

**The Word Ṭūbā: Blessedness**

The verse's reward is given in a single word that has delighted the commentators for its richness: *ṭūbā* — "blessedness." The word is the feminine of *aṭyab*, "best, most pleasant," and it is used in Arabic as a noun of felicitation — a blessing invoked upon someone, meaning "goodness be to him." The commentators record several interpretations of what *ṭūbā* denotes, and they are best read as expanding circles of one meaning. Al-Ṭūsī transmits that it refers to a good life in this world; others transmit that it refers to Paradise itself; and still others — including al-Rāzī and al-Ṭūsī — transmit the famous tradition that *ṭūbā* is a tree in Paradise, the tree whose shade and fruit the believers enjoy, as reported from the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"Ṭūbā is a tree in Paradise, the distance of a hundred years' journey; the garments of the people of Paradise are cut from its leaves."* (transmitted in the commentaries and the collections on the descriptions of Paradise)

The several readings converge on a single reality: *ṭūbā* is the total blessedness of the believer — the good life now, the Garden then, and within the Garden, the tree that is itself a summary of every delight. The word is chosen precisely because it can hold all of these at once, a single syllable containing the whole of the reward.

**The Beautiful Return**

The verse's second promise is *"a beautiful return"* (*wa-ḥusnu maʾāb*). The word *maʾāb* is from the root *ʾ-w-b*, to return — the same root as *inābah*, the turning of verse 27 — and it names the believer's homecoming to God. The phrase "beautiful return" recurs in the Qur'an as the summary of the believer's end:

> *"And for those who do good, there is the best, and more besides."* (Qur'an 10:26)

The commentators explain the phrase's two dimensions. On the one hand, the return is beautiful in its destination — the believer returns to God and is received with mercy and honor, not with the reckoning of the neglectful. On the other, the return is beautiful in its manner — the believer dies in a state of faith, departs in peace, and arrives to the greeting that verse 24 described. The "beautiful return" is thus the entire arc of the believer's end: the death that is peaceful, the arrival that is welcomed, and the home that is the Garden.

**The Correspondence of the Two Promises**

The verse's two promises — *ṭūbā* and *ḥusn al-maʾāb* — correspond to the two words of the verse's opening formula. Faith is answered by *ṭūbā*, the blessedness of the inner life — the peace, the good life, the garden-state that begins in the heart before it appears in the Abode. Righteous deeds are answered by the beautiful return — the outward journey's end, the homecoming earned by the walking. The verse thus completes the sūrah's portrait of the believer by attaching to each element of the portrait its matching reward: the one who believed has blessedness, and the one who acted righteously has the beautiful return. The two are given together because they are one destiny seen from within and from the end.

**The Sūrah's Structural Contrast**

This verse also performs a structural function in the chapter that deserves notice. Verse 25 gave the covenant-breakers "the curse and the evil abode"; verse 29 gives the believers "blessedness and a beautiful return." The two verses are the chapter's moral poles, and between them the sūrah has laid out the full case. The contrast is exact down to the vocabulary: the curse against the blessedness, the evil abode against the beautiful return. The reader who has followed the chapter to this point has been shown, in parallel columns, the two possible endings of a human life, and the verse's function is to close the column of the believers with the same finality with which verse 25 closed the column of the covenant-breakers.

**A Reflection on the Verse's Economy**

For all its brevity, this verse is a complete theology of the believer's reward, and its economy is worth a final appreciation. In one sentence it names the condition (faith and righteous deeds), the inner reward (blessedness), and the outer reward (the beautiful return), and it does so with two of the richest words in the Qur'an's vocabulary of felicity. The verse teaches, in miniature, the whole relationship between effort and reward that the chapter has been unfolding: the effort is the turning, the remembrance, the patience, and the deeds; and the reward is the blessedness that begins now and the return that completes it. The believer who recites this verse carries in it both the promise and the program — what is given and what it is given for.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:30

> **Thus have We sent thee unto a community before whom other communities have passed away, that thou mayest recite unto them that which We have revealed unto thee; yet they disbelieve in the Compassionate. Say, "He is my Lord; there is no god but He. In Him do I trust and unto Him do I turn."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Messenger to the Last Community**

The verse opens by situating the Prophet ﷺ in the long line of divine messengers: *"Thus have We sent thee unto a community before whom other communities have passed away."* The commentators explain the phrase's meaning: this community — the Arabs of the Prophet's time, and through them the final community of mankind — is not the first to receive a messenger but the latest in a succession that began with the earliest generations. Al-Rāzī and al-Nasafī note that the verse deliberately connects the Prophet's mission to the missions before him: the same God who sent Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus has now sent Muhammad, and the community to which he is sent stands at the end of the line of communities that have "passed away." The verse is thus a statement of continuity — the message is not a novelty but the continuation of an ancient and unbroken address.

**The Task of Recitation**

The purpose of the sending is given in the next clause: *"that thou mayest recite unto them that which We have revealed unto thee."* The verb *tatluwa* — "recite" — is the Qur'an's word for the delivery of revelation, the reading aloud of what was sent down. Al-Ṭabarī explains the clause's plain sense: the Prophet's charge is to recite to his people what God has revealed to him — nothing more and nothing less. The recitation itself is the mission. The verse thus returns to the theme of verse 7: the Prophet is a conveyer, and his task is the faithful delivery of the message, not the manufacture of signs or the compulsion of belief. The sūrah will state this most explicitly in verse 40: *"thine is only to proclaim."*

**The Rejection of the Compassionate**

The verse then records the community's response: *"yet they disbelieve in the Compassionate"* (*wa-hum yakfurūna bi-al-Raḥmān*). The phrase is historically specific and theologically pointed. The commentators transmit the occasion: the Makkans, who had never used *al-Raḥmān* — "the Compassionate" — as a name of God, were scandalized when the Prophet called them to worship the Compassionate, and they asked derisively, "And who is the Compassionate?" The Qur'an records the objection:

> *"And when it is said unto them, 'Prostrate to the Compassionate,' they say, 'And who is the Compassionate? Shall we prostrate to that which thou commandest us?' And it increases them in aversion."* (Qur'an 25:60)

Another report, transmitted in connection with the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyah, recounts the same aversion in a political setting: when the treaty was being written with the words "In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful," the Makkan negotiator objected, "We do not recognize the Compassionate!" The verse's point is thus both historical and doctrinal: the disbelievers rejected the very name of God under which He had sent His messenger — a name that expresses the mercy that is the essence of the message. To disbelieve in the Compassionate is to disbelieve in mercy itself.

**The Prophet's Own Declaration**

The verse then turns to the Prophet's response, and it is a model of the believer's posture under rejection: *"Say, 'He is my Lord; there is no god but He. In Him do I trust and unto Him do I turn.'"* Ibn Kathīr explains each clause. "He is my Lord" — I affirm the lordship and divinity of the very One in whom you disbelieve. "There is no god but He" — the affirmation of oneness, the *shahādah* itself. "In Him do I trust" — in all my affairs, I rely upon Him alone. "Unto Him do I turn" — in repentance I return to Him, for this is His right, which no one else shares. The four clauses are the believer's complete confession: lordship, unity, trust, and return — the whole of faith compressed into a single breath.

**The Posture of the Unshaken Conveyer**

The verse's structure deserves attention, because it models something the Qur'an teaches throughout: the correct posture of the one who conveys truth in the face of rejection. The disbelievers reject the name of the Compassionate; the Prophet does not descend into argument or despair or retaliation; he simply declares his own faith more firmly. The rejection does not touch the message; it only clarifies the confessor's confession. The verse thus teaches the believer that the proper answer to the world's refusal is not the weakening of one's own certainty but its strengthening — the refusal of others becomes the occasion for the deepening of one's own trust and return. This is why the verse's four clauses are in the first person singular: "He is *my* Lord... *I* trust... *I* turn." What others do cannot be controlled; what I do is mine, and the verse hands the believer the words for it.

**The Universal Import**

Finally, the verse's opening — "a community before whom other communities have passed away" — carries a universal implication that the commentators draw out. If other communities have passed away and this community is the last in the line, then the message delivered to it is the final address, and its content — the oneness of the Compassionate — is the content of every address that came before. The disbelievers' rejection is thus not the rejection of a new teaching but the repetition of the old refusal that every community has offered its messenger in turn. The Prophet is not alone in his rejection; he stands in the company of Noah and Abraham and Moses and every warner, and the verse places him there deliberately, so that the one who conveys the truth in a resistant age may know that his resistance is nothing new, and that the confession he makes — "He is my Lord" — is the confession that has sustained every messenger before him.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:31

> **Were there a Quran whereby the mountains were set in motion, or the earth was cleft, or the dead were made to speak! Nay! Unto God belongs the affair altogether. Do not those who believe understand that if God had willed, He would have guided mankind all together? And those who disbelieve, calamity will never cease to befall them because of that which they have wrought, or to alight close to their abode until God's Promise comes. Truly God will not fail the tryst.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demand That Would Not Be Satisfied**

The verse opens with an exclamation whose grammar the commentators carefully unfold: *"Were there a Quran whereby the mountains were set in motion, or the earth was cleft, or the dead were made to speak!"* The construction is one of the Qur'an's most powerful rhetorical devices — an unfulfilled wish, an imagined condition, a hypothetical stated only to be dismissed. Al-Wāḥidī and others transmit the occasion: the disbelievers of Makkah, mocking the Prophet, said to him, "Ask your Lord to move these mountains for us, to cause rivers to gush from the ground, and to bring our dead ancestors back to life so that they may speak to us — and then we will believe." The verse answers their demand by imagining the very Quran they described — a Quran that moved mountains, cleft the earth, and raised the dead — and then dismissing the entire fantasy with a single word: *Nay!*

**The Reply of 6:111**

The verse's dismissal is the sūrah's contribution to a theme the Qur'an treats in full elsewhere, and the parallel passage in 6:111 states the principle with maximum clarity:

> *"Even if We sent down the angels unto them, and the dead spoke to them, and We gathered all things in front of them, they would still not believe, unless God wills."* (Qur'an 6:111)

The principle is that the problem is not the absence of signs but the state of the heart. A heart that has chosen disbelief would not be changed by a mountain's motion, because its refusal is not based on evidence and cannot be cured by more evidence. The verse's "Nay!" is thus not an argument but a diagnosis: the demand for a moving mountain is not the hunger of a seeker but the evasion of a refuser, and no quantity of miracles would satisfy a hunger that is not there.

**"Unto God Belongs the Affair Altogether"**

The dismissal is followed by the affirmation that grounds it: *"Unto God belongs the affair altogether"* (*li-Allāhi al-amru jamīʿan*). Ibn al-Jawzī explains the clause's force: the guidance of the Makkans — and, universally, the whole management of affairs — is in God's hand. If He wills that they believe, they will believe; if He does not, then no sign will benefit them. The clause is the sūrah's standing answer to every demand that the course of events be rearranged to suit the skeptic: the affair is not in the hands of the one who demands, nor even in the hands of the one who delivers, but in the hands of God alone. The verse thus restores the correct order of agency — human beings act and choose, but the outcome and the guidance are God's.

**The Question to the Believers**

The verse then turns to its own audience with a question that is really a teaching: *"Do not those who believe understand that if God had willed, He would have guided mankind all together?"* The question accomplishes two things at once. First, it explains why universal belief has not happened: not because God is unable, but because He has not willed it — He has willed, instead, a world in which human beings choose, and in which belief is therefore a genuine act rather than a compulsion. Second, it consoles the believers, who grieved over the persistence of disbelief: the question tells them that the stubbornness of the disbelievers is not a failure of the mission but a feature of the world's design, the necessary condition of the freedom that makes faith meaningful. The question's logic is the logic of free will itself: if all were compelled to believe, belief would be worthless; therefore God has left the choice real, and the reality of the choice entails the reality of its refusal.

**The Relationship Between God's Will and Human Guidance**

This verse is a fixed reference point in the Qur'an's teaching on the relationship between divine will and human guidance, and it must be stated precisely. The verse says two things that must be held together. On the one hand, it says that if God had willed, He would have guided everyone — which means that guidance, in its final execution, is God's act. On the other hand, the verse's whole context is an appeal to human responsibility — the disbelievers are blamed for their disbelief, which would be meaningless if their state were nothing but God's compulsion. The tradition's synthesis, worked out by al-Rāzī, Ibn Taymiyyah, and the mainstream commentators, is that God's will does not override human will but encompasses it: God has willed that human beings choose, and His guidance and misguidance operate *through* the choice, rewarding the turning and abandoning the refusal, as verse 27 already taught. The verse is thus not a denial of freedom but a description of its limits — the choice is real, and the outcome remains God's.

**The Calamity That Will Not Cease**

The verse then turns to the disbelievers with a threat whose imagery is drawn from siege warfare: *"And those who disbelieve, calamity will never cease to befall them because of that which they have wrought, or to alight close to their abode until God's Promise comes."* The word *qāriʿah* — "calamity, striking blow" — is the Qur'an's term for a disaster that strikes suddenly, and the verse promises that such blows will not cease: they will strike the disbelievers themselves — afflicting their persons, their children, and their property, as al-Qurṭubī explains — or they will strike down close to their homes, so that the terror of the near miss is theirs without the blow. The cause is named: *"because of that which they have wrought"* — the disbelief and its deeds. The calamity is not arbitrary; it is the harvest of the wrongdoing, the sūrah's earlier law of verse 11 now applied: they altered what was in themselves, and so God altered what was around them.

**"Until God's Promise Comes"**

The calamities continue, the verse says, *"until God's Promise comes"* — *ḥattā yaʾtiya waʿdu Allāh*. The commentators explain the Promise in two readings that are not contradictory. Many, including Ibn Kathīr, read it as the conquest of Makkah — the promise of victory that would come to the Prophet and overtake the disbelievers in this very world, ending the cycle of their calamities with their final defeat. Others read it as the Day of Judgment, the ultimate tryst at which every account is settled. The two readings converge, for the conquest was the fulfillment of one promise that pointed to the fulfillment of the greater one. The verse thus gives the disbelievers' history its complete shape: calamity upon calamity in this world, until the promise — first the conquest, then the judgment — closes the account.

**"Truly God Will Not Fail the Tryst"**

The verse closes with one of the Qur'an's most solemn affirmations: *"Truly God will not fail the tryst"* (*inna Allāha lā yukhlifu al-mīʿād*). The word *mīʿād* is the appointed time, the promise, the tryst — and the verse's claim is that God's appointment cannot be missed. The phrase recurs throughout the Qur'an as the seal upon its promises of judgment:

> *"And they ask thee to hasten the punishment. Truly God will not fail His tryst."* (Qur'an 29:53)

The affirmation is the verse's final answer to the mockers who began it: they demanded signs and hastened the punishment, and the verse ends by assuring them — and the believers — that the appointment is certain. The disbelievers' delay of belief and the believers' delay of vindication are both, in the end, a matter of the tryst, and the tryst is kept. The verse's structure is thus complete: the fantasy is dismissed, the principle is stated, the threat is pronounced, and the certainty is sealed — a full account of why the Quran does not need to move mountains, and of what will happen, instead, to those who demanded that it should.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:32

> **Certainly messengers were mocked before thee. But I granted respite to those who disbelieved; then I seized them. How, then, was My Retribution!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Consolation of Precedent**

The verse turns to the Prophet ﷺ with a consolation drawn from history: *"Certainly messengers were mocked before thee"* (*wa-la-qadi stuhziʾa bi-rusulin min qablika*). The emphatic *la-qad* — "certainly, indeed" — asserts the fact as beyond doubt, and the point is its very familiarity. The mockery the Prophet now faces is not a new calamity peculiar to his mission; it is the standing fate of every messenger, the tax that every bearer of truth has paid to every generation of scoffers. The Qur'an makes the point again and again, always for the same purpose — to steady the Prophet by placing him in a company:

> *"And certainly messengers before thee were mocked, but those who scoffed were encompassed by that which they had mocked."* (Qur'an 6:10)

The consolation is precise: mockery is not evidence against the message but evidence of its authenticity, for the false prophet is not mocked — he is ignored or flattered; it is the true one whom the world cannot ignore and will not accept, and so mocks.

**The Pattern of Respite and Seizure**

The verse's second clause gives the divine pattern that followed the mockery: *"But I granted respite to those who disbelieved; then I seized them."* The two verbs — *amlaytu*, "I granted respite, I gave them length," and *akhadhtu*, "I seized" — form the Qur'an's fixed account of how God deals with the mockers. The respite comes first, and its length is part of the test: the disbelievers are given time, prosperity, and every opportunity to turn, and their mockery is not answered blow for blow. Then, when the term is full, comes the seizure — sudden, complete, and final. The same pattern is stated in 22:44 with almost identical words:

> *"But I granted the disbelievers a respite, then I seized them. And how, then, was the change I wrought!"* (Qur'an 22:44)

The pattern's logic is the sūrah's own: the respite is mercy, and the seizure is justice; the interval between them is the space in which repentance remains possible, and its closure is the point at which the possibility ends.

**"How, Then, Was My Retribution!"**

The verse closes with the Qur'an's most characteristic exclamation of warning: *"How, then, was My Retribution!"* (*fa-kayfa kāna nakīr*). The word *nakīr* — from *nakara*, to disavow, to find something blameworthy — denotes the punishment that is inflicted in denunciation of the wrongdoing, the divine rejection made visible. The exclamation's force is not informational but rhetorical: it invites the listener to imagine, and to shudder at, what became of the nations that were seized. Al-Qurṭubī supplies the unspoken completion: the question means, "What do you think of what I did with them? The same shall I do with the idolaters among your people." The exclamation thus folds the past into the present: the retribution that overtook the mockers of old is not a closed chapter but a standing warning, and its "How!" is addressed as much to the Makkans of the Prophet's time as to any later reader.

**The Two Audiences of the Verse**

The verse, read carefully, addresses two audiences at once, and its art lies in speaking to both with a single sentence. To the Prophet ﷺ it is consolation: you are mocked as they were mocked, and your patience will be vindicated as theirs was. To the mockers it is warning: your mockery has a precedent, and the precedent has an ending — the respite will not last, the seizure will come, and then you will ask, with the generations before you, how the retribution was. The single verse thus does double work, steadying the messenger while unsettling the mocker, and the two effects reinforce each other: the more firmly the Prophet is consoled, the more certainly the mocker is warned, because both rest on the same unbroken record.

**The Mercy Hidden in the Warning**

Finally, it should be noted that the verse, severe as it sounds, is itself an act of mercy. The warning is issued *before* the seizure, not after; the mockers are being told, in advance, exactly what their mockery will cost, while the respite is still running and the door still open. The verse's "then I seized them" is a threat only to the one who persists; to the one who turns, it is the very warning that saves. This is the Qur'an's consistent method with the disbelievers throughout: the description of the retribution is delivered within the period of respite, as the last mercy offered to the one who might still accept it. The verse, read in this light, is not a sentence of doom but a plea — the final, urgent disclosure of the pattern, given while there is still time to break it.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:33

> **What of He Who attends to every soul in accordance with what it has earned? Yet they ascribe partners unto God. Say, "Name them! Or will you inform Him of something He does not know upon the earth, or are these vain words?" Nay! But their plotting has been made to seem fair unto those who disbelieve, and they have been turned from the way. And whomsoever God leads astray, no guide has he.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Implied Comparison**

The verse opens with a question whose comparison is left unstated, and Ibn Juzayy and the commentators supply it: *"What of He Who attends to every soul in accordance with what it has earned?"* The full form of the question is: is He Who watches over every soul and recompenses it according to its deeds — like those idols that some worship? The comparison is implied rather than explicit, but its force is unmistakable, because the sūrah has already dismantled the idols at length. The idols neither watch nor recompense; they neither know nor act; they have no relationship whatever to what a soul earns. The One Who "attends to every soul" — the verb *qāʾim*, "standing over, attending to," carrying the sense of vigilant supervision — is the One before whom every soul's ledger is open and every deed is weighed. The question thus repeats, in a single clause, the whole argument of verse 16: the difference between the true God and the false gods is the difference between supervision and nothingness.

**The Scandal of Partnership**

The verse then states the scandal in its simplest form: *"Yet they ascribe partners unto God"* (*wa-jaʿalū li-Allāhi shurakāʾ*). The word *shurakāʾ* — "partners, associates" — is the Qur'an's term for the idols and intermediaries the Makkans set alongside God, and the verse places the ascription in immediate contrast to the supervision just described. The logic is devastating: the One who stands over every soul, who will recompense every deed, is given partners who can neither watch nor recompense anything. The verse's "yet" — *wa-* — marks the absurdity: after all that has been established, they still associate.

**"Name Them!"**

The Prophet is then commanded to answer the ascription with a challenge: *"Say, 'Name them!'"* (*qul sammūhum*). The challenge is the Qur'an's standing device for exposing the emptiness of the partners, and the commentators explain its force. The idolaters are invited to name their gods, to specify who exactly these partners are — and the invitation is a trap, because the moment they name them, the names themselves reveal the poverty of what they name: al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt — carved stones, dead ancestors, invented titles with no reality behind them. The demand to name is the demand to give an account, and the partners cannot survive the account.

**The Threefold Alternative**

The verse then offers the idolaters' position in its only three possible forms, and dismantles each: *"Or will you inform Him of something He does not know upon the earth, or are these vain words?"* The first possibility is that the partners are real beings of whom God is unaware — a possibility the commentators dismiss at once, for God's knowledge encompasses everything, and to "inform" Him of something would be to claim that something exists outside His knowledge, which is impossible. The second possibility is that the partners are known to God but are real — but if they were real and known, they would be part of His creation and His servants, not His partners, and the sūrah has already established that nothing created can benefit or harm itself. The third possibility is the truth, and the verse names it: *"or are these vain words"* — *ẓāhirin min al-qawl* — words that are surface with no depth, claims with no referent, names with no named. Ibn Juzayy glosses the phrase: words with no inward meaning, resting on a false opinion. The idolaters' gods are, in the final analysis, merely sounds — and the verse has reduced the entire pantheon to this, its actual status.

**"Their Plotting Has Been Made to Seem Fair"**

The verse then turns from the argument to the explanation of why the argument fails: *"Nay! But their plotting has been made to seem fair unto those who disbelieve, and they have been turned from the way."* The word *makr* — "plotting" — is significant, and the commentators connect it to the sūrah's own verse 42: *"unto God belongs plotting altogether."* The idolaters' "plotting" is their scheming against the truth, their stratagems of evasion and justification, and the verse's diagnosis is that this plotting has been *zuyyina lahum* — made to seem fair to them. The passive construction is the Qur'an's fixed way of describing the self-deception of the disbeliever: their own wrongdoing appears to them as right, their own plotting as prudence, their own idols as wisdom. The theme recurs throughout the Qur'an:

> *"And thus We have made the deeds of every community to seem fair unto them."* (Qur'an 6:108)

The second clause completes the diagnosis: *"and they have been turned from the way"* (*wa-ṣuddū ʿan al-sabīl*) — their self-deception has become their exclusion, the fair-seeming plot has become the blocked road.

**"Whomsoever God Leads Astray, No Guide Has He"**

The verse closes with the sūrah's fixed formula for the final state: *"And whomsoever God leads astray, no guide has he"* (*wa-man yuḍlili Allāhu fa-mā lahu min hād*). The formula recurs across the Qur'an in several forms:

> *"Whomsoever God leads astray, thou wilt not find a way for him."* (Qur'an 4:88)

> *"Whomsoever He leads astray, thou wilt find no protector to lead him aright."* (Qur'an 18:17)

The formula states the finality of the misguidance that has been chosen and confirmed. But it must be read, as always, in the light of the verse's own logic: the leading astray is the sealing of a self-deception that the person himself authored. God leads astray the one whose plotting has been made fair to him and who has been turned from the way — the misguidance is the verdict upon the choice, not the cause that preceded it. The verse's last line is therefore not a fatalistic sigh but a solemn conclusion: the one who has reached this state has exhausted the remedies, and no guide remains, because the guide was refused while the refusal was still curable.

**The Verse's Complete Movement**

Read whole, the verse performs the sūrah's complete argument against idolatry in four movements: the contrast between the Supervisor and the partners; the challenge to name the partners; the reduction of the partners to vain words; and the diagnosis of why the vain words persist. It moves from theology to polemic to explanation, and it ends where every account of willful disbelief in the Qur'an ends — with the recognition that the misguidance is both chosen and, once chosen, final. The verse's lesson for the reader is the lesson of the whole sūrah: the truth stands fully evidenced, the alternatives stand fully exposed as words, and the only thing that remains is the direction in which the heart turns — toward the Supervisor of every soul, or toward the names that name nothing.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:34

> **Theirs shall be punishment in the life of this world, and surely the punishment of the Hereafter will be more wretched; they shall have none to shield them from God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Punishments**

This verse continues the description of those whom God has led astray, and it names their fate in both worlds: punishment in this life, and a punishment in the Hereafter that is more wretched still. The verse's structure is a deliberate escalation — first the lesser, then the greater — and its purpose is to remove the disbelievers' final illusion, the assumption that their worldly prosperity or their escape from immediate disaster means they have escaped altogether. The sūrah has already established the principle in verse 31, with its calamities that "never cease to befall" the disbelievers; this verse now states the principle's full scope.

**Punishment in the Life of This World**

The commentators explain what the worldly punishment consists of, and their list is deliberately broad. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, and al-Baghawī transmit that it refers to the variety of trials and afflictions that overtake the disbelievers in this world: death, enslavement, captivity, defeat in battle, illness, famine, and the ruin of their enterprises. The word *ʿadhāb* — "punishment" — is used here for the worldly afflictions not as random misfortunes but as the divinely appointed consequences of wrongdoing, the sūrah's own law of verse 11 applied to the disbelievers: they altered what was in themselves, and so their condition was altered against them. The Makkan disbelievers would see this punishment with their own eyes in the battles and the conquest that followed; but the verse's claim is general and applies to every age — the wrongdoing carries its recompense within the world itself, even before the greater recompense arrives.

**The Hereafter's Punishment Is More Wretched**

The verse's central clause is the comparison: *"and surely the punishment of the Hereafter will be more wretched"* (*wa-la-ʿadhābu al-ākhirati akhzā*). The word *akhzā* — "more wretched, more disgraceful" — is the comparative of *khizy*, which means shame and disgrace as much as pain. The comparison is not merely quantitative — more of the same — but qualitative: the Hereafter's punishment is *more disgraceful*, because it is the punishment of the exposed, the retribution delivered in the sight of all creation, with no concealment and no defense. The Qur'an uses the same comparative elsewhere with variations that together map the full superiority of the final punishment:

> *"And the punishment of the Hereafter is greater, if they but knew."* (Qur'an 68:33)

> *"And the punishment of the Hereafter is more severe and more lasting."* (Qur'an 20:127)

The three comparisons — greater, more lasting, more disgraceful — are three dimensions of the same truth: the worldly punishment is partial, temporary, and private; the final punishment is total, permanent, and public.

**"None to Shield Them from God"**

The verse closes with the reason the punishment cannot be escaped: *"they shall have none to shield them from God"* (*wa-mā lahum mina Allāhi min wāq*). The word *wāq* — "shielder, protector, one who wards off" — is the verse's key term, and the commentators explain its force: it is the one who would stand between the punished and the punishment, deflecting the blow. The verse's claim is that no such shield exists. The same word and the same claim recur in the sūrah and elsewhere:

> *"And they shall have none to shield them from God."* (Qur'an 13:37; 40:21)

The claim completes the sūrah's long dismantling of the idols. The disbelievers had taken protectors — the idols of verse 14, the partners of verse 33 — precisely in the hope that they would shield them. The verse now states the outcome: on the day the punishment comes, there is no shield at all, because the shields they had taken were never shields, and the only true Shield is the God from whom they seek protection against nothing. The verse thus closes the circle: the refuge they built was an illusion, and the illusion is now revealed in the moment when refuge matters.

**The Pedagogical Order of the Verse**

The verse's order deserves a final note, because it is a study in the Qur'an's method of warning. It does not begin with the Hereafter's punishment; it begins with the worldly punishment, the one the disbelievers can actually see and verify, and it uses it as the step toward the greater one. The method is the Qur'an's characteristic pedagogy: anchor the warning in the observable, then extend it to the unobservable. The disbeliever who has seen calamity strike in this world is given the key to the next world: if this is the lesser punishment, what must the greater be? The verse's comparison is thus a ladder, and its final clause — no shield from God — is the step at the top, the point at which the warning becomes total.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:35

> **The parable of the Garden that has been promised to the reverent: with rivers running below, its food everlasting, as is its shade. That is the ultimate end of those who were reverent, while the ultimate end of the disbelievers is the Fire!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Parable That Is Not Merely a Parable**

The verse opens with a word that has occasioned much commentary: *mathalu* — "the parable of, the likeness of." The commentators, including al-Zamakhsharī, explain its use here with precision. The word does not mean that the Garden is a mere figure or a poetic fiction; it means that what follows is a *description* — the likeness, the portrait, of the Garden as it has been promised, rendered in terms the human mind can grasp. The Garden itself is real; the description is a parable in the sense that all language about the unseen is a parable, translating a reality beyond experience into the images of experience. The Qur'an's method here is the same as everywhere: it describes the indescribable by the nearest and best of what the hearer knows, and then lets the hearer understand that the reality exceeds the image.

**The Garden of the Reverent**

The promise is addressed to *al-muttaqūn* — "the reverent, the God-fearing" — and the sūrah has been describing these people since verse 19: the pure-minded who fulfill the pact, join the bonds, fear their Lord, are patient, pray, spend, and repel evil with good. The verse now gives them their home, and its first feature is the Qur'an's most famous image of Paradise: *"with rivers running below"* (*tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār*). The image recurs throughout the Qur'an as the signature of the Garden, and its appeal is rooted in the experience of the first audience — a desert people for whom running water was the very substance of life and luxury. But the image's meaning transcends its origin: the rivers that run below the Garden's dwellings signify a life in which provision is constant, effortless, and abundant, the opposite of the scarcity and labor that mark the present world.

**Food That Never Ends**

The verse's second feature is *"its food everlasting"* (*ukuluhā dāʾim*) — the food of the Garden is perpetual, without interruption and without end. Al-Zamakhsharī connects this to the Qur'an's description of the Garden's fruits in 56:33:

> *"Neither out of reach, nor forbidden."* (Qur'an 56:33)

The two descriptions together make the point: the food of the Garden is not seasonal, not rationed, not subject to exhaustion, and not gated by any prohibition. In this world, every food has its season and its scarcity and its limit; in the Garden, the provision is *dāʾim* — enduring, the same yesterday and forever. The word answers a specific human anxiety, the anxiety of running out — the harvest that fails, the store that empties, the future that cannot be secured. The Garden's food is the end of that anxiety.

**Shade That Does Not Withdraw**

The verse's third feature is *"as is its shade"* (*wa-ẓilluhā*) — the shade is likewise everlasting. The image is again drawn from the experience of the first hearers: in the Arabian sun, shade is not a luxury but a survival, and the shade of this world is always temporary — it moves with the sun, shrinks at noon, and disappears at night. The Qur'an elsewhere makes exactly this contrast the point:

> *"Hast thou not considered thy Lord, how He extends the shade — and had He willed, He could have made it still — then We make the sun a guide upon it?"* (Qur'an 25:45)

The Garden's shade, by contrast, does not withdraw. It is *dāʾim* like the food, a shelter that does not end, a coolness that does not pass. The three features — rivers, food, shade — are thus a single promise stated three times: in the Garden, nothing runs dry, nothing runs out, and nothing passes away. The *dawām* — the permanence — is the essence of the promise.

**The Two Ultimate Ends**

The verse then states its conclusion in a single contrast that gathers the whole chapter: *"That is the ultimate end of those who were reverent, while the ultimate end of the disbelievers is the Fire!"* The word *ʿuqbā* — "ultimate end" — is the same word used in verse 22 for the believer's "reward of the Abode," and its repetition here closes the sūrah's long comparison. The reverent and the disbelievers have walked through the whole chapter in parallel — the fulfillers and the breakers, the joiners and the severers, the patient and the mocker — and the verse now shows where the two roads terminate: one in the Garden of permanence, the other in the Fire. The exclamation mark is not in the original Arabic, but the verse's final word — *al-nār*, "the Fire" — is itself a kind of exclamation, the single syllable in which the whole warning is compressed.

**The Structural Place of the Verse**

This verse is the chapter's hinge, and its position deserves notice. The sūrah has spent its first half arguing for the truth and the Hereafter; it now pauses, exactly halfway, to lay the two destinations side by side in their fullest and simplest form. Everything that follows — the treatment of the People of the Book, the judgment in Arabic, the consolation of the Prophet — will be an elaboration of this single contrast. The verse's function is to fix the picture in the reader's mind before the argument resumes: two roads, two ends, one Garden and one Fire, and the choice between them is the choice the entire chapter has been pressing. The reader who reaches this verse has been given, in three short features and one sharp contrast, the whole of what the Qur'an asks him to decide.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:36

> **And those to whom We have given the Book rejoice in that which has been sent down unto thee. And among the parties are those who reject some of it. Say, "I have only been commanded to worship God, and to not ascribe partners unto Him. Unto Him do I call and unto Him is my return."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Responses to the Revelation**

The verse introduces a new set of respondents to the Qur'an — the People of the Book, the possessors of earlier scripture — and divides them into two groups according to their response. The first group *"rejoice in that which has been sent down unto thee"* (*yafraḥūna bi-mā unzila ilayka*); the second — *"among the parties"* — *"reject some of it"* (*yunkira baʿḍahu*). The verse thus continues the sūrah's survey of mankind's responses to revelation, adding to the believers and the disbelievers the more complex case of those who accept part and reject part.

**Those Who Rejoice**

The commentators identify the rejoicing group with precision. Al-Ṭūsī explains that they are the Prophet's Companions and those who believed in him — and the verse's phrase "those to whom We have given the Book" is read, in this first instance, as referring to the believers who have received the Qur'an itself. But the phrase also extends to a specific group among the People of the Book: those Jews and Christians who recognized in the Qur'an the fulfillment of their own scriptures and accepted it — the group the Qur'an describes elsewhere in moving terms:

> *"And when they hear what has been sent down unto the Messenger, thou seest their eyes overflow with tears because of the truth they recognize. They say, 'Our Lord, we believe; so write us among the witnesses.'"* (Qur'an 5:83)

The word *yafraḥūna* — "rejoice" — is deliberately chosen, for the Qur'an has just rebuked those who "rejoice in the life of the world" (verse 26). The two rejoicings are contrasted point for point: the one rejoices in the provision, the other in the revelation; the one in what passes, the other in what endures. The believer's joy at the Qur'an is the Qur'an's own standard of true joy, and the commentators note that this rejoicing is itself a sign of faith — the heart that is not gladdened by revelation has not yet recognized it.

**Those Who Reject Some of It**

The second group is *"among the parties"* (*min al-aḥzāb*) — the factions, the sects — and Ibn Kathīr identifies them as the Jews and the Christians. Their distinctive error is partial acceptance: *"those who reject some of it."* The commentators explain the anatomy of this rejection with care. They accepted those parts of the Prophet's message that agreed with their own books and traditions — the stories of the prophets, the general call to God, some legal rulings — and they rejected what was specific to the new revelation: above all, the prophethood of Muhammad ﷺ himself, and the obligations that followed from it. The partial acceptance is, in the Qur'an's analysis, the subtlest form of rejection, because it dresses disbelief in the garment of belief. The sūrah will return to this theme in verse 37 with the warning against following caprice.

**The Logic of Total Acceptance**

Beneath the verse's description lies a principle the Qur'an states explicitly elsewhere: revelation must be accepted as a whole, because it comes from a single source. To accept part and reject part is to make oneself the judge of revelation rather than its recipient — to set one's own preference above God's address. The Qur'an denounces exactly this:

> *"Do you believe in part of the Book and disbelieve in part? And what is the recompense of those who do so among you, but disgrace in the life of this world, and on the Day of Resurrection they shall be consigned to the most severe punishment."* (Qur'an 2:85)

The verse's placement of the partial accepters alongside the outright disbelievers is thus deliberate: both, in the end, have refused the message, the one openly and the other by selective consent.

**The Prophet's Unchanging Commission**

The verse then gives the Prophet's answer, and it is the same confession he was given in verse 30: *"Say, 'I have only been commanded to worship God, and to not ascribe partners unto Him. Unto Him do I call and unto Him is my return.'"* The four clauses are the complete statement of the message in its irreducible core: worship of God alone, rejection of all partners, the call to God, and the return to God. The commentators note that this confession is placed here as the answer to *both* groups — to the rejoicing believers it is the confirmation of what they accepted; to the partial rejecters it is the definition of what they must accept. The Prophet's mission is reduced to this single, unchanging content, and no faction's partial acceptance or rejection can alter it.

**The Placement of the Verse in the Sūrah**

The verse's position is significant for the sūrah's overall argument. The chapter has moved from the idolaters to the People of the Book, and it now addresses the full spectrum of human response to revelation: the believers who accept all, the disbelievers who reject all, and the parties who accept part and reject part. The verse's confession — worship God, ascribe no partner, call to Him, return to Him — is the criterion that sorts all three groups. It is, in effect, the sūrah's summary of the entire message, placed at the point where the message is being delivered to its last and most complex audience. The next verse will draw the conclusion for the Prophet himself, with the warning that the message must be delivered whole, whatever the cost.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:37

> **Thus have We sent it down as a Judgment in Arabic. Yet if thou followest their caprices after the knowledge that has come to thee, thou shalt have no protector against God, and no defender.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Judgment in Arabic**

The verse opens with a description of the Qur'an that gathers two of its essential features into one phrase: *"Thus have We sent it down as a Judgment in Arabic"* (*ḥukman ʿarabiyyan*). The word *ḥukm* — "judgment" — is the first feature, and the commentators read it on two levels. Al-Qurṭubī transmits that it refers to the whole Qur'an in its function of judging between truth and falsehood: the Qur'an is the *ḥukm*, the decisive criterion, that separates right from wrong with finality. Others read it more narrowly as the legal rulings contained in the Qur'an — the *aḥkām* that govern the believer's life. Both readings are true, for the Qur'an judges in both senses: it judges between the claims of truth and falsehood, and it legislates the rulings by which the believer lives. The word *ʿarabiyyan* — "in Arabic" — is the second feature, and the verse's point is that this judgment has been sent down in the language of its first audience, so that they might understand it fully and have no excuse of incomprehension.

**The Wisdom of Revealing in the Language of the People**

The revelation's Arabic raises a question the commentators answer at length, and their answer is a principle of divine pedagogy. Why Arabic? Because, as 14:4 states:

> *"We have sent no messenger, save in the language of his people, that he might make clear unto them."* (Qur'an 14:4)

The message is addressed to human beings through human language, and a message delivered in an unknown tongue would be a message not delivered. God therefore chose Arabic because He chose an Arab messenger, and the Qur'an is Arabic because the Prophet's first people were Arab. The Qur'an celebrates this fact repeatedly — *"an Arabic Quran"* (12:2), *"a clear Arabic tongue"* (16:103) — and the commentators are careful to add what the Arabic does *not* mean: it does not mean that the Prophet was sent only to the Arabs. The Qur'an states the universality of the mission explicitly:

> *"And We sent thee not, save as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner to mankind entire."* (Qur'an 34:28)

The Arabic is the vehicle of a universal message, not the boundary of its audience — the language was chosen for the first hearers, and the message extends to all hearers thereafter.

**The Warning Against Caprice**

The verse then turns to its real subject with a warning addressed to the Prophet ﷺ but intended, as al-Qurṭubī notes, for his entire community: *"Yet if thou followest their caprices after the knowledge that has come to thee, thou shalt have no protector against God, and no defender."* The word *ahwāʾ* — "caprices" — is the plural of *hawā*, and it is the Qur'an's term for the untutored desire, the inclination that pulls without knowledge or restraint. The commentators supply the specific content: the "caprices" are the desires of the Makkan idolaters and the People of the Book that the Prophet be led to worship other than God, to turn to a direction other than the Kaʿbah, or to soften or alter the judgment in their favor. The warning's grammar is significant: it is conditional — *"if thou followest"* — and addressed to the Prophet himself, the most protected of men from error, in order to teach by the strongest possible example that the command binds everyone. If even the Prophet is warned against following caprice, then no one else may follow it with safety.

**"After the Knowledge That Has Come to Thee"**

The warning's decisive phrase is *"after the knowledge that has come to thee"* (*baʿda mā jāʾaka min al-ʿilm*). The commentators explain its force: the sin is not the absence of guidance but its rejection. The one who follows caprice *after* knowledge has come is not the ignorant wanderer but the knowing defector, and his guilt is measured by the knowledge he abandoned. The phrase recurs throughout the Qur'an as the fixed marker of this specific guilt:

> *"And if thou followest their caprices after the knowledge that has come to thee, thou shalt be among the wrongdoers."* (Qur'an 2:145)

The phrase also points to the psychological structure of the sin: caprice is not the absence of knowledge but its rival — the desire that contends against what the mind already knows. The Qur'an's remedy is therefore not more knowledge but the discipline that makes knowledge rule over desire, and the verse's warning is the boundary marker that keeps the two in their proper order.

**"No Protector Against God, and No Defender"**

The verse closes with the consequence: *"thou shalt have no protector against God, and no defender"* (*mā laka mina Allāhi min waliyyin wa-lā wāq*). The two words — *walī*, protector, and *wāq*, defender — return to the sūrah's earlier vocabulary, echoing verses 34 and 11. The point is the same made throughout the chapter: there is no shield against God, no patron who can stand between the disobedient and the consequence of his disobedience. The one who follows caprice after knowledge has put himself outside the protection he thought he was securing, and the verse states the result with finality: he will find no protector and no defender, because the only true Protector is the God whose command he abandoned.

**The Verse's Permanent Warning**

Read for its permanent teaching, the verse is one of the Qur'an's clearest statements of the intellectual and moral discipline that religion requires. Knowledge has come; caprice remains; and the entire religious life is the struggle to keep the first ruling over the second. The verse's warning is addressed, through the Prophet, to every reader: the one who lets desire overturn knowledge after knowledge has arrived has no refuge, because the refuge was in the knowledge, and it has been surrendered. The verse thus closes the sūrah's treatment of the People of the Book's partial acceptance with the diagnosis of its root cause — not ignorance but caprice — and with the warning that no faction's pressure, no desire's pull, and no fear of consequence justifies the following of *hawā* over *ḥukm*.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:38

> **Indeed We sent messengers before thee and appointed for them wives and progeny, and it is not for a messenger to bring a sign, save by God's Leave; and for every term there is a Book.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Humanity of the Messengers**

This verse answers one of the Makkans' standing objections to the Prophet's message: the objection that a true messenger should not be a mortal man with a family and human needs. The disbelievers said, in the Qur'an's own record:

> *"And they say, 'What ails this Messenger, who eats food and walks in the markets? Why is there not an angel sent down unto him to be a warner with him?'"* (Qur'an 25:7)

The verse's answer is a fact of sacred history: *"Indeed We sent messengers before thee and appointed for them wives and progeny."* Every messenger God sent was a human being, and the messengers had wives and children — they married, they fathered, they lived in households, they ate and walked and slept. Abraham, Moses, David, Solomon — all of them were men with families. The objection of the Makkans was thus not only wrong but historically illiterate: the very pattern they demanded — an angelic messenger without human needs — had never existed, and the messengers God actually sent had always been human. Al-Kāshānī and others note the verse's purpose: it is a rebuke of the objection, exposing it as a demand for something God had never promised and never done.

**Why the Messenger Had to Be Human**

Beneath the historical answer lies a principle the commentators draw out: the messenger *had* to be human, because the message is a model, and only a human being can model a human life. A message delivered by an angel could be heard but not imitated; an angel does not marry, does not raise children, does not struggle with the temptations and weariness of the flesh. The Qur'an's doctrine is that the messenger is the *uswah ḥasanah* — the beautiful pattern — and a pattern for human beings must itself be human:

> *"Indeed, in the Messenger of God you have a beautiful pattern for whosoever hopes for God and the Last Day, and remembers God much."* (Qur'an 33:21)

The verse's mention of wives and progeny is therefore not a concession but a proof: the Prophet's household life — his marriage, his fatherhood, his family's joys and griefs — is itself part of the message, the lived demonstration that the human condition is not an obstacle to prophethood but its very medium. The objection that he eats and walks in the markets is answered by the fact that eating and walking and marrying are precisely what a human being must be shown how to do for God.

**The Sign Comes Only by God's Leave**

The verse then returns to the sūrah's other standing objection — the demand for signs — with a principle stated verbatim in 40:78: *"and it is not for a messenger to bring a sign, save by God's Leave."* The principle completes verse 7's answer: the messenger is not the author of his signs. He does not produce miracles on demand, because the miracle is not his to produce; it comes by God's permission, at God's time, for God's purpose. The Makkans demanded signs *of the Prophet* as though he were a performer obliged to satisfy his audience, and the verse corrects the entire framework: the messenger conveys, and God alone decides what sign, if any, shall accompany the conveyance. The demand is thus misdirected at its root — it is addressed to the messenger when it should be referred to God.

**"For Every Term There Is a Book"**

The verse closes with a clause whose meaning the commentators unfold in two complementary directions: *"and for every term there is a Book"* (*li-kulli ajalin kitāb*). The first reading, transmitted by al-Māwardī and others, takes the clause as a statement of divine decree: for every matter God has determined a fixed term, and that term is recorded in a Book — the Preserved Tablet, in which all decrees are inscribed. The signs, the punishments, the promises — each has its appointed time, and the time is written. This reading ties the clause to the preceding principle: the sign comes by God's leave, and the leave is a decree with a term, and the term is in the Book.

The second reading, transmitted by al-Baghawī and al-Zamakhsharī, takes the clause as a statement of the universality of revelation: every age, every term of history, has had its Book — its scripture, its messenger, its revealed law. On this reading the verse completes the sūrah's theme of the continuity of prophecy: the messengers were many, each with his appointed term, and each term had its Book. The two readings converge on the sūrah's larger point: the Prophet's mission is not an anomaly but the latest installment in a single, ordered, written plan — a plan in which every term has its Book, and the Qur'an is the Book of the final term.

**The Verse's Consolation**

The verse's total effect is consolation, and it is worth stating plainly. To a Prophet besieged by objections — "he is only a man," "why does he not bring a sign?" — the verse answers with the complete framework that dissolves both objections at once. The messengers before him were men with families; the signs were never theirs to bring; and the whole course of prophecy has proceeded by a written plan in which every term has its Book. The Prophet is thereby placed in his true position: not the inventor of a new and suspect claim, but the latest bearer of the oldest and most consistent pattern in human history. His humanity is the pattern's very substance, and his signs are God's to send, and his Book is the term's appointed Book. The one who understands this verse is freed from both the embarrassment of the messenger's humanity and the anxiety of the demand for miracles — because both were answered before either was raised.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:39

> **God effaces what He will and establishes, and with Him is the Mother of the Book.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse of Effacement and Establishment**

This verse is one of the Qur'an's most discussed statements about the divine decree, and it has generated a whole literature of theological and legal reflection. Its claim is compressed into five words: *yamḥū Allāhu mā yashāʾu wa-yuthbit* — "God effaces what He will and establishes." The two verbs — *maḥā*, to efface, to blot out, to erase; and *athbata*, to establish, to fix, to confirm — describe a single divine activity seen from its two sides: the removal of what was and the fixing of what is. The verse's apparent paradox — that God both effaces and establishes — is the point, and the commentators have labored to state it precisely.

**Al-Rāzī's Explanation: The Rhythm of All Things**

Al-Rāzī gives the verse's broadest and most satisfying reading, and it deserves to be quoted in its substance. God effaces and establishes, he explains, in accordance with what the divine will has decreed: He brings things into existence at one moment and makes them nonexistent at another; He gives life at one moment and causes death at another; He bestows riches at one moment and causes poverty at another. The effacement and establishment are the very rhythm of created existence — nothing in the world is static, everything is in the hand of God, being called into being and being called back, being fixed and being erased, in a continuous divine administration. On this reading, the verse is the Qur'an's most concentrated statement of the world's complete dependence on God's ongoing will: the universe is not a finished artifact left to run, but a canvas on which God is, at every moment, both erasing and drawing.

**The Verse and the Question of Abrogation**

The verse's other major reading connects it to the question of *naskh* — the abrogation of one ruling by another within the Qur'an itself. On this reading, "God effaces what He will and establishes" means: God removes from the scripture what He wills and confirms in it what He wills, so that a ruling given at one time may be replaced by another at a later time, as the welfare of the community requires. The commentators note that this reading is supported by the verse's context, following as it does upon "for every term there is a Book" — the succession of Books and terms implies the succession of rulings within the Books. The principle of abrogation, properly understood, is a mercy: the Law is not a rigid cage but a living guidance, and God, who knows every age, adjusts the ruling to the age within the framework He has set. The verse is thus a proof that the divine address is responsive, not static.

**The Two Readings Reconciled**

The commentators are careful to note that the two readings — the cosmic and the legislative — are not rivals but levels of one truth. At the cosmic level, God effaces and establishes in the world of being: life and death, wealth and poverty, existence and nonexistence. At the legislative level, God effaces and establishes in the world of revelation: rulings and scriptures, commands and dispensations. Both are manifestations of the single divine attribute of free, wise administration; both teach the same lesson — that nothing in creation, whether a star or a statute, stands by its own power, but all stands by God's continuous will. The verse's two verbs thus embrace both the universe and the Qur'an, and the believer who reads them sees the same hand at work in both.

**The Mother of the Book**

The verse closes with its most famous phrase: *"and with Him is the Mother of the Book"* (*wa-ʿindahu ummu al-kitāb*). The *Umm al-Kitāb* — "the Mother of the Book" — is one of the Qur'an's names for the transcendent source of revelation, and the commentators identify it with several closely related realities. Al-Ṭabarī transmits that it is the fundamental source from which the Qur'an and all revealed books are transcribed — the archetypal Book in which all revelation is one. Others, including al-Baghawī, al-Kāshānī, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī, identify it with the Preserved Tablet, the *al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ* of 85:22:

> *"Nay, it is a glorious Quran, in a Preserved Tablet."* (Qur'an 85:21–22)

And still others, with al-Rāzī, identify it with God's own knowledge — the knowledge in which all things are eternally present, of which the written Books are only the temporal expressions. The three identifications are one reality seen at three depths: the Mother of the Book is the source, the Tablet, and the knowledge — the single origin from which all that is effaced and established, in world and scripture, proceeds.

**The Fixed and the Changing**

The verse's final clause resolves the apparent paradox of its first. God effaces and establishes — the world and the scriptures change — but *with Him* is the Mother of the Book, the fixed source, the eternal original, in which nothing is effaced because all is eternally established. The distinction is the verse's deepest teaching: there is the world of change, where things are effaced and established, and there is the divine presence, where the original abides unchanged. The changing is not a sign of disorder but of administration; the fixity is not a rival to the changing but its ground. The believer is thus taught to hold both truths at once — that his own life, with its gains and losses, its birth and death, is a tissue of effacement and establishment in God's hand, and that behind the tissue stands the Mother of the Book, in which his whole story is already written, whole and unchanged.

**A Practical Reflection**

For the reader, the verse is a source of both sobriety and trust. Sobriety, because it removes the illusion of permanence: what I have can be effaced, what I lack can be established, and neither is in my control. Trust, because it removes the companion illusion of chaos: the effacement and establishment are not random but administered, and their source is the Mother of the Book, the fixed original in God's keeping. The verse thus trains the believer in the Qur'an's characteristic posture toward change — neither clinging to what may be effaced nor despairing of what may be established, but resting in the knowledge that both the erasing and the writing are in the hand of the One with whom the original abides.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:40

> **Whether We show thee a part of that which We promise them, or We take thee [unto Us], thine is only to proclaim, and Ours is the Reckoning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Release from the Burden of Outcome**

This verse delivers to the Prophet ﷺ — and through him to every conveyer of truth — one of the Qur'an's most important consolations: the release from the burden of outcome. The verse's structure is a pair of alternatives followed by a division of responsibilities. The alternatives: *"Whether We show thee a part of that which We promise them, or We take thee [unto Us]"* — whether the Prophet lives to see the promised punishment befall the disbelievers, or dies before it arrives. The division: *"thine is only to proclaim, and Ours is the Reckoning"* — the Prophet's task is the delivery, and the outcome is God's. The verse thus severs the messenger's peace from the results of his message, and in doing so it removes the heaviest weight that any bearer of truth carries.

**The Two Possible Futures**

The verse's alternatives are stated with deliberate completeness, and the same structure recurs twice in the Qur'an in almost identical words:

> *"Whether We show thee a part of that which We promise them, or We take thee [unto Us], unto Us shall be their return."* (Qur'an 10:46)

> *"Whether We show thee a part of that which We promise them, or We take thee [unto Us], unto Us shall be their return; then God is Witness over what they do."* (Qur'an 40:77)

The two possibilities cover the whole of what could happen: the Prophet might witness the punishment in his lifetime, or he might not. The verse's point is that *neither* possibility changes his task or his standing. If he sees the punishment, his task is done and the vindication is God's; if he does not, his task is still done and the reckoning still belongs to God. The messenger is thus freed from the anxiety of the timetable — the question of *when* the promise will arrive is removed from his shoulders, because the arrival is not his affair.

**"Thine Is Only to Proclaim"**

The verse's central clause is the sūrah's clearest statement of the prophet's office: *"thine is only to proclaim"* (*innamā ʿalayka al-balāgh*). The particle *innamā* — "only" — confines the duty to its single, essential core: *balāgh*, the conveying, the delivery, the proclamation of the message. The same term appears in the Qur'an's most emphatic statement of the prophetic charge:

> *"O Messenger! Convey that which has been sent down unto thee from thy Lord; and if thou dost not, thou wilt not have conveyed His message."* (Qur'an 5:67)

The duty of *balāgh* is thus not a minimal requirement but the whole of the mission, and the verse's "only" is not a diminishment but a liberation: the prophet's success is measured by the fidelity of the delivery, not by the response of the delivered. This is the principle that has sustained every preacher, teacher, and reformer who has ever faced an unresponsive audience — the certainty that the task is the conveying, and that the converting is God's.

**"Ours Is the Reckoning"**

The verse's final clause completes the division: *"and Ours is the Reckoning"* (*wa-ʿalaynā al-ḥisāb*). The *ḥisāb* — the reckoning, the accounting — is the judgment of the delivered, and it belongs to God alone. The commentators explain the clause's full meaning: when the disbelievers return to Us, We shall recompense them — the reckoning of their response is not the messenger's to conduct or even to worry over. The clause echoes the Qur'an's other statements of the same division:

> *"Then truly with Us lies their reckoning."* (Qur'an 88:26)

The verse's logic is thus a clean separation of two offices that human beings constantly confuse: the office of the one who delivers and the office of the One who judges. The messenger delivers; God judges. The confusion of the two — the conveyer who imagines he must produce results, the teacher who imagines he must compel belief — is the source of endless anxiety and, often, of the very coercion that corrupts the message. The verse heals the confusion by drawing the line: delivery is yours, judgment is Mine.

**The Verse's Psychology**

The psychological importance of this verse is hard to overstate, and it applies far beyond the office of prophecy. Every human being who has ever tried to convey something true — a parent to a child, a teacher to a student, a friend to a friend, a voice to an indifferent world — has felt the specific despair of the unconverted audience, the fear that the failure of the message to land is the failure of the one who delivered it. The verse's answer is to split the two: the delivery is your duty and your success; the reception is the hearer's choice and God's reckoning. The conveyer who internalizes this verse is freed to do his work well, without the desperation that comes from needing the result — and, the tradition observes, he usually does the work better for it, because the desperation was never helping. The verse is thus not only a theology of prophecy but a therapy for every bearer of truth: proclaim, and leave the reckoning to its Owner.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:41

> **Have they not considered how We come upon the land, reducing it of its outlying regions? And God judges; none repeals His Judgment, and He is swift in reckoning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shrinking of the Land**

The verse opens with a question addressed to the disbelievers: *"Have they not considered how We come upon the land, reducing it of its outlying regions?"* (*a-wa-lam yaraw annā naʾtī al-arḍa nanquṣuhā min aṭrāfihā*). The image is of a territory being eaten away at its edges — its borders contracting, its outlying districts falling one by one. The commentators explain the verse's historical reference with precision. The "land" is the land of the disbelievers — the territory of Makkah and its environs — and the "reduction of its outlying regions" is God's gradual erosion of the disbelievers' domain as the Prophet's message advanced: village after village, tribe after tribe, region after region coming over to Islam, so that the land of disbelief shrank at its borders while the land of faith grew. Ibn Juzayy and others read the verse as a direct reference to the conquest of Makkah and the surrounding territories — the promise of victory that the sūrah has been building toward since verse 31.

**The Law of Gradual Conquest**

The verb *nanquṣu* — "We reduce, We diminish" — is worth attention, because it names a specific divine strategy. God does not, in this verse's picture, annihilate the disbelievers' land in a single stroke; He reduces it *from its outlying regions* — little by little, from the edges inward. The commentators note the pedagogical wisdom of this gradualness: the reduction at the borders is a warning delivered before the stroke reaches the center, giving the people of the center time to reflect and to turn. The Qur'an's own teaching on the gradual approach of the punishment elsewhere confirms this reading — the calamity "alights close to their abode" (verse 31) precisely so that the near miss may serve as a summons. The shrinking of the land is thus mercy in the form of warning: each lost region is a message to the regions that remain.

**A Universal Principle**

The commentators also read the verse at a more universal level, and this reading gives it its permanent force. The reduction of the land is the Qur'an's standing law of the rise and fall of powers: every empire, every civilization, every false order is gradually reduced at its edges before it falls, because falsehood, as the sūrah's own parable taught, is the froth that rises and then breaks. The verse's question — "have they not considered?" — is thus addressed to every age: consider the history of the powers before you, and you will see the law in operation — the dominion that looked eternal, eroded at its borders, and then gone. The question is the Qur'an's invitation to read history as a text, and the reading always yields the same lesson: the land of falsehood shrinks, because God judges.

**"And God Judges; None Repeals His Judgment"**

The verse then states the principle that underlies the image: *"And God judges; none repeals His Judgment"* (*wa-Allāhu yaḥkumu lā muʿaqqiba li-ḥukmihi*). The verb *ḥakama* — "judges, decrees" — and the noun *ḥukm* — "judgment, decree" — are the roots of the *ḥukman* that opened verse 37, and the sūrah is here drawing the connection: the judgment sent down in Arabic is the judgment that governs the rise and fall of lands. The word *muʿaqqib* — "one who repeals, who follows up to overturn" — is the verse's key term: it is the one who comes after a decree to set it aside. The verse's claim is that no such one exists for God's judgment. The decrees of men can be appealed, reversed, amended; the decree of God, once it arrives, is final, and there is no court above His court, no power behind His power, no force that can undo what He has done. The word is the same root as the *muʿaqqibāt* — the attendant angels — of verse 11, and the echo is deliberate: the angels who follow in succession to guard are the very pattern of the order against which no one can follow to repeal.

**"He Is Swift in Reckoning"**

The verse closes with the divine title that seals the warning: *"and He is swift in reckoning"* (*wa-huwa sarīʿ al-ḥisāb*). The title recurs throughout the Qur'an as the constant companion of the warnings of judgment:

> *"And God is swift in reckoning."* (Qur'an 2:202; 3:19; 5:4; 24:39; 40:17)

The commentators explain the title's two dimensions. On the one hand, it means that God's accounting is swift in its *arrival* — the reckoning comes quickly, and the one who counts on delay counts on nothing. On the other, it means that God's accounting is swift in its *execution* — the judgment, once rendered, takes no time at all, for God needs no deliberation, no investigation, no gathering of evidence; the whole of a soul's record is before Him in an instant. The title thus completes the verse's threat: the land is reduced gradually, as a warning; but the reckoning itself, when it comes, is swift — and there is no repealing it.

**The Verse's Two Audiences**

Like several of the sūrah's verses, this one addresses two audiences in a single movement. To the disbelievers it is a warning: the erosion you see at the borders of your land is the advance of the judgment, and no appeal will stop it. To the believers it is a promise: the reduction of the false order is not an accident of politics but a divine decree, and the shrinking they watch is the working of the judgment that cannot be repealed. The two audiences are united by the verse's closing title — the Swift in reckoning — which stands over both the warning and the promise as the single ground of their certainty.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:42

> **Indeed those who were before them plotted, but unto God belongs plotting altogether. He knows what every soul earns, and the disbelievers will know whose is the Ultimate Abode.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Plots of the Past and the Plot of God**

The verse opens with a statement of historical fact joined to a statement of divine supremacy: *"Indeed those who were before them plotted, but unto God belongs plotting altogether"* (*wa-qad makara alladhīna min qablihim fa-li-Allāhi al-makru jamīʿan*). The word *makr* — "plotting" — is the Qur'an's term for the stratagems of the disbelievers against the messengers: the schemes, the conspiracies, the hidden plans to silence or destroy the bearers of truth. The verse's first clause asserts that such plotting is as old as prophecy itself — the nations before the Makkans plotted against their messengers just as the Makkans now plot against the Prophet. Its second clause then draws the conclusion that dissolves all plotting: unto God belongs plotting altogether. Every scheme, every stratagem, every hidden design is ultimately within the compass of God's own plan, and His plan prevails over them all.

**The Meaning of "Plotting" When Attributed to God**

The attribution of *makr* to God requires the same careful interpretation that verse 13's *miḥāl* required, and the commentators supply it. God's "plotting" is not deceit, for God cannot deceive; it is the divine counter-scheme, the perfect and just arrangement by which the plotters' own plots are turned against them. The Qur'an states the principle in several forms:

> *"And they plotted, and God plotted; and God is the best of plotters."* (Qur'an 3:54)

> *"So they plotted a plot, and We plotted a plot, while they perceived not."* (Qur'an 27:50)

The commentators explain the relationship precisely: the disbelievers' plot is an attempt to defeat the truth; God's plot is the arrangement by which the truth defeats the plotters — their own scheme becomes the instrument of their undoing. This is why the Qur'an says that evil plotting "besets none but its authors":

> *"Yet evil plotting besets none but its authors."* (Qur'an 35:43)

The verse's claim — "unto God belongs plotting altogether" — is thus a statement of total sovereignty: the plotters plot within a world that God administers, and the administration is such that no plot against the truth can finally succeed, because the truth's victory is itself part of the administration.

**"He Knows What Every Soul Earns"**

The verse then grounds the divine supremacy in the divine knowledge: *"He knows what every soul earns"* (*yaʿlamu mā taksibu kullu nafs*). The clause returns to the sūrah's earlier teaching — verse 33's God Who "attends to every soul in accordance with what it has earned" — and it supplies the reason no plot can succeed. The plotter's scheme depends on secrecy: the plan works only if it remains hidden until it strikes. But the verse's point is that nothing is hidden from God, for He knows what every soul earns — every secret intention, every hidden step, every concealed design is open to His knowledge before it is even executed. The plotter, in the Qur'an's picture, is a man digging in the dark under the eye of the One who sees in the dark. The divine knowledge is thus the ground of the divine counter-plot: God cannot be outplotted, because He cannot be surprised.

**"Whose Is the Ultimate Abode"**

The verse closes with its warning and its promise compressed into a single clause: *"and the disbelievers will know whose is the Ultimate Abode"* (*wa-sa-yaʿlamu al-kuffāru li-man ʿuqbā al-dār*). The word *ʿuqbā al-dār* — "the Ultimate Abode" — is the phrase that has run through the whole sūrah: verse 22 promised the believers "the reward of the Abode," verse 24 declared "how excellent is the Ultimate Abode," verse 35 stated that the Garden is "the ultimate end of those who were reverent." Now the phrase returns as the subject of a deferred disclosure: the disbelievers *will know* whose is the Ultimate Abode. Al-Bayḍāwī explains the clause's function: it is an elucidation of the divine plotting — the plot's outcome is that the question of the Abode, which the disbelievers now dispute, will be settled before their eyes. Al-Rāzī adds the psychological force: the clause puts fear into the disbelievers' hearts, for it tells them that although they are ignorant now of the end of affairs, the Hereafter will disclose to them who has won the Abode. And al-Ṭabarī states the disclosure's content: when they meet God on the Day of Judgment, the disbelievers will be cast into the Fire and the believers will enter the Garden, and then they will know — with the knowledge of the witnessed — whose was the Ultimate Abode all along.

**The Structure of Certainty**

The verse's three clauses form a single logical chain, and it is worth stating the chain plainly, because it is the sūrah's final argument. The plotters plotted; but all plotting belongs to God; because God knows what every soul earns, no plot can outrun His knowledge; and therefore the disbelievers will know — at the end — whose is the Abode. The movement is from human scheming to divine sovereignty, from divine sovereignty to divine knowledge, and from divine knowledge to the final disclosure. Each clause follows from the one before, and the conclusion follows from all three. The verse is thus a compact theodicy of history: the apparent success of the plotters is temporary, the hidden knowledge of God is total, and the final disclosure is certain. The reader is left with the verse's single, unshakeable assurance: the plot against the truth is already, in the divine knowledge, the truth's victory.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:43

> **And those who disbelieve say, "You have not been sent." Say, "God and whosoever possesses knowledge of the Book suffices as a witness between you and me."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Objection and the Final Witness**

The sūrah ends where its argument began — with the disbelievers' rejection of the Prophet's mission — and it answers that rejection with the chapter's final and most elevated appeal. The objection is the bluntest of all: *"You have not been sent"* (*lasta mursalan*). It is not a demand for a sign, not a quarrel about the Resurrection, not a quibble about the message's content; it is the denial of the messenger's office itself. After forty-two verses of signs, arguments, parables, warnings, and promises, the disbelievers' last word is the simple refusal: you are not a messenger. The sūrah has been answering this denial all along; its final verse now answers it with a witness.

**The Appeal to God as Witness**

The Prophet is commanded to answer with a single, unshakeable statement: *"God and whosoever possesses knowledge of the Book suffices as a witness between you and me"* (*kafā bi-Allāhi shahīdan baynī wa-baynakum wa-man ʿindahu ʿilmu al-kitāb*). The first witness is God Himself. The appeal to God as witness is the Qur'an's standing refuge of the Prophet under accusation, and it appears throughout the revelation:

> *"Say, 'God suffices as a witness between you and me; He knows what is in the heavens and the earth.'"* (Qur'an 29:52)

The logic of the appeal is precise: the claim that a man has or has not been sent is, in the end, a claim about a fact known to God alone; and when the parties dispute, the matter is referred to the One who knows. The Prophet does not need to prove his office to those who deny it; he needs only to invoke the witness who will settle it — and the settling, as the sūrah's own verse 42 has just said, will come with the disclosure of the Ultimate Abode. God suffices as a witness, because God's testimony is the truth, and no further arbitration is needed beyond it.

**"Whosoever Possesses Knowledge of the Book"**

The second witness is *"whosoever possesses knowledge of the Book"* (*man ʿindahu ʿilmu al-kitāb*), and this phrase has occasioned one of the richest discussions in the commentary tradition, because it has been read at several distinct levels. The first reading, transmitted by al-Bayḍāwī and Ibn ʿAjībah, takes it generally: the witness is anyone who knows the Book — who knows the Qur'an and its inimitable nature, and who can therefore testify, from knowledge, that this Book is not the work of an impostor. On this reading, the knowledge of the Book is itself the witness: the more one knows the Qur'an, the more certain one becomes of its divine source, and those who know it best are the strongest witnesses to the one who brought it.

**The Witness of ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām**

The second reading gives the phrase a historical reference. Ibn Kathīr transmits that "whosoever possesses knowledge of the Book" refers, in the first instance, to the learned converts among the People of the Book — above all ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām (d. 43/663), the Jewish scholar of Madinah who recognized in the Qur'an the signs of the awaited Prophet recorded in the Torah and embraced Islam, and others like him among the Jewish and Christian converts who testified to the Prophet's truth from the scriptures they knew. The Qur'an itself appeals to this kind of witness in 46:10:

> *"Say, 'Have you considered, if it is from God and you disbelieve in it, while a witness from the Children of Israel bears witness to the like thereof and believes?'"* (Qur'an 46:10)

On this reading, the verse's second witness is the testimony of the scripture-people themselves — those who knew the Book and recognized the Messenger in it. The disbelievers' denial is answered not only by God's witness but by the witness of those who knew the earlier Books best.

**The Shiʿi Reading: ʿAlī and the Imams**

The third reading belongs to the Shiʿi tradition, and it must be recorded alongside the others. Al-Qummī and al-Ṭūsī transmit that "whosoever possesses knowledge of the Book" refers to the Imams of the Prophet's household — *aʾimmat āl Muḥammad* — generally, and to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib specifically. This reading connects the verse to 27:40, where the one "who had knowledge of the Book" — a figure traditionally identified, in the Shiʿi tradition and in some Sunni reports, with a servant of Solomon, and read by the Shiʿa as prefiguring ʿAlī — performs a miraculous act by that knowledge. The Sunni tradition, represented by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, reads the verse's phrase in the general and historical senses described above. The disagreement is a matter of well-attested difference between the traditions, and both can be stated with their sources, as the commentators themselves do, without distortion of either.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Signature**

Beyond the historical questions, this final verse performs a structural function that deserves emphasis. The sūrah began with the detached letters and the declaration that what was sent down to the Prophet is the truth; it ends with the disbelievers' denial of that truth and the appeal to God and the people of knowledge as witnesses. The whole chapter has moved between these two poles — the truth declared and the truth denied — and its final verse gathers the whole movement into one statement: when the truth is declared and denied, the matter is referred to its witnesses, and God suffices. The verse is thus the sūrah's signature, the point at which the argument closes and the appeal is lodged with the only court that matters.

**The Last Word: A Lesson in Finality**

The verse's final lesson is about the proper ending of disputes. The Prophet does not end the sūrah with a victory over the disbelievers in argument, nor with a threat, nor with a plea; he ends it with a referral — to God and to the knowledge of the Book. This is the Qur'an's characteristic finality: the truth is delivered, the denial is recorded, and the matter is entrusted to its Witness. The believer who reaches this verse has been given the whole of the sūrah's case and the whole of its posture — to proclaim, to argue, to warn, and, at the last, to refer. The chapter that began with letters and thunder ends with a witness — and between the two, the reader has been led through the signs of the cosmos, the arguments of the intellect, the parables of truth and falsehood, the portraits of the two destinies, and the consolations of the messenger, until the final word is spoken and the case is, with God, complete.
