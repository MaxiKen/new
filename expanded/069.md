# Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah (Chapter 69) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day of the Undeniable Reality**

Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah, "The Undeniable Reality," is one of the most emphatic and sustained eschatological chapters of the Qur'an. It belongs to the late Makkan period, revealed, in the better view of the scholars, shortly after Sūrah 67 (al-Mulk), with which it shares a striking thematic bond. Sūrah 67 had closed the door on the charge that the Prophet ﷺ was possessed, ending with the grim picture of a man's hand brought to his throat and the assertion that *"what is in your breasts"* — unbelief and rejection — had been *"concealed,"* only for them to be brought face to face with what they had denied (67:9–10, 29). Sūrah 69 now answers that same challenge in a different register: it begins by naming, three times and in a rising crescendo, the *Ḥāqqah* — the reality that cannot be denied — and it ends by vindicating the Prophet himself, sweeping away the accusations that he was a poet or a soothsayer. The sūrah thus forms, in the words of the classical commentators, a complete response to the arrogance that had dismissed the Day of Judgment and the messenger who warned of it.

The sūrah's name is taken directly from its first three verses — a procedure the Qur'an follows frequently, as in the case of Sūrah 101 (al-Qāriʿah, "the Striking" and 56 (al-Wāqiʿah, "the Event"). This is not merely a bookkeeping device. It signals, as the commentators note, that the sūrah's entire message is concentrated in a single overwhelming idea: the reality that arrives with irresistible certainty and that no power of denial can dissolve. The word *ḥāqqah* is the emphatic (literally *mubālaghah*) form of *ḥaqq*, "truth" or "reality," and the whole chapter is built as a sustained meditation on the difference between what people *claim* to be real and what actually *is* real.

**The Architecture of the Sūrah**

The structure of al-Ḥāqqah is remarkably symmetrical and unfolds in four clear movements, each of which reinforces the others. The opening verses (1–12) issue a warning against repeating the fate of those who denied reality before: the Arabian tribes of ʿĀd and Thamūd, destroyed by wind and by the overwhelming blast; Pharaoh and the generations before him; and the "subverted" people of Lot, the inhabitants of Sodom and Gomorrah. The second movement (13–17) describes the convulsions of the Day itself — the single blast of the trumpet, the grinding of the earth and mountains, the rent sky, and the angels bearing the Throne of their Lord, eight of them on that Day. The third movement (18–37) is the great moral diptych of the sūrah: those who receive the book of their deeds in their right hand, and those who receive it in their left. The fourth and final movement (38–52) turns upon the Prophet and the Book: an oath by the seen and the unseen; the assertion that this is *"the speech of a noble messenger"* and not the speech of a poet or soothsayer; the severe conditional that had the Prophet fabricated anything against God, God would have seized him; and the closing command to glorify the Name of the Lord, the Magnificent.

This structure is itself a moral argument. The sūrah moves from the fate of nations of the past, to the dissolution of the cosmos, to the individual's accounting, and finally to the proof of the revelation itself. The progression gives the reader a complete eschatology — history, cosmology, psychology, and epistemology woven into one. The reader who traces this movement understands that the Qur'an does not merely warn of a coming judgment; it argues for the truth of that judgment, and it ties that truth to the very messenger who announced it.

**The Connection to the Previous Sūrah**

The commentators, and particularly al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr, note the deliberate link with Sūrah al-Mulk. Al-Mulk had established God's sovereign power over life and death, and had closed with a rhetorical question aimed at the disbelievers: *"Tell me, if your water were to sink away, who would bring you flowing water?"* (67:30). Al-Ḥāqqah opens by answering a different but related question: having been told of the Day, the disbeliever asks, in effect, "Where is this reality you speak of?" The sūrah answers with the threefold *Ḥāqqah* — it is coming, it is inevitable, and you will be apprised of it whether you believe it now or not. Where al-Mulk spoke of God's sovereignty in the natural order, al-Ḥāqqah speaks of His sovereignty in the moral order — the inevitable settling of accounts.

The sūrah's late-Makkan placement is also significant. By this time in the Prophet's mission, the Quraysh had heard the stories of past nations repeatedly; they had witnessed the challenge of the Qur'an's inimitability; and they had begun to escalate their smear campaign against the Prophet, calling him a poet, a soothsayer, and a man possessed. This sūrah answers those accusations head-on. It does so not with defensiveness but with the calm confidence of a messenger who knows exactly who he is and what he carries.

**The Method of the Commentary**

What follows is a complete, verse-by-verse expansion of the sūrah. Each verse is treated as an independent discourse — as the verse-by-verse method demands — drawing upon the Arabic language and its roots, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and others whose initials appear in the source as R, IK, Ṭ, Z, Bḍ, and Sy), the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, and the living reasoning that connects this fourteen-hundred-year-old revelation to the conditions of the modern human being. The source text in the workspace refers to scholars by abbreviation — R for al-Rāzī, IK for Ibn Kathīr, Ṭ for al-Ṭabarī, Z for al-Zamakhsharī, Bḍ for al-Bayḍāwī, Q for al-Qurṭubī, JJ for the translators of the source, Sy for al-Suyūṭī, and others — and the expansion below names them in full wherever they appear. The result is intended to be scholarly, reverent, reasoned, and, above all, useful — a chapter that opens the heart to the gravity of the coming reality while never losing sight of the mercy that stands beside the warning.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:1

> **The Undeniable Reality!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Refuses to Be Ignored**

The sūrah opens with a single word, placed at the head of the chapter like a seal stamped upon a royal pronouncement: *al-Ḥāqqah*. This is not a sentence but a declaration — a noun, derived from *ḥaqq* (truth, reality, right, due), intensified into an emphatic form that carries the weight of absolute certainty. The commentators note the morphology carefully. The pattern *faʿʿālah* (the doubled medial radical) is used in Arabic for intensification and for things that are firmly established, even overwhelming. Thus *al-ḥāqqah* is not merely "the truth"; it is the truth that presses upon every soul, that cannot be avoided, that establishes itself regardless of what anyone thinks or says. Al-Rāzī observes that the form itself conveys the sense of something that *realizes itself* — that comes to pass against all resistance.

In the immediate context of the sūrah, all the classical scholars agree that *al-Ḥāqqah* designates the Day of Resurrection and Judgment. Yet they disagree, significantly, over *which aspect* of that Day is being named. Some said the name refers to the fact that on that Day, all that the disbelievers rejected in this life will be realized and made evident — that the truth they denied will become undeniable, present before their eyes. Others said it refers to the fact that every person will then see the true nature of his or her own deeds, as the sūrah itself later phrases it: the people of the left hand are forced to *"admit their sin"* (67:11). Still others saw the name as pointing to the *certainty* of the Day itself — its inevitability, the fact that it is a reality due (ḥaqq) from God, one that He has pledged and will not break.

**Why the Name Is a Warning and a Promise**

The beauty of the word is that it gathers into itself two opposite emotions and binds them. For the believer, *al-Ḥāqqah* is a promise: the wrongs of this world — the apparent triumph of tyrants, the suffering of the innocent, the unpunished cruelty of the powerful — will finally be righted. The reality that justice is not a fiction but a due debt will come due. For the disbeliever, *al-Ḥāqqah* is a threat: every denial, every act of arrogance, every dismissal of the warning will be confronted by a reality that cannot be argued with. The single word thus speaks to both halves of humanity at once. It is precisely this duality that the sūrah's opening is designed to achieve.

There is a psychological precision here worth pausing over. Human beings, as the Qur'an repeatedly insists, prefer to live in a world of comfortable illusions. They tell themselves that the tyrant will win, that the weak will be forgotten, that death is the end, that there is no accounting. The Qur'an counters not with a lengthy philosophical refutation but with a single emphatic noun: *real.* The reality is coming. The entire sūrah is, in a sense, a sustained unpacking of this one word.

**The Undeniable Reality and Modern Denial**

This verse speaks with peculiar power to the modern temperament. Our age is characterized by a certain form of denial that is not always loud — it is often quiet and sophisticated. People deny the reality of the moral order not by shouting but by systematically excluding it from consideration: by treating ethics as subjective preference, by reducing justice to power, by insisting that death is merely a biological event with no significance beyond the body. The modern world has its own forms of *ḥāqqah*-denial, its own versions of the ancient boast that there is no reality beyond what we can measure and control. The verse stands as a quiet, patient correction: that which is real does not cease to be real because we refuse to acknowledge it. The Day is *al-ḥāqqah* — not merely because God says it will happen, but because it is the truth about what human life actually is: a journey toward an accounting, a fulfillment of a purpose, a return to a Lord.

The believer, in the meantime, is invited to live already as though the reality were present — because it is. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the person who keeps the reality of the Day before him is the one who truly understands what life is for. When asked *"Who are the most intelligent of believers?"* he replied, as recorded in the source for verse 20:

> *"The most frequent in recalling death, and the best prepared for what follows it."* (transmitted from Ibn ʿUmar and others; Ibn Mājah 4252 and others)

To hold the *Ḥāqqah* in the mind is not to be morbid or depressed. It is to be wise — to see things as they are, and to live accordingly.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:2

> **What is the Undeniable Reality?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Follows the Declaration**

The second verse of the sūrah asks a question: *mā al-ḥāqqah?* — "What is the Undeniable Reality?" At first glance this seems strange, because the previous verse has just named it. Why ask what something is after declaring its existence? The commentators explain that the question is not an inquiry into the *existence* of the reality but into its *nature* and *magnitude* — its true reality and its essence. The Arabic construction *mā hiya* (or here *mā al-ḥāqqah*) is used precisely to ask about the *quiddity* of a thing: not whether it exists, but what it is like, how great it is, what it involves.

This rhetorical device — announcing something and then immediately asking about it — is a well-known feature of strong Arabic speech. It has the effect of magnifying the subject and awaking the listener's imagination. The Qur'an uses it elsewhere, as at the openings of Sūrah 101 (al-Qāriʿah) and in the very sūrah that follows this pattern most closely. The question is not being asked because God, or the angel, or the messenger does not know the answer. It is asked on behalf of the hearer, who must be made to feel the enormity of what he has been denying. It is a question designed to shake the soul awake.

**The Magnified Question and the Limits of Reason**

Al-Rāzī and the other commentators draw attention to the repetition of the question in verses 2 and 3, which mirrors the pattern of Sūrah 101: *"The Striking. What is the Striking? And what shall apprise thee of what the Striking is?"* (101:1–3). The three-part structure — declaration, question, intensified question — is not redundant. It is a deliberate rhetorical climb, each stage raising the emotional and intellectual tension. By the third verse, the text has made clear that the reality is so great, so far beyond ordinary comprehension, that even the one asking — the angel of revelation, the Prophet — is invited to recognize that human and angelic minds cannot grasp it fully. It must be *told* like news rather than derived like a theorem.

This is a profound epistemological point that the Qur'an makes repeatedly: the reality of the Hereafter is beyond mere rational comprehension. It cannot be constructed by pure logic or reached by philosophical reasoning alone. It is a truth that must be *reported* — a matter of *ghayb* (the unseen) — and the proper response is not skepticism but receptive trust. As the Qur'an says in another place, *"But man is not given to understand..."* — and the sūrah itself will emphasize, at its close, that the reality is properly grasped only by *"attentive ears"* (69:12) and *"for the reverent"* (69:48).

**The Question That Is Also an Invitation**

There is a beautiful practical implication here. The question "What is the Undeniable Reality?" is not just rhetorical in the sūrah; it is a question that every human soul is invited to ask of itself. What is it, actually, that is real? What is the truth that stands behind all the busy-ness of daily life? Most people, when they are honest, do not live as though death were a reality; they live as though it were a distant, postponable fact. But the Qur'an asks: what if the reality you have been postponing is the very thing that gives your life meaning? What if the accounting is not a distant threat but the point of existence? The question is posed to wake the soul, not to frighten it. The soul that asks "what is the reality?" and then opens itself to the answer has already taken the first step toward guidance.

This is why the verse's question, at its deepest level, is an act of mercy. God does not need to know the answer; the servant does. And the servant who genuinely asks — not in mockery or in idle curiosity, but in the search for truth — will find that the answer has been given in the very next words of revelation, and throughout the sūrah that follows. The reality is the Day of the Great Accounting, the day on which every human being will meet the consequences of a life lived as though there were no reality beyond it — and will find that there is something much greater, and much more just, than they had imagined.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:3

> **And what shall apprise thee of the Undeniable Reality?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Heightening of the Question**

The third verse now declares the question with a noticeable escalation of phrasing: *wa mā adrāka mā al-ḥāqqah?* — "And what shall apprise *thee* of the Undeniable Reality?" The key word is *adrāka* — a verb that means "to make known," "to inform," "to cause to understand." It is stronger and more vivid than the simple *mā* question of the previous verse. The Arabic expression *mā adrāka* is used in the Qur'an, as al-Qurṭubī and others note, precisely when the thing being discussed is something of surpassing grandeur and gravity — something so great that ordinary human knowledge and even direct experience cannot fully convey it. It is as though the text is saying: "What could possibly bring you to know this? What power of imagination could grasp it?" The phrasing implies that the reality vastly exceeds the capacity of the hearer to comprehend it through his own resources.

The choice of *adrāka* over the more ordinary *aʿlamaka* ("what has made you know") is deliberate and significant. *Adrāka* comes from the root *d-r-k*, which also gives us the word for "the end," "the limit," and "reaching" or "overtaking." It carries the sense of grasping something at its farthest boundary, of finally laying hold of a thing that eluded you. The question, rendered fully, is: "What could make you *reach* the reality — what could bring your comprehension all the way to that which lies beyond the reach of ordinary grasp?" The reality cannot be held by the mind as one holds a stone; it must be reached by the heart, and even then only through the report of God.

**The Rhetoric of the Crescent and the Nature of the Unseen**

The commentators saw in this threefold repetition a specific rhetorical function: the constant recurrence of the word is meant to *emphasize* that the Day of Judgment is beyond mere rational comprehension. The more the Qur'an insists on the reality and then asks "what could apprise you of it?", the more it leads the reader to the edge of the horizon — and then invites him to look beyond. This is the structure of faith itself, as the Qur'an understands it. Faith is not the rejection of reason; it is reason recognizing its own limits and consenting to be carried by revelation beyond those limits.

The closest human analogy is the experience of the vast. Imagine a person standing on the edge of a great ocean or beneath a night sky filled with stars. He can name the ocean; he can measure the distance to the nearest star; but the *reality* of the ocean or the sky — the sheer might, the age, the scale — eludes any inventory. He knows *that* they are there, but the full *what* of their existence is beyond him. The Qur'an is doing exactly this with the Day of Judgment: it insists on the fact, and then acknowledges humbly that the full magnitude is beyond the telling. The sūrah's whole force lies in this combination — absolute certainty about the fact, reticence about the detail.

**The Intimacy of the Address**

There is another subtlety in the phrase *mā adrāka* — its direct address. The pronoun *kā* (thee) makes the question intimate and personal. The sūrah is not speaking abstractly about the Day; it is speaking *to* the hearer. In the Prophet's case, the address is to the messenger himself, which is a profound honor — even the Prophet ﷺ, the recipient of revelation, is depicted as being apprised of the reality by God, not by his own unaided intellect. In the reader's case, the address draws the reader into the question: you, who read this, what could apprise *you*? The question becomes a personal summons. The reader is no longer a spectator; he or she is the one being asked.

This is the essential work of the opening three verses. They establish the Day as absolute reality, acknowledge that it is beyond full comprehension, and then turn the whole matter around to press upon the individual soul. Before a single nation — ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh — is named, before the blast of the trumpet, before the book is given to the right or left hand, the reader is already being measured. The Undeniable Reality is not a distant event; it is the meaning of every life that will ever be lived.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:4

> **Thamūd and ʿĀd denied the calamity.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Transition from the Abstract to the Historical**

The sūrah now pivots from the abstract declaration of the Day to concrete historical evidence: *kadhdhabat Thamūd wa ʿĀd bi-al-qāriʿah*. "Thamūd and ʿĀd denied the calamity." The word *qāriʿah* — rendered here "the calamity" — is closely related in meaning to *al-ḥāqqah*; indeed it is used as another name for the Day of Judgment, as in the title of Sūrah 101. The root *q-r-ʿ* carries the sense of striking, pounding, knocking, striking suddenly with a shock. The "striking" or "calamity" is the Day that strikes the world with its catastrophic onset. The verse thus announces: what was denied is not merely an abstraction but the Day itself — the Day of striking reality.

The choice of these two tribes is not arbitrary. ʿĀd and Thamūd were the two most celebrated "ancient Arabs" of Arabian memory — legendary peoples of great power and civilization who had inhabited the regions of what is now the Empty Quarter and the Ḥijāz. They were remembered in the poetry of the pre-Islamic Arabs, and their ruins, particularly the carved tombs of Thamūd at al-Ḥijr (Mada'in Ṣāliḥ), were a standing witness to their former might. To the Makkans, these were not obscure foreign peoples; they were the descendants of their own reputed ancestors, the bearers of their own claimed heritage of power and wealth. The verse therefore strikes home twice: it warns of a Day, and it points to nations that actually denied such a Day and were destroyed because of it.

**The Nations That Denied and the Nations That Follow**

The accounts of ʿĀd and Thamūd are among the most detailed and repeated narratives in the Qur'an. Thamūd was sent the Prophet Ṣāliḥ, who was given the famous she-camel as a sign — a creature of God that came forth from the rock, a living proof that the Lord of the world was the same Lord who sustained them. But the tribe, in their arrogance, conspired to hamstring the camel and kill her, defying both the sign and the messenger; see 7:73–79; 11:61–68; 26:141–58; 54:23–31. ʿĀd, for their part, were a people of great prosperity and physical stature — *"the like of which had not been created in the land"* (89:8) — whose abundance turned to decadence and whose pleasure turned to insolence, so that they rejected the Prophet Hūd; see 7:65–72; 11:50–60; 41:15–16.

The Qur'an does not narrate these stories merely as history. It narrates them as a *typology* — a repeating pattern. The pattern is always the same: God sends a warning and a sign; a people, drunk on power and wealth, denies the warning and despises the sign; and then the reality that they denied arrives, and it does so with a force that they could not resist. The two tribes are the ancestors, geographically and culturally, of the very audience being addressed in Makkah. The sūrah is saying, in effect: "You are not different. You are their heirs. The warning that was given to them is now given to you — and the same reality that overtook them will overtake you unless you heed it."

**The Warning That Is Also a Gift**

The repetition of these stories across so many sūrahs — over a dozen accounts of ʿĀd and Thamūd — is itself a form of mercy. It shows that God does not abandon a people to destruction without warning after warning. The historical record became a standing curriculum for the later generations. As al-Rāzī observes, the point is not to terrify but to awaken: these were real peoples, with real cities, real art, real armies — and they are gone, and the reality they denied is still here, and it is still coming. Whatever pretensions a modern people might have — whether of science, of civilization, of power, of "progress" — the same lesson applies. Civilization is not a shield against the reality of the Day. Only awareness and right action are.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:5

> **As for Thamūd, they were destroyed by the overwhelming.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ambiguity of the Overwhelming**

The sūrah now specifies the judgment that fell upon Thamūd: *fa-ammā Thamūd fa-uhlikū bi-al-ṭāghiyah*. "As for Thamūd, they were destroyed by the overwhelming." The word *ṭāghiyah* is rich with meaning and has occasioned scholarly discussion. It is derived from the root *ṭ-gh-y* — to exceed the limit, to transcend the boundary, to be tyrannical or rebellious. The noun form *ṭāghiyah* can mean "that which exceeds the limit of severity" — a catastrophe of utmost intensity. The commentators, following al-Rāzī, identify several possible referents for this "overwhelming."

First, it can refer to the *manner* of their destruction: one of the calamities that actually befell Thamūd. In some accounts, they were destroyed by a *cry* (ṣayḥah) — a terrifying blast of sound that shattered them, as in 11:67 and 54:31. In others, they were destroyed by an *earthquake*, as in 7:78. In still others, by a *thunderbolt* (ṣāʿiqah), as in 41:13 and 51:44. The word *ṭāghiyah* gathers these particulars under a single concept: a blow that surpassed all bounds of severity, that exceeded any measure of punishment a people might imagine. Second, the word can carry a moral meaning: *ṭāghiyah* as "rebellion" or "upheaval" (a form of *ṭughyān*). In this reading, Thamūd were destroyed *by their own sinfulness* — by the rebellion that had become a settled part of their character. The two readings are not mutually exclusive; indeed the verse operates on both levels at once. The physical calamity that overwhelmed them was the outward expression of an inward rebellion that had overwhelmed their hearts.

**The Moral Root of the Punishment**

There is a third reading, particularly penetrating, which al-Rāzī reports: *ṭāghiyah* can mean "those who overwhelmed or oppressed." In this sense, Thamūd were destroyed because they allowed themselves to be persuaded — to be "overwhelmed" — by a powerful minority or a single individual who urged them on to their destruction. The reference here is to the notorious group of nine who "spread corruption in the land" (27:48) and who plotted to hamstring the she-camel of God and leave her for dead (91:13; see 54:23–30). The tribe did not act as a unified malignant body; it was led, or rather misled, by a clique that overwhelmed the rest. This is a striking and modern-sounding insight: ordinary people can be carried to ruin not by their own innate wickedness but by their surrender to a persuasive, corrupt minority.

The implications are sobering. Human beings are socially malleable; they can be swept into terrible decisions by the momentum of a group, by the prestige of a few loud or powerful voices, by the fear of being the odd one out. Thamūd, the Qur'an implies, were not all equally responsible for the crime; but they were responsible for their *complicity* — for allowing the corrupt few to set the course of the whole. The verse therefore addresses not only the tyrants of history but the silent majorities who enable them. It is a warning against passive acquiescence to evil, against the quiet surrender of one's own conscience to the crowd.

**The History That Rhymes**

The fate of Thamūd is one of the most vividly rendered in the Qur'an precisely because it is one of the most accessible. A powerful people, a clear sign, a small corrupt cabal, a passive majority, a moment of choice — and then the overwhelming blow. The story does not require ancient study to be understood; it is a template that has been replayed in every century, in every land, in every type of society, from empires to small towns. The overwhelming *ṭāghiyah* that destroyed the tribe was not merely a meteorological or geological event; it was the working out of a moral law that the Qur'an asserts as universally as any law of physics: that a society which consents to tyranny and rejects the grace that was offered to it will, in God's own time, be shaken beyond recovery.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:6

> **And as for ʿĀd, they were destroyed by a howling, raging wind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wind That Carried Away a Nation**

The sūrah now turns to the second tribe: *wa ammā ʿĀd fa-uhlikū bi-rīḥin ṣarṣarin ʿātiyah*. "And as for ʿĀd, they were destroyed by a howling, raging wind." The verse identifies with precision the instrument of ʿĀd's destruction, and the two adjectives it uses are each a word of considerable depth. The first, *ṣarṣar*, describes the wind in terms of its *sound* and its *temperature*. Al-Rāzī notes two principal interpretations. The first is auditory: *ṣarṣar* is a wind that makes a severe and intense noise — howling, shrieking, a wind whose roar is itself a terror. The reduplicated form (the doubling of *ṣ-r*) mimics the very sound it denotes — a pattern Arabic has for onomatopoeic words. The second is thermal: *ṣarṣar* describes a wind so extremely cold that its biting chill feels as though it burns. In this reading, the wind is not merely loud but searing — a cold so intense that it pierces the body and, in the words of Ibn Kathīr, cuts straight to the heart, extinguishing life itself.

The second adjective, *ʿātiyah*, describes the wind's *force* and its *dominion*. It comes from the root *ʿ-t-w*, which carries the sense of being disobedient, insolent, and overstepping. As a description of a wind, *ʿātiyah* indicates that it overcame the people in such a manner that they could find no cover — it was not a wind they could hide from, seek refuge from, or withstand. It also indicates the utmost in strength and severity. The wind did not merely blow; it *prevailed*; it *dominated*; it required absolute surrender. The two adjectives together paint a picture of a catastrophe that was at once audible, freezing, irresistible, and inescapable.

**The Symbolism of the Instrument**

The choice of wind as the instrument of ʿĀd's destruction is theologically significant. ʿĀd were a people of extraordinary physical power — the Qur'an describes them as possessing bodies of unusual stature and building cities of immense grandeur, including the legendary city of Iram of the pillars (89:7–8), believed by many to be an unprecedented feat of engineering. They gloried, as al-Qurṭubī notes, in their own strength. Yet they were destroyed not by a rival army, not by a plague, not by an earthquake — but by a *wind*. The very air they could not see, the element that sustains every breath and yet lies beyond a person's grasp, was turned against them. It is the ultimate demonstration of God's sovereignty: the thing that they breathed in pride was the thing that crushed them. There is no wall, no fortress, no armament that can shield against the wind of God when He commands it.

This also connects ʿĀd to a recurring Qur'anic theme: the humiliation of the proud by the humble and the invisible. Pharaoh was drowned in water; Korah was swallowed by the earth; the people of Lot were turned upside down and rained upon; Thamūd were blasted by a cry; and ʿĀd were swept away by wind. Each proud nation was undone by a force that its power could not purchase, its walls could not keep out, and its pride could not imagine. The sequence is a sobering reflection on the fragility of human grandeur. Every power that has ever believed itself permanent has been shown to be dependent upon the permission of a Power above it.

**The Universality of the Lesson**

The story of ʿĀd is not merely antiquarian. The Qur'an tells it in the present to every generation that reads it. Human civilizations habitually make the same error as ʿĀd: they mistake the *means* of their prosperity for the *ground* of it. A society that grows powerful may come to believe that its power is self-generated; a city that is wealthy may come to believe that its wealth is self-produced. But all of it — the wind that fills the sails, the rain that fills the rivers, the soil that yields the harvest, the health that sustains the workers — is a gift, held at the permission of the Giver. To forget the Giver is to be prepared, in the Qur'an's moral economy, for the gift to be withdrawn. The wind that was a mercy became a scourge because those who received it treated it, and the whole of their prosperity, as if it were their own. It is precisely this point the sūrah is making when it says, in the very next verse, that the wind was imposed upon them for seven nights and eight days — a reminder that the gift of a favorable wind and the blow of a destructive one are both from the same Hand.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:7

> **He imposed it upon them for seven nights and eight days consecutively, so that thou might see the people felled as if they were hollowed palm trunks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Precise Duration of the Catastrophe**

The sūrah now gives a hauntingly specific detail about the ʿĀd's destruction: *sakhkharahā ʿalayhim sabʿa layālin wa thamāniyata ayyāmin ḥusūman*. "He imposed it upon them for seven nights and eight days consecutively." This is one of the few passages in the Qur'an that numbers the duration of a historical punishment, and the specificity itself carries force: the wind was not a momentary gust but a sustained, relentless disaster that lasted for over a week. The word *sakhkharahā* — "He imposed it" — is significant. It is from the same root as *taskhīr*, which means to subject, to compel, to make subservient. In the natural order, wind is a servant of God; here that servant is *commanded* against a people. The word conveys that the wind was not loose and chaotic but *yoked* — held in service to a divine purpose for a fixed, deliberate, and measured span.

The word ḥusūm, translated "consecutively," is rich with ambiguity and has been interpreted in several ways. Some take it to mean "in succession" — seven nights and eight days in a row, without interruption, one calamity following upon another, unbroken. Some understand it differently: *ḥusūm* can also indicate one calamity following after another in a sequence of blows, as though the disaster struck, then struck again, then again. The commentators also note the derivation of the word from *ḥasama*, "to sever," and thus read it as a wind that severs all good and demolishes all blessings — a wind that cuts the people off from everything they had, that severs the bond between them and their prosperity. All these readings converge on the same picture of a prolonged, intensifying, and utterly unrelenting punishment.

**The Image of the Hollowed Palm Trunks**

The verse then offers one of the most vivid and memorable images in the Qur'an: *fa-tarā al-qawma fīhā ṣarʿā ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin khāwiyah*. "So that thou might see the people felled as if they were hollowed palm trunks." The comparison is both visual and symbolic. Palm trunks — and specifically the hollowed, dead trunks of date palms — are tall, straight, and empty. When a wind is powerful enough to blow them down, they fall not as gnarled, tumbled masses but as long, straight, hollow cylinders, lying in rows on the ground. The image conveys scale: the bodies of the people were strewn like toppled palm trunks, lying prostrate, lifeless, and hollow — the life that had given them uprightness was gone.

Al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, and others note the symbolism. The palm is an emblem of Arab life: a source of food, shade, and shelter, a symbol of vitality. To be compared to its hollowed, fallen trunks is to be stripped of all that vitality. It is a picture of what is left of a great people when the reality they denied arrives: not a glorious ruin, not a memorial, but a field of empty, toppled forms. There is a further subtlety. The Qur'an notes the *fragility* underlying apparent strength. The palm trunk looks strong and upright, tall and imposing. But it is essentially hollow — brittle, light, easily felled. The comparison therefore indicts the substance of the people themselves: they looked great, but beneath the appearance they were hollow — built on pride and denial rather than on genuine substance.

**The Witness of Literature and the Fate of Empires**

This image of the people of ʿĀd strewn like palm trunks is repeated in Sūrah 54:20, where the howling wind is said to tear out people *"as if they were uprooted palm trunks."* The repetition is deliberate: the Qur'an does not tire of pointing to this scene because it encapsulates a truth that every later society tends to forget. Civilizations are built on faith and trust, on the invisible bonds of justice, gratitude, and reverence. When those bonds are cut — when a people come to rely solely on their own strength and to believe that the source of their prosperity is themselves — they become, in the moral order, exactly what the hollowed palm trunk is in the natural order: tall, impressive, and empty. And the stronger the wind that tests them, the more completely they fall.

The seven nights and eight days are also a detail of mercy. The destruction was spread over more than a week — not a single instantaneous bolt but a sustained, unmissable, prolonged event. The people had time to see it coming, to reflect, to cry out. The commentary tradition notes that God does not strip a people of their opportunity for repentance without giving them time. That the wind persisted and yet the people did not turn is itself the indictment. The prolonged warning was the final grace, and the persistence in denial was the final sin.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:8

> **So dost thou see any remnant of them?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Demands an Audit**

The sūrah closes the account of ʿĀd with a single, devastating rhetorical question: *fa-hal tarā lahum min bāqiyah?* "So dost thou see any remnant of them?" The question is not merely rhetorical in the ordinary sense; it is a summons to witness and to reflection. The verb *tarā* — "do you see" — directs the hearer's gaze outward, to the world around him. The question assumes the reader lives in the vicinity of the lands of ʿĀd, or at least has access to their memory and their ruins. The answer, on the surface, is "no": there is no remnant of ʿĀd; they are gone. But the deeper answer the verse seeks is the recognition of *why* they are gone, and the acceptance that this is not a historical accident but a moral necessity.

The question also has a subtle function in the structure of the sūrah. It pivots the reader from the fate of the past to the present. If ʿĀd are gone, and if their removal was the consequence of their denial, then the reader who is standing on the same moral ground — who denies the same reality — is standing on the edge of the same abyss. The verse does not say this explicitly; it leaves the reader to draw the conclusion. In this, it mirrors the method of all good moral teaching: it does not hammer the point but places the evidence before the person and trusts the conscience to do its work.

**The Vanishing of the Proud as a Moral Proof**

The absence of ʿĀd is not merely a fact of history; it is a proof of the reality the sūrah is describing. The people who denied the Day of Judgment are themselves gone, and their disappearance is a kind of prefiguring of the Day. Just as the world will be dismantled and the proud will find no refuge, so the proudest of the ancient tribes found no refuge, no remnant, no memory that could save them. The Qur'an returns to this idea in Sūrah 11, where the ruin of the unjust is described as a *"removal such as when a people is uprooted"* (11:44), and in Sūrah 51, where God's decree against ʿĀd was that they be destroyed by "the barren wind."

Al-Rāzī and al-Tabarī remark that the question is also meant to correct a natural human disposition. When people hear of a past catastrophe, they are prone to a kind of morbid curiosity, even a secret sense of superiority: "That was them; we are different." The verse forbids this comfort by pointing to the standing *possibility* that the present generation is no different. The anthropological record itself becomes a standing warning: behind every ruin lies a confidence that history would not be repeated. The ruins of ancient civilizations that dot the modern world — the empty cities of the Nabataeans at al-Ḥijr, the remains of great kingdoms in Iraq and Syria and the Yemen — are a visible testimony to the same moral law. They are not merely interesting archaeological sites; they are, in the Qur'an's reading, a gallery of warnings.

**The Question Addressed to the Modern Reader**

The question "So dost thou see any remnant of them?" is not confined to the seventh-century listener. It is asked of every generation, and it presses with special urgency upon the modern reader, who lives in a time of unprecedented power, technological confidence, and, often, moral complacency. The modern world has its own grand achievements, its own sense of permanence, its own belief that the future will be an uninterrupted continuation of the present. The sūrah interrupts that confidence with a simple question: where are the peoples who believed as you believe? Where are the empires that were certain of their eternity? The question does not demand a despairing answer; it demands a *humble* one. The proper response is not terror but taqwā — the protective awareness of which the Fātiḥah and al-Baqarah spoke — the recognition that all of existence is held by One whose permission is the only true guarantee of any continuance.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:9

> **And Pharaoh and those before him, and those subverted brought iniquity.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shift to the Nations of the Scripture**

The sūrah now shifts its gaze from the Arabian tribes to the peoples of the older scriptural traditions, those nations whose histories were known to the People of the Book and which the Qur'an repeatedly invokes as witnesses. The verse reads: *wa jāʾa Firʿawnu wa man qablahu wa al-muʾtafikāt bi-al-khāṭiʾah*. "And Pharaoh and those before him, and those subverted, brought iniquity." The phrase "brought iniquity" — *bi-al-khāṭiʾah* — indicates that these were the doers of sin, the bearers of transgression; the word is the intensive noun of the act of sinning, and its construction here suggests that the iniquity was a settled, habitual, and deliberate feature of their lives.

This is the second of the sūrah's moral catalogues. Where ʿĀd and Thamūd represent the paganism of Arabia, Pharaoh represents the tyranny of Egypt — the arrogant king who defied Moses, and the descendants of those who came before him in the same spirit of defiance. The mention of "Pharaoh" in the Qur'an almost always refers not to a specific named king but to the archetype of the tyrannical ruler who sets himself against God's messenger. It is a title, not a name — the word Firʿawn was a royal title in ancient Egypt, applied to the sovereign, and the Qur'an uses it to evoke a whole class of despotic power.

**Those Before Him and the Chronology of Defiance**

The phrase *man qablahu* — "those before him" — refers to the generations that preceded Pharaoh, the peoples among whom the traditions of sin and defiance were established. It is important to note here the commentators' observation about a possible alternative reading. The same consonants, with a different voweling, can be read *wa man qibalahu* — "those who were with him" — meaning those who followed Pharaoh, his party and his allies. Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr both report this alternative, and the ambiguity is not a flaw but a richness. Whether the verse is describing the tyrants of earlier ages or the associates of Pharaoh's own court, the moral point is identical: this was a line of rulers and their followers who did not merely err but *brought iniquity*.

The mention of Pharaoh and his predecessors also sets up a theme the sūrah will return to: the false claim of sovereignty. Pharaoh's great sin, as the Qur'an narrates it in Sūrah 79, was his declaration: *"I am your lord most high"* (79:24). He claimed for himself what belongs to God alone — the right to be obeyed as the supreme authority over human beings. The sūrah's catalogue of destruction is thus a catalogue of the forms that this usurpation of divine authority has taken: the Arabian tribes usurped it through pride and social contempt; Pharaoh usurped it through political tyranny; and the people of Lot, the "subverted," usurped it through moral corruption.

**The Subverted: The Cities That Were Turned Over**

The third group named is *al-muʾtafikāt* — "those subverted." This is one of the Qur'an's most compact and evocative descriptions of the people of Lot. The word derives from the root *-f-k*, which carries the senses of overturning, inverting, and flinging down, but also of falsifying and lying. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, and al-Qurṭubī are agreed that these are the inhabitants of Sodom and Gomorrah, the cities to which Lot was sent as a messenger, and which were overturned by the divine punishment for a corruption so severe that it had no precedent. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the nature of their corruption: *"And We sent Lot, and he said to his people, 'You commit the abomination that none in the worlds have committed before you'"* (7:80–84; 11:77–83). The cities were literally turned upside down, and the verse names them with a word that carries both the physical overturning and the moral falsity that caused it.

The word *muʾtafikāt* also carries the sense of "those that were overwhelmed," those struck by the flood of punishment. The root *-f-k*, in the fourth form (*iftaʿala*), can mean to be turned over, to have the upper and lower parts reversed — which is precisely what happened to Sodom. The verse thus compresses into a single noun both the crime and the punishment: they were the falsified and corrupt ones, and they were the flipped and destroyed ones.

**The Three Faces of Iniquity**

Taken together, the verse's three groups represent three distinct and archetypal forms of that iniquity which the Qur'an warns against. The Arab tribes represent the sin of *social and moral arrogance* — the belief that power, wealth, and lineage make one superior and immune to consequence. Pharaoh represents the sin of *political tyranny* — the domination of human beings in the name of a false lord, the usurpation of the sovereignty that belongs to God. And the people of Lot represent the sin of *sexual and social corruption* — the violation of the natural moral order in a way that undermines the fabric of society.

The sūrah's moral is that no single form of human wickedness escapes the reckoning. Whether a person sins through pride, through power, or through appetite, the same reality — the *ḥāqqah* — awaits. There is no category of privilege, no social class, no historical period that is exempt. And the verse is not ahistorical in its reference: the Qur'an repeatedly points out that these were real, historically attested peoples, and their fates were known to their contemporaries — and, in the case of the people of Lot, to the very culture of the ancient Near East.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:10

> **They disobeyed the messenger of their Lord, and He seized them with a devastating blow.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One Sin Behind Every Ruin**

Having named the peoples, the sūrah now identifies the single crime that unified them and led to their destruction: *fa-ʿaṣaw rasūla rabbihim fa-akhadhahum akhdhatan rābiyah*. "They disobeyed the messenger of their Lord, and He seized them with a devastating blow." The verse reduces the histories of ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh, the nations before him, and the people of Lot to a single common denominator: disobedience to the messenger of their Lord. This is the central theological lesson of the sūrah's first section. Whatever the particular form of each people's sin — pride, tyranny, corruption — the essential crime was the same. They rejected the one whom God had sent to them, the bearer of the warning and the guide to the truth. And because they rejected him, God *seized* them.

The word *akhadhahum* — "He seized them" — is weighty. It is the verb of God's *taking hold* of a people, of His arrest and apprehension of them. The Qur'an uses this image repeatedly for divine judgment: God "seizes" the sinner not as an indiscriminate force but as a sovereign who lays hold of a subject who has broken covenant. It implies both power and purpose. The blow is not random; it is the working of a just authority. Al-Rāzī emphasizes that the "seizure" is always morally grounded: it follows directly upon the disobedience, and it is precisely calibrated to it.

**The Devastating Blow: *Akhdhatan Rābiyah***

The description of the punishment as *rābiyah* — "devastating" — is another word of considerable depth. The root *r-b-w* carries the sense of increase, growth, and mounting. A *rābiyah* punishment is one that *surpasses* and *exceeds* — a blow that goes beyond every measure and every precedent. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī interpret this in two principal ways. The first is quantitative: the punishment was more severe than anything a people had faced before, exceeding the punishment of all previous generations. The second is temporal: the punishment was devastating in this world but becomes even more severe in the Hereafter — a *rising* punishment, one that does not end with death but continues and intensifies on the Day of Judgment. The Qur'an makes this point explicitly elsewhere in reference to those who disbelieved in Noah: *"They were drowned, then made to enter a Fire"* (71:25). The worldly blow is the beginning of the reckoning, not the end.

The word *rābiyah* thus captures a reality that is difficult to grasp: the punishment of the Hereafter is not merely "more" than the punishment of this world — it is of a completely different order, and the worldly destruction is only a foretaste, a first installment that will be followed by a more complete reckoning. This is why the Qur'an, in this very sūrah, will describe the disbeliever's fate after the earthly catastrophes of ʿĀd and Thamūd and Pharaoh, and then go on to describe the fire, the chain, and the filth of the Hereafter. The earthly blow of the ancient peoples is the *demonstration*; the eschatological blow is the *consummation*.

**The "Message" Reading and Its Implication**

Al-Qurṭubī reports a significant alternative reading of the word "messenger" here. The Arabic word *rusūl* — "messengers" — can be read, with a slight change, as *risālah* — "the message." In this reading, the verse would mean: "They disobeyed the *message* of their Lord," rather than "the *messenger*." The difference is subtle but important. The "messenger" reading emphasizes the *person* who bore the warning — the prophet who was rejected, despised, and eventually vindicated. The "message" reading emphasizes the *content* — the warning itself, the guidance, the command. In the message reading, the verse could refer to any of the disbelieving peoples mentioned across the sūrah, and indeed across the whole Qur'an: every people that rejected the message of God, whatever form it took, was seized for that rejection.

The two readings, however, ultimately converge. A messenger without a message is meaningless; a message without a messenger is unrealizable. The Qur'an insists that God does not leave a people without a warner — *"And there was never a people without a warner in it"* (35:24) — and so the rejection of the message is always, in practice, the rejection of the messenger who brought it. The sūrah's use of both dimensions — the messenger of the verse and the message of the closing verses — frames its whole argument.

**The Lesson for Every Generation**

The verse compresses a profound principle of moral accountability. Human beings are not judged merely by their private deeds but by their response to the guidance that has been made available to them. The peoples of antiquity were not destroyed because they lacked access to truth; they were destroyed because, having access to truth, they refused it. The same applies to every generation. The sūrah's warning is that knowledge without obedience is a liability, not a benefit. A person who has heard the message of God and turned from it is in a position of greater danger, not lesser. This is the moral asymmetry that the Qur'an emphasizes throughout: those who are given light and refuse it are judged more severely than those who never saw it at all. It is a profound and sobering truth for a modern world in which the message of God is available, recited, and broadcast on every tongue.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:11

> **Truly when the waters overflowed, We carried you upon the ship,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ark as the Opposite of the Catastrophes**

The sūrah now turns from the destroyed peoples to a contrasting scene — the deliverance of Noah and the believers. This is the third and final element in the first section's historical panorama: after the ruined tribes and the fallen tyrant comes the salvation of the righteous. The verse reads: *innā lammā ṭaghā al-māʾu ḥamalnākum fī al-jāriyah*. "Truly when the waters overflowed, We carried you upon the ship." The word *ṭaghā* — "overflowed" — is the same root as *ṭāghiyah* used earlier for the "overwhelming" that destroyed Thamūd. The use of the same root is deliberate and pointed. The waters that *overflowed* (ṭaghā) — that exceeded their bounds, that rose in a catastrophic flood — were the instrument of a similar overwhelming. But where the overwhelming of Thamūd destroyed, this overwhelming of the waters *carried* the believers in the ark. The same power of God, the same exceeding of bounds, is here turned from judgment to mercy.

The word *jāriyah* — "the ship" — is literally "that which runs" or "that which flows." It is a descriptive noun for a vessel because a ship is precisely something that runs upon the water, borne by it rather than resisting it. The Qur'an's word for the ark is thus a word that captures the fundamental relationship of the saved to the catastrophe: they did not fight the flood; they were borne upon it, carried by God. This is the deep spiritual meaning of the ark: it is not that the believers escaped the flood because they were stronger than it, but because they submitted to God and were carried by His mercy.

**The Address "You": The Miracle of Descent**

The pronoun *kum* — "you" — is the pivot of the verse. When God says "We carried *you* upon the ship," He is addressing the contemporary believers — the Muslims who were hearing this sūrah — even though they were not present at the time of Noah. Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr explain this in the manner that the earlier commentaries recorded: *"We carried your forefathers while you were still in their loins."* The deliverance of Noah's family was, in a real sense, the deliverance of all their offspring. The believers of Muhammad's community were the descendants of those who had been saved on the ark, and the same theme is expressed in Sūrah 36:41: *"And a sign unto them is that We carried their progeny in the full-laden ark."*

This is a profound teaching about the continuity of God's mercy. The act of salvation is not an isolated event in the distant past; it is a thread that runs through the generations and reaches, in a sense, to every believer. The reader is not an idle spectator of Noah's deliverance; he or she is a beneficiary of it. The faith that made the ark a place of safety is the same faith that makes the present life a place of safety. The verse reminds the believer that he is not a self-made survivor but the heir of a long line of deliverances, carried by a mercy that has never abandoned the righteous.

**The Ark and the Ship as a Standing Sign**

The deliverance of Noah is not presented merely as ancient history; it is presented as a *sign*. In the Qur'an's vocabulary, a sign (āyah) is not a mere illustration but a proof — an event that testifies to a permanent truth about God and about the moral order. The ark is a sign of God's power to save, and of the necessity of faith and obedience to be saved. The flood is a sign of God's power to destroy those who reject the message and its bearer. Together, they form a complete lesson about the two possibilities that lie before every human being: to be carried by God's mercy, or to be overwhelmed by God's justice.

For the modern reader, the ark remains a sign in a world of obvious dangers. Modern humanity faces dangers of its own construction — environmental collapse, nuclear catastrophe, pandemics, the erosion of moral order. Yet the principle is constant: safety is not found in self-sufficient power but in obedience and trust in God. The ship of salvation sails upon the very waters that destroy the proud, because it has been built according to God's command and borne by God's mercy. Whatever one's circumstances, the choice is always the same: to build according to the command of the Lord and be carried, or to trust in one's own strength and be swallowed.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:12

> **that We might make it a reminder for you, and that attentive ears might take heed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Behind the Deliverance**

The preceding verse described the act of salvation; this verse states its purpose: *li-najʿalahā lakum tadhkiratan wa taʿiyaha udhunun wāʿiyah*. "That We might make it a reminder for you, and that attentive ears might take heed." The sentence is a declaration of divine intent. God saved the believers on the ark not merely out of favor but for a pedagogical purpose: to make the event a *reminder* (tadhkirah), a standing lesson, a permanent warning and encouragement. The word *tadhkirah* — "reminder" — is one of the Qur'an's favorite self-descriptions. The Qur'an calls itself a *tadhkirah* repeatedly, and it uses the word for the many historical events it narrates. A reminder is something that calls the mind back to a truth it already, in some deep sense, knows — hence the Qur'anic metaphor of awakening rather than of teaching something entirely new. The people who heard of Noah's deliverance already knew, or ought to have known, that God saves the righteous and destroys the wicked. The event serves to *awaken* that knowledge.

The word *tadhkirah* here can refer either to the ship itself or to the entire event of the Day of Judgment that the sūrah describes. Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr note both possibilities. In one reading, the *it* is the ship: the ark is preserved, in some sense, as a sign. In another reading, the *it* refers to the Day of Resurrection and Judgment that the sūrah is describing — the *ḥāqqah* itself is the great reminder. The two readings need not be mutually exclusive; indeed, the whole section is being woven into a single argument in which the historical deliverances of the past point forward to the great deliverance and judgment of the last Day.

**The Attentive Ear: The Organ of Faith**

The second half of the verse introduces a striking and specific image: *taʿiyaha udhunun wāʿiyah* — "that attentive ears might take heed." The word *wāʿiyah* — "attentive," "listening," "receiving" — is an active participle that describes the ear that truly hears, that takes in, that retains. It is not the ear that merely passes sound but the ear that *receives* it into the heart. The Qur'an distinguishes sharply between having ears and truly hearing, and it repeatedly criticizes those who have ears but do not hear (7:179; 25:73). Here it defines the opposite: the *wāʿiyah* ear is one that is not obstructed, not dulled by heedlessness, not hardened by pride — one that permits the reminder to enter and to transform.

The verse is closely akin to a verse in Sūrah 50: *"Truly in that is a reminder for whosoever has a heart, or gives ear as witness"* (50:37). The two verses together define the two organs of spiritual receptivity: the *heart* (qalb) that grasps meaning, and the *ear* (udhun) that receives it. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Zamakhsharī note that the "attentive ear" is the ear attached to a living heart — the ear that, when it hears the truth, does not let it slip away but holds it and ponders it.

**The Ear of ʿAlī and the Tradition of Reception**

The commentators, particularly al-Wāḥidī (W), Ibn Kathīr (IK), al-Qurṭubī (Q), al-Rāzī (R), and al-Ṭabarī (Ṭ) preserve traditions connecting this verse to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib. According to one account, the Prophet ﷺ said to ʿAlī: "God has commanded me to draw you closer to me and not exclude you, and that I teach you so that you understand; and God has taken it upon Himself that you understand." Then this verse was revealed. According to a more widespread account, when this verse was revealed, the Prophet said, *"I asked my Lord that He make it the ear of ʿAlī"* — meaning that he prayed that ʿAlī would be the one whose ear is *wāʿiyah*, the one who truly receives and retains. ʿAlī is then reported to have said: *"I never heard anything from the Messenger of God but that I remembered it."* (These reports are recorded in the works of al-Wāḥidī on the occasions of revelation and are cited by the major commentaries.)

Whatever the exact standing of these reports in the science of hadith — and the tradition records them with varying chains — the point they bear is a genuine one: the verse invites each believer to pray to become one whose *ear takes heed*. The person who reads the Qur'an and truly receives it is the person who has asked to be made receptive. This is not mere memorization; it is the discipline of listening — a deliberate, prayerful opening of the heart to the meaning that is being delivered. In a culture saturated with noise, the *wāʿiyah* ear is a profound spiritual achievement: the ear that is not drowned out, not distracted, not selective, but open, attentive, and ready to receive.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:13

> **Then when a single blast is blown in the trumpet**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn to the Cosmos**

The sūrah now leaves the historical panorama of past nations and shifts to the direct description of the Day itself. The connective *fa-idhā* — "Then when" — is important: it links the historical warnings to the cosmic event they were pointing toward. The catastrophes of ʿĀd and Thamūd and Pharaoh were prefigurings, foreshadows; the *single blast* is the reality. The verse reads: *fa-idhā nufikha fī al-ṣūri nafkhatun wāḥidah*. "Then when a single blast is blown in the trumpet." The word *nufikha* — "is blown" — is from the root *n-f-kh*, which means to blow with the mouth, to emit breath. The image is of breath entering a hollow instrument — the trumpet of the archangel. The word *ṣūr* — "trumpet" — is a natural form, a great horn. The Qur'an mentions the blowing of the trumpet in numerous sūrahs, but here it is emphatically a *single* blast: *nafkhatun wāḥidah* — one blow, one sound.

The commentators, following al-Bayḍāwī (Bḍ), al-Qurṭubī (Q), al-Rāzī (R), and al-Ṭabarī (Ṭ), are mostly of the view that this single blast is the *first* blow — the blast that will herald the end of the world, the "blast of the overpowering" that extinguishes all life and begins the dissolution of the cosmos. This is the blast that, according to the traditions, will be blown by the Archangel Isrāfīl (Seraphiel), the angel entrusted with the trumpet. Some commentators, notably Ibn Kathīr (IK) and the translators of the source (JJ), hold that the blast refers to the *second* blow — the one that will herald the Day of Resurrection, raising the dead from their graves. The ambiguity is not a defect, for the sūrah is describing a single momentous event in which the old order ends and the new begins, and the two blasts together bracket the entire eschaton.

**The Two Blasts and the Sound of Eternity**

The Qur'an has much to say about the two trumpet blasts, and the classical commentators discuss them in detail at 39:68 and 78:18. The first blast (on one understanding) is the *bash* that annihilates: *"And it will be blown in the trumpet, and whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on the earth will be stricken, save whom God wills"* (39:68). The second blast is the *resurrection*: *"Then it will be blown again, and behold, they will be standing, looking"* (39:68). The verse in al-Ḥāqqah, with its emphasis on a *single* blast (nafkhah wāḥidah), points most naturally, in the judgment of the majority of commentators, to the first blow — the blow of dissolution. The next verse confirms this reading, for it describes the earth and mountains being borne away and ground up, which belongs to the annihilating phase of the Day rather than to the resurrection proper.

The image of the blast is profoundly unsettling and yet deeply meaningful. Sound is, in some sense, the most immaterial of the senses; it can fill a space without occupying it, penetrate the body without touching it. The Qur'an uses sound as a medium of divine power — the command "Be!" (Kun) that creates, and now the blast that destroys. The *single* blast is also a reminder of the unity and decisiveness of the divine act. There is no partial destruction, no gradual fade, no negotiation. When God commands, the entire fabric of the present world is unfastened in a single breath.

**The Horror and the Hope of the Blast**

For the believer, the blast is at once a terror and a relief. It is a terror because it ends the life of the world and summons every human being to an accounting. It is a relief because it brings to a close the long, weary history of injustice, oppression, and denial. The Qur'anic picture of the Day is not a mere threat; it is the promise that the present order of things — in which the wicked often prosper and the righteous often suffer — will not be allowed to continue indefinitely. The blast is the moment at which the *ḥāqqah*, the reality that has been denied, finally and irrevocably *is*.

The verse also captures a fact that modern science has made vivid: the whole of the physical world is, in its deepest constitution, fragile and contingent. It is held together not by its own necessity but by the permission of its Creator. The same power that set the stars in their courses can, in a moment, collapse them. The believer who recites this verse in prayer is reminded that his or her security lies not in the permanence of the world but in the permanence of God. The most deeply rooted mountain, the most massive ocean, the most distant galaxy — all are held by a single permission, and the Day of the single blast will show that the permission was, all along, the only true reality.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:14

> **and the earth and mountains are borne away and ground up in a single grinding,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dissolution of the Solid**

The verse continues the description of the Day: *wa ḥumilati al-arḍu wa al-jibālu fa-dukkatā dakkatan wāḥidah*. "And the earth and mountains are borne away and ground up in a single grinding." The image is of the solid foundations of the world being lifted, carried away, and then pulverized. The mountains, which human beings regard as the most permanent, the most immovable features of the landscape, are here shown to be lifted like objects of no weight at all. The verb *ḥumilat* — "are borne away" — indicates that they are not merely disturbed; they are *carried*, suspended, displaced. The earth itself is borne away, losing all the stability that human beings take for granted.

The word *dukkatā* — "ground up" — is one of the most striking in the verse. It is the verb *dakka*, which means to crush, to pulverize, to reduce to dust, to strike with violence so as to flatten. The Qur'an uses it of the mountains being made like *"scattered dust"* (56:6) and of the mountain being *"struck"* in Sūrah 19:90 and elsewhere. The doubling here — *dakkan dakkah* — an infinitive cognate that strengthens the verb — indicates a single, complete, decisive grinding. It is not a crumbling over time but an instantaneous pulverization, a single act of reduction. The mountains that once held up the sky, that formed the firm backbone of the land, are ground into nothing.

**The Precision of the Cosmic Dissolution**

This is one of a remarkable cluster of Qur'anic passages that describe the fate of the mountains on the Day of Judgment. In Sūrah 27:88, the Qur'an says: *"And thou seest the mountains, which thou dost suppose are solid, pass away like clouds — the work of God, Who perfects all things."* In Sūrah 18:47, the mountains are said to be moved and the earth leveled. In Sūrah 52:10, the mountains are set in motion; in 70:8–9, the day when the sky is like molten brass and the mountains like tufted wool; in 73:14, when the mountains are violently shaken; in 78:20, when they are set in motion and become a mirage; in 81:3, when the mountains are set in motion; in 89:21, the earth is shivered; and in 101:5, the mountains become like carded wool. The repetition of this image across the Qur'an is itself an indication of its importance.

The repetition carries a profound teaching. Human beings, as the Qur'an and the prophets have always noted, are prone to find their security in the solid and the permanent. Mountains are the ultimate symbol of that permanence — the anchor of geography, the boundary of nations, the emblems of stability and duration. The Qur'an systematically dismantles this security by showing that what is most solid will be made most mobile, that what is most permanent will be shattered in an instant. The message is not morbid; it is liberating. It teaches the believer not to invest ultimate confidence in the world's apparent solidity — whether the solidity of wealth, of institutions, of reputation, of health, of the body — but to invest it in the One who is truly permanent and whose order cannot be shaken.

**From the Grinding to the Event**

The "single grinding" also anticipates the next element of the sūrah's eschatological sequence. If the earth and mountains are ground up and borne away, then the familiar landscape of human life — the fields, the cities, the roads, the graves — is entirely transformed. This is the precondition for the "Event" that is about to be named in the next verse. The Qur'an's eschatology is not a mere abstract theology; it is a vision of the complete remaking of reality. The world we know, with all its comforting permanences, will be entirely dissolved — not because God delights in destruction, but because the present world is a provisional order, a testing-ground, and it must give way to the world of the final reckoning where justice is perfectly executed.

For the believer, the message is one of profound and cleansing honesty. The things we cling to — the houses, the mountains of our achievement, the solid earth of our plans — are all, in the end, being carried away. This is not a statement of material denial but of spiritual reorientation. The believer is taught to build on what cannot be carried away: faith, justice, mercy, and the remembrance of God. As the earlier sūrahs put it, *"wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world, but that which endures — righteous deeds — are better in reward with thy Lord, and better as a source of hope"* (18:46). The grinding of the mountains is the ultimate confirmation of that truth.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:15

> **on that Day the Event shall befall,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Certainty of the Event**

The verse now states with unadorned simplicity: *fa-yawmaʾidhin waqaʿati al-wāqiʿah*. "On that Day the Event shall befall." The word *wāqiʿah* — "the Event" — is the feminine emphatic noun from the root *w-q-ʿ*, meaning to fall, to befall, to happen, to strike. It is the same word that names Sūrah 56 (al-Wāqiʿah), and the repetition is significant. The verse's simplicity is deceptive: it declares the *certainty* and *inevitability* of the culmination of the Day. The Event is the reality that the whole sūrah — indeed the whole eschatological teaching of the Qur'an — has been pointing toward. After the blast, after the grinding of the mountains and the bearing away of the earth, the Event befalls. It does not merely happen; it *befalls*, as something that overtakes and overwhelms.

The commentators, following the logic of the preceding passage, understand the "Event" here as the end of the world and the arrival of the Day of Resurrection. In Sūrah 56:1, the same phrase *idhā waqaʿati al-wāqiʿah* opens the chapter with a declaration of the Event's occurrence: *"When the Event befalls — and there is no denying its befalling — it lowers and raises."* (56:1–3). The Event is thus the great transition, the moment at which the order of this world ends and the order of the next begins. It "lowers and raises" — it brings down the proud and lifts up the humble, humbles the tyrants and exalts the righteous.

**The Event as the Climax of the Sūrah's Argument**

The placement of the verse is rhetorical. The sūrah has spent the earlier portion naming the destructions of past peoples, then describing the dissolution of the cosmos, and now it names the Event itself. The effect is cumulative: the reader who has followed the history and the cosmology is now confronted with the actuality. The historical catastrophes and the cosmic convulsions are all *prefigurations* of this one Event. The events in the lives of ʿĀd and Thamūd and Pharaoh were "events" in the limited sense — they happened once and then passed. But this Event is the event of all events, the one toward which all history moves, the one that gives meaning to all that came before.

The theological point is crucial. The Qur'an does not view history as a meaningless cycle or a random accumulation of happenings. It views history as a journey toward a destination. The Event is not an interruption of history but its *completion*. The sūrah is insisting that human life and human history have a direction and a purpose, and that they will culminate in a moment of final reckoning when every moral debt is settled, every act is weighed, and every soul is given the justice that is its due.

**The Practical Meaning of an Event That Will Befall**

For the believer, the declaration that the Event *shall befall* is a statement of total trust. It is the promise that the apparent chaos and injustice of the world will not be the final word. The tyrant who dies unpunished, the oppressor who escapes the consequences of his cruelty, the innocent who suffocates in silence — these are not the end. An Event is coming that will rewrite every account. This is not escapism; it is the ground of moral effort. A person who believes that injustice will finally be rectified has a powerful motive to fight for justice in the present. A person who believes that there is no final account has no reason other than self-interest to resist tyranny.

The verse also calls the believer to a particular readiness. Because the Event is certain but its timing is unknown, the Qur'an consistently exhorts to watchfulness: *"So wait for the Day when the sky brings a visible smoke"* (44:10), and to the Prophet ﷺ is attributed the counsel to be prepared at all times. This is not an anxious watchfulness but a settled readiness — the recognition that one's life is not a rehearsal but the real thing, and that each day is lived in view of the Event. The believer who lives in this way does not fear the Event; he or she *awaits* it, because it is the fulfillment of everything that faith has been building toward.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:16

> **the sky shall be rent asunder; for that Day it shall be frail.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Heaven That Shall Be Rent**

The verse turns from the earth to the sky: *wa inshaqqati al-samāʾu fa-hiya yawmaʾidhin wāhiyah*. "The sky shall be rent asunder; for that Day it shall be frail." The verb *inshaqqat* — "is rent asunder" — comes from the root *sh-q-q*, meaning to split, to cleave, to tear open. The image is of the firmament, which human beings perceive as a unified, unbroken dome above the earth, being torn apart. The Qur'an uses this image in several places: *"The sky shall be rent asunder"* in 55:37, 73:18, and 84:1; and *"the sky shall split open"* in 82:1; and it connects the rending of the sky to the Day of Judgment in 25:25 and 42:5. The image is one of a universal dissolution. If the earth is ground up and the sky is torn asunder, then the entire structure of the perceptible world — the stage on which human life is played out — is being dismantled.

The word *wāhiyah* — "frail" — is deeply suggestive. It comes from the root *w-h-y*, which itself contains the sense of weakness, frailty, slackness, and decrepitude. A *wāhiyah* thing is one that has become weak and will inevitably collapse. The word is used in the Qur'an for the roof of a house that is on the point of falling (18:13) and for the walls of a building that are about to collapse. Applied to the sky, the word is striking: the sky, which human beings regard as the most vast and enduring of all the things they see, is declared to be *frail* on that Day. The same sky that is the emblem of distance, permanence, and reach is revealed as a veil that will be torn.

**The Frailty of All That Seems Permanent**

The theological teaching is unmistakable. Human beings are prone to find a false security in the vastness and apparent permanence of the natural world. The stars, the heavens, the sea, the mountains — these seem to be beyond all change, all decay, all insecurity. But the Qur'an systematically reveals that all of this, being created, is subject to its Creator's command and has within it no permanent substance. The sky is frail because it is *created*; its strength is borrowed. It holds together only as long as God wills it to hold together.

There is a deep analogy here with human life. The human being, too, often seems strong and secure — a body, a mind, a fortune, a reputation. But all of these are, like the sky, *frail*. They are held together by a borrowed strength. The person who knows this does not live in anxiety but in wisdom. Knowing that the created world is fragile, the believer builds his or her hope on the Uncreated, on the One who does not change, whose covenant does not fail, whose mercy is not subject to decay. The rending of the sky is the final demonstration that only God is the true refuge.

**The Rending of the Sky as the Opening to the Divine**

The sky being rent asunder also has a positive dimension in the Qur'anic vision. In Sūrah 78:19, the sky is described as being opened *"as if it were gates."* The rending of the sky is not merely destruction; it is the opening of the veil between the present world and the unseen. Beyond the torn sky, the believer glimpses the throne of the Lord, the angels, and the reality of the Hereafter. The image of gates being opened suggests access rather than closure — the world is not ending in a vacuum but opening onto a greater and more real existence.

This is the eschatological hope that underlies the terror of the Day. The dissolution of the present world is not the end of meaning but the beginning of its fullest realization. The sky that limited human vision is torn apart so that the human being may finally see what is beyond it. The frail sky gives way to the realm of the divine, where every mystery is resolved and every veil is lifted. For the believer who has lived in readiness, this is not a horror but a homecoming.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:17

> **And the angels shall be at its sides; that Day eight shall carry the Throne of thy Lord above them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Angels at the Sides of the Rent Sky**

The verse now presents a scene of monumental grandeur: *wa al-malaku ʿalā arjāʾihā wa yaḥmilu ʿarsha rabbika fawqahum yawmaʾidhin thamāniyah*. "And the angels shall be at its sides; that Day eight shall carry the Throne of thy Lord above them." The phrase *ʿalā arjāʾihā* — "at its sides" — is rendered by some as "at its ends." The word *arjāʾ* is a plural form meaning the sides, the edges, the extremities of something. The angels, in their multitudes, are described as standing at the boundary of the torn sky, at the edges of the dissolving cosmos. They are present as witnesses to the divine order and as bearers of the divine Throne. On that Day, the veil between the celestial and the earthly is removed, and the angels are seen in their true function.

The verse's focus then narrows to the Throne of the Lord — *ʿarsha rabbika* — the Throne of *your* Lord. The personal pronoun in "your Lord" is an address to the Prophet ﷺ, and it has a tender intimacy. The sūrah, which has been describing the terrors of the Day and the destruction of the world, now points to the Throne of *thy* Lord. The reader is reminded that the One who is dismantling the world is the same One who is the Lord of the messenger and of the believer. The power that grinds the mountains is the power that protects the righteous. The terror of the Day is the mercy of the Lord of the believers.

**The Eight Who Carry the Throne**

The mention of *thamāniyah* — "eight" — has been the subject of sustained discussion among the commentators. The majority, following Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī, understand the number to refer to eight angels who carry the Throne of God. This is supported by the traditions. A ḥadīth is reported — cited by al-Bayḍāwī (Bḍ), al-Qurṭubī (Q), al-Rāzī (R), and al-Suyūṭī (Sy) — in which the Prophet ﷺ is said to have described the number of the Throne-bearers: *"Today those carrying the Throne are four, but when the Day of Resurrection comes, God supports them with four more; so they will be eight."* (This is recorded in the hadith literature and cited across the tafsīr tradition.) Some commentators, such as al-Rāzī, note that "eight" might also be read as "eight thousand," and that it could refer to individuals, to ranks, or to rows of angels. Al-Zamakhsharī likewise allows this wider reading. But the plain reading, supported by the hadith, is eight.

The number eight has a symbolic value in Islamic geometry and cosmology. As the commentators note, the octagon forms the transition from the square — which represents the earth, the four elements, the four directions — to the circle of the dome or the sphere, which represents Heaven. The octagon is the point of transition from the earthly to the celestial. It is thus significant, the commentators observe, that the sūrah moves progressively from the lowest level — the earth being ground up in verse 14 — up through the rent sky in verse 16, and finally to the highest level, the Throne of the Lord borne by the eight in verse 17. The sūrah's eschatology is a vertical ascent: from the dust of the earth, through the torn heavens, to the Throne of the Lord.

**The Throne and the Sovereignty of God**

The "Throne" (ʿarsh) in Islamic theology is the greatest of God's creations, and the Qur'an speaks of it frequently — *"the Lord of the Throne"* (17:42, 23:86), *"the Merciful, established upon the Throne"* (20:5), and the bearing of the Throne by the angels (40:7). The Qur'an is careful to establish that the Throne is a *created* thing — the angels carry it — while God Himself is utterly beyond all spatial and creaturely limitation. The verse is thus a window into the cosmos of the Divine Kingdom: a hierarchy of creation, with the angels at the heights, bearing the Throne, beneath their Lord. The image is one of perfect order, power, and glory.

For the believer, the scene is a comfort in the midst of dissolution. The world may be ground up and the sky may be rent, but the Throne of the Lord is *carried* — it is held aloft, secure. The order of the Divine Kingdom is not affected by the collapse of the material world. The throne is not shaken; the angels do not falter; the Lord is not in any need of the world that He is dismantling. The believer's security is not in the earth below, which is being ground up, nor in the sky above, which is being rent, but in the Throne above all, which is being borne by the angels of the Lord. The verse is thus a profound statement of where true security lies.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:18

> **That Day you shall be exposed; no secret of yours shall be hidden.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Arraignment and the Exposure**

The sūrah now turns from the cosmic drama to the human subject. After the earth is ground up, the sky rent, and the angels ranged at the borders of the Throne, the human being is brought forward: *yawmaʾidhin tuʿraḍūna lā takhfā minkum khāfiyah*. "That Day you shall be exposed; no secret of yours shall be hidden." The verb *tuʿraḍūn* — "you shall be exposed" — is from the root *ʿ-r-ḍ*, and it carries the sense of presenting, bringing forth, laying open, and also of offering and displaying. In its passive form here, it means to be *brought forth* and *laid open*. Al-Rāzī notes that the word could also be rendered "you shall be brought forth," and he connects it to the verse in Sūrah 11:18: *"And who does greater wrong than one who fabricates a lie against God? It is they who will be brought before their Lord."* The Day is thus a great *arraignment* — a bringing of all human beings before their Lord, in full view, with nothing concealed.

The word *tuʿraḍūn* is particularly important because it establishes the theological ground of the judgment: the exposure is something God does, and it is total. The human being does not choose to be exposed; the exposure is the act of the Divine, and it is complete. This is in contrast to the life of this world, in which human beings are able to conceal their inner states, their motives, their secret deeds, their private thoughts. The Day is the moment at which the veil of concealment is entirely removed.

**The Three Arraignments**

The commentators preserve a tradition that describes the way the people are brought before their Lord. A ḥadīth is reported — cited by Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī — concerning the three arraignments: *"The people are brought forth on the Day of Resurrection for three arraignments (ʿarḍāt): during the first two arraignments they will argue and make excuses; at the third, the scrolls will be cast into their hands, so that some will take with their right hand and others will take with their left."* (This is recorded in the traditions and cited across the tafsīr.) The picture is vivid and grave: first the argument and the excuse (the human being still hoping to negotiate, to explain, to justify), and then the decisive moment when the record of deeds is placed in the hand. The three arraignments correspond to the deepening stages of the reckoning, and they culminate in the deliverance of the book.

The second phrase, *lā takhfā minkum khāfiyah* — "no secret of yours shall be hidden" — is packed with meaning. The word *khāfiyah* is a subtle noun meaning "a secret thing" or "something hidden." The phrase states decisively that nothing from among the hidden things — the secrets, the private acts, the concealed intents — will remain hidden on that Day. The Qur'an makes this point repeatedly: *"the Day when they come forth with naught concerning them hidden from God"* (40:16), and the limbs themselves will testify (24:24; 36:65; 41:20). Al-Rāzī notes two levels of this exposure. The first is the theological fact that nothing is hidden *from God* — that all of it was always fully known to Him, and now it is made manifest. The second is that the *inner states* of human beings — the truth of what they were, their real motives, their genuine condition — will be made known, with the result that the believers will rejoice and the disbelievers will grieve. Alternatively, al-Qurṭubī notes the phrase can be rendered, *"None among you shall be hidden"* — meaning that no human being can evade the judgment, no one can slip away, no one can be absent from the assembly.

**The Intimacy and the Irony of the Exposure**

For the believer, the verse is a profound source of both fear and consolation. There is fear in the acknowledgement that nothing is hidden — that every private sin, every concealed hypocrisy, every secret cruelty will be laid open. But there is also great consolation: everything good and hidden, every secret act of charity, every private prayer, every suppressed tear shed in secret for God's sake, will also be laid open and rewarded. The believer who has lived in this world concealing his or her good works from the eyes of others on the model of the Prophet's teaching (*"When you give charity, let not your left hand know what your right hand does"*) will find that the hidden good is not lost but preserved and now displayed.

There is also a deeper and more psychological point. Human beings are often engaged in an enormous effort of self-concealment in this life. We present certain faces to certain audiences; we manage our reputations; we hide our fears, our failures, our secret hopes. The Day removes the need for, and the possibility of, this elaborate performance. At the same time, the removal of concealment is a kind of freedom. The human being is finally, completely, and honestly known — and, in the case of the believer, known and loved in a way that the hidden sins are forgiven and the hidden good is celebrated. The believer is not exposed to be destroyed; he or she is exposed to be received.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:19

> **As for one who is given his book in his right hand, he will say, "Here, read my book.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Book and the Right Hand**

The sūrah now arrives at the moral heart of the chapter: the division of humanity into those who receive their book in the right hand and those who receive it in the left. The verse reads: *fa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahu bi-yamīnihi fa-yaqūlu hāʾumu iqraʾū kitābīh*. "As for one who is given his book in his right hand, he will say, 'Here, read my book.'" The *book* (kitāb) on the Day of Judgment is the record of deeds in which every act of the person's life has been written. The Qur'an describes it elsewhere: *"And the Book will be set in place, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it"* (18:49); *"and every human being we will cause to be bound to him his fate, and on the Day of Resurrection We shall bring forth to him a book which he finds spread open"* (17:71); *"and whoever is given his book in his right hand, he will be reckoned with an easy reckoning"* (84:7–8).

The *right hand* is richly symbolic. Throughout the Qur'an, the right is associated with blessing, honor, precedence, and the company of the righteous. The term used for the people of the right hand is *aṣḥāb al-yamīn* — "the companions of the right" (56:27, 38). The left, by contrast, is associated with the book being given *from behind the back* (84:10–12), with constriction, disadvantage, and the company of the left (*aṣḥāb al-shimāl*, 56:41). The right hand here signifies honor, proximity to God, and the announcement of good. The person who receives the book in the right hand is one whose record is good, whose life was led in faith and obedience, and who is now welcomed.

**The Joyful Demand: "Here, Read My Book"**

The words of the recipient are striking and full of joy: *hāʾumu iqraʾū kitābīh* — "Here, read my book." Literally, it is an eager, triumphant invitation: "Come, here! Take and read my book." There is no fear in this person's voice, no hesitation, no reluctance. Whereas the person given the book in the left hand will cry out in desperate pain (as the next verses describe), the person given the book in the right hand *wants* it read. This is a profound indicator of the condition of the soul. The righteous person is not ashamed of his or her record. The person who lives a life of integrity has no fear that the record will be exposed, because the record is, in fact, the account of a life lived in fidelity — and the person knows that God's mercy has covered the inevitable imperfections.

There is also a remarkable reversal here that the commentators note. In this world, people are often eager to *conceal* their deeds, especially from public scrutiny. On the Day, the believer is eager to *display* them. The transformation is the result of the character of the deeds themselves. The believer's deeds were done for God, and they are now displayed before God — the very One for whom they were done. The person who performed an act of charity for the sake of a secret reward now wishes that all could see the reward; the person who gave in secret now finds the secret in full view. The desire to have the book read is the natural extension of a life that was lived knowingly in the presence of God.

**The Psychological Portrait of the Saved**

The portrait that the sūrah paints of the person of the right hand is one of confidence, joy, and peace. The confidence is not arrogance; it is the settled ease of a person who has done what could be done and trusted the rest to God. The joy is not worldly; it is the deep joy of a soul whose whole life is finally, verifiably, authenticated. There is a lesson here about the psychology of the believer. The person who lives rightly in this world tends to develop a kind of inner calm, a freedom from the exhausting work of self-justification and image management. Because the believer's worth is not measured by the opinion of others but by the record that God keeps, the believer is free to live with honesty and to face the disclosure of that record without fear.

The verse also has an important modern resonance. In a culture that is obsessed with external approval, with privacy, with reputation management, with the endless curation of a public image, this verse offers a profound reorientation. It asks the person to consider: what would it be like to be completely known, completely exposed, and completely unafraid? That is the condition of the righteous on the Day. And it is a condition that is built, here and now, by living without concealment — by being honest with oneself and with God in this life.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:20

> **Truly I knew for certain that I would meet my reckoning."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Foundation of the Confidence**

The second phrase spoken by the person of the right hand explains the source of his confidence: *innī ẓanantu annī mulāqin ḥisābīh*. "Truly I knew for certain that I would meet my reckoning." The word *ẓanantu* is the key and has been a subject of careful discussion among the lexicographers and commentators. In classical Arabic, *ẓann* can carry two opposite senses. In one sense, it means "opinion" or "supposition" — and can even convey doubt or uncertainty. In another sense, it means knowledge, certainty, and conviction. The translator of the source has rendered it, following the majority of the commentators in this context, as "I knew for certain." The Quranic use of *ẓann* in this sense is attested: *"Those who are certain that they are to meet their Lord"* (2:249; 6:158) uses the same root in the confident sense, and the word is used of the Prophet Abraham in his seeking of certainty (2:260).

The meaning is plain: the person given the book in the right hand was *certain* — while still living in this world — that he or she would meet the reckoning. This certainty informed the person's entire life. The person did not live as though death might not come, nor as though the accounting might not happen. The person lived consciously in the presence of the Day, and that awareness shaped every action. This is the central quality that distinguished the person of the right hand.

**Certainty as a Way of Life**

The verse is thus a complete description of the psychology of the believer. The believer stands in this world and carries within the mind the unshaken conviction that a reckoning will come. This is not a morbid preoccupation; it is a decisive orientation of the soul. It is like the difference between two travelers. One travels unaware, taking gifts as if they would last forever, certain that there is no final destination. The other travels with the knowledge that the journey ends, and that a welcome, or a door, awaits at the end. The second traveler arranges his baggage differently; he saves for the end, he prepares, he hurries not in panic but in purpose.

The source records the Prophet's ﷺ answer about the most intelligent of believers, which aligns directly with this verse. When asked, *"Who are the most intelligent of believers?"* the Prophet replied — as recorded in the tradition cited at this verse:

> *"The most frequent in recalling death, and the best prepared for what follows it; they are the most intelligent."* (transmitted from Ibn ʿUmar; Ibn Mājah 4252 and related)

The person who remembers death is not a person weighed down by depression. The person who remembers death and prepares for what follows it is precisely the person who lives this life *most* fully and meaningfully. The certainty of the reckoning is not an enemy to a good life; it is the enabling condition of it. When the reckoning is real, then the choice to do good is real; when the choice to do good is real, then the good is genuinely significant. The certainty transforms a life of mere pleasure and survival into a life of purpose and striving.

**The Reading of "Doubt" and the Merciful Alternative**

Al-Rāzī and others note that the alternative sense of *ẓann* — as doubt or uncertainty — opens an alternate reading, one that yields a merciful interpretation. If the word here means not "certainty" but "expectation in the face of uncertainty," the verse would speak of a person who was *not entirely certain* of the final outcome, who had a measure of unease about his or her standing, and who nevertheless followed the commands of God and His Messenger, doing what was required and trusting the rest to God. On this reading, the person is rewarded not only for perfect certainty but for faithfulness under imperfect certainty. This is a profound encouragement for the ordinary believer, who often struggles with doubt and uncertainty about where he or she stands. The verse, read in this way, teaches that the essential thing is not the absence of doubt but the presence of obedience. Even the uncertain, the anxious, the one who wavers — if they act with fidelity and put their trust in God — are welcomed among the people of the right hand.

This dual reading is characteristic of a deep and merciful scripture. The Qur'an speaks at once to the person of profound certitude — whose life has been shaped by unshakable conviction — and to the person of ordinary faith — whose life is a struggle toward certainty. Both are described as belonging to the right hand, and the difference between them is measured not by the strength of the conviction but by the reality of the obedience. It is a message of great hope to every believer who has ever asked, in the privacy of the heart, "Am I truly one of them?"

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:21

> **So he shall enjoy a life contenting,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fruit of the Certainty**

The sūrah now describes the reward of the person of the right hand. After his confident and joyful declaration, the verdict is pronounced: *fa-huwa fī ʿīshatin rāḍiyah*. "So he shall enjoy a life contenting." The word *ʿīshah* — "life" — here refers to a manner or state of living, a mode of existence. The word *rāḍiyah* — "contenting" or "satisfying" — is the active participle of *raḍiya*, "to be content, to be pleased, to be satisfied." The phrase *ʿīshatin rāḍiyah* is commonly rendered "a life of contentment," "a life that is pleasing," or "a life that is contented and satisfying." The image is of a life that fully satisfies, that is not marred by discontent, that brings the soul to a state of peace.

The pairing of words is subtle. Al-Rāzī and others note that *rāḍiyah* can be taken in two ways: the life that is *contenting* (it gives content to the person who lives it), or the life that is *contented* (the person who lives it is content). Both senses converge: it is a life in which the soul is fully at peace, because all the longings that this world could never satisfy are finally fulfilled. The life of the Garden is not primarily a life of objects, of physical pleasures, of material abundance — although the sūrah will go on to describe those — it is fundamentally a life of *satisfaction*, a life in which the restless heart is finally quieted.

**The Contentment That the World Cannot Give**

The verse speaks to one of the deepest truths of human experience. Human beings are restless; they seek satisfaction in an endless series of things — in wealth, in pleasure, in achievement, in reputation, in novelty. Yet none of these fully contents the soul. The person who acquires all that he or she can imagine frequently discovers that the acquisition did not bring the contentment that was promised. The Qur'an names this restlessness and its cure. The contentment of the Hereafter is not the pleasure of a moment but the peace of a settled condition. It is the life in which the soul's true object — God Himself — is finally and fully realized.

There is a verse in Sūrah 89 that captures this contentment in another way: the address to the righteous soul on the Day — *"O soul at peace! Return to thy Lord, content (rāḍiyah) and contenting (mardiyyah)"* (89:27–28). The same root, *r-ḍ-y*, runs through both passages. The soul that is *rāḍiyah* (content) is also one that is *mardiyyah* (well-pleasing to God). The contenting life and the pleasing life are the same life. The believer's peace and God's good pleasure are not two separate rewards but one and the same state.

**The Connection to Life in This World**

The verse also has a transformative implication for present life. The believer is taught that the satisfaction of the soul is not finally dependent on the conditions of this world. The person who has faith and lives rightly will find a measure of contentment here as well — not because the world is perfect, but because the believer's identity is not dependent on the world. When a person's deepest hope and trust are in God, then the fluctuations of worldly fortune — the loss of a job, a disappointment, a setback, a sorrow — do not destroy the person's peace. The believer can suffer and still be at peace, because the source of the peace is not in the circumstances but in the One who is greater than the circumstances.

The "life contenting" is thus both a promise for the Hereafter and a teaching for the present. The believer who lives in the awareness of the reckoning, who does good and trusts in God, already tastes something of the contentment that will be perfected in the Garden. And the believer who does not taste it in this world is promised that it is not lost but stored, to be given in full on the Day.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:22

> **in a lofty Garden**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Garden That Is High and Noble**

The sūrah now locates the contenting life: *fī jannatin ʿāliyah*. "In a lofty Garden." The word *jannah* — "Garden" — is the Qur'an's principal name for Paradise. Its root meaning is "covered" or "hidden," from the sense of a garden whose foliage covers and conceals the earth beneath it, giving shelter and shade. The *jannah* is thus a place of abundance, verdancy, and protection. The adjective *ʿāliyah* — "lofty" — comes from the root *ʿ-l-w*, meaning to be high, exalted, elevated. The Garden is described as *high* and *exalted*, suggesting both physical elevation and moral and spiritual eminence.

The commentators note the significance of this elevation. The Garden is described as *lofty* to distinguish it from the matters of this world, which are low and base. The things of this world — its pleasures, its goods, its honors — are, in the Qur'an's scale, "low" (danī). The Garden, by contrast, is high and exalted. The one who is given the book in the right hand is raised to a high station. The description of the Garden as *ʿāliyah* thus points to the exaltation of the believers themselves — their promotion to a rank above all that is earthly and base.

**The Garden as the Highest Place**

In the eschatological geography of the Qur'an, the Garden is not merely a horizontal destination but a *vertical* one. It is described in terms of ascent: it is above, it is elevated, it is near to the divine Throne. The sūrah's own structure, moving from the grinding of the earth, through the rent sky, to the Throne of the Lord borne by the angels, has been a movement of ascent. The Garden is located at the summit of that ascent. The believer who was carried through the dissolution of the world is not lost in the process; he or she is *raised*.

The phrase also carries a note of intimacy. The Garden is the *lofty* Garden, the Garden of the divine presence. In another verse, the Qur'an describes the proximity of the righteous — *"the companions of the right"* — as being *"in gardens of delight"* (56:27), and it repeatedly mentions the Garden as a place of peace and nearness to God (89:28–30). The elevation is not merely physical grandeur; it is the elevation of the soul into the presence of its Lord. The Garden is *lofty* because it is where the believer is closest to God, where the veil between the servant and the Lord is thinnest, and where the soul's deepest aspiration — to be near God, to know Him, to be received by Him — is fulfilled.

**Loftiness as a Measure of Value**

There is a practical and moral dimension to the word *ʿāliyah*. The Qur'an uses the contrast between high and low to teach about value. The person who lives for the low things of this world — for wealth, for status, for pleasure alone — has oriented his or her life downward. The person who lives for God, for justice, for mercy, for the Garden, has oriented his or her life upward. The adjective *ʿāliyah* is thus both a description of a place and an invitation to a mode of life. The believer is called to set his or her sights high, to pursue what is genuinely exalted, and to resist the gravitational pull of the low.

In modern terms, the verse speaks against the reduction of life to the satisfactions of the moment. The consumer culture of the modern world is, in ecursed, a culture of the *low* — of novelty, of sensation, of accumulation, of comfort. The verse invites the person to imagine a *high* life — a life oriented toward what is truly lofty, what is genuinely excellent, what is worthy of the human soul. The loftiness of the Garden is the measure of what the human being is actually for: not to be contented with the low, but to be raised to the high.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:23

> **with low-hanging clusters.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fruits Within Easy Reach**

The sūrah continues the description of the Garden: *quṭūfuhā dāniyah*. "With low-hanging clusters." The word *quṭūf* is the plural of *qaṭf*, meaning clusters of fruit, especially bunches of grapes and other hanging fruits. The word *dāniyah* comes from the root *d-n-w*, meaning to be near, close, at hand. The clusters are described as *near* and *low-hanging* — so close that they are easily reached by the people of the Garden. Al-Ṭabarī explains that this indicates the fruits are readily accessible and that no one will be turned away from them; no one will have to struggle, climb, or reach far to obtain nothing. The bounty of the Garden is *proximate* and *immediate*.

This image is repeated in the Qur'an in several places. In Sūrah 55:54, the Garden is described as having fruits *"near at hand."* In Sūrah 76:14, it says: *"Its shade shall be close above them, and its clusters shall be made to hang low."* The image of the low-hanging clusters is an image of the *ease* of the Garden. There is no toil, no difficulty, no competition, no scarcity. The fruits are not high on a tall tree requiring a ladder; they are within reach, in abundance, at all times.

**The End of Toil and the Ease of the Garden**

The image carries a profound theological and psychological message. This world, the Qur'an teaches, is a place of toil and effort — *"We have surely created man in toil"* (90:4). The life of the present is characterized by struggle: the farmer wrestles with the soil, the merchant with the market, the worker with the day, the seeker with the uncertain. Even the pleasures of this world require effort and have limits: hunger returns after the meal, thirst after the drink, the pleasure ends and the need returns.

The Garden is the place where toil ends. The fruits are low-hanging; they are near; they are abundant. The pleasures of the Garden do not require the exhausting labor of this world, nor do they end. The image of the low-hanging clusters is thus a promise of rest and ease — a rest not of idleness but of perfect satisfaction, the ease of a soul that has arrived and needs to strive no more.

The tradition records for this verse a ḥadīth, cited by the commentators at 22–23, in which the Prophet ﷺ described having seen the Garden: *"Verily I saw the Garden and came upon a cluster of grapes. Had I taken it, you would have eaten of it so long as the world remained."* (Recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from the vision of the Prophet and reported across the tafsīr.) The cluster was so abundant that had it been taken, its fruit would have fed everyone for the entire duration of the world. This gives a sense of the measureless abundance of the Garden — abundance not of scarcity but of overflow.

**The Proximity as an Image of Divine Generosity**

The low-hanging clusters also speak of the generosity of God. The pleasures of the Garden are described in terms that are entirely accessible — within reach, at hand, near. There is nothing in the Garden that is withheld, nothing that is beyond the creatures's capacity, nothing that requires an impossible payment. The image is the opposite of the way the pleasures of this world are often distributed — rare, competitive, expensive, and unequally shared. In the Garden, God's generosity is unbounded and unhindered.

This has a bearing on how the believer views God in this world. The God who makes the clusters low-hanging in the Garden is the same God who is generous in this world. The Qur'an repeatedly teaches that God's mercy encompasses all things, that God's provision is for every creature, and that the good of this world is only a foretaste of the goodness of the next. The believer who appreciates the beauty of a ripe fruit or the refreshment of water or the shade of a tree is tasting, in a small way, the generosity that will be made perfect in the Garden. The low-hanging clusters are a reminder of the nature of the One who gives: He does not give grudgingly, nor sparingly, nor at arm's length. He gives from near, in abundance, and with delight.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:24

> **Eat and drink in enjoyment for that which you did in the past in days gone by.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Invitation to Enjoyment**

The sūrah now records the welcome that the people of the Garden receive: *kulū wa-ishrabū hanīʾan bimā aslaftum fī al-ayyāmi al-khāliyah*. "Eat and drink in enjoyment for that which you did in the past in days gone by." The command *kulū wa-ishrabū* — "Eat and drink" — is an imperative of honor and welcome. It is God himself, or the angels, or the voice of welcome, that bids the believers to eat and drink. The word *hanīʾan* — "in enjoyment" — is a rich term. It comes from the root *h-n-ʾ*, and it carries the sense of things that are wholesome, palatable, agreeable, and free from any harm or pain. The eating and drinking of the Garden are not merely physical; they are a delight, a source of pleasure and well-being, unalloyed by the burdens that accompany the pleasures of this world.

The phrase *bimā aslaftum* — "for that which you did in the past" — explains the basis of the reward. The enjoyment is given *because of what was done before*. The word *aslaftum* comes from the root *s-l-f*, meaning to proceed, to precede, to go before, and it carries the sense of *sending ahead* — of deeds done in this world that are, in effect, sent ahead to the next. This is a profound image. The deeds of this world are not left behind at death; they are *preceded* into the Hereafter. The believer who does good in this world is, in the exact sense of the word, *sending ahead* provision that will be received on the arrival.

**The "Days Gone By" and the Days of Fasting**

The phrase *al-ayyāmi al-khāliyah* — "days gone by" — has a rich ambiguity. Literally it means "the days that have passed," the days that have gone by, referring to the days of the person's life in this world. But the word *khāliyah* also carries the sense of "empty" or "hollow" — the same root as the "hollowed palm trunks" of verse 7 (*aʿjāz nakhlin khāwiyah*). This opens a striking interpretation. Al-Rāzī and al-Suyūṭī note that, read in this way, the phrase can be understood as a reference to the *days of fasting* — the days that were deliberately made "empty" of food and drink for the sake of God. The believer who fasted — who emptied his or her days of worldly consumption — is now invited to eat and drink in enjoyment, because those empty days are the very cause of this fullness.

This is a beautiful and meaningful reading. The life of the believer is one of discipline and self-restraint in this world. The believer fasts, gives, prays, and limits the indulgence of the appetites. This is not a denial of the goodness of creation but a discipline of it. The believer who keeps the fast learns to be the master of hunger rather than its slave. And the reward of that discipline is the fullness of the Garden, in which the pleasure is not merely permitted but *invited* — not merely allowed but made the very substance of the welcome.

**The Enjoyment That Is the Wages of Faith**

The verse makes explicit a principle that governs the whole of the Qur'an's eschatology: the reward of the Hereafter is not arbitrary, nor is it a free gift disconnected from human action. It is a *return* — a remuneration, a wage, a consequence. The believers are told to eat and drink "for that which you did in the past." The word *bimā* — "for that which" — establishes a causal connection between the deed and the reward. In Sūrah 52:19 and 77:43, the same formulation appears: *"Eat and drink in enjoyment for that which you used to do."* The reward is the outcome of the deed, a justice that God has established and will not violate.

This is not to reduce the reward to a mere transaction, as though the believer earned Paradise as a wage commensurate with the deed. The Qur'an elsewhere insists that no human deed can truly earn God's mercy, and that entrance into Paradise is by God's grace. But it also insists that the grace is not arbitrary: it is given through means, in response to deeds, in the form of a moral order in which good and evil are real and have consequences. The verse holds together both the sovereignty of God's grace and the reality of human accountability. The believer is welcomed to eat and drink "for that which you did" — not because the deed *earned* the Garden, but because the deed *qualified* the person for the Garden's welcome, and because God, in His justice and mercy, honors the moral order He has established.

**A Life of Preparation for Days of Fullness**

The practical lesson is plain and urgent. The days of this life are, in a very real sense, the days in which the person prepares for the days of the next. The believer who lives these days in faith, in fasting, in charity, in patience, and in remembrance is *sending ahead* provision. The believer who lives these days in heedlessness — in indulgence, in selfishness, in the pursuit of the low — is, at the same time, emptying the storehouse of the next. The sūrah's invitation to eat and drink in enjoyment is thus an invitation that is *functional*: it reflects what the person did to deserve it. The person who wishes to hear those words of welcome at the end must live now in a way that makes them true.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:25

> **And as for one who is given his book in his left hand, he will say, "Would that I had not been given my book.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Diptych Completed: The Left Hand**

The sūrah now completes the diptych by turning to the opposite fate. The verse reads: *wa ammā man ūtiya kitābahu bi-shimālihi fa-yaqūlu yā laytanī lam ūta kitābīh*. "And as for one who is given his book in his left hand, he will say, 'Would that I had not been given my book.'" The contrast with the previous passage is total and deliberate. The person of the right hand *eagerly* invites others to read his book; the person of the left hand *wishes he had never been given it at all*. The *left hand* — as opposed to the right — is the sign of dishonor, disadvantage, and exclusion. The Qur'an contrasts the two in Sūrah 56:8–9, where the companions of the right and the companions of the left are separated, and in Sūrah 84:10, where the book is given *"from behind his back."*

The person given the book in the left hand is described with one of the most poignant expressions of anguish in the Qur'an: *yā laytanī lam ūta kitābīh* — "O would that I had not been given my book." The phrase *yā laytanī* — "would that I" — is an expression of longing for an impossible state, a regret for the past that cannot be undone. The person does not merely grieve; he *wishes away* even the receipt of the record. This is a profound indication of the depth of the shame. The person is so overwhelmed by the horror and guilt of what the book contains that the very act of being handed the book is a torment worse than any physical punishment.

**The Reconciliation of the Two Passages**

The commentators note an apparent tension between this verse and the passage in Sūrah 84, where the person given the book *from behind his back* is described as *"crying out for destruction"* and *"entering a Blaze."* How do the two images — the left hand and behind the back — relate? The majority of commentators, following Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Zamakhsharī in their discussion of Sūrah 84, reconcile them by noting that the person's left hand will be tied behind his back. The book is given in the left hand, but the left hand is bound behind the back, so that the giving of the book is both from the left and from behind. This is a profoundly uncomfortable image. The person who in this world reached for power and pleasure with his left hand — as it were, grasping at the low and the base — finds his hand bound behind his back and his record thrust upon him from behind.

**The Shame That Is Greater Than the Fire**

Al-Rāzī records a striking interpretation of the person's wish. The person says, "Would that I had not been given my book." This may imply a desire to avoid punishment altogether. But al-Rāzī offers a deeper reading: the person's wish indicates that the *shame* at witnessing one's deeds is worse than the pain of the Fire itself. The person would prefer to be cast into the Fire without being forced to endure the shame of judgment and reckoning. The idea is a profound psychological insight. Human beings can endure physical pain more readily than public disgrace. To be seen, fully and truly, in the ugliness of one's life — to have the record of one's secret cruelties, hidden hypocrisies, and concealed sins laid open before all — is, for the guilty, a torment that exceeds punishment itself.

The modern world, with its culture of exposure and its fear of public humiliation, makes this verse newly intelligible. The fear of being fully known in one's failure is one of the most powerful anxieties of human life. The Qur'an anticipates this fear and places it at the heart of the eschatological drama. On the Day, the guilty are not merely punished; they are *seen*. And the seeing is, for them, the beginning of the torment.

**The Cure for the Fear of Exposure**

The verse's implicit teaching is that the cure for the fear of this exposure is the life of the person who is not afraid — the person of the right hand — who lives in such a way that being fully known is a joy rather than a horror. The person who is honest with God in this world, who repents, who does good, and who trusts in mercy has nothing to fear from exposure. The person who lives by concealment, by hypocrisy, by the management of appearances, is the person who faces the dread of being seen. The choice is laid bare in the sūrah's own structure: both the person of the right and the person of the left are given their book; the difference is entirely in the life that produced the record. The person who fears the book should change the life that fills it.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:26

> **And did not know of my reckoning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Loss That Follows the First**

The second clause of the person's lament continues: *wa lam adri mā ḥisābīh*. "And did not know of my reckoning." The verb *adrī* — "I know" — is the same root as the *adrāka* of the opening verses, and its recurrence is deliberate. In the opening of the sūrah, the question was asked of the Day: *"What shall apprise thee (adrāka) of the Undeniable Reality?"* Now the person of the left hand says, in effect: "I did not let it apprise me. I did not come to know of my reckoning." The word *ḥisābīh* — "my reckoning" — is the accounting, the settling of accounts. The person is lamenting that he or she did not take the reckoning into account during the life of this world.

The lament is not merely that the person did not know the *details* of the reckoning — the time, the form, the particulars. The lament is that the person did not *take it seriously*, did not *live as though* a reckoning would come. The person of the right hand said, "Truly I knew for certain that I would meet my reckoning." The person of the left hand says, "And I did not know my reckoning." The two verses are perfect mirrors. The difference between the saved and the lost, as this sūrah frames it, is not primarily a difference of outward deeds but a difference of *awareness* — whether a person lived in the consciousness of the reckoning or in the heedlessness of it.

**The Heedlessness That Is the Root of Ruin**

The verse points to a profound truth about the psychology of sin. Most human beings who end up in ruin do not choose ruin; they drift into it through heedlessness. They do not intentionally set out to destroy themselves; they simply fail to take the reality of ends, of consequences, and of the reckoning seriously. They live as though the present moment were all there is, as though the future did not exist, as though death and judgment were abstractions. The Qur'an names this state *ghaflah* — heedlessness — and it is, in the Qur'an's moral diagnosis, the root of most of the evil that besets humanity.

The person who does not know of the reckoning is not necessarily a person who never heard of it. The person may have heard the reminders again and again. The tragedy is that the reminders did not *reach* the person — or rather, the person did not let them reach him. The person heard the sound but did not take heed; the person's ears were present but not *wāʿiyah* (attentive), to use the term from verse 12. The person of the left hand is, in this sense, the exact opposite of the "attentive ear" that the earlier verse praised.

**The Awareness That Changes Everything**

The practical force of the verse is to drive home the importance of *awareness*. The believer is called to live in the consciousness of the reckoning, not in the heedlessness of it. This awareness does not produce depression or paralysis; it produces wisdom. The person who knows that a reckoning is coming will order his life accordingly: he will do what matters, he will leave what does not, he will prepare, he will be at peace. The person who does not know — or refuses to know — will squander the days on what vanishes and find, at the end, that the storehouse is empty.

In this sense, the verse is a call to *coming to know* one's reckoning. The knowledge is available — indeed, the whole of the Qur'an is a reminder of it. The person who reads the Qur'an, who ponders its warnings, who takes seriously the Day, has already begun to *know* the reckoning. The person who turns away, who lets the knowledge of the Day remain a distant abstraction, is the one whose lament will be, "And I did not know of my reckoning." The verse thus places the responsibility squarely on the human being: the knowledge was offered; the choice to accept or to let it pass was the person's own.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:27

> **Would that it were the final end.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wish for the Finality of Death**

The third clause of the lament brings an image of terrible poignancy: *yā laytahā kānati al-qāḍiyah*. "Would that it were the final end." The pronoun *hā* in *laytahā* — "would that it" — refers back to the *reckoning* or to the whole event of the death, judgment, and the situation of the person. The word *al-qāḍiyah* comes from the root *q-ḍ-y*, meaning to finish, to settle, to pass judgment, to bring to an end. The phrase could be rendered, "Would that it had been the decisive end" — that death had been the last event, the final settlement of the story.

The commentators, following al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī, understand the verse to mean: *"Would that death had been the final end, and there had been no Resurrection."* The person is longing for the annihilation that he or she had once assumed or hoped for. In this world, the disbeliever often comforted himself with the thought that death is the end — that there is no resurrection, no reckoning. Now, on the Day, the person realizes the full weight of what that wish concealed. The person is saying: "How I wish that what I once believed were true — that death ends everything. But it is not true. There is a reckoning." The wish is made in the awareness that the reckoning is unavoidable, and it is therefore a wish that is impossible.

**The Reversal of the Worldly Comfort**

This verse is a profound reversal of the disbeliever's worldly confidence. In this life, those who deny the resurrection often treat the idea with scorn, as though the belief in a reckoning were a burden or a superstition. What they do not realize is that the denial of the reckoning is itself the foundation of a deep, quiet unease. The person who believes that death is the final end must confront the fact that all the meaning of life — all the good, all the love, all the justice — perishes with the body. There is a despair at the bottom of that world-view, even when it is covered over with pleasures and distractions.

The Day reverses the wish. Now the person who wanted death to be the final end discovers that death was precisely not the final end. The final end, the *qāḍiyah* that truly settles everything, is the Day of the Lord. And rather than being a relief, it is a horror, because the person's account does not stand. The verse teaches a sharp truth about the human relationship to death. Death is not the answer to the problem of evil; it is the doorway to the reckoning. The person who faces death without faith faces the doorway with dread, and the dread is not relieved by the door but deepened by what lies beyond it.

**The Yearning for a Clean End**

There is yet another, more subtle reading of the verse, one that speaks with sympathy. The person of the left hand is depicted as wishing that death had been the final end. This can be read not merely as a wish for annihilation but as a wish for a *clean* end — an end after which there would be no shame, no exposure, no horrifying revelation of one's deeds. The person is so overwhelmed by the shame that he wishes the story had simply stopped with death. This is a poignant and human emotion. It is the wish of the person who has done things he cannot bear to have seen.

The verse does not allow this wish, but it registers its depth. It shows the terrible reality of a soul that has no ground of hope. The believer, by contrast, does not wish for death to be the end; the believer knows that death is a transition, and that beyond it lies either the mercy of God or the justice of God. The believer's hope in the reckoning is the difference. The person who has no hope in the Hereafter is left with only the wish for the last end. The person who has hope in the Hereafter faces death with the confidence of the person of the right hand.

**The Teaching for the Living**

The verse—as the whole lament of the person of the left hand—is a teaching for the living. It uses the voice of the lost to show the living what the end is like. The person who is living and can still change is given the chance to see himself in the person of the left hand and to say, in time: "I do not wish that death were the end, because I have prepared for what is beyond it." The sūrah's method is to make the listener hear, in advance, the words that will be spoken on the Day — and to make the listener wish, while there is still time, to be among those who speak other words, the words of the right hand.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:28

> **My wealth availed me not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Testament of a Lifelong Delusion**

The fourth clause of the lament is one of the most devastating in the whole sūrah: *mā aghnā ʿannī māliyah*. "My wealth availed me not." The negative particle *mā* here introduces a shattering admission: everything that the person had gathered, accumulated, and prided himself upon has proved utterly useless in the face of the reckoning. The word *aghna* — "availed" — is from the root *gh-n-y*, which carries the sense of sufficing, enriching, and making independent. The person is declaring: the wealth did not *enrich* me; it did not make me sufficient; it did not save me. All the money, all the possessions, all the assets that the person counted as the substance of his worth have been shown to be worth nothing in the very place where worth is truly measured.

The commentators note that the negative particle *mā* can also be read interrogalive, yielding a rhetorical question: *"What has my wealth availed me?"* This is perhaps even more pointed, since it suggests the person is not merely stating a fact but demanding an account — looking back at the life's work and asking, in bitter defiance that collapses into despair, where it has all gone. Either reading leads to the same bitter conclusion: wealth has no purchase in the Hereafter.

**The Illusion of Self-Sufficiency**

The broader Qur'anic theme is unmistakable. In Sūrah 18:46, God says: *"Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world, but that which endures — righteous deeds — are better in reward with thy Lord, and better as a source of hope."* The word *zīnah* — "adornment" — is carefully chosen. Wealth and children are the *decoration* of this life: they are beautiful, they give pleasure, they are valued. But a decoration is exactly that — it is not the substance, not the durable thing, not the foundation. When the house collapses, the decoration does not save it.

The verse exposes the deep structure of a certain kind of human self-understanding. Many people build their entire identity on wealth, on status, on what they own and what they can control. They believe that their security lies in their assets. But wealth has a fundamental limitation: it cannot buy health when the body fails, it cannot buy life when death comes, it cannot buy the forgiveness that only God can grant, and it cannot alter the record that only God will read. The person who has placed ultimate confidence in wealth has placed it in the one thing that is most guaranteed to fail at the moment it is needed most.

**The Modern Echo: The Poverty of Accumulation**

The verse speaks with astonishing directness to the modern world. Modern economies are built on the accumulation of wealth, on the belief that growth, consumption, and material assets are the measure of progress and the guarantee of security. Yet the verse's testimony is that, in the face of the reckoning, wealth *avails not*. This is not a rejection of wealth or of the legitimacy of earning and owning; the Qur'an elsewhere affirms the goodness of provision and the legitimacy of trade and work. It is, rather, a reordering of priorities: wealth is a *means*, not an end; it is a *trust*, not a source of true independence; it is a *test*, not a badge of success.

The person who has been given wealth is, in the Qur'an's view, in a position of great responsibility. The wealth is a test of whether it will be used for the good — for the support of the needy, for the establishment of justice, for the furtherance of the things that endure. If it is so used, it becomes a means of reward. If it is hoarded, flaunted, and trusted in, it becomes a liability. The verse's bitter admission — "my wealth availed me not" — is the voice of the one who discovered too late that the treasure he had stored on earth had not been transferred to the treasure of the Hereafter, for the treasure of the Hereafter is the one the believer sends ahead by spending in the way of God.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:29

> **My power has passed from me."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Collapse of the Last Support**

The fifth and final clause of the lament brings the inventory of loss to its end: *halaka ʿannī sulṭāniyah*. "My power has passed from me." The word *sulṭānīy* — "my power" — is rich. It comes from the root *s-l-ṭ*, which carries the sense of authority, sovereignty, dominion, and the legitimate warrant to command and to control. *Sulṭān* in the Qur'an is often used for the *argument*, the *proof*, the *authority* that a person has to make a claim. The person is here declaring the total loss of this. The word *halaka* — "has passed" — literally means "has perished" or "has been destroyed." The person is saying: my power, my authority, my warrant, my ability to argue, my claim to command — all of it is gone, utterly destroyed.

Al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī note that *sulṭān* here carries the sense of both authority and *ḥujjah* (the proof and argument). The person who receives the book in the left hand has no longer any control over his own destiny and no longer has any capacity to argue. In this world, the person may have been a great authority — a ruler, a leader, a debater, someone who could command and compel and persuade. On the Day, all of that is gone. The person has no power to resist the judgment, no authority to command a reprieve, no argument to offer against the manifest reality of the record.

**The Silence of the Limbs and the End of Argument**

The Qur'an elsewhere describes the scene of the judgment in terms that make the loss of *sulṭān* vivid. In Sūrah 24:24, the tongue, hands, and feet bear witness: *"on the day when their tongues, hands, and feet testify against them concerning what they used to do."* In Sūrah 36:65, God says: *"This Day We seal their mouths, and their hands speak to Us, and their feet testify to what they used to earn."* In Sūrah 41:20, the very ears, eyes, and skins of the disbelievers will testify against them. The people of the left hand, who in this world were so adept at argument, at excuse, at self-justification, find that on the Day their own limbs betray them. There is no longer any ability to argue, because the truth is manifest, and the evidence testifies.

The loss of *sulṭān*, moreover, means the loss of the support that every human being tends to rely on. In this world, people rely on various *sulṭān* — on money, on position, on connections, on reputation, on eloquence, on power. All of these are forms of authority and warrant that enable a person to get by and to assert himself. On the Day, every one of these supports is stripped away. The person stands naked of all the devices by which he had been accustomed to control his world. There is no money to buy influence, no position to command respect, no argument to persuade, no force to compel. All that remains is the person and the record.

**The True Power That Does Not Perish**

The verse's grim picture points, by contrast, to the true power — the *sulṭān* that does not perish. The believer who fears God, who relies on Him, who holds to a faith that is not dependent on the things of this world, is the one whose power is of the right kind. The Qur'an says: *"whosoever is given wisdom, it is but given an abundant flow of goodness"* (2:269). The believer's true authority is in righteousness, in the standing that God gives, in the record of a life lived in fidelity. That authority is not lost at death; it is confirmed at the judgment.

The sequence of the person of the left hand is a gradual stripping away of everything. First, the book is wished away. Then, the reckoning is lamented. Then, the finality of death is wished. Then, the wealth avails nothing. And finally, the power is gone. The five clauses are a complete anatomy of the loss of a self that was built on the wrong foundations. The person built his life on wealth and power and the strength of worldly standing, and one by one, each of these proves to be nothing. It is the exact inverse of the person of the right hand, who built on the awareness of the reckoning, on obedience and trust — and who found, at the end, that everything he had built was real and durable and eternal.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:30

> **Take him and shackle him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to the Guardians of Hell**

The sūrah now pivots from the word of the lost to the word of the divine command. The first clause is terse and terrible: *khudhūhu fa-ghullūh*. "Take him and shackle him." The imperative is addressed to the angels who govern Hell — the guardians, the *mālāʾikah* of the Fire. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī identify the addressees as the angels of punishment. The command is the divine verdict being carried out. The person who has just spoken the five clauses of lament is now seized. The word *khudhūhu* — "take him" — is a command of arrest and apprehension. The person who once stood with all his power and wealth is now powerless to resist the angels who seize him. There is no appeal, no reprieve, no negotiation. The command of the Lord is executed immediately and irresistibly.

The word *fa-ghullūh* — "and shackle him" — comes from the root *gh-l-l*, meaning to bind, to fetter, to put an iron collar or shackle around the neck. The *ghull* is a chain or collar placed around the neck, and the verse uses it to describe the binding of the guilty. In the Qur'an, the *ghull* appears frequently as a feature of the punishment of the disbelievers: *"and when We put on them iron collars"* (7:157), *"with fetters and chains"* (14:49), and *"in iron collars"* (36:8). The act of shackling is the beginning of the treatment of the guilty, and it is described without any elaborate description — a bare, stark command.

**The Suddenness of the Reversal**

The power of the verse lies in its abruptness and its brevity. The sūrah has just devoted five clauses to the lament of the person of the left hand, in which he recounted the loss of his wealth and his power. Then, immediately, the divine command: "Take him and shackle him." There is no pause, no interlude, no commentary, no transition. The literary effect is dramatic: the person is still speaking, still lamenting, and the command to seize him cuts across his words. It is as though the entire soliloquy of self-pity is interrupted mid-sentence by the voice of justice.

The contrast with the person of the right hand is complete and deliberate. The person of the right hand was welcomed — told to eat and drink in enjoyment, in a lofty Garden with low-hanging clusters. The person of the left hand is seized — told to be taken and shackled. Both verdicts are pronounced by the same Lord, and both are the exact reflection of the lives that produced them. The sūrah's structure is a mirror: what the person's life made him worthy of, he receives.

**The Immediacy of Justice and the End of Delay**

There is a deep moral and theological lesson here about the nature of divine justice. In this world, justice is often delayed, partial, and imperfect. The guilty often escape; the innocent often suffer; the process is slow and full of error. On the Day, the delays of this world come to an end. There is no period of waiting, no range for appeal, no possibility of corruption. The verdict having been rightly rendered, the sentence is immediately executed. The justice of God is both perfect and prompt.

The believer is taught, by this image, not to despair of justice in the face of the apparent triumph of the wicked in this world. The believer who sees the oppressor prospering, the tyrant unpunished, the fraudster uncaught, is reminded that the Day will come when the command of "take him and shackle him" will be spoken, and it will be spoken with a power that no earthly authority can resist. The sūrah's picture is not merely a theological doctrine; it is a balm for the heart of the oppressed, a promise that the apparent impunity of the wicked is a postponement, not a pardon.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:31

> **Then cast him in Hellfire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Progression of the Punishment**

The second command continues the treatment of the guilty: *thumma al-jaḥīma ṣalūh*. "Then cast him in Hellfire." The word *thumma* — "then" — indicates a sequence: first, take him; then, shackle him; then, cast him in the Fire. The progression is deliberate. The guilty person is not simply thrown into the Fire at once; he is first seized and shackled, then cast. This reflects the Qur'an's emphasis on the justice of the sequence — the person is bound and then delivered. The word *ṣalūh* — "cast him" — comes from the root *ṣ-l-y*, which in the second form (*ṣallā*) means to roast, to burn, to make enter the Fire. It is the same root as the word for the Fire itself (*ṣalāʾ* being a form of heat and burning). The command is to make the person enter the *jaḥīm* — the fierce, blazing Fire.

The word *al-jaḥīm* is one of the Qur'an's several names for Hell. It comes from the root *j-ḥ-m*, which carries the sense of a fierce, blazing, intensely hot fire. Each of the Qur'an's names for Hell — *jahannam*, *jaḥīm*, *lazā*, *saʿīr*, *saqar*, *ḥuṭamah*, *hāwiyah* — emphasizes a different aspect of its torment. *Jaḥīm* emphasizes the *intensity* and *blazing* nature of the punishment. It is the Fire in its fiercest form, the fire that burns and consumes.

**The Unquenchable Fire and the Justice of the Sequence**

The Qur'an is careful, throughout, to present the punishment of the Fire as a just consequence of human choice, not as an arbitrary cruelty. The person who is cast into the Fire is the person who has just been shown to be unworthy of anything else. The record has been read, the wealth has been shown to be of no avail, the power has been stripped, and the person has been seized and shackled. The Fire is not the first thing that befalls the guilty; it is the last thing in a sequence that begins with the person's own deeds and the exposure of the record.

For the believer, the image of the Fire is a powerful summons to vigilance and to repentance. The Qur'an repeatedly warns of the Fire, not to abuse the believer but to awaken him. The person who contemplates the Fire with the proper understanding — that it is the consequence of a life built on the wrong foundations — is moved to check the foundations of his own life. The Prophet ﷺ taught the believers to seek refuge from the Fire and to live in a way that keeps it distant. The image of the blazing *jaḥīm* is not a source of despair but of urgent hope: the person who is living can still turn, can still repent, can still be among those who find the opposite of the Fire.

**Hellfire as the State of Self-Imposed Alienation**

There is, in the contemplative traditions, a deeper understanding of the Fire that connects it to the very nature of the human soul. The Garden is the state of a soul in harmony with its Lord, content in His presence. The Fire is the state of a soul in alienation from its Lord — a soul that was built on self-sufficiency, on pride, on the denial of the reality, and that now experiences the full consequence of that alienation. The Fire is not merely a place of physical torment; it is the outward expression of an inward state. The person who was so thoroughly self-centered, so devoted to wealth and power and self, is now given, in the language of the Fire, the reality of his separation from all that is genuinely good and genuinely real. The blinding of the Fire corresponds, in the moral order, to the blinding of a life lived away from the light of God.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:32

> **Then put him in a chain whose length is seventy cubits.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chain of Boundlessness**

The third command is the most detailed of the three: *thumma fī silsilatin dharbuhā sabʿūna dhirāʿan fa-uslukūh*. "Then put him in a chain whose length is seventy cubits." The word *silsilah* — "chain" — is the standard word for a chain, a link of metal. The *dharb* — or in one reading, *dhurʿ* — "length" — is the measurement, a cubit (the distance from the elbow to the fingertips). Seventy cubits is a vast length. The command is to cause the person to *enter* the chain, to be bound within it, to pass into its links. The word *fa-uslukūh* — "then put him" — is from the root *s-l-k*, meaning to cause to enter, to insert, to make pass through. The person is made to enter the chain, to be enclosed and bound by it.

The commentators note that the number seventy in classical Arabic is used metaonymically to denote *many* — an indefinite but large number. Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī explain that here it implies a length known only to God, far exceeding anything the human mind can measure. The chain is thus a symbol of the boundlessness of the punishment. It is not a finite length that a person could measure and understand; it is a vastness beyond comprehension. The use of "seventy" — a number that in Arabic idiom conveys "many" — points to that which only God knows.

**The Chain That Passes Through the Body**

The image of the chain is developed in the commentary tradition. Al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Rāzī note that the chain is said to go directly through one's body — entering and coursing through the person. This is a chilling image of the completeness of the binding. The person is not merely bound externally; the punishment is internal, a chain that threads through the very being. The Qur'an elsewhere speaks of the fetters and chains of the guilty: *"On that Day thou wilt see the guilty bound together in fetters"* (14:49). The chain of seventy cubits is the fullest expression of this binding.

The image also connects to the Qur'an's broader teaching about the punishment being *proportional* to the sin. The person who was bound in his life to the low, to the worldly, to the pursuit of wealth and power and self — who was, in a sense, a slave to these — is now bound in a chain. The outward binding of the Hereafter corresponds to the inward binding of the life. The person who refused to be bound by the commands of God, who rejected the guidance and the reminder, is given the binding that he earned.

**The Meaning of Being "Bound"**

The chain has a deep moral and psychological resonance. Human beings who live in rebellion against God, who pursue the self at the expense of all else, are in a profound sense *already bound* — bound by their desires, their habits, their addictions, their fears, their pride. They are unfree even as they imagine themselves free. The chain of the Hereafter makes this unfreedom visible and permanent. The person who was a slave to wealth, to power, to appetite is given the chain — the very binding he chose in the life of this world, now made eternal and inescapable.

The believer, by contrast, is the one who is truly free. The believer is freed from the bondage of the self and the world by submission to God. The Qur'an speaks of the believer's liberation: *"so whoever disbelieves in the false deity and believes in God, he has grasped a firm handhold that will not break"* (2:256). The believer's freedom is not the absence of law but the freedom from the tyranny of the false god — of self, of the world, of appetite, of pride. The chain of the guilty is the visible counterpart of the freedom of the righteous. The sūrah draws the contrast starkly: the righteous eat and drink in a lofty Garden, the guilty are bound in a chain of seventy cubits.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:33

> **Truly he did not believe in God the Magnificent,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Diagnosis of the Crime**

The sūrah now states the reasons for the punishment. After the commands of the three verses of punishment, the text returns to explain *why* the person is being treated thus. The first reason: *innahu kāna lā yuʾminu bi-Allāhi al-ʿaẓīm*. "Truly he did not believe in God the Magnificent." The word *kāna* — "he was" — is important; it indicates a habit, a sustained state, a settled condition, not a single lapse. The person *was* a disbeliever; unbelief was his standing character, not a momentary failing. The crime is the corruption of the intellect: the failure of the fundamental act of knowing and acknowledging God.

The phrase *bi-Allāhi al-ʿaẓīm* — "in God the Magnificent" — is significant. The name *al-ʿAẓīm* — "the Magnificent," "the Mighty," "the Great" — is one of God's beautiful names, and it emphasizes His grandeur, His majesty, His exaltedness. The person did not believe in the Magnificent God — the God whose greatness fills the heavens and the earth, whose power is beyond all measure, whose authority is over all things. The failure to believe in *this* God is the root of all the other failures. Al-Rāzī notes that this is the corruption of the *intellect* — the deepest and most fundamental human faculty, the faculty by which the human being perceives the truth of God's existence and grandeur.

**The Root of All the Other Evils**

The verse establishes the primacy of belief in God. All the other sins of the person of the left hand — the heedlessness of the reckoning, the trust in wealth, the reliance on power, the neglect of the needy — flow from the failure to believe in God. If a person truly believed in God the Magnificent — if he truly grasped the reality of the One who is majestic, powerful, and just — then he would necessarily order his life accordingly. He would fear the reckoning, because the Magnificent God is also the Just Judge. He would not place ultimate trust in wealth, because the Magnificent God is the true source of all provision. He would not rely on his own power, because the Magnificent God is the true possessor of all authority. And he would care for the needy, because the Magnificent God cares for the needy and commands that they be given their due.

The diagnosis is thus a diagnosis of the intellect. Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī emphasize that unbelief is a *corruption of the intellect* — a failure of the essential faculty by which the human being is meant to know his Lord. This is a striking and important theological point. In the Qur'anic view, the rejection of God is not the pinnacle of rational sophistication; it is the deepest irrationality. It is a failure to perceive the truth, a blindness to the most evident reality — the reality of the Creator and the magnificence of His creation. The person who denies God is not a free-thinker; he is one who has corrupted the highest faculties of his being.

**The Magnificence of God and the Depth of the Sin**

The use of *al-ʿAẓīm* is not accidental. It is precisely the *magnificence* of God that the person denied. This points to a particular form of unbelief: the denial of God's glory and grandeur. The person may have believed in some kind of power, or in the world itself, or in his own strength. But the person did not believe in the *Magnificent* God — the God who is above all, the God who is the ultimate reality behind all things. To deny the Magnificent God is to deny the source of all magnificence, the reality that sustains all that exists.

The verse therefore calls the human being to measure himself against the true scale. The human being is small, finite, fragile, and mortal. The Magnificent God is vast, eternal, powerful, and majestic. The person who denies this reality, who forgets his own smallness or rebels against it, is engaged in a profound self-deception. He is a creature refusing to acknowledge his Creator. And the just consequence of that refusal, as the sūrah has shown, is the binding of the creature in a chain — the very bondage to himself that he chose.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:34

> **and did not urge feeding the indigent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Failure of the Action**

The second reason for the punishment is the counterpart of the first: *wa lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi al-miskīn*. "And did not urge feeding the indigent." If the previous verse diagnosed the corruption of the intellect (the failure to believe in God), this verse diagnoses the corruption of the action (the failure to care for the needy). The two together constitute the complete diagnosis of the person's ruin: he did not give God His due (belief) and he did not give the human being his due (charity). Ibn Kathīr emphasizes this pairing: the person failed in the two great obligations — toward God, and toward His creatures.

The verb *yaḥuḍḍu* — "urge" — is from the root *ḥ-ḍ-ḍ*, meaning to incite, to urge, to encourage, to spur others on. The verse does not say the person simply *failed to feed* the indigent himself; it says the person failed to *urge* others to feed the indigent. This is a subtle and important point. The person was not merely negligent in his own giving; he was negligent in *promoting* giving, in *summoning* others to give, in *establishing* a culture of giving. The word *yaḥuḍḍu* indicates a communal and social responsibility. The person was not just a non-giver; he was a non-advocate of giving.

**The Social Duty Beyond the Private Act**

The verse thus establishes a principle of great breadth: one is not only to give charity oneself, but also to *urge* others to give charity, thereby promoting social justice on a broader scale. This is a key Qur'anic teaching. In Sūrah 107:3, the Qur'an condemns the one who *"does not urge the feeding of the poor"* — the same verb. In Sūrah 89:18, the same phrase appears: *"Nor urge you the feeding of the indigent."* The Qur'an treats the failure to urge the feeding of the poor as a serious sin in its own right, not merely a secondary failure.

This is a profound social teaching. It means that the human being, in the Qur'anic view, is not only an individual but a member of a community, and the community's moral state is the responsibility of its members. The person who remains silent in the face of hunger, who does not speak out on behalf of the poor, who does not encourage others to give, is himself implicated in the injustice of the society. The verse is a call to social action, to advocacy, to the promotion of justice — not merely to private charity.

**The Quraysh's Objection and the Modern Echo**

The source notes the connection of this verse to the objection posed by the Quraysh in Sūrah 36:47. When the believers urged the Quraysh to spend of what God had given them, the Quraysh replied with a cruel and dismissive logic: *"Are we to feed those whom, if God willed, He would feed? You are in naught but manifest error."* (36:47). The Quraysh's argument was a kind of fatalistic atomism: they claimed that if God had wanted the poor to be fed, God would feed them; therefore there was no obligation on them to give. This argument is both a misuse of the doctrine of divine will and a denial of the human responsibility that God has established.

The same objection, in various forms, persists in the modern world. It appears in the claim that poverty is inevitable, that the poor are poor because of their own fault, that the rich have no obligation, that the market or the state or fate will sort things out. Against all of these the Qur'an stands firm: the feeding of the indigent is an obligation, and the urging of others to feed the indigent is a further obligation. The one who is given wealth is given it as a test and a trust, and the failure to feed the poor — and to urge others to feed them — is a failure of the very action that defines the moral life.

**The Two Great Obligations as the Measure of the Person**

Taken together, verses 33 and 34 are a complete statement of the moral failure of the person of the left hand. He failed in the relation to God — he did not believe in God the Magnificent. And he failed in the relation to his fellow creatures — he did not urge the feeding of the indigent. The two failures are inseparable. In the Qur'an's view, the proper relation to God necessarily issues in the proper relation to God's creatures. A person who truly believes in God the Magnificent, the Lord who is kind to the poor and who commands that they be sustained, will act accordingly. The person who fails in charity has thereby revealed the poverty of his faith. The person who cannot love his neighbor has revealed that he does not truly know his Lord. And it is this compound failure — the failure of intellect and the failure of action — that leads to the chain, the Fire, and the shame of the Day.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:35

> **So today he has no loyal friend here,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Solitude of the Reckoning**

The sūrah continues the description of the punishment, moving from the physical to the relational. The verse reads: *fa-laysa lahu al-yawma hunā ḥamīm*. "So today he has no loyal friend here." The word *ḥamīm* — "loyal friend" — is from the root *ḥ-m-m*, and it carries the senses of a close kin, an intimate, a warm and loyal friend. In the Qur'an, *ḥamīm* is often used to describe the intimate protector, the one whose warmth and loyalty would shield a person in time of need. The statement is that, on that Day, there is no such friend for the person of the left hand. All the relationships that the person built his life on — all the allies, the companions, the trusts, the friendships — are gone, and none of them stands with him.

The word *hunā* — "here" — is significant. It locates the absence in the place of judgment, the plain of assembly. The person is in the great gathering, surrounded by humanity. Yet in the midst of the gathering, there is no friend. The loneliest moment of the person's existence is within the assembly of all humanity. The verse paints a picture of profound isolation: the person is in the crowd of the reckoning, surrounded, and yet utterly alone.

**The Solitude That Is the Essence of the Judgment**

The Qur'an emphasizes, in several places, the solitude of the reckoning. Though all human beings stand together on the same plain (18:47; 20:106; 79:14), the Qur'an also says that every human being *"shall come unto Us alone"* (19:80). The experience of the reckoning is a solitary one. Everyone will be too consumed with his or her own state to ask about the state of another, as the Qur'an says in Sūrah 70:10: *"And no loyal friend (ḥamīm) shall ask about a loyal friend (ḥamīm)."* And in Sūrah 80:37: *"For every man that Day his affair shall suffice him."* The person who depended in this world on friends, on companions, on alliances — the person who prided himself on his network, his connections, his circle — finds that on the Day the network is dissolved and the circle is empty.

The isolation is more severe for the disbeliever, because the things the disbeliever relied upon are now exposed as worthless. The Qur'an says of the disbelievers: *"And the wrongdoers shall have no loyal friend"* (40:18), and *"so today we have set them one against another, and those who were their patrons have left them"* (5:105). The folly of everything upon which the disbeliever had relied is exposed, and all recourse is cut off (2:166).

**The Modern Illusion of Connection**

This verse speaks with great force to the modern condition. We live in an age of unprecedented connection — social media, networks, digital communities, infinite links to other human beings. Yet the depth of true connection, of loyalty, of *ḥamīm*, may be thinner than ever. People may have thousands of "friends" in the digital sense and no *ḥamīm* — no warm, loyal, self-sacrificing intimate. The verse cuts through this illusion. It asks the person to consider: when the moment of true need comes — the moment of death, the moment of the judgment when every support is stripped — who will stand? The person who built only superficial connections will find no *ḥamīm*.

The verse thus issues a profound challenge: the person who wishes to have a *ḥamīm* on the Day must first *be* a *ḥamīm* in this life — must build warm, loyal, generous, sacrificial relationships, and above all must build the relationship that truly sustains, the relationship with God. The believer's true *ḥamīm* is God Himself and those whom God makes close. The believer who has God does not experience the utter solitude of the day, for even when all human friends are consumed with their own affairs, the One who never fails stands with the believer.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:36

> **nor food, save filth,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Starvation of the Soul**

The sūrah now moves to another deprivation: *wa lā ṭaʿāmun illā min ghislīn*. "Nor food, save filth." The contrast with the person of the right hand is complete and intentional. The person of the right hand was told, "Eat and drink in enjoyment." The person of the left hand has no food but *ghislīn* — a term of extraordinary repulsiveness. The word *ghislīn* appears in the Qur'an only this once, and its meaning has been the subject of much discussion among the lexicographers and commentators.

Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr note the several interpretations of *ghislīn*. Some say it is the worst kind of food endured while in the Fire — the most repellent, the most vile, the most abominable of all torments. Others — including al-Rāzī (following the translators of the source and others) — say it refers to the oozing pus, the vile excretions, the foul discharge that the denizens of Hell are said to eat. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the food of the Fire: *"the food of the hellish is the bitter thorny fruit of ḍarīʿ, which neither nourishes nor satisfies hunger"* (88:6–7), and *"no food save the pus of wounds (ghislīn), which none eat save the iniquitous"* (69:36–37). The word *ghislīn* is thus a word of total revulsion, a food that is the opposite of nourishment, a "food" that is not food at all but a form of punishment.

**The Punishment as a Reflection of the Sin**

The commentators observe that the punishment of the Fire is not arbitrary but reflects the nature of the sin. The person who in this world refused to feed the indigent, who was not even moved to urge others to feed the hungry, is now given a "food" that is itself a form of filth. The sin of withholding food — of disregarding the hunger of others, of closing the hand and the heart to the needy — is met with a punishment in which food becomes a source of torment rather than of sustenance.

There is a deep moral counterpoint here. In this life, food is a gift from God, a sign of His mercy, a means of establishing community and generosity. The person who treated food — and the provision of God — as merely his own possession, who hoarded it, who withheld it from the hungry, has inverted the meaning of the gift. In the Hereafter, the gift becomes a curse. The person who did not feed the indigent is now unable to be fed himself, and what is offered to him is filth. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have stated, in the commentary traditions, that the people of the Fire will be made to eat of the foulnesses of the Fire, so that the punishment is a complete inversion of the mercy they rejected.

**The Warning Against the Hardening of the Stomach and the Heart**

The verse carries a profound warning for the modern world. There is an ancient and recurring human tendency to harden the heart toward the hungry. Modern societies, even when they are immensely wealthy, often tolerate levels of hunger and deprivation that are a scandal. The Qur'an's message is that the treatment of the hungry is not merely a social issue but a moral issue with eschatological consequences. The person who closes the hand to the indigent is not merely being ungenerous; he or she is setting in motion a chain that leads to the filth of the Fire.

But the verse also contains a message of hope, because it is addressed to those who can still act. The person who reads the description of the person of the left hand, who has no food but filth, is being shown the consequence of a life of neglect — and is being invited to do the opposite. The believer who gives, who feeds the hungry, who urges others to feed them, is storing up a provision that is the opposite of the filth. The believer is, in the words of the earlier verse, *sending ahead* a good provision for the days of the Hereafter. The person who is generous to the poor in this world finds, in the next, the welcome of the person of the right hand: eat and drink in enjoyment.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:37

> **which none eat, save the iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Restriction of the Filth to the Wicked**

The verse completes the description of the food of Hell: *lā yaʾkuluhu illā al-khāṭiʾūn*. "Which none eat, save the iniquitous." The word *khāṭiʾūn* — "the iniquitous" — is the plural of *khāṭiʾ*, one who sins, who errs, who commits offense. The same root appears in the earlier verse describing the peoples of Pharaoh and the subverted, who *"brought iniquity"* (69:9, *bi-al-khāṭiʾah*). The recurrence of this root is deliberate and links the whole of the sūrah's teaching. The iniquitous — those who established themselves in sin, who sinned habitually, who made sin a way of life — are the ones who will eat the filth. The punishment is reserved for those who deserve it, and it is precisely proportionate to their condition.

This verse establishes an important principle: the punishments of the Hereafter are *distributed according to moral desert*. The filth is not given to everyone; it is given to the iniquitous. The righteous do not eat the filth; they eat and drink in enjoyment. The justice of God is scrupulously exact. Each person receives precisely what his or her life has merited. The Qur'an never tires of insisting on the precision and justice of the divine reckoning: *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it"* (99:7–8).

**The Identification of the Iniquitous**

The use of the word *khāṭiʾūn* — rather than, say, *kāfirūn* (disbelievers) — is significant. It links the punishment squarely to the commission of wrong, to the doing of iniquity, not merely to the holding of a doctrine. The people who eat the filth are those who *did* wrong. And the wrong, as the sūrah has specified, is the compound failure: they did not believe in God the Magnificent, and they did not urge the feeding of the indigent. The iniquitous are not merely those who held a false belief; they are those whose false belief issued in the actual neglect of their fellow creatures.

This gives the verse a point of universal moral appeal. Whatever a person's doctrinal convictions, the moral teaching is clear: the person who does iniquity — who neglects the hungry, who oppresses, who closes the heart to the needy — is the person described here. The verse calls upon all people to examine the fruits of their beliefs, to attend to what they actually do for the poor and the weak.

**The Contrast That Returns at the End**

The sūrah's great diptych — the right hand and the left hand, the feast and the filth, the companionship and the solitude, the mutual relationship and the isolation — is the moral center of the chapter. It is the place where the aqidah of the sūrah is made concrete. The sūrah, which began with the cosmic *ḥāqqah*, with the nations destroyed, with the blast and the grinding and the rent sky, has now descended to the most ordinary and most revealing of all human acts: what we do with our bread, and whether we feed the hungry. The eschatology of the sūrah is not a detached cosmogony; it is the working out of the most human of obligations. And the contrast at the end — the one who eats and drinks in the Garden, the one who eats only filth — is the culmination of the whole. Everything that the believer does in this world, every act of giving, every refusal to neglect the poor, is a vote for the one destiny rather than the other.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:38

> **So I swear by what you see**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shift to the Oath and the Vindication of the Revelation**

The sūrah now makes a dramatic shift. Having described the destinies of the right hand and the left hand, it turns to a new section: an oath that vindicates the revelation and the Prophet. The verse reads: *fa-lā uqsimu bimā tubṣirūn*. "So I swear by what you see." The construction is emphatic. The *fa* — "so" or "then" — connects the oath to the preceding argument. The word *lā* — rendered here as part of the oath formula — is the particle of negation, but in the Qur'anic oath *lā uqsimu* it functions as a particle of emphasis. Al-Rāzī notes that *lā uqsim* can be rendered "Nay, I swear" — an oath that begins with "nay" to emphatically refute a mistaken assumption and to introduce a solemn affirmation. The oath is God swearing by the things that human beings see — the visible world, the world of empirical perception.

The word *tubṣirūn* — "that which you see" — is from the root *b-ṣ-r*, which is the root of vision and sight. The Qur'an often contrasts *baṣar* (sight, perception) with *baṣīrah* (insight, spiritual discernment). To swear by what people *see* is to invoke the very things they perceive, the visible world in all its detail — the heavens, the earth, the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the seas, the human face, the whole realm of the senses.

**The Oath as the Foundation of Testimony**

The Qur'an frequently swears by created things — by the sun, the moon, the stars, the day, the night, the wind, the sky, the earth, the plain, the horse, the olive, the fig, and numerous other parts of creation. The oath by creation has a theological purpose: it calls upon the very signatures of the Creator's power and wisdom as witnesses to the truth of the message. If the visible world is a book of signs, then the visible world can be called to testify to the truth of the revelation that explains those signs. The oath also serves a psychological purpose: it arrests the attention of the listening audience, it signals the gravity of what is about to be said, and it stakes, as it were, the credibility of the Speaker on the truth of the assertion.

This is the first of a two-part oath. The next verse will complete it, swearing by the thing that is *not seen*. Together, the two parts of the oath bracket the entire spectrum of reality: the visible and the invisible, the world of the senses and the world of the unseen. The sūrah is about to affirm that the Qur'an is the speech of a noble messenger — and it does so by swearing by both the seen and the unseen.

**The Oath and the Ground of Faith**

The oath carries a subtle but important theological point about the relationship of the visible to the invisible. The visible world is a sign, a testimony, a pointer. But the visible is not itself the whole truth; it is the sign of an unseen and greater reality. By swearing "by what you see," the Qur'an affirms the reality and value of the visible world as a sign of the invisible. And by immediately adding "and by what you see not," it affirms the reality of the invisible, which the visible points toward. The believer trusts both: the signs of the visible that lead to the object of the invisible, and the reports of the invisible that are brought by the messenger. The oath thus lays the foundation for the affirmation of the revelation that follows.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:39

> **and by what you see not,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of the Oath**

The oath is completed: *wa mā lā tubṣirūn*. "And by what you see not." The structure of the oath is now complete: I swear by what you see, and by what you see not. The two parts together constitute a comprehensive oath by the totality of reality. The first part invokes the visible world — the realm of the senses, the things that daily press upon the human being. The second invokes the unseen — the realm of the mystery, the things that lie beyond perception, the truths that cannot be detected by any instrument.

Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr note that "what you see" refers to what people witness — the present world, the visible, the things that can be perceived and observed. "What you see not" refers to the mysteries that remain unseen — the Hereafter, the angels, the soul, the realities of the Garden and the Fire, the angelic hosts, the events of the Day. In al-Rāzī's phrasing, the two parts of the oath correspond respectively to *this world* and *the Hereafter*. This is a profound correspondence, because the whole sūrah has been about the relationship between the visible world and the unseen reality. The oath is swearing by the seen and the unseen, and then it will proceed to affirm the truth of the message that speaks of both.

**The Seen and the Unseen: A Single Testimony**

The oath by the unseen is remarkable, because it would appear to be a paradox: how can one swear by something that is not seen? The theological answer is that the unseen is not unreal merely because it is unseen. The unseen is *more real* than the seen, precisely because the seen is its sign and shadow. The Qur'an teaches that the unseen is the foundation of the seen: the seen is created by the unseen (God and His command), sustained by the unseen, and will be dissolved back into the unseen order. The oath thus affirms the reality of the unseen with the same seriousness as the seen — indeed, with greater seriousness, because the unseen is the more permanent and more fundamental reality.

For the believer, this has a profound implication for how reality is understood. The believer does not limit reality to what can be perceived. The believer recognizes that the most important realities — God, the soul, the judgment, the Hereafter — are unseen, and yet are the very things that give the visible world its meaning and its direction. The believer is not a person who denies the visible; the believer is a person who understands the visible by reference to the invisible. A person who sees only the seen is, in the Qur'an's view, blind; a person who sees the seen as a sign of the unseen is the one who truly sees.

**The Oath That Guarantees the Truth**

The purpose of the oath is now clear. The Qur'an is about to assert that the speech it describes is the speech of a noble messenger. To lend weight to this assertion, it invokes the whole of reality — the seen and the unseen — as its witness. The Qur'an stakes its claim on the totality of truth: this is not a message about one part of reality while denying the other; it is a message that encompasses both, that speaks of the visible as a sign and the invisible as the reality. The oath thus prepares the reader for the most solemn affirmation of the sūrah's final section: the truth of the revelation and the vindication of the messenger.

---

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:40

> **truly it is the speech of a noble messenger,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Central Affirmation of the Final Section**

The sūrah now states the affirmation that the oath serves: *innahu la-qawlu rasūlin karīm*. "Truly it is the speech of a noble messenger." The word *innahu* — "truly it is" — is the emphatic particle, introducing a statement of certainty. The *hu* — "it" — refers to the Qur'an, the divine speech. The affirmation is that this speech is the speech of a noble messenger. But it is perhaps not immediately obvious which "messenger" is meant: the Archangel Gabriel, who brought the speech from God, or the Prophet Muhammad, who received and delivered it? This question is the subject of important discussion among the commentators.

This exact verse, with the identical wording, appears both here and in Sūrah 81:19: *"Truly it is the speech of a noble messenger."* The commentators note that the two occurrences may refer to different messengers. In Sūrah 81, the context is a defense of the revelation against the charge that it came from a *"satan"* or a *"jinn"*, and the verse there is followed by a refutation of the charge that it is *"the speech of a satan outcast."* In that context, "noble messenger" most naturally refers to the Archangel Gabriel — the messenger who conveyed the revelation from God to the Prophet — because the charge being refuted is about the *source* of the revelation being a devil or a jinn, not a noble angel.

**The Reference to the Prophet in This Sūrah**

In the present sūrah, however, the context is different. Here the affirmation is followed by verses 41–42, which refute the charge that the Prophet was a *poet* or a *soothsayer*. The accusation here is about the *messenger* being a poet, a soothsayer — i.e., about Muhammad himself. Therefore, in this instance, the majority of the commentators — including Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī — read "noble messenger" as a reference to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. The "it" — the Qur'an — is the speech of the Prophet, who was noble, and who delivered what was given to him faithfully and without alteration. The verse affirms that the one who brought the speech is *karīm* — noble, honorable, generous, distinguished — in a way that refutes the demeaning characterizations of him as a poet or a soothsayer.

The word *karīm* — "noble" — is chosen with care. *Karīm* means noble, generous, honorable, distinguished, dignified. It is a word of the highest praise, and it is applied to Gabriel elsewhere (in the phrase *rasūlun karīm* in 81:19, where it refers to the angel) and to the Prophet throughout. To describe the one who bore this speech as *karīm* is to set him apart from the low and the common, from the poet who panders, from the soothsayer who stalks for profit. The messenger is noble, and his speech is accordingly noble.

**The Speech of the Noble Messenger and Its Implication**

The affirmation that the Qur'an is "the speech of a noble messenger" has important implications for how the believer understands the nature of the revelation. The Qur'an is not the product of the Prophet's own intellect, nor is it a forgery, nor is it a work of human art. It is *qawl* — a speech — that the noble messenger conveyed. In the Islamic understanding, the Qur'an originates from God; it is conveyed by Gabriel, the trusted angel, to the noble Prophet, who delivered it exactly as he received it. The chain of transmission is holy: God, through the angel, to the Prophet, to the community, to every generation of believers.

The verse thus refutes two errors at once. The first is the error of those who say the Qur'an is a human fabrication — that the Prophet invented it. The second is the error of those who say the Qur'an is the speech of a devil or an evil spirit. The truth is between and above both: the Qur'an is the speech of a noble messenger, a speech that flows from God through the holy to the Prophet, and it bears the mark of its noble origin in every word.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:41

> **and not the speech of a poet. Little do you believe!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refutation of the Charge of Poetry**

The sūrah now refutes the first accusation: *wa mā huwa bi-qawli shāʿirin qalīlan mā tuʾminūn*. "And not the speech of a poet. Little do you believe!" The disbelievers of Makkah had accused the Prophet of being a poet — but not in the modern, complimentary sense. In pre-Islamic Arabia, poetry was the highest form of artistic expression, but it was also, in the view of the Arabs, a form of *inspired utterance* that could be used for entertainment, for boasting, for magic, or for the performance of the *soothsayer* (kāhin). To call the Prophet a poet was to suggest that his speech was a product of human genius or artistic imagination, not of divine revelation. It was also, on a deeper level, to dismiss his message as a kind of fiction — a beautiful but unreal composition.

The Qur'an refutes this with a single negation: *wa mā huwa bi-qawli shāʿir* — "and it is not the speech of a poet." The Qur'an is not the product of poetic invention. This refutation is grounded in the Qur'an's own self-understanding. The Qur'an is not poetry; it is not rhyming prose; it is not the product of the poet's craft. The earlier sūrahs had already described the poetic challenge as parallel to the challenge of inimitability: the poets of the Arabs were the masters of eloquence, and the Qur'an challenged them to produce the like. This challenge itself — the persistent inability of the poets to meet it — is a standing proof that the Qur'an is not the speech of a poet.

The second half of the verse, *qalīlan mā tuʾminūn* — "little do you believe" — is a devastating observation. The particle *qalīlan* — "little" — modifies the verb *tuʾminūn* — "you believe." The Qur'an says that the disbelievers believe *little* — that their belief is so small as to be almost nonexistent. Al-Rāzī notes that "little" can also mean "not at all," as in the Arabic idiom "You visit us so little" meaning "You do not visit us at all." The verse thus says: your belief is not merely deficient; it is essentially absent. The people who reject the Qur'an, who attribute it to poetry, do not believe it at all.

**The Destructive Power of the Mislabel**

The verse speaks to a deep pattern in the human response to truth. When truth is inconvenient, human beings often try to *mislabel* it rather than refute it. Rather than engage with the content, they attack the category. Rather than answer the argument, they accuse the arguer. In the case of the Prophet, the accusation of poetry was a way of dismissing the message without answering it — of reducing the divine speech to a human genre so that it could be safely ignored. The same pattern repeats in every age: the messenger of truth is dismissed as a dreamer, a fanatic, an idealist, a poet, an outsider, a troublemaker — so that the message need not be confronted on its merits.

The verse warns against this mental evasion. It insists that the message cannot be dismissed by a label. The truth of the Qur'an is not determined by what the disbelievers call it; it is determined by its content, its challenge, its inimitability, and its witness. The believer is called to engage with the reality of the message rather than to reduce it to a category. And the verse's final clause — "little do you believe" — is a pointed reminder that labels are not a substitute for the recognition of truth: no matter how the message is labeled, the reality remains, and the Day of recognition will come.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:42

> **Nor is it the speech of a soothsayer. Little do you reflect!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refutation of the Charge of Soothsaying**

The sūrah now refutes the second accusation: *wa lā bi-qawli kāhinin qalīlan mā tadhakkarūn*. "Nor is it the speech of a soothsayer. Little do you reflect!" The *kāhin* — "soothsayer" — was a figure in pre-Islamic Arabia who claimed to receive information from the unseen, often in a state of trance or through the aid of jinn. The soothsayer spoke in cryptic, rhyming riddles, often with a touch of rhyme (*sajʿ*), and his pronouncements were consulted for luck and guidance. To call the Prophet a *kāhin* was to suggest that his revelation was a form of veiled divination, a set of cryptic sayings for hire. It was to reduce his message — with all its coherence, its ethical depth, its narrative power, its law — to the level of the fortune-teller's rhymed slogans.

The Qur'an refutes this in one stroke. The Qur'an is not the speech of a soothsayer. Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr note that the two refutations together are the classical method of *reductio ad absurdum*: the Qur'an is neither the speech of a poet (which would be judged by the standards of beauty and eloquence) nor the speech of a soothsayer (which would be judged by the standards of cryptic divination). It is something else entirely — the speech of God, conveyed by a noble messenger, bearing a message that is neither a beautiful fiction nor a cryptic riddle but a summons to truth, to justice, to the worship of the One God.

The second clause, *qalīlan mā tadhakkarūn* — "little do you reflect" — is the counterpart of "little do you believe" in the previous verse. Here the Qur'an says: little do you *reflect*, little do you *remember*. The verb *tadhakkarūn* is from the root *dh-k-r*, which carries the double sense of remembering and reflecting, of recalling and considering. The disbelievers are accused of failing to reflect, of failing to remember. Al-Rāzī and the commentators note that *tadhakkarūn* can mean "you remember" as well as "you reflect," and on this reading the verse refers to the fact that the disbelievers remember only a few of the Prophet's positive qualities — his good character, his trustworthiness, his kindness to kin, his abstinence — but do not remember enough to benefit from the memory. They know him to be honest; they know him to be trustworthy; they know him to be truthful; and yet they do not connect this memory to the truth of his message. They remember his virtues but refuse to acknowledge his prophethood.

**The Failure of Reflection and the Failure of Belief**

The pairing of the two verses — "little do you believe" and "little do you reflect" — is deliberate. Belief and reflection are linked. The person who reflects on the truth comes to believe; the person who refuses to reflect is the person who does not believe. The Qur'an repeatedly criticizes the disbelievers for their failure to reflect — *"Do they not then reflect on the Qur'an?"* (4:82, 47:24), *"Have they not journeyed in the land so that hearts might reason?"* (22:46). The accusation here is that the disbelievers, in refusing to call the speech of the Prophet anything other than poetry or soothsaying, have closed off the avenue of reflection. They have rejected the Qur'an not after reflection but because of its absence.

Al-Rāzī adds a supplementary note: the particle "little" can be interpreted to mean that a *little bit* of belief or reflection does enter the hearts of those who oppose the Prophet, but that it leaves quickly — a momentary spark of recognition that is immediately extinguished by pride, habit, or conformity. This is a subtle and psychologically acute observation. Many people who oppose the truth have fleeting moments of recognition, moments in which they sense the force of the message, only to suppress it. The Qur'an's warning is that these fleeting recognitions, ceaselessly suppressed, can become a permanent darkness. The person who truly benefits from the message is the one who lets the spark of reflection grow into the fire of belief.

**The Challenge to the Modern Reader**

The verse's accusation of "little do you reflect" is a standing challenge to every reader. The Qur'an calls upon each person not to dismiss the message by labeling it — not to reduce it to a category, not to file it away as poetry or myth or superstition or literature — but to *reflect* upon it. The message demands reflection, and the person who reflects is the person who may come to believe. The reader who approaches the Qur'an with an open mind, willing to ponder and to consider, is the reader who fulfills its command. The person who refuses to reflect, who dismisses the message without examining it, has already decided the outcome — and, as the sūrah has warned, that outcome is the one that no one can afford.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:43

> **It is a revelation from the Lord of the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Positive Affirmation After the Two Negations**

Having negated the false attributions — it is not the speech of a poet, nor of a soothsayer — the sūrah now states the positive truth: *tanzīlun min rabbi al-ʿālamīn*. "It is a revelation from the Lord of the worlds." The word *tanzīl* — "a revelation" or "a sending-down" — is one of the Qur'an's most important self-descriptions. It comes from the root *n-z-l*, which means to descend, to come down, to be sent down. The Qur'an is *tanzīl* — the thing sent down from above. This single word gathers the whole doctrine of revelation: the Qur'an originates with God, descends from the divine realm to the human, and is not a human production. The very form of the word — an intensive noun (*tanzīl* rather than the simple *nuzūl*) — emphasizes the completeness and reality of the descending.

The phrase *min rabbi al-ʿālamīn* — "from the Lord of the worlds" — is a solemn designation of the source. The Qur'an does not come from the Prophet's own mind; it does not come from the angels in the abstract; it does not come from the jinn. It comes from *the Lord of the worlds* — the One who is the Lord of all the worlds, all the realms of existence, all the creatures that inhabit them. The phrase *rabb al-ʿālamīn* is one of the most frequent divine designations in the Qur'an, appearing in the Fātiḥah and throughout. It affirms God's universal lordship: He is not merely the Lord of one nation, one people, or one realm, but the Lord of the worlds — of the heavens and the earth, of the visible and the invisible, of the created order in all its limitless breadth.

**The Significance of the "Lord of the Worlds"**

The designation *rabb al-ʿālamīn* carries a pointed theological message. The term *rabb* — "Lord" — is not a mere name but a name of relation. It conveys the sense of the One who nourishes, sustains, fosters, and governs. The *rabb* is the one who owns, who raises, who cares for, who looks after. To say that the Qur'an is a revelation from the *Lord of the worlds* is to say that it comes from the One who knows all the worlds because He made them, who cares for all their inhabitants, whose mercy and justice extend to all. It is not the speech of a local deity, not the wisdom of a tribal chief, not the poetry of a rival. It is the speech of the Lord of all.

The repetition of this verse in Sūrah 56:80 is noted by the commentators. In both instances, the verse is a rebuke to those who say that the Qur'an is a collection of "fables of those of old" (see 6:25; 8:31; 16:24; 23:83; 25:5; 27:68; 68:15; 83:13) — or that it is the speech of a poet (69:41) — or a soothsayer (69:42). The rebuke is that these attributions are all false because the Qur'an is *tanzīl* from the Lord of the worlds. The source of the Qur'an determines its nature. If it is from the Lord of the worlds, then it is beyond all the categories of human speech, and all the dismissive labels of the disbelievers are not merely wrong; they are acts of profound misrecognition.

**The Claim That Changes Everything**

For the believer, this verse is a foundation. The entire weight of the Qur'an, its moral authority, its binding force, its claim upon the human conscience, rests on this assertion: *it is a revelation from the Lord of the worlds.* If this is true, then the Qur'an is not a text to be admired, appreciated, or selectively followed; it is the command of the Lord of the worlds, to be obeyed, to be lived, to be the measure of the life. If this is true, then the person who reads the Qur'an is not merely reading; he or she is hearing the speech of the One who made the heavens and the earth, who knows the secrets of the heart, who will judge the living and the dead.

The verse thus stands as the hinge of the sūrah's final section. The disbelievers had characterized the speech in human terms — poetry, soothsaying. The sūrah replies: no, it is revelation from the Lord of the worlds. And on this hinge turns everything: the gravity of the message, the certainty of the judgment, the reality of the Garden and the Fire, and the vindication of the messenger. The one who accepts that the Qur'an is a revelation from the Lord of the worlds accepts it all; the one who rejects it rejects the Lord of the worlds Himself.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:44

> **Had he ascribed any statements to Us,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Hypothetical of the Fabricator**

The sūrah now advances a powerful counterfactual argument. The verse reads: *wa law taqawwala ʿalaynā baʿḍa al-aqāwīl*. "Had he ascribed any statements to Us." The word *taqawwala* is from the root *q-w-l*, meaning to say, to speak; the form *taqawwala* carries the sense of *inventing* speech, *fabricating* a saying, *attributing* something to a source that did not say it. The verse is a conditional: *law* — "if" or "had." Its meaning is: had the Prophet fabricated the Qur'an — had he invented sayings and attributed them to God, as the disbelievers charged — then a specific consequence would follow. The consequence is stated in the next verses.

This is a classic argument from the nature of the messenger and the penalty for forgery. The sūrah is saying: the charge that the Prophet fabricated the Qur'an is a charge that is not only false but could not have been true, because the penalty for such fabrication — if it were possible — would have been the punishment of the fabricator. The Qur'an presents the Prophet as being under the same moral and legal order as everyone else; he would not be exempt from the just consequence of a crime that he did not commit.

**The Crime of Fabrication Against God**

The Qur'an treats the fabrication of lies against God as among the gravest of crimes. In Sūrah 2:79, it says: *"So woe unto those who write the book with their hands, then say, 'This is from God,' that they may sell it for a paltry price. So woe unto them for what their hands have written, and woe unto them for what they earn."* In Sūrah 6:93, it says: *"Who does greater wrong than one who fabricates a lie against God, or says, 'It has been revealed unto me,' though naught has been revealed unto him, and one who says, 'I will send down the like of what God has sent down'?"* (See also 3:94; 6:21–24, 144; 7:37; 10:17; 11:18; 18:15; 29:68; 61:7.) The Qur'an consistently reserves its most severe condemnation for those who attribute falsehood to God.

The logic of this severity is clear. To speak falsely against God is to attempt the highest form of corruption: it is to use the name of the ultimate authority to deceive, to mislead, to coerce. It is to take the very highest and distort it for the lowest ends. Therefore the penalty for it — were it committed by a prophet — would be the severe judgment of God. The sūrah's counterfactual therefore establishes that the Prophet, being under the same moral law as everyone, could not have fabricated the Qur'an, because the penalty would have consumed him. And since he did not suffer that penalty, and delivered the message faithfully and was vindicated by God, the charge of fabrication is shown to be false.

**The Argument as a Defense of Prophetic Integrity**

The argument of these verses is a defense of the *integrity* of the Prophet ﷺ. It asserts that the Prophet was not a fabricator, and that if he had been, God would have punished him. The argument rests on a profound claim: God does not allow falsehood to be attributed to Him without consequence. God is not a passive recipient of human slander; He is the One who defends His own truth, who vindicates the honest messenger, and who punishes the liar. The fact that the Prophet delivered the Qur'an and was not so punished — indeed was confirmed, supported, and eventually triumphant — is a testimony to the truth of his message.

This is an argument that can be appreciated both religiously and rationally. From a rational standpoint, the argument points to the observable character of the Prophet's life: a man who was known for his honesty (*al-Ṣādiq al-Amīn* — the truthful, the trustworthy) even before his prophethood, who bore persecution rather than abandon his message, who never sought worldly gain, and who produced a text whose own challenge to the finest poets was never met. From a religious standpoint, the argument is a matter of faith: the Qur'an teaches that God would not permit His messenger to be a fabricator, and that the integrity of the prophetic office is protected by God Himself.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:45

> **We would have taken him by the right hand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Divinely Wielded Power of Vindication**

The sūrah now states the consequence of the hypothetical fabrication. The verse reads: *la-akhadhnā minhu bi-al-yamīn*. "We would have taken him by the right hand." The verb *akhadhnā* — "We would have taken" — is the same root as the *akhadhahum* ("He seized them") used of the peoples who disobeyed their messengers in verse 10. The recurrence of the root is deliberate and carries a pointed message. The same power that seized the disobedient peoples would have seized the Prophet had he fabricated lies against God. The Prophet is not exempt from the divine justice; he is protected from committing the crime.

The phrase *bi-al-yamīn* — "by the right hand" — is rich with meaning. Al-Rāzī and the other commentators note that to take a person "by the right hand" in this context is a gesture of power and seizure. It is the act of grasping the right hand or the right arm — the hand that in this sūrah is associated with receiving the book, with authority, with the capacity to act. To take a person by the right hand is to seize the source of his strength, to arrest the hand with which he acts, to strip him of the power of his own action. Al-Rāzī summarizes: taken by the right hand indicates that God would have removed all power from him. The very gesture that for the person of the right hand is one of honor (the book given with joy) is, for the fabricator, the gesture of seizure and disarmament.

**The Complete Powerlessness Before the Divine**

The image of God taking the fabricator by the right hand is a picture of total divine sovereignty and complete human helplessness. The fabricator, however powerful, however eloquent, however charismatic, is seized by the One who is infinitely more powerful. There is no resistance. The grasp of God is not one that a creature can escape. In this, the fabricator is placed in the same position as the disbelieving peoples who were seized for disobeying their messengers. The moral parity is complete: those who rejected the messengers were seized; the messenger who fabricated would likewise be seized.

The verse also carries a note of reassurance for the believer. The Prophet ﷺ, who was maligned and persecuted for twenty-three years, is declared by God to be one whom He Himself would vindicate — and who, being truthful, was not seized but was established. The believer is taught that the integrity of the revelation is protected by God, and that the truth of the message cannot be undermined by the slanders of the disbelievers. No matter how much the disbelievers accuse, God knows the truth, and God protects the truth.

**The Right Hand and the Reversal of Symbolism**

The sūrah uses the right hand in two contrasting ways, and the contrast is illuminating. For the person of the right hand, the right hand is the hand of honor — the hand that receives the book and that may read it with joy. For the hypothetical fabricator, the right hand is the hand of seizure — the hand by which God takes hold of him to disarm him. The same symbol, applied by the divine justice, is either a mark of honor or an instrument of judgment, depending on the state of the person. This is a profound reminder that the symbols of honor and the instruments of judgment are both in the hand of God, and that a person's standing before God determines which he or she receives.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:46

> **Then We would have severed his life vein.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Deepening of the Consequences**

The second consequence of the hypothetical is even more severe. The verse reads: *thumma la-qaṭaʿnā minhu al-watīn*. "Then We would have severed his life vein." The word *watīn* — "life vein" — is a highly specific and significant term. It is understood by the commentators and the lexicographers to refer to the *aorta* — the great artery that rises from the heart and is the principal vessel of life. In classical Arabic, the *watīn* is the vital organ or the main artery; severing it is the cause of certain and immediate death. The Qur'an uses this precise anatomical term to describe the most certain and complete termination of life.

Al-Rāzī and the other commentators note that the sequence is significant. First, God would have taken the fabricator by the right hand — seizing him, disarming him, removing his power. Then, God would have severed his life vein — ending his life. The two acts are inseparable: the seizure precedes the termination, and the termination is total and certain. The fabricator would not merely have been disgraced; he would have been destroyed. The penalty for fabricating against God, had it been committed, would be nothing less than the complete and immediate cessation of life by the direct action of God.

**The Certainty of the Divine Retribution**

The image of severing the *watīn* is one of the most vivid in the entire Qur'an's description of divine retribution. It is an image of total and certain execution. God does not merely punish; God can end a life in an instant, without the need of any intermediate cause. The *watīn* — the aorta — is the most vital of all the bodily vessels. To sever it is to guarantee death. The Qur'an's use of this precise term leaves no room for ambiguity: had the Prophet fabricated, his life would have been ended by God in the most direct and certain manner.

This is a clear refutation of the idea that the Prophet could have "gotten away" with fabrication. The Qur'an insists that no one, not even a prophet, can fabricate against God with impunity. God's retribution is certain, immediate, and total. The fact that the Prophet did not suffer this end is itself a testament to the truth of his message: he did not fabricate, he was not seized, and his life was not cut short. He lived, preached, taught, and was ultimately vindicated by God.

**The Universal Application of the Principle**

The verse also carries a universal application. The principle that no one can fabricate against God with impunity extends to all people, not only to the Prophet. All those who make false claims of revelation, who attribute their own words to God, who speak falsely in the name of religion — all are under the same warning. The Qur'an's condemnation of the fabricators is severe: *"So woe unto those who write the book with their hands, then say, 'This is from God'"* (2:79), and *"Who does greater wrong than one who fabricates a lie against God?"* (6:93). The verse teaches that the divine justice is no respecter of persons. The false prophet, the corrupt cleric, the fabricator of divine words — all will face the same just retribution.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:47

> **And none among you could have shielded him from it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Impossibility of Interposition**

The third consequence completes the counterfactual. The verse reads: *fa-mā minkum min aḥadin ʿanhu ḥājizīn*. "And none among you could have shielded him from it." This is addressed to the disbelievers, to those who had accused the Prophet of fabrication. The meaning is: had the Prophet fabricated lies against Us, and had We then seized him and severed his life vein, then *none of you* — the disbelievers, his enemies, his would-be defenders — would have been able to shield him, to protect him, to interpose yourselves between him and the divine retribution. Even if all of the disbelievers had rallied to his defense, even if the whole of Quraysh had gathered to protect him, they could not have prevented the decreed judgment of God.

The word *ḥājizīn* — "shielding" — comes from the root *ḥ-j-z*, meaning to intercept, to screen, to act as a barrier between two things. The verse says that no one could serve as a *barrier* between the fabricator and God's retribution. The disbelievers, who prided themselves on their power, their alliances, their numbers, would have been utterly powerless. No human agency could interpose itself between a person and the decree of God.

**The Absolute Sovereignty of God**

The verse is a declaration of the absolute sovereignty of God. It establishes that no power, no number, no alliance, no strength, no shield can prevent the execution of the divine will. God's retribution is not a force that can be resisted, interposed against, or delayed by any creature. The Qur'an makes this point throughout: *"If God cause harm to touch you, there is none who can remove it except Him"* (6:17), *"And you will not be able to frustrate Him"* (11:20), and the story of Muhammad ﷺ being protected in the cave of Thawr by God's own intervention.

The verse is a rebuke to the disbelievers' sense of power. They imagined that they could protect themselves, and by extension, they imagined they could have protected the Prophet had he been a fabricator (or they imagined they could protect themselves from the consequences of their own denial). The verse removes this illusion: none among them could shield him. The power they thought they had was illusory; the power belongs to God alone.

**The Defense of the Prophet and the Security of the Truth**

The verse also carries a note of profound consolation for the believer. The Prophet ﷺ, throughout his mission, was surrounded by enemies, persecuted, threatened, and given many opportunities to compromise or to abandon the message. Yet God protected him. The Qur'an's historical record — the Prophet's survival against overwhelming odds, his escape from the assassination attempt at Makkah, his protection in the cave, his ultimate triumph — is a testimony to the divine protection. God does not allow the truthful messenger to be destroyed, and God does not permit the enemy to intercept the course of the divine purpose.

The believer who trusts in God is taught a similar truth: the person who stands for truth, who delivers the message of God, who lives in fidelity, is under the protection of God. No enemy, no alliance, no power can ultimately harm the one whom God protects. This is not a guarantee of worldly immunity from hardship — the Prophets and the believers suffered greatly — but it is a guarantee of ultimate vindication. The truth of God is not dependent on human support; it is sustained by God, and the one who bears it is ultimately secure.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:48

> **Truly it is a reminder for the reverent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return to the Theme of the Reminder**

The sūrah now begins the final affirmation of the revelation with a statement of its purpose: *wa innahu la-tadhkiratun lil-muttaqīn*. "Truly it is a reminder for the reverent." The emphatic particle *innahu* introduces the statement. The *hu* — "it" — refers, in the majority view, to the Qur'an, which has been the subject of verses 40–43 and 50–52. Al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, and the translators of the source all take the pronoun as referring to the Qur'an. But some commentators note the possibility that "it" refers to the Day of Resurrection and Judgment described in the earlier verses. Both readings are coherent, and the best is that the verse's primary reference is to the Qur'an, which is indeed the great reminder — while the Day is also a reminder in its own right. The reference to the Qur'an as a reminder is more supported by other verses.

The word *tadhkirah* — "reminder" — is one of the Qur'an's favorite self-designations. It comes from the root *dh-k-r*, meaning to remember, to recall, to be mindful. The Qur'an is a *tadhkirah* because it calls the human being back to the remembrance of what he or she already, in the deepest sense, knows: the existence and grandeur of God, the reality of the reckoning, the obligations of justice and mercy. The Qur'an is not presenting an entirely new, alien truth; it is reminding the soul of the truth that is written in its own nature and that the heedlessness and sinfulness of the world have caused it to forget.

**The Reminder as God's Method of Guidance**

The Qur'an's description of itself as a reminder reveals its method and its aim. It does not seek to persuade by sheer force of philosophical argument, nor to compel by threat alone, nor to entertain by narrative. It seeks to *remind* — to call the human soul back to what it knows, to stir the memory that has been buried under the accumulations of heedlessness. The Prophet ﷺ described the human soul as having been born in a state of pristine recognition of God, and the world's distractions as causing it to forget. The Qur'an is the reminder that reawakens that original recognition.

This is why the Qur'an is effective precisely for those who are *reverent* — the *muttaqīn*, the God-wary, those who are mindful of God and open to being awakened. The person who has protected his or her soul from the hardening effects of heedlessness, who has kept the capacity for awe and reverence alive, is the person who responds to the reminder. The person who has allowed the heart to harden, who has suppressed the memory of God, who has closed the ear, does not benefit from the reminder — for the reminder cannot enter a closed heart.

**The Reverent as the Recipient of the Reminder**

The mention of the *muttaqīn* here connects the sūrah to the opening of Sūrah al-Baqarah, where the same word defined the recipients of the Book's guidance: *"This is the Book in which there is no doubt, a guidance for the reverent (muttaqīn)"* (2:2). The recurrence of *muttaqīn* is no coincidence. The Qur'an's guidance is for the reverent; its reminder is for the reverent; its benefit is for the reverent. The quality of taqwā — protective mindfulness, the awareness of God that makes a person careful and receptive — is the precondition for the Qur'an to work in a soul.

This is the same principle that the earlier commentators, following the source for 20:2–3, noted: *"We did not send down the Qur'an unto thee that thou shouldst be distressed, but only as a reminder unto one who fears [God]"* (20:2–3). And, in this vein, Sūrah 11:114 says of the Qur'an: *"This is a reminder for those who remember."* The reminder is for those who have the capacity to be reminded — the spiritually awake, the reverent, the mindful.

**The Invitation to Become Reverent**

The verse is thus both a declaration and an invitation. It declares that the Qur'an is a reminder for the reverent. And it invites the reader to become one of the reverent, so as to receive the benefit of the reminder. The person who reads this sūrah and recognizes the gravity of the Day, the reality of the reckoning, the contrast between the right hand and the left hand, and the truth of the revelation, is being moved toward taqwā. The person who responds with mindfulness and obedience is entering the company of the *muttaqīn*, the ones for whom the reminder is a light and a guide.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:49

> **And truly We know that among you are those who deny.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The All-Encompassing Knowledge of God**

The sūrah now states a truth that tempers the previous verse's promise with a solemn warning: *wa innā la-naʿlamu anna minkum mukadhdhibīn*. "And truly We know that among you are those who deny." The emphatic *innā* and the construction *la-naʿlamu* — "We certainly know" — establish the verse on the foundation of God's perfect knowledge. God knows, with absolute certainty and completeness, that among those who hear this reminder there are those who will deny it. The knowledge is not a guess or an inference; it is the settled, omniscient knowledge of God, who knows what is in the hearts and who knows the deeds that will flow from them.

The word *mukadhdhibīn* — "those who deny" — is from the root *k-dh-b*, meaning to belie, to call false, to deny. It is the same root as the *kadhdhabat* ("denied") of verse 4, where Thamūd and ʿĀd "denied the calamity." The recurrence is deliberate: the sūrah links the denial of the present generation to the denial of the ancient peoples who were destroyed. Those who deny are standing on the same moral ground as Thamūd and ʿĀd, and they face the same warning.

**The Knowledge as Warning and Consolation**

The verse serves two functions. First, it is a warning. The disbelievers, who think they can deny the Qur'an and the Day without consequence, are told that God knows precisely who they are. Their denial is not hidden; it is fully known to God, and it is fully accountable. The person who denies the reminder cannot hide behind anonymity, behind the crowd, behind the pretense of ignorance. God knows. The denial is recorded, and the reckoning will come.

Second, the verse is a consolation for the believer and for the Prophet. The Prophet ﷺ, who preached for years and saw many of his own kin and tribe turn away, was sometimes grieved by the rejection. This verse reassures: God knows. It is not a failure of the Prophet's message that so many denied it; it is an effect of the perfect knowledge and eternal decree of God. The Prophet's task was not to make everyone believe but to deliver the message clearly. And the fact that God knows who will deny is not a determinism that excuses the deniers; it is the ground of God's perfect justice, for He will judge each person precisely according to what he or she knew and did.

**Al-Rāzī's Observation and the State of the Heart**

Al-Rāzī remarks on the distinction between those who benefit from the reminder and those who deny it. Those who stay on guard against excessive love for this world — who protect their hearts from the love of wealth, power, and pleasure — will be reminded and admonished by the Qur'an and will benefit from it. But those who incline to the world, whose hearts are turned toward the low and the transient, will deny the Qur'an and not draw near to it. The verse thus points to the psychological and moral condition that determines the response to the reminder.

This is a profound insight. The same text can be a mercy for one person and a source of deepened denial for another, depending on the state of the heart. The person who is oriented toward God and the Hereafter reads the Qur'an and is drawn closer. The person who is oriented toward the world reads the same words and is unmoved, or even hardened. The difference is not in the text but in the heart that receives it. The Qur'an is a reminder for the reverent, and the non-reverent — those who deny — are, in a sense, self-excluded from its benefit by the state of their own hearts. The verse thus calls upon the reader to examine the orientation of the heart, for that orientation determines whether the reminder will be a light or a stone.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:50

> **It is a source of regret indeed for the disbelievers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reversal of the Disbelievers' Expectation**

The sūrah now makes a statement that turns the tables entirely: *wa innahu la-ḥasratun ʿalā al-kāfirīn*. "It is a source of regret indeed for the disbelievers." The word *ḥasrah* — "regret" — comes from the root *ḥ-s-r*, which carries the senses of grief, regret, remorse, and the deep sorrow of loss or failure. The pronoun *innahu* — "it" — refers, in the majority of the commentaries, to the Qur'an. The Qur'an, which the disbelievers dismissed and denied, is a source of regret for them. In this world, they regarded the Qur'an as a burden, a nuisance, a threat. In the Hereafter, they will regard it as the source of their deepest regret — the reminder they ignored, the grace they rejected, the light they turned their backs upon.

Al-Rāzī and al-Suyūṭī note the different readings of this verse and the following one. Some see both verses as referring to the Qur'an. Others see verse 50 as referring to the Day of Judgment and verse 51 to the Qur'an. Still others read verse 50 as referring to the disbelievers' denial of the Qur'an itself. The predominant reading, on the authority of al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, and others, is that *ḥasrah* refers to the Qur'an being a source of regret for the disbelievers.

**The Qur'an as a Proof For or Against**

The Qur'an's nature as a source of regret is grounded in the principle that the Qur'an is a *proof*, either *for* a person or *against* a person. A ḥadīth states, as the source notes: *"The Qur'an is a proof, either for you or against you."* (recorded by Muslim, from Abū Mālik al-Ashʿarī and others). The same Book that guides the believer, that is a light and a mercy, is the very evidence that condemns the disbeliever. The disbeliever cannot say, on the Day, "I did not know" or "No reminder was given." The Qur'an was there, recited, recorded, available. Its presence is a proof against the disbeliever, because it was ignored.

The sūrah has already established this point in the description of the two destinies: the person of the right hand *knew for certain* the reckoning; the person of the left hand *did not know* it — because he did not take heed. The reminder was given to both. The difference was the response. And the reminder that was given is now the source of regret for those who ignored it.

**Al-Qurṭubī's Exposition of the Regret**

Al-Qurṭubī writes, as the source records: *"The Qur'an is a source of regret for the disbelievers on the Day of Resurrection when they see the reward of those who believe in it... and it is their source of regret in this world when despite their opposition to it, they are not able to answer the challenge to bring a sūrah like it (2:23; cf. 10:38)."* The regret is thus twofold. In this world, the disbelievers are humiliated by their inability to meet the Qur'an's challenge and frustrated by its inimitability. In the Hereafter, the regret is the pain of seeing the reward of those who believed and the loss of what they themselves forfeited. The regret is not merely an emotion; it is a reality — the deep and permanent sorrow of a person who has lost what cannot be regained.

This is the theme of the *ḥasrah* that runs through the Qur'an: the disbelievers will say, on the Day, "O would that we had obeyed" and "O would that we had not denied." The regret is the recognition that the opportunity was real and was lost. The sūrah's picture is a call, in the present, to avoid that regret — to accept the reminder, to believe, and to be among those for whom the Qur'an is not a source of regret but a source of guidance, mercy, and joy.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:51

> **It is indeed the truth of certainty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Affirmation of the Revelation**

The sūrah now seals its affirmation of the revelation with the most decisive and comprehensive statement: *wa innahu la-ḥaqqu al-yaqīn*. "It is indeed the truth of certainty." The emphatic construction (*innahu la-*) marks this as a statement of absolute conviction. The phrase *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* — "the truth of certainty" — is one of the most important technical terms in the Qur'an's epistemology of faith. It is the highest level of certainty, beyond mere opinion (*ẓann*) and even beyond ordinary knowledge (*ʿilm*). In Qur'anic terminology, the levels of certainty are often described as: *ʿilm al-yaqīn* (the certainty of knowledge) — knowing a truth as a matter of information; *ʿayn al-yaqīn* (the certainty of beholding) — seeing the truth with one's own eyes; and *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* (the truth of certainty) — the truth that is so firmly established that it is fully realized, the highest and most undoubting certainty.

In Sūrah 56:95, the identical phrase appears at the end of the preceding sūrah's thematic parallel: *"Truly this is the truth of certainty."* And in Sūrah 15:99, the believer is urged: *"And worship thy Lord until the certainty (al-yaqīn) comes to you"* — meaning until death, which is the arrival of the highest certainty. The phrase *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* is thus a term that reaches the very summit of the Qur'an's talk about certainty and truth.

**The Truth of Certainty as the Ground of Faith**

The affirmation that the Qur'an is the "truth of certainty" is the foundation on which the whole of the believer's faith rests. If the Qur'an is the truth of certainty, then it is not a matter of speculation, of probability, of guesswork, or of mere opinion. It is a truth that is certain beyond all doubt, a truth that can be relied upon utterly, a truth that is the firm and unshakable reality of the universe. The believer who holds to this truth is not building on sand; the believer is building on the rock of the absolute.

The phrase also carries a moral and experiential dimension. The "truth of certainty" is not just an intellectual proposition; it is a truth that the believer *experiences* and *lives*. The person who truly believes that the Qur'an is the truth of certainty does not merely assent to it; the person orders his or her entire life by it, trusting it in every circumstance, because it is certain. The certainty is not a matter of the head alone but of the heart and the hand. It is the truth that one is willing to live by and to die for.

**The Place of This Verse in the Sūrah's Argument**

This verse is the climax of the sūrah's fourth and final movement. The sūrah has been building toward this affirmation. It began with the cosmic declaration of the *ḥāqqah*. It moved through the destruction of nations, the dissolution of the world, the judgment of the right and the left hands. It then confronted the disbelievers' accusations — that the Qur'an was the speech of a poet or a soothsayer — and refuted them. It affirmed that the Qur'an is a revelation from the Lord of the worlds, and it answered the charge of fabrication with the counterfactual of the right hand and the severed life vein. It affirmed that the Qur'an is a reminder for the reverent, and that God knows those who deny. And now it seals the entire argument with the declaration: the Qur'an is the truth of certainty.

The word *ḥaqq* also ties the conclusion to the sūrah's opening. The sūrah began with *al-ḥāqqah* — the Undeniable Reality, the emphatic form of *ḥaqq*, truth and reality. It ends with *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* — the truth of certainty. The truth that was declared at the beginning is re-affirmed at the end. What was *ḥaqq* — real — is now known to be *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* — certain. The circle is complete: the reality that cannot be denied is the truth that is known with certainty. The sūrah's whole argument is an ascent from the declaration of reality to the possession of certain truth.

## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:52

> **So glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Magnificent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command of Response**

The sūrah ends with a command — the proper response to all that has been said: *fa-sabbiḥ bi-ismi rabbika al-ʿaẓīm*. "So glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Magnificent." The *fa* — "so" — connects the command to the preceding affirmation. Because the Qur'an is the truth of certainty, because it is the revelation from the Lord of the worlds, because the Day is real and the reckoning is certain, *therefore* glorify the Name of your Lord. The response to the truth is not merely intellectual assent but worship. The command is to *glorify* — *sabbiḥ* — from the root *s-b-ḥ*, which means to praise, to exalt, to declare freedom from all imperfection and all deficiency. To *subḥān* (glorify) is to declare God's transcendence, His perfection, His freedom from all that is not worthy of His majesty.

The phrase *bi-ismi rabbika* — "by the Name of thy Lord" — is significant. The command is to glorify *by* or *with* the Name of the Lord. The Name is the means of the glorification. To glorify the Name of the Lord is to invoke Him by His beautiful names, to praise Him as *al-ʿAẓīm* — the Magnificent, the Mighty, the Great. The verse is thus an invitation to *dhikr* (remembrance), to the constant invocation and praise of God. It is the *tadhkirah* (reminder) made active in the life of the believer: the reminder becomes remembrance, the recollection becomes glorification.

**The Name "the Magnificent" and the Sūrah's Theme**

The verse closes with the divine name *al-ʿAẓīm* — "the Magnificent." This name is deliberate and ties the whole sūrah together. In verse 33, the person of the left hand was condemned because he *"did not believe in God the Magnificent (al-ʿaẓīm)."* Now, at the end of the sūrah, the believer is commanded to *glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Magnificent (al-ʿaẓīm).* The sūrah is thus a complete circle: it names the sin (the failure to glorify and believe in the Magnificent God) and it prescribes the cure (the glorification of the Magnificent God). The person who is saved is the person who glorifies the Magnificent; the person who is lost is the person who refuses to glorify Him.

The name *al-ʿAẓīm* — the Magnificent, the Mighty, the Great — is one of the most exalted of God's names. It is a name that encompasses all the meanings of majesty, grandeur, glory, and sovereign perfection. To glorify the Magnificent is to recognize and to proclaim that God is above all the petty claims of the creatures, above all the false sovereigns of this world, above all the pride and the power and the wealth that the sūrah has shown to be worthless. The glorification of the Magnificent is the ground of all right living, because it places God at the center and all else in its proper place.

**The Verse as a Parallel to the Closing of Sūrah 56**

The source notes the parallel to Sūrah 56:95–96, where the same formula appears: *"So glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Magnificent."* The two sūrahs — 56 (al-Wāqiʿah) and 69 (al-Ḥāqqah) — are indeed closely related. Both are late Makkan sūrahs concerned with the Day of Judgment; both describe the division of humanity into the companions of the right and the left; both affirm the truth of the Qur'an; and both close with the same command to glorify the Name of the Lord, the Magnificent. The parallel is not accidental; it is an intentional linkage of two of the most powerful eschatological sūrahs, reinforcing the same message: in the face of the reality of the Day, the proper response is the glorification of God.

**The Response That Changes the Life**

The command "glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Magnificent" is not a mere formula; it is a comprehensive orientation of the life. The person who glorifies the Magnificent has accepted its implications: that God is the ultimate reality, that the Day is certain, that the warnings are true, that the world's wealth and power are nothing, and that the true life is the one lived in the remembrance and obedience of God. The glorification is the antidote to the heedlessness that the sūrah has condemned. It is the remedy for the denial that the sūrah has refuted. It is the answer to the regret that the sūrah has described. The person who glorifies the Name of the Lord, the Magnificent, is the person who stands on the side of the right hand, who knows for certain the reckoning, who lives in awareness, and who will be told to eat and drink in enjoyment, in a lofty Garden, with low-hanging clusters.

The sūrah, which began with the overwhelming declaration of the *ḥāqqah* and the threefold question about the Undeniable Reality, ends with the quiet, steadfast command of worship. The reality of the Day, which the sūrah has established through history, cosmology, psychology, and the vindication of the messenger, now becomes the ground of a life. The believer who has read this sūrah and understood it is not merely warned; he or she is called. And the call is simple, profound, and life-transforming: *Glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Magnificent.*
