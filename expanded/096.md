# Sūrah al-ʿAlaq (Chapter 96) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Where the Qur'an Begins**

Sūrah al-ʿAlaq is nineteen short verses long — a page in most printed copies of the Qur'an, less than two minutes of unhurried recitation — and yet in the history of revelation it occupies a position no other passage can share. The overwhelming majority of Qur'anic scholars, early and late, hold that its first five verses were the first words of the Qur'an ever revealed to Muhammad ﷺ. Everything else in the Book — the law of inheritance, the story of Joseph, the Throne Verse, the farewell instructions of Sūrat al-Māʾidah — descends after these five lines and, in a sense, out of them. When a Muslim opens the muṣḥaf at Sūrah 96 he is reading page 597 of 604 in the standard Madinan printing; when he opens the chronology of revelation, he is reading page one.

The sūrah takes its name from the word *ʿalaq* in the second verse, usually translated "a blood clot" or "a clinging form" — the tiny attached mass from which the human embryo develops. Because of its opening imperative it is also known by two other traditional names: *Sūrat Iqraʾ* ("Recite!") and *Sūrat al-Qalam* ("The Pen") — though the latter is more commonly applied to Sūrah 68. Each of the three names points to one of the sūrah's three great themes: the command to receive revelation, the origin of the human being, and the written transmission of knowledge.

**Two Revelations Within One Chapter**

The sūrah as it stands in the muṣḥaf is not a single sitting of revelation. The commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī and others — distinguish clearly between two portions. Verses 1–5 came in the cave of Ḥirāʾ at the very beginning of prophethood, before Muhammad ﷺ knew himself to be a prophet at all. Verses 6–19 came some years later, in the public Makkan phase, when the Prophet ﷺ had begun praying openly at the Kaʿbah and the Quraysh leadership — above all Abū Jahl ʿAmr ibn Hishām — had begun trying to stop him.

That the two portions were later placed in one chapter is itself instructive. The opening five verses describe what God gives: creation, nobility, teaching, the pen, knowledge that man did not previously possess. The remaining fourteen describe how a human being can respond to those gifts: with denial, self-sufficiency, and the active obstruction of another person's prayer. Placed side by side, they form a complete argument. Verses 1–5 establish the debt; verses 6–19 diagnose the ingratitude; verse 19 prescribes the cure — prostration.

**The Night of the First Revelation**

The circumstance of the first five verses is preserved in one of the most carefully transmitted narrations in Islamic literature, reported by ʿĀʾishah and recorded at the head of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. Before the revelation came, the Prophet ﷺ had been given true dreams that arrived, in her words, *"like the break of day in the morning"* — a six-month apprenticeship, as later scholars described it, in receiving truth from beyond himself without terror. Then solitude was made beloved to him, and he would withdraw to the cave of Ḥirāʾ on the mountain now called Jabal al-Nūr, three miles from Makkah, for nights of worship at a time, returning to Khadījah for provisions.

There, in the last third of Ramadan in the year corresponding to 610 CE — he was forty years old, twelve years before the migration to Madinah — the angel came:

> *"The Angel came to him and said, 'Recite!' He said: 'I said, I am not one who recites.' He said: 'Then he seized me and pressed me until I could bear no more, then released me and said, "Recite!" I said, "I am not one who recites." So he seized me and pressed me a second time until I could bear no more, then released me and said, "Recite!" I said, "I am not one who recites." So he seized me and pressed me a third time, then released me and said: "Recite in the Name of thy Lord who created; created man from a clinging clot. Recite, and thy Lord is the Most Noble."'"* (al-Bukhārī 3; Muslim 160, from ʿĀʾishah)

He returned to Khadījah with his heart trembling, saying *"Cover me, cover me,"* and after his fear subsided he told her what had happened and said *"I fear for myself."* Her reply is among the most remarkable statements ever made by a human being in a moment of crisis:

> *"Never! By God, God will never disgrace you. You keep the ties of kinship, you bear the burden of the weak, you help the destitute, you are generous to your guest, and you assist those afflicted by calamity."* (al-Bukhārī 3)

She then took him to her cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal, an aged and blind Christian who had transcribed scripture in Arabic. Waraqah identified the visitor immediately as *"the Nāmūs whom God sent down upon Moses,"* wished he were young enough to see the days ahead, and warned: *"No man has ever brought what you have brought without being treated with hostility."*

The whole scene — the pressing, the refusal, the trembling, the wife's steady faith, the old Christian's recognition — is the birth certificate of the Qur'an as a historical event, and the commentary that follows draws on it repeatedly, because verses 1–5 cannot be understood apart from the human situation in which they were first spoken.

**The Structure of the Argument**

The nineteen verses fall into four movements, and reading them as a sequence reveals the logic that governs the chapter:

1. **Verses 1–5: The gift.** Recite — in the Name of the Lord who created, who made man from a clot, who is Most Noble, who taught by the pen, who taught man what he did not know. God is presented in two capacities: Creator of the body, Teacher of the mind.
2. **Verses 6–8: The refusal.** Man transgresses because he *sees himself as self-sufficient* — and the antidote to that illusion is placed immediately after it: *"Truly unto thy Lord is the return."*
3. **Verses 9–16: The obstruction.** The self-sufficient man does not merely disbelieve; he interferes with a servant who prays. The verses interrogate him with a series of questions, then warn him of being seized by the forelock.
4. **Verses 17–19: The confrontation and the cure.** Let him summon his supporters; God will summon the guards of Hell. And to the believer: obey him not; prostrate and draw near.

**Why This Chapter Was the Right Beginning**

There is a scholarly question worth raising at the outset: of everything God could have said first, why *this*? The answer emerges from the words themselves. The first command is not "believe," not "pray," not "fight," not "give" — it is *"Recite."* The first divine attribute named is *Creator*. The first fact stated about the human being is his humble biological origin. The first divine gift praised after creation is *teaching*. In a society where perhaps a handful of men in Makkah could write, and where honor was measured by lineage and wealth, the opening word of the final revelation announced a civilization built on reading, on a Lord who creates, and on the equality of all human beings who all began as a clot.

The commentary that follows treats each of the nineteen verses as an independent discourse, in strict order, with each verse quoted individually and expanded through its Arabic vocabulary, its cross-references in the Qur'an, the authenticated Prophetic reports that bear upon it, the analyses of the classical commentators, the logic of its claim, and its consequences for the way a person actually lives. Verses that the source commentary discusses in groups — 1–5, 4–5, 9–10, 11–14, 15–16, 17–18 — are here separated and given full individual treatment. Nothing has been abbreviated on account of the sūrah's brevity; a short chapter that opened the Qur'an deserves to be read slowly.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:1

> **Recite in the Name of thy Lord who created,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Word God Spoke to the Last Prophet**

*Iqraʾ* — "Recite," "Read," "Proclaim aloud." One word, three letters in the Arabic script, and with it the final revelation to humanity begins. Everything that Muslims call the Qur'an — 114 chapters, 6,236 verses, twenty-three years of descending speech — unfolds from this single imperative delivered in a cave on a mountainside outside Makkah to a forty-year-old man who had never sought it.

It is worth pausing on how startling that beginning is. Consider what a founder of a movement might be expected to say first: a claim of authority, a rallying cry, a promise of victory, a list of enemies. Consider what a religion might be expected to open with: a creed, a ritual, a prohibition. The Qur'an opens with none of these. It opens with a verb of receiving. Before the human being is asked to *do* anything for God, he is commanded to *receive* something from God. The relationship is established in its correct order at the very first instant: God gives, man receives; God speaks, man recites.

**The Root Q-R-ʾ: Gathering Before Uttering**

The verb *qaraʾa* comes from the root *q-r-ʾ*, whose underlying sense in classical Arabic lexicography is *to gather, to collect, to bring things together*. Ibn Fāris and later lexicographers note that from this root comes *qaryah* (a settlement — where people are gathered) and *qurʾ* (a menstrual cycle or purity interval — a gathering of blood in the womb, hence its legal use in 2:228). Reading and reciting are called *qirāʾah* because the reciter gathers letters into words and words into meanings, holding scattered sounds together into a single coherent utterance.

This etymology matters. It tells us that *iqraʾ* is not the passive scanning of marks on a page. It is an act of assembly — taking what is dispersed and making it whole. The Qur'an itself is named from this root: *al-Qurʾān*, "the Recitation" or "the Gathering-into-speech," the Book that collects into one address everything a human being needs to know about his origin, his duty, and his destination.

**Why "Recite" and Not "Read"**

Translators have long debated whether *iqraʾ* here means "read" or "recite." The commentary tradition, and the historical setting, favour *recite*. The Prophet ﷺ was handed no scroll. There was nothing before his eyes in the darkness of the cave. His own answer to the angel establishes this: *mā anā bi-qāriʾ* — commonly rendered "I am not one who reads / I cannot read," and understood by most scholars either as "I do not know how to read" or as "I am not a reciter — what shall I recite?" Both readings are grammatically defensible; both point to the same fact, which the Qur'an states elsewhere about him:

> *"He it is who sent among the unlettered ones a Messenger from among themselves, reciting unto them His signs, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and Wisdom."* (Qur'an 62:2)

And:

> *"And thou didst not recite before it any Book, nor didst thou inscribe one with thy right hand; then those who follow falsehood would have doubted."* (Qur'an 29:48)

The unlettered condition of the Prophet ﷺ is not a defect to be apologized for; the Qur'an presents it as evidentiary. A man who cannot write cannot be suspected of having composed from written sources. The vessel was empty of prior text, so that what filled it could not be mistaken for its own.

Yet the choice of *recite* over *read* also carries a positive theological weight. Islam's scripture was to be, first and last, an oral event. It was memorized before it was compiled, heard before it was seen, and to this day the primary mode of its transmission is a living chain of voices from teacher to student. The first word of the Qur'an ordained the medium of the Qur'an.

**The Pressing in the Cave: Why the Angel Squeezed Him**

The narration of ʿĀʾishah in al-Bukhārī preserves a physical detail that scholars have long reflected upon: three times the angel seized the Prophet ﷺ and pressed him until he could bear no more, releasing him each time with the same command:

> *"Then he seized me and pressed me until I could bear no more, then released me and said, 'Recite!'"* (al-Bukhārī 3)

Commentators offer several complementary explanations. The pressing removed any possibility that the experience was a dream or an imagining — it was bodily, painful, undeniable, and therefore evidential. It also communicated the sheer weight of what was being placed upon him; revelation is described elsewhere as *qawlan thaqīlā*, *"a weighty word"* (73:5), and the Prophet ﷺ would later be seen sweating on cold days when it descended. And the threefold repetition established, at the outset, that this command was not negotiable and not conditional on the recipient's sense of qualification. He said three times, in effect, "I cannot"; the answer three times was, "Recite."

There is a lesson here for anyone who has ever been given a responsibility beyond his felt capacity. The Prophet ﷺ did not begin as a confident man declaring a mission. He began as a frightened man saying "I am not able," and being told to do it anyway — and being given, in the same breath, the only resource that makes any task possible: *the Name of thy Lord*.

**"In the Name of thy Lord": The Authority Behind the Act**

The command is not merely *iqraʾ*. It is *iqraʾ bi-smi rabbik* — "Recite in / by the Name of thy Lord." The Arabic preposition *bi-* here carries at least three senses that the commentators draw out, and all three are true simultaneously:

*By means of.* Recite with His Name as your instrument and enabling power — since the reciter has no natural ability to do this, the ability must come from the One who commands it.

*In the name of / on the authority of.* Recite as His spokesman, not your own. Nothing that follows is the reciter's opinion. This single phrase forever separates the Prophet ﷺ from the poets and soothsayers of Arabia, who spoke in their own names or in the names of their familiar spirits.

*Beginning with His Name.* Recite by first invoking Him — which is why the Muslim tradition of opening every act with *bismillāh* traces its spirit to this verse, and why every sūrah of the Qur'an save one begins with the basmalah.

Notice too that the divine title used is *rabb* — Lord, Master, Nurturer — and not the personal name *Allāh* in the first instance. The root *r-b-b* denotes ownership, sovereignty, and above all *tarbiyah*: the gradual raising of something from a lesser state to its completion. A *rabb* is one who owns, sustains, and develops what is his. And the possessive is intimate: *rabbika*, *thy* Lord — the Lord of *you*, personally, this trembling man in this cave. Before the world was told anything about God, the Prophet ﷺ was told that God belongs to him and he belongs to God.

Al-Rāzī and others observe why the attribute of Lordship precedes any mention of majesty or judgement: the very first encounter with God in the Qur'an is framed as care, not threat. The One issuing this overwhelming command is the same One who has been raising you since you were nothing.

**"Who Created": Absolute Creation, Stated Without an Object**

The verse ends with a grammatical peculiarity that the classical commentators seized upon. *Alladhī khalaq* — "who created" — and then the sentence stops. Created *what*? No object is supplied.

In Arabic rhetoric, when a transitive verb is deliberately left without an object, the effect is generalization: the act is asserted absolutely, in every direction, without limit. The meaning is: the Lord who creates — full stop; the Lord whose defining act is bringing into existence, of whom everything that exists is a product. Al-Ājurrūmī's grammatical tradition calls this *ḥadhf al-mafʿūl li-ifādat al-ʿumūm*, omission of the object to convey universality. The verse thus first asserts creation as such — the origination of all things from nothing — before the next verse narrows the lens to a single created thing, the human being.

This is why the commentator cited in the source material reads *created* in verse one as a reference to God's creating all that exists, with verse two supplying the particular case. The movement is from the cosmos to the embryo in the space of one breath.

**Why Creation Is the First Divine Attribute Mentioned**

Of all that could have been said about God at the first moment of revelation, why *creation*? The reasoning is logically prior to everything else in religion.

If God created you, then He owns you; if He owns you, His command over you is not an imposition but a right. If He created you, He knows you: *"Does He who created not know? And He is the Subtle, the Aware"* (67:14). If He created you from nothing, He can raise you after death, which is the argument the Qur'an makes again and again against those who denied resurrection: *"And he sets forth for Us a parable and forgets his own creation, saying: Who will give life to bones when they are decayed? Say: He will give them life who produced them the first time, and He is Knower of all creation"* (36:78–79).

So the first verse of the Qur'an is not merely devotional; it is the foundation stone of an argument. Establish that God creates, and revelation, accountability, and resurrection all follow as consequences. Deny it, and nothing else in the Book can be argued for.

**Knowledge Anchored in God: The Verse's Enduring Principle**

There is a reason Muslim civilization read this verse as its intellectual charter. The command is not "Recite" alone, which would sanctify information as such; it is "Recite *in the Name of thy Lord who created*." Knowledge is commanded, and in the same sentence it is oriented. Learning that is detached from the Creator can build instruments of great power without producing a single reason to use them well; learning tethered to the Creator carries its own ethics with it, because the learner never forgets that he is a creature studying another creature under the gaze of the One who made both.

The practical consequence for an ordinary reader today is direct. Every act of study — a student before an examination, a physician before a diagnosis, an engineer before a design, a parent before a difficult conversation — can be entered under this verse's rubric: *in the Name of thy Lord who created*. That phrase converts a technical task into an act of worship and simultaneously restrains it, because a person who is genuinely conscious of working in God's Name will not falsify data, will not exploit a client, and will not use his competence to harm.

**The First Command Is Still Open**

One final observation. The imperative *iqraʾ* was addressed to a particular man on a particular night, but the Qur'an preserved it in the present tense of every reader's encounter. Whoever opens the Book is met by its first revealed word, still in the imperative, still addressed to a person who cannot yet do what is being asked: *Recite*. And the same enabling clause is still attached to it: *in the Name of thy Lord who created*. The one who commands the recitation supplies the capacity for it.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:2

> **created man from a clinging clot.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Universe to the Womb in One Step**

The previous verse asserted creation absolutely, with no object: *the Lord who created*. Now the lens narrows to a single specimen out of everything that exists — *al-insān*, the human being — and to a single moment of his making. The rhetorical movement is deliberate and devastating in its economy. First: God creates everything. Then: and here is what He made *you* out of.

Al-Ṭabarī and others note the article in *al-insān*: it is generic, denoting the species rather than one individual, so the statement applies to every human being who has ever lived. Whatever a man imagines himself to be — chieftain of Quraysh, emperor, celebrity, scholar — his personal manufacturing record is identical to that of the slave he despises.

**The Word ʿAlaq: What the Arabic Actually Says**

*ʿAlaq* is the plural or collective form of *ʿalaqah*, from the root *ʿ-l-q*, whose primary meaning is *to hang, to cling, to be suspended, to attach oneself to something*. From this root Arabic derives *ʿallaqa* (to hang something up), *ʿalāqah* (a relationship — an attachment between two parties), *taʿalluq* (emotional attachment or dependency), and *ʿilāqah* (the strap by which a sword or waterskin is hung).

Classical lexicographers give *ʿalaqah* three overlapping applications: (1) a clot of congealed blood; (2) a leech, the small aquatic creature that attaches itself to a host and draws blood; (3) anything that clings or hangs suspended. The commentators of the Qur'an generally rendered it "a clot of blood," which is the most common English translation, but the root-sense of *clinging* is inseparable from the word and is why many modern translators prefer "a clinging clot" or "a clinging form."

The word thus contains, in three letters, a description of the earliest human being: something small, dark, blood-rich, hanging attached to the wall of the womb, drawing its nourishment from another body. It is difficult to imagine a more precise term available in seventh-century Arabic — a language with no anatomical vocabulary for embryology — for the stage in question.

**Where the Clot Falls in the Qur'an's Account of Gestation**

This verse gives one stage; other passages give the sequence. The most detailed is in Sūrat al-Ḥajj, which invokes the stages of human development as an argument for the resurrection:

> *"O mankind! If you are in doubt concerning the Resurrection, then [consider that] We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging clot, then from a lump of flesh, formed and unformed, that We may make clear to you. And We cause what We will to remain in the wombs for an appointed term, then We bring you forth as infants, then that you may reach your maturity."* (Qur'an 22:5)

And in Sūrat al-Muʾminūn, with the stages after the clot spelled out:

> *"And indeed We created man from an extract of clay. Then We placed him as a drop in a secure lodging. Then We made the drop into a clinging clot, then We made the clot into a lump, then We made the lump into bones, then We clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought him forth as another creation. So blessed is God, the best of creators."* (Qur'an 23:12–14)

And, in the sūrah that follows this one in chronological themes of denial:

> *"Was he not a drop of sperm emitted? Then he was a clinging clot, and He created and fashioned, and made of him two mates, male and female. Is not such a One able to give life to the dead?"* (Qur'an 75:37–40)

The sequence in these passages is consistent: *turāb* (dust) → *nuṭfah* (a drop) → *ʿalaqah* (a clinging clot) → *muḍghah* (a chewed-looking lump) → *ʿiẓām* (bones) → *laḥm* (flesh) → *khalqan ākhar* ("another creation"). The verse under discussion names the third stage.

**The Prophetic Report on the Stages**

The stages are also given in a well-known ḥadīth recorded by both al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Ibn Masʿūd:

> *"Verily the creation of each one of you is brought together in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop, then he is a clinging clot for the like of that, then he is a lump of flesh for the like of that. Then an angel is sent to him and breathes into him the spirit, and he is commanded with four matters: to write down his provision, his lifespan, his deeds, and whether he will be wretched or happy."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643)

Two things deserve notice. First, the ḥadīth confirms the ordering of the Qur'anic terms. Second, and more importantly for the theology of this sūrah, it links biological formation with divinely written destiny — the same coupling of *creation* and *knowledge* that runs through verses 1–5. The embryo is not only being formed; it is being *written about*. The pen appears in the womb before it appears in verse 4.

**Why This Particular Stage Was Chosen**

Of the several stages available, why does the first revelation mention the clot?

Because it is the least impressive. Dust is common but neutral; the drop is a beginning; bones and flesh already resemble a person. But *ʿalaqah* is the stage at which the future human being is at his most obscure — a speck of clotted blood clinging to a wall, invisible without magnification, indistinguishable to the naked eye from waste. The Qur'an selects precisely this image and holds it up to the man who considers himself self-sufficient — which is exactly what verse 7 will accuse him of being.

The rhetorical architecture of the sūrah depends on this. In verses 6–7 the human being will be charged with transgressing *because he considers himself beyond need*. Verse 2 has already answered him in advance: you were a clot. Al-Qurṭubī's reading brings this out — the mention of humble origin is placed at the head of the chapter as the standing refutation of every arrogance that appears later in it.

**The Bridge to Sūrat al-Tīn**

The source commentary makes a subtle observation worth preserving and expanding. In the arrangement of the muṣḥaf, the sūrah immediately preceding this one contains the lines:

> *"We indeed created man in the most beautiful stature; then We cast him to the lowest of the low, save those who believe and perform righteous deeds."* (Qur'an 95:4–5)

Read against those verses, 96:1–2 can be heard as a commentary on them: *created* in verse 1 corresponds to the most beautiful stature, and *created man from a clinging clot* corresponds to the recollection of lowliness. The human being is both — a creature of noble form and of contemptible origin, and his moral life consists in remembering both facts at once. Forget the first and he despairs; forget the second and he becomes Abū Jahl.

**Two Beginnings, One Lesson: Adam and Every Man**

The Qur'an describes two distinct human origins. Adam was made from clay: *"Indeed, the likeness of Jesus with God is as the likeness of Adam: He created him from dust, then said to him, 'Be,' and he was"* (3:59). His descendants are made from the drop: *"then He made his progeny from an extract of a base fluid"* (32:8). Both accounts humble. Neither origin is glorious in itself; the glory is entirely in what God did with the material.

This is the theological point that the doctrine of *tawḥīd* rests upon in this sūrah. Nothing in clay or in a clot explains sight, speech, conscience, memory, or the capacity to receive revelation. If a clot became a man who can read, the explanation lies wholly outside the clot. The verse is therefore not merely descriptive; it is an argument for the existence and continuous agency of a Creator, delivered in the form of a biological fact that every listener could verify in his own family.

**The Resurrection Argument Embedded Here**

The Qur'an repeatedly uses embryology to answer those who found bodily resurrection absurd. The logic is a simple *a fortiori*: the One who produced a speaking, reasoning person out of a clinging clot has already performed a greater feat than reassembling that same person from dust.

> *"He it is who originates creation, then brings it back — and that is easier for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27)

Anyone who accepts that he began as a clot has, in principle, already conceded the possibility of resurrection; he simply has not noticed. The verse plants that concession in the first minutes of the Qur'an's existence.

**Equality Written into Biology**

There is a social consequence to this verse that the early community felt keenly. Makkan society was organized by descent: the honour of Hāshim, the standing of Makhzūm, the contempt reserved for slaves and clients. Into that hierarchy came a revelation declaring that every human being, without exception, began as the same unremarkable clinging speck.

The sūrah itself will apply the principle within a few lines. In verse 10 the one being prevented from praying is called *ʿabd* — a servant, a slave — and the one preventing him is a chieftain. The chapter that opens by telling all men they came from a clot ends by telling one particular nobleman that his prayerful "slave" outranks him.

The same logic is stated openly elsewhere:

> *"O mankind! Truly We created you from a male and a female, and made you peoples and tribes that you may come to know one another. Surely the most noble of you before God is the most reverent of you."* (Qur'an 49:13)

**What the Verse Asks of a Person Today**

Practically, this verse is a discipline of memory. Modern life offers many mechanisms for forgetting one's origin: titles, followings, achievements, wealth, credentials, the flattering mirror of a curated public image. The verse prescribes a counterweight that costs nothing and takes a second: remember what you were made from.

ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is reported to have said that a man is safe from arrogance so long as he remembers two things — his beginning and his end. Between a clot and a grave there is a brief interval in which a person may be given wealth, strength, and influence; the interval is real, but it is an interval, not an identity.

And there is a gentler application. The person who is crushed by a sense of worthlessness may also read this verse and find relief in it: God took a clinging clot and made from it a being capable of knowing Him. If that is what He does with a clot, no human being has grounds to regard himself as beyond repair.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:3

> **Recite! And thy Lord is the Most Noble,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command Repeated: Why Twice?**

For the second time in three verses the imperative comes: *iqraʾ*. Repetition in the Qur'an is never accidental, and the commentators offered several explanations for this one, most of which are complementary rather than competing.

Some, including a strand reported in al-Qurṭubī, held that the repetition is *taʾkīd* — emphasis — reinforcing a command given to a man who had three times protested that he could not comply. Given the scene in the cave, this reading has obvious force. He said *"I am not one who recites"*; the command simply returned.

Others, including al-Rāzī, held that the two imperatives have different objects: the first is a command to recite *for himself* — to receive and internalize the revelation — and the second is a command to recite *to others* — to convey it. On this reading verse 1 makes him a reader and verse 3 makes him a messenger, and the mission is complete in outline before the fifth verse is reached.

A third reading, favoured by those attentive to the structure of the passage, notes that each imperative is followed by a different divine warrant. The first is grounded in *creation*: recite, in the Name of the Lord *who created*. The second is grounded in *generosity and teaching*: recite, for thy Lord is *the Most Noble, who taught by the pen*. The two commands thus rest on the two great categories of divine act — bringing into being, and bestowing knowledge upon what has been brought into being.

**"Wa rabbuka al-akram": The Grammar of Reassurance**

The Arabic is *iqraʾ wa rabbuka al-akram*. The clause beginning with *wa* is what grammarians call a *jumlah ḥāliyyah* or a circumstantial/causal statement, which is why translators render it either as a parallel declaration ("Recite! And thy Lord is the Most Noble") or as a reason ("Recite, for thy Lord is the Most Noble"). Both are defensible and the meaning converges: the ground on which the frightened, unlettered man is to attempt the impossible is the character of the One commanding him.

This is psychologically exact. A person told to do what he cannot do will collapse unless he is told something about the one who is asking. The verse supplies precisely that: your Lord is *al-akram*. He is not a taskmaster extracting labour; He is the most generous of all givers, and He does not command a servant to produce what He has not equipped him to produce.

**Al-Akram: The Superlative of Karam**

*Al-akram* is the *ism al-tafḍīl*, the elative or superlative form, from the root *k-r-m*. The noun *karam* is one of the great words of Arabic moral vocabulary, and it does not map neatly onto a single English term. Its field of meaning includes:

- **Generosity** — giving abundantly, without being asked, without expectation of return, and without reproaching the recipient afterwards.
- **Nobility** — an unblemished lineage and an elevated station.
- **Excellence of character** — the whole cluster of virtues that make a person admirable: forbearance, magnanimity, forgiveness, keeping of covenants.
- **Intrinsic worth** — as when the Qur'an calls the Qur'an itself *karīm* (56:77) and the Throne *karīm* (23:116).

In pre-Islamic Arabia, *karam* was the supreme virtue of the ideal man: the chief who slaughtered his last camel for a guest, who protected the one who sought his protection, who forgave the insult he had the power to avenge. Poetry is saturated with it. The source commentary makes an observation of real historical interest here: in pre-Islamic verse, *karīm* was said of *people*, not of God. The Qur'an's application of the superlative *al-akram* to the Lord therefore introduced to the Arabs an aspect of the divine they had not previously articulated. Their gods were to be feared, bargained with, and placated. This Lord was to be recognized as more generous than the most generous man they could imagine.

**Why the Superlative Is Not Merely Rhetorical**

There is a logical argument buried in the elative form. Human generosity, however great, is limited in three ways: the giver's resources are finite; the giver may expect something in return, if only gratitude or reputation; and the giver gives out of something he did not himself create.

Divine generosity is free of all three limits. God's resources are inexhaustible: *"And if you were to count the blessings of God, you could never number them"* (14:34). He gains nothing from the giving: *"O mankind! You are the ones in need of God, and God is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy"* (35:15). And He gives out of what He Himself originated, giving both the gift and the recipient's capacity to receive it.

Hence *al-akram*, in the absolute: not merely the most generous among givers, but the only giver whose generosity has no external source and no motive of self-interest.

**The Two Supreme Gifts Named in This Sūrah**

Read in sequence, verses 1–5 identify what the divine generosity has actually produced, and the list is short and total: *existence* and *knowledge*.

He created you when you were nothing (v. 1–2). He then taught you what you did not know (v. 4–5). Between those two acts lies the whole of what a human being is. Remove creation and there is no one; remove teaching and there is a creature without orientation, unable to name a single thing, unable to distinguish benefit from harm.

The commentator cited in the source material captures this: God blesses human beings with the greatest of blessings — creation and revelation — and it is on this basis that He is named *al-akram*. Every other gift a person can enumerate (health, family, wealth, security) is a subdivision of the first; every other guidance (conscience, teachers, books) is a subdivision of the second.

**Generosity in the Manner, Not Only the Substance**

The classical scholars observed that *karam* concerns not just *what* is given but *how*. Three features of divine giving are noted repeatedly in the tafsīr tradition, and they are visible in this very sūrah:

*He gives before being asked.* Muhammad ﷺ did not petition for prophethood. He went to the cave to worship, not to negotiate. The revelation arrived unrequested.

*He gives to those who have not earned it and even to those who oppose Him.* The disbelievers addressed later in this sūrah were breathing His air and eating His provision while denying Him. Sustenance in this life is given to believer and rejecter alike, which is itself an argument for divine generosity rather than mere transaction:

> *"Each We supply — these and those — from the bounty of thy Lord; and the bounty of thy Lord is not restricted."* (Qur'an 17:20)

*He accepts little and returns much.* The Prophet ﷺ transmitted the divine saying:

> *"Whoever intends a good deed and does not do it, God writes it with Him as a complete good deed; and if he intends it and does it, God writes it with Him as ten good deeds up to seven hundred times over, or more. And whoever intends an evil deed and does not do it, God writes it with Him as a complete good deed; and if he intends it and does it, God writes it as one evil deed."* (al-Bukhārī 6491; Muslim 131)

That arithmetic — an unlimited multiplier on good, a strict single count on evil, and credit for an unperformed intention — is the operational definition of *al-akram*.

**The Nobility That Forgives**

Ibn Kathīr and others connect *karam* especially to forbearance and pardon. The generous one is the one who has the power to punish and withholds it, who covers faults instead of publicizing them. This is why the Prophet ﷺ taught the supplication for the Night of Decree in these terms:

> *"O God, You are Pardoning, You love pardon, so pardon me."* (al-Tirmidhī 3513, from ʿĀʾishah)

And why the divine saying records:

> *"O son of Adam, were your sins to reach the clouds of the sky, and then you sought My forgiveness, I would forgive you and not mind. O son of Adam, were you to come to Me with sins nearly filling the earth, and then you met Me without associating anything with Me, I would bring you forgiveness nearly the like of it."* (al-Tirmidhī 3540, from Anas)

For the reader of this sūrah, that attribute has practical urgency. The chapter is about to describe human rebellion in unsparing terms (vv. 6–16). Yet the God who describes it has already named Himself *al-akram* before the accusation is made. The door is identified before the offence is catalogued.

**Why This Attribute Anchors the Command to Learn**

There is a specific reason the imperative to recite is paired with generosity rather than with power. Had the verse read "Recite, for thy Lord is the Most Mighty," the relationship would be one of compulsion. Instead the reader is told that the One asking him to learn is the One who is most inclined to give. Learning is therefore framed as *receiving from an inexhaustible source*, not as extracting something scarce.

That framing has consequences for the character of the student. A knowledge-seeker who believes he is drawing from a limited well becomes competitive, secretive, and anxious. One who believes he is receiving from *al-akram* becomes generous with what he learns, because he did not pay for it and cannot exhaust it. The scholarly ethic of the Muslim tradition — the free teaching circles in mosques, the refusal of many early scholars to take payment for ḥadīth transmission, the obligation to answer a sincere question — grew in the soil of this verse.

**The Practical Discipline: Ask Much**

If God is *al-akram*, then the size of a request is not an obstacle. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"When one of you supplicates, let him be resolute in the request, and let him not say, 'O God, give me if You wish' — for none can compel Him."* (al-Bukhārī 6338; Muslim 2679)

And in another narration, when asking, *"let him ask abundantly, for he is asking his Lord."*

The person who understands verse 3 stops rationing his prayers. He does not decide in advance which of his needs are too small to mention or too large to hope for. He is dealing with *al-akram*, whose treasuries are not diminished by giving and whose generosity, as this sūrah's first listeners were being taught for the first time, exceeds the most legendary generosity in Arab memory.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:4

> **who taught by the Pen,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Generosity Demonstrated: How the Most Noble Gives**

Verse 3 named God *al-akram*, the Most Noble and Most Generous. Verses 4 and 5 supply the evidence. The grammar makes this explicit: *alladhī ʿallama bil-qalam* is a relative clause describing *rabbuka al-akram* — "thy Lord is the Most Noble, *the One who taught by the Pen*." The nobility just claimed is immediately cashed out in a concrete act, and the act chosen is not conquest, not the granting of wealth, not even the giving of life. It is *teaching*.

This is a remarkable selection. When human beings praise a great benefactor they usually list what he built or gave away. When God introduces His own generosity at the opening of the final revelation, the flagship gift is instruction.

**The Verb ʿAllama: Teaching as an Intensive, Sustained Act**

*ʿAllama* is the second form (*faʿʿala*) of the root *ʿ-l-m*, "to know." The doubling of the middle radical in this form typically conveys intensity, causation, or repetition. *ʿAlima* means "he knew"; *ʿallama* means "he caused to know, he taught" — and the intensive pattern suggests a process rather than a single transfer: teaching that is repeated, patient, graduated.

That morphological nuance matches the reality of revelation. The Qur'an was not handed down in a single volume on a single night; it descended over twenty-three years, verse by verse, situation by situation. The Qur'an itself explains the pedagogy:

> *"And those who disbelieve say: Why has the Qur'an not been sent down upon him all at once? Thus, that We may strengthen thy heart thereby, and We have recited it in a measured recitation."* (Qur'an 25:32)

God is not merely an informer; He is a *teacher*, and the form of the verb says so.

The same root gives *ʿilm* (knowledge), *ʿālim* (a knower, scholar), *ʿālam* (world — that by which the Maker is known), and *ʿalāmah* (a sign, a marker). The linguistic family itself suggests a worldview: the world is a set of signs, knowledge is the reading of them, and the scholar is the one who has learned to read.

**Al-Qalam: The Instrument That Defeats Death**

*Al-Qalam* — the pen — comes from the root *q-l-m*, meaning to trim, pare, or cut. A *qalam* was originally a reed cut and sharpened at the tip; from the same root come *qulāmah* (nail clippings) and *taqlīm al-aẓāfir* (trimming the nails). The Arabic word thus preserves the physical act of preparing the writing instrument: a reed cut to a point.

The definite article — *the* Pen, not *a* pen — invites the layered reading the tradition gave it, discussed below. But even at the plainest level, the significance of the instrument deserves attention.

Speech is the natural medium of knowledge; it is also the most fragile. A spoken word exists for a second and then depends entirely on the memory of whoever heard it. Writing changes the situation absolutely. It allows knowledge to outlive the knower, to travel beyond the range of a voice, to be checked, corrected, compared, and accumulated. Every civilization that has built anything cumulative has done so because writing let one generation begin where the previous one stopped.

A single seventh-century line thus identifies the mechanism of civilization itself and attributes it to God. And it does so in a society where literacy was almost absent: historians of early Islam note that only a small number of men in Makkah could write at the time of this revelation. The first revelation of the Qur'an praised, as a supreme divine gift, a technology that its immediate audience barely possessed.

**Three Pens: The Classical Typology**

The commentators, as the source material records, distinguished three principal kinds of pen, and the distinction is worth expanding because each level teaches something different.

*The first pen — the Pen of Destiny.* This is the Pen God created and commanded to write all that would be until the Day of Resurrection. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The first thing God created was the Pen. He said to it: 'Write.' It said: 'My Lord, what shall I write?' He said: 'Write the measure of all things until the Hour comes.'"* (Abū Dāwūd 4700; al-Tirmidhī 2155, from ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit)

Sūrah 68, which the tradition holds to be among the earliest revelations after this one, opens with an oath by this instrument: *"Nūn. By the Pen and what they inscribe"* (68:1). At this level, the pen signifies divine knowledge and decree: reality itself is *written* before it is enacted, which is why the Qur'an speaks of a *"Preserved Tablet"* (85:22) and of a *"Clear Register"* (36:12).

*The second pens — the pens of the recording angels.* These write the deeds of every human being:

> *"And indeed, appointed over you are watchers, noble scribes, who know whatever you do."* (Qur'an 82:10–12)

> *"He utters not a word but that with him is an observer ready."* (Qur'an 50:18)

The book of deeds handed to each person on the Day of Judgement is the product of these pens: *"Read thy book! Sufficient is thy soul this day as a reckoner against thee"* (17:14). It is a striking symmetry that the first command of the Qur'an is *Recite/Read*, and one of the last commands a human being will hear is also *Read* — this time his own record.

*The third pens — the pens of human beings.* The ordinary reed, quill, or keyboard by which people record and transmit what they know. That the Qur'an dignifies this humblest of the three by mentioning it in the first revelation is precisely the point: the human pen is a participation, at a creaturely level, in something God does at the level of creation.

**Teaching Through an Intermediary: The Theological Point**

There is a subtlety in the phrase *ʿallama bil-qalam* that the theologians noticed. God did not say "He taught," full stop. He said "He taught *by the pen*" — that is, through a means, an instrument, a secondary cause.

This establishes a principle that runs through Islamic thought: God, who needs no instrument, nevertheless works through instruments, and gratitude for the instrument is part of gratitude for Him. Rain comes from God, and through clouds. Healing comes from God, and through medicine. Knowledge comes from God, and through pens, teachers, books, and effort.

The consequence is a balanced attitude toward means. A believer neither worships the instrument nor despises it. He studies hard, buys the book, attends the class, keeps the notebook — and knows the whole time that the outcome is a gift, not a purchase. The Prophet ﷺ combined both attitudes in a single instruction:

> *"Be eager for what benefits you, seek help from God, and do not be incapable."* (Muslim 2664, from Abū Hurayrah)

**Writing as the Guardian of Revelation**

There is a historical dimension to this verse that could not have been visible to its first hearers. The religion inaugurated by these words became, within a generation, a religion of the written text in an unusually rigorous sense. The Prophet ﷺ appointed scribes for revelation — Zayd ibn Thābit, Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, Muʿāwiyah and others — who wrote each passage as it came on parchment, palm stalks, and shoulder blades, under his supervision, alongside the primary memorization by heart. Abū Bakr ordered the collection of these materials into a single codex after the deaths of many memorizers at the Battle of Yamāmah; ʿUthmān standardized and distributed copies to the major cities.

The insistence on *both* channels — memory and writing, chest and page, each verifying the other — is a direct outgrowth of a scripture whose first revelation honoured both the recited word (*iqraʾ*) and the written one (*bil-qalam*). Neither channel alone would have been as secure; together they produced a text whose stability across fourteen centuries and three continents has no close parallel among ancient books.

The same instinct produced the discipline of ḥadīth criticism, the writing down of chains of narration, the biographical dictionaries recording the reliability of tens of thousands of transmitters, the *ijāzah* system of certified transmission — an entire scholarly civilization built on the conviction that knowledge must be recorded, attributed, and checked.

**Why Reading Came First and Writing Second**

The source commentary preserves a fine observation: the mention of recitation (v. 1) precedes the mention of writing (v. 4) because, although both convey knowledge, speech can exist without writing but writing cannot exist without speech. Writing is a notation *of* speech; the sound is prior to the symbol.

The ordering also mirrors the life of the individual and of the species. A child speaks years before he writes. Human communities spoke for millennia before any of them inscribed. And the Qur'an itself was recited before it was written. The verse sequence follows the natural order of knowledge: hearing, then speaking, then recording.

**The Pen as a Moral Instrument**

Because the pen is powerful, the Qur'an also treats it as morally weighted. The longest verse in the Qur'an, the verse of debt (2:282), is essentially an instruction manual for the ethical use of writing: *"O you who believe! When you contract a debt for a stated term, write it down... and let a scribe write it between you in justice, and let no scribe refuse to write as God has taught him."*

That last clause — *"as God has taught him"* — is a direct echo of the verse under discussion. The scribe who was taught by God's generosity owes a duty of honesty in the exercise of the skill. Conversely, the Qur'an issues one of its most severe warnings against the misuse of writing:

> *"So woe to those who write the Book with their own hands, then say, 'This is from God,' that they may purchase thereby a small price. So woe to them for what their hands have written, and woe to them for what they earn."* (Qur'an 2:79)

The same instrument that preserves truth can manufacture falsehood at scale. A lie spoken dies with the hearing; a lie written can deceive for centuries. Anyone who publishes — a journalist, a textbook author, a scholar, a person composing a message to thousands of followers — is operating an instrument that this verse identifies as a divine gift and that verse 2:79 warns can incur a divine curse.

**A Contemporary Reading**

The pen has, in the modern world, multiplied into forms unimaginable to the first audience: printing presses, cameras, hard drives, servers, algorithms that recommend to hundreds of millions what to read next. The principle of the verse is indifferent to the technology. Every instrument by which knowledge is fixed and transmitted is a descendant of the pen praised here, and every such instrument carries the same double character: a gift from *al-akram*, and a trust that can be betrayed.

For an individual, the verse suggests three practical habits. *Write what you learn* — the tradition's insistence on recording is not nostalgia; unwritten knowledge decays. *Verify what you read* — a civilization that built the science of chains of transmission would not recognize the modern habit of forwarding unverified claims. And *acknowledge the source* — the one who taught by the pen is God, so intellectual arrogance is the least defensible of all arrogances, a point the sūrah is about to make with considerable force in verses 6 and 7.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:5

> **taught man that which he knew not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Completion of the First Revelation**

With this verse the first revelation ends. Five lines: a command, a Creator, an origin, a Benefactor, an instrument, and now the substance of the gift itself — *ʿallama al-insāna mā lam yaʿlam*, "He taught the human being what he did not know."

The sentence is deceptively plain, and yet it makes a claim about the entire human condition. Every item of knowledge any person possesses was, at some earlier moment, absent from him. There is no exception. The most learned scholar who ever lived began knowing nothing, and the verse traces the entire distance from that nothing to whatever he now knows back to a single source.

**"Mā lam yaʿlam": The Emphasis on Prior Ignorance**

The construction is precise. Not simply "He taught man," but "He taught man *that which he did not know*." The relative clause insists on the starting point. Arabic could have said *ʿallama al-insān al-ʿilm* — "He taught man knowledge" — but that would have described a transfer without recalling the recipient's original poverty.

The Qur'an states the same fact directly elsewhere:

> *"And God brought you forth from the wombs of your mothers knowing nothing, and gave you hearing, sight, and hearts, that perhaps you may give thanks."* (Qur'an 16:78)

Notice the concluding clause: *that perhaps you may give thanks*. The recollection of former ignorance exists to produce gratitude. A person who forgets that he once knew nothing will experience his knowledge as an attribute of himself; a person who remembers will experience it as a deposit placed with him.

This is the precise psychological hinge on which the sūrah turns. Verses 6 and 7 will describe a man who transgresses because *he considers himself self-sufficient*. Verse 5 has already identified the fault line: forgetting that one was taught.

**Who Is "Man" Here? Three Readings**

The commentators offered three interpretations of *al-insān* in this verse, and al-Qurṭubī records them.

*Adam.* Some held that the reference is to the first human being, whom God taught the names of all things:

> *"And He taught Adam the names, all of them; then He presented them to the angels and said, 'Inform Me of the names of these, if you are truthful.' They said, 'Glory be to Thee! We have no knowledge save what Thou hast taught us. Truly Thou art the Knower, the Wise.'"* (Qur'an 2:31–32)

That episode is the archetype of this verse. Adam's superiority over the angels was not strength, longevity, or purity of substance — it was *taught knowledge*, and the angels' own confession (*"no knowledge save what Thou hast taught us"*) is verse 5 spoken by its most exalted creatures.

Ibn Kathīr draws exactly this connection in his comment on the sūrah: the human being is honoured and ennobled through knowledge, the very faculty that distinguished Adam from the angels.

*Muhammad ﷺ.* Others held that *man* refers to the Prophet ﷺ specifically, since the Qur'an addresses him in almost identical language:

> *"And God has sent down upon thee the Book and Wisdom, and has taught thee what thou didst not know. And God's bounty upon thee is immense."* (Qur'an 4:113)

On this reading the verse is a direct reassurance in the cave: the man who has just said three times *"I am not one who recites"* is told that inability is no obstacle, because teaching the unknowing is precisely what this Lord does.

*Every human being.* Most commentators, as the source notes, take the general sense: the verse describes the manner in which God teaches all people, not only prophets. This reading absorbs the other two rather than excluding them — Adam and Muhammad ﷺ are the two supreme instances of a universal rule.

**Two Channels of Divine Teaching**

Islamic scholarship traditionally distinguishes two ways in which God fulfils this verse.

*Teaching through revelation (waḥy).* Knowledge that human faculties could never generate on their own: the reality of God's names and attributes, the purpose of creation, what happens after death, the details of what pleases and displeases Him. No amount of laboratory work or philosophical reflection could establish the number of daily prayers or the fate of the soul. This category arrives only from above, through prophets:

> *"Just as We sent among you a Messenger from among yourselves, reciting to you Our signs, purifying you, teaching you the Book and Wisdom, and teaching you what you did not know."* (Qur'an 2:151)

That verse contains the identical phrase — *wa yuʿallimukum mā lam takūnū taʿlamūn* — applied to the prophetic mission.

*Teaching through created faculties and experience.* Knowledge that human beings acquire by using the instruments God installed in them: senses, reason, memory, language, and the capacity for inference and experiment. The Qur'an regards this too as divine teaching, because the instruments and their reliability are His gift. Every discovery is a reading of signs He placed and a use of faculties He built.

The verse does not distinguish the two, which is itself significant: the Qur'an does not treat "religious knowledge" and "worldly knowledge" as belonging to different owners. The astronomer and the jurist are both receiving from the same Teacher, differing only in which of His signs they are reading.

**The Argument Against Intellectual Arrogance**

There is a strict logical argument in this verse, and it deserves to be laid out plainly.

Every human being begins in total ignorance. Whatever he later knows must therefore have come from outside himself: from his senses, from other people, from the structure of his mind, from revelation. Not one of these did he manufacture. His eyes were given; his brain was given; his language was inherited from a community he did not choose; his teachers were provided; the very laws of nature that make inference reliable were established without consulting him.

Therefore no one has grounds to be arrogant about knowledge. The one thing a knowledgeable person can honestly claim credit for is effort — and even the capacity for effort was given.

Al-Ghazālī and others noted that of all forms of pride, intellectual pride is the most spiritually dangerous, because it disguises itself as merit and because it directly attacks the posture required for further learning. A person proud of his wealth may still listen; a person proud of his knowledge has stopped listening by definition. The Prophet ﷺ described the essence of arrogance in a way that captures this:

> *"Arrogance is rejecting the truth and looking down on people."* (Muslim 91, from Ibn Masʿūd)

Rejecting the truth is the epistemic half; contempt is the social half. Both are refusals to receive — from God and from His creatures respectively.

**The Prophet's Own Prayer for More**

If knowledge is entirely a gift, then the correct response is not accumulation for its own sake but petition. The Qur'an contains only one command to the Prophet ﷺ to ask for an increase of anything, and the thing is knowledge:

> *"And say: My Lord, increase me in knowledge."* (Qur'an 20:114)

The Prophet ﷺ also taught a supplication that treats knowledge as something that can be beneficial or otherwise:

> *"O God, I ask You for beneficial knowledge, wholesome provision, and accepted deeds."* (Ibn Mājah 925, from Umm Salamah)

And he sought refuge from its opposite:

> *"O God, I seek refuge in You from knowledge that does not benefit, from a heart that is not humble, from a soul that is not satisfied, and from a supplication that is not answered."* (Muslim 2722, from Zayd ibn Arqam)

The category of *knowledge that does not benefit* is a necessary corollary of verse 5. If knowledge is a trust from the Teacher, then it is answerable: it must produce something. Information hoarded, or used for harm, or held without changing the holder, is a gift misapplied.

**The Honour of the Learner and the Teacher**

Because this verse locates human dignity in taught knowledge, Islamic civilization placed extraordinary value on both learning and instructing. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever treads a path in search of knowledge, God will make easy for him thereby a path to Paradise. The angels lower their wings in approval of the seeker of knowledge, and everything in the heavens and the earth — even the fish in the depths of the water — seeks forgiveness for the scholar. The superiority of the scholar over the worshipper is like the superiority of the moon over the rest of the stars. The scholars are the heirs of the prophets; the prophets bequeathed neither dinar nor dirham, but bequeathed knowledge — so whoever takes it has taken an abundant share."* (Abū Dāwūd 3641; al-Tirmidhī 2682, from Abū al-Dardāʾ)

And of teaching:

> *"The best of you are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it."* (al-Bukhārī 5027, from ʿUthmān)

> *"Whoever calls to guidance shall have a reward like the rewards of those who follow him, without that diminishing their rewards in the least."* (Muslim 2674, from Abū Hurayrah)

These reports form a coherent economy: knowledge is given freely by God, so it is to be passed on freely by His servants, and the passing on multiplies rather than depletes it. That is the human imitation of *al-akram*.

**Knowledge, Freedom, and Responsibility**

There is an ʿaqīdah dimension here. If human beings are taught, then they are accountable — because accountability presupposes the possibility of knowing. The Qur'an is explicit that God does not punish those who were never informed:

> *"And We never punish until We have sent a messenger."* (Qur'an 17:15)

Divine justice therefore rests on divine teaching. God did not create beings, leave them ignorant, and then judge them. He created, He taught, and then He holds responsible. Verse 5 is thus quietly one of the pillars of the Qur'an's theodicy: the same generosity that gave knowledge is what makes the coming judgement fair. Verse 8 — *"Truly unto thy Lord is the return"* — depends on verse 5 for its justice.

**The First Five Verses as a Complete Worldview**

Standing back, the opening passage of the Qur'an contains a compressed philosophy:

*Reality has an Author.* He created — absolutely (v. 1).
*Human beings are creatures, and humble ones.* From a clinging clot (v. 2).
*The Author's disposition toward His creatures is generosity.* He is *al-akram* (v. 3).
*He communicates, and He preserves communication.* He taught by the pen (v. 4).
*Human dignity consists in received knowledge.* He taught man what he did not know (v. 5).
*And the appropriate response is to receive it.* Recite (vv. 1, 3).

Nothing in that sequence is arbitrary, and the order cannot be rearranged without breaking the logic. It is a remarkable thing for five lines delivered in a cave to a man who could not read.

**What Happens When Verse 5 Is Forgotten**

The sūrah's second half, revealed years later, is essentially a case study in the denial of verse 5. Abū Jahl — whose given name ʿAmr ibn Hishām was replaced by the community with a title meaning "father of ignorance" — was among the most intelligent and eloquent men of Quraysh. He was not lacking in information. What he lacked was the recognition that his intelligence was a loan.

The result is described immediately: *"Nay, truly man transgresses, in that he considers himself self-sufficient."* A person who believes his knowledge originates in himself will eventually believe he does not need the One who gave it. That is the road from verse 5 to verse 6, and the sūrah walks it in a single step.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:6

> **Nay, truly man transgresses**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A New Revelation, Years Later**

At this verse the sūrah changes register, and with it, historical setting. The commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī and others — report that verses 6 to 19 descended several years after the opening five, during the public phase of the Makkan mission, and that their immediate occasion was the conduct of Abū Jahl ʿAmr ibn Hishām, the Quraysh chieftain who had made it his business to obstruct the Prophet ﷺ and, in particular, to prevent him from praying at the Kaʿbah.

The two revelations were nevertheless joined in one chapter, and the join is an argument. Verses 1–5 described a Lord who creates, ennobles, and teaches. Verse 6 begins the description of how a creature who has received all of that can still turn against the Giver. Placed together, gift and refusal, the chapter becomes a complete moral anatomy.

**Kallā: A Word of Rebuke and Reversal**

The verse opens with *kallā*, translated here as "Nay." It is a particle of emphatic negation and rebuke, used in the Qur'an thirty-three times, all of them in the second half of the Book — the Makkan sūrahs addressed to a society in denial. Grammarians describe its function in two ways: as *radʿ wa zajr*, a rebuke and a restraint ("Stop! Not so!"), and as *ḥaqqan*, an affirmation of what follows ("Truly, indeed").

Both senses are active here. The word first slams a door on something and then opens onto a statement of fact.

What is being rejected? The commentators suggest that *kallā* here dismisses the whole attitude that verses 1–5 should have made impossible. God created you, ennobled you, taught you what you did not know — *and yet*: no, man does not respond with gratitude. He transgresses. Al-Ājurrī's line preserved in the source captures it: despite the great blessings of creation and knowledge, people deny them and are ungrateful for them, and so become deniers and rebels.

The particle also carries a tone that any reader can feel. It is the sound of a claim being cut off mid-sentence. It is what one says to a person walking confidently toward a cliff.

**Al-Insān Again: The Same Word, the Other Half of the Story**

The verse uses the same word as verse 2: *al-insān*, the human being. This is not accidental. The sūrah's two halves are held together by this single term. In verse 2, *al-insān* is the passive recipient of creation — a clot given form. In verse 6, the same *al-insān* is the active rebel.

The lexicographers derive *insān* from one of two roots, and both are illuminating. Some derive it from *uns* — familiarity, intimacy, sociability — because the human being is made for relationship and cannot flourish alone. Others derive it from *nisyān* — forgetfulness — because forgetting is the human being's defining weakness. The Qur'an itself notes this of Adam:

> *"And We had made a covenant with Adam before, but he forgot; and We found in him no firm resolve."* (Qur'an 20:115)

Verse 6 is *nisyān* in action. Nothing in the verse says that man *disproves* God's blessings; it says he *transgresses* despite them. Rebellion here begins in forgetting, not in argument.

**Ṭaghā: The Root That Means Overflowing the Limit**

The verb is *yaṭghā*, from the root *ṭ-gh-y*, and it is one of the Qur'an's most physically vivid moral terms. Its literal sense is *to exceed the proper bound, to overflow, to burst past a limit*. Arabic uses it of floodwater: when a river rises past its banks and destroys what lies beyond, the verb is *ṭaghā*. The Qur'an itself uses it precisely so of Noah's flood:

> *"Truly, when the water overflowed (ṭaghā al-māʾ), We carried you in the sailing ship."* (Qur'an 69:11)

From the same root come *ṭāghūt* (a false object of worship, anything that exceeds its rightful station and claims what belongs to God) and *ṭughyān* (tyranny, insolent excess). Pharaoh is the Qur'an's chief exhibit:

> *"Go to Pharaoh; truly he has transgressed (innahu ṭaghā)."* (Qur'an 20:24; 79:17)

The metaphor is exact and worth dwelling on. Water is not evil. Water is life — *"and We made from water every living thing"* (21:30). What makes a flood destructive is not the substance but the breach of boundary. Precisely the same is true of the human capacities named in this sūrah. Intelligence, ambition, wealth, strength, appetite, even religious zeal — none of these is evil. They become destructive when they overflow the channel God set for them.

This gives the verse a definition of sin that is remarkably free of puritanism. Sin, in this framing, is not the possession of desires or powers; it is their overflow past limit. And the corresponding virtue, *taqwā*, is not the suppression of the river but the maintenance of the banks — an awareness of where the edges are.

**Transgression as the Predictable Result of Unrestrained Capacity**

Why does the verse present transgression as a general human tendency rather than the vice of a particular villain? Because the Qur'an is describing a mechanism, not merely condemning a man.

The mechanism is this: capacity, when it is not consciously referred back to its Giver, tends to expand until something stops it. A person with a little power will use it up to the boundary of what he can get away with; a person with more power will extend the boundary. The restraint that human beings feel in weakness is often not virtue but incapacity — as becomes obvious the moment capacity increases.

This is why the Qur'an so often couples transgression with prosperity rather than hardship. Qārūn transgressed with wealth (28:76–81); Pharaoh with kingship (79:17–24); the people of ʿĀd with strength — *"Who is greater than us in power?"* (41:15). The sūrah under discussion will name the specific fuel in the very next verse: *seeing oneself as self-sufficient*.

**Why the Statement Is Universal but Not Deterministic**

A careful reader will ask: does *"truly man transgresses"* mean that every human being inevitably rebels? The Qur'an's own usage answers this. It repeatedly makes sweeping statements about *al-insān* and then names the exceptions:

> *"Truly man was created anxious: fretful when evil touches him and grudging when good touches him — save the performers of prayer."* (Qur'an 70:19–22)

> *"Truly man is in loss — save those who believe and perform righteous deeds, and exhort one another to truth and exhort one another to patience."* (Qur'an 103:2–3)

The pattern is diagnostic, not fatalistic. These verses describe the human default when the soul is left to itself, and then identify what changes the default: faith, prayer, righteous action, and mutual exhortation. Human beings are not condemned to transgression; they *drift* toward it, exactly as an unsteered boat drifts with the current. Steering is possible, and the sūrah names the steering mechanism in its final verse: *prostrate and draw near*.

This preserves free will and moral responsibility, which the coming judgement (v. 8) requires. If transgression were compelled, the warning of verses 15–18 would be unjust.

**The Nafs That Commands Evil, and Its Rehabilitation**

The Qur'an's psychology gives a name to the inner faculty that overflows: *al-nafs al-ammārah bi-l-sūʾ*, the soul that incites to evil, in the words of Joseph:

> *"Yet I do not absolve my own soul. Truly the soul commands to evil, save that whereupon my Lord has mercy. Truly my Lord is Forgiving, Merciful."* (Qur'an 12:53)

The Prophet ﷺ built the acknowledgment of this into the opening of every sermon:

> *"...and we seek refuge in God from the evil of our own souls and from our evil deeds."* (Muslim 868, from Ibn Masʿūd)

Notice the order: from the evil of *our souls* first, and then from *our deeds*. The deed is downstream. Verse 6 names the current; the following verse will name the spring that feeds it.

**A Mirror Rather Than an Accusation**

There is a pastoral point in the verse's use of the generic noun. It would have been easy for the Qur'an to say "Abū Jahl transgresses" — and every commentator agrees he was the occasion of the passage. Instead it says *al-insān*, *man*. The effect is to place every reader inside the sentence.

This is a deliberate technique in the Qur'an's rhetoric. A named condemnation lets the audience take the side of the accuser. A generic description makes the audience examine themselves. The listener in Makkah who despised Abū Jahl was still obliged to ask: and where do *I* exceed my limits?

The practical form of that question in ordinary life is quite concrete. Transgression rarely announces itself in dramatic form. It looks like the manager whose authority over work becomes authority over dignity; the parent whose right to guide becomes a claim to own; the speaker whose confidence in an argument becomes contempt for the one arguing back; the person of means whose legitimate enjoyment quietly expands until it crowds out obligation. In each case something good has simply left its banks.

**The Preventive Measure the Sūrah Supplies**

Because *ṭughyān* is a matter of boundaries, the remedy is boundary-awareness — which is precisely what the Qur'an calls *taqwā*, from the root meaning to shield or guard oneself. A useful analogy: driving a powerful vehicle on a mountain road at night. The power of the engine is not the danger; the danger is speed without regard for the edge. The careful driver is not frightened, and he is not slow to the point of uselessness. He is *aware* — of the drop, of the curve, of the limits of his tyres. That awareness is what makes the journey both fast and safe.

Verse 6 tells the driver that the road has edges and that human beings habitually forget them. Verse 8 will remind him where the road ends. And verse 19 will hand him the steering wheel.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:7

> **in that he considers himself beyond need.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Diagnosis Beneath the Symptom**

Verse 6 stated the symptom: man transgresses, overflows his limits. Verse 7 supplies the cause, and the Arabic is exact: *an raʾāhu istaghnā* — "because he sees himself as having become independent, self-sufficient, in no need."

This is one of the Qur'an's great diagnostic statements. It does not attribute rebellion to ignorance of God's existence, nor to intellectual objection, nor to the appeal of forbidden pleasures. It attributes it to a *perception of self-sufficiency*. Strip away the theology for a moment and the observation is simply true of human behaviour: people obey when they feel dependent and disregard when they feel they no longer need anything from the other party. The verse identifies that mechanism and applies it to the relationship between the creature and the Creator.

**The Root Gh-N-Y and the Verb Istaghnā**

The root *gh-n-y* denotes richness, sufficiency, and freedom from need. From it come *ghinā* (wealth, sufficiency), *ghanī* (rich, needless), and — crucially — one of God's names: *al-Ghanī*, the Self-Sufficient, the One utterly free of all need.

The verb here is *istaghnā*, in the tenth form (*istafʿala*), which characteristically means *to seek, claim, or regard oneself as having* the quality of the root. *Istaghfara* is to seek forgiveness; *istakbara* is to make oneself great, to be arrogant. *Istaghnā* is therefore not merely "he became rich," but "he claimed or considered himself needless."

Add the preceding verb: *raʾāhu*, "he sees himself / he regards himself." The verse thus describes a *perception*, not a fact. Nowhere does it say man *is* self-sufficient. It says he *sees* himself so. And that gap between perception and reality is precisely where the tragedy lies.

The translation offered in the source — "beyond need" rather than the more literal "self-sufficient" — is well chosen, because English "self-sufficient" carries a positive connotation of maturity and independence, while the Arabic here means something closer to the delusion of not requiring God.

**The Only Being Who Is Truly Beyond Need**

The theological force of the verse depends on the fact that *al-Ghanī* is a divine name. When a human being regards himself as *mustaghnī*, he is not merely making an error of accounting; he is claiming a divine attribute. This is why the sin described here is treated so gravely: it is a species of usurpation.

The Qur'an corrects the accounting repeatedly and bluntly:

> *"O mankind! You are the ones in need of God, and God is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy."* (Qur'an 35:15)

> *"And God is the Self-Sufficient, while you are the poor."* (Qur'an 47:38)

> *"Whosoever purifies himself, purifies himself only for the good of his own soul. And unto God is the journey's end."* (Qur'an 35:18)

The relationship is asymmetrical in the most absolute way possible. God gains nothing from human worship and loses nothing from human rebellion. In the famous divine saying transmitted by Muslim:

> *"O My servants, if the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, were as pious as the most pious heart of any one man among you, that would not increase My kingdom in anything. O My servants, if the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, were as wicked as the most wicked heart of any one man among you, that would not decrease My kingdom in anything."* (Muslim 2577, from Abū Dharr)

The one who "sees himself beyond need" is therefore misreading which party in the relationship is dispensable.

**The Illusion Examined**

It is worth taking the illusion seriously enough to examine it, because it is not stupid. The self-sufficient man has evidence. He has money in the bank, health in his body, respect in his community, skill in his hands. When he wants food, he buys it; when he is sick, he pays a doctor; when he needs help, he calls people who owe him favours. Nothing in his daily experience *feels* dependent.

The Qur'an's answer is to interrogate the inventory. Where did the health come from? Who supplied the intelligence that earned the money, the stable body that permits the skill, the functioning of a heart that has beaten unsupervised for decades, the rain that grew the food he purchased, the air he has never once had to arrange for? Every item in the ledger of self-sufficiency was received.

The verse's placement makes this argument without stating it. Immediately before the accusation of self-sufficiency stand four verses cataloguing what the accused was given: existence, form from a clot, the nobility of his Lord, the pen, and knowledge he did not have. Verse 7 is only shocking because verses 1–5 have just been read. A man who was a clinging clot and who knew nothing now regards himself as needing nothing.

**Wealth as the Classic Trigger**

Although *istaghnā* covers every form of imagined independence, the classical commentators noted that wealth is the most common trigger, and the Qur'an's case studies bear this out.

Qārūn, given treasures whose keys burdened strong men, replied to those who counselled him: *"I have been given it only because of knowledge I possess"* (28:78) — the exact fusion of financial and intellectual self-sufficiency. The earth swallowed him.

The owner of the two gardens said: *"I do not think that this will ever perish, and I do not think the Hour will come"* (18:35–36) — self-sufficiency producing, as it always does, denial of the return.

The Qur'an also gives the general rule:

> *"And whatever of blessing you have, it is from God; then when harm touches you, unto Him you cry for help."* (Qur'an 16:53)

That verse is the empirical test of verse 7. The man who believes he needs nothing behaves entirely differently the moment his aeroplane loses altitude. The Qur'an notes the pattern with something close to irony:

> *"And when harm touches man, he calls upon Us — lying on his side, or sitting, or standing. But when We remove his harm from him, he passes on as though he had never called upon Us for a harm that touched him."* (Qur'an 10:12)

Self-sufficiency, then, is not a settled philosophical position. It is a mood that comfort produces and crisis dissolves.

**The Historical Referent: Abū Jahl**

The commentators identify Abū Jahl as the occasion of this passage, and the identification illuminates the verse. He was wealthy, of noble Makhzūmī lineage, quick-witted, and a leading figure in the tribal council. He had, by the standards of his society, every material reason to feel that he required nothing from anyone — least of all from an orphan of Banū Hāshim claiming to speak for God.

His recorded objection was not primarily doctrinal. Ibn Isḥāq preserves his complaint to the Banū Makhzūm: they had competed with Banū ʿAbd Manāf in honour and generosity until they were neck and neck, and then Banū ʿAbd Manāf said, "A prophet has arisen among us" — and how could Makhzūm ever match that? "By God, we will never believe in him." That is not an argument about God; it is an argument about status. Self-sufficiency in its purest form: I do not need this to be true, and I cannot afford it to be true.

**Wealth Is Not the Problem; the Perception Is**

It would be a misreading to take this verse as a condemnation of prosperity. The Qur'an calls wealth *khayr*, good (2:180), and the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"There is nothing wrong with wealth for one who has taqwā."* (Aḥmad; Ibn Mājah 2141)

The companions included men of great means — Abū Bakr, ʿUthmān, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf — who used wealth to fund the community. The verse does not attack *having*; it attacks a *seeing*. A rich man who knows he is a beggar before God is not described in this verse at all; a poor man who feels he owes God nothing is.

The Prophet ﷺ redefined the term itself:

> *"Richness is not in the abundance of possessions; richness is the richness of the soul."* (al-Bukhārī 6446; Muslim 1051, from Abū Hurayrah)

This is *ghinā* correctly located: sufficiency is a state of the heart's contentment with God, not a bank balance — the opposite of the counterfeit *istighnāʾ* condemned here.

**The Social Consequences of Imagined Independence**

The verse links self-sufficiency to *transgression* (v. 6), and the link is not only vertical but horizontal. A person who believes he needs nothing from God usually also behaves as though he needs nothing from people, and that produces a recognizable social type: dismissive of advice, contemptuous of the weak, impatient with obligation, incapable of apology.

The Qur'an captures the behavioural signature elsewhere:

> *"And do not turn your cheek away from people in scorn, nor walk exultantly in the earth. Truly God loves not any vainglorious boaster."* (Qur'an 31:18)

Notice the sūrah's own trajectory: the man who considers himself beyond need (v. 7) is, within two verses, found preventing another man from praying (vv. 9–10). Contempt for God and contempt for people arrive together. The one who owes nothing to Heaven will not tolerate someone kneeling in his courtyard.

**Modern Forms of the Same Illusion**

The verse's diagnosis has, if anything, more purchase now than in seventh-century Makkah, because modern life is engineered to conceal dependence. Food appears in shops without a visible farm; water arrives in pipes without a visible river; illness is managed by systems the patient does not understand; risk is transferred to insurers. The felt experience of needing nothing has never been so easy to sustain.

Technological and intellectual achievement supply a further layer, and it is the layer this sūrah anticipates. The chapter that praised the pen and taught knowledge also warned that the taught creature would forget he was taught. A civilization can sequence a genome and still be the *insān* of verse 7 if it concludes from its competence that it has outgrown its Creator.

The Qur'an's response is not anti-intellectual — it opened by commanding reading. Its response is to insist that competence and dependence are compatible, and that only the confusion of the two produces *ṭughyān*.

**Restoring Accurate Self-Perception**

Because the verse identifies a false *seeing*, the remedy is corrected sight, and Islam institutionalizes it in daily practice.

Five times a day, the Muslim places his forehead — the seat of his pride — on the ground. In the opening chapter he recites *"Thee alone we worship and from Thee alone we seek help"* (1:5), an explicit declaration of insufficiency. Fasting removes food and water and returns him within hours to the awareness that he is a creature who cannot last a day unsupplied. Zakāt breaks the fantasy that his property is his own creation. Ḥajj strips away the clothing that marks status. Even the sunnah of saying *bismillāh* before eating and *al-ḥamdu lillāh* after it is a rehearsal of dependence at the most ordinary level.

None of these practices adds anything to God. Every one of them corrects the seeing described in this verse. And that is why the sūrah, having diagnosed the disease in verse 7, will end by prescribing the single act that most completely contradicts it: *"But prostrate and draw near"* (v. 19).

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:8

> **Truly unto thy Lord is the return.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Dismantles the Illusion**

Verse 7 described a man who sees himself as needing nothing. Verse 8 answers him in seven Arabic words: *inna ilā rabbika al-rujʿā* — "Truly, unto thy Lord is the return."

The refutation is not an argument about his wealth or his influence. It concedes them entirely and then points at their expiry. Whatever independence he imagines is a lease, and the lease ends. Every self-sufficient man in history has gone back, and gone back to the same place.

Al-Rāzī observes the placement: this is the natural, logical rejoinder to *istighnāʾ*. If self-sufficiency were permanent, arrogance would at least be coherent. Since it terminates in a summons, arrogance is not merely wrong; it is irrational.

**Al-Rujʿā: The Grammar of Return**

The word *al-rujʿā* is a verbal noun (*maṣdar*) from *r-j-ʿ*, "to return, to go back." Arabic possesses several nouns from this root — *rujūʿ*, *marjiʿ*, *rajʿah* — and the Qur'an selects the form *rujʿā*, which appears only here in the entire Book. The pattern *fuʿlā* carries a sense of finality and comprehensiveness; classical rhetoricians associate it with emphasis and completeness.

Three grammatical features intensify the statement:

*Inna* — the particle of emphatic assertion: "truly, certainly, without doubt."
*Ilā rabbika* — the prepositional phrase is placed *before* the subject, which in Arabic produces restriction: to your Lord and to no one else.
*Al-rujʿā* — definite, absolute: *the* return, the one that matters.

And once again the possessive is intimate: *rabbika*, *thy* Lord. Even in the verse of accountability, the divine title is Nurturer rather than Judge. The One before whom the arrogant man will stand is the same One who raised him from a clot.

**Return, Not Arrival: Why the Word Matters**

The Qur'an could have said "unto thy Lord is the journey" or "the destination." It says *return* — and the choice encodes an entire metaphysics.

To return is to go back to where one came from. The word presupposes an origin. It tells the human being that death is not a departure into the unknown but a homecoming to the One who sent him. This is the theme the Qur'an states in the words Muslims say at every death:

> *"Those who, when affliction befalls them, say: Truly we are God's, and truly unto Him we return."* (Qur'an 2:156)

That formula, *innā lillāhi wa innā ilayhi rājiʿūn*, is the most frequently repeated application of this verse's root in Muslim life. It contains two claims: ownership (*we belong to God*) and return (*to Him we go back*). The first makes the second unsurprising. Property returns to its owner.

The Qur'an drives the point with the metaphor of a wandering that ends:

> *"Did you then think that We created you in vain, and that unto Us you would not be returned?"* (Qur'an 23:115)

**The Logical Structure: Purpose Requires Accountability**

There is a rigorous argument in this verse, and it is worth stating formally.

If a creature is made by a wise Creator, the making has a purpose. If there is a purpose, there is a standard of success and failure. If there is a standard, there must be an assessment — otherwise the standard is decorative. And since assessment plainly does not conclude in this life (the tyrant dies in his bed, the martyr dies in the street), the assessment must occur elsewhere. Therefore: *unto thy Lord is the return*.

The Qur'an makes the argument from justice explicitly:

> *"Or do those who commit evil deeds suppose that We shall make them like those who believe and perform righteous deeds, equal in their living and their dying? Evil is the judgement they make!"* (Qur'an 45:21)

> *"Shall We treat those who believe and perform righteous deeds like those who work corruption in the earth? Or shall We treat the reverent like the wicked?"* (Qur'an 38:28)

These are rhetorical questions with a single answer available to any moral intuition. A universe in which Pharaoh and Moses have identical ultimate outcomes is not a universe governed by wisdom. Belief in the return is therefore not an eccentric add-on to belief in God; it is the necessary completion of belief in a *just* God.

**Why This Belief Is Placed Between Diagnosis and Warning**

Structurally, verse 8 sits between the description of the disease (vv. 6–7) and the description of its most vicious symptom (vv. 9–16). That position is deliberate.

The self-sufficient man's practical creed is that consequences can be managed. He can pay a fine, hire an advocate, outlast an enemy, buy a reputation. Verse 8 introduces a consequence that cannot be managed: an appointment with the One who owns him, at a time he does not choose, before a Judge who cannot be bribed, lobbied, deceived, or avoided.

> *"And be mindful of a Day on which you shall be returned unto God. Then every soul shall be paid in full for what it earned, and they shall not be wronged."* (Qur'an 2:281)

Muslim commentators note that this verse (2:281) is widely reported to be among the last verses revealed — so that the Qur'an's chronology begins with *"Recite in the Name of thy Lord who created"* and effectively ends with *"be mindful of a Day on which you shall be returned unto God."* Creation at the beginning, return at the end. The whole revelation is bracketed by the two ends of the human journey.

**What the Prophet ﷺ Taught About the Reckoning**

The reports about the return are numerous and specific. On the certainty of the questioning:

> *"There is not one of you but that his Lord will speak to him, with no interpreter between them and no screen to veil Him."* (al-Bukhārī 7443, from ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim)

On the content of the questioning:

> *"The feet of a servant will not move on the Day of Resurrection until he is asked about four things: about his life and how he spent it, about his knowledge and what he did with it, about his wealth — how he earned it and how he spent it — and about his body and how he used it."* (al-Tirmidhī 2417, from Abū Barzah)

That list reads as a point-by-point audit of exactly the assets that produce the illusion of verse 7: lifespan, knowledge, wealth, and physical strength. Each item a person counts as evidence of independence appears in the questioning as a borrowed item to be accounted for.

And on the appropriate posture in advance:

> *"The intelligent one is he who takes account of himself and works for what comes after death; and the incapable one is he who follows his desires and entertains hopes about God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2459, from Shaddād ibn Aws)

Self-audit before the audit — the practice that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is famously reported to have summarized: *"Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account."*

**The Return as Consolation, Not Only as Warning**

It would be a mistake to read this verse only as a threat. For the addressee of verses 9–10 — the servant being prevented from praying — the same sentence is a promise.

In Makkah at the time of this revelation, the believers were a harassed minority. Slaves like Bilāl were tortured in the sun; Sumayyah was killed; the Prophet ﷺ had filth thrown on him while prostrating. In such a situation, the fact that there *is* a return is the only thing that makes patience rational. A world without a return is a world in which the torturer and the tortured end identically, and in which endurance is merely a slower defeat.

The Qur'an offers the return as the ground of hope:

> *"And do not suppose God to be heedless of what the wrongdoers do. He only defers them to a Day when eyes will stare."* (Qur'an 14:42)

The oppressed reader hears verse 8 as: *your case has been recorded and will be heard*. The oppressor hears: *your file is open*. It is the same sentence.

**How Belief in the Return Reorganizes a Life**

Practically, the doctrine functions as a change of time horizon, and time horizon governs behaviour more than almost anything else.

A person planning for one evening behaves differently from one planning for a decade; a person planning for eternity behaves differently again. Verse 8 extends the horizon past death, and every ethical decision recalculates accordingly. Cheating that yields a profit this quarter becomes a loss when the ledger is longer than the quarter. Patience that yields nothing this year becomes an investment. Anonymous good becomes worth doing, because the audience that matters never left the room.

This is the practical genius of the belief: it makes integrity *rational* rather than merely admirable. A person who genuinely holds verse 8 does not need surveillance to be honest, because he has never for a moment been unobserved.

**The Death Every Person Watches and Forgets**

There is a strange feature of human psychology that this verse targets. Every person has seen death. Everyone has buried someone, attended a funeral, watched a body carried out of a house. The evidence for mortality is the most abundant evidence there is. And yet almost no one lives as though it applies personally.

The Prophet ﷺ prescribed a simple corrective:

> *"Remember frequently the destroyer of pleasures — death."* (al-Tirmidhī 2307; al-Nasāʾī 1824, from Abū Hurayrah)

Not morbid preoccupation, but periodic recalibration. He also permitted, indeed encouraged, visiting graves for this purpose after initially prohibiting it:

> *"I had forbidden you to visit graves; now visit them, for they remind of the Hereafter."* (Muslim 976, from Buraydah)

A cemetery is verse 8 made visible. Every person in it was, at some point, busy, needed, and certain of tomorrow. Every one of them made the return.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Argument**

Read in sequence, the sūrah's logic to this point is complete and tight:

God created you (vv. 1–2) — so He owns you.
God ennobled and taught you (vv. 3–5) — so you owe Him.
You nevertheless overflow your limits (v. 6) — because you imagine you need nothing (v. 7).
But you are going back to Him (v. 8) — so the imagining is temporary and the debt will be collected.

Everything that follows in the sūrah — the man who forbids prayer, the forelock, the guards of Hell — is the elaboration of what happens when a creature meets verse 8 without having taken it seriously. And the sūrah's closing instruction is what it looks like to have taken it seriously: to bring the forehead to the ground now, voluntarily, rather than to be dragged by it later.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:9

> **Hast thou seen him who forbids**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From General Diagnosis to a Named Case**

The sūrah has so far spoken in general terms: *man* transgresses, *man* imagines himself beyond need. Now the camera turns and fixes on a single figure. The Arabic is *a-raʾayta alladhī yanhā* — "Have you seen the one who forbids?"

The commentators are unanimous that the immediate referent is Abū Jahl, who had threatened to plant his foot on the Prophet's neck if he found him praying at the Kaʿbah. But the verse withholds his name, and the withholding is deliberate — a technique the Qur'an uses repeatedly, describing a man by his act rather than his identity so that the description outlives the man. Abū Jahl died at Badr in the second year after the Hijrah; *"the one who forbids"* did not die with him.

**"A-raʾayta": A Question That Is Not Really a Question**

The opening phrase *a-raʾayta* literally means "have you seen?" but in Arabic idiom it functions in several ways beyond literal sight, and the commentators note all of them.

It can mean *"tell me about"* — an invitation to consider a case, as one might say in English, "Consider the man who...".
It can express *astonishment* — "Have you ever seen anything like this?"
It can be a *summons to judgement* — placing the listener in the position of a witness or juror who is being shown evidence and invited to draw the obvious conclusion.

All three senses are alive here, and the third is dominant, because the same interrogative form is repeated at verses 11 and 13. The passage is structured as a series of questions put to an audience — a rhetorical cross-examination in which the Prophet ﷺ, and every subsequent reader, is asked to look at the defendant and see what he is actually doing.

This is a more powerful device than direct condemnation. To be told "Abū Jahl is wicked" invites agreement or disagreement. To be asked "Have you seen the man who stops someone from praying?" forces the listener to picture the act — and the act condemns itself.

**The Verb Yanhā: The Anatomy of Prohibition**

*Yanhā* comes from the root *n-h-y*, "to forbid, to restrain, to prohibit." It is the standard Qur'anic term for issuing a prohibition, and it appears in the great formula of communal duty: *al-amr bi-l-maʿrūf wa-l-nahy ʿan al-munkar*, "enjoining right and forbidding wrong."

The choice of this verb produces a devastating irony that the classical commentators drew out. Forbidding is, in principle, a noble function. God forbids; prophets forbid; the believing community is charged with forbidding. The whole moral architecture of Islam includes an office of prohibition:

> *"You are the best community brought forth for mankind: you enjoin right and forbid wrong, and you believe in God."* (Qur'an 3:110)

Abū Jahl has taken up that office and inverted it. He is exercising the faculty of prohibition — against prayer. He has become the perfect photographic negative of a believer: the same energy, the same authority, the same public role, aimed at exactly the wrong target.

The Qur'an notes this inversion as a standing feature of hypocrisy and disbelief:

> *"The hypocrite men and the hypocrite women are of one another: they enjoin wrong and forbid right."* (Qur'an 9:67)

**Interference as a Distinct and Graver Category of Sin**

There is a moral escalation between verse 6 and verse 9 that deserves close attention.

Verses 6–7 describe a man who transgresses in himself — arrogant, ungrateful, self-satisfied. That is serious, but it is contained. He harms himself.

Verse 9 describes a man who reaches out and interferes with someone else's relationship with God. The sin has crossed a boundary from private to public, from self-destruction to obstruction. This is the difference between refusing medicine and blocking the pharmacy door.

The Qur'an consistently treats this second category as more severe, because its damage compounds:

> *"And who does greater wrong than one who prevents the mosques of God from His Name being remembered therein, and strives for their ruin?"* (Qur'an 2:114)

That verse contains the Qur'anic formula for maximal injustice — *"who does greater wrong than..."* — and applies it to exactly the act described here.

The Prophet ﷺ described the multiplication of guilt in the general principle:

> *"Whoever calls to misguidance shall bear a burden like the burdens of those who follow him, without that diminishing their burdens in the least."* (Muslim 2674, from Abū Hurayrah)

The one who forbids prayer is not accountable for one sin; he is accountable for every prostration he prevented.

**Why Prayer Specifically Provoked Him**

It is worth asking why Abū Jahl's rage focused on prayer rather than on doctrine. The Prophet ﷺ had been proclaiming monotheism verbally for years, and while that produced hostility, the specific eruption recorded here concerns the physical act of ṣalāh at the Kaʿbah.

Several reasons converge, and they remain instructive.

*Prayer is visible.* A belief held privately threatens no one's social order. A man prostrating publicly in the sanctuary is making a claim in the most public currency available — bodily, spatial, undeniable.

*Prayer is a declaration of allegiance.* To bow to God in the courtyard of a shrine housing three hundred and sixty idols is to declare the idols irrelevant on their own ground.

*Prayer is contagious.* Doctrine spreads slowly; visible worship spreads by imitation. Abū Jahl, as a competent political operator, understood which of the two he needed to stop.

*Prayer sustains the believer.* Whatever else the persecution took from the Muslims, prayer kept regenerating their resolve. Cut it off and the community starves. He was, in his own terms, attacking the supply line.

The commentators observe the implicit compliment in this hostility: the enemy's choice of target tells you what your most important asset is.

**The Perennial Pattern**

The Qur'an frames this figure as recurring, not exceptional. Pharaoh sought to prevent the Israelites' worship and their departure; the people of the ditch burned believers for nothing but their faith (85:8); the Quraysh boycotted, tortured, and exiled. In every case the mechanism is the same: someone in possession of worldly authority perceives an act of worship as a challenge to that authority — and, in a certain sense, he is right. A person who kneels before God has withdrawn a portion of his deference from every human power.

The pattern persists in forms adapted to their eras: outright prohibition of worship by hostile states; social penalties for visible religious practice; workplaces or families in which a person praying is mocked or pressured; and — the most common and least discussed variety — the interference of an individual who cannot tolerate someone close to him becoming more religious than he is.

Al-Qurṭubī's reading opens this scope: the verse's application is not confined to Abū Jahl but extends to anyone who obstructs obedience to God, whether by force, by ridicule, or by manipulation.

**The Softer Forms of Forbidding**

Because the historical case is dramatic — a chieftain threatening violence — readers may wrongly conclude that the verse has no bearing on their own conduct. The classical scholars were less comfortable letting the reader off.

Consider the range of behaviours that fall under *yanhā*, forbidding, in substance if not in form:

The friend who reliably schedules gatherings so that prayer is awkward and then makes light of the one who excuses himself.
The relative who treats a young person's new commitment to religion as an embarrassment to be managed.
The employer who, without ever issuing a prohibition, arranges matters so that observance costs promotion.
The parent who is proud of every accomplishment except worship.
The mockery, the raised eyebrow, the phrase "don't overdo it" — instruments that accomplish socially what Abū Jahl attempted physically.

None of these is comparable in gravity to threatening the Messenger of God ﷺ. But all of them share the essential structure the verse identifies: standing between a servant and his Lord.

**A Question Left Deliberately Incomplete**

A final structural observation. Verse 9 does not finish its sentence. *"Hast thou seen him who forbids"* — forbids *whom*, and *what*? The answer is held back until verse 10: *"a servant when he prays."*

Arabic rhetoric calls this suspension a device for concentrating attention. The listener is made to wait one beat, and in that beat he supplies possibilities: forbids charity? forbids speech? forbids trade? Then the object lands, and it is the last thing a decent person would think to forbid.

The pause is the point. Whatever the reader imagined, the reality is worse.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:10

> **a servant when he prays?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Completion of the Question — and Its Shock**

The suspended clause of verse 9 now lands: *ʿabdan idhā ṣallā* — "a servant when he prays." The sentence that began *"Have you seen the one who forbids..."* concludes with the most defenceless object imaginable.

Every word here is chosen for its effect. Not "a man," but *a servant*. Not "who speaks against him," but *when he prays*. The one being obstructed is doing nothing to anyone: he is on the ground, forehead down, addressing his Lord. The obstruction has no possible justification, and the verse's rhetorical strategy is simply to state the situation and let the audience's own moral sense deliver the verdict.

**ʿAbd: The Word That Reverses the Hierarchy**

*ʿAbd* comes from the root *ʿ-b-d*, whose fundamental meaning is servitude and worship. An *ʿabd* is a slave or servant; *ʿibādah* is worship; *ʿubūdiyyah* is the condition of servanthood. The lexicographers add a nuance: the root originally denoted a path made smooth and level by much treading (*ṭarīq muʿabbad*) — the servant is one who has been made pliant, whose resistance has been worn away by submission.

The Qur'an's use of *ʿabd* for the Prophet ﷺ in this verse — and the commentators agree that the servant here is primarily Muhammad ﷺ — is deeply significant, because *ʿabd* is the highest title the Qur'an ever gives him. It is used at the supreme moments of his life:

> *"Glory be to Him who carried His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque."* (Qur'an 17:1) — the Night Journey.

> *"And that when the servant of God stood calling upon Him, they nearly swarmed over him."* (Qur'an 72:19) — the recitation before the jinn.

> *"Blessed is He who sent down the Criterion upon His servant."* (Qur'an 25:1) — the giving of the Qur'an.

Where other traditions elevate their founders by ascribing divinity, the Qur'an elevates its Prophet by ascribing servanthood. He himself insisted on the distinction:

> *"Do not extol me as the Christians extolled the son of Mary. I am only a servant, so say: the servant of God and His Messenger."* (al-Bukhārī 3445, from ʿUmar)

Two ironies operate in this verse simultaneously. First, the word that Makkan society used contemptuously — *ʿabd*, a slave, the lowest social category — is here the badge of the highest human station. Second, the man doing the forbidding is the "free" nobleman, and he is in fact the slave: to his status, his lineage, his fear of losing rank. The Prophet ﷺ named this inversion:

> *"Wretched is the slave of the dinar, the slave of the dirham, the slave of fine clothing. If he is given, he is pleased, and if he is not given, he is displeased."* (al-Bukhārī 2887, from Abū Hurayrah)

Every human being serves something. The only question is whether he has chosen his master or been captured by one.

**The Generic Force of the Indefinite Noun**

*ʿAbdan* is indefinite — "a servant," not "the servant." Arabic grammarians note that the indefinite in such contexts generalizes. The primary referent is the Prophet ﷺ, but the verse is framed so that the crime is not "forbidding Muhammad" but "forbidding *a praying servant*, whoever he may be."

This is why the verse continued to have force after Abū Jahl's death, and why al-Qurṭubī reads it as covering all who obstruct worship. Anyone who stands between any worshipper and any prayer has stepped into the position the verse describes.

**Idhā Ṣallā: Caught in the Very Act of Worship**

The clause *idhā ṣallā*, "when he prays," specifies the moment of the interference, and the specification aggravates the offence. The servant is not being stopped from politics, from preaching, from recruiting. He is stopped mid-worship.

The verb *ṣallā* comes from the root *ṣ-l-w*. Lexicographers connect it to *ṣilah*, connection, and note also the root's association with the *ṣalawān*, the two lower back bones that flex in bowing — so the word carries both the inner meaning (connection with God) and the outer form (the bending body). Prayer in Islam is precisely this fusion: an inward turning that must take a bodily shape.

To interrupt a person at prayer is therefore to intervene at the exact point where a creature is touching his Creator. The Prophet ﷺ described that point:

> *"When one of you stands in prayer, he is in intimate conversation with his Lord."* (al-Bukhārī 531; Muslim 551, from Anas)

And in the divine saying on the opening chapter of the Qur'an, God describes the prayer as a dialogue in which He answers each verse the worshipper recites (Muslim 395). Abū Jahl's project was to interrupt that conversation.

**The Historical Incident Behind the Verse**

The reports preserved by Muslim and by the commentators fill in the scene. Abū Jahl asked the Quraysh whether Muhammad ﷺ still placed his face on the ground among them, and when told that he did, he swore by their gods that if he saw him doing it, he would tread on his neck or grind his face into the dust.

Muslim records the outcome:

> *"He came to the Messenger of God ﷺ while he was praying, intending to tread on his neck. Then suddenly he turned back on his heels, shielding himself with his hands. It was said to him: What is with you? He said: Between him and me there is a trench of fire, terror, and wings. The Messenger of God ﷺ said: 'Had he come near me, the angels would have seized him limb by limb.'"* (Muslim 2797, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The narration is instructive on several levels. Abū Jahl had every advantage — numbers, authority, a public setting, an unarmed opponent absorbed in prayer — and he retreated from something no one else could see. The passage that follows in the sūrah (vv. 15–18) makes exactly this point: he may call his supporters; there are other forces in the field.

Another well-known incident from the same period, recorded by al-Bukhārī, shows the same man in the same posture: while the Prophet ﷺ was prostrating near the Kaʿbah, ʿUqbah ibn Abī Muʿayṭ, at Abū Jahl's instigation, placed the entrails of a slaughtered camel on his back, and he remained in prostration until Fāṭimah came and removed it (al-Bukhārī 240). The Prophet ﷺ then supplicated against the ringleaders by name, and the narrator, Ibn Masʿūd, adds that he later saw them all slain at Badr.

**Why Prayer Is Worth the Enmity**

If prayer attracts this degree of hostility, it is reasonable to ask what makes it so central. The Qur'an and the Sunnah give a consistent answer.

It is the first thing to be assessed after death:

> *"The first thing a servant will be held accountable for on the Day of Resurrection is his prayer. If it is sound, the rest of his deeds are sound; if it is corrupt, the rest of his deeds are corrupt."* (al-Ṭabarānī; al-Tirmidhī 413, from Abū Hurayrah, with similar wording)

It is the pillar that distinguishes belief from its absence:

> *"Between a man and disbelief and polytheism is the abandonment of prayer."* (Muslim 82, from Jābir)

It is the only obligation given directly, without an intermediary, during the Night Journey — and it was given in the same Makkan period as this sūrah's later verses. It is the practice that structures the day into five returns to God, making transgression (v. 6) difficult to sustain and self-sufficiency (v. 7) impossible to forget. And it produces measurable moral change:

> *"Recite what has been revealed to thee of the Book, and perform the prayer. Truly the prayer forbids indecency and wrong."* (Qur'an 29:45)

Note the verb in that verse: *tanhā* — prayer *forbids*. The same root as *yanhā* in verse 9. The Qur'an sets the two prohibitions against each other: Abū Jahl forbids the prayer; the prayer forbids indecency. Whoever succeeds in his prohibition determines what the society becomes.

**The Believer's Response: Do Not Stop**

The most important practical lesson of this verse is what the Prophet ﷺ *did* under obstruction: he kept praying. He did not relocate the prayer to a private house to avoid provocation, nor abandon the Kaʿbah, nor shorten his prostration when he knew the camel's entrails were coming. The account in al-Bukhārī has him remaining in sujūd until the filth was physically removed.

This establishes a principle that the sūrah's final verse will state as a command: *"Obey him not; but prostrate and draw near"* (96:19). The response to interference is not negotiation and not retaliation — it is continuation.

For a believer today, the application is proportionate but real. Prayer is not to be surrendered to inconvenience, embarrassment, or social pressure. The Muslim who steps out of a meeting to pray, who prays in an airport corner, who tells a friend plainly that he will be back in five minutes, is performing the act this verse defends. Very few will face a chieftain's boot. Most will face a mild awkwardness — and the verse's implicit question is whether awkwardness is really enough to stop a servant from meeting his Lord.

**The Dignity of the Interrupted Worshipper**

One last note on the verse's tone. Although the passage is an accusation against the obstructor, its emotional centre is the protection of the obstructed. God, in the first revelation to reach mankind on this subject, positions Himself explicitly on the side of a lone servant on the ground being harassed by a powerful man.

Whoever is in that position — a convert facing a hostile family, a worker praying in a place where it is not welcome, a prisoner denied the means of worship, a young person mocked for taking religion seriously — is described in the Qur'an not as marginal but as the one whose case God takes up personally. The chieftain's name had to be supplied by the commentators. The servant is in the text.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:11

> **Hast thou seen if he is upon guidance,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cross-Examination Continues**

For the second time the interrogative arrives: *a-raʾayta in kāna ʿalā al-hudā* — "Have you considered: what if he is upon guidance?" The rhetorical structure of the passage is now unmistakable. Three times in five verses the Qur'an asks the listener to *look* (vv. 9, 11, 13), building a case in the manner of a prosecutor presenting exhibits.

But there is an ambiguity in this verse that the classical commentators discussed at length, and the ambiguity is fruitful rather than problematic. Who is the "he" who might be *upon guidance*?

**Two Readings of the Pronoun**

*The dominant reading: the praying servant.* On this understanding, the questions of verses 11–12 concern the man being obstructed. The sense becomes: *Consider — what if this servant you are trying to stop is in fact upon guidance and calling to reverence? Do you understand what you are doing?* The obstructor is being asked to weigh the possibility that he is not merely bullying a man but attacking the truth itself. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr transmit this as the primary sense.

*The secondary reading: the obstructor himself.* On this understanding the question turns on Abū Jahl: *Have you considered whether he — the one forbidding — is himself on guidance, or enjoining reverence?* Read this way, the question is bitterly ironic. A man who prevents prayer plainly cannot claim either guidance or piety as his motive, so the question exposes the absence of any legitimate ground for his behaviour.

Both readings are grammatically available, and both were held by respected scholars. They are also complementary rather than contradictory: taken together, they place two men side by side and ask which of them fits the description. One is prostrating; the other is threatening. The question of who is "upon guidance" answers itself.

**"Upon" Guidance: A Preposition That Carries Meaning**

The phrase is *ʿalā al-hudā*, literally "upon guidance," and the preposition *ʿalā* deserves attention. Arabic could have said *fī al-hudā*, "in guidance," or *maʿa al-hudā*, "with guidance." *ʿAlā* suggests being *mounted upon*, *established on top of*, *carried by* — a position of stability and elevation.

The Qur'an uses this construction deliberately and repeatedly:

> *"Those are upon guidance from their Lord, and those are the successful."* (Qur'an 2:5)

The image is of standing on solid, raised ground, seeing the way ahead. By contrast, the Qur'an describes error with prepositions of immersion and wandering: *fī ṭughyānihim yaʿmahūn*, *"in their transgression they wander blindly"* (2:15). The guided are *upon*; the misguided are *in*.

**Hudā: The Word for Being Shown the Way**

*Hudā* derives from the root *h-d-y*, "to guide, to lead the way, to direct." The Arabs used it of the guide who leads a caravan across trackless desert — the man who reads the stars and the dunes and keeps a hundred travellers alive. From the same root come *hadiyyah* (a gift, something directed to someone) and *hadī* (a sacrificial animal directed to the Sacred House).

The desert imagery underlies the theological term. Guidance in the Qur'an is not decoration on a life that would otherwise be fine; it is the difference between arriving and dying of thirst. The Arabs knew what it meant to be without a guide.

The tradition distinguishes two levels of divine guidance, and the distinction matters for this passage:

*Hidāyat al-dalālah wa-l-irshād* — guidance as showing the way. This is universal; God has shown all people the road through prophets, scripture, reason, and conscience: *"And We guided him to the two paths"* (90:10); *"As for Thamūd, We guided them, but they preferred blindness to guidance"* (41:17).

*Hidāyat al-tawfīq* — guidance as the granting of actual acceptance and success. This is God's alone to give: *"Truly thou dost not guide whom thou lovest, but God guides whom He will"* (28:56) — a verse revealed, the commentators report, regarding the Prophet's uncle Abū Ṭālib, who protected him for a lifetime and did not accept the message.

Abū Jahl received the first kind in abundance. He heard the Qur'an, watched the Prophet's character for forty years, and by several reports privately conceded the man's truthfulness. What he lacked was the second — and the reason the Qur'an gives for its withholding is his own choice, which the sūrah has already named: he saw himself as beyond need.

**The Question as a Demand for Self-Examination**

Beneath the rhetoric, the verse is asking the obstructor to perform an act he has never performed: to consider that he might be wrong.

This is the single hardest cognitive act for a person committed to a position, and it is the act that faith requires. The verse does not demand instant conversion. It demands only the entertaining of a possibility: *what if he is upon guidance?* Any honest man in Makkah, aware of the Prophet's forty-year reputation for truthfulness, could at least have granted the question.

The Qur'an elsewhere asks for exactly this minimum:

> *"Say: I exhort you to one thing only — that you stand for God, in pairs and singly, then reflect. There is no madness in your companion; he is only a warner to you before a severe punishment."* (Qur'an 34:46)

Note what is being asked: stand up, alone or in twos, away from the crowd, and *think*. The Qur'an's confidence is that private, unhurried reflection tends toward faith, and that resistance is usually sustained by the pressure of the group. That is why Abū Jahl's objection, as the historians preserve it, was framed in terms of tribal competition rather than argument.

**Why Sincerity of Motive Is Being Probed**

Verses 11 and 12 together interrogate motive: is he upon guidance, or enjoining reverence? These are the only two grounds on which interference in another person's religious practice could ever be defensible — namely, that the interferer is himself rightly guided and is genuinely calling the other to piety.

The Qur'an is therefore applying a fair test rather than a rigged one. If a man stops another from an act, the question "on what ground?" is legitimate and answerable in principle. A father who stops his son from a harmful act is intervening on the ground of guidance and concern. The verse simply asks Abū Jahl to produce his ground.

He has none. He is not stopping the prayer because prayer is harmful, nor because he possesses a better path, nor out of concern for the Prophet's welfare. He is stopping it because it threatens his standing. The interrogation exposes the absence of any principle behind the power.

**The General Principle for Opposing Others**

Extracted from its historical setting, the verse establishes a test that applies to everyone who is inclined to interfere with another person's religious life — including, importantly, religious people inclined to interfere with each other.

Before objecting to what someone else is doing in matters of faith, two questions must be answerable: *Am I on guidance in this matter — do I actually know?* And: *Am I calling to reverence — is my motive their nearness to God?*

If the answer to either is no, the objection is not correction; it is the behaviour of verse 9. Much of what passes for religious criticism in any age fails both tests: it is opposition rooted in ignorance of the ruling, or in ego, faction, envy, or the desire to be seen as the more rigorous party. The Qur'an's cross-examination is not addressed only to disbelievers.

**God Sees the Motive, Not Only the Deed**

The passage's interest in what lies behind the act — guidance, reverence, denial, turning away — culminates in verse 14: *"Does he not know that God sees?"* The sequence establishes that God's seeing extends to intentions.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the principle that governs all Islamic ethics:

> *"Deeds are only by intentions, and every man shall have only what he intended."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907, from ʿUmar)

And:

> *"God does not look at your bodies or your forms, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds."* (Muslim 2564, from Abū Hurayrah)

The verses under discussion are the Qur'anic ground of that teaching. Two men may perform an identical outward act — one restraining another person — and be at opposite ends of the moral scale depending on whether the restraint issues from guidance and reverence or from arrogance and denial.

**A Question Still Waiting for an Answer**

The verse ends without resolution, because verse 12 completes the conditional and verse 13 turns it around. Rhetorically, the listener is held in suspension across four verses, which is precisely how a cross-examination works: the questions accumulate, and no answer is offered, because the defendant has none.

That silence is the verdict. The strongest indictment of the man who forbids prayer is not a sentence pronounced against him but a question he cannot answer.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:12

> **or enjoins reverence?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of the Test**

Verse 11 asked whether the man in question is *upon guidance*. Verse 12 completes the conditional: *aw amara bil-taqwā* — "or enjoins reverence." The two verses form a single question with two branches, and between them they cover the whole of religious integrity.

*Guidance* concerns the person himself: is he on the right road? *Enjoining reverence* concerns his effect on others: does he call people toward God? The pairing is not accidental — it maps precisely onto the Qur'an's standard formula for a complete religious life:

> *"By Time! Truly man is in loss, save those who believe and perform righteous deeds, and exhort one another to truth and exhort one another to patience."* (Qur'an 103:1–3)

Belief and righteous action (the private dimension) plus mutual exhortation (the public dimension). Verses 11 and 12 ask whether the obstructor possesses either. He possesses neither, and the questions expose the void.

**Amara: The Positive Counterpart to Yanhā**

The verb here is *amara*, "to command, to enjoin, to instruct." It is the exact counterpart to *yanhā*, "to forbid," in verse 9 — and the Qur'an has deliberately placed the two verbs three lines apart.

The believing community's mandate is *al-amr bi-l-maʿrūf wa-l-nahy ʿan al-munkar*: commanding right and forbidding wrong. Verse 9 showed a man performing the second half of that formula against the truth. Verse 12 now asks whether he performs the first half at all — whether he has ever, in his life of authority and influence, used his voice to command anyone toward God.

The question is unanswerable in his case, and the structure makes the failure legible: he forbids what should be commanded, and he commands nothing that should be commanded. His moral compass is not merely broken; it is reversed on both axes.

**Taqwā: Awareness That Functions as a Shield**

*Taqwā* comes from the root *w-q-y*, which means to guard, to shield, to protect from harm. From it come *wiqāyah* (protection, a preventive covering) and *taqiyyah* (self-protection). A *muttaqī* is literally one who has taken a shield.

The older English rendering "God-fearing" captures only part of the sense and misleads on the rest. Fear is one component, but the operative meaning is *protective mindfulness*: the settled awareness that one's actions have consequences before God, which produces careful conduct. It is not paralysis, and it is not gloom.

The classical definitions are worth citing. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib is reported to have described it as:

> *"Fear of the Majestic, action upon the revelation, contentment with little, and preparation for the day of departure."*

And Ubayy ibn Kaʿb was asked by ʿUmar about taqwā. He answered by asking whether ʿUmar had ever walked a path full of thorns. ʿUmar said yes. Ubayy asked what he did. ʿUmar said: *"I gathered up my garment and took care."* Ubayy said: *"That is taqwā."*

That exchange is the best available analogy, and it can be updated without loss. Taqwā is the state of a driver on a mountain road at night: not frozen with fear, not crawling at a useless pace, but moving purposefully with continuous awareness of where the edge is. The awareness is not an obstacle to the journey; it is what makes the journey survivable. A person with taqwā lives fully — works, marries, laughs, trades — while remaining conscious of the drop.

**Why Taqwā Is the Qur'an's Master Virtue**

Of all the qualities the Qur'an could have made central, it chose this one, and the reason is structural: taqwā is not a single act but the *condition* that generates right acts across every situation.

Rules can be enumerated, but no list can cover every circumstance a person will face. Taqwā is portable. It travels into situations no jurist anticipated, because it is an internal disposition rather than an external code. The man who has it will behave rightly in a situation for which he has no ruling, because he is aware of God in it.

The Qur'an accordingly makes it the sole measure of human worth:

> *"O mankind! Truly We created you from a male and a female, and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Truly the most noble of you before God is the most reverent of you."* (Qur'an 49:13)

The most explosive claim in that verse, in seventh-century Arabia, is what it *removes* from the scale: lineage, wealth, colour, tribe, language, and freedom or slavery. Everything Abū Jahl possessed was declared irrelevant to rank, and the one thing the prostrating "servant" possessed was declared decisive.

Taqwā is also the stated purpose of the two great acts of worship. Of fasting: *"O you who believe! Fasting has been prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you, that perhaps you may be reverent"* (2:183). Of the entire creation and revelation project: *"O mankind! Worship your Lord, who created you and those before you, that perhaps you may be reverent"* (2:21). And of sacrifice: *"Their flesh does not reach God, nor their blood; rather it is your reverence that reaches Him"* (22:37).

And it is repeatedly presented as the practical key to difficulty:

> *"And whoever is reverent of God, He will make for him a way out, and will provide for him whence he never expected."* (Qur'an 65:2–3)

**What the Prophet ﷺ Was Actually Doing**

The two questions of verses 11 and 12 describe, in fact, an exact portrait of the Prophet ﷺ — which is why the majority reading takes the pronoun to refer to him. He *was* upon guidance, and he *was* enjoining reverence. That was, in its entirety, what he was doing in Makkah: praying, and calling people to be conscious of God.

He was not raising an army, seizing property, or organizing an insurrection. The list of his offences, from the standpoint of Makkan power, consisted of worship and speech. And this is why the questions of verses 11–12 humiliate the obstructor: the case against the accused, when read aloud, turns out to be a commendation.

The same rhetorical move occurs elsewhere in the Qur'an:

> *"And they resented them for nothing other than that they believed in God, the Mighty, the Praiseworthy."* (Qur'an 85:8)

State the crime plainly and the persecution collapses under its own absurdity.

**The Danger of Enjoining Without Practising**

There is a warning implicit in the pairing of these verses, and the tradition applied it to believers, not only to Abū Jahl. Verse 11 concerns being upon guidance; verse 12 concerns telling others to be reverent. The order is instructive: the personal state precedes the public call.

The Qur'an rebukes those who reverse it:

> *"Do you enjoin piety upon people and forget your own souls, while you recite the Book? Will you not then understand?"* (Qur'an 2:44)

> *"O you who believe! Why do you say what you do not do? Great is the hatred in the sight of God that you say what you do not do."* (Qur'an 61:2–3)

And the Prophet ﷺ described the consequence in one of the most sobering images in the ḥadīth literature:

> *"A man will be brought on the Day of Resurrection and cast into the Fire, and his intestines will spill out, and he will circle around them like a donkey circling a mill. The people of the Fire will gather around him and say: 'What is wrong with you? Did you not enjoin good and forbid evil?' He will say: 'I used to enjoin good and not do it, and forbid evil and do it.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3267; Muslim 2989, from Usāmah ibn Zayd)

This does not mean that only the perfect may speak. The scholars were clear that the imperfect must still enjoin good — otherwise no one would ever speak, since no one is perfect. What is condemned is the *disjunction maintained without concern*: knowingly telling others what one has no intention of doing oneself.

**Reverence as the Content of All Prophetic Preaching**

It is worth noticing that the Qur'an summarizes the message of prophet after prophet in the single phrase *"Will you not be reverent?"* Noah says it (26:106), Hūd says it (26:124), Ṣāliḥ (26:142), Lūṭ (26:161), Shuʿayb (26:177). The word is *a-lā tattaqūn*.

So when verse 12 asks whether the obstructor "enjoins reverence," it is asking whether he does what every prophet in history has done. The measure being applied is the highest available, and the question quietly identifies the praying servant with the entire prophetic line while identifying his opponent with the entire line of those who resisted it.

**The Test Applied to Ourselves**

Finally, the verse gives every reader a two-question audit that can be run in seconds and is uncomfortably clarifying.

*Am I upon guidance?* — Do I actually know that what I am doing accords with what God revealed, or am I acting on habit, culture, convenience, or assumption?

*Do I enjoin reverence?* — Does my influence on the people around me move them toward God-consciousness or away from it? Family, colleagues, friends, and anyone who reads what I write are all within range of an answer.

Everyone influences someone. The verse's implicit claim is that there is no neutral position: a person is either, in some measure, calling toward reverence, or, in some measure, standing in the way of it. Verses 9 and 12 are the two options, and the sūrah is asking which of them describes us.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:13

> **Hast thou seen if he denied and turned away?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Question, and the Turn**

The interrogation reaches its third and final movement: *a-raʾayta in kadhdhaba wa tawallā* — "Have you considered if he denied and turned away?"

The rhetorical direction now shifts decisively. Verses 11 and 12 raised the *positive* possibilities — guidance, reverence — as hypotheticals whose obvious application is to the praying servant. Verse 13 raises the *negative* possibility, and its application is equally obvious. As the source commentary notes, the commentators differ over whether "he" in this verse continues to refer to the same subject as verses 11–12 or turns to Abū Jahl; the majority sense reads this as pivoting to the obstructor.

Read that way, the passage becomes a complete forensic argument: *Consider this praying man — what if he is guided and calling to God? And consider this other man — what if he is a denier who has turned his back? Then think about what the first is doing and what the second is doing, and understand that God sees it all.*

**Kadhdhaba: Not Merely Disbelieving, but Calling Truth a Lie**

The verb *kadhdhaba* is the second form (*faʿʿala*) of *k-dh-b*, "to lie." In this intensive form it does not mean "he lied"; it means *he declared someone else a liar, he pronounced the truth false*. It is the standard Qur'anic term for the rejection of revelation, appearing hundreds of times.

The distinction between *kafara* (to disbelieve, to cover over) and *kadhdhaba* (to declare false) is worth noting. One can fail to believe out of ignorance or doubt. *Takdhīb* is active and vocal: it takes a stand, makes a public assertion, and attacks the credibility of the messenger.

This is precisely the position of Abū Jahl, and the Qur'an elsewhere describes the peculiar dishonesty involved:

> *"We know indeed that what they say grieves thee. Yet it is not thee that they deny; rather it is the signs of God that the wrongdoers reject."* (Qur'an 6:33)

Al-Ṭabarī and others transmit the well-known exchange in which Abū Jahl said to the Prophet ﷺ words to the effect: *"We do not call you a liar; but we deny what you have brought."* His objection was never to Muhammad's personal honesty — the man he had known for forty years as *al-Amīn*, the Trustworthy. It was to the implications of the message for his own position.

This is a general pattern the Qur'an describes with precision:

> *"They knowingly rejected them, out of wrongdoing and arrogance, though their souls were convinced of them. So behold the end of the workers of corruption!"* (Qur'an 27:14)

Denial, in the Qur'an's psychology, is frequently not a failure of evidence but a refusal of consequence.

**Tawallā: The Body Language of Rejection**

The second verb, *tawallā*, comes from the root *w-l-y*, which concerns nearness, alliance, and turning. The fifth form *tawallā* means *to turn away, to turn one's back, to withdraw*.

The pairing of the two verbs traces a sequence that the Qur'an uses repeatedly:

> *"But he neither confirmed nor prayed; rather he denied and turned away."* (Qur'an 75:31–32)

> *"But as for him who denies and turns away, God will punish him with the greatest punishment."* (Qur'an 88:23–24 — of the one who turns away)

First the tongue denies; then the body departs. And the order is significant. Denial is a verbal act that can still, in principle, be argued with. Turning away removes even that possibility. The one who turns his back has ended the conversation.

There is a physical honesty in the image. A man who is genuinely uncertain leans in and asks questions. A man who turns his back has already decided, and his decision precedes his reasoning.

**Why Deniers Leave Rather Than Argue**

The Qur'an notes that its most vehement opponents avoided sustained engagement with it. They warned each other:

> *"And those who disbelieve say: Do not listen to this Qur'an, and make noise during it, that perhaps you may prevail."* (Qur'an 41:26)

The strategy is telling. A confident opponent invites a hearing; these men organized to prevent one. Ibn Isḥāq preserves the account of al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, asked by Quraysh to devise a description of the Qur'an they could use against it, who admitted after listening: *"By God, it has a sweetness, and upon it is a beauty... it is not the speech of a man."* And then he devised the accusation of sorcery anyway, because his standing required it.

Turning away is the necessary companion of denial precisely because the truth is dangerous to remain near.

**Denial and Prayerlessness: The Diagnostic Pair**

Sūrat al-Qiyāmah supplies a striking commentary on this verse, describing the same type in the same words and adding what he did *not* do:

> *"For he neither gave in charity nor prayed, but he denied and turned away, then went off to his family with arrogance."* (Qur'an 75:31–33)

Set that next to the sūrah under discussion, and the symmetry is exact. Here in Sūrat al-ʿAlaq the denier not only fails to pray himself; he tries to prevent someone else from praying. The two passages describe the same disease at two stages of severity: first abstention, then obstruction.

**Turning Away as a Progressive Condition**

There is an ʿaqīdah point here about how the heart hardens, and the Qur'an is explicit that it is a process with a mechanism.

> *"Nay, but that which they used to earn has rusted upon their hearts."* (Qur'an 83:14)

The Prophet ﷺ described the same accumulation:

> *"Trials will be presented to hearts as a reed mat is woven, stick by stick. Any heart that absorbs them will have a black mark placed upon it, and any heart that rejects them will have a white mark placed upon it, until hearts become of two kinds: one white like a smooth stone, which no trial can harm as long as the heavens and the earth endure; and the other black and overturned like an inverted vessel, which neither recognizes good nor rejects evil, except what is soaked into it of its own desires."* (Muslim 144, from Ḥudhayfah)

The image of the *inverted vessel* is exactly *tawallā* rendered as a permanent posture. A cup turned upside down cannot be filled — not because there is no water, but because of its own orientation. Rain falls on it and runs off.

And the Qur'an states the reciprocal consequence:

> *"So when they deviated, God caused their hearts to deviate."* (Qur'an 61:5)

This is a critical point of divine justice. The hardening is not arbitrary. It follows the person's own repeated choice; God's act confirms a direction the servant has already chosen. Free will is preserved, and so is the seriousness of habitual refusal — which is why the Qur'an urges immediacy: *"Has the time not come for those who believe that their hearts should be humbled to the remembrance of God?"* (57:16).

**Everyday Forms of Denial and Turning Away**

Few readers will recognize themselves in Abū Jahl's outright *takdhīb*. But the second verb is more familiar than the first, and the pair is worth examining honestly.

A person may affirm every article of belief and still practise *tawallā*: knowing a religious obligation and changing the subject in his own mind; sensing that a reminder applies to him and reaching for a distraction; recognizing that a relationship, a source of income, or a habit needs to change and deciding not to think about it today.

Modern life supplies the turning-away with unlimited instruments. It has never been easier to be interrupted at exactly the moment reflection begins. Nobody needs to deny anything; one only needs never to be still long enough to face it.

The Qur'an's counsel for this is the injunction it gives to a man being told the same story about himself:

> *"So flee unto God! Truly I am a clear warner unto you from Him."* (Qur'an 51:50)

The remedy for turning away is turning toward — the literal meaning of *tawbah*, which comes from a root meaning to return.

**Why This Question Precedes the Warning**

Verse 13 is the last of the three questions, and verse 14 delivers the line that ends the interrogation: *"Does he not know that God sees?"*

The order is legally exact. First the conduct is established (v. 9–10: he forbids a servant at prayer). Then the possible justifications are examined and found absent (vv. 11–12). Then the defendant's own state is named (v. 13: denial and withdrawal). Only then is the decisive fact introduced — that all of this has occurred in full view of the One who will judge it.

It is worth observing what the passage does *not* do. It does not curse him, insult his lineage, or mock him. It asks questions and states facts. The Qur'an, addressing the man who threatened to grind its Prophet's face into the dust, argues rather than rages — a standard of discourse that its followers have not always matched, and which the very next verse grounds in the knowledge that God is watching the one who speaks as much as the one spoken about.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:14

> **Does he not know that God sees?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Ends the Cross-Examination**

Three times the passage has asked *"Have you seen...?"* — inviting the human audience to look. Now the direction of sight reverses: *a-lam yaʿlam bi-anna Allāha yarā* — "Does he not know that God sees?"

The listener has been the observer for five verses. Suddenly the scene is revealed to have contained another observer all along, and the entire episode — the threat, the boot, the prostrating servant, the courtyard of the Kaʿbah — is disclosed as having taken place in full view.

This is the pivot of the sūrah. Everything before it describes behaviour; everything after it describes consequence. And the hinge between them is not a punishment but a *fact*: God sees.

**"Does He Not Know?": The Charge of Culpable Ignorance**

The interrogative *a-lam yaʿlam* is a rebuke rather than an inquiry. Arabic rhetoricians classify this as *istifhām inkārī*, a question posed to deny or reproach rather than to obtain information. The English equivalent would be an incredulous "Doesn't he realize...?"

There is precision in the choice of verb. The verse does not say "does he not *believe*" but "does he not *know*". Even the pagans of Makkah affirmed that Allāh was the Creator:

> *"And if thou wert to ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they would surely say: God."* (Qur'an 31:25; also 29:61, 43:87)

Their disbelief was not atheism; it was the assignment of partners to God and the denial of resurrection and revelation. But a Creator who is unaware of His creation was not part of their theology either. So the verse presses the point: *you already know this — act like it.*

The gap the verse exposes is the most common gap in human religiosity: not the absence of belief, but the failure of belief to reach behaviour. A man can affirm that God sees and still behave, in the operative moments of his life, as though no one is watching. The Qur'an calls this a failure of *knowing* in the fullest sense — knowledge that has not become effective.

**Yarā: Sight as a Divine Attribute**

The verb is *yarā*, "He sees," from the root *r-ʾ-y* — the same root as *a-raʾayta*, "have you seen," which opened verses 9, 11, and 13. The passage is built on a deliberate play: the human beings are asked to see, and then reminded who really sees.

Among God's names is *al-Baṣīr*, the All-Seeing, and the Qur'an ties it explicitly to accountability:

> *"Truly God is Seer of His servants."* (Qur'an 3:15; 40:44)

> *"And He is with you wherever you are, and God sees whatsoever you do."* (Qur'an 57:4)

> *"No vision comprehends Him, but He comprehends all vision; and He is the Subtle, the Aware."* (Qur'an 6:103)

Divine seeing differs from human seeing in every respect. It requires no light, no proximity, and no direction of attention; it is not divided among objects; it does not weaken with distance or time; and it penetrates what human sight cannot reach at all:

> *"He knows the treachery of the eyes and what the breasts conceal."* (Qur'an 40:19)

That verse is remarkable in its specificity: the *treachery of the eyes* — the momentary glance whose meaning is understood by no one but the one who cast it. Human courts can judge only actions. Divine sight reaches the flicker.

**Iḥsān: The Positive Form of the Same Knowledge**

The Prophet ﷺ turned the fact of divine sight into the highest station of religion. In the ḥadīth of Gabriel, when asked to define *iḥsān* (excellence), he replied:

> *"That you worship God as though you see Him; for if you do not see Him, truly He sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8, from ʿUmar)

The second clause is verse 14 stated positively. The same fact that condemns Abū Jahl elevates the worshipper. Divine sight is not primarily a surveillance system; it is the ground of intimacy for those who welcome it and of exposure for those who do not.

This is the essential difference between the Islamic conception and the modern experience of being watched. Surveillance by an unknown power produces anxiety and performance. Being seen by the One who created you, knows your circumstances, and is described as *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* produces something else entirely: the relief of not having to explain oneself, and the comfort of an unfailing witness for the wronged.

**Why This Knowledge Changes Behaviour**

There is a simple, empirically observable principle behind the verse: human conduct is dramatically affected by the perception of being observed.

People drive differently when a police vehicle is visible. Employees work differently when the manager is present. The most reliable predictor of petty dishonesty is the belief that no one will find out. Every society builds its ethics partly on this fact, which is why it invests so heavily in cameras, audits, and inspections.

The Qur'anic claim is that there has never been an unobserved moment. If that claim is true, then the distinction between public and private conduct — the distinction upon which most human hypocrisy rests — is an illusion.

The Prophet ﷺ warned about precisely the people who exploit that illusion:

> *"I certainly know people of my nation who will come on the Day of Resurrection with good deeds like the white mountains of Tihāmah, and God will make them scattered dust." Thawbān said: "O Messenger of God, describe them to us and make them clear to us, lest we be among them unknowingly." He said: "They are your brothers, and from your race, and they take from the night as you do; but they are a people who, when they are alone with the prohibitions of God, violate them."* (Ibn Mājah 4245, from Thawbān)

That last clause — *when they are alone with the prohibitions of God* — describes the state of a person who has never internalized verse 14.

**Consistency as the Fruit of the Verse**

The practical outcome of believing that God sees is a single trait: consistency between the public and the private self.

This is the trait the early Muslims prized above almost all others. It is reported of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb that he tested a shepherd boy by asking him to sell one sheep from a large flock and tell his master it had been taken by a wolf. The boy's reply — *"Then where is God?"* — is the sole content of this verse, spoken by an illiterate youth in a desert with no witnesses.

The same principle produced the well-known counsel attributed to the early ascetics: if you must sin, at least do so where God cannot see you — an impossible condition whose statement is itself the argument.

**Justice for the One Being Watched Over**

There is a second audience for this verse, and it is easily overlooked. The praying servant of verse 10 also hears *"God sees."*

For a persecuted minority in Makkah, this was not a threat but the entire foundation of endurance. Bilāl under the rock in the desert heat, Sumayyah killed by a spear, Khabbāb branded with hot stones — none of them had a court to appeal to, a newspaper to publicize their case, or an army to defend them. What they had was the assurance that the events were being recorded by One who would act.

Al-Bukhārī records Khabbāb's account of coming to the Prophet ﷺ while he rested in the shade of the Kaʿbah and complaining of their suffering. The reply was not a promise of immediate rescue but of certain outcome:

> *"Among those before you, a man would be seized and a pit dug for him, and he would be placed in it, and a saw would be brought and placed on his head and he would be cut in two, and combs of iron would be raked over the flesh beneath his bones — and that would not turn him from his religion. By God, this affair will be completed, until a rider travels from Ṣanʿāʾ to Ḥaḍramawt fearing none but God, and the wolf for his sheep — but you are being hasty."* (al-Bukhārī 3612)

For everyone who has suffered an injustice that no one else witnessed or believed, verse 14 is the assurance that the record exists and is accurate.

**Why God's Seeing Precedes God's Seizing**

Note the structure of what follows. Verse 14 states that God sees; verse 15 states what God will do — *"Nay! If he ceases not, We shall surely seize him by the forelock."*

Seeing precedes seizing, and the sequence expresses divine justice. God does not act on suspicion or on partial evidence. Every judgement rests on complete knowledge of the deed, the intention, the circumstance, and the opportunity. The Qur'an insists on this:

> *"And We shall set up the just balances for the Day of Resurrection, so no soul shall be wronged in aught. And even if it be the weight of a mustard seed, We shall bring it forth — and We suffice as reckoners."* (Qur'an 21:47)

The doctrine has a consequence that ought to comfort more than it frightens. A person judged by an all-seeing God will be judged with the full context: what he endured, what he was not given, what pressures he was under, what he intended but could not accomplish. Human judgement is unjust precisely because it sees so little. Divine judgement is just because it sees everything.

**Living Under the Gaze**

The verse leaves the reader with a practical discipline that requires no institution and no equipment: the recollection, at the moment of decision, that the moment is being seen.

It applies at the ordinary scale far more often than at the dramatic one. The unrecorded portion of a business transaction. The sentence written about someone who will not read it. The task performed to an inferior standard because no one will inspect it. The kindness withheld because it would earn no credit. In each case the question of verse 14 is available, and answering it honestly settles the matter.

And it applies, finally, in the direction the sūrah is heading. The one who knows he is seen while sinning also knows he is seen while prostrating — alone, at night, with no one to admire him. That is the state the last verse of the sūrah will command, and it is worth nothing at all unless verse 14 is true.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:15

> **Nay! If he ceases not, We shall surely seize him by the forelock—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Kallā: Rebuke Turning into Sentence**

For the second time in the sūrah the particle *kallā* appears — "Nay!" The first (v. 6) rebuked the ingratitude of the human being in general. This one is aimed at a specific man in a specific posture, and it functions as the answer to the question just asked.

Verse 14 asked: *Does he not know that God sees?* Verse 15 replies: *Nay* — he does not know it, or he does not act as though he knows it; and here is what follows.

The classical commentators, as the source notes, take these verses as precisely this: an answer to verse 14, meaning that the man did not know, and therefore did not cease. The particle carries the force of an interruption. It cuts off a course of action that is still in progress.

**The Conditional Clause: A Door Deliberately Left Open**

*La-in lam yantahi* — "if he does not desist." The threat is conditional, and the condition matters enormously.

God does not say "We shall seize him." He says "if he does not stop, We shall seize him." Even at the point of maximum severity — addressing a man who has threatened the Messenger of God ﷺ and attempted to trample him during prostration — the door of repentance remains open. Stopping is still an available option, and the entire punishment is suspended on his refusal.

This is a consistent feature of Qur'anic warning. The threat is instrumental: its purpose is to produce the cessation, not to relish the punishment.

> *"Say to those who disbelieve: if they desist, what is past will be forgiven them; but if they return, then the precedent of the ancients has already gone before."* (Qur'an 8:38)

That verse uses the same verb — *yantahū*, from *n-h-y*, the very root of *yanhā*, "he forbids," in verse 9 of this sūrah. There is a fine irony in the wordplay: the man who *forbids* others is being told to *forbid himself*. The same root, turned inward, becomes the means of his rescue.

The historical record confirms that the door was real. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb was among the most violent opponents of Islam and became its second Caliph. Khālid ibn al-Walīd commanded the cavalry that broke the Muslim lines at Uḥud and later led their armies. ʿIkrimah, the son of Abū Jahl himself, fought the Muslims for years and died a martyr in Syria. Every one of these men was, at some point, the addressee of a verse like this one, and every one of them desisted.

Abū Jahl did not. He was killed at Badr in the second year of the Hijrah, still refusing. The verse's condition was tested in history and the outcome recorded.

**Nasfaʿan: The Violent Verb**

The Arabic *la-nasfaʿan* is emphatic in three ways at once: the emphatic *lām* prefix, the energetic *nūn* suffix (*nūn al-tawkīd*), and the imperfect verb form conveying imminence. English cannot easily reproduce the effect; "We shall most certainly and forcefully seize him" comes close.

The verb *safaʿa* itself is unusual — it appears only here in the Qur'an. The lexicographers give it a cluster of harsh meanings: to seize violently, to drag, to strike, to sear or blacken with fire or heat. Some noted that a *safʿah* can refer to a scorching mark, and connected this to the disgrace and blackening of the face on the Day of Judgement, when *"faces will be darkened"* (3:106).

The choice of a rare and forceful verb is itself rhetorical. The man's own violence — the threatened foot on the neck — is answered in kind, but by a hand he cannot resist.

**Al-Nāṣiyah: The Forelock and What It Means**

*Nāṣiyah* denotes the hair of the forehead, the forelock — the front lock of hair growing above the brow.

Seizing a man by the forelock was, in Arab custom, a gesture of total domination. A captive taken in battle was led by the forelock; the victor who grasped it demonstrated that the captive's head — the seat of his pride, his sight, his will — was in another's hand. To release a man's forelock (*al-nāṣiyah al-mafkūkah*) was to grant him his freedom. The image was universally legible to the first audience.

The head is also, of course, the part of the body that Abū Jahl proposed to use against the Prophet ﷺ — the boot on the neck, the face in the dust. And it is the part of the body the Prophet ﷺ was placing on the ground when threatened. The whole conflict of the sūrah is a conflict over whose head goes down.

The Qur'an uses the same image elsewhere for the seizure of the guilty:

> *"The guilty shall be known by their marks, and they shall be seized by the forelocks and the feet."* (Qur'an 55:41)

And, most significantly, for God's absolute grip on all creatures, in the words of the Prophet Hūd:

> *"Truly I have put my trust in God, my Lord and your Lord. There is no creature that crawls but that He holds it by its forelock. Truly my Lord is on a straight path."* (Qur'an 11:56)

That verse is the theological key to this one. The forelock of every living thing is *already* in God's hand at every moment. Verse 15 does not describe God acquiring a grip He did not have; it describes Him closing a grip He has always held. The tyrant standing in the Kaʿbah issuing threats was, throughout, being held by the hair.

The Prophet ﷺ made this the content of a supplication for distress:

> *"O God, I am Your servant, son of Your servant, son of Your maidservant. My forelock is in Your hand. Your judgement upon me is executed, Your decree concerning me is just..."* (Aḥmad 3712, from Ibn Masʿūd)

The believer says voluntarily what the tyrant will learn by force: *my forelock is in Your hand*.

**The Plural "We": Majesty, Not Multiplicity**

The verse says *la-nasfaʿan*, "**We** shall seize." The Qur'an alternates between the singular ("I") and this plural of majesty, and the alternation is meaningful. The singular is used in contexts of intimacy and pure monotheism — *"Truly I am God; there is no god but I, so worship Me"* (20:14). The plural of majesty is used in contexts of power, command, and the deployment of created forces.

Here the plural signals overwhelming might, and it anticipates verse 18, where the forces in question are named: the guards of Hell. A man who is about to summon his supporters is being told, in the grammar itself, whose retinue is larger.

**Punishment in This World and the Next**

Commentators differ on whether the seizure describes a worldly humiliation or the punishment of the Hereafter, and the tradition generally holds that both are intended.

In this world, the promise was fulfilled with a precision that the early community found striking. Abū Jahl was cut down at Badr by two young men of the Anṣār, boys who asked which man was Abū Jahl and then converged on him. He was found dying among the fallen, and it was ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd — a poor, slight man whom Abū Jahl had once despised — who placed a foot on his neck to finish him. Al-Bukhārī preserves the episode of the battle in several narrations. The man who swore to put his foot on the neck of a praying servant ended with a servant's foot on his own.

In the Hereafter, the seizing by the forelock is described in the passages already cited, and its meaning is the same: a total loss of the autonomy that verse 7 imagined.

**Why the Punishment Fits the Crime**

There is a precise moral symmetry in this image that repays attention.

The crime of verses 6–7 was *ṭughyān* born of imagined self-sufficiency: the belief that one answers to no one and can be seized by nobody. The punishment is to be seized — physically, publicly, by the head. The illusion is not refuted with an argument; it is dissolved by demonstration.

The Qur'an frequently constructs punishments as the exposure of the sin's underlying falsehood. Those who were too proud to bow will find bowing impossible when it would help them: *"On the Day when the shin shall be laid bare and they shall be called to prostrate, they will not be able"* (68:42). Those who hoarded wealth will be branded with it (9:35). Those who concealed truth will be shown their own records.

The principle is that the Hereafter makes visible what was already true. Abū Jahl was always held by the forelock; on that day he will merely feel it.

**The Continuing Application**

Al-Qurṭubī and other commentators are careful to note that the specificity of the historical referent does not limit the verse. The warning attaches to the description, not to the name. Whoever forbids a servant from prayer, denies the truth, turns away, and does not desist stands where Abū Jahl stood.

There is also a quieter application to the believer. The condition is *"if he does not desist"* — and desisting is a possibility offered to everyone, in every category of wrong, for as long as life continues. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"God accepts the repentance of a servant so long as the death-rattle has not reached his throat."* (al-Tirmidhī 3537, from Ibn ʿUmar)

That is the mercy embedded in the grammar of this terrifying verse: it is a conditional, and the condition is still open for anyone reading it.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:16

> **a lying, sinful forelock.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Names the Forelock's Character**

Verse 15 announced the seizure. Verse 16 describes what is being seized: *nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭiʾah* — "a lying, sinful forelock."

Grammatically this is a *badal*, a substitutive appositive: the indefinite *nāṣiyah* restates the definite *al-nāṣiyah* of the previous verse, now qualified by two adjectives. Arabic rhetoricians note that this construction singles out and holds up the object for inspection. The verse functions like a hand lifting a captive's head so that the face can be seen.

**A Forelock That Lies: The Figure of Speech and Its Force**

Strictly speaking, a lock of hair cannot lie and cannot sin. Arabic rhetoric calls this *majāz ʿaqlī* — attributing to a part or an instrument what properly belongs to the whole person, or attributing to a thing the quality of its owner's action.

Two explanations are given in the tradition, and both are illuminating.

The first is *synecdoche*: the forelock stands for the whole man, as when English says "we need more hands" for workers. The forelock is chosen because it is the front of the head, the visible identity, the part one grasps. Naming it names him.

The second is more pointed. The forelock crowns the forehead, and behind the forehead is where the thinking, the planning, and the deciding happen. The lie is fabricated there before the tongue speaks it; the sin is intended there before the limb performs it. Some commentators drew attention to the fact that the front of the head is precisely the seat of deliberation and of the will — the command centre from which the whole enterprise of denial and obstruction is run. So the description is not arbitrary: the accusation is being lodged at the source.

There is a further irony. The forehead is also the part of the body that a worshipper places on the ground in prostration. Abū Jahl's forehead never touched the earth in worship, and it is precisely by this that he will be dragged. The organ he refused to lower voluntarily is the one that will be seized involuntarily.

**Kādhibah: The Lie Behind the Position**

The first adjective, *kādhibah*, comes from the root *k-dh-b* — the same root as *kadhdhaba*, "he denied / declared false," in verse 13. The passage is thus internally bound: the man called the truth a lie, and the description returns the word to its owner. He is not the detector of a falsehood; he is its source.

The Qur'an frequently reverses accusations in this way:

> *"They will say: By God, our Lord, we were not idolaters. Look how they lie against themselves, and how what they used to fabricate has forsaken them!"* (Qur'an 6:23–24)

The lie in question here operates at several levels, and it is worth separating them.

*The lie about the message*: declaring revelation false while privately recognizing the Prophet's honesty.
*The lie about motive*: presenting a defence of ancestral religion when the actual driver was tribal rivalry and the fear of losing rank.
*The lie to himself*: the belief of verse 7, that he was beyond need — the foundational falsehood from which all the others grow.

That third one is the deepest, and it explains the others. A man who has successfully deceived himself will deceive everyone else without effort, because he is not conscious of lying.

**Khāṭiʾah: Sin Committed Deliberately**

The second adjective, *khāṭiʾah*, comes from the root *kh-ṭ-ʾ*. Arabic makes a distinction here that English blurs, and it is important.

*Akhṭaʾa* (fourth form) means to err unintentionally, to make a mistake — the *khaṭaʾ* that Islamic law treats leniently, as in accidental killing (4:92) and in the supplication *"our Lord, take us not to task if we forget or fall into error"* (2:286). The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"God has pardoned for my nation their mistakes, their forgetfulness, and what they are coerced into."* (Ibn Mājah 2045, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

*Khaṭiʾa* (first form), from which *khāṭiʾah* derives, means to sin deliberately, to commit a wrong knowingly. The Qur'an uses this precise form for calculated wrongdoing:

> *"Truly Pharaoh and Hāmān and their hosts were sinners (khāṭiʾīn)."* (Qur'an 28:8)

> *"And the people of Pharaoh, and those before them, and the overturned cities committed sin (bil-khāṭiʾah)."* (Qur'an 69:9)

So the verse is not describing a man who stumbled. It is describing one who knew and chose. The two adjectives together — lying and deliberately sinning — cover the two faculties by which a person goes wrong: the intellect that assents to falsehood, and the will that acts on it. Neither excuse is available to him. He is not mistaken, and he is not misled.

**Why the Description Is Given Before the Punishment Is Carried Out**

There is a principle of justice embedded in this small verse. Before the seizure is executed, the reason is stated. The Qur'an does not present punishment as raw power; it presents it as a verdict following a stated finding of fact.

The same pattern recurs throughout the Book. The people of Noah, of ʿĀd, of Thamūd, of Lot are each destroyed only after the charges are enumerated and the warning delivered. God announces the principle directly:

> *"And We never punish until We have sent a messenger."* (Qur'an 17:15)

> *"And thy Lord would never destroy the towns unjustly while their people were reformers."* (Qur'an 11:117)

For the doctrine of divine justice this matters. God's punishment is never arbitrary, never disproportionate, and never uninformed — a point verse 14 has just established from the side of knowledge (*God sees*) and this verse establishes from the side of due process (the charge is read).

**Truthfulness as the Opposite Standard**

Because the forelock is condemned for lying, the sūrah implicitly commends the opposite, and the tradition treats truthfulness as the foundation of character.

> *"Truthfulness leads to righteousness, and righteousness leads to Paradise. A man keeps speaking the truth until he is recorded with God as a truthful one. And lying leads to wickedness, and wickedness leads to the Fire. A man keeps lying until he is recorded with God as a liar."* (al-Bukhārī 6094; Muslim 2607, from Ibn Masʿūd)

The mechanism described in that ḥadīth is the same one this verse presupposes. A lie is at first something a person *does*; with repetition it becomes something he *is*, until the description attaches to him permanently — a *lying forelock*, an identity rather than an act.

And the reverse is equally available. The same ḥadīth says a man keeps telling the truth *until he is recorded with God as a ṣiddīq*. Character is the accumulation of repeated choices, and the Qur'an's descriptions are the final tally.

**The Contrast the Sūrah Is Building**

Set the two men of this sūrah side by side as the text has arranged them:

One is called *ʿabd*, a servant, and is described *praying* (v. 10). The other is described by his forelock, *lying and sinful* (v. 16).
One places his forehead on the ground voluntarily. The other's forehead is seized.
One is the object of another man's forbidding. The other is the object of God's seizing.

By the standards of Makkan society in the year this was revealed, the first man was powerless and the second was among the most powerful men in the city. The Qur'an's ranking is exactly inverted, and history vindicated the ranking within a decade.

**What This Verse Asks of the Reader**

The temptation with a verse like this is to read it as a portrait of someone else. But the two adjectives are ordinary vices, not exotic ones. Lying and deliberate sin are within the reach of every person, and the verse locates them in the forehead — in the place where a person plans.

The practical question the verse poses is therefore about what one does with one's forehead. It has two possible careers. It can be the workshop where falsehood is manufactured and wrongdoing is arranged, and be described as this verse describes it. Or it can be the part of the body that touches the ground five times a day in acknowledgement of the One who made it.

The Prophet ﷺ described the sign that will remain on the second kind:

> *"God has forbidden the Fire to consume the traces of prostration."* (al-Bukhārī 806; Muslim 182, from Abū Hurayrah)

The sūrah's last verse will name that alternative explicitly. Before the chapter ends, every reader will have been offered both futures for the same small patch of skin above the eyes.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:17

> **So let him call his cohorts.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Challenge Issued**

*Fal-yadʿu nādiyah* — "So let him call his assembly." The verse takes the form of an imperative, but it is not a command in the ordinary sense. Arabic rhetoricians classify this as *amr lil-taʿjīz* — an imperative issued to demonstrate the other party's incapacity. It is the form of speech used when one says to a person making threats: *Go ahead, then. Call them.*

The Qur'an uses this device repeatedly against those who claimed alternative sources of power:

> *"Say: Call upon those you claim besides God; they possess not the weight of a mote in the heavens or on the earth."* (Qur'an 34:22)

> *"Say: Then bring a Book from God which is a better guide than these two, and I shall follow it, if you are truthful."* (Qur'an 28:49)

The invitation is safe to extend because the outcome is certain. Nothing exposes an empty claim faster than an opportunity to make good on it.

**Nādī: The Word for the Assembly**

*Nādī* comes from the root *n-d-w / n-d-y*, meaning to call, summon, or gather. A *nādī* is a place of gathering or the people gathered in it — a council, a club, an assembly. The related *nidāʾ* is a call or summons; *munādī* is a herald.

For the first audience the term had a concrete referent. Makkah's governing council met at *Dār al-Nadwah*, the assembly house built by Quṣayy ibn Kilāb near the Kaʿbah, where the chiefs of Quraysh deliberated on war, trade, marriage alliances, and — as the sīrah records — on how to deal with Muhammad ﷺ. It was in that house, according to Ibn Isḥāq, that the conspiracy to assassinate him was finally agreed, prompting the Hijrah.

The word therefore does double duty. It names Abū Jahl's supporters and it evokes the institution of Makkan power itself: the room where decisions were made, the network of allies, the tribal muscle. Translating it "cohorts" or "supporters" captures the people; translating it "assembly" or "council" captures the institution. Both are meant.

The Qur'an uses the same word elsewhere with a note of reproach, describing the people of Lot:

> *"...and you commit evil in your assemblies (nādīkum)?"* (Qur'an 29:29)

A gathering is not neutral. It amplifies whatever its members bring to it.

**The Incident Behind the Verse**

The occasion is preserved in the commentary tradition and reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, as the source material records. The Prophet ﷺ was praying when Abū Jahl approached and said, *"Did I not forbid you from this?"* The Prophet ﷺ rebuked him sharply. Abū Jahl replied, *"You know very well that there is no one in Makkah with a larger following than mine"* — an explicit appeal to numbers as the decisive argument.

Verses 17 and 18 were revealed in response. Ibn ʿAbbās commented:

> *"By God, had he called his assembly, the guards of Hell would have seized him."*

Muslim's narration of the parallel incident (2797), already cited, records what actually happened when he approached: he retreated in terror, describing a trench of fire and wings between himself and the Prophet ﷺ, and the Prophet ﷺ said that had he come closer, the angels would have seized him limb by limb.

The pattern is worth noting. The man who boasted of the largest following in Makkah fled alone from something his following could not have helped him with.

**The Sociology of the Threat**

Abū Jahl's argument — *I have more men than you* — is the oldest argument in politics, and the Qur'an takes it seriously enough to answer it directly rather than dismiss it.

It is worth understanding why it seemed so compelling in that setting. Pre-Islamic Arabia had no state, no police, and no courts with enforcement power. Security came entirely from the tribe. A man without a clan's protection could be killed with impunity; a man with a large clan was effectively untouchable. Numbers were not a metaphor for power; they were the whole of it. Abū Jahl was making, by the standards of his world, a decisive and factual point.

The Qur'an's response does not deny that he had the numbers. It changes the frame of reference — which is what the following verse does.

**Why Numbers Fail as a Measure of Truth**

There is a logical error embedded in Abū Jahl's boast that the Qur'an addresses throughout, and it deserves to be named: the confusion of majority with correctness.

> *"And if thou obeyest most of those on earth, they will lead thee astray from the way of God. They follow naught but conjecture, and they do but surmise."* (Qur'an 6:116)

> *"But most of mankind believe not."* (Qur'an 11:17; 13:1; 40:59)

The Qur'an consistently portrays truth as historically the minority position. Noah preached for nine hundred and fifty years and boarded the ark with a handful. Abraham is described as *"a nation unto himself"* (16:120) — one man constituting a community. Moses faced Pharaoh with his brother. The Prophet ﷺ began with a wife, a cousin, a friend, and a freed slave.

The Prophet ﷺ described his own vision of the prophets on the Day of Resurrection:

> *"The nations were shown to me, and I saw a prophet with a small group with him, and a prophet with one or two men with him, and a prophet with no one with him at all."* (al-Bukhārī 5752; Muslim 220, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

This is a corrective that every age needs and that the modern age needs acutely, since it has built machinery for measuring and manufacturing majorities — polling, virality, trending metrics, engagement counts. The number of people who hold a position is data about the people, not about the position.

**What an Assembly Cannot Do**

The deeper point of the verse concerns the limits of collective human power, and the Qur'an lists them plainly.

An assembly can protect a man from other men, but not from death: *"Wherever you may be, death will overtake you, even if you be in lofty towers"* (4:78).

It can influence opinion, but not reality: the Makkan council's unanimous verdict that Muhammad ﷺ was a poet did not make the Qur'an poetry.

And it cannot appear at the judgement:

> *"And you have certainly come to Us alone, as We created you the first time, and you have left behind what We bestowed upon you."* (Qur'an 6:94)

> *"And each of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone."* (Qur'an 19:95)

The gathering that a person spends his life cultivating disperses at the exact moment he most needs it. The Prophet ﷺ described the funeral procession:

> *"Three things follow the deceased: his family, his wealth, and his deeds. Two return and one remains: his family and his wealth return, and his deeds remain."* (al-Bukhārī 6514; Muslim 2960, from Anas)

Worse, the Qur'an describes the assembly turning on its own members:

> *"Friends on that Day will be enemies to one another, save the reverent."* (Qur'an 43:67)

**The Believer's Own Assembly**

There is a positive counterpart that the sūrah's structure implies. Abū Jahl's assembly was a council of intimidation; the believer also has gatherings, and the tradition describes them in exactly opposite terms:

> *"No people gather in one of the houses of God, reciting the Book of God and studying it among themselves, but that tranquillity descends upon them, mercy envelops them, the angels surround them, and God mentions them to those with Him."* (Muslim 2699, from Abū Hurayrah)

> *"God has angels who roam the roads seeking out the people of remembrance. When they find a people remembering God, they call out: Come to what you seek."* (al-Bukhārī 6408; Muslim 2689, from Abū Hurayrah)

Two kinds of assembly, then, and the sūrah sets them in implicit contrast: one that can be summoned and will not come, and one that arrives unsummoned. The difference is not in the number of human beings present but in what surrounds them.

**Standing Alone When the Assembly Is on the Other Side**

The most immediate practical value of this verse is for anyone who has found himself outnumbered in a matter of principle — the employee who will not sign off on the false figure, the student who will not join in the mockery, the family member who holds a position everyone at the table has agreed to ridicule.

The pressure in such moments is not intellectual; it is social, and it is felt in the body. Human beings are built for belonging, and standing against a room is genuinely difficult.

The Qur'an's answer in this verse is not that the assembly does not exist or does not matter. It is that the assembly is not the only party present. Verse 18 will name who else is in the room. And the sūrah has already told the reader, in verse 14, that the One who sees was watching the whole time.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:18

> **We shall call the guards of Hell.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Boast**

*Sa-nadʿu al-zabāniyah* — "We shall summon the guards of Hell." The response to Abū Jahl's assembly is a counter-summons, and the rhetorical construction is exact: the same verb, *daʿā*, "to call," is used on both sides. He calls; God calls. The parallel structure invites the comparison and then makes it absurd.

The prefix *sa-* marks the near future — a decisive and imminent act, not a distant possibility. And the pronoun is again the plural of majesty. The confrontation is deliberately framed as two parties each summoning their supporters, so that the reader can weigh them.

**Al-Zabāniyah: The Word and Its Origins**

*Zabāniyah* is a rare and forceful Arabic word, appearing only here in the Qur'an. Lexicographers derive it from *zabana*, meaning to push violently, to thrust, to shove away. A she-camel that kicks with her hind legs when milked is called *zabūn*; the *zibnīy* is a strong pusher. The Arabs also used the term for the strongest of a group of police or enforcers — those who physically remove people.

The word is generally understood as the plural of *zibniyah*, and in Qur'anic usage it denotes the angels appointed over the Fire: the ones who seize, thrust, and drive the condemned. The translation "guards of Hell" captures the function; the root captures the manner.

The Qur'an describes them elsewhere:

> *"O you who believe! Guard yourselves and your families against a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, over which are angels, stern and severe, who disobey not God in what He commands them, and do what they are commanded."* (Qur'an 66:6)

> *"And what will make thee know what Saqar? It leaves nothing and spares nothing, scorching the skin. Over it are nineteen. And We have appointed none but angels as wardens of the Fire."* (Qur'an 74:27–31)

> *"And those in the Fire will say to the keepers of Hell: Call upon your Lord to lighten for us a day of the punishment."* (Qur'an 40:49)

Their chief is named in the Qur'an:

> *"And they will cry: O Mālik, let thy Lord make an end of us! He will say: Truly you shall remain."* (Qur'an 43:77)

**The Mismatch Made Explicit**

The confrontation the verse constructs is the entire point, and it repays being laid out.

Abū Jahl's assembly consists of men of Quraysh: perhaps a few hundred, armed with swords, holding tribal authority, capable of boycott, torture, exile, and murder — real power, effective within the borders of a small city, for the duration of a human lifetime.

The other party consists of angels described as *"stern and severe,"* who never disobey a command, whose strength is such that the Prophet ﷺ said of a single one of them, Gabriel, that he had six hundred wings, each pair filling the horizon (al-Bukhārī 3232).

The Qur'an lets the reader perform the arithmetic. This is why the verse needs no adjectives of triumph and no gloating. The bare statement of who will be summoned is sufficient.

**Why the Answer Comes in Kind**

There is a rhetorical elegance in answering a boast about numbers with a counter-summons rather than with a theological rebuke. God does not reply, "Numbers are irrelevant." He replies on Abū Jahl's own chosen terrain — a contest of retinues — and wins it decisively.

This is a pattern in the Qur'an's engagement with its opponents: it meets people inside their own frame and shows the frame collapsing. When the Quraysh boasted of eloquence, they were challenged to produce a chapter. When they boasted of ancestry, they were told that the most noble is the most reverent. When they boasted of numbers, this verse was revealed.

**Angels as Enforcers of Justice**

A note on ʿaqīdah is appropriate here, because the image of angelic punishers can be misread.

The Qur'an presents the *zabāniyah* not as sadistic beings who enjoy inflicting harm, but as creatures of perfect obedience executing a just sentence: *"who disobey not God in what He commands them, and do what they are commanded"* (66:6). They are the instruments of a verdict, not its authors. There is no independent malice in them. Their severity is the severity of justice itself, which does not soften for a criminal's status.

This matters, because it distinguishes the Islamic conception from a mythology of tormenting demons. Hell in the Qur'an is administered, not chaotic. It has wardens, gates, levels, and a governing decree. Even the punishment is ordered.

It also matters for the moral logic of the sūrah. Abū Jahl's power in Makkah rested on men who could be bribed, intimidated, persuaded, or tired out. The forces named in this verse cannot be reached by any of those means. The distinction between the two assemblies is not only one of strength but of *incorruptibility*.

**The Unseen Forces Available to the Believer**

The sūrah shows one side's reinforcements. The Qur'an elsewhere shows the other side's, and the contrast completes the picture.

At Badr, where Abū Jahl was killed, the Muslims numbered slightly over three hundred against roughly a thousand:

> *"When you sought help of your Lord and He answered you: I shall reinforce you with a thousand angels, rank upon rank."* (Qur'an 8:9)

In the cave during the Hijrah, with the pursuers' feet above them and Abū Bakr in fear:

> *"...the second of two, when they were in the cave, when he said to his companion: Grieve not; truly God is with us. Then God sent down His tranquillity upon him and supported him with hosts you saw not."* (Qur'an 9:40)

And the general principle:

> *"And none knows the hosts of thy Lord save He."* (Qur'an 74:31)

The believer is therefore never counting correctly when he counts only the visible. This is the practical consolation of the verse for anyone who is outnumbered: the tally on the table is not the tally in the field.

**How the Confrontation Actually Ended**

The verse was revealed in Makkah while Abū Jahl was at the height of his influence, and its claim looked, at that moment, extravagant. The Muslims were a persecuted minority who could not pray safely in their own city.

Roughly a decade later, at the wells of Badr in Ramadan of the second year after the Hijrah, Abū Jahl marched out at the head of the assembly he had boasted of. He was killed there, and with him a substantial part of the Makkan leadership. The Qur'an commented on the outcome:

> *"Soon shall the multitude be routed and turn their backs."* (Qur'an 54:45)

Al-Bukhārī records that the Prophet ﷺ stood over the bodies of the Quraysh chiefs cast into the well at Badr and addressed them by name, saying: *"Have you found what your Lord promised to be true? For I have found what my Lord promised me to be true."* When asked how he could speak to the dead, he replied that they heard him as well as the living, though they could not reply (al-Bukhārī 3976, from Anas and Ibn ʿUmar).

The verse's prediction was not left as an abstraction. The community that first heard it lived to see it verified.

**The Verse's Enduring Instruction**

Two lessons follow for any reader.

*For the one tempted to intimidate:* whatever leverage a person holds over others — employment, money, physical strength, social standing, institutional authority, an audience — is held within a system he did not create and cannot exempt himself from. The forces that guarantee justice are not on the payroll.

*For the one being intimidated:* the visible balance of power is a partial report. This does not license recklessness, and the Qur'an does not tell believers to seek confrontation; the Makkan community was commanded to patience for thirteen years. But it does mean that the fear which normally drives a person to abandon principle is founded on incomplete information.

And the sūrah is not going to end here, with the two armies facing each other. It ends with a single instruction to the individual believer about what to do while all of this is going on — and the instruction is not to fight, argue, or flee, but to put his forehead on the ground.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:19

> **Nay! Obey him not, but prostrate and draw nigh.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Kallā and the Sūrah's Resolution**

For the third and final time the sūrah says *kallā* — "Nay!" The first (v. 6) rebuked human ingratitude; the second (v. 15) cut off the obstructor's course of action; this one closes the argument and turns, at last, from the accused to the believer.

Commentators offer two readings of this *kallā*, as the source notes. It may be a final rebuke to Abū Jahl — *no, it will not be as you imagine; your assembly will not save you*. Or it may function as a transition: *no more of this; here is what you are to do.* Both hold, and the verse then delivers three imperatives in rapid succession: do not obey him; prostrate; draw near.

After nineteen verses of creation, teaching, ingratitude, obstruction, warning, and confrontation, the entire chapter lands on a single instruction to an individual. And that instruction is not to argue, retaliate, organize, or flee. It is to worship.

**"Obey Him Not": The Command to Continue**

*Lā tuṭiʿhu* — "do not obey him." The verb *aṭāʿa* means to comply, to submit to another's direction. What is being forbidden here is not merely agreement with Abū Jahl's views but compliance with his prohibition. He said: stop praying. The command is: do not stop.

The phrasing is deliberately narrow and therefore powerful. It does not say "fight him," "curse him," or "shame him." It says: do not do what he told you to do. The resistance prescribed is the simple continuation of the act he tried to prevent.

The same instruction is given in the sūrah generally held to be among the next revealed:

> *"So obey not the deniers. They wish that thou wouldst compromise, so that they would compromise."* (Qur'an 68:8–9)

That second verse names the mechanism the first is guarding against. Opposition rarely demands total surrender at once; it proposes an exchange — soften this, and we will soften that. The Makkans offered exactly such deals: worship our gods for a year and we will worship yours for a year; take wealth, kingship, and marriage into the noblest families, and drop the matter of the idols. Sūrat al-Kāfirūn was the answer to those proposals, and this verse is its precursor: no compliance, and no negotiation over the act of worship itself.

The principle is stated as a general rule of obedience by the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"There is no obedience to a created being in disobedience to the Creator."* (Aḥmad 1095; also al-Ḥākim, from ʿAlī)

> *"Hearing and obeying are obligatory upon the Muslim in what he likes and dislikes, unless he is commanded to disobey; if he is commanded to disobey, there is no hearing and no obeying."* (al-Bukhārī 7144; Muslim 1839, from Ibn ʿUmar)

This establishes the moral architecture of Islamic obedience: it is real and extensive — to parents, to leaders, to teachers, to lawful authority — and it is *bounded*. The boundary is exactly where compliance would require disobedience to God. Verse 19 is the earliest statement of that boundary in the Qur'an's chronology.

**"Wasjud": The Command to Prostrate**

*Wasjud* — "and prostrate." The verb comes from the root *s-j-d*, which denotes lowering oneself, submitting, and specifically placing the forehead on the ground. From it come *sajdah* (a prostration), *masjid* (the place of prostration — the mosque), and *sājid* (one who prostrates).

The choice of this specific act as the culmination of the chapter is exact, and it works on several levels at once.

*It is the physical opposite of the sin diagnosed.* The disease of the sūrah is *ṭughyān* born of imagined self-sufficiency (vv. 6–7) — the overflowing of one's proper station. Prostration is the bodily enactment of correct station: the highest part of a man placed on the lowest surface available.

*It is the precise act that was forbidden.* Abū Jahl's threat concerned the placing of the face on the ground. The command answers the prohibition in its own terms.

*It is the posture in which the servant is most vulnerable and most protected.* A prostrating man cannot see an approaching enemy. The Prophet ﷺ remained in that posture while filth was placed on his back. The sūrah's answer to danger is not defensive vigilance but deeper surrender.

The Qur'an presents prostration as the condition of all creation, not only of human beings:

> *"Hast thou not seen that unto God prostrates whosoever is in the heavens and whosoever is on the earth, and the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, the beasts, and many among mankind?"* (Qur'an 22:18)

Note the qualifier at the end: *many* among mankind — not all. Everything else in existence prostrates by nature and necessity; only the human being does so by choice, and only he can refuse. That capacity to refuse is what makes the voluntary prostration worth something.

**"Waqtarib": The Command to Draw Near**

*Waqtarib* — "and draw near." The verb is the eighth form of *q-r-b*, "to be near," and the form conveys deliberate effort to approach: *seek nearness, come closer*.

This is the goal of the entire sūrah, and it recasts everything before it. The chapter has not been merely a warning; it has been an invitation. The purpose of religion is here stated in a single word: *nearness*.

The Prophet ﷺ identified the moment at which nearness is greatest:

> *"The closest that a servant is to his Lord is while he is prostrating; so make abundant supplication therein."* (Muslim 482, from Abū Hurayrah)

This is a striking teaching. One might expect the moment of greatest closeness to be while standing in recitation, addressing God with His own words. Instead it is the moment of maximum lowliness. The logic is consistent throughout Islamic spirituality: the servant rises by descending. The Prophet ﷺ stated the principle generally:

> *"No one humbles himself for the sake of God but that God raises him."* (Muslim 2588, from Abū Hurayrah)

The divine saying transmitted by al-Bukhārī describes what the sought nearness produces:

> *"My servant does not draw near to Me with anything more beloved to Me than what I have made obligatory upon him. And My servant continues to draw near to Me with voluntary acts until I love him. And when I love him, I become his hearing with which he hears, his sight with which he sees, his hand with which he strikes, and his foot with which he walks. Were he to ask Me, I would surely give him; and were he to seek refuge in Me, I would surely grant him refuge."* (al-Bukhārī 6502, from Abū Hurayrah)

The verse's command and the ḥadīth's promise fit together precisely: *iqtarib* — draw near — and this is what drawing near yields.

**Why the Sūrah Ends Here and Not with the Punishment**

Structurally, the Qur'an could have ended this chapter at verse 18, with the guards of Hell summoned and the tyrant seized. That would have been a coherent, and satisfying, conclusion to a passage about persecution.

Instead the last word goes to the individual worshipper, and the reason is theological. The sūrah is not, finally, about Abū Jahl. He was the occasion, not the subject. The subject is the relationship between a creature and his Creator, established in verses 1–5, damaged in verses 6–7, tested in verses 9–16, and restored in verse 19.

The chapter therefore returns to where it began. It opened with a command — *Recite* — and it closes with two commands — *prostrate and draw near*. Both are addressed to the same man, and both describe the same relationship in different registers: receiving God's word, and submitting God's body. Knowledge at the start, worship at the end; and the sūrah's warning is precisely about the person who takes the first without the second.

**The Verse of Prostration**

This verse is one of the passages of the Qur'an at whose recitation a prostration is prescribed — *sajdat al-tilāwah*, the prostration of recitation. Muslim records:

> *"When the son of Adam recites a verse of prostration and prostrates, Satan withdraws weeping, saying: Woe unto me! The son of Adam was commanded to prostrate and he prostrated, so his is Paradise; and I was commanded to prostrate and I refused, so mine is the Fire."* (Muslim 81, from Abū Hurayrah)

The report links this verse to the primordial refusal. Iblīs's fall, as the Qur'an narrates it, came from a single act of non-prostration rooted in a claim of superiority: *"I am better than he; Thou hast created me of fire, and hast created him of clay"* (7:12). That is the identical structure as verses 6 and 7 of this sūrah — transgression arising from self-assessment.

So when a reciter reaches the end of Sūrat al-ʿAlaq and places his forehead on the ground, he is doing three things at once: obeying the last command of the chapter, reversing the sin the chapter diagnosed, and performing the act that the first rebel in creation refused.

There is one further symmetry, noted by the commentators. The first passage revealed of the Qur'an ends with the command to prostrate; and the first prostration ever demanded of any creature was demanded of the angels before Adam. The Book that begins with reading ends its first chapter of revelation with bowing — knowledge completed by submission.

**Prayer as the Answer to Persecution**

The practical instruction of this verse is worth stating plainly, because it is counterintuitive.

The Makkan Muslims were being tortured, boycotted, and killed. The revealed response, throughout the thirteen Makkan years, was not armed resistance — permission for that came only after the Hijrah (22:39). It was patience, and prayer.

This was not passivity. Prayer under prohibition is itself the most direct form of defiance available: it declares that the prohibiting authority lacks jurisdiction. And it accomplishes something no confrontation can, which is the preservation of the persecuted person's inner state. A community that responds to oppression with hatred is deformed by its oppressors even if it wins. A community that responds with worship remains itself.

The Prophet ﷺ practised this personally. Ḥudhayfah reported:

> *"When something distressed the Prophet ﷺ, he would pray."* (Abū Dāwūd 1319)

And the Qur'an prescribes it in general:

> *"And seek help in patience and prayer; and truly it is burdensome, save for the humble."* (Qur'an 2:45)

**What the Sūrah Leaves in the Reader's Hands**

Nineteen verses that began in a cave with a frightened man being told to read end with an instruction that any reader can carry out in the next sixty seconds, at no cost, requiring no permission from anyone.

The chapter has established that God created him, ennobled him, taught him what he did not know, sees everything he does, and will receive him back. It has shown him the shape of the life that forgets these facts, and named where that life ends. And it closes by handing him the one action that contradicts all of it — the placing of the forehead on the ground — and the promise of what that action is *for*: not merely obedience, and not merely safety, but nearness.

*Wasjud waqtarib.* Prostrate, and draw near.
