# Sūrah Muḥammad (Chapter 47) — Original Expanded Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

Sūrah Muḥammad is a Madinan chapter concerned with the moral reality of allegiance. It contrasts belief with rejection, examines the pressure of conflict, exposes hypocrisy, and ends by calling the community to generosity. Its central question is not merely who claims faith, but whose conduct demonstrates that claim. The following commentary is newly written from the source themes, with the initial file used only for orientation and verse identification.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:1

> **1 Those who disbelieve and turn from the way of God—He has made their deeds go astray.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The moral cost of active rejection**

The opening contrasts two movements: turning away from revelation and obstructing others. Kufr is not treated as a neutral absence but as a chosen covering that redirects effort away from its proper end.

**Arabic vocabulary, grammar, and rhetorical movement**

The principal terms of this verse must be heard in relation to their Arabic roots and to the movement of the sentence. The Qur’an frequently chooses a verb rather than a static noun so that the reader sees a moral process unfolding: a person turns away, believes, obeys, spends, remembers, becomes hardened, or returns. That verbal quality matters here. The verse is not offering a detached definition; it is showing how conviction becomes visible in decisions and how repeated decisions shape the heart. Its grammar also places responsibility in the human sphere while preserving God’s complete knowledge and authority. The result is neither crude fatalism nor self-sufficient autonomy. Human beings choose, respond, resist, repent, and are accountable; God guides, judges, forgives, and grants the final success.

The verse’s central expression—1 those who disbelieve and turn from the way of god—he has made their deeds go astray.—also carries a wider Qur’anic resonance. Across revelation, guidance is described as light, a straight path, healing, and life; rejection is described as darkness, crookedness, disease, and death. These are not merely poetic opposites. They explain why an apparently private attitude eventually affects conduct, relationships, institutions, and the destiny of a community. **Expanded Commentary** The language therefore joins inner orientation to outward consequence: what is loved and trusted within eventually governs what the hands build and what the tongue defends.

**Revelation, scholarship, and the wider Qur’anic argument**

Classical exegetes such as al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and Ibn ʿAbbās’s transmitted school would read this verse alongside its surrounding passage rather than isolate one phrase from the chapter’s argument. That method protects interpretation from exaggeration. A warning about conflict must be read with the Qur’an’s rules of justice and restraint; a promise of Paradise must be read with the requirement of faith and righteous action; a condemnation of hypocrisy must not become a license for declaring the hidden state of particular people. Relevant Qur’anic principles include:

> **“Indeed, God commands justice, excellence, and giving to kinsfolk, and He forbids indecency, wrongdoing, and transgression.”** (Qur’an 16:90)

This wider principle gives the verse its proper balance. It calls the reader to serious commitment without permitting arrogance, cruelty, or careless judgment. It also shows the coherence of the Qur’an: doctrine, worship, social responsibility, and the Hereafter are not separate compartments but parts of one moral order.

**Psychology, society, and practical application**

The human problem addressed here remains recognizable. People often desire the reputation of conviction without the inconvenience of obedience; they prefer a public identity that costs little, or they mistake strong emotion for settled faith. This verse provides a test: does a claimed belief make a person more truthful, more dependable, more merciful, and more willing to correct wrongdoing? In a family, workplace, mosque, or political community, the same test distinguishes service from performance. The believer should apply it first to the self: identify the place where fear, status, appetite, resentment, or comfort is quietly overruling principle. Then make one concrete correction—restore a trust, speak honestly, repair a relationship, give what is due, or return to prayer. Such action turns commentary into worship and protects the verse from becoming merely information.

**A disciplined response to the verse**

The most responsible way to carry this verse into life is to combine understanding with action. First, read it in its immediate context, so that neither promise nor warning is detached from the balance of the chapter. Second, examine the motive it exposes: am I seeking truth, or merely protecting comfort, reputation, group loyalty, or personal advantage? Third, translate the answer into a measurable act before the lesson fades—a repaired obligation, a restrained reaction, a truthful conversation, a private act of charity, or a renewed commitment to worship. This method protects religious reflection from becoming a performance. It also preserves hope: a person who recognizes a deficiency and turns sincerely has not been defeated by the verse; he has received the opportunity to respond to it. The chapter repeatedly directs the believer away from self-congratulation and toward steadfast, accountable obedience.

A sound reading also keeps the verse connected to the accountability of the Hereafter. The believer does not measure success only by immediate approval, victory, wealth, or emotional relief. Actions are weighed by truth, justice, sincerity, and their effect on people. This perspective gives courage without arrogance and patience without passivity. It teaches the reader to work with the means available, seek God’s help, and leave ultimate judgment to Him. In this way the verse becomes a living discipline rather than a historical quotation: it corrects intention, governs conduct, and directs hope toward the One who knows what is hidden and disclosed.
