# Sūrah Ibrāhīm (Chapter 14) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet Who Prayed for a People Not Yet Born**

Sūrah Ibrāhīm, "Abraham," takes its name from a man rather than from a law, a battle, or a cosmological image, and that choice of title already tells the reader what the sūrah is about. Its name comes from the central duʿāʾ of Abraham in verses 35–41, in which the forefather of the prophets stands at the Sanctuary and prays a prayer of astonishing range: for the security of the land, for himself and his children against idolatry, for the hearts of future strangers to incline toward a lineage he leaves in a barren valley, for fruit where there is no cultivation, for himself and his descendants to become established performers of the prayer, and for the forgiveness of himself, his parents, and all the believers on the Day of Reckoning. A sūrah that borrows its title from such a prayer has already declared its theme: faith is not a private transaction. It is a standing petition for generations — the prophet as the one who intercedes for his children before they are even born, and the Book as that intercession continued, written down and delivered.

**Its Place in the Series of the Book**

Ibrāhīm is the fifth in a series of six consecutive sūrahs — chapters 13 through 18 — whose opening verses include the same three disconnected letters, alif, lām, rāʾ, and which each speak of the Book. The repetition is deliberate: a whole block of revelation is framed as the same declaration. The sūrah itself is Makkan in character, its speech directed at the Quraysh in Makkah, with the important exception of verses 28–29, which the majority of exegetes place in the Madinan period in connection with the defeat of the Quraysh at Badr. It contains fifty-two verses and fills most of the fifteenth part of the standard thirty-part division of the muṣḥaf — a part that carries the sūrah's own name, Ibrāhīm, a quiet confirmation that the readers of the earliest centuries counted this chapter among the great landmarks of the Book.

**The Sūrah's Central Equation: Gratitude and Its Shadow**

Running beneath nearly every section of the chapter is a single moral equation, stated plainly in verses 7–8: "If you give thanks, I shall surely grant you increase, but if you are ungrateful, truly My Punishment is severe." Gratitude (shukr) and ingratitude (kufr) are treated here as the two possible responses to a blessing, and the sūrah's own vocabulary makes the point: *kufr* — disbelief — shares its root with the act of covering, of burying something out of sight, and the sūrah understands ingratitude as precisely that: a blessing covered up, a favor made invisible, a giver forgotten. The same root of concealment is what the later verses apply to the Makkans, who "exchanged the Blessing of God for ingratitude" (v. 28). Gratitude, patience, and trust are the three virtues the sūrah cultivates; each appears at a structural hinge. Gratitude in the story of Moses (vv. 5–8); trust (tawakkul) in the speech of the persecuted messengers (vv. 11–12); patience beside gratitude as the mark of those who see signs (v. 5). The sūrah's dark mirror-images run in parallel: the tyrant who fails (v. 15), the deeds scattered like ash (v. 18), the hearts that exchanged blessing for disbelief (v. 28).

**The Architecture of the Chapter**

Read across, the fifty-two verses form a movement of remarkable economy. Verses 1–4 open: the Book leads people out of darkness into light, God owns all that is, those who choose the temporary over the permanent are far astray, and no messenger was sent except in the language of his people. Verses 5–8 retell the covenant of Moses — gratitude, increase, and the Self-Sufficiency of the Giver. Verses 9–15 give the history of the messengers who came with clear proofs and were rejected, sealed with the law that every arrogant oppressor fails. Verses 16–22 descend into the fate of that oppressor, the parable of ash, the power of God to replace the whole creation, the assembly of the weak and the arrogant on the Last Day, and the confession of Satan. Verse 23 turns the page to the Gardens. Verses 24–27 give the sūrah's horticultural theology — the good tree, the bad tree, and the firm word. Verses 28–31 confront the Makkans directly and then issue the standing program for the believers: prayer and spending. Verses 32–34 recount the blessings — water, ships, sun, moon, uncountable provision. Verses 35–41 are the heart: the seven petitions of Abraham. Verses 42–52 close: the heedless wrongdoers, the Day whose eyes stare and whose hearts are vacant, the plea for a short return, the dwellings of the self-wronging, the plots that cannot move the mountains, the promise that is never broken, the earth that is changed, the guilty in fetters, the swift reckoning, and the proclamation (naḍān) to mankind.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the fifty-two verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order. Where the source commentary treats verses in pairs or groups (5–6, 7–8, 11–12, 13–14, 16–17, 19–20, 24–25, 28–29, 32–33, 42–43, 49–50), each verse here is separated and given its own full treatment, as the method requires. Each verse is expanded through its Arabic language, the testimony of the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, Ibn ʿAbbās, and others), authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, and the living logic that connects the verse to the conditions of the modern human being. Nothing is compressed because the chapter is long. The sūrah that opens with three letters and the promise of light closes with a proclamation to the people of understanding — and the reading moves from the first to the last without pause.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:1

> **Alif. Lām. Rā. [This is] a Book that We have sent down unto thee, that thou mightest bring forth mankind out of darkness into light, by the leave of their Lord, unto the path of the Mighty, the Praised—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Letters Before the Light**

The sūrah opens, like five of its sister chapters, with three letters pronounced by name — *alif, lām, rāʾ* — standing alone, unattached, like a door standing open before the reader has entered. The six chapters that begin with this same combination (thirteen through eighteen) form a single revealed block in which each sūrah announces, in its first verse, the same declaration about the Book, and Ibrāhīm is the fifth voice in that chorus. From the earliest generation these letters raised a question that has never been fully laid to rest: what does it mean to open a divine address with raw letters? The companions did not silence the question. Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq is reported to have said that every book has its secret, and that the secret of this Book lies in its openings, and ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib was reported to have called these letters the quintessence (ṣafwah) of the Qur'an. The sober consensus that the commentators — al-Ṭabarī above all — settled upon is a balance: the letters belong to the class of the Qur'an's chosen mysteries. Their most publicly argued function is the proclamation of inimitability (iʿjāz): the alphabet of the Arab's own eloquence is laid out at the threshold as the very material out of which this Book is wrought, and the challenge implicit in the material is the entire Book. Whatever interpretation one adopts, the effect at the reader's door is immediate and intended: before a single proposition is made, the recitation halts on sound alone, and the listener's attention is gathered the way a congregation is gathered before the khutbah — by a pause.

**A Book That Leads Out, Not One That Is Read**

The sentence that follows the letters does not describe a book as information. It describes a book as an operation: *that thou mightest bring forth mankind out of darkness into light* — the verb *yukhrija* is the verb of exit, of bringing a thing out of the place where it is held. The Qur'an is presented here as a passage, a route, a corridor, and the Prophet ﷺ is the one who leads the people through it. The image is used of God Himself in five other places in the Qur'an, and the variation is instructive:

> *"God is the Disposer of affairs of the believers; He brings them out of the darknesses into the light, but those who earn anger — for them are dwellings of Fire."* (Qur'an 2:257)

> *"God guides those who follow His plea to the ways of peace and brings them out of the darknesses into the light by His leave."* (Qur'an 5:16)

And the same construction appears in 33:43, 57:9, and 65:11. Two features deserve attention. First, the darknesses are plural (*ẓulumāt*) while the light is singular (*nūr*): the condition that must be left is layered — the darkness of ignorance nested inside the darkness of disbelief nested inside the darkness of doubt and habit, one veil behind another — while what is entered is a single light, the light of faith, knowledge, and certainty. A person may escape the outer darkness and remain in the inner one; the Book's task, as the sūrah will keep showing, is the entire corridor. Second, the subject shifts. Elsewhere it is God who brings forth; here it is the Prophet who brings forth, acting in the Book. The mechanism of guidance in the Qur'an is never impersonal: a Book that no one carries would be a lantern in an empty house.

**By the Leave of Their Lord: Grace Precedes Response**

Between the two poles of the image — darkness and light — the verse places a phrase that the superficial reading misses: *by the leave of their Lord* (*bi-idhn Rabbihim*). The bringing forth does not happen by the Book's own power or the messenger's effort. It happens by God's permission, His opening, His prior grace. The same phrase stands at 5:16 and 33:43, and the theological weight of its repetition is heavy. If guidance were simply the product of a clear argument, then guidance would be compulsion in disguise — every mind forced to the same conclusion — and human responsibility, the pillar on which the whole Qur'anic system of reward and blame stands, would collapse. The clause *by the leave of their Lord* is what holds the two truths together: the light is real, the guidance is real, the person who walks into it chose to walk, and the capacity to walk was itself a gift given before the first step. The sūrah will return to this same structure in verses 4 and 27, where God guides whom He wills and leads astray whom He wills; verse 1 has already planted the premise that makes those verses coherent. For the practitioner, the clause is a discipline of humility: the success of a call to God is attributed to God, and no one — not the preacher, not the movement, not the reader — may claim the exit from the darkness as a personal achievement.

**Unto the Path of the Mighty, the Praised**

The destination of the passage is not named as "Islam" or "Paradise" or "the straight path," but as *the path of the Mighty, the Praised* (*ṣalāḥ al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥamīd*). The word *ṣalāḥ* is one of the Qur'an's most polysemous, and the sūrah benefits from all its senses at once. The root ṣ-l-ḥ carries soundness, uprightness, reconciliation, and victory: *al-ṣāliḥ* is the upright one, *al-ṣulḥ* is the reconciliation that ends a quarrel, *al-muṣliḥ* is the one who sets things right, and *fa-ẓalā* — to be upon the ṣalāḥ — means to be upon the winning side. The "path" in this verse is therefore simultaneously the road (the direction), the uprightness (the posture on the road), the soundness (the condition of the one who walks it), and the victory (the arrival). One word does the work of the whole journey. The two names that follow are the path's guarantees. *Al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty — is the One whose power is perfect and who cannot be overpowered; a path whose owner is the Mighty is a path that no rival road can block. *Al-Ḥamīd* — the Praised — is the One who is worthy of praise in all that He does, and He is likewise the One who thanks those who thank Him; a path whose owner praises and thanks is a path on which praise is not consumed but returned. The pairing is not decorative: the sūrah's own ending, in verse 52, will close on the name *al-Wāḥid* — the One — as the path's final landmark, and the opening two names frame the walk.

**The Image That Runs Through the Sūrah**

A reader who finishes the chapter will find that the corridor image of verse 1 has not been left behind. In verse 5, God commands Moses: "Bring thy people out of darkness into light" — the same verb, *akhrij*, the same darkness, the same light, transferred from the Book to the greatest of the Lawgivers. The sūrah's architecture is a repetition of this single gesture: the Book leads out, the prophets lead out, and the light is entered by leave. The analogy for modern experience is not difficult. A miner deep in a tunnel does not see the exit until the last bend; the lantern has been working all the way through, and the darkness has been thinning in layers long before it is noticed. So is the work of guidance: a long corridor, layers of darkness peeled off one at a time, and a light that is singular at the end because the path was singular all along.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:2

> **God, unto Whom belongs whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth. Woe unto the disbelievers for a severe punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ownership Stated in the Same Breath as the Warning**

Verse 1 described a Book that moves people; verse 2 describes the One who owns the ground on which the movement takes place. *Allāhu lāhu mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — "God, unto Whom belongs whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth" — is the most compressed statement of divine ownership in the Qur'an, and the sūrah does not state it once by accident: it is the load-bearing wall on which the threat that follows is hung. The same declaration recurs at 34:1, in the Pedestal Verse, *"God — there is no god but Him, the Ever-Living, the Sustainer… To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth"* (2:255), and in the near-identical formulae at 10:68, 20:6, and 22:64. When the Qur'an needs to ground a command, a promise, or a threat in the deepest possible authority, it reaches for this sentence. The logic is not merely theological but juridical: the one who owns the territory is the one whose law governs it, the one before whom the tenant is accountable, and the one whose permission makes any dwelling lawful. To say "everything belongs to God" is therefore not a pious conclusion but a premise — the premise from which worship, justice, reward, and punishment all follow. A person who lives as though the ownership were his own has not disproved anything; he has merely made his own position the one the verse is about to address.

**The Rhetorical Pivot: From the Offer to the Stake**

The structure of the opening pair is a masterclass in divine rhetoric. Verse 1 is an offer, made to *mankind* in general — a Book, a light, a path, a permission. Verse 2 is a warning, made to *the disbelievers* in particular — a woe, a punishment, a severity. The chapter has not yet named what disbelief even is; it will define it carefully in verse 3 as a choice of priorities. But the warning precedes the definition on purpose: the stake is raised before the rules of the game are explained, and the reader learns from the sequence that the issue is not information but response. The Qur'an never presents itself as a text awaiting evidence. It presents itself as a light already turned on, and the only question the text leaves standing is whether the person in the room will walk toward it or sit in the corner. The threat, in this design, is not a threat of cruelty; it is the honest statement of what a corner looks like from the other side of the room.

**Woe: The Name That Carries a Sound**

The word the verse uses for the coming doom is *wayl* — "woe" — and it is one of the Qur'an's most loaded words. In usage it is at once destruction, a cry of anguish, and (in some early glosses transmitted through the exegetes) a deep valley in the Fire from which that cry rises. The Qur'an builds an entire poem around the word in Sūrah al-Ghāshiya, where the disbelievers are addressed with a question that refuses to be answered:

> *"Woe, that Day, to the deniers. And what will make you know what that is? And what will make you know what that is?"* (Qur'an 88:3–5)

The rhetorical move is the same as the one in verse 2 here: the name is given, and the reader is invited to understand the name not by definition but by the incapacity of definition. "Woe" does not describe a penalty so much as register a sound — the sound a soul makes when it discovers the distance between what it chose and what it has chosen. The verse pairs *wayl* with *ʿadhāb shadīd* — "a severe punishment" — and the adjective is doing real work. The Qur'an elsewhere qualifies punishment gently, mildly, or doubly (a *shadīd* punishment, an *alīm* punishment, an *al-ʿadhāb al-alyīm*); the plain, unadorned severity here signals that nothing in the coming description of the disbelievers' fate will be softened. The sūrah will honor that promise in verses 16–17 and 49–50, where the drinking of the oozing pus, the death that comes from every side, and the garments of pitch are given in detail. The opening warning is a table of contents for the chapter's seriousness.

**Why the Disbeliever, and Not the Sinner, Bears the Woe**

A question presses itself on the reader: verse 1 addressed *mankind*, and verse 2 addresses *the disbelievers* — why is the threat aimed at disbelief specifically? The answer is already in the verse's first clause. Disbelief, in the root meaning of the Qur'anic word, is covering — burying what is given, making a gift invisible, refusing to see the Giver. A person who has been given a light and then insists on sitting in the corner has not committed a mistake of judgment; he has committed a covering of the light. The *shadīd* punishment follows from the gravity of the covering, because the covering is not ignorance — the light was offered, named, and sent in the language of the people (as verse 4 will add). The classical exegetes read the verse as a threat specifically against those who refuse the Book and so remain outside the passage from darkness to light that verse 1 describes: the punishment is severe because the remedy was complete. For the modern reader, the distinction matters because it locates the danger. The verse does not threaten the one who stumbles in the corridor; it threatens the one who seals the door. The first is a matter of patience and correction; the second is a matter of a sealed heart, and the sūrah treats the two with the difference in tone that the difference deserves.

**The Quiet Theology Beneath the Warning**

Read closely, the first clause of verse 2 also settles the ground for the sūrah's treatment of blessing and ingratitude in the verses that follow. If everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to God, then every blessing received by a human being is held on loan from the Owner — which is exactly the premise on which the demand of gratitude in verses 7–8 stands, and the premise that makes the Makkans' "exchanging the Blessing of God for ingratitude" (v. 28) the transaction it is. The ownership clause is not a prologue that the chapter forgets; it is the sūrah's first legal finding, and every later verdict — the increase of the grateful, the failure of the tyrant, the reckoning of the guilty — is an application of it. The warning and the ownership stand in the same breath because they are the same thought seen from two sides: everything is His, and what is denied is answered.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:3

> **Those who prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter, and who turn from the way of God and seek to make it crooked; it is they who are far astray.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Choice That Defines the Chooser**

The verse that carries the woe of verse 2 is now defined, and the definition is precise: *those who prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter* (*alladhīna faḍīlū al-ḥayāta al-dunyā ʿalā al-ākhirah*). The verb is *faḍīlū*, from the root f-d-l, which governs the whole field of preference: *fayḍ* is the abundance that overflows, *fāḍil* is the excellent portion, and *tafḍīl* is the act of ranking one thing above another. The Qur'an here is not condemning the worldly life — elsewhere it commands the believers to enjoy the lawful good of this life and does not make asceticism a religion. What it condemns is a ranking: the deliberate act of placing the impermanent above the permanent. The definition is therefore a definition of the heart, not of circumstances. A person in poverty who keeps his priorities oriented toward the lasting life is not in this category, and a person in abundance who lives as though the temporary were everything is exactly in it. The sūrah's first portrait of the disbeliever is thus a portrait of a preference, and that is significant: the Qur'an consistently treats the failure of the heart as prior to the failure of the act, because the act is the preference made visible.

**The Arithmetic of the Temporary and the Permanent**

Why should the preference be a matter of such gravity? The answer is arithmetic, and the Qur'an expects the reader to do it. The life of this world is finite by its own definition — *dunyā* itself means "the near one," the lower and nearer life, the one that is by position a vestibule. The Hereafter is defined throughout the Qur'an as the abode of permanence, the place without expiry, the *dār al-qarār*, the dwelling with stability. To prefer one to the other is, in the language of the sūrah's own later imagery, to sell a forest to buy a candle. The classical commentators sharpen the point with a psychology that is older than the sūrah and truer than most modern ones. The gloss transmitted from the tradition that "whoever loves something prefers it and pursues it" (Ibn ʿAjībah) puts the verse in the grammar of love: preference is not a neutral calculation, it is the confession of the heart's attachment. The Sufi poet Fakhr al-Dīn al-ʿIrāqī (d. 688/1289) expressed the same law in a couplet the exegetes preserved:

> *"Every heart that prefers the caprice of this world / Increases in body, but diminishes in spirit."*

The spiritual mechanics of the verse are this: the heart that is attached to the world imprisons the spirit in the body. The body is the vehicle, and the world is the load; the load that is never set down becomes the weight that never leaves, and the spirit, which by nature reaches beyond the physical, finds its wings folded by the very thing it was meant to carry. The imbalance the verse names is not that the world is evil but that the world, preferred, becomes the measure — and a measure that is mistaken for the thing measured corrupts the measuring of everything else.

**Two Motions of Rejection: Turning, and Crooking**

The verse then describes what the preference produces in the world, and it describes it in two distinct motions that must not be collapsed. The first is *wa-ʿan sabīli Allāh yaʾturūna* — "and who turn from the way of God": a personal turning, the individual's own face angled away from the path. The second is *wa yurīdūnahā ʿiwajan* — "and who seek to make it crooked": a social distortion, the active attempt to bend the path itself so that others cannot walk it straight. The two verbs are not synonyms. The first describes withdrawal; the second describes sabotage. The Qur'an records the same compound motion against the same category of people at 4:167, 7:45, 9:34, 11:19, 22:25, and 47:1, 32, and 34, and the recurrence shows that the sūrah sees in this pair a standing pattern of how truth loses ground in a society: not by being refuted, but by being abandoned by some and bent by others. The word *ʿiwaj* — crookedness, curvature — is the opposite of *istiwāʾ*, uprightness, and the contrast is geometric rather than merely moral: a crooked path is not a false road on the map, it is a road that starts right and curves, so that the traveler who set out correctly arrives wrong. That is the precise damage the verse describes. The crooking is not the substitution of an obvious false way for the true one; it is the subtle deflection of the true one — the redefinition of God's way as fanaticism, as backwardness, as a private matter with no public claim, as something that can be enjoyed in the spirit while being disregarded in the life. The traveler is never told he is on the wrong road; he is merely walked, step by step, along a curve.

**The Modern Industry of Crookedness**

The pattern is not historical. The modern world contains, in abundance, the machinery of *ʿiwaj*: a media environment that does not deny God but keeps Him at the periphery of public life; ideologies that substitute the nation, the class, or the self for the one who is worshipped; consumer systems that do not forbid worship but saturate the mind until worship becomes background noise. The Qur'an's own diagnosis of this condition, recorded in another sūrah, is worth placing beside verse 3, because it describes the end product of the crooked path — a person who still believes, in the way the Makkans believed, but at the edge:

> *"And among the people is one who worships God on the edge; if a good touches him, he is reassured by it; but if a trial strikes him, he turns on his face. He has lost what is near and what is far. And what they worship besides God — that is indeed the extreme error."* (Qur'an 22:11)

The "worshipper on the edge" is the person the crooked path produces: faith as a hedge, devotion as a habit, God as a contingency. Verse 3 explains how the person gets there. First the preference; then the personal turning; then the social crooking; then the edge.

**Far Astray: A State, Not a Process**

The verse closes with *idhā hum qad ghawū* — "it is they who are far astray" — and the grammar of the closing is as deliberate as its content. The construction is perfective: the straying is not described as happening but as accomplished. These people are not on the way to being astray; they have arrived. The word *ghaww* adds distance: not merely lost, but far lost — the gap between them and the path is now a gap, not a hesitation. The sūrah will complete the image at the end of its first section in verse 18, where the deeds of the disbelievers are described as ash that the storm scatters and the state is named *al-ḍall al-ʿaẓīm*, the extreme error. The echo is exact: the *ghaww* of verse 3 is the *ḍall* of verse 18 seen from a distance — the same error, measured at the beginning and at the end. For the reader, the verse's psychology is its sharpest teaching. The most dangerous kind of being lost is not the stumbling kind. The stumbler feels the ground give way and looks around. The *qad ghawū* — the one far astray — walks with full confidence, corrects others, and never looks. The verse, which began as a definition, ends as a warning to the reader: the test of the path is not the declaration of direction but the distance, and the distance is measured, in the end, by where the feet stop.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:4

> **And We have sent no messenger, save in the language of his people, that he might make clear unto them. Then God leads astray whomsoever He will and guides whomsoever He will. And He is the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messenger as a Human Tongue**

The fourth verse moves from the Book to the messengers, and its first clause is a historical law stated in the strongest form the Arabic allows: *wa-mā arsalnā min rasūlin illā bi-lughāti qawmihī* — "We have sent no messenger except in the language of his people." The law covers every prophethood without exception. Noah spoke to his people in their tongue, Moses in his, Abraham in his, and the Qur'an was revealed, the sūrah will confirm in other places, in clear Arabic (43:3; 12:2), because the one it was sent to were Arabs. The reasoning, as the classical commentators give it, is not aesthetic but epistemic: a message must be understandable to be received, and a messenger must know the idiom of his people — their words, their proverbs, their assumptions — in order to make the message clear (*li-yubayyina lahum*). The purpose clause in the verse is the verb of clarification: *mubayyin*. A messenger in a foreign language is a messenger in a foreign land; he cannot correct a misunderstanding he cannot hear, and he cannot reach a heart whose words he does not know. The law of the verse also answers, in advance, the objection that the Qur'an's case for itself is exceptional: the sūrah that announces "no messenger except in the language of his people" is itself spoken in the language of its addressees, and it says so in the same breath. The coherence is the point. Revelation does not arrive as a decree from an alien court; it arrives as a word in the mother tongue, which is why the earliest listeners could test its eloquence the way they tested a poet's.

**Clarification Is the Messenger's Office**

The phrase *that he might make clear unto them* deserves its own weight, because it defines the entire prophetic office. The messenger is not sent to create new knowledge out of nothing, to impose an alien system, or to dazzle. He is sent to *mubayyana* — clarification: the removal of the veil that lies between the people and what they are capable of knowing. The verb *bayyana* in the Qur'an is the verb of making manifest, of bringing into the light, and it is used throughout the Book of the Qur'an itself, which is repeatedly described as *mubīn*, a clarification. The sūrah's opening image of the passage from darkness into light (v. 1) is thereby completed in verse 4: the Book is the light, and the messenger is the one who holds it in the faces of the people. The office is a service of clarity, and its failure — when it happens — is never a failure of the message's clarity but a failure of the receiving. The verse has already implied this in its structure: the messenger is sent *so that* he makes clear; the making clear is on the sender's side, completed. What follows is on the receiver's side.

**The Hard Clause: God Leads Astray and Guides**

The second half of the verse is the one the reader has been taught to find difficult, and the sūrah does not ease it: *thumma Allāhu yudhillu man yashāʾu wa yahdī man yashāʾu* — "Then God leads astray whomsoever He will and guides whomsoever He will." The word *thumma* — "then" — is the hinge, and the exegetes have always attended to it: the leading astray and the guiding do not precede the human response, they follow it. The sequence of the verse is a defense of responsibility. The messenger is sent; the message is clarified; the people respond by their own choice; and *then* the divine consequence settles — guidance is granted to the one who turned, and the leading astray befalls the one who hardened. The leading astray, in the Qur'an's consistent usage, is not a compulsion laid on a free heart; it is the withdrawal of the light from one who has turned his face from it, the hardening that a repeated refusal produces, the desertification of a heart that was once watered. The same structure appears at 4:88–90 and 4:143, and the commentators' standard resolution is that God's "leading astray" is the answer to a human "turning away," and the two are never presented as independent of each other. The logic can be stated plainly: a person who keeps angling his face away from a light is not compelled by the light to remain in darkness; the light was offered, and the covering was his own. The verse is therefore not a statement of arbitrary divine caprice but a statement of the consequences of choice — the same consequences the sūrah will describe, in full, at the end of the chapter, when the weak address the arrogant and the arrogant can offer no excuse.

**Why This Clause Stands Here**

The placement of the clause is itself a commentary. The sūrah has just defined the disbeliever as the one who prefers the world, who turns, and who crooks the path (v. 3), and it has just named the threat (v. 2). Between the definition and the threat, God states the law that governs the difference between guidance and loss: it is a matter of will, human and divine, meeting. The clause also protects the messengers. A messenger who fails to convert is not a failed messenger; the messenger's office ends at clarification, and the outcome is the domain of the One who guides and leads astray. The sūrah will lean on this protection in verses 11–12, where the persecuted messengers speak of their trust in God precisely because the outcome was never in their hands. For the reader in any age, the clause is a double discipline: against the arrogance of the one who claims to possess the light ("I made them believe"), and against the despair of the one who has been rejected ("nothing I said worked"). Both claims are answered by the same sentence: the word is sent, made clear, and then the outcome belongs to God, the Mighty, the Wise — the two names that close the verse, naming power and wisdom as the two attributes under which the whole of guidance and loss are conducted.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:5

> **We indeed sent Moses with Our signs, "Bring thy people out of darkness into light, and remind them of the Days of God. Truly in that are signs for all who are patient, thankful."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Light of the Book and the Light of the Lawgiver**

The fifth verse brings Moses onto the stage, and the first thing the attentive reader notices is that the sūrah has not introduced a new image but has repeated its opening one. God sent Moses "with Our signs" and commanded him, in the very words of the command: *Bring thy people out of darkness into light* — the same verb (*akhrij*), the same darkness, the same light, that verse 1 attributed to the Book and its bearer. The sūrah is now stating a doctrine of continuity: the light that the Qur'an carries is not a new light kindled in a new age. It is the same light that Moses carried out of Egypt. The Book of verse 1 and the signs of verse 5 are one passage, and the corridor from darkness into light has a history. This is why the sūrah can move so quickly from the abstract opening to a historical narrative without a transition: the narrative is not an illustration of the opening, it is the opening's own precedent. The signs (*āyāt*) with which Moses was sent are, in the Qur'an's consistent usage, not merely wonders but marks of truth — the rod, the white hand, the deliverance of the people, the parting of the sea — each one a visible word in the language of the people, exactly as verse 4 requires of every messenger.

**The Days of God: A Blessing and a Warning in One Word**

The command to Moses contains the phrase that gives the verse its depth: *remind them of the Days of God* (*udhkurhum bi-awwal Allāh*). The word *awwal* is the plural of *yawm*, day, and in the Qur'an the "day" is never merely a unit of time. It is a cosmic period (22:47: "a day with your Lord is like a thousand years of what you count"); it is an event of decisive weight (the Day of Judgment, the Day of the Earthquake, the Day of the Trumpet); and it is used idiomatically for the great, memorable occurrences that define a people's history. The classical commentators gathered both senses into the reading of this verse, and both are needed. In one sense, the Days of God are the blessings: God's deliverance of the Children of Israel from bondage, His salvation of the sons, His parting of the sea, His giving of the Torah — the days that constitute the people's own story of rescue. In the other sense, they are the warnings: the days in which God dealt with the communities of the disbelievers, the punishments of Pharaoh and his people, the fates of the nations that came before — the days that constitute the universe's story of accountability. The reminder is therefore two-edged: remember what God did for you, and remember what He did to those who refused Him. The exhortation to remind (*udhkurhum*) is addressed to Moses, but the reader of the sūrah learns from it the method of all prophetic speech: reminder is not information transfer. It is the reopening of eyes that have grown used to their own history.

**Signs for the Patient and the Thankful**

The verse closes by telling the reader who will actually see the signs: *truly in that are signs for all who are patient, thankful* (*la-ʿallīna ṣābirīna wa shākirīna*). The signs are in the Days of God — in the blessing and in the warning, in the past and in the pattern. But a sign (*āyah*) is not a sight; it is a sight for a particular eye. The sūrah states the condition of the eye in two virtues, and the pairing is the theological core of the verse. Patience (*ṣabr*) is the capacity to bear what is given — trial, hardship, the long waiting. Gratitude (*shukr*) is the capacity to receive what is given — blessing, gift, the unearned mercy. The two are not separate capacities; they are the two directions of the one disciplined heart. A person who is grateful but not patient falls apart under the first serious trial, because the blessing has not taught him to endure. A person who is patient but not grateful falls apart under the first long blessing, because the gift has bred in him either arrogance or entitlement. The two together are what the sūrah will need in the verses ahead: the patience of the persecuted messengers (v. 12), the gratitude of the people of Moses (v. 7), the patience and gratitude that are the standing condition of seeing signs (this verse). The tradition of the Prophet ﷺ states the pairing in the sharpest form:

> *"Faith consists of two halves: one half is patience, and one half is gratitude."* (al-Tirmidhī, from Muʿādh ibn Jabal)

And in the companion report that defines the branches of faith, the greatest branch is the testimony of Oneness and the lowest is removing a harmful object from the road, with modesty counted as a branch of faith (Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah) — a scale that runs, in practice, from the patience of the heart to the gratitude of the hand.

**The Practical Reading: A Test of the Reader**

The verse is, in its closing clause, an address to the reader of the sūrah, not merely to the people of Moses. The Days of God are the reader's own days — the days in which the reader was delivered from a poverty, a disease, a fear, a ruin; and the days in which the reader saw, from a distance, what happened to a people that had every advantage and used it against its own good. The question the verse asks is not whether the reader believes in the Days of God; it is whether the reader's heart is of the kind that sees them. The sūrah's method is consistent: it does not argue the reader into the light; it tells the reader what kind of person the light is for. The patient and the thankful — the one who can bear and the one who can receive — are the two names of the person for whom the passage of verse 1 is real rather than described. The verse is therefore a self-test dressed as a historical command, and it is one of the sūrah's quietest and most effective turns.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:6

> **And [remember] when Moses said unto his people, "Remember God's Blessing upon you, when He saved you from the House of Pharaoh, who inflicted terrible punishment upon you, slaying your sons and sparing your women—and in that was a great trial from your Lord—"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Command: Remember the Blessing**

The sixth verse gives the reader the exact words of Moses's first exhortation to his people, and the first command is not "worship," not "obey," not even "believe" — it is *remember*: *udhkurū niʿmata Allāh ʿalaykum* — "remember God's Blessing upon you." The sequence is a theology of how faith is built. The path from the heart of the exodus to the heart of worship does not begin with a proposition; it begins with a memory. Moses does not ask his people to accept a doctrine; he asks them to reopen an experience. The blessing (*niʿmah*) is the object of the remembering, and the object is specified in the next clause — the salvation from the House of Pharaoh — so that the memory has content, so that it is not a vague gratitude but a gratitude with a history. The Qur'an's method in this sūrah is thereby revealed: it does not build religion from the top down (from creed to practice) but from the bottom up (from the felt experience of rescue to the recognition of the Rescuer). The same method will govern the sūrah's treatment of Abraham in verses 35–41, where the prophet's prayers are grounded not in abstractions but in the concrete valley, the concrete house, the concrete fruit.

**The House of Pharaoh: A System, Not a Man**

The Arabic of the verse specifies the object of the rescue with a word the English translation renders as "the House of Pharaoh" (*āl Firʿawn*). *Āl* is the word for family, lineage, household, and in the Qur'an's usage it regularly extends to the entire system of power that a great figure commands — his court, his administration, his armies, his culture. The "House of Pharaoh" is therefore not a palace; it is a civilization of oppression, the whole apparatus by which a people is held. The verse then describes the apparatus's operation with two details that are precise and terrible: *slaying your sons and sparing your women* — the infanticide of the male children of the enslaved people, the keeping of the women as a labor force. The detail is not decorative; it is the specific horror that a particular people actually suffered, and the Qur'an's insistence on the particular is one of its distinguishing marks. The oppression is not an abstraction; it is a practice with a victim, and the blessing that ended it is a rescue from a named practice. The reader is meant to hold the particular, because the particular is what makes the gratitude real. A gratitude without a particular — a general thankfulness for "things going well" — is not the gratitude the verse is asking for.

**The Great Trial: The Blessing That Is Also a Test**

The verse closes, mid-sentence, with a clause that the reader may not expect: *and in that was a great trial from your Lord* (*wa-fī dhālika balāʾun min Rabbikum ʿaẓīm*). The deliverance from Pharaoh — the greatest blessing in the people's history — is also called a trial (*balāʾ*). The word *balāʾ* in the Qur'an carries the whole field of testing, and the test takes more than one form: it is the trial of hardship, the trial of blessing, and the trial of the blessing that contains a hidden test. The classical commentators note that the great trial here can refer to the deliverance itself, because the deliverance is what it is — a rescue that also sets a question. The question is: what will you do with the freedom? A people rescued from slavery is not thereby rescued from the possibility of becoming its own enslaver; a people delivered from Pharaoh is not thereby delivered from the temptation to return, in heart if not in body, to the old order. The same point is made in the sūrah's own treatment of the Children of Israel elsewhere in the Qur'an, where the people, immediately after the deliverance, demand visible gods and fall into the calf (2:51; 7:141–154; 20:83–88). The "great trial" of verse 6 is therefore the trial of the rescued: the test of whether the blessing produces gratitude and obedience, or whether it produces the assumption that the rescue was their own achievement. The clause is a warning dressed as a description, and it is one of the sūrah's most important quiet teachings: the greatest blessing is also the greatest test, and the two are not in tension — they are the same event.

**The Psychology of Forgetting the Blessing**

The reason the command to remember the blessing stands at the head of the exhortation is that the forgetting is the standing danger. The blessing, once it is received, becomes familiar; the familiar becomes invisible; the invisible becomes, in the heart, absent. The people of the exodus were, in the space of a generation, a people who had forgotten what had been done for them — a people who could stand before a mountain and demand visible gods. The Qur'an's method of counteracting the forgetting is the periodic reopening: the repeated command to *remember* (*udhkurū*) that runs through the sūrah and through the Qur'an. For the modern reader, the verse names a mechanism that is universal and personal. The blessing of a health that is only noticed when it is taken; the blessing of a home that is only noticed when it is threatened; the blessing of a freedom that is only noticed when it is curtailed. The forgetting is not a moral failing in the first instance; it is a feature of the human relation to the familiar. The discipline the verse teaches is the periodic reopening — the deliberate act of returning the blessing to visibility, of naming it, of attributing it to its source. The practice is the one the sūrah will make explicit in verse 7, where the remembering of the blessing is transformed into the gratitude that God promises to increase.

**The Structure of the Sentence: A Blessing With a Source**

A final note on the verse's grammar, which is doing quiet work. The blessing is not said to be "upon you" in a general sense; it is said to be *God's* blessing (*niʿmata Allāh*) upon you, and the source is named before the content is described. The order of the sentence — the blessing, its source, its content — is the order of the gratitude it is building: the gift is recognized, the Giver is named, and the particular is reopened. The verse is therefore not only a historical report; it is a model of the act of gratitude, structured in its own syntax. The reader who follows the order of the verse — gift, Giver, particular — is following the order of the gratitude the sūrah is teaching, and the teaching is not abstract: it is the shape of the sentence itself.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:7

> **and when your Lord proclaimed, 'If you give thanks, I shall surely grant you increase, but if you are ungrateful, truly My Punishment is severe!'"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God's Own Formula of Gratitude**

The seventh verse is not Moses's speech; it is the speech of God, reported within the frame of the story. The form of the divine address is a proclamation (*wa-idh qāla Rabbukum*) — a statement of law, not a request, not a hope. God states the formula in the most direct grammar the language allows: *if you give thanks, I shall surely grant you increase; if you are ungrateful, truly My Punishment is severe* (*idhā shakartumazīzzanna lakum, wa-in kufirtum fa-inna ʿadhābī la-jīm*). The two clauses are the entire sūrah's moral equation in a single breath, and the rest of the chapter — the ash of verse 18, the exchange of verse 28, the uncountable blessings of verse 34 — is the equation worked out in detail. The verse is therefore the sūrah's doctrinal center of gravity, and its placement, immediately after the exhortation to remember the blessing (v. 6), is deliberate: the remembering is the preparation, and the formula is the law that the remembering activates.

**The Threefold Structure of Gratitude**

The word *shukr* (from the root sh-k-r) is one of the Qur'an's most precise, and the classical tradition gave it a definition that the sūrah's usage fully supports: gratitude is the acknowledgment of the blessing with the heart, the praise of the Giver with the tongue, and the right use of the gift with the limbs. The three parts are not a ritual sequence; they are the three organs of a single act. The heart that does not acknowledge the blessing cannot produce the tongue that praises it, and the tongue that praises what the heart does not feel is a sound, not a gratitude. The hand that wastes or misuses the gift while the mouth recites thanks is the same. The sūrah's demand of the grateful is therefore total: it is not a sentiment, it is a state of the whole person, in which the received gift is held in the right relation to its source and put to the right use. The word *kufr* (ingratitude, from the root k-f-r, to cover) is the exact opposite in the same language: the heart that buries the blessing, the tongue that does not name the Giver, the hand that treats the gift as its own. The two words are not opposites in the way that light and dark are opposites in a room; they are opposes in the way that a door that opens and a door that is bricked up are opposites — the same threshold, the two possible states of it.

**The Logic of Increase: Why Gratitude Produces More**

The first clause of the formula — *I shall surely grant you increase* (*lāizzanna lakum*) — is the one the reader most wants to understand, because it is the one that sounds, on the surface, like a transaction. The classical commentators, and the spiritual tradition that grew on them, give the logic, and the logic is not commercial. The increase works on three levels. First, the relation: the one who acknowledges the blessing remains in a living relation with the Giver, and the relation is the channel through which the blessings flow. The one who covers the blessing severs the relation, and the channel closes. The increase is not a payment; it is the continuation of a relation that the gratitude has kept open. Second, the use: the grateful hand puts the gift to its right use, and the right use of a blessing multiplies it — a health used in work produces more than a health used in sloth, a wealth used in its right channels produces more than a wealth hoarded or squandered. The increase is the fruit of the right use. Third, the gratitude itself: the act of thanking is itself a blessing, a state of the heart that is a good in its own right, and the one who thanks receives, in the thanking, a portion of the increase that the one who does not thank does not receive at all. The tradition of the Imams carried the point in a report the exegetes preserved:

> *"Whenever God bestows a blessing upon a servant and he acknowledges it with his heart and praises God for it with his tongue, the blessing does not finish except that God commands that the servant receive an increase."* (Reported from Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, transmitted in the exegetical literature)

The mechanism, in the plainest terms: the blessing is not a fixed sum that is spent; it is a flow, and the gratitude is the condition of the flow's continuing.

**The Second Clause: The Severity of the Covering**

The second clause of the formula — *truly My Punishment is severe* (*fa-inna ʿadhābī la-jīm*) — is the one the reader most wants to soften, and the sūrah does not soften it. The adjective *ajīm* is a word of weight: it is the punishment that is severe in itself, not severe in relation to something else. The verse is stating that the covering of the blessing is not a minor offense that the Giver overlooks; it is the offense that the Giver has named, in His own words, as one that carries a named consequence. The severity is not arbitrary; it is proportional to the gravity of the act. The act is not the withholding of a coin; it is the covering of the light — the same covering that the sūrah has already identified, in verse 3, as the defining act of the disbeliever. The formula of verse 7 is therefore the law behind the woe of verse 2: the woe is severe because the offense is a covering, and the covering is the offense the Giver has named as such. The two clauses of the formula — the increase and the severity — are not two separate policies; they are the two faces of the one law of the relation, and the sūrah's treatment of the Makkans in verses 28–30 is the law applied to a particular people who had, in God's judgment, chosen the second clause.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Pivot**

A structural note: verse 7 is the sūrah's pivot. Before it, the sūrah has described the Book, the warning, the disbeliever, the law of the messengers, and the command to Moses. After it, the sūrah will give the people of Moses's response (v. 8), the history of the rejected messengers (v. 9), the fate of the tyrant (v. 15), the parable of ash (v. 18), the fate of the Makkans (v. 28), the program of the believers (v. 31), the uncountable blessings (v. 34), and the prayers of Abraham (vv. 35–41). Every one of these developments is a working-out of the formula stated in verse 7. The sūrah's architecture is therefore not a sequence of topics; it is a single law, stated once in the sharpest form, and then demonstrated from every angle. The reader who holds verse 7 in mind while reading the rest of the chapter will find that the chapter is not a collection of verses but a single argument, and the argument's first premise is the one God stated in His own words.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:8

> **And Moses said, "If you are ungrateful—you and whosoever is on the earth all together—[know that] verily God is Self-Sufficient, Praised."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exaggeration of the Collective Refusal**

The eighth verse is Moses's reply to the formula of God, and the reply takes the form of a counter-hypothesis of extraordinary scale: *if you are ungrateful — you and whosoever is on the earth all together* (*fa-in kufirtum antum wa-man fī al-arḍi jamīʿan*). Moses does not say "if you are ungrateful"; he says "if you and everyone on the earth, together, are ungrateful." The exaggeration (*mubālagha*) is deliberate, and the exegetes have always attended to it. The point is not that a collective refusal is more likely than an individual one; the point is to remove every possible source of human leverage from the equation. The reader is invited to consider the largest possible refusal — the refusal of the whole earth, every person in it, in a single act of ingratitude — and to observe what happens to the Giver when that refusal is complete. The answer is the next clause, and the answer is that the Giver loses nothing. The verse is a theological demolition of every form of bargaining. A person who bargains with God on the basis of his own importance — "you need me to believe," "my rejection costs you something" — is addressed by this verse directly: even the largest possible rejection costs the Giver nothing, and the bargaining is void.

**God Is Self-Sufficient, Praised**

The reply to the collective refusal is two names: *verily God is Self-Sufficient, Praised* (*inna Allāha kana Ghanīyan Ḥamīdā*). The two names are the sūrah's own pair, and the sūrah will close, at verse 52, on a name that belongs to the same family. *Al-Ghanī* — the Self-Sufficient — is the One who needs nothing, who is not affected by the presence or absence of a single creature, who loses nothing when the whole earth turns its back. The name is stated throughout the Qur'an in the same grammatical form as here, in the perfective, as a settled attribute: God *is* the Self-Sufficient, not "becomes" the Self-Sufficient when a refusal is made. The refusal does not change God; the refusal reveals what God already is. *Al-Ḥamīd* — the Praised — is the companion name, and the pairing is exact: the One who is Self-Sufficient is also the One who is Praised, and the two are not independent. The Self-Sufficiency is what makes the praise real — a praise directed to a being who needs the praise is a praise that is, in the end, a transaction; a praise directed to the One who needs nothing is a praise that is free. The two names together state the two sides of the relation: the Giver is complete without the creature, and the Giver is the object of praise without the praise being a condition of His completeness. The verse is therefore a statement of the tawḥīd of need: the creature is the one in need, and the Giver is the one without need, and the relation between them is not a relation of mutual dependence but a relation of a complete being toward an incomplete one.

**The Rhetorical Shift: From Threat to Majesty**

A reader who compares verse 7 and verse 8 will notice a shift in the register that is one of the sūrah's quiet masterstrokes. Verse 7 is God's formula: it contains a threat (the severe punishment) and a promise (the increase). Verse 8 is Moses's reply, and the reply removes the threat from the Giver's side entirely. The threat in verse 7 is real — the sūrah will honor it in its later verses — but the threat is a statement about the consequence of the refusal for the refuser, not a statement about what the refusal costs the Giver. Moses's reply in verse 8 clarifies the point: the refusal costs the Giver nothing, and the severity of the punishment is not the severity of a being who has been hurt but the severity of a law that answers a covering. The shift in register — from the divine formula, which contains a threat, to the prophetic reply, which removes the threat from the Giver — is the shift that re-centers the whole discussion. The discussion is no longer "what will God do if we refuse?"; the discussion is "what will we do to ourselves if we refuse?" The psychological effect of the shift is the point. A heart that was in a bargaining position — a heart that was, in the end, negotiating — is humbled by the reply, because the reply removes the one thing the bargaining depended on: the assumption that the Giver was in a position to be moved by the creature's refusal. The verse is a discipline of the heart, and the discipline is the tawḥīd of need.

**The Practical Consequence: The Gratitude of the One Who Knows**

The practical consequence of the verse is a change in the character of the gratitude. The gratitude of the one who does not know the verse is a gratitude that is, in part, a transaction: the one who thanks is, in the end, protecting his own interest, because the one who does not thank loses the increase. The gratitude of the one who knows the verse is a gratitude that is free: the one who thanks thanks a being who needs the thanks not, and the thanks is therefore an act of the heart's own orientation, not a payment. The sūrah's demand of the grateful, in light of verse 8, is therefore a higher demand than it appears to be on the surface: the gratitude is not a strategy for securing the increase; the gratitude is the heart's recognition of the Giver's Self-Sufficiency, and the increase is the consequence, not the motive. The verse is one of the sūrah's most important quiet teachings, and its teaching is not a doctrine about God but a doctrine about the character of the human act of gratitude. The gratitude that is a strategy is not the gratitude the sūrah is asking for; the gratitude that is a recognition is.

**The Verse and the Sūrah's Closing Name**

A final structural note: the two names of verse 8 — *al-Ghanī* and *al-Ḥamīd* — are the sūrah's own pair, and the sūrah's closing verse (52) will name God as *al-Wāḥid* — the One — in the context of the proclamation to mankind. The three names — the Self-Sufficient, the Praised, the One — are the three sides of the same tawḥīd, and the sūrah's movement from verse 8 to verse 52 is, in part, a movement through the three names. The sūrah begins its theological statement in verse 8, in the context of the refusal of the people of Moses, and it closes its theological statement in verse 52, in the context of the proclamation to the people of understanding. The two statements are the same statement, made at the beginning and the end, and the middle of the sūrah is the working-out of the statement in the lives of particular people. The architecture of the sūrah is, in this respect, a closed form: the theological center is stated early, and it is returned to at the end, and the reader who holds the two verses in mind while reading the middle will find that the middle is not a sequence but a single sustained demonstration.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:9

> **Has not the account come to you of those who came before you—the people of Noah, and ʿĀd, and Thamūd, and those who came after them? None knows them but God. Their messengers brought them clear proofs, but they thrust their hands into their mouths and said, "Verily we disbelieve in that wherewith you have been sent, and we are in grave doubt about that to which you call us."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A History That Repeats Is a History That Warns**

The ninth verse breaks the frame of the story of Moses and opens the sūrah's second great section: the history of the messengers who were rejected. The opening question — *has not the account come to you?* (*aʿlamat lakum naẓīran*) — is the Qur'an's standard device for summoning the reader's own knowledge, and the word *naẓīr* (parable, counterpart, analogue) names the method. The Qur'an does not present history as a chronicle; it presents it as a pattern. The three peoples named — the people of Noah, the ʿĀd, the Thamūd — are the three most notorious of the destroyed nations, and the sūrah will return to the pattern in its treatment of the Makkans in verse 45, where the Makkans are told, in effect, that they are living in the dwellings of the people of the pattern and that the pattern is still in force. The question of the verse is not "do you know the story?"; it is "do you know the pattern?" and the reader is invited to answer before the verse continues.

**The Three Peoples and the One Pattern**

The three peoples are named, and the sūrah's own cross-references in the rest of the Qur'an give the reader the full pattern. The people of Noah (Sūrah 71; 7:59–64; 11:25–48) — the first community to be destroyed by a flood, after a prophet who lived among them centuries and was rejected by his own people. The ʿĀd (7:65–72; 11:50–60; 41:15–16) — the people of the prophet Hūd, a people of strength and stature who were destroyed by a wind that left no trace. The Thamūd (7:73–79; 11:61–68; 26:141–58; 54:23–31) — the people of the prophet Ṣāliḥ, a people who carved their dwellings into the rock and were destroyed after they had killed the she-camel that was the sign. The pattern is the same in all three: a prophet, a message, a refusal, a punishment. The sūrah will describe the refusal in detail in the next two verses, and it will describe the punishment in the verses that follow. The pattern is not a sequence of isolated events; it is a law, and the law is the one the sūrah stated in verse 7: the refusal of the blessing carries a named consequence.

**None Knows Them but God**

The clause *none knows them but God* (*wa-mā yaʿlamuhum illā Allāh*) is one of the sūrah's quietest and most important, and the exegetes gave it two readings, both of which are needed. In the first reading, the clause is a statement about the exact number of the communities that came after the three named — the "those who came after them" (*wa-man baʿdahum*) — and the number is known to God alone, not to the reader. The second reading takes the clause as an expression of the vastness of the number, the way the Qur'an uses the same construction at 25:38 to indicate the great multitude of the destroyed settlements: *how many a prosperous city We have destroyed, and how many a city you do not know how it became thus*. The two readings are not in tension; they are the two sides of the same point. The history of the refusal is larger than the reader's knowledge, and the reader's ignorance of the full scope of the history is itself a lesson: the pattern is not a closed list of three peoples; it is an open pattern, and the reader is, in the end, standing inside it. The verse's "none knows them but God" is therefore not a limitation on the reader's knowledge; it is an expansion of the reader's sense of the scale of what is at stake.

**Clear Proofs: The Quality of the Evidence**

The verse states what the messengers brought: *clear proofs* (*al-burhān al-mubīn*). The word *burhān* is the word for a proof that is visible, demonstrable, leaving no room for the claim of ignorance; it is not a hint, not a suggestion, not a matter of faith alone. The messengers of the three peoples brought, in the Qur'an's own account, signs that were visible and testable — the flood that came as a warning before it came as a punishment, the she-camel that was a sign that could be seen and touched, the power of the ʿĀd that was itself a sign of what God was capable of doing. The point of the clause is to remove the one possible defense the rejected people might have offered: that the evidence was insufficient. The sūrah states, in advance, that the evidence was sufficient in quality, and the failure that followed was not a failure of the evidence but a failure of the heart. The distinction is one of the sūrah's most important, and it is the distinction that the sūrah will apply, in the end, to the Makkans: the evidence was not insufficient; the hearts were.

**Thrusting the Hands into the Mouths: The Body's Confession**

The verse closes with an image that is one of the Qur'an's most vivid and most underappreciated: *they thrust their hands into their mouths* (*fa-ʿaqqudū aṣābihum ilā afwāhihim*) — they bit their fingers, the classical reading, either in the rage of their intense enmity or in the effort to suppress a laugh that was mocking. The image is not decorative; it is the sūrah's statement of the body's confession. The hand is the organ of the deed, and the mouth is the organ of the word; the two are thrust together in an act that is neither a deed nor a word but a gesture that contains both. The gesture is the body's way of saying what the mouth will not say in a sentence: the rage, the mockery, the refusal. The Qur'an's method in the sūrah is consistent: it does not describe the rejection in the abstract; it describes it in the body. The reader is invited to see the rejection not as a proposition but as a posture, and the posture is the one the sūrah will return to, in its full physical detail, at the end of the chapter, when the guilty are bound in fetters and the Fire covers their faces. The gesture of the hands in verse 9 is the first physical description of the rejection, and the sūrah's final physical description of the fate of the rejecters is the last; the two are the two ends of the same body-language, and the middle of the sūrah is the working-out of the gesture in the history of the three peoples.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:10

> **Their messengers said, "Is there any doubt concerning God, the Originator of the heavens and the earth? He calls you that He might forgive some of your sins and grant you reprieve till a term appointed." They said, "You are but human beings like us. You desire to turn us away from that which our fathers used to worship. So bring us a manifest authority!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Answers the Doubt**

The tenth verse gives the messengers' reply to the rejection of verse 9, and the reply opens with a question that is the sharpest of the sūrah: *is there any doubt concerning God?* (*a-ḍallu fī Allāh*). The question is not a request for information; it is a strike at the foundation. The doubt the rejected people have named — *we are in grave doubt about that to which you call us* — is, the messengers reply, a doubt that is misplaced. The doubt is not about the method of the call; the doubt is about the Caller. Before the question of how the people should live is settled, the question of where being comes from must be settled, and the messengers place the second question at the head of the discussion. The answer to the question of the doubt is the next clause: *the Originator of the heavens and the earth* (*muḥdhir al-samāwāti wa al-arḍ*). The word *muḥdhir* is from the root ḥ-d-r, to bring forth into being, to cause to exist, and it is the word for the One who has brought the heavens and the earth into existence from nothing. The answer is a cosmological fact, not a theological proposition: the One who has done this — who has brought the entire order of the visible world into being — is the One about whom the doubt is being raised, and the doubt is, in the end, a doubt about the fact. The messengers' question is therefore a demand that the rejected people confront the fact before they continue to doubt the call.

**The Call: Forgiveness and Respite**

The next clause states the purpose of the call: *he calls you that he might forgive some of your sins and grant you reprieve till a term appointed* (*yaʿlakum la-yaghfira lahum min dhunūbihim wa-yuʾakhkhirkum ilā ajalin musammā*). The purpose is double, and the two parts are the two mercies of the call. The first is the forgiveness (*al-maghfirah*) — the remission of the sins that have already been committed, the clearing of the account that the past has written. The second is the respite (*al-muʾakhkhar*) — the extension of the future, the granting of time until an appointed term (*ajal musammā*), which the exegetes identify as death, the term that is appointed for every soul. The call is therefore not a demand; it is a double gift: the purification of the past and the extension of the future. The word *some* (*baʿḍ*) in "forgive some of your sins" is a word the exegetes discussed at length, and the discussion is worth the reader's attention. In the first reading, the word *some* is literal: there are sins that God does not forgive, and the two named in the sūrah's own cross-references are the sin of setting up partners beside God (shirk, 4:48) and the violation of the rights of God's servants, whose forgiveness is entirely in the hand of the one who has been wronged. In the second reading, the word *some* is a feature of classical Arabic rhetoric in which the indefinite is used for the definite, and the sense is "all." The two readings are not in tension in the sūrah's context, because the sūrah's own later verses will return to the question of the unforgivable sin in verse 36, where the prayer of Abraham is qualified by the same limit. The point of the clause, in either reading, is that the call is a call to forgiveness, and the forgiveness is real, and the limit of the forgiveness is stated, in the sūrah's own structure, in the verse that follows the prayer.

**"You Are But Human Beings Like Us"**

The rejected people's reply is the oldest of the objections the Qur'an records, and the sūrah's placement of it here is deliberate. *You are but human beings like us* (*basharun amthālunā*) — the objection is not that the messengers are false, not that the message is unclear, not that the proof is insufficient. The objection is that the messengers are human, and the human cannot be the bearer of the divine. The Qur'an records the same objection against every prophet: the people of Lūṭ, at 11:27, say the same words; the people of Makkah, at 17:94, say, in effect, "Shall we abandon our gods for a human like us?"; the same objection appears at 21:3, 23:24 and 33, and 64:6. The recurrence is the sūrah's own argument: the objection is not a new argument raised against a particular messenger; it is a standing objection, raised by every rejected people, and the standing character of the objection is part of its diagnosis. The objection is not a failure of the evidence; it is a failure of the imagination. The rejected people cannot conceive of a human being as the bearer of the divine, and the inability to conceive is itself the evidence of the rejection. The messengers' reply, in the next verse, will address the objection directly, and the reply will be the concession that the sūrah's argument depends on.

**The Demand for a "Manifest Authority"**

The rejected people close with a demand: *so bring us a manifest authority!* (*fa-ʾitūna bi-sultānin mubīnin*). The word *sultan* is the word for a proof that is manifest, a demonstration that leaves no room for the claim of ignorance, and the demand is, on its surface, a reasonable one: the rejected people are asking for evidence. The sūrah's treatment of the demand is therefore not a dismissal; it is a recognition that the demand is the last reasonable demand the rejected people can make, and the recognition is part of the diagnosis. The demand is reasonable, and the messengers' reply in the next verse will address it, and the reply will be the one that the sūrah's argument depends on: the authority is not something the messengers can produce by their own power; the authority is something that comes by the leave of God, and the messengers are the conduit, not the source. The demand is therefore not answered by a new proof; it is answered by a statement about the nature of the proof, and the statement is the one that the sūrah will develop in verses 11–12.

**The Structure of the Exchange: A Pattern in Four Moves**

A structural note on the exchange of verse 10: the exchange is a pattern of four moves, and the pattern is the same in every one of the sūrah's treatments of the rejected messengers. The first move is the rejection (v. 9). The second move is the messengers' question, which strikes at the foundation (v. 10, first clause). The third move is the statement of the purpose of the call, which names the double mercy (v. 10, second clause). The fourth move is the rejected people's reply, which is the standing objection and the demand for a manifest authority (v. 10, third clause). The pattern is not a sequence of isolated events; it is a law, and the law is the one the sūrah stated in verse 7: the refusal of the blessing carries a named consequence, and the refusal is not a failure of the evidence but a failure of the heart. The reader who holds the pattern in mind will find that the sūrah's treatment of the Makkans in verses 28–30 is the pattern applied to a particular people, and the application is the sūrah's final demonstration of the law.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:11

> **Their messengers said unto them, "We are but human beings like yourselves, but God is gracious unto whomsoever He will among His servants. And it is not for us to bring you an authority, save by God's Leave; so in God let the believers trust."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Concession That Is the Answer**

The eleventh verse is the messengers' reply to the objection of verse 10, and the reply takes a form the reader may not expect: the messengers do not contest the objection; they concede it. *We are but human beings like yourselves* (*innā basharun amthālukum*) — the same words the rejected people used as an objection are taken up by the messengers and made into a statement of fact. The move is one of the sūrah's most important rhetorical techniques, and it is a technique the reader of the sūrah is invited to learn. The strongest weapon in the defense of a position is not the refutation of the opponent's strongest point; it is the concession of the point, followed by the demonstration that the point proves nothing. The messengers concede the human, and they then turn the concession: the human is not the objection; the human is the condition of the office. A messenger who is not human is not a messenger to the human; a messenger who is human is a messenger who stands where his people stand, who speaks their language (as verse 4 requires), who bears what they bear, and who can therefore be a guide to them. The concession is therefore not a weakness; it is the strength of the position, and the strength is the one the sūrah's argument depends on.

**God Is Gracious unto Whomsoever He Wills**

The next clause states the reason the human can be the bearer of the divine: *but God is gracious unto whomsoever He will among His servants* (*wa-yammanu Allāhu ʿalā man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi*). The word *yammanu* is from the root m-m-n, to bestow, to confer, to show favor, and it is the word for a gift that is not earned, a favor that is given by the choice of the Giver. The clause is therefore a statement about the nature of the prophetic office: the office is not a merit that the human has achieved; the office is a grace that God has bestowed on the one He wills. The rejected people's objection — that the human cannot be the bearer of the divine — is answered by the statement that the bearer of the divine is not the one who has achieved the bearing but the one to whom the bearing has been given. The grace is the condition, and the grace is God's, and the human is the recipient. The clause is also a statement about the tawḥīd of the sūrah: the one who bestows the grace is the same One who is the Originator of the heavens and the earth (v. 10), and the grace is the same in kind as the creation — a gift that is not earned, a favor that is given. The messengers' reply is therefore not a defense of their own status; it is a statement about the nature of the divine gift, and the statement is the one that the sūrah's argument depends on.

**No Authority Except by God's Leave**

The next clause addresses the demand of verse 10 directly: *and it is not for us to bring you an authority, save by God's Leave* (*wa-mā yakuwminā an najīʾakum bi-sultānin illā bi-idhni Allāh*). The clause is a statement about the nature of the proof, and the statement is the answer to the demand. The demand was for a manifest authority, and the messengers' reply is that the authority is not something they can produce by their own power; the authority comes by the leave of God, and the messengers are the conduit, not the source. The clause is therefore a statement about the tawḥīd of the proof: the proof is not a human achievement; the proof is a divine gift, and the human is the one through whom the gift is delivered. The statement is also a guard against the deification of the messenger, which is the one danger the sūrah's argument must avoid. A messenger who produces the proof by his own power is, in the end, a rival of God; a messenger who delivers the proof by the leave of God is a servant of God, and the distinction is the one that the sūrah's tawḥīd depends on. The clause is therefore not a limitation on the messengers; it is a protection of the tawḥīd, and the protection is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

**So in God Let the Believers Trust**

The verse closes with a command that is addressed not to the rejected people but to the followers of the messengers: *so in God let the believers trust* (*fa-ʿalā Allāhi yaṭmaʾin al-muʾminūn*). The shift in address is one of the sūrah's quietest and most important, and the shift is the turn from the defense of the position to the discipline of the one who holds the position. The rejected people have heard the defense; the believers are now told what the defense requires of them. The requirement is trust (*ṭumaʾnīnah*, from the root ṭ-m-n, the settling of the heart), and the trust is directed to God, not to the messenger, not to the proof, not to the outcome. The trust is the anchor of the heart, and the anchor is the one that the sūrah will develop, in full, in the next verse, where the trust is specified as the trust of the one who has been guided and who bears the torment of the rejected. The command is therefore not a conclusion; it is a beginning, and the beginning is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the defense of the position is complete, and the discipline of the one who holds the position is the next matter. The verse is therefore a turn in the sūrah's structure, and the turn is from the argument to the character, and the character is the one the sūrah will develop in verses 12–15.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:12

> **And why should we not trust in God, when He has guided us in our ways? And we shall surely endure patiently, however you may torment us. And let those who trust, trust in God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Is an Answer**

The twelfth verse is the messengers' continuation of the reply, and it opens with a rhetorical question that is, in effect, a statement: *and why should we not trust in God?* (*wa-man yuʾmin bi-Allāh*). The question form (*man*) is the Arabic form of "what is it that would prevent us?," and the question is not a request for a reason; it is a declaration that no reason exists. The trust is not a doubt that is being examined; the trust is a fact that is being stated, and the statement is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The question is therefore not a weakness in the messengers' position; it is the strength of the position, and the strength is the one that the sūrah's argument depends on. The trust is settled, and the settling is the one that the sūrah will develop in the rest of the verse.

**The Reason: He Has Guided Us in Our Ways**

The next clause states the reason for the trust: *when He has guided us in our ways* (*tawāfinā*). The word *tawāf* is the plural of *ṭarīq* in the sense of the path, the road, the course, and the verb *tawāfā* is the verb of being set on the right course, of having one's steps aligned with the truth. The reason for the trust is therefore not a general statement about God's goodness; the reason is a particular statement about the guidance that has been given. The messengers do not trust in God because God is good in the abstract; they trust in God because God has guided them in their particular ways, in their particular paths, in their particular courses. The reason is therefore a particular, and the particular is the one that makes the trust real. A trust without a particular — a general trust in a general goodness — is not the trust the sūrah is asking for; the trust with a particular — the trust in the One who has guided in the particular way — is. The clause is therefore a statement about the character of the trust, and the character is the one the sūrah's argument requires: the trust is not a sentiment; the trust is a relation to a particular guidance.

**Patience in the Face of the Torment**

The next clause is the one that gives the verse its weight: *and we shall surely endure patiently, however you may torment us* (*fa-naṭhīr in tathībūnā*). The word *tathībūnā* is from the root th-y-b, to torment, to afflict, to cause pain, and it is the word for the torment that the rejected people are actually inflicting on the messengers — the expulsion, the mockery, the pressure, the threat. The messengers' reply to the torment is patience (*ṣabr*), and the patience is stated in the future tense: they *shall* endure patiently. The patience is not a description of a present state; the patience is a declaration of a future commitment. The messengers are not saying "we are patient now"; they are saying "we shall be patient when the torment comes." The declaration is one of the sūrah's most important quiet teachings, and the teaching is the one that the sūrah will apply, in the end, to the believers who are persecuted in Makkah: the patience is not a reaction to the torment; the patience is a commitment that precedes the torment, and the commitment is the one that makes the torment bearable. The verse is therefore a model of the patience, and the model is the one the sūrah's argument requires.

**The Two Words for Trust: Settling and Entrusting**

The verse closes with a command that returns to the trust, and the command uses a word that is different from the word of verse 11: *and let those who trust, trust in God* (*fa-ʿalā Allāhi ya-ṭmaʾin alladhīna tāmanū*). The distinction is one of the sūrah's most important linguistic features, and the exegetes have always attended to it. Verse 11 used the word *yaṭmaʾin* (from the root ṭ-m-n, the settling of the heart) and addressed the command to *the believers* (*al-muʾminūn*). Verse 12 uses the word *yāmanū* (from the root t-w-k-k, the entrusting of one's affairs) and addresses the command to *those who trust* (*alladhīna tāmanū*). The two words are not synonyms; they are the two sides of the one virtue. The *ṭumaʾnīnah* (the settling) is the gift: it is the state of the heart that God grants to the believer, the anchor that is given, the settling that is received. The *tawakkul* (the entrusting) is the discipline: it is the act of the believer who entrusts his affairs to God, the active reliance that is maintained, the entrusting that is practiced. Verse 11 states the gift; verse 12 states the discipline. The two together are the whole of the virtue: the settling that is given, and the entrusting that is maintained. The sūrah's treatment of the trust is therefore not a single statement; it is a two-part statement, and the two parts are the gift and the discipline, and the discipline is the one that the believer is required to practice.

**The Practical Consequence: The Trust That Has Been Tested**

The practical consequence of the verse is a change in the character of the trust, and the change is the one the sūrah's argument requires. The trust of the one who has not been tested is a trust that is, in the end, a hope: the one who trusts is trusting in the expectation that the trust will be confirmed, and the confirmation has not yet come. The trust of the one who has been tested — the one who has borne the torment and who has, in the bearing, confirmed the trust — is a trust that is real, and the reality is the one the sūrah is asking for. The verse is therefore a statement about the character of the trust, and the character is the one that is produced by the test: the trust that has not been tested is a trust that has not been known. The sūrah's demand of the believer, in light of verse 12, is therefore a higher demand than it appears to be on the surface: the trust is not a sentiment that is maintained in the absence of the test; the trust is a commitment that is confirmed in the presence of the test, and the confirmation is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:13

> **But those who disbelieved said to their messengers, "We shall surely expel you from our land, or you shall revert to our creed." So their Lord revealed unto them, "We shall surely destroy the wrongdoers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Ultimatums of Power**

The thirteenth verse records the moment at which the rejection of the pattern (vv. 9–12) becomes an act of power: the rejected people move from the objection to the ultimatum. The ultimatum is stated in the form of a disjunction, and the disjunction is the two options that the powerful offer to the persecuted: *we shall surely expel you from our land, or you shall revert to our creed* (*la-najrimakum min arḍinā illā an taʿūda fī dīninā*). The two options are the two that history has repeated in every age: leave, or conform. The sūrah's treatment of the ultimatum is not a moral outrage; it is a diagnosis, and the diagnosis is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The ultimatum is not a reasonable demand; the ultimatum is the expression of a false premise, and the false premise is the one the sūrah will expose in the next two clauses.

**The False Premise: The Truth Is a Matter of Geography or Conformity**

The two options of the ultimatum rest on two false premises, and the sūrah's diagnosis names both. The first premise, in the first option (expulsion from the land), is that the truth is a matter of geography: the land belongs to the rejecters, and the messenger is a stranger in the land, and the stranger's presence is a violation of the land's order. The premise is false, because the land does not belong to the rejecters; the land belongs to the One who is the Owner of all that is (v. 2), and the messenger's presence in the land is not a violation but a presence that is permitted by the Owner. The second premise, in the second option (reversion to the creed), is that the truth is a matter of conformity: the creed of the land is the creed that the messenger must adopt, and the messenger's refusal to adopt is a refusal to conform to the order. The premise is false, because the creed of the land is not the creed of the truth; the creed of the truth is the one the messenger bears, and the conformity to the creed of the land is the conformity to the false. The ultimatum is therefore not a demand that the messenger can meet; the ultimatum is a demand that rests on two false premises, and the false premises are the ones the sūrah will expose.

**The Word "Revert": A Home That Was Never the Messenger's**

A linguistic note on the second option, which the exegetes attended to carefully: the verb used is *taʿūda* — "you shall revert," from the root ʿ-w-d, to return. The word "revert" implies a return to a prior state, and the rejecters' use of the word is therefore a subtle claim: they are implying that the messengers once belonged to their creed, and that the reversion is a return to an original state. The claim is false, and the exegetes note it: the messengers had never followed the false creed, and the "reversion" is not a return but an adoption. The use of the word "revert" is therefore a rhetorical move by the rejecters: they are trying to frame the messenger's refusal as a betrayal of an original loyalty, and the framing is the one that makes the ultimatum seem reasonable. The sūrah's exposure of the framing is part of its diagnosis: the ultimatum is not a reasonable demand; the ultimatum is a demand that has been framed to seem reasonable, and the framing is the one that the sūrah will expose.

**The Answer: A Death Sentence for the Wrongdoers**

The verse closes with the answer of God, reported in the sūrah's own voice: *so their Lord revealed unto them, "We shall surely destroy the wrongdoers"* (*qāla Rabbuhum la-yuhliqna al-ẓālimīn*). The answer is addressed to the messengers, not to the rejecters, and the address is the point. The messengers have heard the ultimatum; God's answer is not a defense of the messengers against the rejecters but a promise to the messengers about the rejecters. The promise is destruction (*al-ihlāq*), and the destruction is directed at the *wrongdoers* (*al-ẓālimūn*). The word *ẓālim* is from the root ẓ-l-m, to put the thing in the wrong place, to cover, to wrong, and it is the word the sūrah has already used in verse 3 to describe the disbeliever. The rejecters of the ultimatum are therefore not merely wrong in their demand; they are wrongdoers in the sūrah's own terminology, and the wrongdoing is the one that carries the named consequence. The answer is therefore not a threat; the answer is the application of the law that the sūrah stated in verse 7: the refusal of the blessing carries a named consequence, and the consequence is the destruction of the wrongdoers. The sūrah's treatment of the pattern is therefore complete in verse 13: the rejection (v. 9), the defense (v. 10), the trust (vv. 11–12), the ultimatum (v. 13), and the answer (v. 13, final clause). The pattern is a law, and the law is the one the sūrah will apply, in the end, to the Makkans.

**The Structure of the Answer: The Promise and the Inheritance**

A structural note: the answer of God in verse 13 is the first half of a two-part answer, and the second half is in the next verse (v. 14), where the promise of the destruction is followed by the promise of the inheritance. The two parts together are the full answer: the wrongdoers are destroyed, and the messengers and their followers are made to dwell in the land after them. The two parts are the two sides of the one law: the destruction of the false and the establishment of the true. The sūrah's treatment of the pattern is therefore not a single statement; it is a two-part statement, and the two parts are the destruction and the inheritance, and the inheritance is the one that the sūrah will develop in verse 14.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:14

> **And We shall surely make you to dwell in the land after them. This is for those who fear My Station and fear My Threat."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inheritance of the Earth**

The fourteenth verse is the second half of God's answer to the ultimatum of verse 13, and the second half is the promise that the first half prepares: *and We shall surely make you to dwell in the land after them* (*wa-lakum mukhlaḍū fī al-arḍ min baʿdihim*). The word *mukhlaḍū* is from the root kh-l-ḍ, to cause to dwell, to make to succeed, and it is the word for the inheritance that follows the removal of the previous occupant. The promise is therefore not a vague hope; the promise is a specific statement: the land that the rejecters claimed as their own (v. 13, first option) will be the land in which the messengers and their followers will dwell, after the rejecters have been removed. The promise is the direct answer to the false premise of the first option of the ultimatum: the land does not belong to the rejecters; the land belongs to the One who is the Owner of all that is (v. 2), and the Owner has promised the land to the messengers and their followers. The sūrah's treatment of the inheritance is therefore the completion of the diagnosis of the ultimatum: the two false premises of the ultimatum are both exposed, and the exposure is the one the sūrah's argument requires.

**The Qur'an's Standing Teaching on the Inheritance**

The promise of verse 14 is not an isolated statement; it is the sūrah's version of a teaching that runs through the Qur'an. The inheritance of the earth by the believers is stated at 24:55:

> *"And God has promised those who believe among you and do righteous deeds that He will surely make them succeed in the land, as He made those who disbelieved before them succeed."* (Qur'an 24:55)

The same teaching appears in the sūrah's own cross-references, and the recurrence is the sūrah's own argument: the inheritance is not a special promise to the messengers of the three peoples; the inheritance is a standing law, and the law is the one that the sūrah will apply, in the end, to the believers of Makkah. The promise of verse 14 is therefore not a historical report about the three peoples; it is a statement of a law that is in force for every community, and the law is the one the sūrah's argument requires. The reader of the sūrah is invited to hold the promise in mind as a law, not as a past event, and the holding is the one that makes the promise real.

**The Two Fears: The Standing and the Threat**

The verse closes with the condition of the inheritance, and the condition is stated in the form of two fears: *this is for those who fear My Station and fear My Threat* (*dhālika li-man khashiya mawqifi wa-khashiya wadhīd*). The two fears are the two sides of the one awe, and the two are the ones the exegetes discussed at length. The first fear is the fear of *My Station* (*mawqif*), and the word *mawqif* is from the root m-w-q-f, to stand, and it is the word for the standing of the human being before God. The exegetes gave the word two senses, both of which are needed: the standing of the human being on the Day of Judgment, the final standing in which the account is settled; and the standing of the human being in God's awareness, the continuous standing in which every act is seen, whether in secret or in public. The two senses are not in tension; they are the two sides of the one reality: the one who stands before God on the Day of Judgment is the one who has been standing in God's awareness all along, and the final standing is the settlement of the continuous standing. The second fear is the fear of *My Threat* (*wadhīd*), and the word is the word for the warning, the threat, the statement of the consequence. The fear of the threat is the fear of the named consequence, the consequence that the sūrah has already stated in verse 7 (the severe punishment) and in verse 13 (the destruction of the wrongdoers). The two fears together are the whole of the awe: the awe of the standing (the awareness of being seen) and the awe of the threat (the awareness of the consequence). The condition of the inheritance is therefore not a single fear; the condition is the two fears, and the two fears are the two sides of the one awe.

**The Logic: The Inheritance Is Earned by the Awe**

The logic of the condition is one of the sūrah's most important quiet teachings. The inheritance is not earned by the merit of the inheritor; the inheritance is promised by the Owner. But the inheritance is conditioned on the awe, and the awe is the one thing that the inheritor can bring. The distinction is the one that the sūrah's tawḥīd requires: the gift is God's, and the condition is the human's, and the two are not in tension. The awe is not a merit that the human brings to the transaction; the awe is the orientation of the heart that makes the human a proper recipient of the gift. A person who has no awe of the standing is not a proper recipient of the inheritance, because the person who has no awareness of being seen is not a person who can be trusted with the land. The condition is therefore not a demand that the human meet in order to earn the gift; the condition is the orientation that the gift requires. The sūrah's treatment of the inheritance is therefore a statement about the character of the recipient, and the character is the one the sūrah's argument requires: the recipient of the inheritance is the one who fears the standing and the threat, and the fear is the one that makes the recipient a proper recipient.

**The Verse and the Sūrah's Treatment of the Makkans**

A structural note: the condition of verse 14 is the condition that the sūrah will apply, in the end, to the Makkans, and the application is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The Makkans are the people who have the land (v. 13, first option), and the Makkans are the people who are being invited to the inheritance (v. 14). The invitation is real, and the condition is real, and the sūrah's treatment of the Makkans in verses 28–30 is the statement that the Makkans have, in God's judgment, not met the condition. The verse is therefore not a historical report about the three peoples; it is a statement of a law that is in force for the Makkans, and the law is the one the sūrah will apply in the verses that follow.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:15

> **And they sought victory, and every stubborn tyrant fails.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**They Sought a Way: The Metaphor of Road-Cutting**

The fifteenth verse is one of the shortest in the sūrah, and the sūrah gives it the weight of a law. The first clause — *and they sought victory* (*wa-ḍarabū ṭarīqan*) — is, in the Arabic, a metaphor that the English translation flattens: the literal sense is "they struck a road," from the verb *ḍarabū* (they struck) and the noun *ṭarīq* (a road, a way). The image is the one of road-cutting: the act of opening a path through difficult terrain, the act of taking measures, of planning, of devising a course of action. The word *ṭarīq* in this construction is the word for the stratagem, the scheme, the course of action that is deliberately chosen. The first clause of the verse is therefore a description of the messengers' (or, in the reading the exegetes give, the believers') seeking of a way against the rejecters — the seeking of divine help, the cutting of the road of the appeal to God. The sūrah's own cross-reference in the story of Shuʿayb, at 7:89, gives the form of the appeal:

> *"Our Lord, decide between us and our people in truth; and You are the best of deciders."* (Qur'an 7:89)

The "seeking of victory" in verse 15 is the act of the one who has exhausted the human means and who turns to the divine, and the act is the one the sūrah will honor in the second clause.

**Every Stubborn Tyrant Fails**

The second clause is the law that the sūrah states in its sharpest and most compressed form: *and every stubborn tyrant fails* (*wa-kullu ʿaẓūn ʿaẓūn khāibūn*). The two adjectives are the two names of the tyrant, and the two are the ones the sūrah's argument depends on. *Al-ʿaẓū* is from the root ʿ-ẓ-y, to persist in arrogance, to be haughty, and it is the word for the one who has chosen the haughtiness as a settled character, not as a passing mood. The tyrant is not the one who is arrogant in a moment of anger; the tyrant is the one whose character is the haughtiness, the one who has made the haughtiness his way of standing. *Khāib* is from the root kh-w-b, to return empty-handed, to be disappointed, and it is the word for the one whose effort has produced nothing, the one who has sought and found nothing, the one whose road has led to nothing. The second clause of the verse is therefore a law: the one whose character is the haughtiness, and who has sought a way against the truth, will return empty-handed. The law is stated in the universal form (*kullu* — every), and the universal is the point: the law is not about a particular tyrant; the law is about the character, and the character is the one that produces the failure.

**The Logic: The Borrowed Height and Its Date**

The logic of the law is one of the sūrah's most important quiet teachings, and the logic is the one the reader is invited to see. The haughty tyrant's power is not his own; the tyrant's power is borrowed. The power is borrowed from the system, from the fear of the people, from the moment, and the borrowing has a date. The date is the one that the tyrant does not see, because the tyrant's character is the haughtiness, and the haughtiness is the inability to see the borrowed nature of the height. The tyrant mistakes the height for his own height, and the mistake is the one that produces the failure: the one who believes he is the height is the one who is most exposed when the height is removed, because the one who believes he is the height has not prepared for the removal. The failure of the tyrant is therefore not an accident; the failure is the consequence of the character, and the character is the one that the sūrah will describe, in full, in the next two verses, where the fate of the tyrant is given in the detail of the drinking and the swallowing and the death that does not come.

**The Verse as the Law of the Sūrah's First Section**

A structural note: verse 15 is the law that closes the sūrah's first section (vv. 9–15), the section that gives the history of the rejected messengers. The section opens with the pattern (v. 9), gives the exchange (v. 10), the defense and the trust (vv. 11–12), the ultimatum and the answer (vv. 13–14), and closes with the law (v. 15). The law is the summary of the section: the pattern of the rejected messengers is the pattern of the haughty tyrant who fails. The sūrah's treatment of the pattern is therefore complete in verse 15, and the sūrah's next section (vv. 16–22) will give the fate of the tyrant in detail, and the detail is the working-out of the law stated in verse 15. The reader who holds verse 15 in mind while reading the next section will find that the next section is not a new topic; the next section is the law of verse 15 worked out in the body of the tyrant.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:16

> **Beyond him lies Hell; and he shall be given to drink of oozing pus,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Subject of the Verse: The Tyrant of Verse 15**

The sixteenth verse opens the sūrah's description of the fate of the tyrant, and the subject of the verse is the one the sūrah has just named in verse 15: *every stubborn tyrant* (*kullu ʿaẓūn ʿaẓūn*). The sūrah does not repeat the name; the sūrah simply moves to the description, and the move is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The law of verse 15 is the statement that the tyrant fails; verse 16 is the statement of what the failure looks like. The description is in the singular — *beyond him lies Hell* (*min baraihi Jahannam*) — and the singular is the point: the law of verse 15 is a universal, but the description is a particular, and the particular is the one that makes the universal real. The reader is invited to hold the particular in mind as the image of the universal: the one who is the haughty tyrant is the one for whom the description of verse 16 is written.

**Beyond Him: The Fire That Is Also Before Him**

The first clause of the verse states the position of the Fire in relation to the tyrant: *beyond him lies Hell*. The preposition *min baraihi* — "beyond him" — is a spatial word, and the spatial word is doing real work in the sūrah's description. The Fire is beyond the tyrant in the sense that it is the destination that follows this life: after the death, the Fire. But the exegetes give the word a second reading, and the second reading is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the Fire is also *before* the tyrant, in the sense that the tyrant is, in this life, standing on the edge of the Fire, and the Fire is in front of him, and the tyrant is about to fall. The two readings are not in tension; they are the two sides of the one reality: the Fire is the destination that follows, and the Fire is the edge that is present. The tyrant's "victory" in this life — the height that he mistakes for his own height (v. 15) — is a single step away from the edge, and the step is the one that the sūrah will describe in the verses that follow. The description of verse 16 is therefore not only a description of the afterlife; the description is a description of the present position of the tyrant, and the present position is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the tyrant is standing on the edge, and the edge is the Fire.

**The Drink of Oozing Pus: The Image of the Torment**

The second clause of the verse gives the first detail of the torment: *and he shall be given to drink of oozing pus* (*yuṣabbu ḥamīman*). The word *ḥamīm* is the word for the scalding liquid, and the exegetes describe it as a mixture of blood and pus, a liquid that oozes from the bodies of the people of the Fire. The image is not decorative; the image is the sūrah's statement of the character of the torment. The torment is not a vague suffering; the torment is a specific experience, and the experience is the one that the sūrah will describe in the next verse, where the drinking is followed by the gulping and the inability to swallow. The image of the pus is the image of the corruption: the liquid that is the torment is the liquid of the corruption, and the corruption is the one that the tyrant's character has produced. The sūrah's treatment of the torment is therefore not a description of a penalty; the description is a statement about the character of the torment, and the character is the one that corresponds to the character of the tyrant: the corruption of the liquid corresponds to the corruption of the character, and the correspondence is the one that the sūrah's justice requires.

**The Verse as the Opening of the Description**

A structural note: verse 16 is the opening of the sūrah's description of the fate of the tyrant, and the description will continue in verse 17, where the drinking is completed and the death that does not come is stated. The two verses together are the full description of the torment, and the description is the working-out of the law stated in verse 15. The sūrah's treatment of the tyrant is therefore a two-part description: the law (v. 15) and the detail (vv. 16–17), and the detail is the one that makes the law real. The reader who holds the two parts in mind will find that the sūrah's description of the tyrant is not a sequence of images; the sūrah's description is a single statement, made in two parts, and the two parts are the law and the detail.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:17

> **which he will gulp down, but can scarcely swallow. Death shall come upon him from every side; yet he will not die, and before him lies a grave punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gulped But Not Swallowed: The Thirst That Will Not Quench**

The seventeenth verse completes the description of the drinking that verse 16 opened, and the completion is the one that gives the description its full weight. The first clause — *which he will gulp down, but can scarcely swallow* (*yatasakkahu fa-lā yasṭīʿu lahu shaʿbā*) — describes the act of the drinking in two parts, and the two parts are the torment. The tyrant gulps the liquid: the act of the gulping is the act of the desire, the act of the one who drinks because the thirst is there. But the tyrant cannot swallow: the act of the swallowing is prevented, and the prevention is the torment. The two together are the image of the thirst that will not quench: the liquid is in the mouth, and the liquid will not pass. The thirst is not satisfied; the thirst is confirmed. The image is one of the sūrah's most vivid, and the image is the one that the sūrah will return to, in its own form, in other places in the Qur'an, where the people of the Fire are described as drinking a scalding water that cuts the intestines (56:44). The sūrah's treatment of the torment is therefore not a description of a single act; the sūrah's treatment is a description of the structure of the torment, and the structure is the one that the sūrah's justice requires: the torment is not a penalty that is imposed from outside; the torment is the consequence of the character, and the character is the one that produces the structure.

**Death From Every Side, Yet No Death**

The second clause of the verse is the one that gives the verse its paradox, and the paradox is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: *death shall come upon him from every side; yet he will not die* (*min kulli maqām yamūt wa-huwa lā yamūt*). The clause describes a state in which every part of the body is affected by the pains that bring about death, and the body does not die. The paradox is not a contradiction; the paradox is the sūrah's statement of the character of the torment. The torment is not the torment of a single part; the torment is the torment of every part, and the torment of every part is the torment of death without the release of death. The verse is therefore a statement about the structure of the torment, and the structure is the one that the sūrah's justice requires: the torment is the full torment, the torment of every part, and the torment is without the release that death would give.

**The Mercy of Death, and Its Withholding**

The clause is also a statement about the mercy of death, and the statement is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. Death, in the Qur'an's consistent teaching, is a mercy: it is the release from the trial of this life, it is the return to the Giver, it is the end of the bearing. The one who dies is the one who is released from the bearing, and the release is the mercy. The sūrah's description of the tyrant in verse 17 is therefore a description of the one for whom the mercy of death is withheld: the tyrant is the one who bears the torment of death without the release of death, and the withholding is the one that the sūrah's justice requires. The description is not a description of cruelty; the description is a description of the consequence of the character, and the character is the one that has chosen, in this life, the torment without the release. The sūrah's treatment of the torment is therefore a statement about the character of the choice, and the choice is the one the sūrah's argument requires.

**Before Him, a Grave Punishment**

The verse closes with a clause that the reader may not expect: *and before him lies a grave punishment* (*wa-mā qaddama lahu ʿadhābun alīm*). The clause states that the torment described in the verse is not the full torment; the torment described is the first torment, and before the tyrant is a greater torment. The clause is the sūrah's statement of the layering of the torment, and the layering is the one that the sūrah's justice requires. The torment is not a single experience; the torment is a sequence of experiences, and the sequence is the one that the sūrah will describe, in its full form, in the verses that follow, where the garments of pitch and the Fire that covers the faces are given in detail. The clause is therefore not a conclusion; the clause is a beginning, and the beginning is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the torment described in verse 17 is the first torment, and the greater torment is the one that follows.

**The Psychology of the Layered Torment**

A final note on the psychology of the description: the layering of the torment is not only a statement about the structure of the afterlife; the layering is a statement about the psychology of the torment. The one who is in a torment and who knows that a greater torment is to come is in a torment that is greater than the torment he is in: the fear of the next is an addition to the present. The sūrah's description of the tyrant is therefore a description of the full weight of the torment, and the full weight is the one that includes the present and the fear of the next. The description is the one that the sūrah's justice requires, and the justice is the one that the sūrah's argument depends on.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:18

> **The parable of those who disbelieve in their Lord: their deeds are as ashes that the wind blows hard on a stormy day. They have no power over aught they have earned. That is extreme error.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ashes: The Deed That Was Once Fire**

The eighteenth verse gives the sūrah's second great parable, and the parable is one of the most devastating images in the Qur'an. The subject of the parable is the deed of the disbeliever: *their deeds are as ashes that the wind blows hard on a stormy day* (*aʿmāluhum ka-ramādin ḥattāhāhā rīhun ʿasīf*). The image is precise in its details, and the precision is the point. Ashes are not nothing; ashes are the residue of a fire that was real. The deed of the disbeliever was once fire: it had warmth, it had effort, it had social recognition, it had, in the world's own terms, success. The wind (*rīhun ʿasīf*) — a violent, stormy wind — is the Day of Judgment, and the wind does not distinguish between the fire and the ash; the wind scatters the residue, and the residue is gone. The parable is therefore not a description of a deed that was never real; the parable is a description of a deed that was real and that is now nothing, and the nothing is the consequence of the wind. The sūrah's own cross-reference at 2:17 gives the same image in the same context, and the recurrence is the sūrah's own argument: the image is not an isolated metaphor; the image is a standing description of the deed of the disbeliever.

**Which Deeds Become Ashes?**

The question the reader asks is which deeds the parable describes, and the exegetes gave three readings, all of which are needed. In the first reading, the deeds are the good deeds of the disbeliever — the charity given, the hungry fed, the kindness shown — and the deeds are nullified by the disbelief, the way the fire is nullified by the wind. In the second reading, the deeds are the evil deeds — the worship of the idols, the injustice, the corruption — and the deeds are nullified in the sense that they produce no weight in the account, because the account is the account of the relation to the Giver, and the relation is the one that the disbelief has severed. In the third reading, the deeds are both — the good and the evil — and the nullification is total. The three readings are not in tension in the sūrah's context, because the sūrah's own later verses will return to the question of the nullification in the treatment of the Makkans, and the treatment will be the one that includes all three. The point of the parable, in any reading, is the same: the deed of the disbeliever is not a deed that stands; the deed of the disbeliever is a deed that is scattered, and the scattering is the work of the wind.

**No Power Over What They Have Earned**

The next clause states the consequence of the scattering: *they have no power over aught they have earned* (*mā kasabū fī shayʾin min quwwah*). The clause is a statement about the Day of Judgment, and the statement is the one that the sūrah's justice requires. On the Day of Judgment, the disbeliever will not receive even a trace of heavenly reward for the deeds that were, in the world's own terms, real. The clause is another way of saying the parable: the ash has no power, the ash is nothing, the ash cannot be gathered back. The sūrah's treatment of the nullification is therefore a statement about the character of the account, and the character is the one that the sūrah's justice requires: the account is the account of the relation to the Giver, and the relation is the one that the disbelief has severed. The clause is also a statement about the futility of the accumulation: the one who has spent a lifetime accumulating what the wind will scatter is the one who has spent a lifetime in the extreme error, and the error is the one the next clause names.

**That Is Extreme Error: The Two Senses of the Word**

The verse closes with the name of the state: *that is extreme error* (*dhālika huwa al-ḍall al-ʿaẓīm*). The word *ḍall* is the word for the error, the being astray, and the sūrah has already used the related word *ghaww* in verse 3 to describe the state of the one who prefers the world. The recurrence is the sūrah's own argument: the *ghaww* of verse 3 and the *ḍall* of verse 18 are the same state, seen from two distances. The adjective *ʿaẓīm* — extreme, great — adds the distance: the error is not a small error; the error is the extreme error, the error that is the farthest from the path. The exegetes give the word a second sense, and the second sense is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the extreme error is also the extreme grief, the grief that the disbeliever will experience at the moment of the realization, the moment in which the deed that was once "real" is seen to be dust. The two senses are not in tension; they are the two sides of the one state: the error in the world, and the grief in the reckoning. The verse is therefore a description of the full weight of the error, and the full weight is the one that includes the scattering and the grief.

**The Counter-Image: The Tree That Stands**

A structural note: the sūrah will give, in verses 24–26, the counter-image of the parable: the good tree, whose roots are firm and whose branches are in the sky, and the bad tree, which is uprooted and has no stability. The two images together are the sūrah's horticultural theology, and the two are the two sides of the one law: the deed that stands and the deed that is scattered. The parable of verse 18 is the first of the two, and the parable of verses 24–26 is the second, and the two together are the full description of the character of the deed. The reader who holds the two images in mind will find that the sūrah's treatment of the deed is not a single image; the sūrah's treatment is a pair of images, and the pair is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:19

> **Hast thou not considered that God created the heavens and the earth in truth? If He wills, He can remove you and bring a new creation,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Consider: The Sūrah's Method of Meditation**

The nineteenth verse opens with the imperative of contemplation: *hast thou not considered?* (*afarāʾayta*). The verb *farāʾayta* is the Qur'an's standard device for summoning the reader's own act of seeing, and the sūrah uses the same command in verse 24 (the parable of the tree) and in verse 28 (the Makkans who exchanged the blessing for ingratitude). The three uses of the command are the sūrah's own statement of its method: the sūrah does not argue the reader into the truth; the sūrah commands the reader to look, and the looking is the act that produces the truth. The command of verse 19 is directed at a particular object: the creation of the heavens and the earth. The object is not a detail; the object is the whole of the visible order, and the whole is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

**Created in Truth: The Purpose of the Creation**

The next clause states the character of the creation: *God created the heavens and the earth in truth* (*khaḍā al-samāwāti wa al-arḍa bi-al-ḥaqq*). The word *al-ḥaqq* is the word for the truth, the reality, the right, and it is from the root ḥ-q-q, to fix, to nail, to be true. The creation is "in truth" in the sense that it is not created in vain, not created as a play, not created without a purpose. The clause is the sūrah's answer to the question that the reader may ask: why does the visible order exist? The answer is that the visible order exists in truth, and the truth is the purpose. The sūrah's own cross-reference at 23:115 gives the same statement in the form of a question:

> *"Then do those who disbelieve think that they will remain in Our world and not return to Us?"* (Qur'an 23:115)

The question of verse 23:115 is the question that the clause of verse 19 answers: the creation is not a world in which the human being remains without a return; the creation is a world that is in truth, and the truth is the return. The sūrah's treatment of the creation is therefore a statement about the purpose of the creation, and the purpose is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

**The Power to Replace the Whole Creation**

The second clause of the verse states the power that follows from the creation: *if He wills, He can remove you and bring a new creation* (*in shāʾa aẓhikhum wa-yāti bi-khalqin jadīd*). The clause is one of the most important in the sūrah, and the importance is the one that the reader is invited to see. The power stated is not the power to destroy a single person; the power stated is the power to replace the whole of humanity, to remove the entire species and to bring a new species in its place. The clause is therefore a statement about the contingency of the whole, and the contingency is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The reader is invited to consider the implication: the human being, the species, the entire order of the human presence on the earth, is not a permanent feature of the universe; the human presence is a feature that can be replaced, and the replacement is as easy for the Creator as the first creation. The clause is a statement about the humility that follows from the awareness of the contingency, and the humility is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

**The Logic: The Warning Against the Arrogance of Permanence**

The logic of the clause is one of the sūrah's most important quiet teachings, and the logic is the one that the sūrah will apply, in the end, to the Makkans. The Makkans are the people who think of themselves as the main characters of the story: the people of the city, the people of the sanctuary, the people who have the land. The clause of verse 19 is the answer to that arrogance: the people who think of themselves as the main characters are the people who can be replaced, and the replacement is as easy for the Creator as the first creation. The clause is therefore a statement about the arrogance of permanence, and the arrogance is the one that the sūrah will expose in the treatment of the Makkans in verses 28–30. The reader who holds the clause in mind will find that the clause is not a cosmological statement; the clause is a statement about the character of the human being who believes he is permanent, and the character is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

**The Verse as the Preparation for the Formula of Ease**

A structural note: verse 19 is the preparation for the formula of ease that will be stated in the next verse (v. 20), where the statement that the replacement is "no great matter for God" is given. The two verses together are the full statement: the power to replace (v. 19) and the ease of the replacement (v. 20). The sūrah's treatment of the power is therefore a two-part statement, and the two parts are the power and the ease, and the ease is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The reader who holds the two parts in mind will find that the sūrah's treatment of the power is not a single statement; the sūrah's treatment is a pair of statements, and the pair is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:20

> **and that is no great matter for God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Formula of Ease**

The twentieth verse is one of the shortest in the sūrah, and the sūrah gives it the weight of a formula: *and that is no great matter for God* (*fa-dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīr*). The formula — *ʿalā Allāhi yasīr*, "easy for God" — is one of the most repeated in the Qur'an, and the repetition is the sūrah's own argument: the formula is not an isolated statement; the formula is a standing description of the character of the divine power. The word *yasīr* is from the root y-s-r, the root of ease, of the opening, of the lightness, and it is the word for the thing that does not produce effort, the thing that is not heavy.

**The Ease Is God's Nature, Not God's Limitation**

A theological note on the formula, which the exegetes attended to carefully: the ease stated in the formula is not a statement about a limitation of God; the ease is a statement about the nature of God. The formula does not say "God finds this easy in the sense that He has effort and the effort is light"; the formula says "the act is easy for God in the sense that the act is not an effort for God, because the act is the expression of the nature." The distinction is the one that the sūrah's tawḥīd requires: the ease is not a quality that is added to God; the ease is the statement of what God is, and what God is is the One for whom the act is not heavy.

**Recreation Is as Easy as the First Creation**

The sūrah's own cross-references give the teaching in its sharpest form. At 36:79–80:

> *"He who created — can He not recreate? Yes, [He can]; for He is the Knower of all things."* (Qur'an 36:79–80)

And at 50:15:

> *"Is the creation of the first [creation] not a greater [power] than the recreation? But you do not understand."* (Qur'an 50:15)

The two cross-references are the sūrah's own argument: the first creation is the greater power, and the recreation is the lesser, and the lesser is easy.

**The Verse as the Closing of the Meditation**

A structural note: verse 20 is the closing of the meditation that verse 19 opened. The two verses together are the full meditation: the consideration of the creation (v. 19) and the formula of the ease (v. 20). The meditation of verses 19–20 is the sūrah's quiet center, and the center is the one that the sūrah's argument depends on: the creation is in truth, the replacement is possible, and the replacement is easy, and the three together are the statement that the sūrah's tawḥīd requires.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:21

> **They will appear before God all together, and the weak will say to those who waxed arrogant, "Truly we were your followers; so can you avail us in any way against the Punishment of God?" They will say, "Had God guided us, we would have guided you. It is the same for us whether we are anxious or patient; for us there is no refuge."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Assembly: All Together, Without Rank**

The twenty-first verse moves the sūrah from the meditation of the creation to the Day of Judgment, and the first image is the assembly: *they will appear before God all together* (*yakhrūjūna ilā Rabbihim jamāʿan*). The word *jamāʿan* — all together — is the point. The assembly is not a procession in which the ranks of this life are preserved; the assembly is a gathering in which the ranks are removed. The image is the sūrah's version of the standing teaching of the Qur'an: the Day is the Day in which the social order of this life is dissolved, and the dissolution is the one that makes the true order visible.

**The Weak to the Arrogant: The Reversal of the Hierarchy**

The next image is the one that gives the verse its weight: *the weak will say to those who waxed arrogant* (*qāla al-ḍaʿafū li-lladhīna istakbarū*). The reversal is the point. In this life, the hierarchy is the hierarchy of power: the arrogant are the leaders, the weak are the followers, the one who was obeyed is the one who gives the orders. The word *ḍaʿaf* is the word for the weakness, the smallness, the being of no account, and the word *istakbarū* is the word for the arrogance, the exalting of the self, the being above. The two words together are the two sides of the one relation: the weak are the ones who were of no account in this life, and the arrogant are the ones who were above, and the two are now in the same assembly, and the assembly is the one in which the weak speak first.

**The Question of the Followers: Can You Avail Us?**

The weak ask the arrogant a question, and the question is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: *truly we were your followers; so can you avail us in any way against the Punishment of God?* The question is a request for help, and the request is the one that the structure of the relation makes inevitable. The weak followed the arrogant in this life; the weak did the deeds that the arrogant directed; the weak are now in the punishment, and the punishment is the consequence of the deeds. The weak's question is therefore not a capricious request; the weak's question is the logical consequence of the relation: the one who followed is now asking the one who led to help. The question is the sūrah's statement of the social structure of the misguidance: the misguidance is not an individual act; the misguidance is a relation, and the relation is the one that produces the question.

**The Leader's Answer: The Chain of Responsibility**

The arrogant answer the weak, and the answer is the one that gives the verse its full weight: *had God guided us, we would have guided you* (*law hadānā Allāhu la-hadaynākum*). The leader says: I could not guide you, because I was not guided. The logic of the chain is the point: the guide must first be guided, the shepherd must first be saved, the one who leads must first be on the path. The leader's confession is therefore not an excuse; the leader's confession is a statement of the structure of the guidance: the guidance flows from God to the one who is guided, and from the one who is guided to the one who follows, and the flow is broken at the first link if the first link is not guided.

**Anxiety and Patience: The Reversal of the Virtue**

The answer continues with a clause that the reader may not expect: *it is the same for us whether we are anxious or patient* (*fa-mā khayrū nā ṭhalū ṣabrun*). The clause states that the two states that are, in this life, the two sides of the one virtue — the anxiety of the one who fears and the patience of the one who bears — are now the same, because the time of choice is over. The reversal is the point: the patience that was a virtue in this life is now meaningless, because the patience is the patience of the one who is waiting for the turn of the event, and the turn has come. The anxiety that was a state in this life is now meaningless, because the anxiety is the anxiety of the one who is expecting the punishment, and the punishment is here.

**For Us There Is No Refuge**

The answer closes with the statement that the sūrah's justice requires: *for us there is no refuge* (*lā manʿā*). The statement is the final word of the leader, and the final word is the one that the sūrah's argument depends on: the one who was the leader in this life is the one who has no refuge on the Day, and the no-refuge is the one that follows from the no-guidance. The verse is the sūrah's version of the teaching that is stated, in its full form, at 40:47–48, where the weak and the arrogant are described in the same scene, and the arrogant's answer is the same: *truly we are all in it; God has indeed judged between His servants*.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:22

> **And Satan will say, when the matter has been decreed, "Verily God made you the Promise of truth; and I made you a promise, but I failed you. And I had no authority over you, save that I called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me, but blame yourselves. I cannot respond to your cries for help; nor can you respond to my cries for help. Truly I disbelieved in your ascribing me as partner aforetime. As for the wrongdoers, surely theirs shall be a painful punishment."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Confession at the End: The Timing of the Speech**

The twenty-second verse gives the sūrah's longest single speech, and the speech is the confession of Satan, reported at the moment when *the matter has been decreed* (*faqada al-amr*) — the moment after the people have taken their places, the Heaven and the Hell assigned, the account settled. The timing is the point: the confession is not made during the life, when the whispering was in force; the confession is made after the life, when the whispering has no force and the truth is manifest.

**God's True Promise and the Betrayed Promise**

The first part of the confession states the contrast that is the whole of the speech: *verily God made you the Promise of truth; and I made you a promise, but I failed you* (*wa-ʿada Allāh al-ḥaqqa wa-ʿadatikum fa-khaḍtum*). The two promises are the two sides of the one relation. God's promise is the promise of the truth: the Resurrection, the accounting, the reward and the punishment — the promise that is real, the promise that is fulfilled. Satan's promise is the promise that failed: the promise that there is no Resurrection, that there is no accounting, that the idols will intercede, that the world is all there is. The word *khaḍtum* — I betrayed you, I deceived you — is the word for the act of the one who has given a promise and has not kept it, and the act is the one that the sūrah's justice requires. The contrast is therefore not a contrast of degree; the contrast is a contrast of kind: the one promise is the promise of the truth, and the other is the promise of the delusion. The sūrah's own cross-reference at 4:120 states the same teaching in the sūrah's own words:

> *"...and Satan promises nothing but delusion."* (Qur'an 4:120)

**No Authority But the Call: The Theology of Free Will**

The second part of the confession is the one that gives the verse its doctrinal weight: *and I had no authority over you, save that I called you, and you responded to me* (*wa-mā kāna lī ʿalaykum min sultān illā an daʿatukum fa-attabaʿtum*). Satan's "authority" over the one who is seduced is not a compulsion; the authority is a call, and the call is answered by the one who responds. The seduction is not a forcing; the seduction is a whispering, and the whispering is answered by the one who chooses to hear. The tradition of the Prophet ﷺ states the same teaching in the sharpest form:

> *"Satan has despaired of being worshipped in the land of Islam, but he will be content with seducing your people and making disputes among you."* (al-Tirmidhī, from Abū Hurayrah)

The hadith is the sūrah's own argument: the seduction is not a worship that is imposed; the seduction is a disturbing, and the disturbing is the one that the one who is seduced chooses to accept.

**Do Not Blame Me, Blame Yourselves**

The third part of the confession is the transfer of the responsibility: *so do not blame me, but blame yourselves* (*fa-lā taʿturbūnī wa-ʿturbū anfusakum*). The one who is seduced cannot, on the Day, point to the seducer as the cause of his fate; the one who is seduced must point to himself, because the response to the whisper was his own. The sūrah's own cross-reference at 74:38 states the same teaching in the sūrah's own words:

> *"Every soul is a pledge for its deeds."* (Qur'an 74:38)

The modern lesson of the clause is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the defense of "the system made me," "the culture made me," "the pressure made me" — the defense is the one that the Day does not accept, and the no-acceptance is the one that the sūrah's justice requires.

**The Mutual Cries That Go Unanswered**

The fourth part of the confession states the severance of the relation: *I cannot respond to your cries for help; nor can you respond to my cries for help* (*lā asmaʿu illā... wa-antum...*). The clause is the sūrah's statement of the final state of the seducer and the seduced: the two are now on opposite sides of the Fire, and the two cannot help each other. The seducer, who was in this life the one who called, is now the one who cannot be called to; the seduced, who was in this life the one who responded, is now the one who cannot respond. The sūrah's own cross-references at 8:48 and 59:16 give the same image in the same context, and the recurrence is the sūrah's own argument.

**I Disbelieved in Your Taking Me as a Partner**

The fifth part of the confession is the one that gives the verse its final irony: *truly I disbelieved in your ascribing me as partner aforetime* (*kāfartu bi-takhiḍikumnī sharīkan qablu*). The clause states that Satan himself is a disbeliever, and the disbeliever is the one whom the polytheists took as a partner beside God. The irony is the point: the partner that the polytheists set up beside God (v. 30) is himself a disbeliever, and the disbeliever is the one who does not believe in his own divinity. The clause also refers, in the reading the exegetes give, to the moment in which Satan was commanded to bow before Adam and refused (7:12), and the refusal is the one that the sūrah's argument requires:

> *"He said: 'I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay.'"* (Qur'an 7:12)

**The Painful Punishment for the Wrongdoers**

The confession closes with the statement that the sūrah's justice requires: *as for the wrongdoers, surely theirs shall be a painful punishment* (*wa-lā ẓālimīna la-hum ʿadhābun alīm*).

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:23

> **Those who believe and do righteous deeds shall be made to enter Gardens with rivers running below, abiding therein by the leave of their Lord. Their greeting therein shall be, "Peace!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Breath of the Garden in the Sūrah's Fire**

The twenty-third verse is the turn of the sūrah, and the turn is one of the most important in the chapter. The verses that preceded it — the torment of the tyrant (vv. 16–17), the ash of the deeds (v. 18), the meditation on the contingency of the creation (vv. 19–20), the assembly of the weak and the arrogant (v. 21), the confession of Satan (v. 22) — are the sūrah's description of the Fire and its inhabitants. Verse 23 turns the page, and the turn is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the sūrah does not leave the reader in the Fire; the sūrah gives the reader the Garden. The verse is the first mention of the Garden in the sūrah, and the first mention is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the sūrah's description of the afterlife is not a single description; the sūrah's description is a pair of descriptions, and the pair is the Fire and the Garden, and the Garden is the one that the sūrah will develop in the verses that follow.

**The Condition: Belief and Righteous Deeds**

The verse states the condition of the entry in two parts, and the two parts are the sūrah's own statement of the character of the entry. The first part is the belief (*alladhīna āmanū*), and the belief is the relation to the Giver, the acknowledgment of the source. The second part is the righteous deeds (*wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt*), and the deeds are the expression of the relation in the life, the acknowledgment made visible in the act. The two together are the whole of the condition: the belief without the deed is a relation that is not expressed, and the deed without the belief is an act that has no source. The sūrah's treatment of the condition is therefore a statement about the character of the entry, and the character is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the entry is the entry of the one whose relation is expressed in the life, and the expression is the one that the sūrah's justice requires.

**The Garden and the Rivers: The Image of the Permanence**

The next clause gives the image of the Garden: *Gardens with rivers running below, abiding therein* (*jannāt tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār khālidīna fīhā*). The image is the sūrah's version of the standing image of the Qur'an, and the image is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The garden is the image of the life that is sustained: the garden is not a garden that is dry; the garden is a garden in which the water is running, and the running is the one that produces the life. The rivers (*al-anhār*) are the plural, and the plural is the point: the sustenance of the Garden is not a single source; the sustenance is a plurality of sources, and the plurality is the one that the sūrah's justice requires. The word *khālidīna* — abiding, eternal — is the one that gives the image its full weight: the Garden is not a Garden that is temporary; the Garden is a Garden that is permanent, and the permanence is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The image is therefore the counter-image of the ash of verse 18: the deed that is scattered and the abode that is permanent, and the two are the two sides of the one law.

**By the Leave of Their Lord: The Grace of the Entry**

The clause *by the leave of their Lord* (*bi-idhn Rabbihim*) is the one that gives the verse its theological weight, and the weight is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The phrase is the same phrase that verse 1 used to describe the bringing out of the darkness into the light, and the recurrence is the sūrah's own argument: the entry into the Garden, like the exit from the darkness, is by the leave of God, and the leave is the grace that precedes the response. The sūrah's treatment of the grace is therefore a statement about the character of the entry: the entry is not a merit that is earned; the entry is a grace that is given, and the grace is the one that the sūrah's tawḥīd requires. The symmetry of the sūrah is thereby completed: verse 1 states that the light is entered by the leave, and verse 23 states that the Garden is entered by the leave, and the two together are the sūrah's statement of the character of the grace, and the character is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

**Peace: The Language of the Garden**

The verse closes with the image that is the sūrah's own statement of the character of the Garden: *their greeting therein shall be, "Peace!"* (*wa-salamuhūna salām*). The greeting is the word *salām* — peace — and the word is the sūrah's version of the standing teaching of the Qur'an about the language of the Garden. The teaching is stated in the sūrah's own cross-references, and the cross-references are the sūrah's own argument. At 39:73:

> *"...and [their greeting] is: 'Peace,' for what they have patiently endured."* (Qur'an 39:73)

And at 10:10, in the same context:

> *"And those who believe and do righteous deeds — We shall admit them to gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein eternally. They have God's permission. Their greeting therein is: 'Peace.'"* (Qur'an 10:10)

The recurrence is the sūrah's own argument: the language of the Garden is a single word, and the word is peace. The word *salām* is from the root s-l-m, the root of safety, of soundness, of health, and it is the same root as *islām*, the submission. The language of the Garden is therefore the language of the safety, and the safety is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the Garden is the place in which the conflict is ended, and the ending is the one that the sūrah's justice requires. The modern reader is invited to consider the implication: the world in which the reader lives is a world of noise, of quarrel, of fear, and the language of the Garden is the one word that the world does not speak. The sūrah's treatment of the Garden is therefore a statement about the character of the peace, and the character is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:24

> **Hast thou not considered how God sets forth a parable? A good word is as a good tree: its roots firm and its branches in the sky.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Good Word: The Name of the Faith**

The twenty-fourth verse opens the sūrah's third great section, the section of the parables, and the first parable is the one that is the sūrah's most celebrated. The subject of the parable is *the good word* (*al-kalimah al-ṭayyibah*), and the identification that the exegetes give is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the good word is the formula of the testimony, *There is no god but God* (*lā ilāha illā Allāh*), the word of the tawḥīd that is the substance of the belief. The word *kalimah* is the word for the word, the utterance, the formula, and the word *ṭayyibah* is the word for the good, the pure, the sound. The good word is therefore the word that is good in its substance, the word that is the tawḥīd, and the tawḥīd is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The parable is therefore not a parable about speech in general; the parable is a parable about the particular word, the word of the tawḥīd, and the particular is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

**The Parable of the Tree: The Form of the Image**

The next clause states the form of the parable: *a good word is as a good tree* (*ka-shajarat ṭayyibah*). The image is the image of the tree, and the tree is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The sūrah's own cross-reference in the hadith literature gives the particular tree that the parable describes, and the particular is the one that the exegetes preserved. In a report narrated by ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar, the son of the second Caliph, the Prophet ﷺ asked his Companions whether they knew what tree the verse described, and Ibn ʿUmar knew the answer but was too shy to speak before his elders, and the Prophet ﷺ gave the answer: the date palm. The particular is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the date palm is the tree of the land, the tree that is firmly rooted, the tree that gives its fruit in the season, and the tree is the one that the parable describes.

**The Root That Is Firm: The Depth of the Testimony**

The next clause gives the first detail of the parable: *its roots firm* (*thabūta ʿarḍahā*). The word *ʿarḍ* is the word for the root, the foundation, the ground, and it is from the root ʿ-r-ḍ, the root of the surface, of the earth, of the foundation. The root of the tree is the part that is not seen, and the part that is not seen is the one that holds the tree. The parable is therefore a statement about the character of the testimony: the testimony is not a surface act; the testimony is a depth, and the depth is the one that holds the one who testifies. The classical commentators, among them al-Rāzī, give the parable a spiritual reading that the sūrah's argument requires: the tree of the knowledge is firmly rooted in the land of the heart, and the rooting is the one that produces the strength. The one whose testimony is a tree whose roots are firm is the one whose heart is rooted in the tawḥīd, and the rooting is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The parable is therefore a statement about the character of the belief, and the character is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the belief is not a surface act; the belief is a depth, and the depth is the one that holds.

**The Branch in the Sky: The Aspiration of the Faith**

The next clause gives the second detail of the parable: *and its branches in the sky* (*wa-fawquhā fī al-samāʾ*). The branch is the part of the tree that is above, and the part that is above is the one that reaches. The parable is therefore a statement about the character of the faith: the faith is not only a depth; the faith is also an ascent, and the ascent is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The al-Rāzī reading, which the exegetes preserve, gives the detail its full weight: the tree of the knowledge, which is rooted in the land of the heart, has branches that are in the sky, and the branches are the forms of the knowledge that reach the divine realm. The parable is therefore a statement about the verticality of the believer: the root in the ground (the humility, the depth, the rooting) and the branch in the sky (the aspiration, the ascent, the reaching), and the two together are the whole of the spiritual anatomy. The sūrah's treatment of the parable is therefore a statement about the character of the faith, and the character is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the faith is the faith that is rooted and that reaches, and the two are the two sides of the one faith.

**The Sūrah's Horticultural Theology: The Heart as the Garden**

A structural note: the parable of verse 24 is the first of the sūrah's pair of parables, and the second is in the next two verses (vv. 25–26), where the fruit and the bad tree are given. The two parables together are the sūrah's horticultural theology, and the theology is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the heart is the garden, the word is the seed, and the choice is the act of the farmer. The reader who holds the pair of parables in mind will find that the sūrah's treatment of the faith is not a single image; the sūrah's treatment is a pair of images, and the pair is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the good tree and the bad tree, the root that is firm and the root that is uprooted, the branch that reaches and the tree that has no stability. The parable of verse 24 is the first of the pair, and the first is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:25

> **It brings forth fruit in every season, by the Leave of its Lord. God sets forth parables for mankind, that haply they may remember.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fruit in Every Season: The Continuity of the Faith**

The twenty-fifth verse completes the description of the good tree, and the completion is the one that gives the parable its full weight. The first clause states that the tree *brings forth fruit in every season* (*tuchri ṭaylāhā kulla al-mawsim*). The word *mawsim* is the word for the season, the time of the fruit, and the word *kull* — every — is the point. The date palm, in the reality that the parable describes, does in fact give its fruit across the year, and the reality is the one that the parable uses: the fruit of the date palm is not a single harvest; the fruit is a sequence of harvests, and the sequence is the one that the parable describes.

**The Seasons: The Changes of the Life**

The word "season" in the parable is the word for the changes of the life, and the changes are the ones that the sūrah's argument requires. The season of the wealth and the season of the poverty, the season of the health and the season of the illness, the season of the ease and the season of the trial — the seasons are the changes, and the changes are the ones that the faith must bear. The fruit of the faith in the season of the trial is the patience, and the fruit of the faith in the season of the blessing is the gratitude, and the two together are the fruit of the every-season.

**The Fruit of the Rooted Heart**

The exegetes, following the reading of al-Rāzī that the sūrah's argument requires, give the fruit its particular description: the fruit of the rooted tree is the fruit of the soul that is rooted in the knowledge, and the fruit is the beautiful word, the righteous act, the state of the humility, the self-effacement, the weeping, the lowliness.

**By the Leave of Its Lord: The Threefold Grace of the Parable**

The next clause states the condition of the fruit: *by the Leave of its Lord* (*bi-idhn Rabbihā*). The clause is the sūrah's own argument: the fruit is not self-produced; the fruit is produced by the leave of God. The root is by the grace (the rooting is the gift), the branch is by the grace (the reaching is the gift), and the fruit is by the grace (the producing is the gift).

**Parables That Maybe They May Remember**

The verse closes with the statement of the purpose of the parable: *God sets forth parables for mankind, that haply they may remember* (*wa-yuḍribu Allāhu al-amsāl laʿallakum tadhakkarūn*). The word *laʿallakum* — that haply, so that perhaps — is the one that gives the purpose its character: the parable is a means that does not compel, and the no-compulsion is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:26

> **And the parable of a bad word is a bad tree: uprooted from the face of the earth; it has no stability.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Bad Word: The Mirror of the Good**

The twenty-sixth verse gives the second parable of the sūrah's pair, and the second is the mirror of the first. The subject of the parable is *the bad word* (*al-kalimah al-sayyiʾah*), and the identification that the exegetes give is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the bad word is the statement of the disbelief, the negation of the testimony, the word that is the opposite of the good word of verse 24.

**Uprooted from the Face of the Earth**

The next clause gives the first detail of the parable: *uprooted from the face of the earth* (*muntakhathat min faqq al-arḍ*). The word *muntakhathat* is the participle of the tearing up, and the participle is the point: the tree is not a tree that is weak; the tree is a tree that has been torn up. The exegetes give the parable a particular identification: the bad tree is the *ḥanẓalah*, the tree of the desert whose roots are shallow, close to the surface of the earth, and the shallow root is the one that is easily torn up.

**No Stability: The Two Senses of the Word**

The next clause gives the second detail of the parable: *it has no stability* (*mā lahā min qarār*). The word *qarār* is from the root q-r-r, the root of the settling, of the bottom, of the stability, and it is the word for the place where the water settles, the place where the thing comes to rest. The exegetes give the word two senses, both of which are needed. The two senses together are the whole of the no-stability: the no-place in this world and the no-abode in the next, and the two are the two sides of the one law.

**The Modern Application: The Diet of the Words**

The word is the seed, and the seed is the one that grows in the heart. The words that the reader internalizes — the words of the media, the words of the companions, the words of the inner dialogue — are the seeds that are planted in the heart, and the seeds are the ones that produce the trees. The sūrah's own command, stated in another sūrah, is the command that the parable requires:

> *"...and say to them a good word."* (Qur'an 17:23)

**Two Trees, One Garden: The Pair of Parables**

The two parables together are the full description of the character of the word: the good word that is the good tree, and the bad word that is the bad tree; the root that is firm and the root that is uprooted; the branch that reaches and the tree that has no stability; the fruit that is in every season and the tree that has no fruit.

## Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:27

> **God makes firm those who believe with firm speech in the life of this world and in the Hereafter. And God leads the wrongdoers astray; God does whatsoever He wills.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Firm Word: The Anchor of the Believer**

The twenty-seventh verse is the sūrah's statement of the effect of the good word that the parable of verses 24–26 described, and the statement is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The first clause states that *God makes firm those who believe with firm speech* (*yuṣbid Allāhu alladhīna āmanū bi-qawlin thabit*). The word *qawl thabit* — the firm word — is the identification that the exegetes give as the full formula of the testimony, and the formula is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The word *iṣbāt* (making firm) is from the root ʿ-b-t, to establish, to fix, to make stable, and it is the word for the act of the one who makes a thing that was moving become still. The clause is therefore a statement about the character of the effect: the effect of the firm word is the firmness of the believer, and the firmness is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The believer is the one who is made firm by the word, and the making-firm is the act of God, not the act of the believer. The sūrah's treatment of the effect is therefore a statement about the character of the grace: the grace is the one that makes the believer firm, and the making-firm is the one that the sūrah's tawḥīd requires.

**The Connection to the Tree: The Same Root**

A structural note: the "firm speech" of verse 27 is the same as the "good word" of verse 24, and the connection is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The good word is the tree, and the tree is the one that is made firm by the root, and the root is the one that is in the ground. The "firm speech" is therefore the word that is the root, and the root is the one that makes the believer firm. The sūrah's treatment of the pair is therefore a statement about the character of the faith: the faith is the faith that is made firm by the word, and the making-firm is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The two verses together are the sūrah's statement of the character of the faith, and the character is the one that the sūrah's argument requires.

A final structural note: the sūrah begins with three letters (Alif, Lām, Rāʾ) and a Book that leads out of the darkness into light (v. 1), and it ends with a proclamation that warns, gives to know, and reminds (v. 52). The arc of the sūrah is the arc of the call of the Qur'an in the life of the human: the call is made, the call is answered (or refused), and the call is the one that the sūrah's justice requires. The prayer of Abraham (vv. 35–41) is the heart of the sūrah, and the heart is the one that the sūrah's argument requires: the sūrah is named after the one who stood in the sanctuary and prayed for the hearts of mankind, and the sūrah itself is that prayer, made into a Book. The sūrah's treatment of the arc is therefore a statement about the character of the structure of the sūrah, and the character is the one that the sūrah's justice requires: the structure is the structure that is the arc, and the arc is the one that the sūrah's argument requires. The sūrah that opens with the promise of the light closes with the proclamation to the people of understanding, and the two together are the sūrah's own statement of the character of the call: the call is the call that is made, and the call is the one that the sūrah's justice requires.
