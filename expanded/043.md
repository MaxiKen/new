# Sūrah al-Zukhruf (Chapter 43) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ornament as a Name and as a Warning**

Al-Zukhruf, "Gold Ornaments," is a Makkan sūrah, generally held to have been revealed after Sūrah 42, al-Shūrā. Some maintain that verse 45 is Madinan. It takes its name from the mention of gold ornaments in verse 35. The classical observers noted that its opening on the nature of the Qur'an and the Mother of the Book follows the close of the previous sūrah:

> *"Thou knewest not what scripture was, nor faith. But We made it a light whereby We guide whomsoever We will among Our servants."* (Qur'an 42:52)

After a brief discussion of revelation (vv. 2–5), the sūrah cites the fate of earlier communities (vv. 6–8) and challenges the Makkan worldview (vv. 9–35). It answers objections: assigning partners (v. 15), claiming angels as God's daughters (v. 19), blaming God's will for idolatry (v. 20), demanding a notable as prophet (v. 31), and likening idols to Jesus (vv. 57–58). False measures of truth — ancestral creed (vv. 22–25) and wealth as rank (vv. 31–36) — are dismantled. Abraham is cited against ancestralism (vv. 26–28); Pharaoh against class theology (vv. 46–56); Jesus against both pagan and later excess (vv. 57–65). An extended contrast of Garden and Fire (vv. 66–78) yields to a last challenge (vv. 79–87) and an injunction of calm peace to the Prophet ﷺ (vv. 88–89).

**How This Commentary Proceeds**

Each of the eighty-nine verses is treated as an independent discourse: translation, expanded commentary, unique mini-headings, and blockquoted Qur'an, Hadith, and classical views. Grouped source notes have been separated verse by verse. Nothing has been shortened because the chapter is long.

## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:1

> **Ḥā. Mīm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth of the Seven Ḥawāmīm**

This sūrah is the fourth in a series of seven consecutive chapters that open with the disconnected letters Ḥā Mīm — Sūrahs 40 through 46 — collectively called the ḥawāmīm. The letters interrupt ordinary speech at the threshold of the Book, the way a call to prayer interrupts ordinary noise: they announce that what follows is not ordinary discourse.

Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq is reported to have said:

> *Every book has a mystery, and the mystery of the Qur'an is in the openings of the sūrahs.*

**Letters as Challenge and as Humility**

Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Zamakhsharī treat such letters as a standing proclamation of inimitability: these are the Arabs' own alphabet, and from them they cannot produce the like of this Book. Al-Rāzī adds a complementary reading: the letters keep a space of mystery at the door of revelation so that the reader begins as a servant, not as an examiner standing over the text.

Sūrah al-Zukhruf will spend its length dismantling false measures of truth — ancestry, gold, rank, and dispute. It is fitting that it opens with letters that refuse every false measure of meaning: they cannot be priced, ranked, or ancestralized. They simply stand, and the Book begins.

**The Ḥawāmīm as a Shared Climate of Tawḥīd**

The seven chapters that open with Ḥā Mīm form a curriculum: they defend revelation, recount destroyed towns, expose the excuses of shirk, and console the Messenger. Al-Zukhruf's particular work is the critique of false valuation — gold, lineage, and dispute. Reciting the letters, the worshipper tastes that every later argument in the sūrah stands downstream of a Voice that does not begin by negotiating with Quraysh.

Ibn ʿAbbās's school treated such openings as among the mutashābih whose full intent is with God, while still allowing the uses the ummah has always drawn: pause, wonder, challenge, and humility. They are sounds Quraysh already owned, rearranged into a threshold.

**Mystery Before Ornament**

This sūrah will name itself after gold ornaments. It is fitting that it does not open with gold, nor with a slogan, but with letters that cannot be priced. Al-Rāzī's point stands: a mystery at the door keeps the reader a servant. In an age that demands immediate explanation of everything, Ḥā Mīm is already a moral exercise.

Thus the disconnected letters are not a delay of the sūrah's work; they are its first work: to place every later word under a sovereignty that does not share the stage with ornament.

**Lived Implication of 43:1**

The believer does not finish this verse as a spectator of seventh-century errors. He asks where the same motion lives in his house, his spending, his arguments, and his friendships, and he returns to remembrance before a companion of the wrong kind is assigned. Scholarship without this turn is only another ornament.

**Arabic and the Classical Thread of 43:1**

The wording Ḥā Mīm: disconnected letters; fourth of the ḥawāmīm; mystery and iʿjāz together. This is the thread al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī pull in different lengths: language first, then occasion, then creed, then the human heart. None of them treats the verse as a slogan. Each treats it as a complete address that can be lived.

**Why the Placement Matters**

Verse 1 of al-Zukhruf sits after what the sūrah has already demolished and before what it will still demolish. Reading it as an isolated proverb flattens the argument. Reading it in order shows a patient Divine pedagogy: reveal the Book, list the gifts, expose the excuses, narrate Moses, reclaim Jesus, open the Garden, close the Fire, then command peace.

**Hadith and Practice**

Where a sound report exists — the companion-jinn (Muslim), the drowning-as-enticement report Ibn Kathīr cites at the drowning, the descent of Jesus as a sign of the Hour, the travel-tasbīḥ built on verses 13–14, the character of saying Peace in 25:63 — the believer's work is to practice, not only to cite. Where no specific report is attached, the verse still binds by its own imperative or indicative. The Prophet ﷺ lived this chapter before it was a chapter in a bound muṣḥaf: holding fast, warning, grieving, and greeting with peace.

**A Modern Scene**

The same motions wear new clothes: ancestralism as nationalism, gold as branding, Pharaoh as propaganda, Jesus as a debating chip, secret converse as encrypted slander, couches as the unexamined good life. Verse 1 still knows these clothes. The honest reader translates without forcing. Then he does the small next obedience the verse implies — thanks, tawḥīd, better friendship, withheld mockery, given peace.

## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:2

> **By the clear Book,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath by the Book That Makes Things Clear**

God swears by al-kitāb al-mubīn — the clear Book. Mubīn is an active participle: it is not merely clear in itself; it makes clear. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī read it as the Book that distinguishes the path of guidance from the path of misguidance. Ibn Kathīr: clear in wording and in meaning. Al-Ṭabarī: the appellation alludes to its being sent down by God rather than composed by human beings.

> *This is a Book We have sent down unto thee, blessed, that they may ponder its signs.* (Qur'an 38:29)

The oath form — wa — places the Book in the rank of things God swears by: dawn, the fig, the sun. The implication is that the clarity of this Book is as real as those created signs. Whoever treats it as obscure has not yet approached it with the honesty it asks.

**Clarity as Mercy and as Evidence**

A book that makes the way clear removes the excuse of confusion. The Makkan hearers of this sūrah could not plead that the message was coded or elite. Clarity is therefore both a gift and a court: after the clear Book, rejection is no longer a problem of information. It is a problem of will.

**Oaths That Honor the Book**

When God swears by the clear Book He ranks it with realities as serious as sunrise. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that an oath honors the thing sworn by and draws attention to what follows: Arabic clarity, the Mother of the Book, and the refusal to withdraw the Reminder.

Mubīn blocks the claim that scripture is too ambiguous to obey. Clarity does not mean every verse is equally easy; it means the path and the warning are not encrypted. A person may still need teachers — the Prophet was sent as explainer — but he cannot claim that God spoke in riddles in order to trap him.

Whoever treats the Book as unclear after a fair reading has located the fog in the will, not in the page.

## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:3

> **truly We have made it an Arabic Quran, that haply you may understand,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Arabic Qur'an so That Understanding Is Possible**

Jaʿalnāhu qurʾānan ʿarabiyyan — We have made it an Arabic recitation — laʿallakum taʿqilūn, that haply you may understand. The verb ʿaqala is the verb of binding and of intellect: to bind meaning so that it can be used. Revelation in the tongue of its first audience is not provincialism; it is justice. A message that arrived in an unknown language would have been a riddle, not a responsibility.

> *Truly We have sent it down as an Arabic Qur'an, that haply you may understand.* (Qur'an 12:2)

Al-Ṭabarī: it should be clear to the Arabs, who then have no excuse for rejecting it. The same principle extends: every later people receives the Book through translation of meaning, but the Arabic remains the criterion of the original, because God chose this tongue as the vessel of the final speech.

**Why Language Matters to Faith**

Faith is not a fog of feelings. It is an address that can be understood, argued with, obeyed, and loved. By specifying Arabic, the verse also protects the text from the claim that it is the product of a foreign tutor — a charge 16:103 answers directly. The Prophet ﷺ spoke this tongue; his people mastered it; the challenge of 2:23 stands in that tongue. Understanding is offered; refusal after understanding is a different sin from never having heard.

**Arabic as Justice, Understanding as Aim**

A revelation that demanded desert Arabs to master a foreign tongue would have contradicted 'God tasks no soul except its capacity.' Sending an Arabic Qur'an is justice to the first hearers. Later non-Arabs enter by learning or by reliable translation of meanings, while the original remains the reference of law and recitation.

Laʿallakum taʿqilūn makes intellection a desired fruit. Islam's quarrel is with caprice and conjecture, not with the bound intellect. A community that recites without seeking to understand has used the mouth and left unused the ʿaql this verse named as the goal.

Language here is mercy: God spoke so that understanding, and therefore responsibility, could occur.

## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:4

> **and truly it is with Us in the Mother of the Book, sublime indeed, wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mother of the Book: Source Above Corruption**

Umm al-kitāb — Mother of the Book — is interpreted by most commentators (Ibn Kathīr, al-Jalālayn, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī) as the Preserved Tablet:

> *Nay, it is a glorious Qur'an, in a Preserved Tablet.* (Qur'an 85:21–22)

The earthly recitation is a descent from an archetype that cannot be edited. ʿAlī — sublime — indicates transcendence beyond falsification. Ḥakīm — wise — may mean determined (muḥkam), or that which makes wise (muḥkim). Al-Rāzī notes that when ʿalī modifies the Qur'an it can mean that this Book transcends earlier scriptures as a miracle remaining for all time.

**What It Means That Wisdom Lives Above History**

If the source of the Book is with God, then fashion, empire, and scholarly fashion cannot finally rewrite it. The believer who recites al-Zukhruf is not handling a tribal pamphlet. He is handling a copy, in time, of a speech whose mother is above time. That is why 15:9 promises preservation, and why 56:77–80 forbids the approach of the impure to its full reality. Sublimity is not distance for its own sake; it is the guarantee that guidance does not rot.

**Source Above Scribes**

Mother of the Book as the Preserved Tablet means there is no rival Qur'an, only a source from which the recited Arabic descends. ʿAlī shields against interpolation; ḥakīm shields against the charge of folly. Al-Rāzī's reading of ḥakīm as that which gives wisdom adds a third: the Book produces wise people when held fast, as verse 43 will command.

A wise, sublime source means guidance does not rot with empires or fashions of the academy.

## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:5

> **Shall We withdraw the reminder from you altogether for your having been a prodigal people?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mercy That Does Not Withdraw the Reminder**

The rhetorical question — shall We withdraw the reminder because you have been prodigal? — expects a no born of mercy. Al-Ṭabarī: shall We withhold the reminder of punishment? Al-Jalālayn: shall We withhold the Qur'an? Ibn Kathīr prefers the second: God continues to reveal even after initial rejection. Prodigality (isrāf) is spending the self beyond the bound of gratitude — wasting the gift of hearing.

A parent does not stop speaking to a wasteful child at the first refusal. The verse teaches that revelation's persistence is not weakness. It is raḥmah continuing after ʿadl would have been entitled to silence. The prodigal people still receive the Reminder; their prodigality will be judged, but it is not allowed to cancel the chance of others, or even of themselves, to hear again.

**The Fear That Speech Might Stop**

Prodigality here is wasted hearing. Every recitation used as background noise participates in a mild form of it. That God does not withdraw the Reminder after rejection is raḥmah. Ibn Kathīr's reading — the Qur'an continued to come — tells callers not to interpret slow response as Divine silence. Second chances are written into tanzīl itself.

Continued revelation after wastefulness is one of the quietest, largest mercies in the chapter.

> **How many a prophet did We send among those of old?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Consolation by History: Prophets Were Many**

Kam arsalnā — how many did We send — opens a census of grief that is also a census of honor. The Prophet ﷺ is not an unprecedented victim of mockery; he stands in a long line. Ibn Kathīr and al-Jalālayn read the verse as consolation. Isolation is one of the devil's first lies to a messenger and to every later caller: you are the first to be laughed at. The verse answers: you are not.

> *Messengers before thee were indeed mocked.* (Qur'an 6:10)

**Census of Messengers**

Quantity is used pastorally. The lonely caller is invited to join a file that includes Noah's centuries and Moses' palace. God did not send one experimental messenger; refusal is refusal of a known pattern.

Read this verse in sequence: it neither repeats the last nor steals the next. Its own burden is complete, and the sūrah's argument advances only because this burden has been carried.

## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:7

> **Yet never did a prophet come unto them, but that they mocked him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mockery as the Recurring Reception of Truth**

Mā yaʾtīhim min nabiyyin illā kānū bihi yastahziʾūn — never did a prophet come but they mocked him. Cf. 15:11; 36:30. Mockery is cheaper than argument. It costs no study and confers the feeling of superiority. Al-Ṭabarī: the derision faced by Muhammad ﷺ is no greater than that faced by those before him.

The social function of mockery is to make seriousness look naive. The verse unmasks that function as ancient, not sophisticated. Every age that laughs revelation off the stage is repeating a script already judged. The caller is taught not to measure truth by the volume of the laugh.

**The Economy of the Laugh**

Mockery bonds the in-group, costs no study, and punishes dignity. Because the laugh is predicted, it is disarmed. Verse 89 will forbid answering mockery with mockery.

## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:8

> **So We destroyed those of greater prowess than them, and the example of those of old has passed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Greater Prowess Did Not Save Them**

The destroyed nations were more numerous, stronger, and left firmer traces (40:82; 50:36). The Makkan opposition, weaker than ʿĀd and Thamūd and Pharaoh, is foolish to think it can succeed where they failed. Mathal al-awwalīn — the example of those of old — is either their way of life (Ibn Kathīr) or the punishment that became a byword, as in 43:56.

Worldly prowess is a false eschatology: it treats survival-so-far as a verdict. The verse teaches the opposite physics. Strength that is used against messengers becomes the very material of the lesson later generations walk past in ruins. Modern analogues — empires, markets, technologies — do not escape the same law. Capacity is not innocence.

**Prowess as a Failed Eschatology**

Greater strength failed; lesser strength will not succeed. Technology is only a new name for prowess. Travel in the Qur'an is often for seeing ends (40:82).

Read this verse in sequence: it neither repeats the last nor steals the next. Its own burden is complete, and the sūrah's argument advances only because this burden has been carried.

## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:11

**Expanded Commentary**

**Water in Due Measure, and the Analogy of Resurrection**

**Measure, Rain, and Graves**

Rain that always flooded would end agriculture; rain that never came would end it otherwise. The annual revival of land is the easier analogue of raising bones. Dead hearts revived by revelation's water are a lawful second harvest of the image.

## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:26

> **And [remember] when Abraham said to his father and his people, “Truly I am quit of that which you worship,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham's Break: I Am Quit of What You Worship**

Against the notables who cling to fathers, Abraham risks his life by quitting his father's worship. For Arabs who claimed honor from Abraham, the citation is double-edged: follow him truly, or admit you follow nearer, lesser fathers. Similar declarations: 6:78; 11:54–55; 6:19.

Barāʾah — being quit — is a legal and spiritual word: disavowal. Love of God outranked love of origin. The verse does not license contempt for parents; Abraham's other stories show courtesy with disagreement (19:42–48). It licenses the moment when worship is the issue and compromise would be shirk. Every believer inherits a version of this test when family, nation, or workplace demands a share of what belongs only to God.

## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:89

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bear with Them and Say Peace — Soon They Will Know**

Fa-iṣfaḥ — turn away, bear with. Say: Peace. Soon they will know — in the Hereafter, or at the Conquest of Makkah (Ibn Kathīr, al-Jalālayn). Some claimed abrogation by the sword-verse (al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī); others held it muḥkam (al-Qurṭubī), in keeping with 25:63–64: the servants of the Compassionate say Peace when the ignorant address them, and 7:199.

The sūrah that opened with mysterious letters and fought gold, ancestry, Pharaoh, and false claims about Jesus ends in a manner: ṣafḥ and salām. Knowledge will arrive; it need not always be installed by the tongue's heat. The Prophet ﷺ is told to remain the calmer party. For the reader, the last ethic of al-Zukhruf is this: after every argument in the chapter, speak peace, and leave the timing of 'they will know' to God.

**Forbearance Is the Last Command**

Bear with them, say Peace, soon they will know — some at the Conquest, all at the Hour. 25:63 remains the character even when battle is elsewhere legislated. After gold, fathers, Pharaoh, and false arguments, the messenger is told to be calm. Copy that calm after doing the work of clarity.
