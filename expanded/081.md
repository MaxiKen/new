# Sūrah al-Takwīr (Chapter 81) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Named for a Folding**

Sūrah al-Takwīr, "The Folding Up," takes its name from a single word in its opening verse — *kuwwirat*, "is enfolded" — and that word sets the key for everything the chapter will do. The root *k-w-r* describes the action of winding and gathering a thing into a round bundle: one wraps (*kawwara*) a turban, folds a garment into a ball, rolls up a scroll. What the sūrah announces with its first syllables is therefore not merely the end of the sun but a *method* of ending: the sun is not shattered or exploded; it is folded, gathered up, rolled into nothing like a robe taken off at the end of the day. The sūrah is known in the older books by the descriptive title "When the Sun Is Enfolded" (*Idhā al-shamsu kuwwirat*), and it is the first sūrah in the order of the muṣḥaf of the thirtieth juz' cluster of Day-of-Judgment chapters that Muslims have traditionally memorized earliest — the cluster of *al-Mufṣaḥ*, the "short, clearly separated" sūrahs of the Makkan period. It contains twenty-nine verses by unanimous count, and there is no disagreement among the scholars of revelation about its Makkan origin.

**Seventh Out of the First**

In the classical catalogues of the chronological order of revelation, al-Takwīr stands seventh — a sūrah from the very first wave of public preaching, when the Muslim community was a small, persecuted handful in Makkah and almost everything that had yet been revealed consisted of vivid, hammering depictions of the Last Day. ʿĀ'ishah described precisely this stage of revelation in the hadith al-Bukhārī transmits:

> *"The first things revealed were short sūrahs in which Paradise and Hell-fire are mentioned, until the people incline toward Islam; then the longer sūrahs with legal injunctions were revealed."* (al-Bukhārī, Ṣaḥīḥ, Kitāb Faḍā'il al-Qur'ān)

Al-Takwīr belongs exactly to that first register: the world is unmade, the records are opened, each soul learns what it sent ahead — and then the sūrah turns, in its second half, to the credibility of the One who announced all of it. Its historical setting explains that turn. The Makkan elite's case against the Prophet ﷺ had hardened into three charges, and the sūrah's second section answers them point by point: he is a soothed soothsayer, he is mad, he is a poet or a conjurer taught by devils. Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah's famous deliberation over the Qur'an — rejected in the words the sūrah of al-Muddaththir preserves, *"This is nothing but magic transmitted; this is nothing but the speech of a human being"* (74:24–25) — is the courtroom atmosphere into which this sūrah was sent. In the same court, al-Takwīr delivers its counter-evidence: oaths sworn by the heavens themselves, the character of the bearer of revelation, the eyewitness horizon of Gabriel's true form, and the unmatched, unwithheld, undevilish text.

**One Sentence, Twelve Terrors**

The first fourteen verses are, in Arabic, a single suspended sentence. Twelve times the clause-initial *idhā* — "when" — is repeated with twelve verbs, all of them passive, and the listener is made to wait, terror piled on terror, until grammatical completion arrives only at verse 14: *"each soul shall know what it has made ready."* The form is the meaning. The postponement of the main verb forces the audience to live, moment by moment, inside the collapse — no relief, no verdict, no destination named until the cumulative dread has fully gathered. And every verb is passive: *kuwwirat* (the sun is folded), *inkadarat* (the stars are shed), *suyyirat* (the mountains are driven off), *ʿushilat* (the camels are abandoned), *ḥushirat* (the beasts are gathered), *sujjirat* (the seas are set ablaze or made to boil), *ʿuqirat* (the souls are paired), *su'ilat* (the infant is questioned), *nutishat* (the scrolls are spread), *kushitat* (the sky is stripped), *saʿirat* (the Fire is kindled), *ʿuridat* (the Garden is brought near). Not one actor is named in the whole sequence — and that is the most terrifying fact the grammar conceals in plain sight: on that Day every creature that seemed to act by its own power, every force that seemed to have a will of its own, becomes a verb without an agent. The sun does not decide to go out; the mountains do not choose to move. One Agent remains, and everything else is done *to*.

**The Inversion of the Created Order**

The events are not a random apocalypse inventory; they systematically unwind the world in the order of its intimacies. Light goes first — the sun folded, the stars spilled — because visibility itself is being withdrawn. Stability goes next — the mountains, the earth's pegs, carried off like dust. Then value: the pregnant she-camel, the most precious asset of the Arabian economy, abandoned unclaimed while all the world's wealth stands empty of meaning. Then nature's habits: wild beasts, which flee each other by design, driven together; the seas, whose meeting is barred by a limit they do not transgress, heaped and burning. Then the human order is unwound with the same twelvefold stroke: souls separated all their lives are re-paired for destiny, the voiceless victim of the worst buried crime is made a public questioner, the hidden ledger of every life is unrolled, and the canopy the earth lived beneath — the sky — is peeled away to reveal the seats of judgment beneath and above it: Fire kindled, Garden brought near. Only then does the sentence resolve: *each soul shall know what it has made ready.* The cosmos is dismantled to clear the floor for conscience. It is no wonder that the great hadith cited by Ibn Kathīr for this sūrah — also transmitted by al-Tirmidhī and graded *ḥasan* — ties the whole sequence to sight itself:

> *"Whosoever wishes to look at the Day of Judgment as if he is seeing it with his own eyes, let him read: When the sun is enfolded, and when the stars fade away; when the mountains are set in motion, and when pregnant camels are abandoned."* (al-Tirmidhī, Kitāb al-Tafsīr)

**The Oaths and Their Answer**

At verse 15 the sūrah pivots with *fa-lā uqsimu* — "So I swear" — into its second movement. Four oaths are taken: by the receding lights, by the swimming, hidden wanderers, by the night as it advances or withdraws, and by the dawn as it breathes. Their response, the clause the oaths exist to confirm, arrives at verse 19: *"Truly it is the speech of a noble messenger, possessed of strength, before the Possessor of the Throne, of high rank, obeyed, trustworthy withal."* The choice of oath-objects is deeply reasoned. What are sworn by are precisely the things the first section showed dissolving — the stars that faded in verse 2, the night and morning whose faithful alternation is the daily resurrection of order after disorder. God swears by His creatures that they are His creatures: regular enough to bear witness, contingent enough to perish on command. And the early Makkan listener had heard this oath-cluster before, in the sūrah revealed just a little earlier in the chronology, al-Muddaththir: *"I swear by the receding wanderers, by the night as it stealthily passes, and by the morning as it breathes"* (74:32–34). Al-Takwīr takes the community's familiar oath and turns it into a courtroom argument for the truth of revelation.

**Six Verses Against Three Accusations**

Verses 19–25 constitute one of the most tightly argued passages in the short Makkan sūrahs: a complete verification of the chain of transmission of revelation. Who carried this text? A noble messenger — the Archangel Gabriel — possessing strength at the throne of the Possessor of the Throne, obeyed among the heavenly hosts and trustworthy in every detail (vv. 19–21). And your companion, the man you have lived with for forty years, is not a madman — he saw Gabriel on the clear horizon; he does not hoard or fake the Unseen he reports; and the text he brings is not the stammer of an outcast devil (vv. 22–25). Against the accusation of madness, the sūrah sets his recognizable sanity and the credibility of an eye-witness of the angel; against the charge of soothsaying, the fact that he withholds nothing of the Unseen and claims none of it; against the ascription to devils, the heavens' own testimony that devils are barred and pelted from the upper air. Every link of the chain — God, Gabriel, the Prophet, the recited Book — is given its qualification: strength, rank, obedience, trust, sight, openness, immunity. This is why the section ends not with a command but with an astonished, almost pitying question: *"So where are you going?"* (81:26) — as if to say: the route to error now has no road-sign left in it.

**Reminder, Will, and Sovereign Mercy**

The final three verses are the sūrah's seal and, remarkably, its mirror of the ending of al-Muddaththir, which closes with almost the same triad — *"Nay, but it is a reminder; so whoever wills, remembers it; and they do not remember except that God wills"* (74:55–56). Al-Takwīr ends: the Qur'an is a reminder for the worlds, for whoever among you wills to be upright — and you do not will except that God, Lord of the worlds, wills (81:27–29). Two truths placed side by side with nothing to soften either: the human will is real, addressed, blamed, honored — *where are you going?* is an address to someone who can still turn; and the Divine will is total — not one act of willing stirs in any heart except by its Leave. The sūrah does not propose a riddle to be solved but a balance to be inhabited: responsibility without autonomy's pride, dependence without determinism's excuse. The whole movement of the sūrah — from the folded sun to the folded scroll of the sky, and from the folded sky to the open record of the self, and from the open record to the will that turns or hardens — lands on one practical instruction: whatever can be sent ahead, send ahead, because every soul will know nothing other than what it has prepared.

**Two Cautions Before the Commentary Begins**

First, a note on the "virtues" literature. Because the sūrah opens with one of the most spectacular scenes in the Qur'an, later collections of devotional ladders attached to it specially recommended night prayers and fasting nights for its recitation; the great majority of those reports were classified among the fabricated by the hadith critics, and nothing soundly transmitted singles out al-Takwīr for a dedicated ritual. What is soundly transmitted is its power to make the Unseen seen — the Tirmidhī hadith quoted above — and the general prophetic practice of reciting the Mufṣaḥ in prayer. Second, a note on interpretation: several verses of this sūrah (the receding wanderers of vv. 15–16, the coupling of souls in v. 7, the enfolded sun of v. 1 itself) were never pinned by the Companions and Successors to a single explanation. The commentary that follows presents the received range of readings, names the one the evidence supports best where the commentators themselves did, and does not pretend certainty God did not grant. The scene of the Day is described in words our world knows — sun, stars, mountains, camels, seas, scrolls — so that every age may watch its own securities folded one by one, and still arrive, with the terror intact, at the sentence that is not about the cosmos at all: each soul shall know what it has made ready.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:1

> **When the sun is enfolded,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb Wound Like a Turban**

The sūrah's first verb — *kuwwirat*, from the root *k-w-r* — is a verb of wrapping and rolling. In the Arabs' speech, *takwīr* is what you do to a turban when you wind it, to a cloak when you gather it into a bundle, to a scroll when you close it for storage. The dictionaries of the language preserve the same root in *kurr*, the coiled band around the base of a turban. So the image the first word of this sūrah carries is not explosion but collection: the sun — a body of fire so vast that nothing the pre-Islamic listener could imagine matched it — is gathered up, wound into a bundle, and put away the way a servant rolls the day's garment at nightfall. Verse 1 of al-Takwīr was, in the idiom of the sūrah, the sunset that never ends.

Note also the grammar. *Kuwwirat* is passive: it is folded, it is made to be folded. The verse will not yet say by Whom — as the introduction to the sūrah observed, all twelve verbs of this first section stand in the passive, and the withheld Agent is the theological point. The sun that empires worshipped, that poets addressed, that calendars obeyed, does not retire by its own decision; it is retired. Whatever independence the cosmos seemed to enjoy is announced here as fiction.

**What Folding the Sun Means**

The earliest commentators declined to choose one picture to the exclusion of the others, because the verb is broad enough to hold several. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr report the main range. One reading takes the enfolding as the extinction of the sun's light — its radiance wiped out, its disk gone dark, since rolling up a garment is a figure for removing what it displayed. A second reading, transmitted with chains reaching back to the time of the Successors, understands the sun as being rolled up and cast into the side of the Divine Throne — the created lights gathered toward their Owner the way an entrusted scroll is returned to the chancery that issued it. A third presses the image literally: one part of the sun is coiled into another until the whole is a wound thing, and it is then hurled away, spent. These are not rival propositions about astrophysics; they are three angles on one certainty — the sun's complete disposal. That certainty is what the verse means.

The third angle is anchored in prophecy as an event, not only a figure. Al-Bukhārī records:

> *"The sun and the moon are folded up on the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī, Ṣaḥīḥ, Kitāb Badʾ al-Khalq, no. 4863, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar)

A sūrah whose first word is "enfolded" and a hadith whose verb is the same word — *kuwwirat* — explain one another: the folding is real, and it extends beyond the sun to the moon, the two great lights of the created order.

**Why the Sun Comes First**

Of all the things that could be folded — oceans, kings, mountains — the sun is folded first, and the sequence's psychology rewards attention. In the order of creation's services, the sun is the master variable: light, the seasons, the harvest, the reckoning of days, the visibility of everything else all follow it. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the sun the measure of time itself — *"the sun runs its course to a resting place appointed by the One Who is Exalted in Might, the Knowing"* (36:38) — and the night follows it as a shadow follows a standard. To unmake the master variable is to unmake everything that depended on it in a single stroke: the stars will fade because there is no more night to show them (v. 2); the mountains will be visible only as loose dust, because the light that made them "solid" in the eye is gone (v. 3). The sequence is causally elegant as well as dreadful.

There is also a deliberate, polemical edge. The sun is the greatest thing anyone in the audience had ever bowed to — and Arabia's neighbors had deified it. The Qur'an had already argued that whatever sets cannot be God: *"When Abraham saw a star rising, he said, 'This is my Lord'; but when it set, he said, 'I do not love that which sets'"* (6:76). Al-Takwīr does not repeat the argument; it stages its conclusion. The act of worshipful love is folded away on the Day, and the worshipper is left standing in the dark that all his devotions to lights could never keep away. The sūrah thus begins its campaign against every lesser trust exactly where the grandest one is.

**An Eclipse With a Warning Attached**

Islam's teaching on eclipses supplies a living commentary on this verse. When the sun was eclipsed on the day Abraham, the Prophet's son, died, people said the heavens had mourned the boy. The Prophet ﷺ denied any such connection and redirected the fear:

> *"The sun and the moon do not eclipse for anyone's death or birth. But they are two of God's signs; when you see them, pray and call upon God, until it is lifted from you."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim)

In the version transmitted by Aḥmad and al-Nasāʾī from Ibn ʿAbbās, graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by Ibn Ḥibbān, there is a moving addition: *"…and when you see it, hasten to remembrance of God and to prayer"* — and in some of the same chains the Prophet ﷺ, at the shadowed sun, spoke of the Hour and its terrors. The prophetic practice trained the community to read every dimming of the sun as rehearsal: a partial eclipse, lifted in an hour, as a pledge of the total enfolding whose lifting never comes. Nature's regularity is the classroom; the Day is the examination.

**What the Unwinding Does to the Human Heart**

Read only as description of the far past's future, verse 1 would already be grand. But the sūrah aims its first hammer-blow at the listener's chest, not his astronomy, and its logic is worth naming plainly. The human being orients his life by whatever he takes to be permanent. The sun is the oldest guarantee a creature has: it has risen at every dawn since his birth, and nothing else he relies on has proven so reliable. Contracts break, health fails, markets crash, even the ground is less certain than the sunrise. The day the sūrah folds that first certainty — the guarantee behind all guarantees — it is telling every heart: you leaned on a thing that can be unwound by its Owner, and you leaned your whole life's arrangement on it. This is what *shirk*, in the sense this sūrah is fighting, really is: not merely bowing to statues, but organizing one's security around anything that can be folded.

The analogy the commentators return to, in one form or another, is the road. The believer's life is a passage through a mountain darkness in which the sun is the great headlight. When the light of the sun is switched off at the far end of the road, only one kind of traveler still reaches his destination: the one who was not trusting the light but the Guide — who kept his hand on the railing of revelation while the lamps still burned. The folded sun is the event that separates, at last and forever, those who trusted the lights from the One Who orders them.

**The First Fold and the Last Sentence**

Finally, the verse must be heard inside the whole suspended period. "When the sun is enfolded" is not a completed thought — the Qur'an says *idhā*, *when*, and then keeps speaking eleven more times before the sentence's main verb arrives: *each soul shall know what it has made ready* (81:14). The sūrah's architecture performs its theology: the great cosmic events are not the story's end; they are the clearing of the stage. The sun is folded so that the scrolls can be opened (81:10); the lights are out because the judgment seat is about to be lit — *"when the Hellfire is kindled and the Garden brought near"* (81:12–13). Every fold in the heavens serves one unveiling: what each soul has in its hands. The first verse of al-Takwīr therefore already contains the whole sūrah's moral direction — the cosmos will not stand as your excuse, because the cosmos was never the point; you are. What will not be folded away, in the end, is you and what you sent ahead.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:2

> **and when the stars fade away;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word for Turbidity and Spilling**

The verb *inkadarat* comes from the root *k-d-r*, which describes what happens when a clear thing turns turbid: water clouded, milk thinned with water, the bloom of youth fading from a face — the dictionaries use exactly that phrase, *inkadara al-shāb*, the young man lost his freshness. The same root's opposite, *ṣafāʾ*, is purity and brightness; stars, the purest visible lights, are here described in the language of milk gone watery and wine gone muddy. Two further senses recorded by al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr widen the image: *inkadara* also means to spill out, the way the contents of a overturned container pour away; and it can mean to fall flat upon the ground. So the stars do not merely go dark. Their light is clouded, their order pours out of the vessel of the sky, and what pours falls. Three verses in the first fifteen lines of this sūrah have already shown a theme — things forced into states alien to their natures: the sun wrapped like a turban, the stars spilled like cloudy milk. This is apocalypse described as the spoiling of everything at its freshest.

The same clause, word for word — *"and when the stars fade away"* — stands at 77:8, in Sūrah al-Mursalāt, and again at 82:2, in the very next sūrah in the muṣḥaf, al-Infitār. That triple repetition across three of the earliest revealed chapters is itself a commentary: the first Muslim hearers were made to hear the stars' dissolution so often that it became one of the fixed images of their faith, memorized before it was ever explained.

**Lamps, Missiles, and Guides — What Exactly Is Lost**

To feel the weight of verse 2, the Qur'an's own inventory of the stars' offices must be laid out. They are adornment and ammunition: *"Indeed, We have adorned the nearest heaven with an adornment of stars, and as a protection against every rebellious devil"* (37:6–7), and *"We adorned the nearest heaven with lamps, and made them missiles for the devils, and have prepared for them the punishment of the Blaze"* (67:5; cf. 41:12). And they are navigation: *"And it is He Who has set for you the stars so that you may guide yourselves by them in the darknesses of the land and the sea"* (6:97). Ibn ʿAbbās — in the wording al-Tirmidhī transmitted and judged *ḥasan* — gathered the same offices into a single saying of the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"God created the stars for three things: as an adornment for the lowest heaven, as missiles for the devils, and as signs by which one is guided. Whoever interprets them otherwise, he has spoken by mere opinion, lost his portion, and said of God what he has no right to say."* (al-Tirmidhī)

The verse at 81:2 extinguishes the three offices one by one. The lamps that adorned the sky go murky; the missiles that guarded the unseen realm lose their flight — and with them, the last bar between heaven and eavesdropping devils, which the sūrah itself will mention two oaths later; the signs that oriented travelers in darkness scatter, and the traveler's sun is already folded. Everything the sky was *for* is being revoked.

**The Death of Astrology, Announced in Its Own Idiom**

The stars that pre-Islamic Arabs consulted were not inert lights. Tribes attributed rain, fortune, birth-hours, and war-outcomes to them — the practice of *al-taʾlīm*, casting lots by the rising of particular stars — and the Prophet ﷺ counted it among the four survivals of paganism in his community, alongside boasting of lineage, belief in omens, and ritual wailing (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah). The God who swears by the stars in verse 15, and who here folds and spills them, has written the whole verdict on astrology into the physics of the Day: that which can be switched off cannot be a lord; that which guides only while the Owner lends it light is a signpost, not a source. The Qur'an even structures its argument around precisely this distinction. Abraham's star — the very object of astrological hope — was the first proof against it: *"When he saw a star rising, he said, 'This is my Lord'; but when it set, he said, 'Indeed, I do not love that which sets'"* (6:76). Al-Takwīr applies Abraham's grammar to every star at once: when they *all* set at once, forever, the love misplaced on them is exposed as a debt.

There is a subtle literary justice here, too, which the classical commentators savored. Soothsayers and star-readers claimed their information came through jinn who eavesdrop on the heavenly orders. In this sūrah, the heavenly orders — the stars — are the first to be dissolved, and the objects of the oath are the stars as they *obey*. What the soothsayer's trade needed (stable, speaking heavens) is what the Day will not leave standing.

**The Spilled Vessel: A Picture of Human Hope**

Read the image psychologically, as the sūrah demands, and *inkadarat* says something about the inner life of every age, not just the night sky of camel-riders. Human beings hang their hopes on what seems fixed above them — dynasties, fortunes, careers, reputations, the bright bodies of this world's admiration. These are a culture's stars: you steer by them. The verse announces the night the steering light pours itself out and turns to turbid water. The sailor who trusted the stars alone is lost the moment they blur; the sailor who trusted the Lord of the stars still has the compass of the fitrah in his hand, and the Qur'an's chart in front of him. This is why, in the sūrah's own moral world, the folding of cosmos and the opening of the soul-book (81:10) belong to one sentence: the outer lights go out only because the inner ones are being finally examined.

The commentators add one quiet consolation inside the terror, which belongs to this verse's other half. The same sky that will be stripped of its stars will be renewed: *"On the Day when the earth is changed to another earth, and the heavens as well"* (14:48). The stars' fading is not annihilation of creation but its dissolution into a second draft — the removal of the old adornment because a better and permanent one is coming, and the sūrah names that permanent scene with its own next breath: *"and when the Garden is brought near"* (81:13). The stars fade; the Garden comes. What goes out is what was never meant to last; what is kindled will never be folded.

**The Verse in the Life of the Listener**

The first Muslim who heard *"and when the stars fade away"* was a desert man who read the night the way city men read a clock or a map. The verse reached him at the joint where his competence ended: the loss of the stars meant the loss of his way home in every sense — navigation, weather-lore, the whole night-lit literacy of his people. That is the prophetic method in the Makkan sūrahs: meet each audience at its deepest dependency and unfold it there. For the modern listener the dependency shifts but the logic is identical — each person should ask what his "stars" are: the things he uses to fix his bearings and to measure the world's sanity. The Day, al-Takwīr says, begins by asking each of us to watch those lights go out one after another, and it will not end there, because you will still be standing, and the Book will have been opened. What you did under those borrowed lights — that is the question the fading stars exist to raise.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:3

> **when the mountains are set in motion,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mountains That Are Made to Journey**

The verb *suyyirat* is the causative passive of *sāra* — to travel, to set out on a journey; the *sīrah* that biographers write is the track of a man's journeying. So the mountains are not merely destroyed in this verse; they are put on the road. What we see as the most stubbornly stationary of things — stone older than memory, fixed as the word "fixed" itself — is described in the act of walking. The Qur'an elsewhere lifts this from a metaphor of the Hour into a description of the world as it currently is: *"And you see the mountains and think them firmly set, but they are passing as the clouds pass — the workmanship of God, Who has perfected all things"* (27:88). Several of the great commentators, al-Qurṭubī among them, note that some of the early readers took that verse to mean the mountains are already in motion in this world, moving with the earth's own motion, the way the clouds they are likened to drift; if that is so, then verse 3 does not impose motion on a still world but removes the veil of apparent stillness. On either reading, one meaning dominates: stillness was never the mountains' property; it was a lease.

**The Pegs Are Pulled: The Tent Unstruck**

The Qur'an's cosmology gives mountains a named function, and the imagery of this sūrah honors it exactly. *"Have We not made the earth a resting place, and the mountains as pegs?"* (78:6–7) — *awtād*, tent-pegs; *"And He has set upon the earth mountains standing firm, lest it should shift with you"* (16:15); *"And We placed within the earth firmly set mountains, lest it should sway"* (21:31). The world, in these verses, is a tent; the earth is its spreading hide; the mountains are the pegs driven through its edges to keep it from billowing. Now watch the order of al-Takwīr's first three verses as the order of striking a tent: first the canopy comes down — the sun folded, the stars spilled, the roof-lights removed; and only then are the pegs pulled — the mountains set in motion. A bedouin audience needed no further vocabulary for what the Day is: the tent of the world, taken down at the end of its appointed night, in the very sequence in which a tent is unstruck.

And once the pegs are gone, the hide of the earth trembles out flat: *"They ask you of the mountains, on what day they will be moved — so My Lord will scatter them as dust, and leave the earth as a level plain, in which you will see neither hollow nor ridge"* (20:105–106). *"The Day when the mountains will be as carded wool"* (70:9) — wool teased out by the carder's iron, holding nothing together. *"People will be as scattered moths, and the mountains as dyed wool"* (101:4–5), the two images in one breath: mankind swarming in blind panic, and what was solid reduced to fluff. The sūrah at 78 warns them of the hour when *"the trumpet will be blown and you will come forth in crowds, and the sky will be opened and become gates, and the mountains will be carried off and become a mirage"* (78:19–20) — *sarāban*, a mirage: the thing that promised water from afar and gives nothing at the spot. That is what the mountains will have turned out to be, in the final accounting of the human heart, if anything else was trusted to quench it.

**Why This Verse Sting**

The force of verse 3 lies in what mountains have always signified to human beings: permanence as such. Proverbs across the languages say of a man that his resolve is "like a rock," his estate "like a mountain"; grief is a mountain on the chest; the child's drawing of "the solid things of the world" would place mountains in it. The Qur'an uses the very same symbol in the opposite direction — not to deny the mountains' strength, which it celebrates as one of the signs, but to reserve ultimacy for their Maker. Hence the verse's quiet logic: if that is what happens to the strongest thing you can see, then you have been misgrading strength, and you have been building on it. The Day of Judgment is, among its other functions, a correction of estimates: the sun, the stars, the mountains were overrated by their beholders, and the re-rating begins with their bodies.

There is an irony that the sūrah itself draws out with the sharpest of its verbs, and it belongs here. God says of the Qur'an: *"Had there been a Qur'an with which the mountains were set in motion (ṣayyirat) — or the earth were torn asunder, or the dead made to speak — nay, but all command belongs to God"* (13:31). The same causative root — the mountains *made to move* — stands in both verses, and the comparison is withering: the mountains are on the road before the recited Word at every recitation, in every age, while the hearts that are softer than stone, hearing the same Word, refuse to move. The mountains will be driven off in the Hour; they are already journeying toward the glory of God in the order of creation; only man, addressed by speech he can comprehend, remains as he was. The mountains' moving on the Day is thus also an argument for the Qur'an's power that the Makkan listeners had refused: what no mountain can resist, the human breast resisted — and the Day will fold that difference away.

**The Level Ground at Last**

Finally, the commentators noticed what the dust of the mountains leaves behind: not chaos, but plainness. *"They will follow the Caller, from whom there is no crookedness and no depression"* (20:107) — a ground with no hollow and no rise, no shade and no hiding-place. Every fold of that landscape in which men had concealed themselves — every valley of refuge, every height of defense, every shadow in which the wrong was done unseen — is flattened at once, in the same stroke that flattens the peaks. This is the verse's moral hinge: the mountains were also the earth's cover; their removal is the end of cover. The world's whole architecture of hiding and being hidden — distance, elevation, darkness behind the ridge — is demolished so that the scene can be one open court under one eye, and every soul standing on level ground with nothing between it and its deeds but what it sent ahead. That is why, when the people of Makkah heard *"when the mountains are set in motion,"* they heard a verse about themselves in particular: a town of granite hills and rock-hewn strongholds, whose people trusted the mountain of their power as much as any tribesman ever trusted stone. "The Day," the sūrah tells them, "is the day the rock walks — and with it, everything you thought was rock."

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:4

> **and when pregnant camels are abandoned;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Precious Thing, Named Exactly**

Arabic has dozens of precise words for the camel at every stage of its life, and the Qur'an selects, for the economy of collapse, the single most valuable object in that entire vocabulary. *ʿIshār* are she-camels in their tenth month of pregnancy — the lexicographers derive the word from *ʿashr*, "ten," and the choice of the tenth month fixes the value: a camel at the end of gestation is worth double, perhaps more, because the camel and the coming calf are present in the belly at once. This is the animal, too, on which the Bedouin household actually ran: its milk fed the family for a season, its hair was woven into the tent, its wool into garments, its young were wealth itself, its back was the transport, its speed the defense. The zakāt on camels set the scale of purity for the entire fiscal system of Islam, and blood-money and dowries were long counted in herds. The pregnant she-camel — red, choice, ten months along — was not an asset among assets; for her owner she was the concentrated form of his lifetime of care. Ibn ʿAbbās glossed *al-ʿishār* simply: "the best of camels, and the most precious of their possessions."

Then the verb. *ʿUshilat*, from a root whose meaning is to be left neglected, uncared for, abandoned to itself — the commentators render it *turikat*, "left." Not stolen, not lost, not traded away. Left standing there, in the condition of maximum value, by the owner himself, walking away.

**Why Value Itself Stops Counting**

The terror of the scene is not that the camels are gone. It is that no one cares that they are going. The sūrah teaches here something psychology has confirmed from every age of disaster: under a sufficiently total threat, the hierarchy of wants collapses from the top down. A man will carry his child before his papers; he will set down the papers at the sound of the fire before he sets down the child. Panic has a geometry: the most abstract, most deferred, most "stored" goods — the portfolio, the vault, the prize herd — are released first, because they are furthest from the breath. So the verse puts the release of the released-at-last at the center of the picture. If you wish to know how absolute the Day is, do not ask whether people flee; ask what they drop — and know that what this sūrah's audience would have died before dropping, they will drop mid-stride, without turning their heads.

This is exactly the inversion that the sūrah's own chronology completes. When wealth — the thing the world was organized *for* — is abandoned, every organization that ran on it ends with it: bargains, alliances, marriages arranged on dowries, feuds fed by camels, pride in herds. The economy of Makkah, which ran the caravans, will look back at itself from that Day as a man in a flood looks back at the counting-house: everything in its place, and the water through it. The Qur'an fixes the law of the moment elsewhere in a sentence that belongs here by right: *"On the Day when the man flees from his brother, and his mother and his father, and his wife and his children — on that Day, every person will have enough to occupy him"* (80:34–37). If family itself cannot hold a man when the trumpet sounds, a camel — however red, however heavy with promise — held him not at all.

**A Word-Play of the Language Itself**

Classical rhetoricians savored a sound in this verse that translation necessarily loses. *ʿIshār* — the ten-month camels — sits within a single breath of *ʿishq*, passion, and the verb that abandons them, *ʿushilat*, is the same letters changed only in their pointing. The most-desired thing, in the most-desired word, becomes the most-abandoned thing. Whether or not one wishes to call it intentional art, its effect on the Arab ear was total: the sūrah took the vocabulary of love and turned it inside out to describe the end of loving. The beloved is not lost; worse — she is left, standing in her best condition, while the heart no longer needs what her belly holds. And the scene's echo reaches beyond the camels. *ʿUshilat* was used by some early readers of a rarer opinion — Ibn Zayd among them — for the rain-clouds (the *muʿṣirāt* of 78:14), so that even the sky's pregnant wealth, the clouds heavy with the desert's real life-blood, is abandoned and let go. On that reading the verse does not single out an Arabian luxury but completes a cosmic picture: everything that was "with young" in creation — the sun's day, the sea's storms, the cloud's rain, the camel's calf — is delivered up at the one moment when no creature wants anything any more.

**The Hadith That the Verse Reads**

To the abandoned treasure the Prophet's teaching adds two companions in memory, one of which the verse explicates. From Anas, in the two Ṣaḥīḥs:

> *"The Hour will not come until a man passes by a grave and says: 'Would that I were in his place.'"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim)

The scene of verse 4, transposed into its full form — and the verse explicates. The wish spoken at the roadside grave is not a longing to meet God; the Prophet ﷺ forbade the desire for death outright, and this wish, set beside that prohibition, is plainly the world's collapse read out loud by a man who has just walked away from everything he owned and loved. Disasters so total that the most precious property is walked away from, and griefs so deep that the man walking away from his camels eyes the grave beside the road and envies its occupant. And at the very moment the sūrah paints that, the same Law closes the door on the wish it breeds: *"Let none of you deliberately wish for death because of calamity that has come upon him; if he must, let him say: 'O God, keep me alive as long as life is better for me, and take me when death is better for me.'"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Anas). The tension is instructive: the Day will exceed the human being's capacity to bear the loss of his ʿishār, and the mercy of the Sharīʿah forbids his anticipatory collapse before it arrives. The sūrah describes the extremity; the Prophet disciplines the heart long before the extremity tests it. Both belong to the lesson.

**What the Listener Is Told, in His Own Currency**

The Makkan was addressed in the idiom of his maximum; the universal principle underneath is that each hearer has an *ʿishār*. Every age and every person has the asset they would never dream of losing, the one their plans sit on like a tent over its peg: the pension and the portfolio, the house and the reputation, the tenure, the harvest, the savings account, the thing one says "secures my future." The verse is not against these things as such — they are the milk and the wool of the world, and Islam legislated their care down to the zakāt on camels. The verse is against the condition of the heart in which they stop being held and start holding: in which the tenth-month camel, as the Prophet ﷺ warned of the other direction, becomes the thing over which a man's whole interior life is arranged. For that arrangement there is one test and it is not fatal: what would you still hand over at the sound of alarm? The answer to that is the soul's true inventory. On the Day, the ʿishār stand abandoned in the open; and what no one will have abandoned, whatever it cost, are the deeds that were given away for God — the only wealth that walks with its owner when everything else is left. *"When a man dies, his deeds end except for three: ongoing charity, knowledge benefited by, and a righteous child who prays for him"* (Muslim 1631). In the economy of that Day, the camel abandoned was loss; the charity abandoned to God's account was the only deposit left standing when the balance was read out.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:5

> **when the wild beasts are gathered,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ungatherables Gathered**

The *wuḥūsh* — wild beasts — take their name in Arabic from *waḥsha*, wilderness and estrangement; they are the creatures by definition untameable, the opposite of the *uns*, the familiar, that governs the camel, the goat, and the household. No herder gathers lions; no horn calls tigers to the plain; the very idea of assembling all the wild things of the earth into one place, in one act, is an impossibility that only the Day could fulfill. And yet here it stands in a sūrah that keeps doing exactly this — forcing into one another whatever nature holds apart: the seas meeting, the souls coupling, the stars falling against their stations. Each of the twelve verbs of the first section breaks one deep habit of the created order; this verse breaks the habit of flight itself. Every creature that ever ran from another will be caught at last in the one assembly from which no creature runs.

The verse's companion in the Book tells why the gathering matters: *"There is no creature on the earth, nor bird that flies with its wings, but that they are communities (umam) like you. Nothing have We omitted in the Book. Then unto their Lord they will be gathered"* (6:38). The animals are *umam* — communities, the same word used for nations — and they, like the nations, will be gathered to their Lord. On that Day *"you will see every community kneeling"* (45:28), the commentators note, and the verse's "every community" does not exempt the beast. The scene the sūrah opens is thus wider than mankind: the whole moral ecology of creation files into court.

**Justice for the Hornless Sheep**

Why would beasts without religious obligation be assembled for judgment? The Prophet ﷺ answered that question in a hadith the great commentators recite at this very verse. In Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim:

> *"Indeed, God will gather the first and the last of creation on a Day that does not pass — and He will judge between them, so that the animals see it with their own eyes. Indeed, God will give the hornless against the horned, and he will take retribution for it; until when all have been requited, He says: 'Be you dust!' And then the disbeliever says: 'Would that I were dust.'"* (Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah)

The sūrah at 78:40 quotes that final cry of the disbeliever — *"Would that I were dust!"* — and the commentators, al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr among them, point at the connection the animals' judgment creates: the beasts, once fully requited, are made dust, and the disbeliever, seeing even the beasts dismissed in honor while he is led on to account, wishes he had been created among the animals that are finished. The animals' trial exists, in other words, partly as an exhibit in yours. The logic is *a fortiori*, and it is devastating: if the horned goat that butted the hornless ewe is not allowed to die unpunished by its victim, then no oppressor of human beings, no buyer of the pregnant camel, no king, no buried-daughter's father, no whisperer of lies and no hoarder of gold arrives at the end of the road unpunished either. The justice that misses nothing in the animal world is the floor, not the ceiling, of what mankind should expect.

**A Rehearsal for the Weight of Small Things**

The gathering of beasts puts a mirror before every human being who has ever lived with beasts, and the sūrah's first audience — herders, riders, owners of camels whose milk was their breakfast — looked straight into it. The Prophet's community knew their animals as employees, not ornaments; and precisely for that reason the Day's audit includes them:

> *"A woman was punished in a prison of Hell for a cat she had tied up, neither feeding it nor letting it eat of the vermin of the earth, until it died."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar)

And against that terror the other mercy of the same register: the man who watered a panting dog and was thanked for it from Heaven (al-Bukhārī and Muslim), and the Prophet's ﷺ verdict on sport: *"Do not take any living thing as a target"* (Muslim), and the warning that whoever shoots even a sparrow without right — for food, and not for sport — will answer for it at the Day when sparrows themselves stand as witnesses against the idle hand (Aḥmad and al-Nasāʾī). These texts, hung on the scene of verse 5, teach a psychology of hidden motives that no civil law can reach: what a person does to a creature that can never sign a contract, file a complaint, or matter to his reputation, is the truest assay of what he is. The tyrant's crimes begin as a thousand small insolences that seemed answerable to no one; the righteous soul is trained first by a thousand small mercies that no one would have missed. The Day will gather both registers, and every voiceless thing that carried a grievance will find its voice.

**The Court of the Creatures: What It Means to Be Addressed**

There is a final, quieter depth in the verse. The beasts are gathered, judged, and dismissed as dust; the humans are gathered, judged, and made to endure. Why the difference? Because the beasts were created as a community of the enabled — given appetite and instinct, but never an address; while mankind was created as the community of the addressed — given appetite and instinct and then the line of revelation that runs, in this very sūrah, from verse 19 onward. The animals' trial closes the circle of the world's unconscious suffering; man's trial opens into the only question that has ever truly been posed to him — did he answer the call, remember the Reminder (81:27), will the willing of the straight path (81:28)? Even the beast will know, on that Day, what it was made ready to do; only the human being is asked to have known it already, from the Book in front of him. That is the weight the Makkan listeners should have felt at verse 5: even the gazelle has its lawyer; and his own Advocate — the Messenger the sūrah will defend, verse 22 onward — was standing in the same room, speaking.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:6

> **and when the seas are made to swell over,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Verb, Two Elements**

*Sujjirat* is the verse's hinge, and classical lexicography gives it two hands. In one, it belongs to *sijr* — the floodwater that rises beyond its banks and runs its course of destruction; from the same root God says of Noah's deluge: *"We opened the gates of the sky with water pouring down, and We caused the earth to gush forth in springs; and the water met for a matter already decreed"* (54:11). In the other, *sujjirat* is tied to *sajīr* — the kindled, roaring furnace; and this second meaning is confirmed in the Qur'an's own parallel passage, *"and when the seas are set ablaze"* — *wa al-baḥr al-masjūr* (52:6). Several of the earliest authorities, Ibn ʿAbbās among them as al-Ṭabarī reports, took the verse literally: the seas themselves become fire. Others took the fire figuratively or consequentially: waters heaped and boiling over as if aflame. That the two meanings coexist in one word is no confusion but the verse's design: on that Day the very medium of extinguishing becomes the medium of burning — element betrays element, water behaves like fire, and what the world uses to quench, God uses to punish. The Study-edition readers of old summed it up in the tersest line: when the seas, by their nature cool and wet, catch fire, what else in creation can claim permanence?

**The Barrier Dissolved**

The seas in the Qur'an are not just water; they are a constitutional arrangement. *"He released the two seas meeting side by side; between them is a barrier they do not transgress"* (55:19–20); *"It is He who has released the two seas, one fresh and sweet, one salty and bitter, and He placed between them a barrier and a prohibiting ban"* (25:53). The two seas' refusal to mix — a mercy for the drinker, a mechanism for the fish, a marvel for the observer — is one of the Book's standing signs of legislation in nature: the world runs on appointed limits (*qadar*), and its obedience to limits is itself the proof of a Lawgiver. Verse 6 breaks the arrangement. When the seas are *sujjirat*, they swell past the very thing that held them; 82:3 gives the same event its other name — *"and when the seas are made to burst forth"* (*fujjirat*) — water escaping its sealed reserves, the deep's locked doors flung open. The Qur'an names one historical precedent so the hearer knows the type of event: the Flood, when the same two verbs — sky pouring, earth springing — met for Noah's people, and *"the water rode them"* (69:11): *"Indeed, when the water rose, We carried you in the sailing ship, so that the one who hears may remember, and the attentive ear may hold it"* (69:11–12). Every sea in history is a rehearsed verse; the Day of al-Takwīr is its final recitation.

**Ink and Ashes: What the Seas Were Really For**

And then the Qur'an performs on the seas an operation that belongs to this verse's deepest layer. Twice God sets all of ocean against His words, and twice the ocean loses: *"Say: If the sea were ink for the words of my Lord, the sea would run dry before the words of my Lord are exhausted — even if We brought the like of it as more ink"* (18:109); *"And if whatever trees on earth were pens and the sea [were ink], replenished by seven seas beside it, the words of God would not be exhausted"* (31:27). Read beside *"when the seas are set ablaze,"* the arithmetic becomes a sermon. The sea is this world's symbol of inexhaustible resource — the treasury of the earth's table, the road of its commerce, the vault of its uncounted wealth. Yet as ink it runs dry before the speech of God is complete; as fuel it burns; as water it overflows. In a single image the sūrah retires all three careers of the sea: ledger, treasury, and flood. What does not run out, does not boil, and does not spill — what remains of all the world's liquidity — is only the Word: *"All that is upon it perishes, and the Face of your Lord will remain, full of Majesty and Honor"* (55:26–27). The Makkan merchant, whose whole horizon was caravan, store-house, and sea-lane, heard verse 6 as an auctioneer's hammer over his entire category of things.

**Why the Seas Belong in the Sequence at All**

Set the verse in the sūrah's own order and its rigor shows: the sun, engine of light (v. 1); the stars, lamps and guides (v. 2); the mountains, pegs of stability (v. 3); the herd, the movable wealth (v. 4); the beasts, the living order beyond man (v. 5); and now the seas, the engines of the earth's weather, the reservoirs of rain, the pantry of nations, the very reason the land is habitable at all. Each verse removes one of the world's supporting functions; together they describe the deactivation of the machine of the world as a whole, part by part — illumination, orientation, anchoring, provision, ecology, climate — as deliberately as an engineer shutting down a system. The sea is the last of these before the sūrah turns, at verse 7, from the outer frame to the soul itself: once climate fails, the only weather left is the storm inside every human breast, and the verses that follow go directly to it. There is also a psychology in the choice, plain enough to name: of all nature's large presences, the sea is the one human beings have always loved to fear and feared to love — the mother of fortune and the eater of sailors. When it swells over its banks, the hearer's inner sea swells with it; the Qur'an, throughout its Makkan register, knows that outer terrors move the soul chiefly by proxy. The seas boil, and the eyes fill — and then verse 7 says: this was never about the water.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:7

> **when the souls are coupled,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb of Yoking**

*ʿUqirat* comes from a root whose governing sense is joining one thing to another: the Arabs yoked (*ʿaqara*) two beasts side by side at the water, and the lexicographers — Ibn Fāris among them — report the root *ʿ-q-r* precisely as "to pair, to couple, to join." The passive verb of the verse leaves the pairing open at first hearing: the souls are *ʿuqirat* — matched up, coupled to something — and the listeners were left, for the moment, not knowing to what. That openness is the verse's rhetorical device: after six verses of the world being dismantled outside the human being, verse 7 moves inside and says: now the human contents of the world will be sorted into pairs. The commentators did not agree on a single object of the coupling, and their disagreement, far from weakening the verse, displays its range.

**The Four Couplings the Ancients Saw**

First: the coupling of souls with bodies. Some of the early authorities read *"when the souls are coupled"* as the reunion of each departing soul with its reconstituted body at the resurrection — the moment man becomes, once more, a unity of clay and breath, a single defendant. Second: the coupling described in the language of marriage. The same verb *zawwaja* — to wed, to pair — which the Qur'an uses of the people of Paradise — *"Thus it shall be; and We shall wed them to wide-eyed maidens"* (44:54); *"Reclining on couches lined with brocade, and We shall wed them to wide-eyed maidens"* (52:20) — is, on this reading, what *ʿuqirat* signifies: each believer joined eternally to his or her purged companion. Third, and darkly symmetrical: the coupling of the misbelieving souls with the satans who were their intimates here — *"And whoever is blinded from remembrance of the All-Merciful, We assign to him a devil; then it shall be a companion (qarīn) to him"* (43:36) — the friendship chosen in this life sealed into a marriage without divorce in that one. Fourth — and the line the evidence pulls most strongly — the coupling of like with like: the Day whose other names are *yawm al-faṣl*, the Day of Sorting (37:21), the Day of Gathering (64:9), separates the categories that worldly life blended together, and matches each soul to the company whose inner shape its own resembled. This last sense is what a famous report — transmitted among the Successors and cited at this verse by al-Ṭabarī's line of commentators — takes as the verse's meaning: *"Every man will be coupled with every person who did the like of what he did."*

**The Law of Spiritual Affinity**

The sūrah thus hands us, in four words, a complete theory of why the last judgment will be just without needing to be arbitrary: on that Day no one is placed anywhere he was not already fitted. Coupling like to like is not a sentence from outside but a disclosure from inside. The hadith teaches the same law in its gentlest form. Anas reported that a man asked the Prophet ﷺ about the Hour, and the Prophet ﷺ asked him, *"What have you prepared for it?"* He said: the love of God and His Messenger. He said:

> *"You will be with the one you loved."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim)

The hadith's shock is in its calm: the ultimate destiny-assignment of a human being is a function of affinity — whom, and what, the heart has actually attached itself to. And the same rule runs through the Qur'an's account of that Day in both directions. For the lovers in God: pairing in gardens. For the lovers of the world, made blind by it, the pairing announced: *"Gather together the wrongdoers and their pairs (azwājahum) and whatever they were worshipping beside God"* (37:22) — the idols, the appetites, and the human allies who encouraged them, all yoked as one team. And for friendships founded on anything other than God: *"Intimate friends (al-akhillāʾ) on that Day are enemies to one another, except the reverent"* (43:67); *"The wrongdoer will gnaw at his hands, saying: Oh, would that I had taken a path with the Messenger! Oh, woe is me! Would that I had not taken so-and-so as a friend! He led me astray from the Reminder after it had come to me"* (25:27–29). The verse, read against this library, is a warning dressed as an event: every alliance you form is a thread you are spinning toward your own coupling; choose the company and you have begun to choose the destination. This is why the Prophet ﷺ made the rule of the *ʿuqra* practical at the start of everything: *"A person is upon the religion of his intimate friend — so let each of you look at who he befriends"* (Aḥmad, al-Tirmidhī, Abū Dāwūd — graded *ḥasan*). His own image for the two pairs of company was the one every Makkan souk knew by its smell: *"The company of the good and the evil is like the seller of musk and the smith's bellows: the musk-seller may anoint you, or you may buy from him, or you will at least leave his gathering smelling good; the blacksmith's sparks may burn your clothes, and you will at least leave his place smelling bad"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim).

**Bodies, Breaths, and the Sorting You Are Building Now**

Two practical reflections close the verse's circle. First, the physical: the classical readers who took *ʿuqirat* of the soul's reunion with the body grounded in it the Qur'an's repeated argument against the resurrection-deniers — *"Say: The Angel of Death, who is entrusted with you, will take your souls; then to your Lord you will be returned"* (32:11). Coupling soul to body, the verse says, is the resurrection in miniature of every self that ever scattered at death; the re-yoking will be as complete as the undoing was. Second, the moral: if like is joined to like, then this life — before any verdict — is already a sorting process. Every consent is a coupling: every "yes" to a truth, a kindness, a companion, a habit of restraint, yokes the soul nearer to its like; every "yes" to a flattery, a theft, an easy cruelty, yokes it nearer to the devils, the Qur'an says in its own chilling physics: *"Do you not see that We send the devils against the disbelievers, urging them on"* (19:83). The Day of Coupling, therefore, is not the moment God begins to classify you; it is the moment He finishes — and announces to everyone what you and your choices had already composed. When, later in this sūrah, the scrolls are spread (v. 10), the reader will discover that his book and his company are the same thing written two ways: the deeds and the souls that matched them.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:8

> **and when the female infant buried alive is asked**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Practice the Verse Names**

*Al-maw'ūda* — the "buried-alive girl" — names one of the documented horrors of pre-Islamic Arabia: *wa'd*, the live burial of newborn daughters. The word itself is loaded: the lexicographers derive *wa'd* from *wa'ada*, to promise or to defer, and the practice received precisely that name — a death deferred by arrangement, the father "promising" the daughter to the ground until her time arrived. Ibn ʿAbbās described the mechanics in a report transmitted by Aḥmad and in the Sunan collections and graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by Ibn Ḥibbān: a man whose daughter was near her delivery — for in some of their customs the burial waited until birth rather than being done at once — would dig for her, and when the child was born he would bury her alive; Ibn al-Musayyab, hearing the narration, is reported to have wept. Other accounts preserved in the histories of the Arabs tell of men killing newborn daughters out of dread of poverty, of fathers entombing them to escape the disgrace of a daughter's capture in a raid, of mothers hiding their female infants. The Qur'an in Sūrah al-Naḥl paints the psychology that produced it: *"And when one of them is given news of a girl, his face darkens, and he is choked with grief. He hides from the people because of the evil of what he has been told. Shall he keep her in humiliation, or press her into the dust? Evil, evil is what they decide"* (16:58–59). Al-Takwīr does not repeat the scene's description. It does something more terrible: it turns the courtroom around.

**The Grammar of Overturned Power**

Notice what the sūrah grants her that the world withheld: a voice in the public square of the Last Day, and an interrogator's seat. *Su'ilat* — *she* is asked. In the grammar of this first section, every verb has been passive, every subject patient — the sun folded, the stars spilled, the beasts gathered — and here the pattern continues in a way that changes everything: the most patient of all the patients, the infant who could not plead, not speak, not even stand, is placed at the center of an inquiry. The passive form carries a precise theology: her agency is being given her by God Himself. In this world no court received her case; no scribe enrolled her grievance; no law recognized her standing. On that Day she is the questioner's object in the very sense that makes her the questioner — and the real defendant, the father who filled in the earth, hears his verdict inside the question asked of her lips. This is what the commentators mean when they observe that *her* interrogation is the interrogation *of the killer*: a question that expects no information — for what sin could an infant have? — but expects an exposure. Every buried child of every century is standing in this verse, and every unburied injustice with her.

**Why the Qur'an Picks This Crime as the Emblem**

Of the many crimes before the Day, the sūrah singles out not the famous violences of power but the quietest one: a family secret, an act with no witnesses, committed in a pit with a father's hands, invisible even to the law of the tribe. The choice is reasoned on several levels. First, it demonstrates the universality of divine justice — the Day will miss nothing, not even a crime the earth itself swallowed (the seas that vomit up their contents in verse 6, and the scrolls that are unrolled in verse 10, frame it as the crime that neither the drowning nor the silence of the earth will hide). Second, it strikes at the deepest root of injustice: the treatment of the human being as disposable property — the girl-child was, in the logic of the practice, precisely that: an economic liability to be erased, an honor-risk to be extinguished. God's answer to a culture that killed the powerless by making the powerless the loud question of the cosmos. Third, the crime's very silence made it the truest test of a soul, and therefore the most reliable indicator of the world that needed changing: a society is measured not by how it sings of its heroes but by what it does with its least members. When the Qur'an halts the entire apocalyptic sequence here — sun, stars, mountains, camels, beasts, seas all stand still while a girl is asked a question — it is teaching the scale of the scales: in God's arithmetic, one child's life outweighs the order of the cosmos, and the cosmos is in fact being dismantled around the fact.

**The Verdict of ʿAqīdah Inside the Verse**

There is, as well, a single line in this scene against the pagans' theology of daughters. The Arabs who buried their girls in the dust had simultaneously — through their goddesses — called the angels "the daughters of God," assigning to their own Lord what they could not bear for themselves: *"For you the males, and for Him the females? That is indeed an unjust division"* (53:21–22). The Qur'an welds the two facts together at the end of Sūrah al-Najm — the practice and the piety exposed as one cowardice. And in al-Takwīr's own register, the buried daughter's question becomes the final argument against every idolatry: whatever gods a people set above the child, the child's case is opened first. Islam's counter-revolution is measurable in the same sūrah-cycle's elsewhere: the girl-child who was killed for being a mouth to feed became, in the Prophet's ﷺ legislation, the daughter whose maintenance was jihad, whose good treatment was a fortress from the Fire, whose raising was the road to Paradise beside the Messenger — a reversal the next verse completes with the words of the hadith al-Bukhārī and Muslim transmit: *"Whoever is tested by something of these daughters, and treats them well, they shall be a shield for him against the Fire."* The sūrah that buried nothing has not yet spoken its verdict on the burial; it asks the question first, and makes the reader's chest the courtroom where it is answered.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:9

> **for what sin she was slain;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Answers Itself**

*Bi-ayyi dhanbin qutilat* — "for what sin was she killed?" The interrogation is a masterpiece of divine courtroom rhetoric, because it is a question whose only possible answer is silence. An infant has no sins. The grammar of rebuke (*istifhām al-taqwīʿ*) is doing the whole work: she will "answer" by the very shape of the question — *for none; for no sin whatever; his daughter's only deed was being born* — and the absence of a defense becomes the presence of the verdict against the killer. The commentators, al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr among them, gloss it exactly so: she is asked in order that the one who killed her be asked. The crime that was committed without her ever speaking is now tried without his ever answering.

Note too the sūrah's verb: not "was buried," not "died" — *qutilat*, she was slain. The passive of the killing names the act as homicide and withholds the name of the murderer, because the indictment has already widened past individuals to a whole moral world; any father, any age, any custom that treats a human being as a disposal is on trial in the passive voice.

**The Moral Axiom Beneath the Question**

The verse's single sentence is one of the strongest statements in the Qur'an of the principle scholars call *fard al-taklīf*, the personalness of responsibility: no innocent soul bears, can bear, or may be made to bear another's guilt. *"And no bearer of burdens shall bear another's burden; and if a heavily-laden soul call upon [another] to carry its load, nothing of it will be carried, though it be a near relative"* (35:18); *"Whoever goes aright, goes aright only for his own soul, and whoever strays, strays only to its detriment; and no bearer of burdens shall bear another's burden. And We never punish until We have sent a Messenger"* (17:15). The girl was, by the Book's own definitions, incapable of burden: not yet addressed, not yet accountable, not yet a *mukallaf*. The Prophet ﷺ fixed the threshold in the ruling transmitted in the Sunan collections from ʿAlī: *"The pen has been lifted from three: the sleeper until he awakes, the child until he reaches puberty, and the one deprived of reason until he regains his mind."* God punishes no one before capacity and before message (17:15); the jahiliyyah buried a child *precisely because* she had no capacity to answer for herself — and the Day reverses the order: the one who could not answer is the one whose silence answers, and the one who received a message — every human being receives it — is the one who must.

And the hadith literature confirms how seriously the earliest Muslims took the crime's rank: the man who asked the Prophet ﷺ which sin was greatest was told, after associating partners with God: *"That you kill your child because he eats with you"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim — the exchange standing in the commentaries on the verse in al-Furqān: *"And those who do not invoke with God another deity, nor kill the soul which God has forbidden except for a right, nor commit adultery"* (25:68)). The motive named in the hadith — the child as an eating mouth — is the exact arithmetic of *wa'd*: provision feared rather than trusted.

**The Two Verses That Unmake the Motive**

So the sūrah-cycle that ended infanticide did not only condemn the act; it dismantled its economics. *"And do not kill your children for fear of poverty — We provide for them and for you"* (17:31); and again, *"…nor kill your children for poverty's sake; We shall provide for you and for them"* (6:151). The two phrasings, side by side, are a lesson in divine pedagogy: where the fear is of a poverty that might come, God promises provision for the child first; where the poverty has already arrived, He promises it for both. The underlying syllogism is unanswerable to the father who buried his daughter for want: if you are the provider of your child — and no one claims to be — why does her mouth terrify you? And if God is the provider of both of you — and He says He is — why did you become His competitor in a murder? *Tawḥīd al-rizq*, the oneness of provision, is thus the theological cure of the social disease: infanticide is, at its root, a failure of belief in the Provider more than a failure of pity for the child — the sūrah strikes at the root.

**The Question the Verse Leaves in the Room**

Finally, the two verses — eight and nine — should be read as a verdict not only on Makkah of the seventh century but on every arrangement, in every century, by which the convenient powerful dispose of the inconvenient powerless: the girl child aborted as the sex she is, the elder finished off by the "kindness" of neglect, the underclass whose deaths are booked as statistics, the buried scandals of institutions. The Day's first legal act in the human court is not the weighing of the great but the unburying of the smallest. God does not open with the tyrants' thrones; He opens with the pit, and with the question spoken softly into the ear of every father and every civilization, which is really spoken into the ears of the judges: *for what sin?* — and the only reply the Day will accept from the defendant is the one the child never had to give: *for none.* Whoever hears that question in this life and arranges his life to survive it — protecting what he can never justify killing — has already begun to prepare a scroll that, when spread, will embarrass no one.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:10

> **when the pages are spread,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word for Unrolling**

*Naṭishat* is one of the rarest verbs in the Qur'anic lexicon, built on a stem the old lexicographers gloss simply as *al-nashṭ*, stretching a thing out: the herder *naṭasha* the skin he had rolled for storage, spreading it taut upon the ground; the scribe's scroll, rolled tight for the treasury, is *naṭhasha* — unfurled — for reading. So the verse's picture is precise: the record of your life is not newly manufactured on the Day; it is *unrolled*. It has existed the whole time, folded, stored, sealed — and now the folds come out one by one, the way the turbaned sun will not come out, because this is the scroll the Day is actually about. Al-Ṭabarī's reading stands as the consensus: the *ṣuḥuf* here are the books of deeds, spread open for their owners to read.

One feature of the Arabic deserves the vernacular reader's notice: the very same word, *ṣuḥuf*, names the earlier revelations — *"This is in the first pages, the pages of Moses and Abraham"* (87:18–19); *"Or has he not been told what is in the pages of Moses, and of Abraham who fulfilled? That no bearer of burdens shall bear the burden of another, and that man shall have nothing but what he strives for"* (53:36–39). In one word the Qur'an binds together what God sent down and what He will read out: the pages given to the life of man and the pages returned to the Judge of man are a single literature. Abraham's scroll and yours cite the same clauses — no bearer bears another's load — which is why verses 8–9 and verse 10 belong to one paragraph of the divine argument: the girl's question and the book's spreading are both appeals to the same written law, published in every revelation.

**The Keepers of the Copy**

How the record is written the Qur'an does not leave to poetry. *"Indeed, over you are guardians, noble recorders, who know whatever you do"* (82:10–12); *"When the two receivers receive, seated on the right and on the left. A man utters no word but with him a watcher, ready"* (50:17–18); *"For each one are angels in succession, before him and behind him, guarding him by God's command"* (13:11). The imagery is chancery-exact: not one recorder but two, a right and a left; not observation alone but *ḥafaza*, guarding, the same verb used of treasuries; and the angels are *kiram, katibin* — noble and precise — so that the scribes themselves disqualify any suspicion of slander in what they write. The Book that legislates the record also vouches for the recorders. This is the logic of verse 10's terror and its mercy in one: nothing small escapes, *"a book that leaves nothing out, small or great, but [includes] it"* — *"And the record will be placed, and you will see the criminals in dread of what is in it, saying: O woe to us — what sort of record is this, that leaves nothing small or great except that it has enumerated it? And they find present what they did; and your Lord wrongs no one"* (18:49). The last clause is the verse's hidden floor: the perfect record exists precisely so that no one is wronged — totality is not vindictiveness; it is the technical condition of exact justice.

**Why the Scroll Is for the Reader, Not the Writer**

The deepest point of the spreading-pages is that God's court does not need the book for information. Whatever is written was never unknown to the Writer — *"Does he not know, He Who created, and He is the Subtle, the Aware?"* (67:14). The record exists, then, for the condemned: *"Read your own book; sufficient is your own self against you today as a reckoner"* (17:14). The theology here answers a perennial objection to resurrection-judgment — what can a court of an omniscient Judge add to His knowledge? — and the answer is moral psychology: verdicts that the accused cannot dispute require the accused's own testimony, and the Day is arranged as an evidence-procession in which every exhibit comes from the defendant's own hands. The scrolls do not inform God; they inform *us*, finally and without appeal, that our lives were real, that their contents counted, that nothing that we did was lost — the deepest reassurance of the verse, heard aright, is not the criminals' dread but the fact that no kindness and no cruelty in the whole history of the world ever evaporated unrecorded. Every tear of the wronged girl in verse 8 has a line in a scroll; every hidden mercy has a page.

**The Unrolled Scroll in the Age of the Permanent Record**

The Makkan hearer imagined his deeds as ink he could not see; modern listeners have had to live with the strange confirmation that the world itself has begun to keep copies: cameras on every corner, ledgers that never forget, a cloud that archives what the heart would rather delete. The technical resemblance makes the verse's meaning more, not less, vivid — and also sharper: the spread scroll has no appeal mechanism, no redaction by the powerful, no expiration of the statute of limitations; and, unlike our archives, it records motives, not just motions, because its keepers are commanded by the Knower of the secrets. The hadith gives the practical rule for a life in front of any record, human or divine:

> *"Every one of my community will be pardoned except the openers of sins. And among the opening of sins is that a servant commits a sin at night, when God has covered him over, and he says in the morning — though God had covered him over — 'O so-and-so, I did last night such and such.' He spent the night veiled by his Lord, and he tears open the covering of God at daybreak."* (al-Bukhārī, no. 6069)

In this world God keeps your scroll rolled: that is His name *al-Sattār*, the Veiler, and it is a mercy, not a lapse — the room of *tawba*, repentance, is open precisely because the folds are still closed. The Day of al-Takwīr begins when the folds become readable — *"That Day you will be laid bare, and none of your secrets will remain hidden"* (69:18) — which is why the sūrah puts the spreading of the pages immediately after the vindication of the buried child: both belong to the abolition of concealment. The sun was folded (v. 1) to make the pages readable (v. 10); all the night-lights were extinguished so that one scroll, held in the right hand or behind the back, could be the only illumination anyone will need. What you have sent forward is already written; the only remaining freedom is what to send next.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:11

> **when Heaven is laid bare;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Skinned Like a Hide**

*Kushitat* is a butcher's word. *Kashṭ* in Arabic is the stripping of the skin from the carcass — the hide peeled off the camel in one pulling. That is what happens to the sky, the verb says: not first shattered but stripped, pulled away as a covering is pulled away, so that what it concealed stands exposed. A few of the earliest readers, and al-Qurṭubī among the later, also took the verb to mean "rolled up," matching the verse where God says: *"That Day We roll up the heaven like a scroll is rolled up for writings; even as We produced the first creation, We shall reproduce it; a promise upon Us — indeed, We are ever to perform it"* (21:104). Still others read it as "removed entirely," and the picture in the Qur'an is one of these three or all together: peeled, rolled, taken down. What every reading confirms is the disappearance of the sky's function as a thing seen-through: from now on, whoever looks up sees *the thing itself* — the Real, with no ceiling between.

Two other verses stand beside this one. *"Have We not made the heaven a protected ceiling (saqfan maḥfūẓan)? Yet from its signs they turn away"* (21:32) — the sky, a canopy under guard; and *"On that Day the heaven will be like molten brass, and the mountains like dyed wool"* (70:8–9). ʿĀ'ishah's explanation of "molten brass" is preserved in al-Bukhārī: like dregs of oil, the brass molten and bubbling — the protective ceiling rendered as a roof of melting metal. And alongside it: *"When the heaven is rent asunder, and when the sky splits open"* (82:1; 84:1) — the same event in the vocabulary of tearing. The Day of al-Takwīr removes every roof, the firmament's roof included.

**The Reversed Scrolls**

Now hold verses 10 and 11 side by side, because their pairing is the sūrah's most beautiful piece of architecture. In this world, the heavens are spread out like an open scroll before every eye — the greatest public document of God's signs, "a manuscript of light" every civilization has read in — while the book of a person's deeds is rolled up, sealed, hidden, unread. On the Day, both are reversed: the hidden scroll of deeds is spread (*nuṭishat*, v. 10), and the open scroll of the sky is folded away and flayed off (*kushitat*, v. 11). What was public becomes private and what was private becomes public: every deed that no one saw is seen by everyone, and every sign everyone saw is taken into the hand of its Owner. The sūrah's ten verbs of inversion have no purer demonstration: the universe is a scroll that closes the instant the books of men open, because the signs were only guarantees of the accounting, not the accounting itself.

The tent imagery, sustained since verse 3, completes itself here. The pegs — the mountains — were pulled (v. 3); now the canopy — the sky — is struck (v. 11). Nothing of the tent stands: the world's unstruck state, which the Arabs knew as the first thing a household does when it must move forever, is the Qur'an's own figure for a creation permanently relocated.

**Exposure: What the Flaying Means for the Inhabitant**

Why strip the sky at all? The answer of the sūrah's own sequence is: exposure. *Kushitat* lies, by near-neighborhood of sound, beside the family of *kashf*, unveiling, and its Day is the Day the Qur'an elsewhere calls the Day of being brought before the records: *"On that Day you will be laid bare (tuʿraḍūn), and no secret of yours remains hidden"* (69:18). Human life under a sky is life under cover — the roof of the world is the first of all the world's roofs: rooms for the crime, night for the whisper, distance for the escape, the very horizon that hides the army and the grave and the pit in which a daughter was laid. The flayed heaven means the end of cover as a category. And notice that the sūrah places the naked sky between the two residences: the stripped vault reveals, at once, the Fire kindled below and the Garden brought nigh above (vv. 12–13) — the ceiling was all that separated the sight of the two destinations from the traveler's eye. Take away the roof, and every human being discovers that he has been walking, from the first dawn of his life, between visible end-points, with only this world's mercy keeping either from his sight.

For the believer the removal is not terror but the sūrah's quiet promise read backwards: the heavens are not stripped because they were hated; they are stripped because better is arriving. *"And hasten to forgiveness from your Lord and a Garden as wide as the heavens and the earth, prepared for the reverent"* (3:133) — when the dwelling is that wide, the ceiling must come off. The peeled sky is the demolition before the real estate of God is displayed; the world's roof was a scaffold, and scaffolds are taken down when the building is finished. That is why this verse stands with two sentences from the same Makkan register that together give the verse its whole emotional range: *"They did not appraise God with His true appraisal, and the earth entirely will be [like a] handful on the Day of Resurrection, and the heavens rolled up in His right hand. Exalted is He, and high above what they associate with Him"* (39:67) — the sky in the hand of the One who made it, an act not of violence but of closure; and then, immediately, the opening of something permanent. The Makkan listener came out of verse 11 with the sky gone from over his head — and the sūrah, seeing his terror, did not leave him there: first the Fire, then the Garden, and only then, in the next verse, the sentence about himself: *each soul shall know what it has prepared.*

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:12

> **when Hellfire is kindled,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fire That Was Banked**

*Saʿirat* — kindled, stirred up into blaze — comes from the ordinary verb of every household's hearth: to make a fire, to fan it, to rouse the coals. *Saʿīr* in the Qur'an is the blazing fire as such, the word attached to the dwellers of Hell at 76:4 — *"Indeed, We have prepared for the disbelievers chains, shackles, and a blazing fire"* — and the word of the Egyptians' threat against Abraham: *"Build for him a structure and cast him into the blazing fire"* (37:97). What the verse announces is therefore not the construction of a new apparatus but the *rousing* of a fire already in existence, banked under its covering of restraint, awaiting its command. The tafsīr tradition, reading the passive voice with its usual discipline, preserves the answer to the obvious question — by what is it stirred? Al-Qurṭubī and others transmit the saying that the Fire is kindled by the wrath of God and by the sins of the children of Adam: its fuel, in the Qur'an's own words, *"is people and stones"* (2:24). The sinner does not merely go to the Fire; the sinner is fuel for it — the moral world of this sūrah has no image of karma more condensed.

That the Fire's heat is not metaphor, and that this world's fires are of one species with it, the Prophet ﷺ made explicit:

> *"Your fire which the sons of kindle is one part of seventy parts of the fire of Hell."* They said: O Messenger of God, if it is as described, then it is nothing [in itself compared with that fire]. He said: *"Its excess over all the fires is sixty-nine parts."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah)

**The Insatiability Verse, and Its Cure**

Hell in the Qur'an is never a static scenery; it is a participant in dialogue, and its voice is one of the most chilling devices of the Makkan sūrahs: *"On the Day We say to Hell: Have you been filled? — and it says: Are there still more?"* (50:30). The fire asks for increase after the nations are thrown in, and the commentators point to the answer given elsewhere — the promise that God will fill it: *"I will surely fill Hell with jinn and men, all together"* (11:119). Yet in the very same moment, the other side of the picture: the hadith of Anas in the two Ṣaḥīḥs, in which Hell keeps being filled and keeps asking *"hal min ziyādah"* until its corners are gathered and its Lord puts His foot over it, and it says: *Qaṭ, qaṭ — enough, enough, by Your might* — nothing in it ever wasted. The eschatology here, as the scholars of Sunnah read it, contains both truths: an appetite that no nation could sate, and a capacity that is not infinite insult but exact provision — enough for every one who was given the choice this sūrah will describe in verses 28–29.

And between the appetite and the filling there is another Makkan verse of terrible lightness, answering the first objection any honest ear raises: why kindle at all? *"What would God do with your punishment if you are grateful and believe? And God is Ever-Appreciative, All-Knowing"* (4:147). The Fire exists because the alternative — that the buried daughter of verses 8–9, the burned villages of the unrepentant, the whole ledger of 81:10's spread scroll, should finally rest unredressed — is what God's own testimony denies Him. Punishment in this sūrah's economy is not vengeance; it is the refusal to let injustice be real and unpayable. ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh, the people of the Well — the Qur'an's other names for the Fire are case law.

**The Angels of the Fire**

A kindled fire needs keepers, and the sūrah-cycle of the Hour introduces them without decoration: *"Over it are angels fierce and severe, who do not disobey God in what He commands them, and they do what they are ordered"* (66:6); *"Over it are nineteen"* (74:30) — the number at which the Meccan scoffers demanded a review of God's staffing, and to which God answered with the single line that ends their case: *"We have made the wardens of the Fire none but angels"* (74:31). These keepers are not cruel by temperament; they are uncorruptible by office — no petitioning, no bribes, no mercy-trade, because mercy in that court has already been spent, in the sending of messengers, the veil of this world, and the long delay of kindling. The point of the angelic detail is the closing of every loophole: when the Fire is staffed by beings who *cannot* disobey, the accused discovers that his sentence has no administrator who can be moved.

**Why Fire Comes Before the Garden**

Al-Takwīr orders the two residences with the Fire first (vv. 12–13), and the order is the sūrah's ethic stated in sequence. Justice precedes favor: before the oppressed are consoled, their oppressors are stopped; before the host opens the feast, the bandits are barred from the road. The first listeners in Makkah lived that sequence in real time — the weak believers of the sūrah's decade were being dragged through their own kindled sands of torture, and this verse answered them exactly as 26:90–91 will pair the two scenes: *"And the Garden will be brought near for the reverent, and the Hell-fire will be shown forth to those who went astray"* — the Fire kindled is the Garden's security. For the persecutors, the same verse held their sentence suspended, unsoftened, right up to the sūrah's very next clause. For every age since, the logic of placing *saʿirat* before *ʿuridat* remains readable: a world that abolishes the idea of a kindled accountability does not abolish punishment — it merely relocates it to the side of the powerful, unanswerable and unlit; the Qur'an's public fire is the one place where the victim's claim is certain, because it is the One who is never bribed who tends it. The verse closes the way all the Day's verses close in this sūrah — not toward spectators, but toward each soul, standing where the Fire can be seen from the Garden, and knowing at last what both were for.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:13

> **and when the Garden is brought nigh,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guests Do Not Come to the House; the House Comes to Them**

*ʿUridat* — is brought near, set out for display — shares its root with the Qur'an's word for the great *ʿaraḍ*, the mustering and the showing-forth: *"That Day you will be brought up for inspection; not one secret of yours will remain hidden"* (69:18). But in this verse the sūrah puts the same verb of exposure to the tenderest of uses: the Garden is paraded, brought close, laid out for the reverent the way a host spreads a table within reach of the honored guest. The parallel verse at 26:90 makes the pairing explicit, and it is worth reading as the sūrah's own commentary on this word: *"And the Garden will be brought near for the reverent, and the Hell-fire shown forth to those who went astray"* — the two residences both moved, each made immediate to its people; and 50:31 adds the note of intimacy: *"And the Garden will be drawn near for the reverent — no distant place."* Nothing is to be traveled toward, negotiated for, or begged through intermediaries. The reward arrives already won, close enough to enter.

There is a wordplay in the Arabic that translation must confess its defeat before. *Jannah*, a garden, is named from *j-n-n*, to conceal — a garden is a place so densely planted that it veils its guest from the burning world, its shade a kept secret. On the Day when the sky is flayed and no cover remains (v. 11) and every secret is unsealed (69:18), the one thing God *does* veil is His friends — drawing around them the only concealment left, and turning it into reward. The Day of total exposure is also the Day of total privacy for the reverent: seen wholly by God, hidden from everything that could ever harm or tax them again.

**The Theology of the Verb: What "Brought Near" Teaches**

Why does the Garden come to the servant, while, in so many other verses, the servants are told to *"race to a Garden whose width is the heavens and the earth"* (57:21; cf. 3:133)? The commentators answer from the grammar: both are true because the striving and the gift belong to different moments of the same covenant. Stride toward it now; it will stride to you then — and the nearer it is made to arrive, the less the running was ever for its purchase. This is the Makkan sūrahs' complete economics of grace: effort real, reward not earned but *prepared* — *"which has been prepared (uʿiddat) for the reverent"* (3:133). The passive verbs of this whole first section of al-Takwīr — everything folded, spilled, stripped, kindled — end here in one more passive that is the sūrah's kindest word: the Garden, like the Fire, is acted upon; both are laid ready before the walk of any human foot. What belongs to us, and to us alone, is the deciding the last verses of the sūrah will address: which of the two we have been making ready.

And the content of the nearness is stated in the two texts the scholars set beside this verse at every printing of them. From the Qur'an: *"No soul knows what is kept hidden for them of joy as a reward for what they used to do"* (32:17) — the very concealment of the Garden's detail is itself part of the delight, the unspoiled anticipation. And the hadith qudsī in the two Ṣaḥīḥs:

> *"God the Exalted said: I have prepared for My servants who are righteous what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, nor has it entered upon the heart of man."* Then, if you wish, recite: *"No soul knows what is hidden for them of comfort for eyes, as reward for what they used to do"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah; the recitation is in Muslim's wording)

**Nighness as News, in the Decade That Needed It**

Historically, the verse was delivered to people for whom "the Garden brought near" was not decoration. The first hearers of al-Takwīr were the persecuted few of Makkah's seventh year of revelation — families like that of Yāsir who were literally burned, slaves like Bilāl over whom a stone was placed in the sun. To them this sūrah gave the sequence in its full legal meaning: the sky will be stripped so that your Fire can be seen coming for your masters (v. 12), and stripped so that your Garden can be seen coming to you (v. 13). Injustice and consolation are both being made visible in the same movement; nothing in the Makkan revelation is abstract eschatology. The grief of that decade is even answered, line by line, by a verse this sūrah's audience would go on to know by heart: *"They say: Praise belongs to God, Who removed grief from us; truly our Lord is Forgiving, Appreciative — Who by His grace settled us in the eternal abode; no weariness touches us there, and no fatigue"* (35:34–35). The removed grief is not minimized; it is *removed* — its existence on that Day is the proof that it was real, and real enough to be paid for.

**Why the Believer Still Walks Carefully**

If the Garden is brought near, why fear at all — why twelve terrors and then this? Because the sūrah's own doctrine of nearness has its stern twin: the Fire, too, is kindled *near*, and proximity is not destiny but disclosure. The classical teachers drew the practical line with the analogy that belongs to this commentary's method: the reverent man on the Day is like a careful driver on a road of cliffs who finds, when he reaches the safe ground, that the whole dangerous stretch was the way in — the Garden's nearness had a path with it, and the path's name is *taqwā*, that walking-awareness the Fātiḥah's readers have been practicing since 2:2. And what keeps the driver careful is not despair but exactly the sūrah's nearness itself: a promised place that has already been prepared does not leave room for either presumption or neglect — presumption, because the preparation was God's and not ours, and the Host withholds the doors from whom He will; neglect, because the Guest-House is close, and a thing close is a thing worth hurrying at. The verse ends the Day's description where the Day itself will end the story of every reader, with the door already at hand and the journey behind it made of nothing but what he — and only he — had made ready.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:14

> **each soul shall know what it has made ready.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb the Sentence Was Waiting For**

Fourteen verses have passed, and now, at last, the suspended period resolves. From verse 1 the hearer has carried twelve "whens" with no main clause — the sun folded, the stars spilled, the mountains marched, the camels abandoned, the beasts gathered, the seas ablaze, the souls yoked, the buried girl asked, the scrolls spread, the heaven stripped, the Fire kindled, the Garden set out — and every one of them was, grammatically, an unfinished promise of consequence. Here is the consequence: *ʿālamat nafsun mā aḥḍarat* — "every soul shall know what it has made ready." The entire theater of the cosmos existed, this sūrah has just told its listeners, to produce a single epistemic event inside each human being: *knowing*. The mountains move so that you finally learn what you did while they stood. This is the most concentrated statement in the Qur'an of why the Hour is called the Day of Decision, the Day the inner becomes outer and the self becomes legible to itself.

**Aḥḍara: The Grammar of Sending Ahead**

The verb chosen for "made ready" is *aḥḍara*, from *ḥuḍūr* — presence. To *aḥḍira* something is to cause it to attend, to summon it to the spot: to make present what was elsewhere, to send ahead and have it arrive. In the vocabulary of travel, it is what a man does with his provisions before the journey — he sends them forward to the destination to be there when he gets there. The same word-root stands in the verse that the classical teachers always recite at this point: *"O you who believe: be conscious of God, and let every soul look at what it has sent forward (mā qaddamat) for tomorrow; and be conscious of God — indeed, God is aware of what you do"* (59:18), and its twin report of the Day: *"And every soul will find present (wuḍiʿata) whatever good it did and whatever evil it did"* (3:30). The sūrah's own final clause, at 75:13, glosses it: *"Man will be informed that Day of what he sent ahead and what he left behind."* The theology of the word is exact: nothing arrives on the Day by memory, invention, or favor; it was all *sent ahead*, by the owner's own hand, years ago, while the scrolls were still being written. Your future is not a blank; it is a warehouse, and you have been stocking it your whole life with every intention, every word, and every dirham.

**Why "Know" and Not "Be Told"**

The verse does not say each soul will be told by an announcer; it says the soul *knows*, and the commentators press this: the knowledge of that Day is unmediated and therefore unarguable. No report to dispute, no record to nitpick, no advocate to instruct; the self stands in direct contact with its own history. It is the same epistemology as the two verses this one sums up — the opened scroll (v. 10) and the flayed sky (v. 11): when the cover is off the heavens and off the pages, the last cover left, the one each soul wears through this world, is lifted: *"You were heedless of this; now We have removed from you your veil, so your sight today is sharp"* (50:22). The sharpness of sight is the punishment of the damned and the cure of the saved. The veil, meanwhile, is the very mercy the sūrah's whole world runs on — heedlessness with a deadline — which is why the hadith of this verse, cited by Ibn Kathīr from the Prophet ﷺ, strikes with its gentleness:

> *"There is none among you but that God will inform him of that which is between him and his two interpreters."* (a well-known hadith transmitted on the meaning of this verse, cited by al-Qurṭubī at it; the two “interpreters” are the recording scribes of 50:17–18)

What God says, in that meeting, is the content of our verse: you will know. The two "interpreters" are the scribes of 50:17–18 — the recorders of verse 10 — and the verse's knowledge is theirs finally read, and his, finally acknowledged.

**The Verse That Was Always the Point**

It is worth retracing one last time what this ending does to the sūrah's beginning. If verse 14 had said "each soul shall see its Lord," the cosmos would have been a spectacle; if it said "each soul shall be judged," the cosmos would have been a threat. It says *know what it has made ready*, and the cosmos becomes a classroom: everything folded in the first thirteen verses was a lesson in the only knowledge that will matter in ours — self-knowledge, final and footnoted. The sun was folded because the soul's own light — the knowledge of what it has done — must finally be the only illumination; and al-Takwīr, which spent twelve verbs taking away everything the human being used to see with, returns in its last verb to the one eye that cannot be blinded: *"Rather, man will be a clear evidence against himself, even if he offers his excuses"* (75:14–15). This is also why the sentence of verses 1–14 ends not in an image but in a mirror; and why the sūrah, having arrived here, does not stop — the knowing of what one has prepared is unbearable news, and news unbearable needs credentials. The twelve oaths of the second section, beginning with the next verse, are precisely those credentials: the God who just described the Day now swears by His sky, His night, and His dawn that the one who told you about it is trustworthy. The Day of knowledge ends, and the argument for the Messenger begins.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:15

> **So I swear by those receding,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pivot of the Particle**

*Fa-lā uqsimu* — "But nay, I swear." The *fāʾ* closes the door of the first section and opens the second; the sūrah's great sentence of the Hour has ended at verse 14, and what follows is not more description but testimony for the Describer's envoy. The particle *lā* before the oath verb puzzled early listeners, because Arabic ordinarily swears *without* it — the Qur'an elsewhere says simply *"uqsimu"*-type oaths, and the oaths of Sūrah al-Qiyāmah read *"lā uqsimu bi-yawmi’l-qiyāmah"* exactly as here (75:1). The grammarians settled the matter: this *lā* is not negation but one of the reinforcing particles that join themselves to an oath without changing its polarity — a device the poets use for weight, and which in the Qur'an always signals a claim demanding total attention. The early exegetes preserved a minority report of readers who took the phrase as *denying* the need to swear — "I do not need to swear" — but the plain sense held by the great commentators is the solemn one: God swears, and His swearing is instruction, not assistance. The Omniscient needs no witness; the witnessed-to news needs witnesses we can feel.

**Khunnas: The Ones That Draw Back**

*Bil-khunnās* is the sūrah's word, and it divides interpreters even as it unites them in a single picture: *the ones that recede, that hide, that withdraw.* It is the plural of *khānis*, from the root *kh-n-s* — the verb of the animal that crouches back into its lair, of the beast that presses out of sight; the same root as *al-khannās* in Sūrah al-Nās: *"the whisperer who withdraws, who whispers in the breasts of mankind"* (114:4–5). So the oath-objects are defined by one movement above all: they *recede*. The commentators list the candidates, and each is meaningful. Al-Ṭabarī transmits, as the strongest line from the Companions and the Successors, that *al-khunnās* are the stars — the wandering lights that course by night and vanish (recede) at dawn; Mujāhid and Qatāda: the stars that appear at night and hide by day; specifically, some said, the five planets visible to the naked eye — which the sūrah's later verses will pair with night and morning (vv. 16–18). Others took the receding ones to be the wild beasts (some read this from Ibn ʿAbbās): the gazelle, the oryx, the panther that shrink back from sight by day — creatures of hiding, sworn by as the sūrah has just told of their Day of gathering. And a third line reads the receding as the motion of the planets in their courses — the "retrograde" stations that stopped ancient astronomers and, as the sūrah describes them here, lights that draw back into concealment before returning to their posts.

What the range of readings shares is the theology of the oath. The *khunnas* are things whose absence is as regular as their presence. They withdraw — and they are *expected back*. To swear by the receding lights of the sky is to swear by the most universal human experience of return: the star that hid is tonight in its place; the beast that shrank into its den is at dawn crossing the open ground; what left the horizon, left it by law, and the law is the oath's point — because the One who swears by *return* is confirming, in the very next answer-verse, the Messenger who came from an Unseen that never hides.

**Why the Sky Must Testify to a Book**

The Makkan challenge hanging over this section was not "is there a Day?" but "is this man's news true?" — and God's response is to call the heavens as character witnesses. Three Qur'anic clusters show how the courtroom is arranged. First, the heavens are the greatest of the *signs for those who reflect*: *"Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the alternation of night and day, are signs for those of understanding — who remember God standing, sitting, and on their sides, and reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth: Our Lord, You did not create this in vain; glory be to You; guard us from the punishment of the Fire"* (3:190–191). The Qur'an makes reflection upon receding stars a road to *dhikr* and to protection from the Fire — precisely the sequence al-Takwīr performs: twelve terrors, four oaths, one testimony, and a Book that saves from the Fire kindled in verse 12. Second, the heavens are themselves sworn by elsewhere for the same kind of claim: *"So I swear by the places of the stars — and it is, if you knew, a tremendous oath"* (56:75–76) — God swears by the stars' *positions* (manāzil, their stations of descent in the solar year) where the sūrah there is answering the deniers who made God's provision a stake in a gambling partnership. Third — and this is the Makkan wound — the stars were the very objects of the diviners' cult, the "lords" whose risings and settings men read for rain and fortune; the hadith forbade the transfer (see the commentary on verse 2), and this oath transfers them back: God swears *by* them; no one may swear *at* them. The *khunnas* that fade at dawn are the same lights that fade forever at the Hour (v. 2): they cannot guide at either end, and they cannot be trusted except as His. What the oath teaches, in the end, is the sūrah's first lesson re-taught by other means: that which recedes cannot rule, and that which rules never recedes.

**The Pause Before the Answer**

One technical beauty closes this verse's share. After verse 15 the answer of the oath does not arrive; it waits through verses 16, 17, and 18, four oaths deep — a suspense structure exactly mirroring the twelve suspended "whens" of the first movement. The Makkan listener, like the one on the mountain road in the dark, is made to walk the whole length of the night, the swimming lights overhead and the breathing dawn ahead, with his ears strained toward the sentence that will not come until the morning is already rising in the recitation. Then, at last, verse 19: *Truly it is the speech of a noble messenger.* The oaths themselves have performed what they testify to: the night came, and the answer came, and the light returned — as the Hour will come, as the Messenger came, as every promise of God has arrived, on time, in its orbit, exactly when the sky said it would. Whoever doubts the Book should, the sūrah implies, count the receding lights first: each of them is a sworn letter of the same fidelity, returned nightly to its place, and every one of them will be folded — but the word that swears by them will not.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:16

> **those that move and set,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Adjectives in One Breath**

*Al-jawāri al-kunnas* — "the swimming ones that draw back." Most reciters and exegetes take the phrase as a second description of the same oath-object of verse 15: the receding stars are at once *jawāri*, "swimming, coursing," and *kunnas*, "receding, veiling." A second parsing — transmitted in al-Ṭabarī and other collections of the early views — makes the two verses two oaths: by the receding ones, and by the swimming ones; on that reading some of the earliest glossers, Ibn ʿAbbās among them in certain chains, took *al-khunnas* for the wild beasts that hide by day, and *al-jawāri al-kunnas* for the stars that course and set, or even for the ships that run and then disappear beyond the horizon (a reading several of the Successors' commentators record, since *al-jawāri* in the Qur'an so often means exactly that: *"and the ships that sail through the sea by His command"* — *al-fulki al-jawāri*, 31:31; 14:32; 35:12; 45:13). The variety of the grammar is again the stability of the image: whatever swims through its medium and then withdraws from eyes — star, beast, or ship — is enlisted in God's testimony.

The verb at the root of *jawāri*, *jarā*, is used of water running, of blood flowing, of the young animal breaking into its first gallop; the heavens in the Qur'an are not static wallpaper but a flowing thing, and the most quoted of its own descriptions is this: *"And it is He who created the night and the day, and the sun and the moon; each swimming (yasbaḥūn) in an orbit"* (21:33; cf. 36:40 — *"every one swimming in an orbit"*). *Yasbaḥūn*: the very verb of swimmers. God swore by bodies in motion — and the motion is the oath's argument, for it is the one public miracle no civilization has ever explained away, only measured: the sky that runs, and runs back.

**The Three Purposes of a Star**

Against the reading "stars," the tradition arms this verse with a decisive prophetic saying transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Ḥākim's Mustadrak — declared *ṣaḥīḥ*, and al-Dhahabī concurred:

> *"God made the stars for three purposes: He adorned with them the lowest heaven, He made them missiles for the devils, and He made them signs by which navigation is taken. Whoever interprets them otherwise, he has spoken by his own opinion, lost his share, and burdened himself with what he did not know."* (al-Ḥākim, Mustadrak, at 81:16)

Read in the order of the verse's two words, the saying unlocks the oath's two titles. They are *jawāri*: swimming — through the sea of their courses, guiding the traveler — *"And He has set landmarks, and by the star they are guided"* (16:16). They are *kunnas*: they draw back — out of the daytime sky, out of the eavesdroppers' reach, and out of every false claim: the missile-function means these same lights are the weapons that keep the devils from the upper air, the police of the Unseen. And that last function earns the verse its place in the sūrah's argument: the accusation to be answered in verses 19–25 is that a jinn brought this Book down. An oath by the very lights that *pelt every rebellious devil* (37:7; 67:5) is the answer before the answer: the heavens this Book appeals to are patrolled against the race whose "inspiration" it is denied. The sky's missiles guard revelation on its way down; the sky's swimming lamps guide the sūrah's audience on their roads home. One heaven, two duties — and God swears by both to prove a third: the trustworthiness of the Messenger between them.

**The Ship, the Market, and the Mind**

For the merchants of Makkah, to whom this sūrah was recited in its seventh year, the *jawāri* of the oaths and the *jawāri* of their harbors were a single experience: they knew stars the way sailors do — as lights that *appear to be reliable precisely because they withdraw*, sinking into the dawn at the exact moment the traveler no longer needs them, returning when the route is dark again. The seafarer's one demand of the sky is regularity, and the Qur'an gives the demand, at the close of its own sea-verse, as the object of the oath's logic: *"Indeed, in that are signs for everyone who is patient and grateful"* (31:31; 14:32). And in the structure of al-Takwīr itself, verse 16 is the hinge between the two great verb-families of the sūrah: the first section's verbs were all things being *done to* the sky at the end of the world (folded, spilled, stripped); the oaths' verbs are things the sky *does* by command, swimming and drawing back inside its appointed law. The Day of the folded sun and the swimming stars is, in the sūrah's arithmetic, not two heavens but one: the same obedient lights will run out their final orbit — and the Book whose truth the orbit testifies to will still be recited.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:17

> **by the night as it approaches,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Walks Both Ways**

*ʿAṣʿasa* is one of Arabic's celebrated self-opposing words — verbs that carry a meaning and its opposite within the same letters, the class the philologists call *al-aḍdād*. In this case the dictionaries record both "to draw near" and "to depart," and the classical exegetes split accordingly: the majority, the line transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and the Meccan and Medinan reciters of the Qur'an's first centuries, read *"bi’l-layli idhā ʿaṣʿaṣ"* as the night when it advances, its darkness setting in; others read it as the night when it withdraws, its black rolling up before the true dawn — a sense the sūrah's own near-double in al-Muddaththir, *"and by the night when it goes by with its whisperings"* (74:33 — *waṣwaṣa*, an onomatopoeic twin), keeps in the ear. And the most careful of the commentators, al-Ṭabarī and al-Ālūsī among them, decline to choose: the word, they say, means both — the verse swears by the night at whichever of its two gates, arrival or departure, and the double sense is not a defect but the point. There is method in that refusal. The night is the moment of the world's two transitions, and the Day the sūrah is describing is the transition at which both faces meet: darkness arriving for some, departing for others, and for every soul one or the other forever.

The sound itself teaches. *ʿAṣāṣ* in Arabic is the footfall, the rustle a man hears in the dark before he sees anything; the verb *ʿaṣʿasa* is used of that moving, coming-on sound. The word for the night's onset is therefore the word for footsteps — the sūrah lets the Makkan listener hear the dark arriving, foot by foot, exactly as the Hour will, "suddenly," the Qur'an says elsewhere, "while they perceive it not" (43:66). Night in this Book is never scenery; it is an event with a gait.

**Night in the Qur'an's Own Ledger**

To weigh the oath, the offices of night in the Qur'an must be laid out, because each is a station of the soul as much as of the sky. Night is *covering*: *"It is He Who made for you the night as a covering, and sleep as a rest"* (25:47); *"and the night as it enshrouds it"* (91:4). Night is *rest and refuge*: *"We made your sleep as rest, and the night as a wrapping"* (78:9–10). Night is *the hour of the chosen*: *"On their sides they little sleep from the night, and at the pre-dawn they ask forgiveness"* (51:17–18); *"Indeed, the rising by night is more forcible and truer to say"* (73:6); *"and glorify with the praise of your Lord ... in the hours of the night"* (20:130). And night is *the veil under which sin is plotted*: *"They hide from the people, but they hide not from God, while He is with them when they devise by night that which He does not approve"* (4:108). Every one of these offices — cover, rest, worship, concealment of the secret — will be revoked or completed on the Day of no night, and that is why this oath can bear the weight it carries: God swears by the night to say that the night is *His* instrument, and instruments are not lords.

**The Night That Was, and the Dawn That Is Coming**

Al-Takwīr set its oaths in the order of experience, and the experience is the whole argument: the night approaches — it is ʿaṣʿasa, footfall, covering, sleep — and every man who has lain down in fear has known that darkness is not an end but a passing state. The Qur'an makes the daily night a parable of the death that is not a terminus: *"It is God Who takes the souls at death, and those that die not during their sleep; He keeps those on which He has decreed death, and lets the rest go about to an appointed term"* (39:42); *"and He it is Who made for you the night as a covering, and sleep as rest"* (25:47). The one who believes in the morning believes by the same token in the waking after the long sleep; and this is precisely the order of the sūrah's next verse. But it is worth stopping on the Makkan listener's actual fear. His town did its murders at night; its caravans feared the night; its idols were unveiled by dawn. The God who swears "by the night as it approaches" is telling such a man two sentences at once: the approaching dark is *known to Me* — it is My creature, sworn by, not dreaded; and the night that approaches *approaches toward something* — toward the morning of verse 18, toward the dawn of the verse 19 testimony, toward the Day whose verdict the first half just recited. Nothing in this Book ends in a ʿaṣʿasa. What advances toward the believer in the night is His protection; what advances toward the tyrant in the night is the sound of footsteps, which the tyrant hears as the wind. Every one of us, at some hour of every life, is lying in the dark and listening. The oath says: and *I* was there before you were.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:18

> **and by the morning as it breathes!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Light That Inhauls**

*Bi’l-ṣubḥi idhā tanaffas* — the last of the four oaths gives the Qur'an one of its most quietly miraculous images. *Tanaffasa* is the verb of breathing: the chest rising, the air drawn in and let out, life measured in draws. To swear by the dawn *as it breathes* is to describe the morning as a living thing waking from the night's unconsciousness — and the image fits the physics of the phenomenon with an uncanny plainness: the first light does not leap, it *expands*, thin and spreading, filling the horizon by degrees the way breath fills a chest, until the day is standing fully inhaled. The commentators note exactly this: al-Ālūsī and others gloss, "the morning rises and its light spreads like the spreading of breath." And the word's root carries the whole sūrah's anthropological theology in one family: *nafs* — self, soul, life-breath — *tanaffus* — breathing — and *nafkh*, the divine blowing of spirit into clay: *"So when I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My spirit, then fall down to him in prostration"* (15:29; 38:72). The morning breathes, and every morning is the Qur'an's daily reminder that the world is being kept alive at each instant by the same act that made man alive; when the sūrah's first section folds the sun and spills the stars, what ceases is not merely light but the breathing — and the return of every dawn between these two facts is one of the things being sworn to. The dawn in the Qur'an is the line of demarcation in worship — *"eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct to you from the black thread of night"* (2:187) — and the thread, thin as an inhalation, is what divides permitted from forbidden at the beginning of every fast, so that the earliest Muslims' very hunger swore the sūrah's oath daily on their tongues.

**The Matched Pairing, and Its Reading**

Verses 17 and 18 are one diptych, and the classical readers explained their relation as a rule: if *"as it approaches"* (v. 17) is taken as night's arrival, then *"as it breathes"* is the rising of morning after it — the normal order of the world; and if *ʿaṣʿasa* is taken as night's departure, the pair describes the pre-dawn itself, darkness lifting as the light expands. Ibn Kathīr records both and inclines to their combination: God swears by the night in both of its great movements and by the morning in its one — the whole circuit of the heavens, closing and opening, is the oath's object. The same pairing stands, slightly varied, in the sūrah revealed just before this one: *"Consider the night when it enshrouds, and the day when it shines forth"* (92:1–2); *"and by the night as it covers, and by the day as it appears"* (93:1–2); *"and the day when it brightens"* (79:35). The Makkan ear heard dawn-oaths as a set phrase of divine testimony — and al-Muddaththir's almost identical triplet (74:32–34) proves that this sūrah's opening of the second section was, for its first audience, recitation of a known sanctity with a new answer appended.

**The Witnessed Recitation**

The dawn is not only light in this religion; it is prayer. *"And recite the Qur'an at the dawn of the day — indeed, the recitation of the dawn is witnessed"* (17:78). And the Prophet ﷺ told his community who the witnesses are — the relay of angelic guardians that changes shift at the dawn prayer, in the hadith of the two Ṣaḥīḥs from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"Among you the angels succeed one another by night and by day, and they gather at the dawn prayer and the afternoon prayer. Those who have spent the night with you ascend, and God — best acquainted with them — asks: 'How did you leave My servants?' They say: We left them at prayer, and we came upon them at prayer."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim)

Set this beside the oath the sūrah has just taken, and the second section's subject appears before verse 19 even arrives. The dawn of verse 18 is the hour at which heaven and earth check each other's books: the night's scribes ascend carrying the record of what was done in the dark (4:108, the verse of night-plots; 50:17–18, the scribes of verse 10), and the morning's mercy descends with it. The Qur'an that is *ḥaqq* was itself, in the memory of the community, sent down *"on a blessed night"* (44:3), through *"the Trustworthy Spirit upon your heart"* (26:193–194) — revelation, in the sūrah's own imagery, is a dawn in the night of the world. When God swears by the breathing morning that *it is the speech of a noble messenger* (v. 19), the Makkan hearer has already been led, through four oaths, to feel the connection: the sky opens its mouth at dawn the way the earth opened its ears in the cave, and both are signs of the one power that brings forth the hidden in its time — the day from the night, the Book from the Unseen, the dead from the graves. *"The day does not overtake the night, nor the night exceeding the day; each in an orbit swimming"* (36:40). The order is the evidence. The evidence is the oath. And the oath has only one thing left to confirm: that a man in Makkah was telling the truth about what he heard from Heaven — which is, exactly, what verse 19 says.

**An Oath the Listener Can Keep**

The four oaths end as they began — in the receding lights and the returning ones — and the practical residue they leave is a discipline of attention. Whoever passes his mornings and evenings inside this sūrah's oath-structure learns to read the sky's faithfulness as a standing sermon: the night arrives and lies, and the morning breathes and does not lie, and the man who has watched ten thousand dawns break over his private darks has seen, in nature's own coin, the promise he is being offered — that no night of his life, not the pit of the buried child, not the cell of the tortured believer, not the long vigil of his own unwept grief, is without its *tanaffus*. The breath of morning is the smallest resurrection; the resurrection is the greatest morning. Between the two, the Book is being recited, and — as the answer of all four oaths now states, with the sky itself swearing to the words that follow — it is not the speech of anyone else.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:19

> **Truly it is the speech of a noble messenger,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer of the Oath**

After four oaths, here it is — the sentence the night, the stars, and the breathing dawn were sworn to confirm: *inna huwa la-qawlu rasūlin karīm*, "Truly it is the speech of a noble messenger." The pronoun *huwa*, "it," is left to find its referent in the recited context: the Qur'an itself, this Book the Makkan listeners were refusing. The sūrah has just described the end of the cosmos, and it now stakes the credibility of that description on a chain of transmission — the revelation was not improvised, dreamed, or snatched; it came, as all verifiable news comes, through a sender, a bearer, and a receiver, and the bearer of this one is *karīm*, noble. In Arabic usage the noble (*karīm*) is the one whose excellence is expected of him and never exhausted — the generous horse, the well-watered valley, the man whose good comes beyond what was asked. The choice of word is an argument: the bearer's nature makes fabrication unthinkable, as the springing of a full well makes thirst unthinkable downstream.

**Gabriel, or the Prophet — and What the Dispute Teaches**

Who is the "noble messenger" of the oath? The overwhelming majority of the commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr in his preferred reading — take it as the Archangel Gabriel, the angel of revelation, for three reasons the tradition weighs: the sūrah's next verse describes one "possessed of strength in the presence of the Lord of the Throne, high stationed, obeyed, trustworthy" (81:20–21), a description the whole Book reserves for Gabriel — *"one of terrible strength (dhū quwwatin), endowed with power — then he stood upright (mustaqīm)"* (53:5–6); and 69:40, repeating our verse verbatim in its account of the Qur'an's origin, sits in a passage about the angelic bearing of the Book. A second group, transmitted also in the early sources, takes the noble messenger as the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ himself — "the speech of a noble messenger," i.e., the Qur'an is the speech of God as delivered in the words of His noble Messenger, whom the community knew face to face; some of the later philologists noted that on this reading the oaths' answer is even closer: the receding lights testify to the trustworthiness of the man who recites under them. Both readings belong to the sūrah's economy of credentials, and the commentators' very disagreement is instructive: the chain runs *God → Gabriel → Muḥammad → the worlds* (as the next verse of the Book itself says: *"It is the revelation (tanzīl) from the Lord of the worlds"* — 69:43; 26:192), and every link in that chain is described with the same two words, *karīm* and *amīn*. To swear by the heavens that *this is the speech of a noble messenger* is to say that the Book has not merely a message but a provenance — and provenance, not content alone, is what was contested in Makkah.

**"The Speech of a Messenger" — and Yet God's Speech**

The phrasing raises the one theological question this verse must not dodge: if the Qur'an is *the speech of a noble messenger*, whose speech is it? The scholars of the Salaf answered from the Book's own usage: an attributed form (*ḍifāfa*) in Arabic carries both honor and transmission, as in God's own words about the she-camel of Ṣāliḥ — *"God's she-camel and her drink!"* (7:73; 54:27) — she is God's by creation and command, and also the people's by assignment: *"so they called upon their companion, and he seized her and hamstrung her"* (54:29). The same is said of the House: *"My House"* (2:125) and *"the House of God."* So the Qur'an is truly "the speech of Gabriel" — he recites it, conveys it, delivers it as recited — while being in its origin the speech of God, *"a revelation from the Lord of the worlds"* (69:43; 26:192). The classical debates on the createdness of the Qur'an — the *miḥna* era, the stand of Imām Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, the Sunnī settlement afterward — were fought, verse against verse, on precisely this phrasing, and the sound conclusion the exegetes drew from it here is that the verse affirms Gabriel's role as transmitter and honor, and says nothing against the Book's divine origin — because in the same sūrah God already called its contents His own decisive speech about the Day whose twelve signs only He can announce. What the Makkan audience heard, at any rate, was simpler and stronger: the accusation "he forged it" is answered at the level of evidence every man of trade understands — the chain of transmission is noble, and the document is not a copy but an original delivered by a courier whose name you know.

**The Verse in the Life of Recitation**

Let the reader note where this sentence falls in the worship of Islam: it is the answer to an oath whose preface is the night, and the early Muslims' own memory placed the Qur'an's arrival on the night of revelation's commencement and sealed the claim with the phrase Gabriel himself authenticated — the hadith of ʿUmar in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, when the angel came in the form of a man to teach the community their religion, and after answering the questions about Islam, faith, and excellence, departed, the Prophet ﷺ said of him: *"This is Gabriel, who came to teach you your religion"* (Muslim 8). The noble messenger of 81:19 is, by the Prophet's own identification in that hadith, a teacher, not a phantom; the Qur'an that 81:27 will call *"a reminder for the worlds"* (v. 27) is, verse by verse, a lesson given by a known authority under a sworn sky — which is exactly how the first listeners were told to test it, and exactly how it has passed, recited from mouth to mouth under the same four oaths, into each generation since, including ours: a Book that arrives, in our hands, through the chain the heavens have just sworn to be unbroken.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:20

> **possessed of strength, before the Possessor of the Throne, of high rank,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Titles in One Line**

The answer of the oath is not one name but a dossier: *dhū quwwa* — possessed of strength; *ʿinda dhī al-ʿarsh makīn* — stationed, established, at the Throne of the Possessor of the Throne; and then verse 21's two crowning words. Each term is placed with evidentiary care, because the verse is answering a claim: not "a voice in the desert," but an officer with credentials at the seat of the universe's governance. Consider the structure of heavenly authority the verse maps: strength at the bottom of the description, nearness at the top of it — power first, then rank — as if to say: whatever he brought you, he had the force to deliver and the standing to be heard at the source.

*Dhū quwwa*: the strength itself is left unnamed, and the commentators filled it in from the Book's other scenes. One reading, transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās among others, takes it as the physical might no creature could resist: the tradition — cited at 53:5 by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr in the commentaries on both sūrahs — that Gabriel seized the towns of Lot in the grip of his strength, lifted them to the height of audible angels, and inverted them: *"So when Our command came, We turned its highest upside down and rained upon them stones of layered clay"* (11:82; cf. 15:73–74). On this sense, *dhū quwwa* is the same phrase God uses of the angel at 53:5: *"Taught by one of terrible strength (dhū quwwatin), endowed with power — then he stood upright"* (53:5–6; cf. 54:37, where it is the "strong chastisement" that seizes the evildoers). A second reading, pursued with particular sympathy by Ālūsī, takes the strength as moral constancy: unbroken obedience from the beginning of creation to its end — the one quality that makes a courier flawless is not power but incorruptibility, and the sūrah says both. A third reading, reported by al-Qurṭubī for those who take the "noble messenger" as the Prophet ﷺ, makes the strength the capacity to bear and broadcast the message through a world of swords and lies — for the Qur'an elsewhere commands even a prophet's heir to take the Book with the same quality: *"O Yaḥyā, hold the Book with strength (bi-quwwa)"* (19:12).

**Stationed at the Throne**

*Makīn* is a court word: settled, installed, of rank no petition can dislodge (the Egyptian king's court in Sūrah Yūsuf speaks the same language of high station, *"makīn amīn"* — 12:54, using precisely the pair this sūrah gives in verses 20–21: rank and trust). To say the bearer of revelation is *ʿinda dhī al-ʿarsh makīn* is to place him, in the listener's mind, at the throne-room door of the King of kings, with the vocabulary of court protocol borrowed from the Book's own stories of God's elect — Joseph in the court of the Pharaoh, and Gabriel in the court of the Real. The Throne itself is the Qur'an's standing figure for sovereign administration, carried by angels who glorify and ask forgiveness for the believers (40:7), raised above the waters (cf. 11:7: *"and His Throne was upon the water"*), bearing the descent of the Spirit by His command (40:15: *"High in ranks, Lord of the Throne (dhū al-ʿarsh), He casts the Spirit (rūḥ) of His command upon whom He wills of His servants, to warn of the Day of Meeting"* — a verse that could have been copied as the tafsīr of our 81:19–21: rank, throne, Spirit, warning). And *"the Lord of the Majestic Throne"* (85:15) confirms which Throne: not the sun, whose course the sūrah just swore by; the angels' Lord, not the stars'. Against every Makkah that bowed to lights, the verse installs the Light-Bearer at a Throne no light of the sky reaches — *"Glorified be God, Lord of the Throne, above what they describe"* (43:82).

**The Ascent, and Why It Matters to a Doubter**

Two further texts show what the phrase *ʿinda dhī al-ʿarsh* implies about the revelation's route. The upward corridor is real and measured: *"To Him ascends the good word, and the righteous deed He raises"* (35:10) — words travel; and *"He manages the affair from the heaven to the earth, then it ascends to Him in a Day whose length is a thousand years of your counting"* (32:5; cf. 70:4: *"the angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a Day whose measure is fifty thousand years"*). The Makkan merchant knew caravans, routes, customs-houses, and the risk of interception; a Book, he was told, that descends along a road guarded by the Throne's own stationed angel (37:6–9, the missiles against every rebellious eavesdropper), traversing a distance measured in decades of human time per single passage — such a Book has a security record no sorcerer, soothsayer, or jinn-friend in the history of Arabia ever produced. This is why verse 20 belongs between the oaths and the accusations: before the sūrah defends the Prophet's sanity (v. 22), it establishes that his information's chain runs to the Throne — the question "did he bring this himself?" is now absurd before the question "could he have been deceived?" Even the sūrah's very next word will be *ṭāʿ* — the heaven's obedience, which is the next verse — because before anyone obeys the message on earth, the heavens have already obeyed it in place, and a courier honored by that court needs no impostor's luck.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:21

> **obeyed, trustworthy withal.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Words That Qualify Every Message**

*Muṭāʿ thammīn amīn*: obeyed — then, trustworthy. The pairing is a complete theory of legitimate authority in two words. Power without trust corrupts the message; trust without authority cannot deliver it. The angel of revelation has both: he is *muṭāʿ*, one whose word the heavenly host obeys, and *amīn*, one whose soul the contents of revelation cannot bend. A courier who was trusted but weak could be robbed on the road; one who was strong but faithless could forge the letters himself — and the Makkan accusation, in the end, was exactly the second: that the man invented what he claimed was sent (cf. 16:105: *"They only invent falsehood who do not believe in God's signs"*). The sūrah's answer: the bearer of the chain has been cleared of both risks — obeyed in heaven (so nothing was taken from him) and trustworthy in soul (so nothing was added to him).

The order itself was debated in the exegetical schools: why is *muṭāʿ*, the obedience of others, mentioned before *amīn*, the integrity of his own heart? Because, said the commentators on both readings of the "noble messenger," authority is the visible credential and integrity the hidden one, and the proof proceeds from the seen to the unseen: you cannot see a heart, but you can see who rises when he enters. For Gabriel, the obedience of the carriers of the Throne and the companies of angels is the public record of heaven; for the Prophet ﷺ — on the other reading of verse 19 — it is the visible miracle of his life that the wildest people on earth obeyed him by love, a submission no tyrant of Quraysh had ever purchased.

**"Obeyed": The Heaven's Yes**

Take the angelic sense first, and the Book provides the roll of those who obey: the bearers of the Throne and its circle — *"Those who bear the Throne and those around it glorify the praise of their Lord and believe in Him, and seek forgiveness for those who believe"* (40:7); the ascending ranks — *"the angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a Day whose measure is fifty thousand years"* (70:4); and, most exactly our verse's office, the commanding voice that is answered without friction, in the sūrah's contrast of the two audiences: *"To Him belongs the true supplication; and those whom they call upon besides Him do not answer them with anything... On the Day of Resurrection they will disown your association"* (35:14) — the idols are deaf; the heaven of the angels answers, and answers without dispute. Revelation is not smuggled; it is dispatched. The God who swears by stars that *recede* (81:15) sends a messenger the heavens *obey*: the same sky that hides its lights nightly at His word escorts His word downward, and the escort is the proof of the seal. On the Prophet-reading, *muṭāʿ* folds the whole law of the Book into one of its most-repeated clauses — *"Obey God and obey the Messenger"* (4:59; 64:12) — and the verse becomes a quiet confirmation of his earthly authority alongside his heavenly rank: obeyed up there, obeyed here, and *"whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed God"* (4:80). The Makkan audience, who prided themselves on independence, are being asked to notice that even the sky has a chain of command, and theirs sits at its end.

**"Trustworthy": The Credential That Precedes the Claim**

*Amīn* — from *amāna*, the same root as *īmān*, faith, which is itself named from security: a man believes in God because he is made safe by trusting Him; a man is called *amīn* because others are safe from him. The Qur'an gives the word its fullest prophetic definition at the head of its account of the revelation: *"The Trustworthy Spirit (al-Rūḥ al-Amīn) brought it down upon your heart, that you may be one of the warners"* (26:193–194) — Gabriel, the Trustworthy, delivering the Trustworthy Book to the Trustworthy servant. And it is one of the Qur'an's great rhetorical facts that every messenger in Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ opens his claim to his people with our exact formula — *"Indeed, I am to you a trustworthy messenger (rasūl amīn)"* (26:107 Noah; 26:125 Hud; 26:143 Salih; 26:162 Lot; 26:178 Shuʿayb) — as if the credential of trust had been issued, in advance, in the same words, in every century: the proof of a messenger is, first, that he is *known* to keep what is entrusted to him. On this earth, the man Muḥammad ibn ʿAbdillāh ﷺ carried that testimony before a single verse was revealed. The historians of Makkah, Ibn Isḥāq among them, preserve the tribunal's verdict in the sūrah's own vocabulary: when the clans quarreled over whom to trust with the Black Stone's placement at the Kaʿbah's rebuilding, they said without deliberation: *"This is the Trustworthy one (al-Amīn); we are satisfied with his judgment."* Forty years among them — the market, the journeys, the marriages, the trusts of their dead stored in his hands — had produced, as the Makkans' own sworn testimony, the adjective this sūrah now installs in heaven's dossier. The sūrah does not argue that he *became* trustworthy when the revelation came; it invokes the court record of the years before, when no one had any cause to watch him. That is the Makkan proof of revelation in one phrase: *trustworthy, withal* — and you all already knew the word for him.

**The Trust the Verse Ends In**

One last connection completes the verse's architecture, and it turns the dossier back upon the listener. In the Qur'an, *amāna* is not only a courier's quality but a creature's test: *"We offered the Trust (amāna) to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, and they declined it and feared it, and man carried it"* (33:72). The heavens that obeyed Gabriel's errand are the same heavens that refused the burden of conscious submission; the angel is *muṭāʿ amīn* by nature, man by choice. The chain whose credentials the sūrah has spent three verses proving — strength, station, obedience, trust — ends at a human heart, in Makkah or anywhere, and the heart is free. This is the exact seam where the sūrah's final section will open two verses hence (81:28–29): the revealed Book is carried to you by a heaven that cannot lie; whether you carry it back is decided by a self that must choose. What the verse leaves standing in the hearer's mind, in the end, is the cleanest epistemic alternative the Makkan court could be given: a trustworthy messenger, obeyed in heaven, delivered the Qur'an in words you hear and can weigh — or your suspicion that the entire chain of creation conspired for your sake, at a cost to its most trusted. The oath of the receding stars is heavy enough to break that second choice; the sūrah turns, from verse 22 on, to the man himself — *your companion* — the witness of the chain whom they all, without exception, had watched for a lifetime.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:22

> **Your companion is not possessed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gentlest Possible Address in the Sharpest Denial**

The verse refutes Makkah's loudest insult with two grammatical pieces of elegance before its content even reaches the ear. First: *ṣāḥibukum* — "your companion." Not "this man," not "your prophet" (the claim is still under dispute, and the sūrah will not assume it): simply, the one who walks the road with you, sits in your assemblies, keeps your trusts — the word *ṣuḥba*, companionship, is built from *ṣaḥba*, the path, and it carries the whole shared road of forty years in its syllables. The commentators, al-Rāzī most attentively of all, point out the force of the address: if madness had seized any man of you, you would know it in a season — how much more in a man you have eaten with, traded with, buried your dead beside, and appointed arbitrator over your stones. The word "companion" is itself the first proof of the predicate "not possessed." Second: the double negation — *mā... bi* — the strongest negative construction Arabic possesses; the sentence does not say "your companion is not mad" but "it is not the case, in any degree, that your companion is one touched by jinn." Denials, like oaths, have registers, and God chose the deepest.

**What "Possessed" Meant — and Why It Was Said**

*Majnūn* is built from the same root as *jinn* — *j-n-n*, to be hidden, veiled; the jinn are the concealed beings, and a *majnūn* is a man veiled: his mind covered over by something unseen that speaks in him. The accusation was not medical but mythological, and the Qur'an records its exact form elsewhere: *"O you upon whom the Reminder has been sent down, you are indeed one touched by jinn"* (15:6); *"the disbelievers said: This is nothing but a sorcerer, a liar"* (38:4) — and the same charge runs through every prophethood: *"And thus We sent no messenger before you to any town but its people said: A magician, or one touched by jinn"* (51:52). The sūrah's answer here is not a refutation of demonology; it is a witness-summons. The evidence offered for the man's sanity is the community's own uncontradicted experience of him.

**The Forty-Year Witness**

The Qur'an elsewhere puts the argument in the Prophet's own first-person courtroom form, and it deserves to stand as this verse's official gloss: *"Say: Had God willed, I would not have recited it to you, nor would He have made it known to you; I have indeed remained among you a whole lifetime before it. Do you not then reason?"* (10:16). Reasoning from *la-bithtu fīkum ʿumuran* — a full lifespan of ordinary life before the revelation — the verse treats familiarity as evidence, and the procedure is epistemically sound in a way the jurists later formalized: knowledge of a man's long public character is *ʿilm al-ishtihār*, diffused certainty, and it outweighs any isolated claim to the contrary. No madman dictated to scribes, for twenty-three years, a legislation whose every clause survived the audit of its enemies; no soothsayer kept his ravings in meter, in law, and in correspondence with events, while the community that could not catch him in one lie of this Book caught him in no lie at all. Their own testimony, preserved in Bukhārī from Abū Sufyān at the court of Heraclius, is the Makkan verdict in its purest form — the emperor asked whether they had ever accused this man of lying before his claim: *"No,"* said Abū Sufyān, unwilling to lie even then. Heraclius concluded: *"He would not have concealed [a lie] from people and then attributed it to God."* The denial of 81:22 has a public document behind it: a sworn confession by the enemy, in a foreign court, thirty years after the verse.

**The Anatomy of a Contrast**

A last detail shows what kind of distinction the sūrah is drawing, and it is a distinction the believer should carry into every claim to supernatural experience. The Prophet ﷺ taught that genuine jinn influence does exist and is subtle: *"The devil circulates in the son of Adam as blood circulates"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Ṣafiyyah) — whispering, not ruling; frightening, not legislating; and the man's faculties stay his own, his memory intact afterward. What the Quraysh alleged was that such a being authored *this*: a Book that commands the mercy the jinn would not want — the girl-child saved, the camel abandoned for God, the orphan's wealth returned, the prayer at dawn, the oneness no spirit could trade on — and forbids every appetite the unseen whisperer would serve. Possession by a devil produces ravings that flatter fear and appetite; this Book does neither: it terrifies and it demands. The sūrah's answer, then, is not that the unseen is imaginary — its whole second section asserts the unseen — but that this text's fingerprint does not match the hand the accusation claims held it. When the verse ends in the flat, familiar, devastating denial — *your companion is not possessed* — it has appealed, in order, to the street that knew him, the minds that reasoned about him, and the Book itself, judged by the enemies who could never return it with a flaw. The Makkan who first heard it had no rebuttal to offer except the one verse 26 will answer: *Fa-ayna tadhhabūn?*

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:23

> **He surely saw him upon the clear horizon.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Vision That Answers the Insult**

The denial of madness (v. 22) is followed immediately by its proof: *wa laqad ra'āhu bi'l-ufuqi'l-mubīn* — "and indeed he saw him on the clear horizon." The logic of the pairing is exact. What the charge said was that the man's words came from a disorder of the mind; the verse answers that the words came from a sight of the eye — a specific, localized, datable experience, "on the horizon," witnessed by the one it transforms, and confirmed by a message whose content no ravings could counterfeit. Sight is testimony's strongest root, and the sūrah grounds the Prophet's ﷺ claim in it, with an oath-borne "surely" (*laqad*): this is not a mood, a music, or a seizure; this was a seeing, and the seer can tell you what he saw.

Who was seen? The sūrah's own chain, verses 19–21, names the one seen: the noble messenger, the angel of the Throne. The great majority of the Companions and the Successors — the line of Ibn Masʿūd and, with precise reservations, ʿĀ'ishah — held that the seeing here is the Prophet's ﷺ sighting of Gabriel in his true form. And ʿĀ'ishah drew from this very verse the boundary of what was seen, refuting an exalted rumor with the Book's text:

> *"Whoever attributes to you that you saw your Lord, he has told the greatest lie — but he saw Gabriel. I never asked him about this verse — 'he surely saw him on the clear horizon' — he said: it is Gabriel; I have not seen him in his true form except twice."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Masrūq's questioning of ʿĀ'ishah)

The two sightings, in the reports of the two Ṣaḥīḥs at 81:23 and 53:13–18, are the horizon here and the Lotus Tree of the far boundary at the Ascension — *"and indeed, he saw him at another descent, at the Lotus Tree of the far boundary"* (53:13–14). The Prophet's ﷺ community, in other words, preserved the vision with the care of archivists: its two occurrences, its form, its limits — no vision of the Divine Essence in this life — fixed by the one who knew his household best.

**The Horizon of the East**

*al-ufuq al-mubīn* — "the clear horizon." Two features of the phrase carry weight. The *ufuq* is the visible rim of the world, the line where sky and earth divide and where light is born or dies: several of the earliest exegetes, among them Ibn Masʿūd in a transmitted gloss, specified the eastern horizon — the place of sunrise, the same edge the sūrah has just sworn by (81:18), so that the vision stands on the very horizon of the breathing morning, and the two are seen together: dawn breaks over Makkah, and on that breaking the angel of revelation stands. *Mubīn* — manifest, plain — does double duty: the horizon itself is "the clear one," the unhidden edge of the visible world, and several commentators (with al-Tabarī's breadth of transmitted senses) read it as active of the seer: the plain, open, undeniable seeing — not a half-glimpse behind a cloud, not a dream, not a private light. The most beautiful reading of the pairing belongs to the balāgha tradition: the vision was granted against the largest open canvas creation offers the eye, the rim of the world made clear by dawn, because what appeared upon it filled it. In the wording of Abū Dharr's hadith in Aḥmad's Musnad, the angel's true form was "six hundred wings," from which fell "much ruby and pearl," and it covered — in another chain, "his great form covered the horizon." The sūrah's word for the horizon and the hadith's verb for covering meet: what could only be seen *on* the rim of everything was in fact the rim of everything.

**An Event Verified by Its Effects**

The Makkan could not check a horizon; but they could, and did, check its consequences, and the tradition records the first verification as a procedural act. Bukhārī preserves the opening of revelation, in which the overwhelmed Prophet ﷺ goes from wife to witness: Khadījah takes him to the aged Waraqa ibn Nawfal, the man who had read the earlier scriptures, and his verdict is a chain-of-testimony formula — *"This is his Nāmūs (the law-bringing angel) whom God sent down to Moses; would that I were a young man, that I might support you when your people drive you out."* And the community that judged the vision by its fruit judged, from the horizon outward, twenty-three years of it: sane law, fulfilled warning, character consistent with what was commanded, no claim to the Unseen except what the verse next denies (v. 24). The verse "he surely saw him" therefore functions not as mysticism but as evidence offered in court: the man who heard the Quraysh's "madman" replied, in God's words, not "my visions are real" but "ask the town I have lived in." A claimant to madness has, by definition, no forty years of unbroken sanity to appeal to; this claimant's entire defense is that appeal, and the sūrah builds its case exactly on the fact that the accusers, of all people, are summoned as the witnesses.

**Why the Vision Mattered to the Listener Then and Now**

For the man of Makkah the horizon-vision settled three of his instincts at once. It tied the revelation to a bearer with standing at the Throne (v. 20) — no devil had reached this air (v. 25). It honored the body and the eye: God's word came to a seeing, hearing, walking man, not to a soul escaped from the world — Islam's lifelong insistence that the sacred enters the ordinary through its faculties, intact. And it located the supernatural in the sky every caravan watched, converting the very objects of his star-lore into the scenery of prophecy: the horizon he used for navigation became, once in his history, the place where heaven's courier stood for a man to see. For every listener since, the verse is the sūrah's reminder that this religion was founded not on a text that fell from nowhere, but on a text delivered by a bearer seen by one whose mind the whole town could audit. "Your companion is not possessed — he saw him." The two verses stand or fall together, and the next verse, in the sūrah's sequence, closes the last remaining doubt of a skeptical customer about a courier whose delivery he is being asked to buy sight unseen.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:24

> **And he does not withhold grudgingly the Unseen.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Readings, One Defense**

The word *ḍanīn* was read in two ways by the earliest Muslims, both preserved in the ʿuṣfari and mutawātir transmission currents the commentators record, and the sūrah's defense is doubled for the price of one. On the reading *ḍanīn*, from *buḥl*, stinginess: he does not hoard the Unseen God has shown him, does not ration the revelation for profit or favor, does not keep the best parts back from the crowd — the claim of a man who sells orphans his own property before his enemies even ask is met at its one vulnerable flank: a religious founder who hoards, controls, profits; this one, the sūrah says, has never withheld a single verse of what came, or a single ruling of what was asked. On the variant *ẓanīn*, from *ẓann*, suspicion — "he is not suspected, suspectable, untrustworthy": the Unseen he reports is not something he is liable to guess, conjecture, or counterfeit; there is no ground for *suspicion* of him in it. Ibn Kathīr notes the second as the preference of many, al-Qurṭubī transmits both, and the two readings converge where the sūrah means them to meet: whether the charge is that he *withheld* the message or that he *invented* it, the same verse crushes both: he neither kept back what he was given, nor added to what he was not given. In the rhetoric of the Makkan court this is a complete evidentiary exhibit: the text before them has no withheld chapters (its completeness the community later sealed in writing), and no suspect clauses (its content is testable at 4:82: *had it been from other than God, they would have found in it much discrepancy*).

**What Withholding Would Have Looked Like**

The stinginess-reading has teeth because the prophetic biography gives the case file. The Prophet ﷺ was asked questions whose answers would have been convenient revelation and were not; on some the sūrah itself answers: *"They ask you about the Spirit. Say: The Spirit is from the affair of my Lord, and you have been given of knowledge only a little"* (17:85) — a revelation that says "I will not tell you" is the documentary proof of one that never withholds what it will tell. The Book came down over decades under pressure — every accident, question, doubt, and battle of a real community — and nothing was held back that they needed: *"O Messenger, deliver what has been sent down to you from your Lord; and if you do not, you will not have delivered His message"* (5:67) — a command that makes delivery itself the prophetic office, and hoarding, not merely the hiding, the failure of it. And the charge that a claimant profits from the Unseen is answered by the sūrah-cycle's own repeated line, which our sūrah's section will carry at its close: *"Say: I ask you for no payment for it; and I am not of the affected"* (38:86) — no fee, and no self-importance. What was "made ready" for him he took; what was asked of him he gave; and the revelation's public completeness, its lack of esoteric grades, mystery schools, and initiated elites — the very architecture of 81:19–29 being delivered in the open market of Makkah to whoever would listen — is the continuing empirical sense of *mā huwa ʿala'l-ghaybi bi-ḍanīn*.

**What Suspicion Would Have Looked Like**

The ẓanīn-reading answers the other accusation: that he is *suspect* about the Unseen — a guesser wearing prophecy. Against suspicion the sūrah's whole second movement has been building a chain of verifications (the oaths of heaven, the angel with station, the sighting on the horizon), and the Prophet's ﷺ own epistemology completes it: he disclaimed precisely the faculties a suspect claimant would need. *"Say: I do not say to you that with me are the treasures of God, nor do I know the Unseen; I only follow what is revealed to me"* (6:50; cf. 7:188, 11:31, 46:9). A man who publicly refuses the title "knower of the Unseen" cannot be charged with trading in what was never his; he is, by his own fixed formula, a receiver, and a receiver's only honor is accurate transmission. The hadith makes the stakes of a single word in this role terrifying rather than rhetorical:

> *"Do not lie upon me deliberately; whoever lies upon me deliberately, let him take his seat in the Fire."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim — the most mass-transmitted of all the Prophet's ﷺ sayings in its wording, and the Muslim addition: "whenever a man relays from me a report while thinking it a lie, he is one of the liars")

A pretender invents; a witness repeats. The sūrah's verse, read against that warning, is the self-qualification of a man who knew his people's memory and staked the truth of every claim on its audit: you watched me, and I watched what was entrusted to me — in the same breath, the denial of miserliness and of suspicion.

**The Verse the Scholars Built a Discipline On**

One footnote on the afterlife of this verse deserves its place here, because 81:24 helped form a rule the tradition applies long past the Prophet's ﷺ person: withholding knowledge. "Whoever is asked about knowledge and conceals it will be bridled on the Day of Resurrection with a bridle of Fire" (Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī — the hadith is judged *ṣaḥīḥ* by Ibn Ḥibbān and others), the scholars of Sunna transmitted; and from the combination of that warning with *mā huwa bi-ḍanīn* they derived the classical duty of the muftī and teacher: the trust of the Unseen's traces — the Book and its transmitted knowledge — must be delivered in full, without grades, prices, or political rationing. The verse thus both defends its Prophet and, in the sūrah's own scheme of transmission, defines his successors: every bearer of the Qur'an afterwards stands beneath the same two negations — not hoarding, not guessing — which is why the great exegetes, from al-Ṭabarī down the centuries, placed at the head of their books of tafsīr the vow to report what was reported, to withhold nothing the chain carried, and to pronounce suspicion upon no bearer of what was not his to guess. The horizon of 81:23 and the openness of 81:24 together make the Book the property of everyone: seen publicly, delivered completely. That is the Makkan proof that ends here; the next verse, in a single clause, buries the last rival theory of where the words came from.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:25

> **Nor is it the speech of a satan outcast.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Alternative, Examined and Rejected**

The Makkan case against the Book had one final version, and the sūrah disposes of it with a single clause. Soothsayer (69:42), poet (69:41), madman (81:22) — each theory has now been met; what remains is the dark one: that the Qur'an is *inspiration from a jinn*, the product of a familiar spirit, the kind of possession Arabia already worshipped and feared. The verse answers at the level of facts the audience could verify about the unseen world, and its answer has three floors. First, the physics of the heavens: the devils who allegedly dictated this Book are barred from the upper air — *"We have guarded against every rebellious devil; they cannot listen in on the Highest Assembly, and they are pelted from every side, expelled, and for them is a lasting punishment"* (15:17–18; cf. 37:7–9; 72:8–9), the guarding being done, as we have just heard (vv. 15–16), by the very stars — *"We adorned the nearest heaven with lamps, and made them missiles for the devils"* (67:5). The same guard that barred eavesdroppers is the evidence of this Book's route. A text that arrived with full knowledge of the Unseen — of the sun's folding, of the grave, of the Balance — cannot have come from eavesdroppers who are shot at for listening. This is the sūrah's own architecture: the very stars by which it swore (vv. 15–16) are the missiles of this verse — the oaths were the evidence. 

**The Adulterated Snatch: How Devil-Speech Actually Arrives**

Second, the nature of diabolical speech: when devils do speak to men — the Qur'an grants the phenomenon — their product is what it describes: *"Do you not see that the devils go about them, confusing them?"* (19:83), *"and the devils inspire their friends so that they may argue with you"* (6:121) — the opponents' arguments included — and the snatch of truth that reaches a soothsayer's ear arrives, in the hadith ʿĀ'ishah reports, deliberately adulterated:

> *"That is nothing. The word of truth is what the jinn snatches and pours into the ear of his client; but when they return it, they add to it a hundred lies."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from ʿĀ'ishah, on the soothsayers)

A jinn-voice can produce fragments; this Book is one continuous, self-consistent, unscoreable unity — the test the sūrah elsewhere puts to its deniers: *"Had it been from other than God, they would have found in it much contradiction"* (4:82). 

**The Content Test: A Book at War With Its Alleged Authors**

Third, the content: revelation from a devil would promote what devils want; this Book dismantles their entire economy — the goddesses dethroned, the sacrifices to jinn forbidden, the soothsayers bankrupted, the stars demoted from lords to lamps, the buried daughters avenged. If the source of the Qur'an were the world of devils, the Qur'an is a war against that world conducted with the enemy's secrets fully known — and the third floor of the argument, then, is simply this: devils do not announce their own expulsion. 

**The Practice That Keeps the Door Shut**

The one verse the sūrah leaves *them*, so to speak, is the verse that outlaws appealing to them: *"When you recite the Qur'an, seek refuge with God from the outcast devil. Indeed, he has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord"* (16:98–99) — a command that has shaped the recitation of every Muslim for fourteen centuries: before a single letter of this Book is read aloud, its reader publicly declares that the devil is *refuged from*, not relied upon. The one-word description of the devil here — *rajīm*, "the stoned, the cursed" — seals it: the accursed is the one whose every channel of access has been, in the sūrah's own cosmology, closed by fire and by command. Where, then, did this speech come from? The sūrah has already sworn its answer through four oaths and five refutations: from the Lord of the worlds, by the hand of the noble messenger, to the heart of your companion. With every rival source refuted, the hearer's own exit is the only road left open — which is exactly what the next verse points to.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:26

> **So where are you going?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Ends Argument**

*Fa-ayna tadhhabūn* — four words, and they close every remaining road. The commentators transmit the sense unanimously: after the signs of the Hour (1–14), the oaths of the sky (15–18), the credentials of the bearer (19–21), the sighting on the horizon (22–23), the openness of the message (24), and the exclusion of devils (25) — where, now, is your going? The question is rhetorical in the technical Arabic sense (*istifhām al-taqwīʿ*, interrogation as overwhelming), but its verb is chosen with a realness that blunts all easy dismissal: *tadhhabūn*, from *dhahaba*, to travel. The hearers are not asked "what do you think?"; they are asked where they are *going* — as if the whole of their obstinacy were a road under their feet that they had never once stopped to look at. A man can resist an argument; the verse treats him instead as a traveler, and travelers can be lost. That is the genius of the Makkan address: the intellect retreats behind positions, but the feet are in the open.

The sentence also functions as a hinge within the sūrah's own road-system. Two verses later the Book will define the destination: *liman shā'a minkum an yastaqīm* — "for whoever among you wills to be upright, to go straight" (81:28). The geometry of the closing verses is therefore already visible here: there is one road — the straight one — and the single question this verse poses is precisely the question of every human being relative to it: *where are you going?* Between "where are you going?" and "to be made straight" there is nothing else in the sūrah; the whole second section is the space in which a person turns onto or turns away from the road, with heaven's own evidence laid along its edges like mile-stones.

**What the First Listeners Heard**

The verse's historical bite should not be smoothed away. The Makkan elite had spent a decade building alternatives to the Prophet's ﷺ claim, and the sūrah has just dismantled each in public: poetry, magic, soothsaying, madness, diabolism. What is left is not a rival theory but a *movement* — the very verb the Quraysh were living: turning away, walking off from the gatherings of recitation, "going" to their assemblies of refusal. The same scene stands in the sūrah this one closely follows in chronology, where God recalls their council about the Qur'an and Walīd's verdict — *"But what is the matter with these people, that they turn away from the Reminder as if they were startled wild donkeys fleeing from a forelion?"* (74:49–51). "Where are you going?" is directed at men in actual flight, and the simile of the previous sūrah lets us see the posture the question catches: the startled animal whose running has outrun its sense — flight as a habit mistaken for a destination.

At the same time the question was the sharpest possible instrument aimed at the *believers* of that small community, who heard it as a mirror: they too were going somewhere, daily — through persecution, trade, family, prayer — and the sūrah, a few verses from its end, makes each of them check the road under him. And the preachers of the tradition — this verse was a fixture of their assemblies of exhortation, as the commentators note — read *fa-ayna tadhhabūn* as the question to ask a congregation at the precise moment a habit turns into a life.

**The Question Kept Alive for the Reader**

Every age needs this verse for its own reason, and the reason is general: human beings are not primarily people who hold false beliefs; they are people going places — along the road of an appetite, a grudge, a career, a company, a drift — and the destination arrives with them, unnoticed, at the end of each week. The sūrah's method, here as throughout, is to interrupt the *going* with the question rather than to prescribe a detailed map: "where are you going?" is sufficient, because a person who actually stops to answer it is, by that very act, already on the straight road. This is why the classical preachers made the verse a standing formula of admonition — al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's circle kept it in their sermons precisely at the point where the audience felt most secure in its directions — and why a modern reader, walking past any advertisement, any argument, any easy indulgence that has begun to organize his calendar, can use the four words as a hand on the shoulder: the sūrah's final section has just proven the Messenger, sworn the case by the sky, and opened the book of the Day; the one thing the Book will not do is travel instead of you. *Fa-ayna tadhhabūn* stands at the end of all evidence as the beginning of decision. The next verses state the two conditions of that decision — the reminder on one side, the will on the other — and seal them with the will's true horizon.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:27

> **It is naught but a reminder for the worlds,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**What a Reminder Is — and Is Not**

*In huwa illā dhikr li'l-ʿālamīn* — "It is nothing but a reminder for the worlds." The sūrah reaches, in this clause, its final definition of the Book it has just authenticated, and the definition is deliberately small: the Qur'an is *not* news of something nobody had ever suspected; it is *dhikr* — remembrance, reminder, and in Arabic, also renown and mention itself. The choice of the word is a whole epistemology. A reminder does not install foreign content into an empty mind; it re-presents to a mind what it already knows and has neglected — which is why the same word names both the Qur'an and the act of worship, and why the hadith makes the Prophet's ﷺ task, in the words God quotes for him, exactly this: *"So remind — you are only a reminder; you are not a taskmaster over them"* (88:21–22; and cf. 16:44, where the Book is sent down "so that you may make clear to mankind what has been sent down, and so that they may reflect"). The Makkan hearers, who had been offered every rival account of the Book for a decade — magic, poetry, soothsaying, diabolism, madness — are now left with the plain description of what it does: it makes them remember. And what it makes them remember is precisely the content of the sūrah's first half: the Day they were built to know, the Lord they had half-known, the trust they had already placed for forty years in the man now reciting it. Even the Qur'an's own self-description in Sūrah al-Ḥijr belongs to this verse's meaning: *"Indeed, We — We have sent down the Reminder (al-Dhikr), and indeed, We will be its guardian"* (15:9): the Reminder is not a draft that accreted; it is kept, verse by verse, the way a covenant is kept by the One who made it.

**For the Worlds: A Scope Tested in the Open Market**

The verse adds the scope: *li'l-ʿālamīn* — for all the worlds. The commentators (al-Ṭabarī's tradition of the Companions' glosses; al-Ālūsī in the later centuries) take *ʿālamīn* here in its fullest extension: every community of rational creation — humans, and with them the jinn who also hear the Qur'an (46:29; 72:1) — and the sūrah's own closing address, "for *whoever among you* wills" (81:28), keeps that breadth from evaporating into abstraction. That the Book is universal is not, in this verse, a boast but a functional statement: a reminder works by reawakening what is already present in every human fitra, and *because* every human being carries that native knowledge, one Book can remind them all. The Prophet ﷺ drew the line between his mission and the provincial missions of earlier ages in one of the most widely transmitted of his sayings: *"Every prophet was sent, for his own people, as a warner; I have been sent as a warner to all mankind"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Jābir). And against the accusation that the reminder had been tailored for a single tribe, the Makkan market knew its counter-charge had failed: the Book had already taken from their vocabulary the very word for the universal (*ʿālam*, a name built from the root of "banner/sign, ʿalāma*) and hung the whole cosmos from it: for the worlds — the upright and the crooked, the first and the last.

**The Modesty of the Mode: Why "Reminder" Answers the Whole Sūrah**

Notice how the word *dhikr* ties this final section to everything the sūrah has said. Verses 1–14 were a reminder of the Day — not new data about the world's end, but the unveiling of what every human soul already knew dimly, the knowledge that makes a person flinch at injustice in a child's grave and fall silent at the edge of the sea. Verses 15–25 were a reminder of the Book's provenance — the answer to an accusation that itself had been a forgetting of what Makkah knew about the man: hence the sūrah's most human address, *ṣāḥibukum*, "your companion" — a reminder of a remembered life. Verse 26 asked *where are you going* — a reminder of the direction every walker has already chosen. And verses 27–29, now, gather the three movements into the sūrah's final word-triad: Reminder (27), will (28), the Will of the Lord of the worlds (29). A reminder does not coerce: that is the verse's defense of God against the charge that belief in this Book was forced, and it prepares the next verse's "whoever wills." A reminder does not vanish: that is its implicit answer to the folded sun — the lights of the world can be rolled up, but the Reminder, 15:9 says, is kept. And a reminder needs an audience that exists: which is the sūrah's last word about the human being in this world and the next, verse 14's "each soul" now enlarged to "the worlds" — nothing here is wasted, no listener is too small, and the God who swears by receding stars does not speak to vanish the speech. For the worlds: not for Makkah only, not for the Arabs, not for the seventh century — and the verse is where every later generation of reciters receives, formally, its own commission to transmit: the Reminder was for us before we were for it, which is why, when the sūrah says it is for the worlds, the worlds are, by that very act, in the room.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:28

> **for those of you who will to go straight,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Condition Written into the Invitation**

*Liman shā'a minkum an yastaqīm* — "for whoever among you wills to go straight." Grammatically the clause completes the previous verse: the Reminder is for the worlds, and its effective audience is the world's willing ones; the sūrah has reached the end of its evidence and enters, at last, the single region where evidence does its work — the human will. The verb *yastaqīm*, "to take the straight road," returns the sūrah to its own road-motif from verse 26 (*where are you going?*) and answers it: there is one going that is not going astray. The morphology matters: *istaqāma* is the form of seeking and undertaking the straight, not of possessing it finished — the upright road is a constant active correction, a traveler's verb, and the sūrah puts the whole religion behind one condition: the will to keep straightening.

What the verse establishes, before any theology of grace, is the fact of address: God speaks *to* someone whose yes or no is real. The Makkan sūrahs never say "believe because I override you"; they say *liman shā'a*, *fa-man shāʼa*, *la ikraha fi'l-dīn* — "whoever wills," "no compulsion in religion" (2:256) — and the Qur'an gives the prophet the permanent limitation of his office in verses this one mirrors from its nearest neighbors in the Makkan sequence: *"You are not a taskmaster over them"* (88:22); *"And say: The truth is from your Lord, so whoever wills — let him believe; and whoever wills — let him disbelieve"* (18:29); *"Had your Lord willed, whoever is on the earth would have believed all of them together. Would you compel the people to become believers?"* (10:99). The logic behind these texts, which the sūrah's own structure performs, is exact: a reminder achieves its purpose only as it is received, and receiving is an act of will; to compel assent would be to abolish the very thing being aimed at — the *amanat*, the freely carried trust of verse 21's vocabulary. Coerced faith is a contradiction in terms, the way a forced smile is a facial injury; and the God who could compel declines, throughout this Book, to be so contradicted for His own revenue.

**Why the Will Is the Whole Battle**

Once the sūrah has located religion at the will, the reader should measure what that costs, and the commentary tradition never flinches at it. To "will to go straight" is the one act every human being is capable of and none performs by accident: appetite, grief, laziness, company, fear, and habit all conspire to make it the least likely event in a day. The hadith of the sūrah's own section on the record (vv. 10, 14) teaches the same thing in legal form: deeds are written where wills are spent. And the Qur'an attaches, to the honest exercise of the will, a promise of increase: *"And those who strive for Us — We will surely guide them to Our ways"* (29:69). That verse, read as the next verse's own companion, contains the sūrah's whole practical theology of effort: the one who *wills* straightness is not left to walk it alone; the guiding is God's reply to the willing — the sūrah's own final verse will say the final word about the source of both. But note carefully the order the two verses keep, against every lazy reading of either: the will is first commanded and credited (28), only then enclosed (29); no one is told he cannot will, and no one is told he willed it by himself. The commentators of the middle school — the Sunnī *ahl al-sunna wa'l-jamāʿa*, in the language of their ʿaqīdah manuals — formulated precisely this order against the two historic errors: the Qadariyya, who made the will a self-owned property, and the Jabriyya, who made it a painted shadow. Here, at 81:28, the shadow theory is dead on arrival: the Reminder is, by definition, addressed to a will; and the verse's own plural, *minkum*, "among you," makes the willing mine, not the cosmos's. Whoever hears "whoever wills" is, in the very act of hearing it, being treated as someone who does — and in the very act of refusing, proves it. This is the verse's quiet apologetic masterpiece: even the objection to free will is an exercise of it.

**From Will to Way: The Verse's Own Definition of Straightness**

What is *al-istiqaama* in the Book's usage? The sūrah lets its neighbors fill the word, and they fill it completely. *"They who say: our Lord is God, and then are upright (istāqamū) — the angels descend upon them: do not fear, nor grieve, and receive the good news of the Garden you were promised"* (41:30) — straightness, in this verse's own vision, is the whole religion held down through time: the creed's point ("our Lord is God") and the road's length ("then"). The Qur'an's command to the Prophet himself is the classical anchor: *"So be steadfast as you are commanded (fa-staqim kamā umirta), and let not those who follow their whims turn you"* (11:112); and the hadith that made it a summary for a whole inquirer: Sufyān ibn ʿAbdillāh al-Thaqafī said: *"Messenger of God, tell me about Islam a statement by which I will ask no one else after you."* He said: *"Say: I believe in God — then be upright (wa-staqim)"* (Muslim). This is what the Reminder in 81:27 is *for*: not for spectacle, not for the folding of suns in the next life; for the daily, willful keeping of the line. And if the Makkan who first heard this verse heard a demand, the community that recited it ever after heard a dignity: the God who swears by the sky needs no slaves He coerces — He asks for willing ones, and treats His reminder as an honorarium paid into the freedom of the creature who accepts it.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:29

> **And you do not will but that God, Lord of the worlds, wills.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse the Two Extremes Have Always Feared**

*Wa-mā tashā'ūna illā an yashā'a llāhu Rabbu'l-ʿālamīn* — and the Makkan revelation, at its full length, closes exactly as its neighbor al-Muddaththir had closed: *"It is a reminder; whoever wills, remembers it — and they do not remember except that God wills"* (74:55–56), and as al-Insān will close it: *"And you do not will except that God wills — indeed, God is Knowing, Wise"* (76:30). No reader of this sūrah should miss that the Qur'an repeats this pairing of verses — human will affirmed, Divine will confessed — with the steadiness of a refrain, precisely because the refrain is fighting a war on two fronts in every generation. One front is the proud: whoever thinks his straightness was his own project, his will's private achievement; the verse takes the ground from under his feet. The other front is the slothful and the despairing: whoever says, since all is by God's will, striving is theater and sin is destiny; the verse — placed *second*, and only after the will was made the whole hinge of the Reminder — takes the excuse from his hands. The two verses in their order (28, then 29) are thus a single law in two breaths: the road is entered by willing, and the willing itself is held, at every moment, in a hand that never once griped it from you — held the way a parent holds a sleeping child's wrist while teaching it to walk.

**What the Classical Doctrine Won Against Its Rivals**

The verse's history is one of Islam's longest arguments. In the first Muslim century the Qadariyya — Maʿbad al-Juhānī and his circle in Basra, followed by the Muʿtazila in refined form — read 81:28 as the whole truth: the servant creates his own acts, and God's will has no share in them; ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar met them with a disavowal the Sunan collections transmit, and the Prophet ﷺ named them in a warning preserved by Abū Dāwūd, al-Nasāʾī, and Ibn Mājah: *"The Qadariyya are the devils of this community."* The opposite school, the hard Jabriyya, read 81:29 as the whole truth — man is a feather in the wind of compulsion — and against them the verse of *liman shāʼa* stood like a wall. The Sunnī settlement, reported from the Companions' circle onward in the formula the creeds still carry, refused both halves: *lā jabr wa-lā tafwīḍ, bal al-manziyla bayna'l-manzilatayn* — "neither compulsion, nor delegation of the affair, but the station between the two stations." Its most careful modern recovery is Ibn Taymiyya's: the servant acts by a real will and a real power that are his, and his acts are therefore truly his — praised, blamed, rewarded, punished as his — while both will and power are, at every instant, created and guided within God's universal will; nothing in the second clause cancels the first, because God's willing of my act and my willing of my act are not two rival causes on one level, but the upper and the lower half of one event, and the proof-text the tradition pins to this, in the hadith of ʿAlī at a funeral in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, is worth reciting to the end, because the Prophet's ﷺ own answer kills fatalism with the verse our sūrah will recite after it:

> *"There is not one of you but his seat in the Fire and his seat in the Garden are written."* They said: O Messenger of God, should we not rely on what is written and leave off action? He said: *"No — act; for each one is made easy toward what he was created for."* Then he recited: *"As for whoever gives and fears God, and affirms the best — We will make easy for him the ease"* (92:5–7). (al-Bukhārī 4949; Muslim 2647)

*Yuyassiruhū* — "is made easy": decree, in the Prophet's reading of our verse, is not a lock but a current; it carries — and the carrying is felt as willingness itself, which is why no man ever stood before God to plead "You willed it" about the pleasure he chose; and in the great hadith of the two prophets in Muslim, Ādam prevails over Mūsā in the quarrel over decree, showing that the chain of prior will runs beneath every blame: Mūsā said: *"O Ādam, do you blame me for a thing God decreed upon me before He created me by forty years, and He made you eat from the tree?"* Ādam said: *"Do you blame me for a thing God decreed upon me before He revealed the Torah to you by four thousand years?"* Then the Prophet ﷺ said: *"Thus Ādam prevailed over Mūsā"* (Muslim 2652). The argument closed both ways — no boasting, no excuse — and the sūrah's own next words open the third door, the one living believers use: *"If you disbelieve, God is Independent of you; yet He is not pleased with disbelief for His servants"* (39:7) — His will permits what His pleasure condemns, and the distinction is the very room in which warning, blame, and mercy all make sense. The Makkan fatalists who said *"If God had willed, neither we nor our fathers would have associated others with Him"* (6:148) were answered in the same key the sūrah plays here: *"Say: Do you possess any knowledge to produce for us? You follow nothing but assumption, and you are nothing but liars"* (6:148).

**The Devotional Grammar of the Verse**

What does a verse like this do to a person who recites it sincerely? It rewrites three reflexes at once. It makes thankfulness the only honest posture toward one's own guidance: *"Rather, God has conferred a bounty upon you by guiding you to the faith, if you are truthful"* — the Qur'an says to the bedouins who claimed more than their hearts had yet attained (49:17) — and the Prophet's practice embodied the same: the most certain of men woke at night until his feet swelled, and when asked why, answered, *"Should I not be a grateful servant"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim) — gratitude, not anxious self-earnings, was the engine of his striving. It makes humility the natural dress of every good deed: the believer who knows his willing was borrowed does not stand over others as a creditor of grace. And it makes reliance the working formula of the road: the two words the tradition made a constant of the tongue, *lā ḥawla wa-lā quwwata illā bi'llāh* — "there is no might to escape a disobedience, and no power for obedience, except by God" — which the Prophet ﷺ named, in the wording of the two Ṣaḥīḥs, *"a treasure of the treasures of the Garden"*: the admission of total dependence, delivered not as poverty but as wealth, a buried treasure at the exact point where the sūrah's reader stands. Even the theological vocabulary softens in this verse's hand: hearts, "between two fingers of the All-Merciful, He turns them as He wills" (Aḥmad and al-Tirmidhī, in the supplication the Prophet ﷺ taught: *"Yā muqalliba'l-qulūb thabbit qalbī ʿalā dīnik — Turner of hearts, keep my heart firm on Your religion"*). The believer who has just heard, over twenty verses, the stars fold and the sky peel, is left with the doctrine that the universe's one unfolded certainty is a giving God who keeps the keeping hearts: the sūrah's final phrase, *Rabbu'l-ʿālamīn*, says it in its most tender form — the worlds' *Lord*, the One who brings them up, sustains them, and completes what He begins.

**The Seal of the Sūrah**

Look back once along the whole arc the twenty-nine verses have walked. It began with twelve passive verbs at the end of the world — the sun folded, the scrolls spread — every creature a thing *done unto*, and it ends at the one active Will behind all passives, while, in between, it placed in a human breast the only verb that is asked of him: *willing* — "whoever among you wills to go straight." The sūrah's first section dissolved the world's false permanences; its second proved the Messenger's word; its seal now sets the word in the hand of the God of hands: you are going somewhere, and the going is real; the Reminder is in front of you, and it is for the worlds; and the will with which you turn — small, wavering, yours — is itself held in a Will that never wavers, which is pleased by you, and Lord of every world including this one. Read as the sūrah closes it — as every generation of reciters has closed it aloud, in prayer, in funeral rooms, at the dawn of a Ramadan — its last verses are not an ending but a surrender of authorship: whoever has folded his pride at 81:29 stands where 81:14's sentence finally reads glad — *each soul shall know what it has made ready* — and what it has made ready turns out to have been, all along, not its will but its answered surrender. Thus ends Sūrah al-Takwīr — the sūrah of the folded sun and the unfolded heart — and all praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds.
