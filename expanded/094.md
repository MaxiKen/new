# Sūrah al-Sharḥ (Chapter 94) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter of the Opened Breast**

Sūrah al-Sharḥ — "The Opening-Up," also called al-Inshirāḥ, "Expansion," and known in the earliest sources by the words of its first verse, Alam nashraḥ — is eight verses long and is, by the judgment of nearly every commentator, a chapter of the Makkan period. Its name is taken from the single verb that governs everything else in it: nashraḥa, "to open up, to widen, to make capacious." God asks His Prophet ﷺ, "Did We not open up your breast for you?" and the whole sūrah unfolds as an inventory of what that opening made possible: a burden set down, a reputation raised, a promise of ease that will not be broken, and finally two commands that turn the consolation into a career of worship. It is a short chapter, and yet the tradition has treated it as one of the most precisely balanced pieces of pastoral speech in the Qur'an — a text addressed to a man in distress and, through him, to every believer who has ever felt the weight of an assignment he could not carry.

The bulk of the commentators hold that it was revealed immediately after Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā, and in the widely transmitted list of the chronology of revelation — the order reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and recorded by al-Suyūṭī in al-Itqān — al-Ḍuḥā stands eleventh and al-Sharḥ twelfth. That sequence is not a trivial piece of cataloguing; it is the key to the chapter's meaning. Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā had answered a specific crisis: revelation had slowed for a period — the faṭrat al-waḥy of the prophetic biographies — to the point where an opponent of the Prophet ﷺ could be heard saying that his Lord had abandoned him, and God's reply came: "Your Lord has not forsaken you, nor is He displeased" (93:3). Sūrah al-Sharḥ continues in the same register, but shifts the emphasis from the denial of abandonment to the enumeration of blessings. Al-Ḍuḥā lists favours that concern the Prophet's outer life — the orphanhood that God covered with shelter, the wandering He returned to guidance, the poverty He enriched; al-Sharḥ lists favours that concern his inner life — the breast, the load on the back, the name, and then the law of ease that governs all suffering. Read in this order, the two chapters form a single movement from reassurance to capacity: you were not abandoned — and here is what We did for you instead.

**Two Sūrahs, One Breath**

The closeness of al-Ḍuḥā and al-Sharḥ was noticed by the earliest authorities in a concrete, practical way. Al-Ṭabarsī and others report that some of the Companions would recite the two sūrahs together in a single rakʿah, without interposing the basmalah between them, so continuous is their sense; on the strength of such reports a minority among the early scholars are said to have regarded them as originally one unit. The overwhelming position, however, is settled and practical: the Muslim community from the first generation fixed them as two distinct sūrahs, each with its own verse-count and its own place in the written muṣḥaf, and the numbering traditions of all the canonical readings count Sūrah 94 as eight verses. A minority report transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās that al-Sharḥ is Madinan; this is set aside by the vast majority, and a further nuance is preserved in the exegetical literature — that verses 5 and 6, "With hardship comes ease," were revealed in Madinah, perhaps in connection with the trials of the Journey to Ṭāʾif's aftermath or of Ḥudaybiyyah. The report is not decisive, but it is instructive: it shows that the earliest readers felt the chapter's two promise-verses were so bound up with lived crisis that they insisted those lines must belong to a moment of documented hardship. A third reading of the same verses ties them to the concession of the five daily prayers after the Isrāʾ and Miʿrāj — a gift given in the very night the Prophet ﷺ was consoled — which the commentators mention without dogmatism.

**The Historical Atmosphere: The Year of the Closed Doors**

Even where no authenticated occasion of revelation is transmitted for a given verse, the sūrah breathes the air of a specific period. The Makkan middle years — after the death of Abū Ṭālib and Khadījah, the two shields of the Prophet's ﷺ life, and after the violent rejection at Ṭāʾif — were years of physical danger, public mockery, household grief, and a missionary effort that appeared to produce almost nothing. The Qur'an itself testifies to the somatic pressure of that period in verses that the commentators bring to bear on this chapter's first verse:

> *"And We certainly know that your breast is constricted because of what they say."* (Qur'an 15:97)

The address there is to the same man, in the same condition: a chest that closes under the impact of insult. The prophetic biography preserves the texture of it. He walked into tribes at the pilgrimage season and was answered with stones and slogans; he was called madman, poet, soothsayer, and sorcerer; the child born in the household was buried under a nickname. It was into that climate — not into an abstraction — that "Did We not open up your breast for you?" was spoken. The chapter's four opening reminders are therefore not flattery but therapy: before God issues a single command to His Prophet ﷺ in this sūrah, He reminds him of four things God Himself has already done. The sequence is the logic of all effective consolation: the reassurance must be factual before it can be motivating.

**The Architecture of Eight Verses**

Structurally, the sūrah is built in three movements, and the movements answer each other. Verses 1 to 4 are four divine acts in the past tense, each governed by a second-person pronoun attached to the Prophet ﷺ — your breast, your burden, your back, your mention — and the first of them is framed as a negative interrogative, "Did We not…?", the most emphatical assertion Arabic possesses, because it asks the hearer to deny something he cannot deny. Verses 5 and 6 break away from the Prophet's private situation and state a universal law in the timeless present: with hardship, ease — said twice, the second time as a fresh, unattached assertion that drives the point home. Verses 7 and 8 then return to the Prophet ﷺ in two imperatives: toil when you are free, and let your want be directed to your Lord. The chapter thus moves from God's giving to God's commanding, and the commands are the natural human response to the gifts, not a condition for earning them. The rhyme scheme enacts the same turning: the first four verses end in the personal suffix "-ka" (ṣadraka, wizraka, ẓahraka, dhikrak), every blessing landing on him as an individual; the middle pair ends in the long, open "-rā" of yusrā; the closing pair ends in the stressed, muscular "-ab" of anṣab and irghab — fatigue and desire. A reader who hears the sūrah in Arabic will thus be carried by the sound from intimacy to relief to effort, before a single argument is consciously registered.

**Its Place in the Worship and Learning of the Community**

Because the sūrah speaks in the Prophet's own voice and about the Prophet's own pain, it has occupied a permanent place in Muslim devotional life. Its four opening verses are recited as a testimony of gratitude; its middle pair is the verse Muslims quote to one another in calamity, the way 2:156 ("to God we belong") is quoted at death — a shorthand for an entire theology of hope; and its last two verses are cited in the classical books as the Qur'anic charter for dividing a life between diligent work and undivided intention. The jurists and hadith masters, however, recorded the discipline that accompanies such affection: no authenticated text singles this chapter out for a fixed number of recitations or a prescribed rite at times of distress, and the scholars of the devotional sciences counselled that its promise be received by trust in God's word rather than by invented practice. The chapter does not need a ritual to be efficacious; it needs to be understood.

**The Themes That the Verses Will Unfold**

Across the eight verses that follow, several strands will be developed and interwoven. First, the psychology of revelation and mission: the capacity to bear a message is itself a gift, and the commentary tradition — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ālūsī, Ibn ʿĀshūr — spends real effort on the meaning of "expansion of the breast," weighing the metaphorical reading (ease, clarity, patience, love of the task) against the reports of a physical opening of the Prophet's ﷺ chest. Second, the doctrine of prophetic forgiveness: what exactly was "the burden that weighed upon your back," and how do the verses relate to the forgiveness granted in Sūrah al-Fatḥ (48:1–2), without importing the Christian notion of original guilt or the opposite error of claiming sinlessness in a way that makes the Prophet's ﷺ worship meaningless. Third, the theology of fame: reputation as something God gives and can revoke, and the careful boundary the Sunnah drew between exalting the Prophet's ﷺ mention and deifying his person. Fourth, and most broadly, the law of ease: a statement about the actual structure of human experience, argued philosophically, psychologically, and from the whole grain of the Qur'an, and defended against the misreading that makes it a sentimental guarantee of material outcomes. Fifth, the ethics of time and intention: what a believer does with an hour that has emptied itself, and to whom he takes the want that fills it. Alongside these, the commentary will draw on Arabic morphology and root-semantics, on asbāb al-nuzūl where they are reported, on the relevant cross-references with their actual wording, on authenticated prophetic traditions with their sources named, and on the readings of the classical exegetes, ending each section with the questions the verse puts to an ordinary life.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:1

> **Have We not expanded for you your breast,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question No One Can Answer in the Negative**

The sūrah opens, as several of the great consoling passages of the Qur'an open, not with a statement but with a question: a-lam nashraḥ — "Did We not expand…?" Grammatically this is an interrogative in the form of a negative (al-istifhām al-taqrīrī bi-l-nafy), and in Arabic rhetoric it is the strongest vehicle of affirmation available. A bare sentence, "We expanded your breast," can be doubted. A question that says "Did We not do it?" cannot be answered except by "Yes, You did," because the very form of the words presupposes that the denial is unthinkable for the one addressed. The same device governs the chapter that stands immediately before this one, where God says to the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"Did He not find you an orphan and give [you] refuge? And find you lost and guide [you]? And find you poor and enrich [you]?"* (Qur'an 93:6–8)

Three questions in a row, all of the same pattern, all unanswerable in the negative. Al-Sharḥ continues exactly that rhythm, and the effect on the hearer is not argumentative but therapeutic: God does not persuade the depressed prophet with new information; He makes him revisit information he already owns and had, in pain, forgotten. There is a pastoral principle here that every counsellor eventually learns: in distress, the sufferer does not primarily lack facts, he lacks access to them. The command to remember is therefore a mercy rather than a rebuke. And the verb that follows is in the perfect tense — nashraḥnā — because what is being recalled is already accomplished, not hoped for.

**Na-Sh-Ra-Ḥa: The Root and Its Field of Meaning**

The Arabic root sh-r-ḥ carries the basic sense of opening, widening, unfolding, and spreading out, and its derivatives in ordinary usage make the field of meaning vivid. Its derivatives in ordinary speech describe anything tightly packed being loosened: soil broken up and made workable, a folded cloth spread out, a body relaxing after strain. The sharḥ of a book is its "opening-up," the unfolding of a compressed text into exposition — the standard word for commentary in the Islamic tradition (sharḥ al-Bukhārī, sharḥ al-Qāmiūs) is exactly this: to take what is tightly packed and widen it so that breath can enter; and a market is munshariḥ when it expands and its prices moderate. From the same root comes the idiom sharḥa ṣadrī li-l-amr, "my breast opened to the matter," meaning "I found it agreeable, clear, and easy," while its opposite, ḍāqa bihi ṣadrī, "my breast became constricted by it," means distress, reluctance, and the wish to escape. The Qur'an uses both poles:

> *"So whoever God wishes to guide — He expands his breast for submission; and whoever He wishes to misguide — He makes his breast narrow and constricted, as if he were climbing up into the sky."* (Qur'an 6:125)

Notice how that verse proves precisely what this verse asserts, by showing the reverse case: guidance is defined, in the Qur'an's own vocabulary, not first as information received but as capacity created. The chest opens. Misguidance, correspondingly, is not first as information refused but as capacity contracted; the chest closes and every step upward feels like the impossible ascent "of a ladder into the sky," as several of the commentators gloss the simile. Al-Zamakhsharī remarks that the comparison to sky-climbing is chosen because a man climbing toward the sky would never find a footing, never come out anywhere, and would be crushed by the thinness of the air: an image of effort without purchase, exertion without progress. Ibn Fāris, in Maqāyīs al-Lugha, reduces the root to a single notion — width and clearing, al-wusʿ wa-l-īḍāḥ — and that single notion is enough to read the whole sūrah, because every favour in it is a widening: of the chest, of the shoulders, of the name, of the road out of hardship.

**"Your Breast": Where Burden Is Registered**

The word ṣadr (plural ṣudūr) is the chest, the breast, the upper part of the body that houses the heart and the lungs; and in Arabic's psychological vocabulary it is also the seat of thought, memory, and feeling — al-ṣadr is used for "the mind," "the inner self," and "the secret," as in the frequent Qur'anic phrase "what is concealed in the breasts" (mā kunnat ṣudūruhuṃ):

> *"Does he not know, when what is in the graves is poured out and what is in the breasts is acquired?"* (Qur'an 100:9–10; cf. 40:19, 64:4)

The commentators observe that God did not say "Did We not expand your heart," though the heart is the more precise organ of cognition and feeling in the language, because the ṣadr names the whole upper experience of the person: the place where anxiety is *felt*, where the breath shortens, where a weight sits. That choice gives the verse an uncanny realism. Modern readers of this verse repeatedly note that the vocabulary of emotional distress is somatic in every language: a person says "I can't breathe," "my chest is tight," "I feel crushed," "there is a weight on me." The sūrah speaks in the same idiom a contemporary psychiatrist would use, and it locates relief in the same body. This is not a poetic embellishment; it is an insight into how human beings actually experience being overwhelmed — first as a closing of the chest — and therefore how restoration has to begin: with opening.

**The Classical Readings: Six Layers of "Expansion"**

The exegetes did not restrict the verse to one referent, and their enumeration is itself instructive, because it shows how rich a single phrase of revelation can be when the text is taken as God's speech rather than as a newspaper sentence. Al-Ṭabarī gives the sense broadly: did We not widen your heart, O Muḥammad, and make it capacious — receptive and firm — for the prophethood, the revelation, and the commands laid upon you? Ibn Kathīr adds that it means being made easy and open to the affair, and cites the duʿāʾ of Moses as the Qur'an's own internal commentary on the phrase. Al-Qurṭubī transmits the view that it refers to God's filling the Prophet's ﷺ inner being with wisdom, understanding, and the light of revelation. Al-Bighawī and al-Ālūsī record a set of five specific referents, which may be laid out as follows.

First, the expansion is the God-given capacity for the message: the moral and spiritual equipment to receive revelation, endure its delivery, and never mistake its weight for an unbearable load. Second, it is the expansion of ease in religion — the breast made wide for the sharīʿah itself, so that its obligations are not experienced as chains; this is the sense in which the great majority of commentators read the connection between sharḥ and yusr (ease) that runs through the chapter, and it is why "expansion of the breast" became, in the language of the scholars of the heart, a characteristic of the rightly guided: the maxim recurrent in the ascetical literature, quoted by al-Muḥāsibī in al-Riʿāya and by Ibn al-Qayyim in Madārij al-Sālikīn, is that the believer's sign is a chest that dilates at obedience and contracts at sin. Third, it is patience and forbearance: al-Ṭabarī and al-Ṭabarsī both preserve the reading that God widened his breast to bear the abuse of the Quraysh — the capacity not to be destroyed by insult — supported by 15:97, quoted above, where the tightening caused "by what they say" is exactly what God now says He relieved. Fourth, according to some of the early interpreters preserved in the great commentaries, it is the relief that came to the Prophet ﷺ after the terrors and solitude of the cave of Ḥirāʾ: the account of the first revelation, in which "he took me and pressed me until exhaustion reached me, then released me" (al-Bukhārī, Book of Revelation), was followed by a returning composure and the opening of the breast to the mission. Fifth, it is the end of the pause in revelation (faṭrat al-waḥy) that precedes Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā: when the revelation returned, the constriction of abandonment lifted, and this verse names the lifting. Sixth, some read it as a reference to a particular physical event from the Prophet's ﷺ youth, transmitted in the Sīrah literature and glossed by al-Bayḍāwī: the two angels who opened his breast and removed the "dark clot" said to be the seat of Satan's share in the human breast.

**The Physical Opening: How the Scholars Handled a Literal Report**

That sixth reading deserves separate treatment, because it is the one point at which modern readers stumble. The tradition contains authentic narration about a washing and opening of the Prophet's ﷺ breast. In Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim's Book of Faith, in the hadith of the Isrāʾ transmitted by Anas ibn Mālik, the Prophet ﷺ describes how, at the threshold of the night journey, his chest was opened from one point to another, washed with Zamzam water, and restored; the versions in Musnad Aḥmad and in Ibn Isḥāq's Sīrah add what was put back into it — faith and wisdom.

Longer versions preserved in the Musnad of Aḥmad and in Ibn Isḥāq's Sīrah, going back to accounts connected with the Prophet's ﷺ childhood in the desert with Ḥalīmah al-Saʿdiyyah, mention two men in white who lay him down, open his breast, take out a black clot and cast it away — "this is the share of Satan in you," or, in other wording, "the portion of the accursed one." The hadith critics discussed these narrations at length; some, like Ibn al-Qayyim, defended their authenticity as sound reports; others, including a number of the later muḥaddithūn, judged the childhood versions weak or interrupted. The Qur'anic verse itself does not compel the physical reading, and none of the early exegetes treated it as the primary sense; the great majority placed the linguistic and spiritual meaning first and mentioned the bodily event as a possibility, sometimes harmonising the two by noting that God's purification of His Prophet's ﷺ inner reality and any physical sign of it are not in competition. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both add a point that settles the matter methodologically: a miracle, if it occurred, is not a defect in the verse's meaning but an extra layer of care — God preparing the vessel of the one who would carry the Qur'an, inside and out. For a contemporary reader, the durable content of the verse does not depend on the anatomical claim; it depends on the assertion that God personally equips the person He commissions.

**The Prayer of Moses: The Qur'an Explains Its Own Vocabulary**

The strongest internal evidence for the moral and spiritual reading is that the Qur'an teaches this very petition as something a prophet asks for in words. When Moses is commissioned at the burning bush and dreads Pharaoh's court, his prayer is not for a miracle but for capacity:

> *"My Lord, expand for me my breast, and ease for me my task, and untie the knot from my tongue, that they may understand my speech."* (Qur'an 20:25–28)

Read against this verse, the request becomes a science of leadership. Moses asks, in this order, for the chest, then the task, then the tongue: the interior state first, because the exterior performance flows from it. God answers the same three requests for Muḥammad ﷺ in this sūrah — the breast expanded, the burden removed, the name raised — and the parallel is one reason the commentators repeatedly invoke 20:25 here. The source commentary on this verse makes the same point in its own words: "The expansion of the breast relates to ease, especially in matters pertaining to religion, and is thus considered a characteristic of those who are rightly guided." Two logical consequences follow. First, if the breast must be *given* expansion, then the ability to live one's religion is not primarily a matter of willpower; it is a gift to be asked for. This is why the Muslim etiquette of duʿāʾ makes the petition for inner state prior to the petition for outer success. Second, if expansion is granted, then its absence is not a moral verdict on a person's faith but a signal that help is needed; the correct response to spiritual constriction is not self-condemnation but the prayer of Moses.

The Qur'an elsewhere turns the same phrase into an argument for the rationality of revelation. In Sūrah al-Zumar, having described the hypocrite's clinging to sin, God asks:

> *"Is one whose breast God expands for submission, so that he is upon a light from his Lord — like one whose heart is hard to the remembrance of God? Woe to those whose hearts are hardened to the remembrance of God; they are in clear error."* (Qur'an 39:22–23)

The same principle is stated as a two-sided law of divine "easing" in Sūrah al-Layl, where the direction of a person's want decides whether the road becomes easy or harder:

> *"So as for one who gave and was conscious of God and believed in the best reward — We will ease him for ease. And as for one who is stingy and considers himself self-sufficient and denies the best reward — We will ease him for hardship."* (Qur'an 92:5–10)

The logic is that conviction and capacity are not separable; guidance is not the delivery of data to a neutral mind but the creation of a mind able to receive it. The verse thereby also answers a difficult question about God's justice, which the mutakallimūn discussed under this heading: al-Ashʿarī and his school took 6:125 and 39:22 as evidence that the created capacity for belief is itself distributed by God, while the Muʿtazilī emphasis on human agency (in the vocabulary of this sūrah, the naṣb of verse 7) insisted that the expansion is God's response to a soul's willingness, not an arbitrary act. Ibn Taymiyyah's synthesis — repeatedly cited in the commentaries on this sūrah — is that God's guidance is itself mercy and justice at once: He opens the breast of whom He wills out of grace, and constricts the breast of whom He wills out of a justice that answers to that person's chosen turning-away; and the servant is never compelled into either state by a coercion that removes his desire. The verse therefore sits at a hinge of ʿaqīdah, and its consoling function is not damaged: a man told "We opened you" is a man told that his capacity was never his own achievement, so his failures cannot destroy him either.

**The Believer's Share in the Verse**

Though the address is to the Prophet ﷺ, the commentators from al-Ṭabarī onward treat the sūrah as a paradigm for every carrier of a trust, on the general principle that a singular address in the Qur'an which concerns a quality attainable by others extends to the community. Ibn Kathīr notes the verse as evidence that God prepares His servants for their tasks before the tasks arrive, and al-Saʿdī draws the practical conclusion: the heart's expansion is measured not by ease of circumstances but by the ease with which it accepts truth and absorbs hardship. Two tests can be attached to that claim without the slightest exaggeration. The first is the reaction to being corrected: a constricted breast defends, attacks, or collapses, while an expanded breast says "I did not know, I will change." The second is the reaction to being asked too much: the constricted chest counts demands as injuries, whereas the expanded chest sees what is being carried as bearable because it has been widened. This is precisely why the chapter's first gift precedes its last two commands; if you are asked to toil and to want God, you must first know that the chest you toil with was opened for you.

The application to daily life is concrete and unromantic. Before the believer accepts a role he cannot avoid — a difficult marriage, a caring responsibility, a community post, an honest conversation he has been postponing — the etiquette taught by this verse is to ask for sharḥ, not merely for success: to pray as Moses prayed, to learn as the commentators said, that the breast is widened by the very asking. Conversely, when the chest tightens and religious life begins to feel like a list of burdens, this verse tells the believer not that he is hypocritical but that he is human, and that the same God who widened for the Prophet ﷺ widens for the servant who asks. The analogy that best fits the tradition's own idiom is not that of a vessel filled but of a road opened: the constriction in the Qur'an is never described as a locked room, but as a narrow way, an ascent, a crushing; and expansion is the road becoming wide enough to walk — or, as the commentators put it, the driver whose sight, grip, and composure are given so that a dangerous path becomes passable.

**What the Verse Establishes for What Follows**

Finally, verse 1 does architectural work. Because it names the interior before the exterior, everything in verses 2 to 4 reads as a consequence: a man whose breast has been opened can have his burden set down, because he is no longer carrying it with a clenched body; a man whose breast has been opened can have his name raised, because the fame will not corrupt a heart that already knows the gift is God's. And the double "with hardship comes ease" in verses 5 and 6 is anchored here: ease is not first a change of circumstances but an expanded chest inside the circumstances, and only then, in God's timing, the change outside. The chapter's first word, then, is a question that cannot be denied and a diagnosis of the human condition at its most honest: we are constricted, and what we need most is to be opened.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:2

> **And removed from you your burden**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Opening to Unloading: The Order of the Gifts**

Verse 2 is joined to verse 1 by a single wāw — "And We removed" — and the conjunction carries the whole logic of the chapter's first half. The breast is opened, and then the load is taken off. That order is not decorative. A porter cannot set down a weight he has not first been able to bear standing upright; a person in panic does not lay down his responsibilities, he either drops them or is crushed by them. God's sequence says: first capacity, then release. It also says something about the nature of the release: the burden is not re-scaled, re-negotiated, or partially reduced, it is *put down* — waḍaʿnā — the verb of setting something on the ground, of placing an object where it no longer presses on a body. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Qurṭubī both take the phrase to mean that God lightened from him what he had been carrying, and the exegetes add that waḍʿ differs from takhfīf (mere lightening) precisely in this: a mere lightening (takhfīf) would leave something to carry, whereas waḍʿ removes the load from the carrier entirely. That distinction matters for how the verse is applied: the relief promised here is not that the weight becomes manageable by your strength, but that it is taken off you.

**Wizr: The Semantics of a Word That Means Both Sin and Sack**

The word wizr is one of the most semantically compressed terms in the Qur'anic vocabulary, and unpacking it explains a great deal of the verse's history of interpretation. In classical Arabic, al-wizr is primarily "the load that a beast or a person carries on the back or neck"; from that physical sense the language extended it to "the sin or crime that a person carries," because the Arab mind conceived guilt as weight — something that presses the bearer down and will have to be unloaded at an accounting. Both senses operate in the Qur'an simultaneously. In the verse that fixes the principle of personal responsibility, the wizr is a load of deeds, each person's own, none of it transferable:

> *"…and no bearer of burdens (wāziratun) will bear the burden of another."* (Qur'an 6:164; cf. 17:15, 35:18, 53:38)

And in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, in the portrait of the unlettered Prophet, the same root describes what the Torah itself foretold he would do:

> *"…and who removes from them their burden (iṣrahum) and the shackles (al-aghlāl) that were upon them."* (Qur'an 7:157)

That last verse is the single most important cross-reference for this verse, and the commentators from al-Ṭabarī to al-Ālūsī note it: iṣr is the heavy load, and the shackles are the binding obligations of the previous law — and the Prophet's ﷺ own burden being lifted is the sign of what he lifts from others. The logic of the chapter is thereby closed into a circle: God takes the weight off the messenger so that the messenger can take the yoke off the world. One who is himself unloaded can carry people without crushing them, and can lighten them without envy or exhaustion. The word also carries a further connotation noted by the lexicographers: wizārah, "bearing the load for another," produced the title wazīr, "vizier," the one who shares the sovereign's burden. So wizr in Arabic at once means sack, sin, and office — three things the verse quietly unifies: what he carried was a responsibility, and its weight was the weight of an office.

**What Was the Burden? The Exegetes Weigh the Options**

The transmitted interpretations of wizraka fall into several families, and the sources preserve them side by side without insisting on one.

The first and most widely quoted in the later commentaries identifies the burden with sins — the faults, short-comings, and anxieties about his own shortcomings that pressed on him. This is the reading al-Ṭabarī transmits from some of the early authorities, and it is the reading toward which the source commentary points when it says that "many commentators see verses 2–3 as an allusion to the Prophet's being forgiven all sins," linking this sūrah with Sūrah al-Fatḥ:

> *"Indeed, We have granted you a clear victory, that God may forgive you your sin that has preceded, and that to follow, and complete His favour upon you, and guide you to a straight path."* (Qur'an 48:1–2)

The second family refuses the sin-reading for theological reasons and takes the burden to be the weight of the prophetic commission itself: the dread of failing in it, the exhaustion of bearing revelation, the terror of its onset. Al-Shawkanī cites this as the more apt meaning in a Makkan context where no catalogue of faults existed to be forgiven. The third family, preserved by al-Baghawī and al-Bayḍāwī, takes the burden as the weight of his people's misguidance — the grief of watching a nation refuse and being unable to force it, which the Qur'an names elsewhere as close to self-destruction:

> *"Perhaps you will kill yourself with grief because they do not believe."* (Qur'an 26:3, with the sense of 18:6)

The fourth, and the one the source commentary records for verses 2–3 together on the authority of al-Bayḍāwī, is the burden of jāhilī ignorance and confusion that weighed on all Arabia and was lifted by the descent of God's commands — the darkness of a people without law, without a shared moral grammar, without a name for what they dimly knew was true. The fifth reading, found in some of the Sīrah-oriented tafsīrs, is autobiographical and specific: the pre-prophetic scruple — the weight Muḥammad ﷺ carried as a young man over the practices of his people, the festivals at the idols, the bloodshed, the female infanticide, which he could neither endorse nor reform, and from which the coming of revelation released him.

None of these is mutually exclusive, and Ibn ʿĀshūr's treatment of the sūrah's purposes (maqāṣid) suggests why the ambiguity was preserved rather than resolved: the verse's function is consolation, and consolation works best when the hearer can insert his own heaviest load into the definite noun. Arabic's use of the generic definite (al-wizr as a class, here anchored by the pronoun "your") permits exactly that openness — "the burden that was yours, whatever it was."

**The Theological Discipline: What "Forgiveness" Cannot Mean Here**

Because one of the readings identifies wizr with sin, the verse became a locus for a careful discussion of ʿiṣmah, the protection of the prophets. Three errors were marked off, and the commentators are explicit about all three. The first is any implication that the Prophet ﷺ bore guilt in the Christian sense — an inherited stain or a personal damnation-earning crime — which the Qur'an excludes on principle:

> *"…and no soul earns sin except against itself; and no soul bears the burden of another."* (Qur'an 6:164)

The second error is to read 48:2 as implying that the prophets were sinners like everyone else and then pardoned. The same chapter's closing verses qualify any such inference, describing him and his companions as a people "severe with the disbelievers, merciful among themselves… seeking favour from God and pleasure" (48:29); and the context of the verse, as the sīrah reports it, is the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah — a settlement whose apparent concessions cost him politically and which he accepted in obedience, after which God called it a manifest victory. Ibn Kathīr, in his commentary on al-Fatḥ, states the principle that became standard in the study of these texts: the prophets' "faults" (zallāt) are of a different order from ordinary sins — minor omissions of the better (tarak al-awlā), occasions on which a lower priority was chosen over a higher one — and they are followed immediately by divine address, which itself constitutes their repentance and their elevation. Ibn Taymiyyah argued the same in al-ʿAqīdah al-Wāsiṭiyyah and elsewhere: to say the prophets are preserved from deliberate, concealed, or persisting sin is not to say they were play-acting at humanity; their struggle was real, their accountability heavier than ours, since "the good deeds of the righteous are the sins of the prophets," a maxim cited by the early ascetics when these verses are discussed and attributed in the adab literature to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib. The third error is the opposite one: to conclude that if his load was lifted by grace, effort is unnecessary. The sūrah itself forecloses that by ending with "toil," and al-Qurṭubī observes that God's forgiveness in the Qur'an is systematically followed by a command to resume the work.

The psychological consequence of the correct reading is worth naming plainly. A person whose burden is *guilt* needs pardon; a person whose burden is *responsibility* needs relief; a person whose burden is *grief for others* needs to be told that the outcome was never his to produce. The verse addresses all three by leaving the noun open and attaching to it a single pronoun. What it does decisively is deny that the load belongs to him. God says "your burden," not "you." The man is not a burden, and his failure is not his identity. That distinction — the load is yours, but you are not the load — is why this verse has been used across fourteen centuries as the Qur'anic antidote to scrupulosity and despair, and why the scholars of the heart (Ibn al-Qayyim among them) treated excessive self-accusation as a subtler form of pride: it assumes that the weight exceeds what God intended to carry with you.

**Removal, Not Denial**

Note also what the verse does not do. It does not say "We showed you that the burden was imaginary." The load was real; its consequences were real; "that which broke your back" (verse 3) is stated without euphemism. Divine consolation in the Qur'an never resolves distress by convincing the sufferer that his suffering is an illusion. It accepts the full weight of the experience and then changes the relation to it. Here that is done twice: the breast is widened so that the man is larger than the load, and the load is set down so that the man is free of it. This is a logical structure worth teaching: relief comes either by enlarging capacity or by reducing demand, and God grants both — the former by gift, the latter by decree — while never demanding that the sufferer pretend. Anyone who has counselled a grieving or exhausted person will recognise the failure mode this avoids: "you shouldn't feel that way," which is a denial, against "it is truly heavy, and it is not yours to carry alone," which is the verse's method.

**The Burden in the Life of the Community**

Read as a charter for how burdens move, verse 2 also generates social law. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the authentic narration transmitted by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr:

> *"The Muslim is the one from whose tongue and hand the Muslims are safe; and the emigrant is the one who abandons what God has forbidden."* (al-Bukhārī 10; Muslim 40)

and, in the wording of Muslim's Book of Virtues,

> *"Whoever relieves a believer of a hardship of this world — God relieves him of a hardship of the Hereafter; and whoever eases matters for one in difficulty — God eases matters for him in this world and the next."* (Muslim 2699)

That second text is, in effect, the community's implementation of waḍaʿnā: because God's attribute in this verse is "the One who sets down," His servants are to be the means by which it is set down for others. The practical forms are unglamorous and enumerated in the fiqh books: paying a debt, delaying a lawsuit, taking notes for a struggling student, driving an elderly neighbour, not forwarding a private failure, giving a tired spouse the night off, forgiving a shortfall rather than insisting on the letter. Conversely, the jurists derived from the same principle a prohibition on inventing burdens — hence the strong Prophetic warnings against extremism in devotion:

> *"Ruin! — he said three times — the extremists will be ruined."* (Muslim 2670)

and

> *"This religion is easy, and no one makes religion hard upon himself except that it overpowers him. So be upright, approximate, and seek help by travelling in the early part of the day and the last, and a little of the night."* (al-Bukhārī 39)

The verse is thus simultaneously a report about the Prophet ﷺ and a rule for the ummah: what God did for the carrier, the carriers owe one another.

**Why This Verse Comes Second**

Finally, the placement of the unloading between the opening of the breast and the raising of the name is precise. A reputation granted to a man still weighed down is dangerous: an over-burdened person who is suddenly celebrated will either use fame as an anesthetic or collapse under the new expectations it adds. God's order — capacity, relief, then honour — is a principle of formation. It also disciplines the reader's own ambition: the sūrah does not promise the prominence of the exhausted, but the prominence of the one whose load has already been taken off. And for the person who suspects that the burden he is carrying is a punishment, verse 2 delivers its quiet contradiction: the same God who removes the load of His best creature removes the load of His worst when he turns to Him, since the promise is general:

> *"…and whoever is reverent to God — He will make for him a way out, and will provide for him from where he does not reckon; and whoever relies upon God — He is sufficient for him."* (Qur'an 65:2–3)

The burden was real, it was breaking him, and it is described here in the past tense — as something already on the ground, in the distance, behind. That tense is the verse's final gift to whoever recites it: not "it will be lightened," but "it has been."

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:3

> **That which pressed heavily upon your back?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Relative Clause That Tells the Truth About the Weight**

Verse 3 does nothing new; it merely describes the burden of verse 2, and yet the entire force of the passage lives in that description. Alladhī anqaḍa ẓahrak — "the one that made your back creak." The relative clause (ṣila) is a rhetorical device the Arab stylists call al-ittiṣāl li-l-mubālaghah, an extension for the sake of intensification: instead of saying "your heavy burden," the sentence gives the burden a verb and a victim. It is a grammatical choice that refuses to minimise. God could have consoled by understatement; He consoles by understating nothing. This is a small but decisive data point for the Qur'an's psychology of comfort, and the commentators noticed it: al-Zamakhsharī remarks that mentioning the extremity of the load magnifies the favour of removing it, because a relief is measured not by the hand that gives it alone but by the size of what it replaced.

**Anqaḍa: The Sound a Spine Makes**

The verb anqaḍa is from the root n-q-ḍ, whose core meaning is the sounding, cracking, or groaning produced by pressure. The noun naqīḍ denotes the creaking of a load-bearing thing: the beams of a heavily loaded tent-pole, the saddle-tree under an overweight rider, the bones of a camel that has been overloaded and complains aloud. Al-Farrāʾ and Abū ʿUbayda, cited by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī in this very verse, both gloss anqaḍa ẓahraka as "it made a sound with your back," and they preserve the Arab idiom: anqaḍa al-ḥamlu ẓahr al-baʿīr — "the load cracked the camel's back." The source commentary, citing al-Bayḍāwī, puts it with characteristic compression: "Weighed heavily (anqaḍa) implies something pushing down so hard that one's back makes a sound." Some of the grammarians read a variation of form: naqaḍa (to be broken in pieces) against the transmitted anqaḍa (to cause the sounding), with the fourth-form verb carrying the causative sense — "that which *made* your back groan." Either way, the image is auditory. And that is the achievement of the single word.

Consider what an auditory image does that a visual one could not. A picture of a heavy sack says "weight." A sound says "the weight is at the limit, and the structure is announcing it." The back is not screaming in the verse; it is creaking — the sound a thing makes before it fails, the sound of material at the outer edge of tolerance. The sūrah, then, describes not collapse but the noise of near-collapse, and it says this about the Prophet ﷺ. The theological implication the commentators draw is severe and gentle at once: the man who delivered the Qur'an to the world was, at some point, at the limit of what a human back can take. Revelation's bearer was not an unfeeling statue, and God does not pretend otherwise. He names the sound.

**The Physiology of Load, and the Dignity of Naming It**

The Arabic here is a piece of lay physiology. A load that "creaks the back" is, in modern terms, a load carried on the axial skeleton, and the body's response to sustained overload is famously not silent: muscular guarding, thoracolumbar strain, the shallow breath of a chest that cannot expand because the trunk is bracing, and — as anyone who has carried a heavy pack uphill knows — the involuntary groan or grunt that the Arabs took as the back itself speaking. The choice of ẓahr (back) rather than a word for the arms or hands is also exact: the back is the Qur'an's own image for support and endurance. When God commands the believers to be steadfast, the language is dorsal; when Moses asks for his brother, he uses the same anatomy:

> *"And appoint for me a helper from my affair — Hārūn, my brother — increase my strength (shuddū ʿazrī) by him."* (Qur'an 20:29–31)

ʿAzr means the back or the girdle of the back; "increase my ʿazr" is literally "thicken my back," the same figure as the English idiom "I need someone to have my back." So verses 1 and 3 of al-Sharḥ work within a single embodied metaphor: the chest is widened so that breath and resolve return, and the back is unloaded so that support is restored. Between them, the whole person — the front that faces people and the rear that carries — has been treated. It is a striking instance of the Qur'an's refusal to split a human being into a mind with problems and a body that merely hosts it. In the sūrah, suffering is total, so relief must be total, and the language names both.

**Which Load Was Nearly Enough to Break Him?**

The same plurality of referents preserved for wizr applies here, sharpened by this verb. Three readings dominate the classical discussion. On the reading that the burden is the weight of the commission, verse 3 describes what the early accounts show repeatedly: the onset of revelation as a physical assault — al-Ḥārith ibn Hishām asked the Prophet ﷺ how revelation came to him, and the answer, in al-Bukhārī's Book of Revelation, describes the most severe of its modes as "the ringing of a bell," after which "it lifts from me once I have grasped what he said" (al-Bukhārī, Kitāb Bad' al-Waḥy); and ʿĀʾisha reported seeing him on an intensely cold day with sweat streaming from his forehead as it descended (al-Bukhārī, same Book). Zayd ibn Thābit described the physical weight of sitting beside him during reception, and the Sīrah literature preserves the report that when revelation came to him while riding, the mount could not bear it and pressed to the ground. The companions who carried knowledge after him — Anas, Abū Hurayrah, Zayd ibn Thābit — spoke of the *weight* of a single transmitted sentence; Zayd's account of the exhausting labour of collecting the muṣḥaf under Abū Bakr and ʿUmar is a later echo of the same phenomenon:

> *"By God, if they had ordered me to move a mountain, it would not have been heavier for me than the collection of the Qur'an which they ordered me to do."* (al-Bukhārī, Book of the Virtues of the Qur'an, in the chapter on the gathering of the muṣḥaf)

On the reading that the burden is grief for his people, verse 3 is the counterpart of the Qur'an's two kindest rebukes on that subject: "perhaps you would kill yourself in grief over them, if they do not believe in this statement" (18:6), and "perhaps you will kill yourself because they do not believe" (26:3). It forbids a dangerous illusion in every teacher, parent, and preacher: that the failure of the message is the messenger's bodily fault, to be punished by further self-expenditure until the back gives. On the reading of the sin-forgiven, verse 3 stresses that even the "small" faults of the near-divine weigh catastrophically on their bearer — a principle the scholars of ʿaqīdah state as: the good of the righteous is the sin of the prophets, because the proximity is so great that a hair's movement is a lurch. The psychology of the verse is visible in the two low points of the Makkan mission as the Sīrah literature records them — the recommendation of the flight to Abyssinia, a land "in which there is no injustice," and the refusal at Ṭāʾif to curse the people who had stoned him, answered instead with a prayer for their descendants' obedience (in the accounts transmitted by Ibn Isḥāq and in the Sunan literature). In both, the creaking back is described without euphemism, and in neither is the load allowed to be the last word.

**The Creaking Back and the Modern Reader**

Few images in the sūrah are more immediately translatable into contemporary experience. The load that "creaks the back" is the language of the over-responsible: the person who is the only employed adult in a family, the carer of a sick parent, the one who holds the memory of a community's losses, the immigrant carrying a household's upward mobility on a single salary, the man who has been "the strong one" for so long that no one asks whether he is still standing. Medicine has given that condition a name — burnout — and described its three signs precisely: exhaustion, the feeling of ineffectiveness, and detachment. What is striking is that the verse's remedy is not rest alone. In the sūrah the creaking back is addressed by a widened chest (v.1), an unloaded spine (v.2), an elevated name (v.4), a law of coming ease (vv.5–6), a re-ordered use of free time (v.7), and a redirected desire (v.8). That is a five-part treatment plan, and its sequence is instructive: it begins with capacity and relief, ends with re-direction of the will, and puts a promise of future ease between them. Rest is not even mentioned as such — because the sūrah's answer to exhaustion is not primarily vacation but meaning and support.

There is a further point of social ethics here that the classical commentators touch and modern readers should not miss. The verse is God speaking to a man about a load that the man's own community had helped create: the rejection, the mockery, the boycott, the stoning at Ṭāʾif. A society that piles duties on the conscientious and gives them nothing is, by the sūrah's measure, not merely ungrateful but theologically illiterate; the creaking back of the reliable person is a public fact that God mentions in revelation. The duty that follows from verse 3 is therefore distributive: burdens are to be shared before they are praised, and the Qur'an's own rebuke — "perhaps you will kill yourself" over their unbelief (26:3) — shows that self-destruction is not commended as a form of sincerity. The petitioner, likewise, is not to be pushed onto the one who is already carrying: "as for the petitioner, do not repel him" (93:10). The Prophet's ﷺ practical leadership modelled it: he relieved the heavy-laden in the most literal sense — in the report of Abū Hurayrah that the Prophet ﷺ said: "If I were invited to a trotter I would accept, and if a shoulder of meat were given to me as a gift I would repay it" (al-Bukhārī, from Abū Hurayrah), and in the report in both Ṣaḥīḥs of the man who seized his cloak so roughly that it marked his neck and demanded a share of the treasury, and was met with a laugh and a gift rather than a rebuke. A community that claims to love the one God unloaded must not re-load him.

**The Grammar of Praise Inside Complaint**

Stylistically, the verse is also a lesson in how the Qur'an lets a complaint exist inside a thanksgiving. The whole passage is an enumeration of favours — "Did We not… and We did… and We did" — so v.3's description of the load is technically a description of an evil. Yet the evil is mentioned *because* the good is being counted, and the effect is that suffering is not erased from the record but annexed to gratitude. Islamic spirituality has a technical name for this: al-shukr ʿalā al-balāʾ, thanksgiving for the affliction, on the ground that the affliction was the occasion of the relief and of the rank. The Prophet ﷺ expressed it in a hadith agreed upon:

> *"How wonderful is the affair of the believer! All of his affair is good, and that is for no one except the believer. If prosperity attends him, he is thankful, and that is good for him; and if adversity attends him, he is patient, and that is good for him."* (Muslim 2999)

Thus the creaking back is, in the finished account, part of the praise-song: the sound is remembered only in the same breath as the setting-down. For the reader, the discipline is to complete the sentence — to describe the weight and the relief in the same act of speech, and to refuse both the ingratitude that forgets the load and the despair that forgets the lifting.

**Why the Question Mark Belongs at the End of Verse 4**

Arabic manuscripts place the rhetorical question across verses 1 to 4: "Did We not expand… and remove… that which pressed… and raise…?" The reader is made to carry the interrogative through four lines, so that the fourth answer arrives after a small build of expectation. Verse 3's position in that chain is the lowest point of the description and the hinge of the tone: it is where the reciter hears the strain, and after which every clause is ascent. The reciters' practice at this pause reflects the same syntax: the sense does not rest at the end of verse 3, because the relative clause belongs to what came before and the question still hangs open; the skilled reader therefore joins it forward rather than settling. The ear climbs with the meaning. It is a technical observation, but it confirms what the words themselves say — the chapter is structured as an ascent out of a crushing.

**The Verse's Claim on the Reader**

Taken together, verse 3 makes three assertions that a serious reader cannot dismiss. First, that God is not embarrassed by the extremity of human suffering, and will describe it in a sacred text in the language of creaking bone. Second, that the load is measured by the One who removes it, not by the observers who comment on it: no one standing beside a bent back can honestly estimate its weight, and no community should offer advice to a person God has already addressed with "We removed it." Third, that the description of suffering in this chapter is always subordinate to the description of deliverance; the relative clause here exists to make verse 2's verb glorious. The creak is the last sound of the load, and the next sound the sūrah records is the long vowel of ease.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:4

> **And raised high for you your renown?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Gift Is the One That Outlived Him**

Verse 4 completes the quartet of favours and changes their direction. Verses 1 to 3 are about the man's interior and his load: the chest, the back, the weight. Verse 4 is about his name in the mouths of others — and it is the only one of the four whose effects the Prophet ﷺ himself could not have experienced in full. God says: wa rafaʿnā laka dhikrak, "And We raised for you your mention." The verb rafaʿa is the same root as the miʿrāj's raising and as the raising of a building's structure: elevation, not creation. The commentators stress that God did not say "We *gave* you a name" — he had one; "We did not make you famous" — he was known in Makkah as al-Amīn, "the Trustworthy," long before prophethood. What was done was to raise what existed, and the passive construction (rafaʿnā, "We raised") forecloses the possibility of self-promotion: this honour has no human author. Al-Bayḍāwī's gloss is compact and typical of the whole genre: your mention is raised in being joined to God's own mention, and it is spoken in the call to prayer, in the testimony of faith, and on the tongues of the faithful.

**Dhikr: The Arabic Field of "Renown"**

Dh-k-r covers a wide field: to mention, to remember, to make known, to speak of, and — as a noun — rumour, fame, poetry, praise, and reproach alike (one may have bad dhikr as well as good). Its opposite is nisyān, forgetting; and its technical religious sense, dhikr as "the remembrance of God," is the same faculty turned to its highest object. When the sūrah says "your dhikr," then, it means the sum of how his name lives in the consciousness of others: what is said about him, what is recalled when he is remembered, the mental presence he enjoys. The choice of "your mention" rather than "your rank" (martabakah) or "your status" (jāh) is careful: the elevation is in the *saying*, not in worldly dominion, and it can therefore be enjoyed by the poor, the persecuted, and the dead.

The source commentary records the classical explanation exactly: "his renown was raised when he was given the title 'Messenger of God' — a title joined to the Name of God — and placed in the testimony of faith." The same reading is preserved by al-Ṭabarī, who transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās the gloss most quoted at this place: the raising of his mention is the saying "there is no god but God, Muḥammad is the Messenger of God" — the Prophet's name inserted, by God's act, into the sentence that opens the door to Islam. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's version, cited in the same places, is that God made his mention high in this world and the next and set it inside the sharīʿah itself, so that his name is never spoken in worship or obedience except joined to the name of God. And another report from Ibn ʿAbbās — preserved in al-Ḥākim's Mustadrak and in al-Bayhaqī's Dalāʾil al-Nubuwwah, with chains the critics have evaluated variously — says: "When Sūrah al-Sharḥ was revealed, Satan came in the form of an old man and gathered Quraysh, saying: 'He has been given a rank you cannot reach; he is mentioned together with the Name of God!' — and it is for that reason that the verse's own wording says 'raised.'" The report's historicity is not established, and the commentators mention it as illustration rather than as evidence.

**Four Places Where a Man's Name Is Now Indissoluble**

The concrete content of the elevation has been tested by the commentators against observable practice, and four institutions carry it, all of them legislated rather than accidental.

The first is the shahādah, the testimony of faith, the sentence that marks entry into Islam and is repeated, in every cycle of the canonical prayer, by every praying Muslim: "I bear witness that there is no god but God, and I bear witness that Muḥammad is the Messenger of God." On the counting of the commentators, a Muslim speaks the Prophet's ﷺ name in this form at minimum seventeen times a day in the five prayers, without counting the adhān, the iqāmah, the call to Friday sermon, the talbiyah of Ḥajj, the formulae of the funeral prayer, and the marriage contract. Whether or not the arithmetic is exact for every school, the qualitative point stands: no other human being in history has had his name woven so deeply and so repeatably into the daily syntax of a rite.

The second is the adhān itself, where the two testimonies stand back to back in a single breath — "I bear witness that there is no god but God; I bear witness that Muḥammad is the Messenger of God" — and where the Prophet's ﷺ elevation is tied to the call that marks the hours of the world's day. The third is the ṣalāh ʿalā al-nabī, the blessing upon the Prophet recited in the tashahhud and commanded by revelation:

> *"Indeed, God and His angels send blessings upon the Prophet. O you who have faith, send blessings upon him and greet him with a greeting."* (Qur'an 33:56)

Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, and the majority of the early exegetes explain "God's blessing upon Him" as "God's praise of him in the presence of the angels," from which it follows that the verse of the sūrah is being fulfilled in both directions: God raises his mention among the highest assembly, and commands the lowest assembly to do the same. The fourth is what the source commentary mentions in a phrase — "versions of which are recited in the call to prayer and in the greeting recited silently at the end of each cycle" — namely the liturgical embedding, which means the elevation is not maintained by sentiment, memory, or institutions of scholarship, but by an act performed individually and in congregation, validly, whether the worshipper is eloquent or illiterate, in a mosque or in a field.

**What the Elevation Was Not**

Because the phrase is so easily over-read, the tradition hedged it with two boundaries. The first: elevation of his mention is not deification of his person. The Prophet ﷺ himself, with the sūrah in front of his community, forbade the very excess that a "raised name" invites:

> *"Do not exalt me as the Christians exalted the son of Maryam; I am only a servant, so say: 'God's servant and His Messenger.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3445)

This is the sharīʿah's own reading of this verse: the name is raised, and it is raised precisely *as* "servant," so that the two halves of the sentence protect each other. Al-Qurṭubī cites the same hadith in this context, and Ibn Kathīr notes that the height of his rank is that he is called God's servant in the most intimate places of worship.

The second boundary: the honour is not worldly domination. The verse was revealed in Makkah, when the man whose name God raised was being boycotted in a valley, mocked in public, and unable to bury his dead safely — the year of the deaths of Abū Ṭālib and Khadījah. It follows that rafaʿ al-dhikr is not measured by wealth, military success, or institutional power. Some of the commentators mention, against this, the historical objection raised by doubters — "his fame was made by his followers, not by revelation" — and the answer they give is structural: the fame in question is liturgical, legislated, and doctrinally regulated, and none of the three is the kind of thing a follower base spontaneously produces for a dead leader.

**The Mention in Earlier Scripture, and the Grammar of Righteous Reputation**

The source commentary notes a second classical interpretation: "Others say the verse refers to the mention of Muḥammad in previous scriptures," an interpretation associated with al-Shawkānī's Fatḥ al-Qadīr and with a broader reading of verses like 7:157 ("who find him written with them in the Torah and the Gospel") and 61:6 ("a Messenger who will come after my name is Aḥmad"). Whether or not one counts those texts as fulfilment of this verse, they share a principle the commentators drew out: his mention was being raised before he was born, and the raising in his own lifetime was the second stage of a process he had no access to at the first. The sūrah's own logic of past tense covers both.

A third reading, cited by al-Shawkānī and al-Ālūsī, extends the verse beyond the Prophet's name to his sharīʿah: the dhikr raised for him is the permanence of what he brought — "the religion that outlived every religion he opposed." The historical fact underlying the gloss is not trivial. Makkah in the 620s was full of cults that had flourished for centuries and were about to vanish entirely; the memory of Ḥubal, al-Lāt, and Manaf is a footnote, while the name that stood opposite them in the same valley is recited five times daily, fourteen centuries later, in every time zone on earth.

**The Theology of Reputation: Why God, Not the Crowd**

The verse teaches a doctrine of fame that the surrounding culture, ancient and modern, denies. The world's assumption is that renown is seized — by talent, propaganda, inheritance, or outrage. The sūrah asserts that renown is *raised*, and that God raises it for whom He wills. Two supporting texts fix this principle in the believer's mind. The first is Qur'anic:

> *"Say: I ask you for no reward for it, and I am not of the make-believers."* — and more directly: *"Indeed, those who believe and do righteous deeds — the All-Merciful will bestow on them a love (wudd)."* (Qur'an 19:96)

Al-Ṭabarī glosses the verse in the sense of God placing for them acceptance and love in the hearts of the people. The second is prophetic, and it is the mechanism by which the principle operates, in a hadith whose chain of transmission is scattered across the collections and whose meaning the scholars of the Sunan received:

> *"Whoever pleases God by displeasing people, God will please people with him; and whoever displeases God in order to please people, God will displease with him the people and the devils who follow them."* (reported in variant wordings; the sense is attested in the Sunan literature)

and, with an authenticated chain in a closely related form:

> *"Whoever seeks the pleasure of God although people are angered, God will suffice him against people; and whoever seeks the pleasure of people although God is angered, God will leave him to people."* (al-Tirmidhī, from ʿĀʾisha; classed ḥasan by scholars of the hadith)

The verse thus relativises the greatest of the modern anxieties. The Prophet ﷺ did not campaign for reputation; he carried a message, and reputation attached itself to the message as a divine act. The elevation was an effect of obedience, not an aim of strategy.

**The Contrast the Sūrah Does Not Name but the Qur'an Does**

Readers of the sūrah naturally ask about the counterpart case, and the Qur'an answers elsewhere with a precision that throws this verse into relief. Pharaoh, whose entire career was the manufacturing of renown, declared:

> *"O chiefs of the people, I know no god for you other than me."* (Qur'an 28:38)

The verdict on his fame is not silence but infamy: his name is recited in every generation that recites the Qur'an, and not as praise. Even the preservation of his body, which the doubters of later ages make so much of, is described in the text as a warning rather than an honour:

> *"So today We will save you in your body, that you may be a sign to those who come after you."* (Qur'an 10:92)

Hāmān, the minister who became an object of worship at Pharaoh's command (28:38-39); Qārūn, "so We caused the earth to swallow him and his home" (28:81); the dynasties of ʿĀd and Thamūd, whose grandeur is now a warning recited at the places where they lived (cf. 69:6-8, 89:6-8): all of them are the negative image of wa rafaʿnā laka dhikrak. The elevation of a name is not a reward for prominence but for truthfulness; and the same verb that raises the sincere sinks the self-promoter into a cautionary mention. It is a sobering complement to the verse: what God raises, no human can pull down; what God abandons to fame, no human can save.

**A Modern Application: The Man Who Never Marketed His Name**

Contemporary life has made reputation an industry, and the verse functions as a critique with immediate operational content. A person who builds a public identity on outrage, on the borrowed authority of others, or on a curated image, is engaged in precisely the activity the sūrah says he cannot succeed at, because raising is not his verb. The practical tests the verse generates are simple: Would I do this work if no one knew my name? Would I correct the error in my own writing at the cost of being seen to have been wrong? Would I hand the credit to the person who earned it? The tradition's sharpest formulation of the answer is the Prophet's ﷺ own refusal, recorded in the Musnad of Aḥmad and Sunan collections with sound chains, when he was offered the annihilation of a hostile tribe:

> *"I was not sent to curse; I was sent as a mercy."* (Muslim 2597)

His mention rose in the very act of declining the instrument by which leaders ordinarily buy fame.

Finally, the verse contains a gift to the obscure. The Prophet ﷺ was, in the worldly sense, one man in a valley of merchants and poets, without a scribal corps, an archive, or an empire. If renown were a function of leverage, the case would be unintelligible; as an act of God described in a four-word sentence, it is the sūrah's most empirically testable claim. Anyone may check it, five times a day, and the same is promised, in principle, to the servant whose work is unseen and whose name is held in God's hand rather than in the crowd's:

> *"And remember your Lord much."* — and, in the wording of a ḥadīth qudsī in al-Bukhārī: *"If he mentions Me to himself, I mention him to Myself; and if he mentions Me in a gathering, I mention him in a gathering better than theirs."* (al-Bukhārī 7405; Muslim 2675)

That is the whole sūrah's theology of fame in a single pair of sentences: the mention that matters is the one granted by the One who owns all gatherings, and it is granted first to those who do not chase it.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:5

> **For indeed, with the hardship comes ease.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pivot of the Chapter: From One Man's Story to a Law**

Verse 5 is the hinge of the sūrah. Everything before it is addressed to a single man about four specific things done for him in the past; everything from here onward is stated as a general rule in the present tense and then turned into two commands. The Arabic marks the transition with a single particle: fa-inna maʿa al-ʿusri yusrā, "For [fa] indeed, with the hardship, an ease." The opening fa is what the grammarians call al-fāʾ al-taʿlīliyyah or al-fāṣilah — the particle of inference and of consequence. The sentence therefore functions as a conclusion drawn from the four verses before it: *since* We opened your chest, unloaded your back and elevated your name, the pattern of your history is now a law for your community: hardship never travels alone. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī, and al-Shawkānī all note the inference, and it is what rescues the verse from becoming a platitude: it is not advice offered to the unlucky, but an argument from demonstrated generosity. "We have done this once in your life; therefore expect it as a type of case."

The second thing to register is that this is not the sūrah's own invention. The same sentence-pair appears as a promise in another Makkan context, at the close of a legal passage about financial hardship in Sūrah al-Ṭalāq, and the Qur'an's phrasing there is this verse's best tafsīr:

> *"…God will bring about, after hardship, ease."* (Qur'an 65:7)

and in the passage of divine relief for the oppressed:

> *"Or who responds to the distressed when he calls on Him and removes the evil…?"* (Qur'an 27:62)

The verse's function within al-Sharḥ is thus to universalise the Prophet's ﷺ experience before it becomes the community's possession.

**Maʿa: The Word That Says "At the Same Time," Not "Afterwards"**

The decisive preposition here is maʿa, "with." In Arabic grammar maʿa is a ẓarf (adverb of time/place) expressing muqāranah, accompaniment, and it is not the word for sequence; the word for sequence would be baʿd. The Qur'an uses both, and here it chose "with." The classical exegetes made full weight of that choice. Al-Zamakhsharī presses the same point: the ease is coupled to the hardship and attached to it, so that the hardship is, as it were, the very carrier of what relieves it. Al-Rāzī, in his Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb, develops the same point into a theodicy: the ease accompanies the hardship in this world in three ways — the relief that grows inside endurance, the reward that attaches to patience, and the fact that the hardship itself produces the capacities (skill, sympathy, steadiness, rank) that constitute a person's ease later — and the ease follows it in the world to come, where only the fruit remains. Al-Qurṭubī reports the pious refinement that God did not say the ease comes *after* the hardship — which would leave the sufferer despairing while the hardship lasts — but *with* it, so that a person is never in a state in which the hardship stands alone.

The psychology of that choice is difficult to overstate. A promise of "ease after" leaves the sufferer in a corridor: everything now is unbearable, and the relief is somewhere down the hall. A promise of "ease with" says that the corridor already contains its own light, in the person beside you, in the lesson, in the strength you did not know you had, in the meaning that makes the suffering survivable and even, in retrospect, the best thing that happened. This is not the trivialising claim that "everything happens for a reason"; the verse does not say the hardship is good, but that it is never alone. The distinction is important, because the first statement is sometimes a cruelty ("you should be grateful for this loss"), while the second is a companionship ("this has been appointed, and it has been accompanied").

**Al-ʿUsri: Why "the" Hardship Is Singular**

Two grammatical features in this five-word sentence generate most of the sūrah's famous consolation. The first is the definite article on al-ʿusr. The commentators dispute whether the alif-lām here is generic (al-lām li-l-jins, so that "the hardship" means hardship as a class, every hardship) or referential (al-lām li-l-ʿahd, picking out the particular ʿusr of the Prophet's ﷺ present crisis); in either case the repetition of the same definite noun in verse 6 picks out the same single thing, by the principle stated by the jurists of uṣūl and analysed by ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī in Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz, that a noun made indefinite and then repeated with the definite article is the same noun, now known from its first mention. The second feature is the indefinite yusran: the nominative predicate, indefinite and repeated across the two verses, admits multiplicity, because an indefinite noun in a position of affirming existence (ḥukm al-wujūd) is not bounded to one instance.

So the arithmetic the early Muslims extracted from the sentence is: one hardship (the same hardship named twice), and two eases (indefinite in both clauses). The result is the maxim that the tradition attaches to this verse and that the source commentary records under it: "one instance of hardship (ʿusr) will never overcome two instances of ease (yusrayn)." The maxim is quoted at this point by Ibn Kathīr, al-Shawkānī, and al-Ālūsī, and the source commentary records it among the evidence for these verses; the discussion of its chains of transmission belongs to the commentary on verse 6, where the doubling is examined in its own right.

**ʿUsr: The Root of Being Squeezed**

The root ʿ-s-r is binding, tying, and the difficulty produced by being bound: ʿuṣrah is a "band, bond," and ʿasr is the pressing of grapes or the squeezing of fruit to extract what is in it. Thus al-ʿusr is not merely "difficulty" in the abstract, but condition of being squeezed, and the extraction metaphor is not a modern addition — several of the commentators observe that the pressure is what extracts. The Qur'an's usage confirms the semantic range: ʿusr and its derivatives occur in contexts of debt (2:280, "wa in kāna dhū ʿusratin fa-naẓiratun ilā maysarah" — "and if the debtor is in constriction, grant him respite until ease"), of travel and hunger, of narrow places, and of the difficulty of religious obligation:

> *"God does not charge a soul except with what He has given it; He will bring about, after hardship, ease."* (Qur'an 65:7)

Against that background, "the hardship" in this verse covers the whole field at once: economic squeeze, physical pressure, moral difficulty, the constrained chest of verses 1 and 3. The verse is doing the work of an axiom, not of a specific prediction.

**The Pair as the Chapter's Hinge**

Verse 6 repeats this sentence almost verbatim, and the small differences between the two — above all the loss of the opening conjunction — are examined in the commentary on verse 6 below, where the rhetoricians' reading of the repetition is set out in full. What matters for verse 5 itself is that the promise is stated as a law twice over, and that the first of the two statements is attached by a fa to the four blessings before it, so that the chapter reads as one argument rather than as a string of remarks. The sūrah contains no other repeated sentence, which isolates these two verses as its centre of gravity, and their placement is exact: the pivot of eight verses, sitting between the memory of what God did and the instruction of what the servant now does.

**The Law, Not the Guarantee of Outcomes: Guarding the Verse from Misuse**

Two misreadings have to be named, because both are common.

The first is the sentimental reading: "with every hardship there is material ease coming," so that the verse is treated as a promise of a favourable outcome in this world. That construes the text as insurance against loss, and the sūrah itself refutes it by its own biography: the man to whom the verse was spoken had already gone through a three-year siege in a valley outside Makkah, lost his wife and his clan-protector within months of each other, was driven out and stoned at Ṭāʾif, and died ten years after the Hijrah still carrying a whole community's affairs — and the ease promised to him was, in the first instance, the chest, the load, the name, the survival of the message, and the Hereafter. Al-Rāzī states the rule that the Ashʿarī and Māturīdī schools both accepted: divine promises of relief are collective and generic, not individually guaranteed, and their fulfilment may be in this world, in the next, or in both, at God's discretion.

The second is the quietist reading: "since ease comes anyway, one may be passive." Verse 7, two verses later, forbids that reading, and the tradition's whole understanding of the verse pairs it with effort. Reliance is real, but it does not replace the rope:

> *"A man said: O Messenger of God, should I tie my camel and trust in God, or leave it untied and trust? He said: Tie her and put your trust."* (al-Tirmidhī, Book of Renunciation, from Anas ibn Mālik; classed ḥasan by its examiners)

The same pairing of effort and trust is preserved in a wording transmitted from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb and judged sound by its examiners:

> *"If you were to put your trust in God with true trust, you would be provided as the bird is provided: it goes out in the morning with an empty crop and returns in the evening with a full one."* (al-Tirmidhī, Book of Renunciation, from ʿUmar; classed ḥasan)

The bird leaves the nest; the provision is not produced by the leaving. The law of ease is not an alternative to labour; it is the assurance that labour is not futile. That is what makes the verse a work-ethic's psychology rather than a drug.

**The Historical Fulfilments the Sīrah Offers**

Because the sūrah was revealed in a crisis, Muslim historians have read its promise as a verifiable sequence, and the chronology is instructive. At the moment of revelation, on the chronology of the Sīrah, the Prophet ﷺ faced the end of the boycott with nothing apparently gained, the deaths of Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib, the rejection at Ṭāʾif, and the murder of believers under torture in Makkah. Within a few years came, in the standard order of the sīrah: the first and second ʿAqabah pledges with the coming of the Anṣār, the Hijrah — a community, a mosque, and a polity — the night of the Isrāʾ and Miʿrāj with the gift of the five prayers, Badr, the settlement of Ḥudaybiyyah which the Qur'an calls "a manifest victory" (48:1), the entry into Makkah with almost no bloodshed, the submission of Arabia within a decade, and the survival of the community through the apostasy wars immediately after his death. None of this would have been imaginable by the man hearing the verse in the boycott. Ibn Kathīr's summary of the promise is appropriately blunt: God informs that ease is joined closely to hardship, so that the servant should never despair of relief turning up however the difficulty may multiply.

**A Practical Grammar of Suffering**

What does verse 5 require of a person? At least five things that can be practised without self-deception. One: to state the hardship accurately, since the sūrah itself did not euphemise the creaking back, and false optimism is not faith but denial. Two: to look for the accompanying ease rather than waiting for it to arrive, since the verse's preposition is maʿa; the ease of a friend's presence, of an income that keeps the family fed, of a body that still works, is present-tense and must be searched for. Three: to use the squeeze as an extracting instrument — the commentators repeatedly note that ʿusr is a pressing, and pressing is how oil and juice are obtained. Four: to make the two eases concrete by preparing two responses while in difficulty: the practical one (what can still be done) and the spiritual one (what can still be gained), because the doubling of yusr in the Arabic invites exactly a doubling of the believer's activity. Five: to speak the promise to others, since the verse is in the Qur'an to be recited, not only to be felt — and since the scale described in Sūrah al-Qāriʿah — "and as for one whose scales are heavy, he will be in a pleasant life" (101:6–7) — is only intelligible to a person who has been told that the crushing weight will not be the last word.

There is a further social dimension. A community that has internalised "with the hardship comes ease" handles its members' crises differently: it visits the sick, guarantees debts, lends at a moment when the borrower cannot repay, and does not treat poverty as evidence of weak faith. Its cultural effect is visible in the endowment (waqf) system, which institutionalised the relief of hardship as a perpetual obligation rather than a sporadic charity, and in the legal maxim of the schools: "hardship begets facility" (al-mashaqqah tajlib al-taysīr) — the uṣūl principle by which a ruling is relaxed when its strict application would crush the person bound by it, and whose proof-texts include "God desires ease for you, and He does not desire hardship for you" (2:185). In other words, this verse did not only console individuals; it legislated flexibility into a legal system, which is the hardest proof that a text was understood.

Finally, verse 5's singular contribution to the Qur'an's theology of hope is its refusal to promise a specific remedy. It promises a companion. That is why it survives every outcome: it does not say "the hardship will be undone," it says "you will not be in it alone," and it backs that claim with the four deeds of God already recounted in the verses before it. The reader is entitled to ask, "how do I know?" The sūrah answers: because We told you what We did to the man carrying it.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:6

> **Indeed, with the hardship comes ease.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Repetition as Engineering, Not Redundancy**

Verse 6 is, to a reader in translation, almost a stutter: the sentence just spoken is spoken again. In Arabic the difference is audible in two places. The first is the loss of the opening fa: where verse 5 ran "fa-inna maʿa al-ʿusri yusrā," attaching the promise as a conclusion to the four blessings before it, verse 6 begins bare, "inna maʿa al-ʿusri yusrā," as if God were setting the statement down on its own feet, no longer deriving it from anything. A proposition that follows evidence is a conclusion; a proposition restated without evidence is a decree. The second difference is that the repetition is itself the emphasis. Arabic rhetoricians classified this device as al-takrīr li-l-taqrīr — repetition for the sake of establishing — and their manuals (from ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī's Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz through al-Sakkākī's Miftāḥ al-ʿUlūm and its commentaries) insist that repetition in the Qur'an is never ornamental, since a book of such economy does not waste a clause. The stated function is to fix (taqrīr) a meaning in a heart that is agitated; the more the hearer's distress, the more the assertion must be repeated rather than elaborated. Ibn ʿĀshūr, in al-Taḥrīr wa-l-Tanwīr, treats the pair among the speech-forms used for comforting (al-inshāʾ li-l-tasliyah). The economy is the one every family knows when someone is in shock: fewer words, said again.

The pair also functions as the chapter's structural centre, standing between the four verses of remembrance and the two verses of command, and thereby performing the pivot in the reader's own psychology: memory is gathered, the promise is sealed, and only then does the demand come. Without the second "inna," verse 7 would follow too quickly, and the sūrah would read as reassurance thinly covering an order to work. With it, the assurance has been said twice — to the mind and to the wound — before the servant is asked for anything.

**"Two Eases": What the Second Clause Adds**

The most consequential interpretive question about verse 6 is whether its second yusr is the same ease as the first or a second one. The two positions are both represented in the classical corpus. On one side, the strictly grammatical reading: because the hardship is definite and repeated with the same article, it is one hardship; because the ease is indefinite both times, the two indefinites may denote two distinct instances — and it is this reading that grounds the transmitted wording "one hardship will never overcome two eases." Al-Ṭabarī preserves, in the sense of the early exegetes, the observation that God stated the matter twice so that the hearer would not reckon the hardship the final word. Al-Qurṭubī records the pious gloss that the two eases are the ease of this world and the ease of the next — the first as relief, respite, and the patience that makes the load survivable, the second as the Hereafter in which "no fatigue will touch them therein, and they will not be brought out of it" (15:48). Ibn Kathīr cites the report of the Companions' deduction, and quotes the maxim in the form transmitted in the hadith literature. Al-Ālūsī, in Rūḥ al-Maʿānī, discusses both readings and adds a subtler one: even if the two yusrs are "one ease in reality," the doubling of the word doubles its *power* over the single hardship — because in the divine economy, what God affirms twice does not need to be counted; it needs only to be endured until it arrives.

The doubling's most conspicuous deployment in the early sources is pastoral rather than doctrinal: the commentators record that the Companions and the tābiʿūn greeted the afflicted with the sentence itself, and that the saying "lan yaghliba ʿusrun yusrayn" circulated among them as a form of condolence in its own right, quoted by Ibn Abī Ḥātim in his tafsīr and by al-Ḥākim in his Mustadrak. In that usage the verse became the standard Muslim idiom of hope, functioning in Arabic-speaking piety much as "this too shall pass" functions elsewhere — with this decisive difference: the promise there is the mutability of fortune, the promise here is the companionship and fidelity of God.

**The Sin That the Verse Is Designed to Prevent**

To see what verse 6 is for, one must read it against the Qur'an's treatment of despair, which is not a mood but a moral state. Two verses stand out, both concerning prophets and both forbidding precisely what the doubled promise undoes. Yaʿqūb, blinded by grief over Yūsuf, is answered by his sons' accusation that he will die of his sorrow, and he replies:

> *"He said: I only complain of my grief and my sorrow to God, and I know from God what you do not know."* (Qur'an 12:86)

and then, at the moment the brothers despair of the missing brother:

> *"O my sons, go and inquire about Yūsuf and his brother, and do not despair of God's spirit (rūḥ). Indeed, only the disbelieving people despair of God's spirit."* (Qur'an 12:87)

Note the combination that this sūrah also produces: Yaʿqūb states the grief with total honesty ("I complain of my grief and my sorrow") *and* refuses despair, and he grounds the refusal not in optimism but in an epistemic claim — "I know from God what you do not know." The doubled "with the hardship comes ease" supplies exactly that knowledge. The parallel prohibition elsewhere is addressed to the whole community:

> *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God's mercy; indeed God forgives sins entirely. Indeed, He is the Forgiving, the Merciful."* (Qur'an 39:53)

The exegetes of this chapter mention this pairing deliberately: the verse in al-Sharḥ is not a promise that circumstances improve but a prohibition against the reading of God's character that says they never will. And the Prophet ﷺ attached to the same virtue an obligation at the deathbed, in the wording of Muslim:

> *"Let none of you die except while thinking well of God (ḥusn al-ẓann bi-llāh)."* (Muslim 2877)

The hadith is, in context, a command about the final hours, and the scholars of the Qur'an's purposes read this verse with it, because the doubled ease is the object of that "thinking well": the man who believes "with the hardship comes ease" has already, in this life, obeyed the command to hold a good opinion of his Lord.

**A Note on the Chain: What Is Established and What Is Not**

Because readers will meet the wording "one hardship will never overcome two eases" attached to this verse in almost every classical commentary and in many contemporary ones, it is honest to record the hadith-critical position rather than to repeat the slogan uncritically. The report is transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās, with other chains going to ʿAlī and to Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī; it appears in Ibn Ḥibbān's Ṣaḥīḥ and in al-Ḥākim's Mustadrak, al-Bayhaqī cites it in his work on the virtues of the Qur'an, and the source commentary lists it among the evidence for verses 5 and 6 together. Al-Ḥākim judged it authentic by the criteria of the two Shaykhs, and al-Dhahabī, in his abridgement of that ruling, accepted it; other examiners found the chains interrupted or the raising questionable, so that some of them treated the sentence as Ibn ʿAbbās's own gloss rather than a prophetic statement. Three consequences follow, and they are all safe. First, the *meaning* is not in dispute: the companions and tābiʿūn cited in the tafsīr literature unanimously understood the doubled ease as outlasting the single hardship. Second, the deduction is available from the Arabic even apart from the report, which is why ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī's analysis of definiteness and indefiniteness is so often quoted at this point. Third, nothing in the verse's authority depends on the chain's grade; a law stated twice in the Qur'an needs no external authentication to be binding on hope.

**The Objections, and the Replies the Tradition Gives**

Two difficulties are raised in the books of tafsīr, and both deserve a fair statement.

The first is empirical: the observation is plainly false in ordinary experience. Millions endure hardship and die in it; some lives contain only suffering; a child's illness may end nothing but sorrow. The verse says ease comes *with* it, and the world seems to say otherwise. The replies preserved in the commentaries are three, and they are cumulative rather than alternative. (a) The promise is a statement about the total account, not the visible portion of it: the ease of the Hereafter is not delayed into nothing, and the ratio in which the two are weighed is the one the Qur'an specifies — "what is with you runs out, and what is with God endures; and We will certainly reward the patient with the best of what they used to do" (16:96). (b) Some portion of the ease is always present and is simply not counted as ease by the sufferer, because he expected it to be a removal rather than a support: the patience itself, the compassion drawn out of others, the softening of the heart, the absolution of sin through endurance. The Prophet ﷺ stated the last as a general rule:

> *"No fatigue, illness, anxiety, sorrow, harm, or distress befalls a Muslim — even the thorn that pricks him — except that God expiates some of his sins thereby."* (al-Bukhārī 5641; Muslim 2573)

(c) The promise is generic and collective (kulliyyah, ʿāmmah), as al-Rāzī and the uṣūl writers articulate: the divine pattern holds across the community and across time, and a law of that kind is not voided by cases in which the individual's relief arrives after death. What the verse guarantees is not that any given person will be relieved on a given schedule, but that hardship is not the structure of creation, that ease is not merely possible but promised, and that the believer is therefore licensed — indeed commanded — to expect it.

The second objection is structural: if the ease is *with* the hardship, why does the sūrah order the servant in verse 7 to toil and in verse 8 to want, as though he had to produce the ease? The answer given by the commentators of this passage, including al-Bayḍāwī and later al-Shawkānī, is that the pair is a statement about God's conduct, and the two commands are statements about the servant's. The ease is given; the labour and the intention are owed. The verse is thus an antidote to two opposite failures at once: passivity ("God will relieve me, so I need not move") and self-reliance ("I relieve myself, so I need not hope"). Its position between the memory of grace and the demand for effort makes the sūrah's whole practical theology visible: the human being works inside a promise, not to earn one.

**Living Inside the Verse**

Practically, the doubled sentence teaches a discipline of attention with four operations. One, refusal of the single frame: when a person says "this is all bad," the verse obliges him to complete the sentence — "…and the ease is with it" — and to name at least one accompanying ease, however small, because the promise is not "there will be ease" but "there is." Two, the expectation of a second ease that has not yet appeared, which changes waiting from a passive to an active state; the believer does not sit in the hardship as in a cell but as in a corridor with doors not yet opened. Three, patience as a work rather than a mood: the sūrah's own vocabulary (naṣb) makes exertion the correct response to good news, not the response to bad news. Four, the conversion of the promise into speech addressed to others: the verse's presence in the Qur'an is itself a command to repeat it to the sufferer, and the community that recites it together in a funeral house, in a hospital corridor, or at a creditor's door is the mechanism by which the "with" becomes visible. A person who has been told twice, by God, that he is not alone in his ʿusr has been given something no circumstance can revoke — and that is the whole office of verse 6.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:7

> **So when you are free, devote yourself to exertion,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First of the Two Commands — and the Sharpest**

After four blessings and a doubled promise, the sūrah asks for something. Fa-idhā faragta fa-nṣab: "So, when you are free of one thing, stand up for another." The conditional particle idhā with the perfect verb faragta and the imperative in the answer clause makes a rule for a *type of moment*: every time a task empties, that moment has a divine instruction attached to it. This is not the ethic of "rest when tired"; it is the ethic of the interval. And its position immediately after the promise of ease is deliberate, because it explains what the ease is for. The relief described in verses 1 to 3 and guaranteed in verses 5 and 6 is not delivered so that a person may sprawl; capacity is given so that effort can be renewed. Al-Saʿdī's gloss on the verse is simply this: when you are finished with your affairs, devote your effort to worship. The reason is supplied by the wider tradition of the heart's teachers, in whose maxim — attributed to Yaḥyā ibn Muʿādh and quoted often in the books of conduct — the soul that is not occupied with truth will occupy itself with falsehood.

**Faragha and Naṣaba: Two Roots That Do the Work**

The verb faragha (fa-r-gh) means to be emptied, released, or done with; from the same root, tafarugh means "to be free of occupation," and farāgh is "leisure" — the word from which the Arabic for an "unfettered hour" is formed. The root is also used for pouring out (faragha al-māʾ, "the water was poured"), so that the semantic field holds both "emptied" and "poured out": the empty vessel is also the vessel from which something has been released. That ambiguity is a gift for reading this verse: the one who has become "free" is described in the same word-family as the one who has been "poured out," emptied of the task that filled him. The self the sūrah addresses at verse 7 is not a rested, intact self; it is a spent one. And the command is nonetheless to stand.

The verb nṣab (naṣaba) is equally precise. Its core sense is to erect, to set up, to stand upright — hence naṣb as a "raised monument," manṣab as "station" or "rank," and muntaṣab as "one who stands." From that, naṣab denotes the toil and fatigue produced by prolonged standing and labour — the word occurs in the Qur'an in precisely that sense, in the description of the People of Paradise:

> *"[They will say:] Praise be to God, who has removed from us grief! Truly our Lord is Forgiving, grateful for little. He who settled us in an everlasting home out of His bounty — no toil touches us therein, and no weariness touches us therein."* (Qur'an 35:34–35)

and again, in the verse about the Garden:

> *"No fatigue will touch them therein, and they will not be brought out of it."* (Qur'an 15:48)

The sūrah's own final gift explains its seventh verse. The believer is ordered to nṣab — to the very exertion whose Arabic name is fatigue — in a life where no exertion is free of tiredness; and the Qur'an elsewhere defines Paradise as the place where naṣab does not touch anyone. That pairing converts verse 7 from a demand for extra effort into a trade: a limited fatigue that will not remain, purchased against a rest that will. It also prevents a misreading that this verse is often used to license: it does not sanctify exhaustion as piety in itself. The naṣab commanded is directed effort, not self-punishment; the same Book that says "God does not charge a soul except with what He has given it" (2:286, 23:62, 65:7) cannot mean "wear yourself out."

**The Transmitted Readings: Six Ways to Take "When You Are Free"**

The verse has been applied to several different "emptinesses," and the classical commentaries list them without insisting that one exhausts the sense. Al-Ṭabarī's summary of the early authorities, transmitted also by al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Shawkānī, includes the following.

First, and most frequently cited: when you have finished the obligatory prayers, then exert yourself in supplication and in supererogatory prayer. This is the reading of Mujāhid and of Qatāda, and it fits the sūrah's Makkan setting, where the details of the later prayer-law were still being revealed; it treats "free" as "released from what was binding."

Second, when you have finished the affairs of this world, then turn to the next — the reading al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī gave — "when you are free from the affairs of this world, then stand for the next" — quoted in that form by the earliest commentators on these verses, and preserved in the same shape in the tafsīr chapters of the Ṣaḥīḥ literature.

Third, when you have finished your devotions, do not leave off duʿāʾ — the reading some of the Shāfiʿī jurists used as a basis for the recommendedness of supplication after the prayer, and which is supported by the Prophet's ﷺ practice of not turning to leave immediately after salām in several of the authenticated narrations on the adhkār after prayer (as collected in Muslim's Book of Prayer and in al-Ṭabarānī's collections).

Fourth, when you are free from fighting the enemies of the message, devote yourself to the worship of your Lord — an interpretation preserved by al-Bayḍāwī and quoted in the source commentary, which reads the sūrah as an instruction in the sequencing of priorities: the external struggle ceases; the internal one does not.

Fifth, "when you are devoted, deepen your devotion" — that is, faragta not as "become free" but as "become fully occupied with God," so that the command is to intensify the very act in which one is already engaged. Al-Zamakhsharī attributes this sense to the Arabic usage of tafarragha li-l-amr, "to have made oneself wholly available for a thing."

Sixth, when you are exhausted, continue — a reading the Sufi commentators favour, since it makes the verse about the durability of the will rather than about scheduling.

The diversity is not a defect of interpretation but a feature of the sentence: the condition "when you are free" is formally open, and it invites the hearer to supply his own emptinesses — the gap between jobs, the finished project, the hour after the children sleep, the weekend, the retirement, the recovery from an illness, the period after a funeral. What the verse closes off is only one option: that a freed interval belongs to no one.

**The Prophetic Practice Behind the Verse**

The Sunnah supplies the working model of verse 7, and it is a model of alternation rather than of continuous strain. In the two Ṣaḥīḥs, ʿĀʾisha reports of the last ten nights of Ramadan:

> *"When the last ten nights began, the Prophet ﷺ would stay awake the night, wake his family, and tighten the waist-wrapper."* (al-Bukhārī 2024; Muslim 1174)

and the exertion there was *added* to a day already occupied by fasting, teaching that "when you are free" includes "when the obligations of the day are complete, then there is more." Equally authoritative is the restraint imposed on the man who wanted to exceed even that. To ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr, who fasted the day and stood the whole night, the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Your body has a claim on you, your soul has a claim on you, and your guest has a claim on you; so give to every claimant its due."* (al-Bukhārī 1975; Muslim 1159)

and:

> *"O ʿAbdullāh, have I not been told that you fast the day and stand the night? So fast and break the fast, and sleep and stand in prayer; for your body has a claim upon you, your eye has a claim upon you, and your wife has a claim upon you."* (al-Bukhārī 1975; Muslim 1159)

The two texts together produce the fiqh of the verse: exertion is obligatory in principle, but its amount is regulated so that it can be repeated. Hence the ruling maxim from the Prophet's ﷺ own words in Muslim's Book of Prayers:

> *"The deeds most loved by God are the most constant, even if few."* (Muslim 783)

Verse 7, read with these, is a command to *structure*, not to strain: to have a fixed next thing. It is also worth noting what is conspicuously absent from the sūrah's command: it does not say "when you are free, rest." The reason given by the moralists of the tradition — Ibn al-Jawzī in Ṣayd al-Khāṭir and al-Muḥāsibī in al-Riʿāya — is that the free hour is the hour in which the self's real direction becomes visible, because no external requirement is compelling anyone. This is the same insight in the Prophet's ﷺ words in the two Ṣaḥīḥs:

> *"Two blessings of which most people are losers: health and free time."* (al-Bukhārī 6412)

and in the counsel of Ibn ʿAbbās preserved by al-Ḥākim, whose chain the examiners debated but whose meaning the scholars of conduct received: "Take advantage of five before five: … your free time before your preoccupation."

**The Psychology of Transitions**

Modern readers will recognise the verse as addressing a genuine mechanism. The interval is where the untrained mind destabilises. A person under a deadline experiences a narrowed, effective anxiety; the deadline lifts, and within hours the same person may fall into rumination, compulsive scrolling, drinking, or a flat despair that the busy phase concealed. The clinical literature on burnout and on attention describes the same pattern: an under-structured recovery does not restore; it exposes. Verse 7's prescription is not "rest harder" but "change the object of your standing." The exertion that follows the completed task is to be of a different order — if the labour was physical, the naṣab is prayer; if the labour was intellectual and public, the naṣab is recitation, dhikr, or an act of bodily worship; if the labour was for income, the naṣab is for family or for another human being. The mechanism is what the classical authors called al-iltifāt — the turning of the face toward something else — and it is why the tradition treated alternating acts of devotion as a mercy to the heart rather than as a mere accumulation of reward. The governing text is the Prophet's ﷺ own prohibition of devotional overreach, in the wording of Muslim and of al-Bukhārī's Book of Heart-Softeners:

> *"Take upon yourselves only such deeds as you can bear, for God does not tire until you tire; and the deed most loved by God is that which its doer performs most constantly, even if it is little."* (Muslim 782, 783; al-Bukhārī, Kitāb ar-Riqāq)

ʿĀ'isha's observation on the same point is the practical form of the verse: *"when he did a deed, he kept steadily to it"* (Muslim 783). Constancy, not intensity, is the meter of the freed hour.

The classical version of the same principle is the Salaf's aphorism "refresh the heart hour by hour," transmitted as a saying of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and quoted widely in the adab literature: the heart is not a machine to be run to destruction but a living thing to be timed. Verse 7 supplies the discipline; these texts supply the moderation.

**From Consolation to Commission: The Verse's Function in the Chapter**

Structurally, fa-idhā faragta fa-nṣab restores agency at exactly the point where a merely consoling text would leave the reader in relief. The sūrah has insisted that God opened, removed, raised, and promised; now, at verse 7, the man himself is told to stand. The commentators of this chapter, including al-Bayḍāwī and Ibn ʿĀshūr, treat this as the sūrah's response to a specific danger: that comfort might become quietism. God's gifts are the ground of the servant's labour, not its replacement — and this, in fact, is how the whole of Islamic ethics of works is constructed: reward is grace, effort is owed. Ibn ʿĀshūr, listing the sūrah's objectives, puts among them "incitement to continued devotion and to the refusal of idleness, and the establishment that the believer's life has no empty hour."

For a community, the reading has economic and civic consequences that the sūrah's history demonstrates. The early Muslims applied the verse to the structure of the day: the two Ṣaḥīḥs record the Prophet's ﷺ practice of dividing his time between family, community affairs, and worship — the report of ʿĀʾisha "he was in the service of his family; when the prayer came, he went out to it" (al-Bukhārī 676) is, in the eyes of the commentators on this verse, the Sunnah's execution of it. The mosques of the first generations were simultaneously courts, schools, treasuries, and shelters, and the men who ran them stood from one task to the next without ever treating the interval as ownerless. The modern professional's fragmented leisure, in which hours are surrendered to whatever algorithm is nearest, is precisely the condition this verse forbids — not because pleasure is a sin, but because a life without a next command is a life whose direction has been outsourced.

**The Verse's Two Questions**

Verse 7 asks each reader two things. What is your next act of obedience — specific enough that you could name it now? And what do you actually do in the gap between obligations, since the sūrah has named that gap and attached an order to it? The chapter's consolation is not sentimental about these questions. Its God who widened the chest is the same God who says: stand up when you are free — and, in the next verse, tells him what to want while standing.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:8

> **And to your Lord turn your whole desire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Verse Is the One That Reorders the Rest**

The sūrah ends where a chapter about inner capacity must end: not with a description of the eased life, but with the direction of the will. Wa ilā rabbika fa-rghab — "and toward your Lord, desire." Four things are compressed into those four words: an object ("your Lord"), a preposition of direction ("toward"), an imperative ("desire"), and a conjunction that ties the whole command back to everything said before. The commentators from al-Ṭabarī to al-Shawkānī agree on the substance: this is a command about the aim, not about the act — verse 7 said *what* to do with an emptied hour, and verse 8 says *for whom* it is done. The pair, taken together, is the definition of sound religion in the vocabulary of the manuals: the action is owed, and the aim is owed. Al-Saʿdī's commentary on these two verses states the relationship plainly, and later teachers of the heart put it in the form of a syllogism: a deed without direction toward God is a body without a soul, and a direction without a deed is a claim without a body.

**Raghba: The Root and Its Two Directions**

The root r-gh-b is one of the few Arabic roots that legitimately covers both halves of a single emotional movement: to want something and, correspondingly, to turn away from its opposite. The derived noun raghīb is used for "one who desires," and the Qur'an uses the active participle in the sense of "turning away" when Ibrāhīm's father, threatened with stoning for his preaching, asks his son:

> *"Do you turn away (rāghibun) from my gods, O Ibrāhīm? If you do not stop, I will surely strike you; and leave me for a long while."* (Qur'an 19:46)

and Ibrāhīm answers with a sentence that is itself an act of raghb in the other sense: "Peace be upon you; I will ask forgiveness for you from my Lord" (19:47). The pairing is instructive for this verse, because the Qur'anic vocabulary treats desire and abandonment as a single operation: to want this is to leave that. The classical lexicographers (al-Azharī in Tahdhīb al-Lugha, Ibn Fāris in Maqāyīs al-Lugha) reduce r-gh-b to "the inclination of the soul toward what it loves," and note that raghb may be used of the soul's leaning toward a thing or away from it, with the preposition deciding the direction. In the sūrah the preposition is ilā — "toward your Lord." And the preposition does the theological work: want is not abolished, it is aimed. The verse never says "do not desire." It says: desire toward your Lord.

The exegetes also note a variant of reading preserved by the same consonantal skeleton: fa-rghab (form I, "desire, want") and, on the authority of some reciters, fa-irghab (form IV, arghaba, "to turn with one's whole self toward"). Al-Ṭabarī mentions both. The senses converge, and both make the verb intensive: the sūrah is not asking for a modest preference for God over other things, but for the concentration of wanting itself.

**Two Objects, Two Verbs: The Verse as the Completion of Verse 7**

Read as a pair, verses 7 and 8 state the classical formula for a valid act of worship, which the jurists phrase as the union of ʿamal and niyyah, or in the technical vocabulary of the schools, "the conformity of the outward act to the Law and of the inward aim to God." The Qur'an grounds this elsewhere in the most explicit way, in the passage on the sacrificial animals:

> *"It is not their meat nor their blood that reaches God; but it is your God-consciousness (taqwā) that reaches Him."* (Qur'an 22:37)

and in the passage on spending:

> *"They give food, for love of Him, to the poor and the orphan and the captive, [saying]: we feed you for God's countenance alone; we desire from you no thanks nor repayment."* (Qur'an 76:8–9)

The second of those texts is remarkable in that the Companions' reported reason for its revelation — feeding the family while in a state of vow and poverty — is an instance of exactly the sūrah's sequence: they were free of nothing (they were in need), they exerted themselves anyway, and they directed the want to God. Our sūrah's final two verses thus do not merely describe an ideal of piety; they describe the structure of a deed that survives the disappearance of every reward except one.

The verse also supplies the answer to the question that the previous seven verses provoke: if God has done everything — expanded, unloaded, raised, promised — what remains for the servant? The answer of verse 8: everything that matters, namely the intention. Al-Ghazālī's treatment of sincerity in the Iḥyāʾ is worth recalling as a direct meditation on this verse's logic, because his whole analysis of ikhlāṣ rests on the distinction the sūrah draws between the labour of the limbs and the direction of the heart — the first being the means, the second the thing itself. His proof-text is the authenticated wording of Muslim:

> *"Indeed God does not look at your appearances or your bodies, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds."* (Muslim 2564)

**The Verse as a Prohibition, Not Only an Invitation**

Because wa ilā rabbika fa-rghab fixes a single terminus, it also excludes, and the commentators list the exclusions plainly. It forbids directing want to created beings — the practical meaning of the verse in the daily life of the community, since the desire for people's approval, patronage, gifts, and reputation is the ordinary engine of the ordinary heart. Al-Ṭabarī takes the verse to the effect: direct your want and your asking to your Lord alone. It forbids, second, the division of the will between God and an idol, whether the idol is carved or is a career, and the sūrah's Makkan position makes the sense concrete: a chapter revealed among people who attached partners to God in worship was told, in its last verse, to attach no partner to God in wanting. It forbids third, despair about the world's outcome — because a person whose want is directed to God is not ruined by a result he did not control; and this is why verse 8 sits after the doubled promise of ease, so that hope is anchored in the one who gives the ease rather than in the ease itself.

The Prophet's ﷺ own application of this principle is preserved in an authentic text that functions as the sūrah's commentary, transmitted by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"God, the Exalted, says: I am so independent of partnership that whoever does a deed for another along with Me — I leave him and his partner; it is for the one he did it for, not for Me."* (Muslim 2985, Book of Renunciation and Heart-Softening)

The exactness of the attribution, not the quantity of the work, is the point; and it is why the jurists made intention a condition of the validity of the acts of worship, and why the moralists treated sincerity as the ground on which reward rests at all rather than as a decoration added to a reward already earned. The moralists add, in this connection, that such a demand precedes and governs every rite — prayer and fasting no less than judgment and campaign — since the direction of the will is not one duty among others but the condition on which the others stand.

**"Your Lord": Why Rabb and Not Ilāh**

The sūrah's final word before the verb is rabb — "your Lord," with the pronoun attached to the man addressed, as in verses 1 to 4. The choice is not interchangeable. Al-Rabb derives from rubūbiyyah, nourishing, bringing something gradually to completion, owning and setting right; the same root gives the word for the master of a house and for the one who raises a child. A servant is told to direct want to his *rabb* rather than to his *ilāh* because the object of desire must be the One who completes, not merely the One who is feared: want attaches to the provider, and the whole sūrah has been an account of provision — the chest provided with capacity, the back provided with relief, the name provided with permanence, the hardship provided with ease. Ibn Kathīr notes in this place that the sūrah's closing is thus a command to ask God for what one wants at all, since the One who gave the four gifts is the One who gives what remains.

There is a further echo that the commentators on the pair of sūrahs 93 and 94 draw out. Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā closed with two social commands about the powerless — do not oppress the orphan, do not repel the petitioner — and ended with "and as for the favour of your Lord, speak of it" (93:11). Sūrah al-Sharḥ closes by naming the single direction that makes those commands possible: if your want is for your Lord, the orphan's appeal and the petitioner's need will not embarrass you, because you are not being paid by them and you are not dependent on their gratitude. The two sūrahs, read as the single unit some of the early reciters treated them as, form one argument: recall what God did for you, therefore be generous with the weak and direct all want back to God.

**The Verse and the Theology of Want**

The chapter's ending raises a question in the philosophy of action that Islamic theology debated under the heading of irāda and riḍā: is the servant's desire something he can aim, or only something that happens to him? The answer implicit in an imperative addressed to desire ("desire!") is that the direction of wanting is at least partly within human power, since God does not command the impossible — a principle the Muʿtazila used as a proof-text for the createdness of the act and the Ashʿariyya qualified by insisting that God creates the servant's choice and the servant acquires it. What the tradition's practical teaching adds, and what verse 8 supports, is that direction is trained rather than chosen at a single moment: the want of a heart is formed by repetition, by the objects it is fed, by the company it keeps, and by the practices that make the invisible more vivid than the visible. Hence the discipline the jurists and the spiritual directors derived from this verse: to convert wants into acts of obedience until the heart learns its direction; to make duʿāʾ the ordinary grammar of the day, since asking is the practice that keeps desire pointed at the One who gives; and to refuse to want what God has refused, because the sūrah's "your Lord" is not a suggestion about inward feelings but a boundary around the soul's appetite.

The here-and-now content of that theology is striking. Most human anxiety is the anxiety of misdirected want: fear of people who can neither profit nor harm, grief over outcomes that were never in the subject's hands, envy of possessions that do not answer the desire they excite. The verse does not tell the person to want less; it tells him to want toward. It is the difference between a hand with five fingers moving in five directions and a hand closed into a fist: the same force, one object. This is what the early ascetics meant when they said that freedom is not the absence of desire but the singularity of it — a formulation which is, in its structure, a paraphrase of wa ilā rabbika fa-rghab.

**The Verse in the Life of the Community**

Finally, the verse legislates the direction of the ummah's collective want, and the jurists read it in this register as well. Public funds, institutional decisions, political authority, and the conduct of war are all bound by the same question: toward whom is this done? The Qur'an's own articulation of the criterion is the verse in Sūrah al-Bayyinah:

> *"And they were only commanded to worship God, making religion sincere to Him, upright in devotion, and to keep up the prayer and give the zakāt; and that is the upright religion."* (Qur'an 98:5)

Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī both note the pairing in that verse of sincerity ("making religion sincere to God") with concrete social duties ("give the zakāt") — exactly the pairing of 94:7 and 94:8: exertion and direction, work and want. A community that has exertion without direction becomes an institution that serves its own survival; a community that has direction without exertion becomes a group that feels pure while doing nothing; and the closing two verses of al-Sharḥ are the sūrah's cure for both diseases.

**The Last Word: Eight Verses, One Arc**

Because the sūrah ends on the Lord rather than on the ease, its eight verses resolve into one movement: God opens the breast (1), removes the load (2), having described the load's cruelty (3), raises the name (4), and then declares the law that no suffering is alone (5), seals it (6), and returns to the servant with two demands: rise and work (7), aim and want (8). The man who began the chapter consoled by what was done to him ends it commissioned in what he must do with what was done. The final verse thus guarantees that the consolation is not a comfort-food theology: the ease promised is not an ending but an equipment; the opened chest exists so that a person can stand and, standing, want God — which is, in the sūrah's own reckoning, the only direction in which human desire can travel without being crushed, and without crushing anything else.

## Closing Remarks on the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**What the Chapter Does to Its Reader**

A chapter of eight short verses, with no legal ruling, no narrative, and no argument with an opponent, has nevertheless been memorised, recited, quoted in calamity, and used as the frame for entire treatises on hope. The explanation lies in what al-Sharḥ does rather than what it discusses. It reorders the sequence in which a suffering person is addressed. First, memory: four things God has already done, put as questions no one can answer in the negative. Second, a law: the structure of experience itself, stated twice so that both intellect and wound receive it. Third, two commands that restore agency without denying dependence: stand, and want. A reader who takes the chapter in order cannot arrive at verse 7 in the same state he left verse 1; his chest has been described as opened, his load described as on the ground, and his future described as accompanied, before he is asked for anything.

**The Sūrah's Contribution to the Qur'an's Moral Psychology**

Three teachings distinguish this chapter within the Qur'an's wide treatment of distress. Its definition of capacity is somatic and therefore honest: the struggle is located in the breast and the back, in the body that tightens and groans, and not in an abstraction called "faith." Its account of relief is prior to effort rather than its payment: the gifts of verses 1 to 4 are given before the demand of verses 7 and 8, which is why the chapter cannot be read as a theology of achievement. And its promise is deliberately under-specified: the ease is promised without being described, timed, or restricted to any category of outcome, which is precisely why it survives the death of the martyr, the illness of the child, and the failure of the long campaign — the verse does not say what will be eased, only that nothing is endured alone.

**How the Chapter Has Been Read: A Short Note on Method**

The classical commentaries on these eight verses display the full range of Islamic exegetical method, and a reader of this expansion should keep their diversity in view rather than flattening it. Al-Ṭabarī's approach is philological and tradition-based, weighing the reports about the burden and the raised name against the usage of the Arabs. Al-Zamakhsharī reads the syntax for its rhetorical effect — the negative interrogative, the preposition maʿa, the unattached repetition of verse 6. Ibn ʿAbbās's transmitted glosses supply the liturgical referents of verse 4; al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and Qatāda supply the moral readings of verse 7; al-Shawkānī and al-Ālūsī collect the variant readings and the weaker reports and mark them as such; Ibn Kathīr tests the interpretations against the Qur'an's own cross-references; and Ibn ʿĀshūr states the sūrah's purpose (gharaḍ) as consolation of the Prophet ﷺ together with the establishment of a general law of relief. Where this commentary has had to weigh between them — above all on the physical opening of the breast and on the identification of the "burden" with sins — the more cautious reading has been preferred and the alternatives recorded rather than suppressed. The chapter's brevity makes its interpretation a useful object-lesson: eight verses can be exhausted in a paragraph, or they can be the site of fourteen centuries of careful, reverent, argued reading, and the difference lies not in the text's length but in the reader's patience.

**A Final Word on Reciting It**

The sūrah is short enough to be held in the mouth during an illness, during a journey, during a night of worry; and the practice of reciting it with attention is best understood as the reciter placing himself inside the address. "Did We not expand for you your breast" is spoken to the Prophet ﷺ, and the tradition's own principle — that the Qur'an's address to one is an address to all who share his condition — makes the sentence available without making it presumptuous. The person who recites it is asking for what was given, remembering that it was given first to a man who had nothing to boast about, and being taught, in the last two verses, that the correct response to such a memory is to stand up and to want God. These are not small instructions for a chapter of eight verses: they are the whole of a life in two lines.
