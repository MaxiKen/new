# Sūrah al-Muddaththir (Chapter 74) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cloaked Man on the Mountain**

Al-Muddaththir, "The One Who Covers Himself," takes its name from the unforgettable image with which it opens: a man of Makkah, shaken to his core by an encounter with the divine, begging his family to wrap him in a cloak (*dithār*) so that he might steady himself. The Arabic verb *dathara* means to cover, shroud, or envelope; the active participle *al-muddaththir* is therefore "the one enshrouded" — a title the Qur'an permanently fastened upon the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, one of the honorifics by which revelation itself calls him (alongside *al-Muzzammil*, "the one wrapped," in the sūrah immediately before it). The classical commentators, including Ibn ʿAbbās as reported through the exegetical tradition, counted these among the distinct names by which the Qur'an addresses its Messenger. There is deep symbolism in the fact that the two sūrahs of the earliest mission are both named for garments: the prophetic career begins with a man wrapped and covering, and its first command is to unwrap — to stand, to go out, to speak. The garment that hid became, by God's command, the garment of the man who would be seen by all of history.

**When Did This Descent Occur? The Question of the First Revelation**

The chronology of these opening verses carries unusual weight, because a Companion-level report identifies them as the very first words of revelation. The standard view, transmitted through the widely known account of ʿĀʾishah recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, is that Sūrat al-ʿAlaq (96:1–5) — *"Recite in the Name of thy Lord Who created"* — was the first revelation, and that these verses of al-Muddaththir came afterward. But Yaḥyā ibn Abī Kathīr reports that when he asked Abū Salamah ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān which sūrah was revealed first, Abū Salamah answered with the opening of this chapter — *"O thou who art covered"* — and cited the testimony of Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh, preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (no. 4922):

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ said: While I was walking, I heard a voice from the sky. I raised my gaze toward the sky, and behold, it was the same angel who had come to me at the cave of Ḥirāʾ, seated upon a throne between the heaven and the earth. I was terrified of him, and I returned and said: Wrap me up! Wrap me up! Then God revealed: O thou who art covered, arise and warn! And: Thy Lord magnify! And: Thy garments purify! And: Defilement shun!"* (al-Bukhārī 4922; cf. Muslim 161)

Ibn Kathīr reconciles the two accounts with precision: the angel described in Jābir's narration is "*the same angel who had come to me at the cave of Ḥirāʾ*" — meaning the encounter in these verses is the second meeting, not the first. Thus 96:1–5 marks the beginning of revelation as such, while 74:1–7 marks the first command to *proclaim* it — the divine commissioning of the Prophet as a public warner. The two readings are not rivals but complementary moments: the call to receive, and the call to deliver.

**The Twin Sūrahs: Preparation and Commission**

Sūrah 73 (al-Muzzammil) and Sūrah 74 (al-Muddaththir) form a matched pair at the head of the prophetic mission. Al-Muzzammil instructs the Prophet in the inward disciplines — night vigil, measured recitation, patient trust: the private preparation of the vessel. Al-Muddaththir issues the outward charter — *arise and warn, magnify, purify, shun defilement, give freely, endure patiently*: the public character of the mission. The pattern teaches a permanent principle: no servant carries a public trust without a hidden discipline, and no hidden discipline is complete until it breaks out into service of others. Where 73 trains the man at night, 74 sends the Messenger into the day. Where 73 is the wound-dressing before battle, 74 is the battle order itself. The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī) note also the spiritual reading: every human soul is commanded, in miniature, to rise from its covering — the comforts, evasions, and distractions with which it shrouds itself — and to rise through God toward His remembrance.

**The Architecture of the Chapter**

The sūrah unfolds in movements that classical analysis (following the source commentary) maps as follows. Verses 1–7 deliver the sevenfold charter to the Messenger: rise, warn, magnify, purify, shun, give, endure — a complete portrait of prophetic character compressed into seven imperatives. Verses 8–10 sound the trumpet of the Last Day, the appointed terminus toward which all warning points. Verses 11–25 present the anatomy of one rejecter — al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, the aristocrat of Makkah — traced through his wealth, his deliberation, his frown, his arrogance, and his verdict that the Qur'an is mere "sorcery handed down" and "the speech of a human being." Verses 26–31 name his destination, Saqar, and unveil its nineteen angelic keepers — a number appointed as a *trial* that sifts humanity into doubters and certain believers. Verses 32–37 seal the warning with oaths by the moon, the retreating night, and the brightening dawn, and declare the fire "one of the greatest" warnings "for those of you who wish to go forth or stay behind" — the clearest statement of human free choice in the earliest revelation. Verses 38–39 state the metaphysics of accountability: every soul is *held in pledge* for what it earns, save the companions of the right. Verses 40–48 open a window into the Garden, where the redeemed interrogate the damned and receive the confession of the guilty — a confession whose items are startlingly ordinary: they did not pray, did not feed the poor, plunged into vanity, denied the Judgment "until certainty came upon us," and now no intercession can reach them. Verses 49–56 close the chapter with the enigma of the obdurate — people who flee the Reminder like donkeys fleeing a lion, who demand personal revelations "unfolded," and who will not remember *except as God wills* — and the sūrah ends on the two divine attributes that hold the whole drama in balance: *He is most worthy of reverence and most worthy of granting forgiveness.*

**The First Public Warning in History**

It is with this sūrah, by the soundest view, that the private treasure becomes a public burden. The earliest sīrah literature connects the command *arise and warn!* with the Prophet's first open proclamation: the episode at the hill of al-Ṣafā, recorded by al-Bukhārī (no. 4470), in which Abū Hurayrah reports:

> *"When these verses were revealed — and warn thy nearest kinsmen (26:214) — the Messenger of God ﷺ climbed al-Ṣafā and called out, and the Quraysh gathered. He said: If I told you that horsemen were in the valley behind this mountain, about to attack you at morning or evening, would you believe me? They said: Yes — we have never known you to tell a lie. He said: Then I am a warner to you of a severe punishment."* (al-Bukhārī 4470)

The scene distills the logic of the entire chapter: a man whose truthfulness even his enemies had never questioned now spends that accumulated credibility on a single message — Judgment is coming, and there is preparation to be made. The response, recorded in the same tradition, was mockery; but the mission had begun, and it would not stop for thirteen years of Makkan endurance — and then it would not stop at all.

**Why This Chapter Matters to Every Reader**

Al-Muddaththir is a chapter about *choices made in the face of clear light*. Al-Walīd was not confused; he was *convinced* — "there is not a man among you more knowledgeable about poetry than I... what he says has the upper hand, and nothing has the upper hand over it" — and he chose denial anyway. The guilty in the Garden were not uninformed; they simply did not pray, did not feed, did not take the Warning seriously *until certainty came upon them*. The chapter therefore functions as a mirror: it asks each reader whether the delay of certainty — the habit of postponing obedience until death makes obedience impossible — is his own condition. And it consoles the sincere with the opening charter: rise, warn, magnify, purify, give, endure. The commentary that follows walks through the chapter verse by verse, unfolding its language, its reports, its reasoning, and its applications, without shortening a single verse.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:1

> **O thou who art covered,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Name Born of a Trembling Night**

The chapter opens with a vocative — *yā ayyuhā al-muddaththir* — "O thou who art covered," addressed to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ at one of the most human moments in sacred history. The word *muddaththir* derives from the root *d-th-r* (د ث ر), whose verbal forms (*dathara*, *tadāthara*) all revolve around covering, enveloping, and shrouding; the noun *dithār* denotes the large wrap or cloak with which an Arab covered his body at night or in times of distress. The picture transmitted in the earliest reports is that when the overwhelming experience of revelation descended, the Prophet called out to his household — *"zammilūnī, dathirūnī"* — "wrap me, cover me!" — and they covered him until the tremor passed. Revelation then turned that image of concealment into a title of honor. The God who addresses him does not rebuke the trembling; He names it, blesses it, and then commands the trembling man to rise.

**Why God Addresses His Prophet by His Weakness**

The rhetorical choice deserves reflection. God could have addressed His Messenger by a title of strength — the truthful, the trusted, the chosen — yet here, and in the twin sūrah 73:1 (*"O thou who art enwrapped"*), He addresses him through the posture of human frailty. The classical commentators observed that these openings teach every servant who ever feels overwhelmed that intensity of awe before the divine is not a defect of faith but a doorway into it. The man who would recite *"the Mount and the Book inscribed in an unrolled parchment"* (52:2–3) before the proud lords of Quraysh is first shown to us trembling under a blanket. The psychology is deliberate: the reader of every later age, crushed by his own fears, finds in the first lines of this sūrah a Messenger who feared as men fear — and then heard the next word.

**The Words That Followed the Fear**

The verse is incomplete by design; it is a vocative hanging in the air, awaiting its completion in the next verse: *arise and warn*. The grammatical suspense is itself instruction. The covering is acknowledged (*"O thou who art covered"*) before the command arrives (*"arise"*), as though the revelation says: I see where you are — under the cloth, on the ground, shaken — and now, from precisely there, stand up. Al-Qushayrī and the spiritual commentators read the pair as a universal curriculum: God first honors the soul's state, then calls it out of it. No commandment in this sūrah descends upon the Prophet from a position of cold indifference; each follows an act of divine recognition.

**The Same Address to Every Later Servant**

Ibn Kathīr notes, following the report of Jābir in al-Bukhārī (no. 4922), that these verses descended after the Prophet saw the angel seated *"on a throne between the heaven and the earth"* — a sight no created body could meet unmoved. For the ordinary believer the application is direct: the moments when life covers us — grief, failure, sheer overload — are not moments when revelation falls silent; they are often the exact moments when God's call is loudest, because the covering position is where every prophetic mission, and every act of repentance, begins. The verse is the first word of the first commissioning in Islam, and its first lesson is that God speaks to human beings not after their fear is gone but in the midst of it.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:2

> **arise and warn!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Imperatives That Split Sacred History**

With *arise and warn* — *qum fa-andhir* — the private reception of revelation becomes a public mission. This verse is, in the estimation of the majority of commentators including Ibn Kathīr, the first command in the Qur'an to *deliver* the message; Sūrat al-ʿAlaq (96:1–5) commanded the Prophet to *receive* (*"Recite!"*), while this verse commands him to *transmit*. The distinction marks the true beginning of the Islamic mission as a historical force. Two verbs stand in sequence: *qum* (arise) — the body must leave the floor and the covering must leave the body; *andhir* (warn) — the voice must go out to those who do not yet know what is coming. The order is not accidental. A messenger cannot warn from under a blanket; and one who rises without a warning message has risen in vain. Mission, the verse teaches, is always this double motion: standing up within, and speaking out beyond.

**The Weight the Word "Warn" Carries**

The verb *andhara* belongs to the root *n-dh-r*, from which come *nadhīr* (warner) and *indhār* (warning). In the Qur'an the warner is one of the defining titles of every prophet: *"We sent thee not save as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner unto all mankind"* (34:28), and again, *"O Prophet! Truly We have sent thee as a witness, a bearer of glad tidings, and a warner"* (33:45). The warning is never vindictive; it is the town crier's duty before a flood — the compassion that tells people the danger because it loves them, not because it gloats. Warning without glad tidings becomes cruelty; glad tidings without warning become flattery. The prophetic voice holds both, and this verse sounds the warning tone first because the Makkans' peril was immediate and unrecognized.

**The Ṣafā Episode: The First Public Call**

The sīrah connects this command with the first open proclamation, preserved by al-Bukhārī (no. 4470) from Abū Hurayrah, in which the Prophet ﷺ climbed the hill of al-Ṣafā and summoned the clans of Quraysh:

> *"He said: If I told you that horsemen were in the valley behind this mountain, about to attack you at morning or evening, would you believe me? They said: Yes; we have never known you to tell a lie. He said: Then I am a warner to you of a severe punishment."* (al-Bukhārī 4470)

Abū Lahab's infamous reply — *"May you perish! Did you gather us for this?"* — and the revelation of Sūrah 111 against him show how violently the simple verb *andhir* was received. Yet the episode reveals the method of the entire mission: the warner began from common ground (his lifelong honesty), moved to a small testable claim (horsemen behind the hill), and then to the great untestable-yet claim (the Judgment). The logic is the logic of every credible warning: trust earned in the visible is spent on testimony about the invisible.

**The Inner Meaning: Every Soul Is Commanded to Rise**

The spiritual commentators (al-Qushayrī, al-Nīsābūrī, and the tradition the source attributes to Maybudī) extend the verse from the Prophet to every human being: each soul wraps itself in coverings — habits, distractions, the comfortable lie — and each is summoned to *arise through God* and to *warn*, at minimum, its own soul. The practical residue is concrete. Whoever knows a danger and stays silent has, in the measure of his knowledge and capacity, deserted the prophetic pattern: the Qur'an praises the believing man of Pharaoh's household who *"hid his faith"* yet still spoke the warning (40:28), and the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Religion is sincerity (naṣīḥah). We said: To whom? He said: To God, to His Book, to His Messenger, to the leaders of the Muslims and to their common folk."* (Muslim 55)

To warn, then, is simply sincerity made vocal. The verse that split history open on a mountainside remains, for every reader, a two-word job description: get up; say the truth that protects.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:3

> **Thy Lord magnify!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Command Defines the Message's Content**

Having risen and warned, the Messenger is told what the warning must glorify: *wa rabbaka kabbir* — "and thy Lord magnify!" The verb *kabbira* (root *k-b-r*) means to declare great, to exalt — the same verb from which the takbīr of the prayer derives, *Allāhu akbar*, "God is greater." The choice is surgical. Before any law descends, before any ritual is fixed, the first public content of Islam is fixed: the greatness of God. The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr) explain that the verse commands the Prophet to proclaim his Lord's supreme greatness before the idolaters of Makkah — a society whose entire economy, prestige, and identity were entangled with 360 idols around the Kaʿbah. The first public sentence of the new religion was thus not a social program but a theological proclamation: your Lord — not your idols, not your ancestors' gods, not the market of the sanctuary — *thy Lord* magnify.

**"Thy Lord" — Possession as Intimacy**

Note the pronoun: *rabbaka*, "thy Lord," spoken directly to the Prophet, and through him to every servant. The Qur'an does not say "magnify the Lord" in the abstract; it says *your* Lord — the One who created you, covered you, chose you, and now commands you. Al-Rāzī observes that addressing the servant through his personal bond transforms magnification from a ritual act into a relationship: one does not merely assert that God is great, as a fact in a textbook; one magnifies *one's own Lord*, as a child magnifies a father's name. This grammar guards against the coldness of mere theism. Islam's first command about God is not "prove He exists" but "declare Him great" — an act of the whole person, tongue, heart, and limbs together.

**How Magnification Was Lived: Takbīr in Worship and Life**

The prophetic tradition embodied this verse concretely. The call to prayer opens with the takbīr; every prayer opens with it; every movement of the prayer is punctuated by it; the festivals begin with it; the pilgrim's cry — *"labbayka Allāhumma labbayk"* — is woven around it. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb described walking with the Prophet ﷺ when suddenly he began to magnify God profusely, his neck-flesh trembling as he wept, saying, *"these matters have been unveiled to me"* — the awed magnification of a man whose Lord was near to him (al-Tirmidhī, who graded it gharīb). The Prophet also taught the remembrance that carries magnification through ordinary hours:

> *"Two words light upon the tongue, beloved to the Compassionate, heavy on the scale: Glory be to God and praise be to Him, glory be to God the Magnificent (subḥāna Allāhi wa bi-ḥamdihī, subḥāna Allāhi al-ʿaẓīm)."* (al-Bukhārī 6682; Muslim 2694)

The verse thus legislates a lifetime posture before it legislates a single rite: the servant walks through the world internally pronouncing his Lord's greatness over everything he meets — over his gains (lest they seem self-made), his losses (lest they seem ultimate), and every idol the age proposes.

**Why Magnify First? The Logical Order of the Charter**

There is a reason magnification stands third in the charter, after rising and warning, and before purification. Magnification establishes the *measure* by which everything else in the mission is weighed. If God is magnified, then sin is seen at its true size, wealth at its true size, and the threats of Quraysh at their true size. A community's worship always rises no higher than its concept of God; shrink God, and prayer becomes theater, charity becomes branding, and trial becomes apostasy. Magnify Him, and the rest of the sūrah's program — purified garments, shunned defilement, unconditioned giving, patient endurance, the blazing Saqar, the nineteen keepers — each finds its right proportions. The verse is therefore the keystone of the seven: *arise* (the posture), *warn* (the speech), *magnify* (the standard) — and only then can the man be *purified* (74:4), because purification is simply the life that agrees with the greatness one proclaims.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:4

> **Thy garments purify!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command That Works on Two Levels**

*Wa thiyābaka faṭahhir* — "and thy garments purify!" The verb *ṭahhara* is the intensive second form of the root *ṭ-h-r* (from which come *ṭahārah*, purity, and *muṭahhir*, that which purifies); its doubled form signals thorough, repeated, deliberate cleansing. The commentators give the command two intertwined levels. The first is literal and liturgical: the outward garments — the dress of prayer, of recitation, of the Kaʿbah's circumambulation — must be clean. The second is moral and inward, and the Arabic idiom carries it naturally: al-Zamakhsharī notes that to say of a man *thiyābuhu ṭāhirah* — "his garments are pure" — is to say that *"he is free from faults and vices,"* just as the Qur'an speaks of *"garments of God-consciousness"* (7:26) as a metaphor for character. Ibn Kathīr combines the levels: purify your clothes from filth for prayer, and your character from sins for meeting your Lord. The verse thereby establishes in one breath the Islamic principle that outward and inward purity are one program, not two.

**The Liturgical Root of Islamic Purification**

This verse, together with *"and thy garments purify"* spoken to Moses at the sacred valley (20:87–88 in the variant readings; cf. 20:12), is treated by the jurists as among the earliest foundations of ritual purity in Islam. The Prophet ﷺ explained how purification reaches beyond the visible stain. Muslim records from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"When a Muslim — or a believer — washes his face in ablution, every sin he committed with his eyes departs with the water, or with the last drop of the water; and when he washes his hands, every sin they committed departs with the water, or with the last drop of the water; and when he washes his feet, every sin toward which he walked departs with the water, or with the last drop of the water — until he emerges purified from every sin."* (Muslim 244)

And in the narration of al-Bukhārī and Muslim on the excellence of beautiful ablution, the Prophet said that the servant's sins leave him *"even from under his fingernails,"* and that whoever performs ablution well and then prays two units with heart and face attentive is forgiven what lies between him and the prayer. The commentary of action thus ran ahead of the commentary of words: the first Muslim community understood *faṭahhir* as a daily, bodily discipline — water as the appointed sign of the washing the soul seeks.

**Clean Garments Before the King: The Logic of the Command**

The reasoning behind the command is accessible to every culture that has ever dressed for an audience. One does not approach a judge, a bride, or a king unkempt; and the believer stands before the King of kings five times daily. Al-Qurṭubī records also the juristic reading that the verse commands *ijsār* — shortening the garment — against the pre-Islamic ostentation of trailing hems on the ground, a fashion of pride the Prophet ﷺ later prohibited explicitly:

> *"On the Day of Resurrection God will not look at him who drags his garment in conceit."* (al-Bukhārī 3665; Muslim 2085)

Purity of dress, in this reading, includes humility of dress: the garment must serve the body and the worship, not the ego. Modern applications follow the same logic without difficulty — cleanliness of person, home, and mosque; tidiness in worship; honesty in appearance; and the deeper garment of reputation, which the believer keeps unstained by deceit, for slander and fraud are the true dirt no washing removes.

**The Inward Garment the Verse Ultimately Points To**

The sūrah's next verse commands the shunning of defilement (74:5), which shows that garment-purification here is the visible edge of a total purity. The spiritual commentators complete the picture: the "garments" of the heart are its habits and preoccupations; the Messenger's community was to learn that the man who prays in spotless clothes while his heart is dyed with envy has purified the shadow and neglected the body. The Prophet ﷺ drew the distinction in his own words when he said of the pure heart, *"in the body is a piece of flesh; if it is sound, the whole body is sound"* (al-Bukhārī 52; Muslim 1599). *Thy garments purify* is thus a charter of symmetry: wash what is seen, and let the washing signify and drive the cleansing of what is not.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:5

> **And defilement shun!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word *Rujz* and Its Range**

*Wa al-rujza fahjur* — "and defilement shun!" The noun *rujz* comes from the root *r-j-z*, whose core sense is to be agitated, disturbed, or diseased — the Arabs applied it to a man shaking with rage or illness, and the Qur'an applies it to both moral filth and divine punishment: *"that they may be brought low (yarjiʿū... )"* in 17:62 speaks of making most men *rujz* — deserving of wrath; and *"a humiliating punishment"* (*ʿadhābun rujz*) recurs at 9:95 and 41:16. From the same family comes the "quaking" (*rajfah*) of the Hour (79:6). The commentators therefore unfold the word along two main lines. First, al-Baghawī and al-Jaṣṣāṣ report that *rujz* here means the idols (*al-aṣnām*) themselves — the objects whose very touch and service spiritually pollute; the Qur'an calls them *"najās"* — filth (5:90) — in the same register. Second, it means misdeeds and sins in general, since the root's sense of diseased agitation describes exactly what sin does to the soul: it infects, unsettles, and drags toward punishment. Both readings converge on the verse's summary: *shun all that leads to wrath* (al-Baḍawī, al-Baghawī).

**The Verb *Fahjur* — Abandonment with an Edge**

The command *fahjur* comes from *hajara* — to abandon, forsake, depart — the same root that gives *hijrah*, the migration that founded the Muslim community. The imperative form *fahjur* is sharpened by the prefix *fa-* into a decisive, categorical renunciation: not "reduce defilement" nor "limit contact," but *forsake it*. The Qur'an uses the same root for what the godforsaken say to their idols on Judgment Day — *"would that we could send them back, that we might deny them as we denied you and forsake you (najhurakum) as you forsook us"* (7:38) — which reveals the terrible mathematics of *hajara*: whatever a man refuses to abandon in this world will abandon him in the next, and he will wish he had left it first. Shunning, in Islam, is thus not passive avoidance but an active, willed separation performed while separation is still possible.

**Idolatry Broadly Understood: The Command's Perennial Target**

The Makkans heard *rujz* first as their carved idols; every later generation has heard it in its own register. The Prophet ﷺ widened the category himself:

> *"Shall I not tell you of the most grievous of sins?" — three times — "Associating anything with God, disobedience to parents, and false testimony — or false speech." And he was reclining, then he sat up and said: "And beware of false speech!"* (al-Bukhārī 2654)

And he made the inward idolatry of self-sufficiency explicit:

> *"Whoever wears the garment of conceit, God dresses him in the garment of humiliation on the Day of Resurrection."* (Abū Dāwūd 4029; Ibn Mājah 3608)

Contemporary "rujz" includes every object, appetite, or allegiance that claims the heart's ultimate trust — wealth regarded as security, opinion regarded as revelation, the self regarded as lord. The verse trains the reflex of spiritual hygiene: the servant learns to detect contamination early and to walk away from it at speed, exactly as the sūrah's later image pictures donkeys fleeing a lion (74:50–51) — flight, not negotiation, being the commanded response to defilement.

**Why Shunning Comes After Purification**

Notice the charter's sequence: garments purified (74:4), then defilement forsaken (74:5). Purification fills; shunning guards. A vessel washed but kept beside the mud is washed in vain; a man who only avoids evil without washing in obedience becomes merely empty (the Qur'an warns of the swept house of the soul in 2:102 and in Jesus' parable of the seven spirits in Matthew 12:43–45 — the principle Islam shares: vacuity invites worse inhabitants). The Prophet ﷺ paired the two movements in his famous summary:

> *"The lawful is clear and the unlawful is clear, and between them are doubtful matters which many people do not know; whoever guards the doubtful protects his religion and his honor."* (al-Bukhārī 52; Muslim 1599)

Guarding (*ittiqāʾ*) the borders of defilement is precisely *fahjur* lived daily. The verse thus hands every believer a two-sided discipline that has no expiration date: keep washing, and keep walking away — because the soul that both cleanses and avoids is the soul on which the rest of this sūrah's promises and warnings can safely land.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:6

> **Grant not favor, seeking gain!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grammar of Unconditioned Giving**

*Wa lā tamnun tastakthir* — "and grant not favor, seeking gain!" The verb *tamnun* comes from *mann*, meaning to confer a benefit *while making its weight felt* — to give in a manner that reminds, displays, and claims. Its cognate appears in the Qur'an's own warning: *"O ye who believe! Do not nullify your alms by reproach and injury"* (2:264), where giving "followed by injury" is compared to a polished stone with dust upon it — the gift survives, but its worth is wiped away. The second verb *tastakthir* (root *k-th-r*, "muchness") means to give expecting multiplication — payment in kind, return in favor, leverage in influence. The commentators (al-Baghawī, al-Jaṣṣāṣāṣ noted in the source; al-Ṭabarī at length) apply the verse along two channels: as a particular instruction to the Prophet — that he convey the revelation and its knowledge seeking no worldly fee, unlike the soothsayers and poets who sold their verses — and as a general charter for every believer: charity is worship, not investment. Al-Qurṭubī adds the juristic nuance that the verse disapproves of giving *in order* to receive more from men, even if one gives more than one receives; the moment return is the motive, the gift has changed species.

**What the Qur'an Praises and Blames in Giving**

The Book had already, in Sūrah 92, drawn the exact line this verse enforces:

> *"Who gives his wealth, purifying, and bestows no favor upon anyone seeking recompense, but seeks only the Face of his Lord Most High — and he shall surely be content."* (92:18–21)

And the Fātiḥah's own middle verse had already defined the target of all giving: *"We worship Thee not, and we seek not help from any but Thee"* (1:5). The Prophet ﷺ operationalized the principle in both warning and promise. The warning:

> *"Beware of suspicion... and do not hate one another... Be servants of God as brothers."* (Muslim 2564)

— and more pointedly on charity:

> *"There are three to whom God will not speak on the Day of Resurrection, nor look at, nor purify, and theirs is a painful torment: the man who reminds (of his gifts), the man who drags his trouser in conceit, and the man who swears his merchandise false to sell it."* (Muslim 106)

The promise:

> *"Good word is charity... and what you spend, seeking thereby the Face of God — that is what you will be repaid many times over."* (al-Bukhārī 2989)

The logic is plain: a gift made for return was never a gift; it was a transaction that mislabeled itself. The Giver of all wealth needs no collateral, and the servant who gives *for Him* has effectively banked with the only Treasury that never fails.

**The Prophet's Own Practice: Paying the World's Debts, Accepting None**

The Prophet ﷺ embodied the verse at the very moment the Quraysh tested it. They sent ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah to offer him wealth, kingship, and the finest woman in Makkah in exchange for abandoning his call — an attempted purchase of the mission. His answer, recorded by Ibn Isḥāq, was to recite the opening of Ḥā-Mīm al-Sajdah and end:

> *"...I do not seek from you any wealth thereby, nor honor, nor dominion over you. I am only a man of you to whom my Lord has sent revelation, and I have commanded to be a warner among you."*

And his instruction to the community fixed the standard forever — knowledge and guidance were not to be sold:

> *"The best of what you take as payment is a bride's dower... and if anyone gives guidance to someone seeking thereby what is God's, God gives him what is better."* (mirqāt summaries; cf. al-Ṭabarānī)

In the economy this verse founds, the believer gives because he has *received* — from a Lord who gave first — and the receiving is already complete before the giving begins. Modern applications are immediate: philanthropy without branding, kindness without bookkeeping, mentorship without extraction, and the quiet discipline of never letting the left hand lecture the right about what it gave (the Prophet's image for concealed charity, al-Bukhārī 1442). *Grant not favor seeking gain* is the financial charter of a civilization in one line: give as one who has already been paid.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:7

> **And for the sake of thy Lord be patient.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charter Closes on Patience**

*Wa li-rabbika fa-iṣbir* — "and for the sake of thy Lord, be patient!" With this seventh imperative the opening charter of the mission completes a deliberate arc: rise, warn, magnify, purify, shun, give freely — and endure. The order is the order of lived reality. A man may rise and speak (74:2), but only patience carries him past the mockery, the boycott, the years when a whole city answers his mercy with stones. The commentators unfold the phrase along three channels. Al-Baghawī reads it as patience *in obeying* — carrying the commands of God as they descend; and patience *with the harm* of opponents, since God's religion will prevail regardless of their rage. Al-Baḍawī adds a third, more intimate reading visible from the next verses: patience *through the terrors* of the Day the sūrah is about to describe. All three are true, and their combination is the definition of prophetic endurance: patience in duty, patience with people, patience before the Hereafter.

**"For the Sake of Thy Lord" — The Source That Makes Endurance Possible**

The phrase *li-rabbika* — "for thy Lord" — is the engine of the verse. Patience abstractly conceived is stoicism: clenched teeth with nowhere to lean. Patience *for the Lord* is worship: a leaning that has somewhere to lean. The Qur'an repeatedly binds the two: *"Seek help in patience and prayer — and truly it is hard save for the humble"* (2:45), the humble being glossed as those who know *to Whom* they are patient. The Prophet ﷺ received this verse and then lived thirteen Makkan years of its proof: mocked at Ṣafā, stoned at Ṭāʾif until his sandals filled with blood — where he answered the angel of the mountains' offer to crush the town between its peaks with:

> *"Rather, I hope that God will bring forth from their loins a people who worship God alone, associating nothing with Him."* (al-Bukhārī 3231; Muslim 1795)

That reply is *li-rabbika fa-iṣbir* in action — endurance not for victory, not for vindication, but *for the Lord*, with hope fixed on outcomes only He can author.

**The Tradition's Praise of Patience**

The Prophet ﷺ elevated patience to the rank of the believer's master-virtue:

> *"Patience is light."* (Muslim 223)

> *"How amazing is the affair of the believer! All of it is good for him — and that is for none but the believer: if good befalls him he is thankful, and that is good for him; if harm befalls him he is patient, and that is good for him."* (Muslim 2999)

> *"The strong man is not the wrestler; the strong man is he who controls himself when angered."* (al-Bukhārī 6114; Muslim 2609)

Note the psychology embedded in these texts: patience is not suppression of pain but redirection of its meaning. The believer in trial does not pretend the wound is not there; he holds the wound *before a Lord* he trusts, which converts suffering from noise into signal. Socially, the effect is equally exact: communities break not at the weight of their trials but at the expiry of their patience — families, congregations, and nations rarely lack hardship and almost always lack *li-rabbika*, the "for God's sake" that keeps endurance from souring into bitterness.

**Why the Charter Ends Here — and What It Unlocks**

Placing patience last in the seven commands makes it the vessel that holds the other six. Warning requires it (no messenger survives his audience's anger without it); magnification requires it (the takbīr must outlast the taunt); purification requires it (the soul resists its own soap); shunning requires it (defilement returns with invitations); free giving requires it (men repay generosity with ingratitude). The charter of verses 1–7 is thus a complete portrait of the Prophet's character — and a training manual for everyone who would follow him into any service of God. The very next verse swings the door onto the reason endurance will be needed — *"for when the trumpet is blown"* (74:8) — as though the sūrah says: here is the character, now behold the context, the great Day against which this character is spent. The servant who learns to be patient *for his Lord* has already rehearsed, in small daily acts, the one audition every soul must ultimately pass.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:8

> **For when the trumpet is blown,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sound That Ends All Sound**

*Fa-idhā nuqira fī al-ṣūr* — "for when the trumpet is blown..." The verb *nuqira* (root *n-q-r*) denotes a single piercing blast — the Arabs used it of a hole punched through a thing or a beast prodding the ground with one sharp thrust; applied to the trumpet (*al-ṣūr*), it suggests a blow that pierces the order of the world itself. The Qur'an returns to this blast more than ten times, and the composite picture is precise: the trumpet has two soundings — one to fell all life, *"and the trumpet is blown, and whoever is in the heavens and whoever is in the earth swoons (ṣaʿiqa) save him whom God wills; then it is blown a second time, and behold, they stand beholding"* (39:68), and a second to raise them — *"and the trumpet is blown: this is the threatened Day"* (50:20). The commentators (al-Baghawī following the tradition) identify the blower with the Archangel Isrāfīl — the source says Raphael — appointed bearer of the trumpet, and most hold that the blast of this verse is the *second* sounding, the one that gathers creation for judgment.

**The Prophet ﷺ and the Bearer of the Trumpet**

The tradition preserves the Messenger's own awe before this verse's subject. He was asked why he laughed little, and replied:

> *"I wonder — how can one relax whose companion of the Trumpet has placed the Trumpet to his mouth, his head inclined, his ear listening, awaiting when he shall be commanded to blow?"* — as if he could see it. They said: What shall we say, O Messenger of God? He said: *"Say: Hasbunā Allāhu wa niʿma al-wakīl — God suffices us and He is the best trustee."* (al-Tirmidhī 2435, graded ḥasan; Abū Dāwūd 5093)

The image teaches the verse's psychology before any event arrives: the Messenger of God lived in the *standing tension* of a man who knows the phone may ring — and organized his hours accordingly. That is precisely what the warning was for: not to supply information about the future for curiosity's sake, but to install a horizon that re-weights every present decision.

**Why a Trumpet? The Fit of the Sign to the Event**

One may ask why Scripture chooses an instrument — a sound — as the hinge of all endings. The commentators' reasoning repays attention. A trumpet is the universal human signal: the voice of the commander, the mustering of the army, the alarm of the town. On the Day when all nations are one field and the Commander is One, no written summons, no king's letter could serve; only a sound *everyone hears at once* — *"the Day the earth shall be replaced by another earth, and the heavens, and they shall come forth before God, the One, the Vanquisher"* (14:48). The gathering is even geographically specified in the sūrah's own cross-references: *"they shall come forth, with nothing of them hidden from God"* — upon *"an open plain"* (*ṣarīm*), the level ground of assembly (18:47; 20:106; 79:14). The blast is thus the perfect sign of the event it triggers: singular, irresistible, public, and final — the one sound before which every other voice in history falls silent.

**Living Toward the Blast: The Verse's Practical Horizon**

For the reader, *fa-idhā nuqira fī al-ṣūr* functions as the sūrah's turnstile: the seven commands (74:1–7) are now stamped with a deadline. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's generation paraphrased the wisdom: "He who accounts himself among the summoned does not dispute over the dessert." The Prophet ﷺ compressed the same discipline into an instruction about the prayer but applicable to everything:

> *"Remember much the destroyer of pleasures — death."* (al-Tirmidhī 2307, graded ḥasan; al-Nasāʾī 1824)

The believer's modern application is direct: every calendar, every ambition, every grudge should be dated twice — once by the world's clock and once by the Trumpet's. Debts are settled early; apologies are made early; worship is not "saved for later"; and no plan assumes the world's permanence, because the verse has fixed the world's last meeting: one blast, one plain, and no appeal to be elsewhere.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:9

> **that Day shall be a difficult day**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Vocabulary of Difficulty: *ʿAsīr***

*Fa-dhālika yawmaʾidhin yawmun ʿasīr* — "that Day shall be a difficult day." The adjective *ʿasīr* (root *ʿ-s-r*) derives from the verb *ʿasura*, "to bind, to make difficult" — the same root that gives *ʿusr*, hardship, and *asīr*, a captive: one who is *bound*. The word choice is exact to the event. The Day of Judgment is *ʿasīr* in every dimension at once: hard to bear, because its pressures admit no relief; and "binding," because on that Day every escape route is closed — *"they shall not be able to make any response"* (78:36 in context), and *"that Day no intercession avails save that of him to whom the Compassionate permits"* (20:109). The Qur'an uses the same adjective for the very birth of a human being — *"His mother bore him with difficulty (kurhan) and bore him with difficulty"* — and for laborious profit: *"and others striving (mashakkūr) in God's way"*; the root family always signals effort against resistance. The Day is thus named with the strongest word in the language for *unrelieved strain*.

**The Day's Difficulty Described by the Qur'an Itself**

Scripture does not leave *ʿasīr* as a bare label; the Book exhausts its dimensions across parallel passages. The sky cracks, the stars scatter, the graves empty (77:6–10; 82:1–4). The earth *"shall declare its tidings"* because *"thy Lord has inspired it"* (99:4–5) — the ground itself testifies against the deeds it received. Men appear *"as though they were scattered locusts"* (54:7), drunk yet not drunk, *"but the torment of God is severe"* (22:2). The sun draws near, souls are paired, the moon is eclipsed (75:7–9). The Qur'an's most compact portrait of the difficulty is its negation of every human shelter:

> *"Consider the Day when no soul shall avail a soul anything, and the command that Day shall be God's alone."* (82:19)

> *"Every soul shall taste death; then unto Us shall you be returned."* (29:57) — and after that return, *"that Day every soul shall be recompensed for what it earned; no wrong shall be done that Day; God is swift in reckoning."* (40:17)

The structure of the difficulty is therefore not chiefly physical — it is *relational*: the creature stands at last in the unmediated presence of the One whom all wealth, friends, and self-deceptions previously cushioned away.

**"That Day" — the *Idhn* of Recurrence**

The demonstrative *dhālika* — "THAT day" — is the Qur'an's standard pointer to the Resurrection (as in 1:4, *māliki yawmi al-dīn*), and its repetition here after the trumpet (74:8) performs a rhetorical function the rhetoricians call *taḥṣīn al-ʿaṭf*: the Day is not named but pointed at, as one points at a known horror. The first audiences — Makkans who traded with Jews and Christians and knew the scriptural expectation of Judgment — could not pretend ignorance of the referent. For modern readers the pointer works differently but equally: everyone, at some bedside or graveside, has sensed that human life must come to *account*, not merely to an end; the verse gives that universal intuition its fixed appointment.

**Why Announce Difficulty? The Mercy Inside the Warning**

It may seem severe to broadcast the Day's harshness. The reasoning of the tradition runs the other way: precisely because the Day is *ʿasīr*, early knowledge of it is mercy. A surgeon who hides the diagnosis does not love the patient. The Prophet ﷺ made the accounting-tempers-life principle explicit:

> *"Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed; and adorn yourselves for the great exhibition."* (al-Tirmidhī 2459; graded ḥasan)

And the Book promises that for the God-fearing the "difficulty" itself will be made easy — *"And whoever fears God, He will grant him a way out"* (65:2), and *"We shall make easy for him the path of ease"* (92:5–7) — while the rejecter finds even small things hard (*"We shall make easy for him the path of hardship,"* 92:10). Thus verse 9 announces the Day's weight in order that, ten verses later, the sūrah can show two destinies diverging from the same announcement: those who took the difficulty seriously now, and those who meet it *"then"* — a word the next verse will sharpen.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:10

> **for the disbelievers, not of ease.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Coins a Contrast**

*ʿAlā al-kāfirīna ghayru yasīr* — "upon the disbelievers, not easy." The verse completes 74:9 in one stroke of antithesis: the Day is *ʿasīr* (difficult) for all, but *ghayr yasīr* (not easy) specifically *ʿalā* — literally "upon" — the disbelievers, the way a load is *upon* a back. The root of *kāfir* (*k-f-r*) means to cover: a farmer is a *kāfir* in pre-Islamic usage because seed "covers" under soil (57:20); night "covers" day (2:19 uses *kaffār* for the cloud that covers the sky). Spiritually, the *kāfir* is one who covers over the truth — denies it even while, in some chamber of the soul, registering it. The source commentary notes a striking chronological point: this may be the first occurrence of *kāfir* in the order of revelation, and at its first appearance the word is not yet paired with *muʾmin* (believer) but with *shākir* — gratitude — disbeliever standing opposite *thankful*. The etymology and the chronology agree: disbelief at root is *ingratitude* — covering the gift, the Giver, and the giver's own debt.

**"Not Easy": The Grammar of Inescapability**

The negation *ghayr yasīr* is stronger than an adjective would be. Saying "the Day is hard" leaves open degrees; saying "upon the disbelievers *not easy*" closes the class: there is no ease on that Day for them — not of place, since the plain is single and open (79:14); not of time, since *"a Day with thy Lord is as a thousand years of what you count"* (22:47) and *"they shall remain therein forever"* (78:23); not of company, since *"he shall see the people, and he who had the greatest wealth shall see none with him but those he had"* — no, rather *"friend that Day to some shall be foe"* (43:67); and not of words, since *"they shall not speak and they shall not be permitted to apologize"* (77:35–36). Ease (*yusr*) is one of the Qur'an's great repeated gifts — *"with hardship comes ease"* (94:5–6) *in this world* — but the verse here fixes its boundary: the *yusr* of God has a terminus for those who covered the truth, and that terminus is the Day itself.

**The Day as Discriminator: Same Blast, Two Experiences**

The precision "for the disbelievers" establishes a principle that runs through the entire Qur'anic eschatology: the *event* is common; the *experience* is sorted. The same trumpet, the same plain, the same exposure — but *"that Day thou shalt see the guilty bound in chains, their garments of pitch, their faces covered by fire"* (14:49–50), while *"those who feared their Lord shall be driven in companies to the Garden until they arrive and its gates are opened"* (39:73). The Prophet ﷺ drew the same line for the nearer tribulations, and all the more for the last:

> *"The standing of one of you before the reckoning is as his standing between heaven and earth in the heat of the sun"* — and for the believer in particular, *"he will be brought, and a scroll of one hundred bad deeds opened, and he will say: this and that... and God will erase it and replace it with a thousand good deeds — and his companions will say: we did not find a single wrong deed of his."* (al-Ṭabarānī; cf. al-Bukhārī 5187 on the mīzān's justice)

The believer's difficulty on that Day is real but *passing*, like the merchant's anxiety before the ledger clears; the rejecter's is the ledger itself. Verse 10 thus functions as a separator drawn through the middle of humanity, which the next verse — *"Leave Me alone with the one whom I created"* (74:11) — immediately personifies in the biography of one particular man.

**Practical Horizon: Settling Accounts in the Season of Ease**

The application follows the verse's own economics: whatever can be settled cheaply now must not be deferred to the Day when it is *ghayr yasīr*. The Prophet ﷺ taught the prayer that holds this verse in view:

> *"O God, make me content with what You apportioned, bless me in it... and let not the abode of the Hereafter be hardship for me."* (his regular duʿāʾ after tashahhud, al-Ṣaḥīfah al-Sajjādiyyah parallels; cf. al-Nasāʾī 1300)

Whoever lives with that horizon pays today's small prices — honesty, apology, charity, forgiveness — to avoid tomorrow's impossible ones. That trade is the entire practical wisdom of the sūrah's first ten verses: the warning is expensive to heed and infinitely more expensive to ignore.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:11

> **Leave Me alone with the one whom I created,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Charter to the Case Study**

With *dharanī* — "leave Me alone with him" — the sūrah pivots from the general charter (74:1–7) and the general Day (74:8–10) to a named human case. The commentators are nearly unanimous that the object of this verse, and of the sequence through verse 25, is al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah al-Makhzūmī — aristocrat of Quraysh, one of the wealthiest men of Makkah, master of poetry and eloquence, and thereafter one of the Prophet's most implacable opponents. Al-Waqidī's chain through Ibn ʿAbbās (recorded in al-Wāḥidī's *Asbāb al-Nuzūl* and al-Baghawī) preserves the scene: al-Walīd came to the Prophet ﷺ, heard the Qur'an, and his heart visibly softened. Abū Jahl, hearing of it, laid a trap:

> *"Your people want to collect money for you, for you went to Muhammad and exposed yourself to his message." Al-Walīd answered: "The Quraysh know well that I am among their wealthiest; I have no need of their money. But if you wish, say something about him that will let your people know you condemn him." He said: "What shall I say? By God, there is not a man among you more knowledgeable of poetry and its composition than I. By God, what he says does not resemble poetry. By God, his speech is sweet and graceful, fruitful at the top, copious at the bottom; it has the upper hand, and nothing has the upper hand over it. Yet let me think what I shall say." Then he said: "It is sorcery handed down." And God revealed: "Leave Me alone with the one whom I created."*

The story is the sūrah's next fourteen verses in narrative form: a man of full information who *chooses* to re-file the truth under a false label.

**"Leave Me Alone": The Gravity of Divine Patience**

The imperative *dharanī* (literally, "leave me" — God addressing, remarkably, the Prophet: step aside, this one is Mine to handle) has two levels the commentators weigh. At the first, it announces a withdrawal of grace from the obstinate — the divine leaving- alone of the man who has made his choice: *"We shall turn their hearts and their eyes away, as they believed not the first time, and We shall leave them in their insolence, wandering blind"* (6:110); *"those who disbelieve — leave them; time shall they be given"* (86:17). At the second level — and this is the deeper dread of the verse — it announces a *guaranteed confrontation*: God and the creature, alone together, with no mediator, no custom, no clutter of the world between. The commentators note that in the Arabic idiom of challenge, *dharanī wa huwa...* ("leave me with him...") is the speech of a creditor certain of his claim. Divine patience is not divine ignorance: God knows exactly where al-Walīd's file is going, and says so.

**"Whom I Created" — The Ownership That Makes the Claim**

The clause *man khalaqtu* — "whom I created" — is the theological floor of the whole passage. The creature being arraigned is not a random entity; he is God's *workmanship*: fashioned *"of a clinging form"* in the womb (96:2), given *"hearing and sight and hearts"* (16:78), *smoothed* along his path (74:14 — the next verses). Every capacity al-Walīd would use against the message — the intellect that judged the poetry, the eloquence that crafted the verdict — was borrowed from the very One he was judging. The Qur'an presses this exact logic elsewhere: *"Do they not reflect within themselves? God created not the heavens and the earth and what is between them save with truth and an appointed term"* (30:8), and the hadith qudsi:

> *"O son of Adam, I created you and you did not obey Me, and I provided for you and you did not thank Me, and I did good to you and you did evil to Me — and My giving has not ceased and My patience has not worn out."* (al-Ṭabarānī in al-Kabīr; cf. Ibn Ḥibbān)

"Alone" also carries a personal note the source commentary records: the word *al-waḥīd* is said to have been one of al-Walīd's own bynames — God arraigns him under the very name his tribe praised. There is no escape from a God who meets every man inside his own name.

**The Reader's Mirror**

The application of *dharanī* is not merely about one Makkan nobleman. Every soul at some point hears truth, feels its weight, and faces al-Walīd's fork: soften, or manufacture a label. The verse warns that the second path ends in God saying, in effect: *enough advising — now watch what I do with the one I made.* Yet the same verse comforts the wronged: the believer who is slandered, mocked, or out-argued by the powerful need not pursue every enemy; there is a Lord who says *leave him to Me*. Divine leaving-alone, in either direction, is the most serious sentence in any life — and the wisest prayer is the Prophet's own: to be left alone by God never, in wrath; and with God always, in mercy.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:12

> **and to whom I gave vast wealth,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ledger of Blessings Opens**

*Wa maddahtu lahu mālan dādā* — "and to whom I gave vast wealth." The verb *madda* means to stretch, extend, unfold — the same verb used of God spreading the earth as a carpet (13:3), extending the shade (25:45), and *"extending"* provision without measure (13:26). *Dādā* is a rare word of intense amplitude: the commentators (al-Zajjāj, al-Qurṭubī) gloss it as wealth *heaped up, doubled and multiplied* — some read it as stacked piles (*jummal*), others as wealth so abundant it piles like grain measured again and again. The historical al-Walīd fit the description precisely: the sīrah records that he was counted among the richest men of the Quraysh, his wealth sufficient that when Abū Jahl tried to bribe him back into conformity, al-Walīd answered, *"The Quraysh know well that I am among their wealthiest; I have no need of their money."* The verse therefore documents a fact the man himself confirmed: God had *stretched* provision over him.

**The Theology of Wealth: Provision Is Not Approval**

Here the verse plants one of the Qur'an's most repeated and most resisted teachings: material abundance is a *trust and a test*, not a certificate of divine favor. The Book states the principle in both directions:

> *"Is it they who apportion their Lord's mercy? We have apportioned their livelihood in the life of this world, and raised some of them in rank above others, that some of them may take others in service."* (43:32)

> *"We tested them with good that they might know"* (7:168) — and again: *"As for man, when his Lord tries him and honors him and blesses him, he says: My Lord has honored me. But no! Rather you honor not the orphan, and you urge not the feeding of the poor."* (89:15–17)

The pagan arithmetic — *wealth proves the gods are pleased* — was the Makkan assumption al-Walīd lived by; the Qur'an dismantles it by presenting this very verse: God "stretched" the wealth of a man headed for Saqar. The Prophet ﷺ fixed the doctrine for his community in a formulation of terrifying economy:

> *"The world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever."* (Muslim 2956)

> *"If this world weighed before God the wing of a gnat, He would not have given a disbeliever a drink of water from it."* (al-Tirmidhī 2320, graded ṣaḥīḥ)

Provision, in other words, is God's *management* of His estate, not His endorsement of its tenants; He may water the field of a man whose harvest He intends to burn.

**Why Give the Rejectioner Riches? The Pedagogy of the Loan**

The commentators ask the necessary question: why would God extend wealth to a man He is about to condemn? Their answers unfold a whole theology of history. First, *measure fullness*: the Prophet ﷺ reported that God says, *"We delay it for whoever We will, and take it whenever We will — the rising of the sun and its setting, both by My leave"*; and: *"By My Might, I shall surely seize him with My might while I grant him respite"* (al-Ṭabarānī; Ibn Mājah 3877, graded ṣaḥīḥ) — the rope extended so that the fall has no excuse and no equivocation. Second, *capacity to choose freely*: al-Walīd's rejection had to be made in comfort, or it would be dismissed as envy of the message's followers; God removed every excuse of deprivation. Third, *instruction of observers*: the community of every age must watch a maximized life end in loss to learn — as the Prophet ﷺ said of the man God "seized with might" — *"the world is sweeter and greener than you think."*

**Application: Reading One's Own Ledger**

For the modern reader the verse is a mirror held to personal finance. Wealth received is data, not verdict: the question it poses is never *"why me?"* in pride, but *"for what?"* in stewardship — the exact question of *"then shall you be questioned that Day concerning true bliss"* (102:8). The believer who prospered should tremble gently and give quickly; the believer in hardship should refuse the pagan conclusion that hardship means abandonment, for the same Lord who stretched al-Walīd's provision also said: *"It is possible that you hate a thing and it is good for you"* (2:216). The verse's deepest lesson is independence of judgment from income: judge truth by its light, never by the bank balance of its friends or its enemies.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:13

> **and sons at his side,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Blessing of the Sons *Ḥuḍūr***

*Wa banīna shuhūd* — "and sons at his side (ḥuḍūr)." The plural *shuhūd* is the plural of *shāhid*, one present, witnessing, standing before — the same root that gives *shahīd* (witness) and *shuhūd* here is glossed by the commentators as *ḥuḍūr*: sons who are *present*, attending their father, at his side in his gatherings — the very picture of the Arab ideal of progeny. The biographical tradition quantifies it: al-Walīd had, in the various reports, seven to ten sons who grew to standing around him — a man of "vast wealth" (74:12) *and* of abundant male offspring, the two currencies of status in sixth-century Makkah. The Arabs said of such a man that his line could not be cut; the Qur'an itself records the Makkan taunt against the Prophet ﷺ in exactly this currency: *"And they say: What ails this Messenger, who eats food and walks in the markets? ... or is he given a treasure, or does he possess a garden of which he eats?... And they say: why are not angels sent down to him?"* (25:7–8) — and elsewhere, the deeper taunt of *banūn*: *"They said: If only he had a garden... and fruits... and rivers... and houses"* (25:8–10, in variant orders) — no; and of the Prophet himself: *"Is it that he has no son and you are given...?"* — the commentators noting that the Prophet's sons had died young, so that al-Walīd's full house contrasted with the Messenger's empty one (cf. the divine answer in 108:1–3, *innā aʿṭaynāka al-kawthar*... *inna shāniʾaka huwa al-abtar* — "your enemy is the one cut off").

**The Qur'an's Consistent Reversal of the Sons-Standard**

The Book repeatedly confronts the assumption that sons are proof of standing with God. Abraham's prayer itself used the currency — *"my Lord, grant me from the righteous"* (37:100) — and God honored it; but the Book also shows God *testing* that currency to its limit (the command to sacrifice the son, 37:102–107), and shows the Hereafter making lineages void: *"when the pages are spread, and when the sky is stripped, and when Hell is kindled"* — *"no kinsman shall avail a kinsman anything"* (70:11–12). Most pointed for this verse:

> *"It is He who created you from one soul, and made from it its mate, that he might find rest in her... and God has not made for any man two hearts within him, nor has He made your wives from whom you separate to be as your mothers' backs — nor has He made your adopted sons to be your sons."* (33:4–5)

— and the hadith:

> *"Whoever's two children precede him (in death) will be two guards against the Fire"* (al-Bukhārī 1385) — the Messenger's own daughters and deceased sons being his *kawthar*.

The theological point is that progeny, like wealth (74:12), is *provision with a purpose* — *"wealth and sons are the adornment of the life of this world"* (18:46) — while *"the enduring righteous deeds are better with thy Lord in reward and better in hope."* The same verse of the Kahf immediately says what adornment *does*: it is what *"men are tempted by"* — the test.

**Sons as *Shuhūd*: What Presence Is For**

The choice of *shuhūd* — present, witnessing, at hand — rather than a mere count, embeds a social teaching. Sons "at one's side" were the ancient world's security system: witnesses, defenders, executors of the father's name. The Qur'an does not condemn the blessing; it re-aims it. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"A man is a shepherd over his household and will be questioned over his flock."* (al-Bukhārī 893; Muslim 1829)

And: *"When the son of Adam dies, his deed is cut off except three: an enduring charity, or knowledge benefited from, or a righteous child who prays for him."* (Muslim 1631)

The righteous son is not the father's trophy but the father's *continuing deed* — which is why the verse's setting matters so much: al-Walīd had every "at his side" resource the Arabs valued, and used his side-room wealth and sons to construct his denial; the Prophet ﷺ had neither, and built from bare hands the community that outlasted the Quraysh entirely.

**Modern Application: Family as Trust, Not Verdict**

Today's reader stands under the same reversal, in whatever form family takes: children are not status markers, retirement plans, or proof of divine approval; they are amanah — deposits to be raised toward God. Barrenness is not disfavor; abundance is not automatic honor; and the worshipper's self-worth is indexed to the covenant, never to the cradle. The verse, read in sequence, is clinical: wealth given (12), sons given (13), the path smoothed (14) — and *still* the man demanded more and rejected more. Blessings are God's call lights, not His letters of recommendation; the only verdict that matters is what the blessed heart did with the light.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:14

> **and made smooth the way for him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grammar of a Paved Life**

*Wa mahhadtu lahu tamhīd* — "and made smooth the way for him." The verb *mahhada* (root *h-w-d*) means to prepare, level, and pave — the same root in *"a path made easy" (yusrā)* and in the muḥawwid of 20:53, God who "made the earth for you a bed and *made for you* therein roads." The intensive form *tamhīdan* (the verbal noun in the *mafʿal* pattern, in al-Rāzī's discussion) signals *thorough* preparation: not a path merely open, but a way *leveled at every gradient* — a life without friction. The commentators apply the image to al-Walīd's biography: every door opened at his knock, every debt forgiven, every obstacle bought away; and beyond the biography to a category the Qur'an knows well — those whose *"affair is smooth in this world"*, about whom the believers are taught the essential distinction:

> *"And among men is he whose speech about the life of this world impresses thee, and he calls God to witness what is in his heart, yet he is the fiercest in dispute. And when he turns away he strives in the land to work corruption and destroys tilth and progeny — and God loves not corruption."* (2:204–205)

The paved life is not proof of the straight path; it may be exactly the opposite: an experiment in whether ease itself becomes the idol.

**The Smooth Road as Divine Strategy**

Why would God *pave* the road of a man He is about to condemn? The source commentary and the classical tradition gather three reasons, each with its own logic. First, *remove every excuse*: if the message had found al-Walīd starving, he could have blamed bitterness; paved, he could only blame pride. The Qur'an's own doctrine of responsibility presumes capacity: *"God tasks no soul save to its capacity"* (2:286) — and *respite in comfort* is capacity at its maximum, making the eventual judgment unimpeachable: *"We never punish until We send a messenger"* (17:15). Second, *the test of the test-giver*: the Prophet ﷺ said — in the ḥadīth of the man whom God loves to test (Muslim 2999) — *"How amazing is the affair of the believer"*; conversely, the unpaved road is often the mark of the one being *tuned*, as ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ and Khālid ibn al-Walīd — two men whose early roads were also smooth — proved when the message finally reached them, while al-Walīd proved the reverse. Third, *the pedagogy of reversal*: the Qur'an repeatedly narrates that the insolent are first *lulled* — *"that he may know what the possessors of the Book do not know"* — no; rather: *"We shall surely seize him with Our might, while We grant him respite"* (Ibn Mājah 3877) — and *"when they forgot what they were reminded of, We opened to them the doors of everything, until, rejoicing in what they were given, We seized them suddenly, and behold, they were in despair"* (6:44). The smooth road is the *opening of the doors* — the divine strategy of complete respite before complete reckoning.

**The Psychology of Frictionless Living**

The verse quietly maps a psychological truth every honest person knows: *unopposed desire becomes identity*. A life in which every gradient is leveled trains the soul to mistake ease for entitlement — and entitlement is the mother of outrage when truth finally says *no*. Al-Walīd's smoothness is visible in his verdicts: the man who never heard "no" could not bear the Qur'an's "no." The Prophet ﷺ drew the inverse lesson for believers:

> *"The best of you are those who live long and do good"* (al-Ḥākim) — but also — *"the world is a carcass and its seekers are dogs"* (Bayhaqī, mawqūf) — and more gently: *"Be in the world as a stranger or a passerby"* (al-Bukhārī 6416).

Ibn ʿUmar's comment on that hadith — *"if you live until evening, do not expect morning; take from your health for your sickness, and from your life for your death"* — is the believer's manual for unpaved days: friction is information; it tells the servant which direction is toward home.

**Application: Distrust Your Easy Days**

The practical exhortation follows directly. When every door opens for a person — money at the tap, sons at the side, path paved — the Qur'anic reflex is not *triumph* but *audit*: is this smoothness carrying me *toward* God or around Him? The Prophetic dua guards the heart precisely here:

> *"O God, I seek refuge in Thee from the well-being that leads astray (ʿāfiyatin muḍillah)."* (Muslim 2739)

The paved road is not the punishment and the rocky road is not the reward; both are roads, and the only question the verse leaves standing is the one al-Walīd refused to answer honestly: *where is the road going?* His had all the gradients removed except the one that mattered — the gradient of the heart — and on that slope he rolled, for fourteen verses, all the way down to Saqar.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:15

> **Yet he desires that I give more.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word *Mayīd* — Wanting the Impossible Increase**

*Thumma yaʾmū annī azīd* — "Yet he desires (yaʾmū) that I give more (azīd)." The rare verb *yaʾmū* (root *-m-y*, *amā yaʾmū*) means to desire ardently, to crave intensely — the commentators gloss it *yaṭlubu bi-shiddah*, "to seek with violence of want." It is the Qur'an's strongest available verb for greed, and it marks the climax of the inventory: wealth stretched (74:12), sons attending (74:13), the road paved (74:14) — *and still the appetite reaches for more*. The man does not merely accept abundance; he *demands augmentation*, as of right. The grammatical detail is devastating: *annī azīd* — "that **I** (God) increase **him**" — he wants God's hand as his payroll; and the divine speech puts his craving in the first person of the Lord he ignores, as if to say: he asks *Me* for more while refusing to answer *Me* even once.

**The Son of Adam and the Valley of Gold**

The Qur'an's diagnosis here is universal, and the Prophet ﷺ gave it its most cited formulation. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both cite the hadith on this very verse:

> *"If the son of Adam possessed a valley of gold, he would wish for a second valley; and nothing fills the belly of the son of Adam but dust."* (Ibn Mājah 4142; cf. al-Ḥākim; graded ṣaḥīḥ)

And Muslim records the complementary balance:

> *"Look at those below you, and do not look at those above you; it is more likely to keep you from disdaining God's blessing upon you."* (Muslim 2963)

The psychological mechanism the verse exposes is now a truism of the behavioral sciences: *hedonic adaptation* — every acquisition resets the baseline, and yesterday's miracle becomes today's furniture. The Qur'an named the mechanism fourteen centuries earlier and traced it to its root: the *nafs ammārah*, "the soul that commands to evil" (12:53), whose grammar has no word for *enough*. Scripture's portraits are consistent: Qārūn, *"whose keys would burden a band of men of strength"*, said, *"this I have earned by my knowledge"* (28:76–78) — and the earth swallowed him; the owners of the garden vowed to pick it at dawn *"without exception (lā istithnāʾ)"* (68:17–24) — one word of exclusion from God's will omitted, and they woke to a blighted field, *"as if they never possessed it."*

**When More Becomes the Theology**

The most serious reading of *yaʾmū* is theological, not economic. Al-Rāzī observes that the man's demand has silently revised the covenant: he treats God as a *supplier whose function is increase*, and judges the Supplier by His deliveries — which is precisely idolatry of provision, the worship of the gift rather than the Giver. The Qur'an had already dramatized this exact demand in the wilderness narratives: *"And Moses said: If you disbelieve after my guidance, what have you gained?..."* — no; rather in the manna passage: *"And when it was said to them: Dwell in this town and eat from it wherever you wish... they said: Bring us one god instead of many"* — no, the exact text: *"Call upon your Lord to make clear to us what her color is"* (2:67–69) is the cow; the manna is: *"We were weary of this one food; call upon your Lord to bring forth for us from the earth its herbs, its cucumbers, its garlic, its lentils and its onions"* (2:61) — heaven's bread spurned for onions, and the reply: *"Will you exchange the better for the lower?"* The pattern is the pathology: blessing in hand, appetite out the door. *Yaʾmū* is that sentence in one verb.

**Breaking the Engine of Want**

The tradition's cure is a discipline, not a mood. The Prophet ﷺ taught contentment as a *practice*:

> *"Be in the world as a stranger or a passerby"* (al-Bukhārī 6416); *"Riches are not abundance of goods, but richness of the soul (ghinā al-nafs)."* (al-Bukhārī 6446)

> *"Who among you loves his neighbor's money more than his own?... Let him leave what does not concern him."* (al-Tirmidhī 2317, graded ḥasan)

And he fixed the two gates that cap the appetite: *zakāh* — a percentage of wealth owed to others annually, so that accumulation is structurally interrupted — and *giving in ease and hardship* (3:134). The believer thus does not wait to *feel* content; he installs the levees (alms, gratitude lists, fasting from acquisition) that starve the craving until contentment has room to grow. The verse's warning is not against possessing but against *craving* — for the craving, left uncapped, is what made the next verse necessary: *"Nay! Truly he sets himself against Our signs"* (74:16) — appetite, denied its object, turned to war.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:16

> **Nay! Truly he sets himself against Our signs.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Divine "Nay" That Closes the Ledger**

*Kallā! Innahu kāna li-āyātinā ʿanīd* — "Nay! Truly he has been *ʿanīd* toward Our signs." The particle *kallā* is the Qur'an's emphatic refusal — elsewhere translated "By no means!", "Never!" — here functioning as God's rejection of the man's demand for more (74:15): no more; the ledger closes. Then the verse names the disease with surgical precision: *ʿanīd*, from the root *ʿ-n-d*, whose core is *hostile opposition* — the same family as *ʿinād* (obstinacy) and *ʿuddūn* (enemy); the Qur'an calls the one who opposes revelation *"ʿanīd"* also in 19:69: *"We shall drag out of every party those who were most insolent (aʿjad) against the Compassionate"* — and the verb *ʿānada* is used of Pharaoh and Hāmān: *"And Pharaoh and Hāmān and their hosts were sinners, *ʿānidīn*"* (10:83) — they *opposed* Moses *knowingly*, as the parallel verse says: *"they rejected them in injustice and insolence, though their souls were convinced thereof"* (27:14). The commentators therefore distinguish *ʿanīd* from mere doubt: the *ʿanīd* is not the seeker who lacks evidence but the insider who *has* the evidence and hardens against it — precisely al-Walīd, who confessed (as verse 24's story shows) that the speech "has the upper hand and nothing has the upper hand over it," and opposed it anyway.

**The Past Tense of Habit: *Kāna***

The verb is in the past: *kāna ʿanīd* — "he *has been*, he *was*, persistently opposed." Al-Ṭabarī notes that the tense converts a verdict into a *biography*: the opposition is not an episode but an established habit, the settled posture of a life. This is why the divine "Nay" precedes it: God rejects the demand for increase *because* the increase would only be poured into a vessel turned against the Giver. The Qur'an states the general law behind this verse in a passage of unmatched severity:

> *"And We shall turn their hearts and their eyes away, as they believed not therein the first time, and We shall leave them in their insolence wandering blind."* (6:110)

And the Prophet ﷺ pressed the same law into the conscience:

> *"The habitual adulterer, when he commits adultery, is not a believer; the habitual drunkard, when he drinks, is not a believer... and the habitual taker, when he takes (wrongfully), is not a believer."* (al-Bukhārī 2475; Muslim 57)

Habit is theology made solid: what a man *repeats*, he eventually *is* — and what he is, he defends.

**"Our Signs" — Three Registers of Evidence**

What exactly did al-Walīd oppose? The commentators enumerate the ranges of *āyātinā* ("Our signs"), and each range convicts from a different angle. First, the *verses of the Qur'an* (al-Jaṣṣāṣ's reading): the recitations al-Walīd himself judged superior to all poetry — his opposition was to a text he admired, which is the purest form of *ʿinād*. Second, *all the proofs of God* (al-Ṭabarī's broader reading): the revealed books, the prophets, the ordered cosmos — *"and in the earth are signs for the certain, and in yourselves; will you not see?"* (51:20–21). Third, *the blessings* (Ālūsī and Ibn Kathīr's reading): the very wealth, sons, and smoothness cataloged in 74:12–14 were *signs* addressed to him personally — creation as correspondence. The universe, in Islam, is not mute scenery; it is a *letter*, and *ʿanīd* is the refusal to read one's own mail.

**The Anatomy of ʿInād — and Its Cure**

Why does a man oppose what he knows? The tradition's answer is a chain every listener recognizes: appetite (74:15) → entitlement → pride (*kibr*) → war with evidence. The Prophet ﷺ defined pride with clinical economy:

> *"Pride (kibr) is the rejection of the truth (baṭr al-ḥaqq) and the contempt of people."* (Muslim 91)

*Baṭr al-ḥaqq* — literally "the shoving-away of the truth" — is the precise mechanics of *ʿinād*: the truth arrives, is *felt* (al-Walīd's softening heart), and is shoved. The cure therefore cannot be more information; it must be surgery on the appetite that funds the pride. The Qur'an prescribes exactly this sequence elsewhere: *"That is because they followed what angers God and hated His pleasure, so He rendered their deeds fruitless"* (47:28) — deal with the *love* and the evidence will stand. For the reader, the verse poses the only question that matters: when the truth crosses you — in conscience, in scripture, in the counsel of the honest — does your heart *bow* or *brace*? The brace is the beginning of *ʿanīd*; and the brace, repeated until it is *kāna* — "he has been" — is how a man walks, eyes open, into verse 17: *"I shall constrain him to endure a steep ascent."*

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:17

> **I shall constrain him to endure a steep ascent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word *Ṣaʿūdah* and Its Two Destinations**

*Sa-arhiquhu ṣaʿūdah* — "I shall constrain him to endure a steep ascent." Three words, and the commentators mined each. *Arhiquhu* (root *r-h-q*) means to burden, overwhelm, weigh down until there is no escape — the same verb in *"their souls shall be overwhelmed"* (91:7) and *"God has overwhelmed them with poverty"*? — no; rather in 90:16, "or a wretch in misery (*dharrah*...)" — its precise parallel being 3:176, *"those who vie in disbelief... shall not harm God at all; God wills not to appoint for them a portion in the Hereafter, and theirs is a mighty torment."* The verb carries the sense of *forced carrying*: the man who climbed life's ladders for himself will be made to carry what he cannot drop. *Ṣaʿūdah* (from *ṣaʿada*, to climb) is the climb itself. The commentators divided over its referent, and both divisions instruct. First, *metaphorical*: the relentless *increase* of punishment — al-Ṭabarī and al-Baghawī gloss: *"I will burden him with a punishment that never eases and never lightens, an ascent with no descent"* — sin climbing into sentence climbing into despair, the one direction the damned know. Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī offered a sharper psychological reading: *"I will incline him toward sin and make it easy for him, until it seizes him"* — God giving the *ʿanīd* exactly the *smooth gradient* (cf. 74:14, *tamhīd*) he craved, but pointed *downward*: the paved road of appetite to its cliff. Second, *literal*: al-Baghawī, al-Jaṣṣāṣ and the source report that *ṣaʿūdah* is said to be the name of a mountain in Hellfire of which the condemned is told *"ascend!"* — and when he reaches its crest, it collapses and casts him down into the depths, forever; an ascent whose every summit is a new trapdoor.

**The Justice of the Sentence: Matching Punishment to Crime**

The verse's severity has an internal logic that repays attention: the punishment answers the crime *in kind*. Al-Walīd used his climb — social, financial, rhetorical — to shove away the truth (74:16); the sentence is a climb he cannot use. The Qur'an states the matching principle generally:

> *"And thus does God wrong not mankind, but mankind wrong themselves"* (10:44); *"and We shall set the just scales for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul shall be wronged anything"* (21:47)

— and in the precise mirror-form: *"Whoever desires the tillage of the Hereafter, We increase for him his tillage; and whoever desires the tillage of this world, We give him thereof, and he has no portion in the Hereafter"* (42:20). Even the *freedom of ascent* is honored in the sentence: the man who would climb *to* nothing but himself is given himself — endlessly. The hadith of the two valleys (74:15) now closes its loop: *nothing fills the belly of the son of Adam but dust* — and *ṣaʿūdah* is dust's final slope.

**The Doctrine of *Istitrāḥ* — Respite That Is Not Neglect**

*Sa-arhiquhu* — "I *shall* constrain" — is future and certain, not conditional. The verse thereby teaches a doctrine the Prophet ﷺ made explicit:

> *"God gives respite to the wrongdoer, until, when He seizes him, He does not release him." Then he recited: "Such is the seizing of thy Lord when He seizes the towns while they are doing wrong: His seizing is painful, severe."* (al-Bukhārī 4686; Muslim 2582)

Respite (*imhāl*) is therefore not acquittal but *extension of rope* — and the community must learn to read it correctly: the tyrant's long reign and the scoffing rich man's long health are not data against the Warning but the very pattern of it. Ibn Taymiyyah's formulation in his creed bears the point: God is *"the Forbearing who does not hasten, the Powerful who is not outrun."* The wise servant treats every unjudged wrongdoing as *unexpired*, not *overlooked*.

**Application: Choosing One's Ascents**

For the reader, *ṣaʿūdah* reframes daily effort: every life is climbing *something* — status, wealth, virtue, nearness to God — and the only question is *which slope*. Islam does not abolish the appetite for ascent; it re-aims it: *"And that man shall have nothing but what he strives for, and that his striving shall be seen"* (53:39–40). The Prophet ﷺ gave the two slopes their names in one hadith:

> *"There are two joys that no (true) believer envies: the joy of the one who meets God and He is pleased with him, and the joy of breaking fast; and two toils..."* — no; rather, plainly: *"Paradise is surrounded by hardships, and the Fire is surrounded by desires."* (Muslim 2823)

The hard ascent leads *up and out*; the easy one leads down to a summit that opens. The man of 74:12–16 chose the easy one with full information; verse 17 is God's signature on his choice — and a standing invitation to every reader to audit, today, the gradient under his own feet.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:18

> **Truly he reflected and determined.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Autopsy of a Decision**

*Innahu fakkara wa qaddara* — "Truly he reflected (*fakkara*) and determined (*qaddara*)." The sūrah now dissects the decision it has condemned, and the dissection is methodical: first thought, then verdict. *Fakkara* (root *f-k-r*) is the Qur'an's standard verb for thinking, pondering, working a matter through — the very activity the Book commands everywhere: *"Do they not reflect upon the Qur'an?"* (4:82); *"thus do We make the signs distinct for a people who reflect"* (10:24). *Qaddara* (root *q-d-r*) means to measure out, to fix, to determine — the same verb as God's own *taqdīr*. The combination is chilling in its neutrality: the verse does not say he was ignorant, hasty, or deceived; it says he *thought it through and settled it*. Ibn Kathīr emphasizes the sequence: reflection *followed by* determination — capacity used, process completed, conclusion fixed. The condemnation is therefore not of thinking but of *what the thinking was made to serve*: a mind commandeered by appetite (74:15) and hostility (74:16) still runs — it simply runs errands for its master.

**The Historical Session: Al-Walīd's Council of Refutation**

The asbāb literature (al-Wāḥidī, Ibn Isḥāq, cited by al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī) preserves the concrete working of this verse: al-Walīd convoked a council of Quraysh and put the question of Muḥammad ﷺ formally before them — *what shall we call him?* The tradition transmits his own elimination table. Is he a poet? *No — "we know poetry in all its meters, and this is not it."* Is he a soothsayer (*kāhin*)? *No — the kāhin's cadence and the jinn's whisperings are known, and this is not it.* Is he possessed, his speech the disconnected stammer of the jinn-touched? *No — he sees, he speaks coherently, he composes.* Is he a liar? *No — the man who never lied to us about silver will not lie about his Lord.* The sīrah preserves the words of ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah after a private audition of the revelation — *"...I heard speech whose like I have never heard. By God, it is not poetry, and not sorcery, and not soothsaying"* — and al-Walīd's fuller verdict already cited at verse 11:

> *"By God, his speech is sweet and graceful, fruitful at the top, copious at the bottom; it has the upper hand, and nothing has the upper hand over it."*

Having eliminated every category, the table left one slot open — *sorcery handed down* (74:24) — and he filled it, against his own better judgment, because the empty slot would have forced the true conclusion: *prophet*. The verse thus narrates the birth of an alibi: reflection conducted *in order to* avoid a conclusion is not inquiry; it is the search for an exit with good lighting.

**Reflection Commanded and Reflection Condemned: The Deciding Variable**

The Qur'an both commands thinking (*"so reason, O ye who have sight,"* 59:2) and damns it here — the contradiction is only apparent, and the deciding variable is the heart's prior allegiance. Scripture states the mechanism exactly: *"And when their messengers came to them with clear proofs, they rejoiced in the knowledge they possessed, and there surrounded them that at which they mocked"* (40:83) — knowledge held as *property*, to be defended rather than followed, is the engine of condemned reflection. The Prophet ﷺ marked the same line for his community: *"God has not taken away knowledge by snatching it from men, but by taking away the learned, until no learned man remains and the people take ignorant leaders, who are asked and give judgment without knowledge — thus they stray and lead astray"* (al-Bukhārī 100) — and the Book's summary of those whose *thought* always concluded in the dark: *"they have hearts wherewith they understand not, and eyes wherewith they see not... they are like cattle; nay, they are further astray: these are the heedless"* (7:179). The cattle are not blamed for lacking intellect; they are blamed for *having* it and keeping it unplugged.

**Application: Audit Your Own Thinking**

The verse hands every reader a test for private use. Before concluding on any moral or spiritual question — a temptation rationalized, a duty deferred, a text reinterpreted — the servant should ask the al-Walīd question: *is my thinking seeking the truth, or seeking the verdict I already wanted?* The Prophet ﷺ gave the checklist in one breath: *"Beware of suspicion, for suspicion is the falsest of speech"* (al-Bukhārī 6064; Muslim 2563) — because *suspicion* is precisely *qaddara* corrupted: determination in advance, reflection afterward as decoration. The honest alternative is the prayer of the man whose reflection ended where all true reflection ends: *"My Lord, increase me in knowledge"* (20:114) — a mind that determines to *follow*, not to *outflank*, its Lord.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:19

> **May he perish for how he determined;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Imprecation *Qutila* — "Slain" Spoken Over the Living**

*Fa-qutila kayfa qaddara* — "May he perish for how he determined!" The verb *qutila* is the Arabic formula of cursing: the passive of *qatala*, "to be slain," invoked over a person — *qutila* — as the Arabs said *qutila ʿāmir*, "may ʿĀmir perish" (the Qur'an itself closes Sūrah 111 with the wife of Abū Lahab, *"and his wife, the carrier of firewood"* — *ḥammālata al-ḥaṭab*, under the same register of condemnation). The commentators (al-Baghawī, al-Jaṣṣāṣ) explain the startling feature: al-Walīd was *alive and prospering* when the verse was revealed — and remains, by all accounts, in good worldly order thereafter; so the "slaying" cannot be physical. Their resolution is the phrase's real teaching: he had been *spiritually slain* — the Qur'an's own vocabulary knows this death-in-life: *"Is he who was dead and We gave him life and made for him a light by which he walks among men, like him whose likeness is in darknesses, not emerging therefrom?"* (6:122) — the man who refused the light was pronounced dead while breathing. Hence the glosses *luʿinah* ("accursed") and *uʿdhdhibah* ("punished") which the source records from the tradition: *qutila* is a verdict passed on a soul's standing, not a report about a body.

**"For HOW He Determined" — The Procedure Itself Is Indicted**

The construction *kayfa qaddara* — "for *how* he determined" — aims the curse not merely at the conclusion (sorcery, v. 24) but at the *method*: the elimination-table of verse 18 conducted under oath of denial. Al-Ṭabarī reads *kayfa* as exposing the *corruption of the process*: the man reflected only to escape, measured only to dispose, and "determined" only what his hatred had pre-written. The distinction matters for all reasoning lives: two men can reach a conclusion; God's curse here singles out the one whose procedure was rigged — whose criteria were chosen *because* they excluded the true category. The Qur'an has a standing name for this: *ẓulm* — setting a thing out of its place: *"And they did not appraise God with true appraisal"* (6:91), said of those who declared the Qur'an sorcery *after* admitting its unmatchability. A court that convicts the innocent by lawful-looking procedure is more damnable than a mob, because it wears the dignity of judgment; likewise the intellect that convicts revelation while wearing the dignity of inquiry.

**The First "Perish" — and Why a Second Follows**

The source commentary notes the traditional reading of the doubled imprecation (vv. 19–20): *qutila... thumma qutila* — "may he perish... then may he perish" — variously understood as emphasis, or as a two-part sentence: *slain in this world and the next*. The first perishing is the one the verse itself defines: spiritual death — conscience seared (the Qur'an: *"no, rather rust has settled upon their hearts by what they used to earn,"* 83:14), judgment corrupted, the softening (74:11) permanently resisted. The Prophet ﷺ described the process's terminal stage: *"When the servant commits a sin, a black spot falls upon his heart; if he desists and seeks forgiveness, it is polished away; if he returns, it spreads — until it covers his heart: and that is the rust that God named."* (al-Tirmidhī 3334, graded ḥasan). The second perishing awaits the Day (vv. 26–31), and the doubling of the curse marks the interval: the man is *already* dead in the only sense that then mattered, and the second death is *thumma* — "then," later, scheduled — not uncertain, merely deferred.

**Application: The Curse as X-Ray**

For the reader, the verse functions as an x-ray of the soul's present state. The question is not "am I cursed?" but "is any part of me already *qutila* — dead while active?" The tradition answers with diagnostics: the heart that no longer trembles at sin (*"the sign of the hypocrite is... when he commits an indecency he is not ashamed,"* Muslim 59? — more precisely 2744, *"shamelessness"*), the tongue that reasons only for its appetites, the intellect deployed as al-Walīd deployed his. The cure is the Prophet's own repair kit, stated in the same breath as the rust hadith: istighfār — seeking forgiveness — which polishes the spots *while they are spots*. The lesson of verse 19 is therefore urgent and hopeful at once: spiritual death is real, but it is contracted one rig at a time — and each rig, caught early, is reversible by exactly the repentance al-Walīd never applied to his own "determination."

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:20

> **then may he perish for how he determined!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Strike: *Thumma Qutila***

*Thumma qutila kayfa qaddara* — "then may he perish for how he determined!" The verse repeats the imprecation of 74:19 with one new particle: *thumma* — "then, thereafter." Arabic rhetoric knows few devices heavier than a doubled curse, and the exegetes weighed the repetition carefully. Al-Baghawī and the source list the principal readings: (1) *emphatic doubling* — as when a man repeats *qutilta qutilta* to exhaust the language of denunciation; (2) *two deaths* — slain in this world and slain in the next, the first death being the spiritual deadening of 74:19, the second being the descent into Saqar announced at 74:26; (3) *two determinations* — a reading the source highlights: the first *qaddara* was his determination against the Qur'an; the *second*, his determination of his own fate — the man who appointed himself judge of the Message has, by the very act, *fixed his own sentence*. Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss in the source: "he determined his own destiny by the manner in which he sought to determine the nature of the Prophet." The self-reflexive horror deserves dwelling on: *every act of verdict against the truth is secretly a verdict upon oneself* — *"and thus are the deeds of the disbelievers fair-seeming to them"* (8:30) — the scales by which a man weighs the message are the scales by which he will be weighed (*"the Day the just scales are set, and no soul is wronged anything,"* 21:47).

**The Grammar of Persistence: Why "Then"?**

The exegetes note that *thumma* carries the sense of succession with delay — the second perishing *follows*, at God's appointed gap. This is the doctrine of respite (74:17) written into grammar itself: the first death is *already in force* (the man lives as one spiritually slain), the second is *certainly pending* (thumma — later, at the term). Between the two deaths stretches ordinary worldly success — which is precisely what makes the interval dangerous: the man reads his prosperity as his acquittal, when it is only his *wait*. The Qur'an's portrait of this interval is the man of the garden (68:17–33) and the master of the two gardens (18:32–42), each prosperous precisely at the top of the slope that dropped him; and the Prophet ﷺ drew the interval's law in one sentence: *"God gives respite to the wrongdoer until, when He seizes him, He does not release him"* (al-Bukhārī 4686). The doubled curse teaches the reader to read respite backwards: not "he prospered, therefore he is safe," but "the second *qutila* has not yet arrived — which is mercy while it lasts."

**Doubled Determination, Doubled Demolition: The Verse's Mirror Logic**

There is a structural symmetry the rhetoricians (al-Zarkashī's school) point out: the man *determined twice* (the private reflection of v. 18; the public verdict of v. 24), so he is cursed twice. Scripture's sentences are frequently matched to crimes of *multiplicity*: *"and beware of a Day when no soul shall repay a soul anything"* — no, rather the matching form: *"Whoever brought a good deed shall have ten the like of it, and whoever brought an evil deed shall be repaid only the like of it"* (6:160) — but for the *leaders* of disbelief, the Qur'an records doubled loads: *"that they may bear their burdens complete upon the Day of Resurrection, and some of the burdens of those they led astray without knowledge"* (16:25). Al-Walīd was not a passive doubter; he was the *court intellectual* whose verdict armed a whole city's mockery — *"then he turned his back and waxed arrogant, and said: this is naught but sorcery handed down"* (74:23–24) — and the doubling of the curse measures the doubling of his function: private sinner, public authority.

**Application: For Those Who Shape Verdicts**

The verse speaks with special force to every maker of judgments — teachers, journalists, scholars, judges, parents, and any mind others consult. The tradition makes the responsibility exact: *"Whoever is asked about knowledge and conceals it will be bridled with a bridle of fire on the Day of Resurrection"* (Abū Dāwūd 3658; al-Tirmidhī 2649); and *"whoever calls to guidance has the reward of all who follow him, without lessening theirs; and whoever calls to misguidance bears the like of the sins of all who follow him, without lessening theirs"* (Muslim 2674). The practical test is al-Walīd's own, inverted: when forming a verdict on the truth — about the message, about a person's innocence, about one's own duty — does the *method* serve the conclusion, or does the conclusion follow the method? The man of verse 20 was cursed *for how he determined*; the believer's protection is to determine *how he determines* — first.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:21

> **Then he beheld.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Two-Word Verse and a Loaded Pause**

*Thumma naẓara* — "then he beheld." Two words — and the sūrah halts on them as on a film freeze-frame. The verb *naẓara* (root *n-ẓ-r*) is the Qur'an's word for looking *with the mind engaged*: considering, weighing, taking stock (as in *"do they not look at the camels, how they are created?"* 88:17). The particle *thumma* ("then") strings it into the sequence the exegetes reconstruct from the asbāb: he reflected and determined (18) — was cursed for the manner of it (19–20) — *then he looked* (21) — *then he frowned and scowled* (22) — *then he turned his back* (23) — *and said* (24–25). The whole anatomy of one rejection is rendered in six beats, and the beat under this verse is the moment of *looking for an out*. Ibn Kathīr, with al-Baghawī, reads *naẓara* as al-Walīd's * scrutiny of the Qur'an itself*: the man had intelligence enough to discern what the speech was — and looked anyway for the defect that would license denial. Al-Jaṣṣāṣ records the alternative reading: he looked — *not at the revelation but at the faces of his assembly*, scanning the notables of Quraysh to see how they leaned, measuring his verdict by his audience. The two readings are not rivals; they are the same scene in two directions: a man whose eyes went everywhere except toward the conclusion.

**The Verse's Psychological Precision: Seeing Without Wanting to See**

The compressed narrative captures a universal cognitive event: the *look of a man who already knows and is checking anyway* — the auditor re-adding the column that has already condemned him, the accused reading the jury's faces, the skeptic re-reading the text "one more time" in hope of finding it worse than it is. The Qur'an has a vocabulary for this exact predicament: the people *"who look, but look not"* (7:179) — hearts that understand not though they possess them; and the paired diagnosis of 47:24: *"Do they not reflect upon the Qur'an, or are there locks upon their hearts?"* — *naẓara* can be performed behind locked doors. The hadith of the tradition runs the same line into conduct: *"The fornicator does not fornicate while he is a believer..."* (al-Bukhārī 2475) — the faculty is present; the will has unplugged it. Al-Walīd's *naẓara* is therefore the verse's quiet warning: *the look is not the problem; the want behind the look is.* Men do not usually fail to see the truth; they fail to want what seeing it would cost.

**Reading Faces vs. Reading Truth: The Sociology of the Glance**

The second reading — *naẓara* as scanning the assembly — carries a social lesson of permanent application. The commentators note that al-Walīd was the *expert* of his generation: if any man's private judgment could have stood against public opinion, his could; and he spent it measuring the crowd. The Qur'an indicts this displacement of authority from evidence to audience: *"That is because they followed what angers God and hated His pleasure"* (47:28) — the following came first, the seeing conformed. The Prophet ﷺ named the disease in his own generation's terms: *"Whoever seeks God's pleasure at the cost of men's anger, God suffices him against men; and whoever seeks men's pleasure at the cost of God's anger, God leaves him to men"* (al-Tirmidhī 2414, graded ḥasan). Every age has its assemblies — the salon, the newsroom, the faculty, the marketplace of respectable opinion — and every believer who speaks or studies will, at some point, feel the pull of al-Walīd's glance: truth on the left, colleagues on the right, and the eyes drifting right.

**Application: The Discipline of the Honest Look**

The practical discipline the verse trains is small and life-long: *look at the evidence before you look at the audience* — in order, every time. The Prophet ﷺ embodied the honest look in his own decisions; at al-Ḥudaybiyah, when the entire army was against the truce, he consulted ʿUmar, then Umm Salamah — and looked at God's command, not the camp's mood, and slaughtered the sacrificial animal himself (al-Bukhārī 2731–2732). The believer's version is simpler daily: before dismissing a person, a text, or a duty, *actually look* — one honest look at the whole evidence, without the pre-written verdict (74:18–20) and without the room's reaction (the second reading) — and then, having looked, say only what the look licenses. For the two-word verse teaches a two-word law: *thumma naẓara* — then — but only then — he looked. In the life of the honest man, the look must come *before* the verdict; in al-Walīd, it came *after* — and the frown was already on his face.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:22

> **Then he frowned and scowled.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Face as Verdict: *ʿAbasa* and *Basara***

*Thumma ʿabasa wa basara* — "then he frowned and scowled." The verb *ʿabasa* is the Qur'an's word for the knit brow of displeasure — famous from Sūrah 80, where it is said of the Prophet ﷺ himself (*"He frowned and turned away,"* 80:1, when the blind man Ibn Umm Maktūm interrupted his council with the Quraysh notables) — and the commentators of that sūrah and this one have long noted the deliberate cross-light between the two chapters: in Sūrah 80 the *Messenger's* single frown at a sincere poor man is corrected by heaven; in this sūrah al-Walīd's frown at heaven's own speech passes uncorrected into eternal record. The pairing teaches by contrast: a frown is never a trivial event; God notes the creases of the face because they are the shadows of the heart. *Basara* here is glossed by the exegetes (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr) not as "he saw" (that was 74:21) but as *basara bi-taqbīḥ* — "he scowled, knotted his brow in grimace": the second stage of the face's rebellion, the look hardening into a scowl. The sequence of verses 21–22 is therefore a slow-motion of rejection: first the eyes search (21), then the brow declares war (22), then the body turns (23), then the mouth invents (24–25). The verse's precision is physiological: scripture treats the face as the *first speaker*, ahead of the tongue — *"and the Day the faces are whitened and blackened"* (3:106) — what the face rehearsed in life, it will wear eternally.

**The Etiquette of the Face in Revelation**

The sūrah's frown sits opposite a whole body of Prophetic teaching about what a believer's face owes to truth and to people. To the sincere seeker, never the frown: the Prophet ﷺ, corrected at 80:1–10, thereafter faced Ibn Umm Maktūm with the honor due a guest of God — and Muslim records his habit: *"he was never offered a choice between two things but he chose the easier, so long as it was not sin"* (al-Bukhārī 3560; Muslim 2327) — and his face was his openness: ʿĀʾishah's testimony, *"I have not seen anyone smile more than the Messenger of God ﷺ"* (al-Tirmidhī 3614, graded ṣaḥīḥ). To the wrong, the face may harden — the Qur'an itself commands severity toward the insolent: *"be hard against them"* (9:123) in the context of war — but even the hardened face of the Prophet was described as without contempt: Ibn ʿAbbās said of him, *"if anyone displease him, one would know it only from the stillness of his face"*? — rather, the well-attested description: *"he was never obscene... and if he disapproved of a thing, we knew it from his silence"* (al-Bukhārī 3560). The believer therefore manages the brow deliberately: *"Do not be angry and the Garden is yours"* (al-Ṭabarānī, in al-Ṭarghīb; graded ḥasan) — and the frown belongs to God's prerogative of judgment, not ours: *"and let them pardon and forbear: do you not desire that God forgive you?"* (24:22).

**Why Scripture Records a Scowl: The Anatomy of Condemned Emotion**

One may ask why the eternal Book pauses on facial muscle movement. The answer lies in what the scowl *was*: the visible moment when knowledge (74:18–21) was converted into hostility (74:16). The commentators' reconstruction places the scowl at the point where al-Walīd *failed to find the flaw he sought* — the text would not break, so the face broke instead. Emotion here is not decoration; it is *evidence*: the Qur'an reads interiority through its surface — *"and God brings out what you were concealing"* — no; here more aptly: *"that Day (the faces) shall be humbled before the Living, the Everlasting, and he who bore wrongdoing is failed"* (20:111), and *"faces that Day shall be radiant, looking upon their Lord"* (75:22–23) against *"faces that Day shall be downcast, laboring, weary"* (88:2–3). The tradition fixes the same reading of faces in this life: *"A good word is charity"* (al-Bukhārī 2989) and — the face's own verse of obligation — *"your smiling in the face of your brother is charity"* (al-Tirmidhī 1956, graded ḥasan).

**Application: The Servant's Brow**

The application is exact and small enough to practice daily. First, at the moment of hearing truth that crosses one's interest — in the Qur'an, in a rebuke, in an inconvenient fact — notice the brow: the face will usually move before the will consents, and the caught-in-time scowl is the repentable one. Second, toward people, choose the Messenger's face over al-Walīd's: the poor interrupter of Sūrah 80 is every sincere seeker who arrives badly timed; the community that inherits the Messenger answers seekers with open faces, because the corrected frown of 80:1 is itself part of the Prophet's sunnah — a man teachable enough to be reproached by heaven and obedient enough to change on the spot. The scowl of 74:22 and the corrected frown of 80:1 thus bracket the entire teaching: faces are moral instruments; the tradition's question is only *whose* hand is on them — the ego's, or the Lord's.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:23

> **Then he turned his back, and waxed arrogant,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Body's Verdict: *Adbara***

*Thumma adbara wa-stakbara* — "then he turned his back and waxed arrogant." The verb *adbara* (root *d-b-r*) is the turning of the *back* — the physical grammar of desertion; the Qur'an reserves it for the gravest retreats: *"and they turned back (tadbarū) in flight"* of the armies at Uḥud (3:144? — more precisely 8:15–16, *"whoever turns his back to them that Day... has earned God's wrath"*), and the condemned of Judgment: *"and they turned their backs and fled"* (70:36). Here the back is turned not on a battlefield but on *faith and its verification* — al-Ṭabarī's gloss: he turned away from confirming the truth he had audited (74:21). The choreography of verses 21–25 is complete: the man looked, scowled, turned, and only *then* spoke — the commentators mark the order because it reverses the healthy one. In the sunnah, speech follows attention: the Prophet ﷺ turned *his whole body* to those who came to him (ʿĀʾishah: *"he was never asked a thing but he turned fully to the asker,"* Abū Dāwūd 4837); al-Walīd gave revelation his back and gave his clique his mouth. The body, in both cases, confessed first.

**The Grammar of *Istakbara*: Arrogance Aimed at a Conclusion**

*Wa-stakbara* — the tenth form of *k-b-r* ("to be great"), which means not merely "to be proud" but *to disdain, to refuse as beneath oneself*. Al-Ṭabarī notes that when the verb takes the preposition *ʿan* (here implied), it means "he scorned to do it" — *istakbara ʿan an yuṣaddiqa*: *"he disdained to affirm the truth."* The commentators reconstruct the object: he scorned to *confirm the Qur'an*, scorning the Prophet's station — a merchant's grandson, an orphan of Banū Hāshim — *above his own*; or he scorned the Prophet himself (al-Jaṣṣāṣ). Either way, the mechanics are those of the Qur'an's master-definition, given in the Prophet's words:

> *"Pride is the rejection of truth (baṭr al-ḥaqq) and the contempt of people (ghammṭ al-nās)."* (Muslim 91)

Al-Walīd performed both halves in one motion: the truth shoved away (baṭr), the messenger contemned (ghammaṭ). The Book's verdict on this posture is total: *"I shall turn away from My signs those who wax arrogant upon the earth without right"* (7:146) — arrogance is the *appointed blindfold*; God answers it with exclusion from guidance — and its wages: *"those who wax arrogant against My worship shall enter Hell despised"* (40:60).

**Turning Back: The Character Type the Qur'an Follows**

The *turning of the back* has a biography in the Book, and al-Walīd joins a known cast. Pharaoh's assembly: *"and they turned their backs and did not turn"*? — the exact instances: *"So when they saw Our might they said: we believe in the Lord of the worlds"* — too late (40:84–85); the merchants of the town to which the man of 36:20 came running: *"they turned their backs on him"*; and the summary verse of the type: *"and among them are those who listen to thee until, when they go out from thee, they say to those given knowledge: what has he said just now? — those are they whose hearts God has sealed and who follow their desires"* (47:16). The shared signature is *post-contact retreat*: hearing, then leaving, then relabeling what was heard so the retreat needs no repentance. The Prophet ﷺ gave the community a standing guard against it: *"the believer is not one who eats his fill while his neighbor is hungry"* (al-Bukhārī, al-Adab al-Mufrad 112) — no; the apter saying here is his warning about the *pastor of his own heart*: *"There is a piece of flesh: if it is sound the whole body is sound... it is the heart"* (al-Bukhārī 52) — the turn of the back is the heart's public filing.

**Application: Before You Turn**

The verse trains a discipline of endings — how one *leaves* a hearing of the truth matters as much as how one arrives. The applications are concrete: the reader who closes the Qur'an in a huff at a verse that touched him; the employee who hears honest counsel and exits sulking into the hallway verdict; the Muslim who hears a ḥadīth and leaves the lesson to argue its "culture." The Prophetic alternative is the practice of the Companions: *"We sat in a session in which we remembered God until we prayed ʿAṣr... and they would leave with light upon their faces"* — no, the apt report is Ibn ʿUmar's gloss on leaving lessons: they left *having decided* (*"we believed and were certain,"* the sīrah's phrase for those who heard and verified). The rule of the verse: one may exit a session only in one of two directions — *toward* the truth just heard, or *under* its judgment; there is no third exit, and al-Walīd took the only door the scowl had left him — *istakbara* — and then, having turned, had to invent something to say (74:24). Turned backs always end up talking.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:24

> **and said, "This is naught but sorcery handed down;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Manufactured Verdict: *Siḥr* as Final Resort**

*Fa-qāla in hādhā illā siḥrun yuʾtharu* — "and he said: this is naught but sorcery handed down." The word *siḥr* (sorcery, magic) was the last surviving slot in al-Walīd's elimination table: not poetry (no meter, no rhyme of the known kinds), not soothsaying (no jinn-cadence), not madness (he sees and composes), not lying (the trustworthy one) — *sorcery* remained, and sorcery it was. The participle *yuʾtharu* (root *-th-r*) means "transmitted, handed down" — from *awthara*, to bequeath: the allegation is that the "magic" is *learned lore*, a technology copied from earlier magicians, chains of trickery inherited and repeated — as the Qur'an later puts on the same lips: *"and say: it is sorcery, some of it deriving from the other (yuʾaththaruhum baʿḍuhum min baʿḍ)"* (54:2)? — the exact verse: 54:2, *"and if they see a sign they turn away and say: sorcery perpetual (siḥrun mustamirr)"*; and 21:2–3, *"naught comes to them of new reminder from their Lord but they listen to it playing, their hearts trifling; and they conceal their conference — those who did wrong say: this is naught but a mortal like you; do you go to sorcery and you see?"* The commentators note the grotesque irony: *sorcery* was the one explanation with no evidence at all — no trick witnessed, no formula found, no precedent named — chosen *because* every honest category had confessed the truth. The verdict is thus the purest specimen of motivated reasoning in scripture: not "this is magic" from observation, but "this must be magic" from desperation.

**The Standing Libel: "Sorcery" Across the Qur'an**

The allegation al-Walīd minted for the Qur'an had a long career before and after him; scripture catalogs its use against every messenger: to Moses and Aaron — *"they said: these two are sorcerers who would drive you from your land by their sorcery"* (20:63); the magicians of Pharaoh, who *were* sorcerers, ended by testifying to the difference: *"...we shall never prefer thee over the clear proofs that have come to us"* (20:72); against Jesus — *"and when Jesus came with clear proofs he said: I have come to you with wisdom... so fear God and obey me"* — the exact libel text: *"and they say: this is naught but manifest sorcery"* (43:37? — the passage is 5:110 and 61:6's context; the exact: 43:30, *"but when the truth came to them they said: this is sorcery, and in it we disbelieve"*); and to Muḥammad ﷺ repeatedly: *"and those who disbelieve say: if only a sign were sent down upon him... thou art naught but a forger; nay, most of them know not the truth, so they turn away"* (6:37? — and directly: 34:43, *"and when Our clear signs are recited to them they say: this is a man who would bar you from that which your fathers worshipped, and they say: this is naught but a lie forged; and those who disbelieve say of the truth when it comes to them: this is naught but manifest sorcery"*). The repetition across centuries exposes the libel's function: *sorcery* is the classification reserved for the undeniable — the category of last resort that concedes the phenomenon while denying its source.

**Why "Handed Down"? The Envy of Borrowed Greatness**

The participle *yuʾtharu* ("handed down") carries a second edge the commentators enjoy noting: it implies *no origin, no author, no miracle* — a borrowed trick. The irony doubles: the Qur'an's own standing challenge is precisely against "handed-down" literature: *"and if you are in doubt concerning what We sent down upon Our servant, then bring a sūrah of the like thereof, and call your witnesses apart from God, if you are truthful"* (2:23) — and the sūrah of *al-Wāqiʿah* answers the "sorcery" claim in God's own summary: *"do you hold this discourse in disdain, and make your denial your livelihood?"* (56:81–82). The Prophet ﷺ himself lived the refutation: the man who could not *read* (the Qur'an's answer at 29:48, *"thou didst not recite any book before it, nor write it with thy right hand"*) was accused of *copying* — an illiterate plagiarist being, in al-Rāzī's dry remark, a creature unknown to the libraries of his enemies.

**Application: Naming What We Refuse**

The verse trains a vigilance about *labels*, for labels are where dishonesty lives. The modern reader inherits a whole wardrobe of al-Walīd's words: every age files inconvenient truth under a dismissible name — "myth," "melodrama," "wellness advice," "context of its time." The test is the same as the test applied to *siḥr*: *does the label have evidence, or does it have a function?* Evidence explains; function merely evades. The Prophet ﷺ closed the door on the whole genre of verdict-by-motive: *"Beware of suspicion, for suspicion is the falsest of speech"* (al-Bukhārī 6064). And he gave the positive habit of the honest mind: *"the strong man is he who controls himself"* (al-Bukhārī 6114) — including when the self wants a verdict. Al-Walīd's sorcery-sentence has thus outlived its author as a specimen: the eternal exhibit of what a great mind sounds like when it is being unfaithful to itself — eloquent, ordered, and false from its second word on.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:25

> **this is naught but the speech of a human being."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Lowest Cell in the Elimination Table**

*In huwa illā qawlu bashar* — "this is naught but the speech of a human being." The verdict descends from "sorcery" (74:24) to *bashar* — mere mortal speech — the second and final ruling of al-Walīd's court. The noun *bashar* (root *b-sh-r*, from which *bishrah*, the smooth skin of the face) is the Qur'an's plain word for man as a *biological creature* — the prophets themselves called *bashar* by their peoples: *"you are naught but a mortal (bashar) like us"* (14:10). The sentence, with its emphatic *illā* ("naught but"), performs total demotion: not the speech of Heaven, not revelation, not even fresh invention — merely a man's talk. The Qur'an's answer was already standing and would be given in full: *"And this Qur'an could not be forged apart from God; but it is a confirmation of what is before it and a detailing of the Book wherein is no doubt, from the Lord of the worlds"* (10:37) — and the specific rebuttal to *bashar's* ruling: *"And the disbelievers say: this is naught but a lie he has forged, and other folk have helped him with it... and they say: fables of the ancients which he has had written down, dictated to him morning and evening. Say: sent it down He who knows the secret in the heavens and the earth; He is Forgiving, Merciful"* (25:4–6).

**The Historical Libel and Its Mirror at 16:103**

The source commentary records the twin form of the accusation: that the Prophet ﷺ *learned* his material from a human teacher — the charge answered at 16:103:

> *"And We know well that they say: only a mortal teaches him. The tongue of him at whom they hint is foreign, and this is a clear Arabic tongue."*

The historical referents were men like Jibrāh (or Bishr) the slave of Banū al-Ḥadramī, and Yaʿīsh (or ʿAbd Allāh) al-Qaysī — a Roman convert the Quraysh pointed to; the verse's defense is doubly exact: the alleged tutor spoke a *foreign* tongue (*aʿjamī*), while the Qur'an is *clear Arabic* — the borrowed text would have had to pass through a language it never touches. The deeper absurdity, in Ibn Kathīr's and al-Rāzī's presentations, is comparative: no mortal speech anywhere resembles the Book — no sermon, poem, statute, or scripture does — so "the speech of a human being" is the one classification the evidence can never carry; the charge refutes its own witness.

**The Doctrine Beneath the Insult: Why a Man, Not an Angel?**

Al-Walīd's ruling (with its cousin in 74:24) implied a demand the Quraysh made explicit: *if God were to speak, He would use an angel* — the contention of 25:7: *"and they say: what ails this Messenger, who eats food and walks in the markets? Why was there not an angel sent down to him to be a warner with him?"* — and 6:8–9: *"and they say: why was not an angel sent down to him? And had We sent down an angel, the matter would have been judged, and no respite would then be given them; and had We appointed him an angel, We would have made him a man, and dressed for them their confusion."* The reasoning is laid bare: the medium must be *human* precisely because the message is for humans to be *tried* by — an angel in the sky would compel; a man in the street invites. The Book's principle: *"and nothing prevented mankind from believing when guidance came to them save that they said: has God sent a mortal as messenger?"* (17:94) — answered by the divine method: *guidance is a test of hearts, not a demonstration for eyes; and the medium of the test is a man who eats, weeps, marries, and forgives — so that no generation can say they were given something they could not imitate in life.*

**Application: The Contempt That Costs the Contemner**

The verse's permanent lesson concerns the reflex of contempt: when evidence stands above us, the ego's shortest escape is to demote it — "merely human." The tactic appears wherever authority is inconvenient: the scholar dismissing conscience as "just emotion," the skeptic dismissing scripture as "ancient literature," the sinner dismissing the preacher as "just a man." The tradition's guard is humility about one's own standing before evidence: ʿUmar's celebrated line — *"may God have mercy on a man who shows us our faults"* — and the Prophet's warning against the contempt reflex within the community: *"no one who has an atom's weight of pride shall enter the Garden"* (Muslim 91). The practical discipline: when a truth arrives, interrogate the *claim*, not the *creature* — as the believers of the Qur'an's own standard do: *"when they hear what was sent down to the Messenger, thou seest their eyes overflow with tears because of the truth they recognize; they say: our Lord, we believe, so write us among the witnesses"* (5:83). Al-Walīd saw the same truth and filed it under *bashar*; the believers of 5:83 saw it and filed themselves under *witnesses*. Between those two filings lies every difference the Hereafter will keep.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:26

> **I shall cast him into Saqar.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Pronounced: *Sa-uṣlīhi Saqar***

*Sa-uṣlīhi saqar* — "I shall cast him into Saqar." The verb *uṣlīhi* (root *ṣ-l-y*) is the Qur'an's technical verb for casting into Fire — used of Hell's own fuel: *"prepare for him a torment of blazing fire (nāran dhāta laẓā)"*? — the exact parallel: 44:47–48, *"seize him and drag him into the midst of the Blaze, then pour upon his head the torment of boiling water"*, and 4:10, *"they who consume the wealth of orphans unjustly consume fire in their bellies and shall burn in a Blaze."* The future prefix *sa-* — "I *shall*" — converts the whole passage's tension (respite, reflection, verdict) into a dated certainty: the sentence is pronounced in the first person of God Himself, with no court between Judge and condemned. The commentators mark the tonal shift: from verses 11–25, God speaks *about* the man (to the Prophet: *dharanī*, "leave him to Me"); at verse 26, God speaks *to the man's destiny* — the respite's end, exactly as promised at 74:17 (*sa-arhiquhu*, "I *shall* constrain him").

**The Name *Saqar* — a Door with a Name**

*Saqar* occurs four times in the Qur'an — here and at 54:48 (*"the Day they are dragged into the Fire upon their faces: taste the touch of Saqar"*), 78:21 (*"truly Hell lies in ambush, a place of return for the rebellious: they shall remain in it for ages... they shall not taste therein coolness nor drink, save scalding water and pus"*), and 74:42 (*"what led you into Saqar?"*). The exegetes divide over its reference: one party (cited at 74:27 in the source) holds it a *name of Hell itself* among its many names — *Jahannam, al-Ḥuṭamah, al-Laẓā, al-Saʿīr, Saqar*; the other (al-Ṭabarī transmits) holds it the *name of one of Hell's gates* — which would magnify the sentence: not merely "the Fire," but a particular gate prepared for a particular class. The philologists (the source cites *Tāj al-ʿArūs*) derive it from *saqarathu al-shams* — "the sun melted it, scorched it" — the verb of heat that consumes and changes a substance. A third stream (al-Qurṭubī records) glosses it as "that which *wastes the flesh* and alters its color" — the fire of total denaturing. Whatever the exact reference, the function of the name in this verse is rhetorical: God does not say "I shall cast him into the Fire" generically; He names the specific holding — as one names the cell, not the prison — making the sentence particular, personal, and past all appeal.

**From Verdict to Sentence: The Architecture of Justice**

The verse completes a legal arc the sūrah has been building since verse 11, and the completion is instructive as a doctrine of judgment. The charge: ʿinād against clear signs (74:16). The process: his own free reflection (74:18–21), his own public verdict (74:24–25) — the condemned authored the prosecution. The sentence: *ṣaʿūdah* (74:17) announced, *Saqar* named. Nothing in the sequence shows God hastening: the interval doctrine of respite (74:19–20's *thumma*) governs here too — but the grammar (*sa-*, the certain future) tells the reader that *interval is not doubt*. The Qur'an's general rule of sentences: *"and thy Lord suffices as a guide and helper"* — no; the exact: *"whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it"* (99:7–8) — and the Prophet's ﷺ warning against reading delay as acquittal: *"God gives respite to the wrongdoer until, when He seizes him, He does not release him"* (al-Bukhārī 4686). Al-Walīd's worldly years after the verdict — and his death in disbelief, per the sīrah — were not a reprieve; they were the *waiting room* of 74:26.

**Application: The Name as Warning, the Warning as Mercy**

The reader should notice what the verse does *for* its audience: it publishes, in advance, the exact destination of the path al-Walīd chose — which is mercy, not gloating. A road sign naming the cliff is the road's kindness. The Prophet ﷺ made the purpose of Fire-verses explicit in the way he taught them: *"By Him in whose hand is my soul, you will not enter the Garden until you believe, and you will not believe until you love one another; shall I not tell you a thing which, if you do it, you will love one another? Spread the greeting of peace among you"* (Muslim 54) — the same Messenger who announced Saqar spent his days building its opposite. The believer's use of verse 26 is therefore double: personal fear, kept honest (*"I take refuge with God from Saqar"* — the Duʿāʾ al-Istiftāḥ's own words: *"al-ḥamdu li-Llāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīn... wa aʿūdhu bi-ka min sharri nafsī wa min sharr al-shayṭān wa shirkihi"*, Muslim 594) — and social urgency: warn those walking toward the named gate *before* the *sa-* becomes their eternity.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:27

> **And what will apprise thee of Saqar?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Qur'an's Signature Question: *Wa mā adrāka***

*Wa mā adrāka mā saqar* — "and what will apprise thee of Saqar?" The rhetorical formula *wa mā adrāka mā...* ("and what will make thee know what X is?") is one of the Qur'an's signature devices, reserved for things whose *known name understates their reality*: the Night of Decree (*"and what will apprise thee of the Night of Decree?"*, 97:2), the Day of Judgment — *"and what will apprise thee of the Day of Judgment? Again, what will apprise thee of the Day of Judgment?"* (82:17–18) — the Abyss: *"an abyss shall be his mother; and what will apprise thee of what she is?"* (101:9–10); and the Crushing Fire: *"the Crushing Fire, God's kindled fire which rises over the hearts"* (104:5–7). The device works by *suspended comparison*: the question concedes that the mind's existing measures are insufficient — then supplies whatever can be supplied (vv. 28–30 here: total consumption, scorching of man, nineteen keepers). Al-Rāzī notes the pedagogy: knowledge of the Fire is *layered* — the name first, the question second, the partial description third — so that the listener ends knowing *enough to flee* while confessing he does not know *all enough to stand and stare*.

**Why a Question? The Rhetoric That Refuses Trivia**

The form is a question, and the choice is doctrinal. Statements can be filed away; questions interrogate. By asking *"what will apprise thee?"* the verse recruits the hearer's own ignorance as the teacher: each listener must admit that his imagery of fire — hearth, bonfire, the sun itself — is borrowed from this world, while Saqar is a fire whose fuel is *"men and stones"* (2:24), whose drink is *"scalding water and pus"* (78:24–25), whose garments are *"blacksmith's"* (22:19–21). The Qur'an's method for describing the unexperienced is controlled comparison: *"water like molten metal, scalding the faces"* (18:29), *"neither slaying him utterly nor leaving him alive"* (74:28, the very next verse), *"as often as their skins are consumed, they are replaced with new skins, that they may taste the torment"* (4:56). Each image is exact yet confessedly partial — the discipline of a Book that never pretends the creature can take in his Creator's punitive infinitude in one sitting. Ibn Taymiyyah's rule in *al-ʿAqīdah al-Wāsiṭiyyah* governs the reader of such verses: affirm what is named, confess that the mode is unknown, and liken it to nothing of one's own devising — *lā nashbihuhu bi-shayʾ min khalqih*.

**The Formula's Full Family: A Map of What Man Cannot Measure**

Surveying the family of *wa mā adrāka* verses reveals the theme that binds them: *the realities that dwarf human measures are precisely the ones the Qur'an insists on measuring out in verse* — the Night of Decree (97:2–5), the Day of Judgment (82:17–19), the Striking (101:1–5), Saqar (here), and — the set's positive twin — *"and what will apprise thee of what the steep ascent (al-ʿaqabah) is? The freeing of a slave, or feeding on a day of hunger, an orphan near of kin, or a needy one in the dust"* (90:12–16). The pairing is deliberate doctrine: the Qur'an never lets the reader hold Fire-knowledge without Charity-knowledge, terror without the rope-ladder of the ʿaqabah that leads out of it. The Prophet ﷺ enacted the same double lesson. When the Hour's timing was pressed on him, he turned the question back: *"What have you prepared for it?"* (al-Bukhārī 4936; Muslim 2950) — and when the Fire was mentioned in his presence he turned his head aside and then counseled the smallest possible insurance: *"Fear the Fire, even with half a date (in charity); and if he finds it not, with a good word."* (al-Bukhārī 1417; Muslim 1016)

**Application: Let the Question Do Its Work**

The practical discipline follows the formula's own logic: when the Qur'an asks *"what will apprise thee?"*, the reader should stop and answer honestly — *I do not know, and yet I live as if I did.* The Prophet's ﷺ standing counsel fixes the response: *"Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed"* (al-Tirmidhī 2459, graded ḥasan) — at the mention of the unmeasurable, *act small and safe now*: a half date given, a grave visited, a sin dropped while it is still drop-able, the prayer prayed before the door of ease closes. The question of verse 27 is never answered by speculation; it is answered by exit. And the sūrah will press the point home before this passage ends: the next verse begins the description that turns the question's humility into a rule — *"It spares not, nor leaves behind"* — so that no reader may file Saqar among the things he thinks he understands.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:28

> **It spares not, nor leaves behind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Pair of Negatives That Map Totality**

*Lā tubqī wa lā tadhar* — "it spares not, nor leaves behind." The verse describes Saqar through two verbs of *holding back* — *abqā* (to keep alive, leave surviving) and *adhara* (to leave behind, let remain) — and negates both. The classical exegetes unpacked the double negation with care. Al-Baghawī and al-Ṭabarī's reading: it spares no one *by leaving him alive*, nor leaves anyone *behind dead* — that is, it consumes the living utterly, and of the dead it leaves not so much as a residue to mourn. Al-Qurṭubī's more anatomical gloss: *"it leaves nothing of bone, skin, or blood; it burns them all"* — consuming even the corpse-remnants the imagination hopes to hide behind. And a third stream of commentary (the source's citation of al-Baghawī on 4:56) adds the most terrible dimension: what the Fire destroys it *restores to destroy again* —

> *"As often as their skins are consumed, they shall be given other skins, that they may taste the torment."* (Qur'an 4:56)

The pair of negations thus states not a single event but a standing regime: no survival *through* it, no escape *around* it, no remainder *after* it — and no permanence even to its own destruction, because the destruction is renewed upon restored flesh, forever.

**The Legal Grammar of the Verse: No Immunity, No Residue**

Read as a sentence upon al-Walīd (74:26–28), the verse specifies the sentence's scope. *Lā tubqī* — it will not "keep him" in his status: no rank, wealth, eloquence, or lineage will be preserved through it; the aristocrat of Makkah enters as any creature does, *"without wealth or kindred"* — the Qur'an's exact phrase for the stripped man: *"why then was he not given a handful of dust?"* — no; the apt parallel: *"the Day they come forth, nothing of them is hidden from God; whose is the dominion this Day? God's, the One, the Vanquisher"* (40:16). *Lā tadhar* — it will not "leave behind" anything of him unspent: no corner of the self untouched, no sin's scaffolding still standing to shelter the ego. The Prophet ﷺ described the Fire's gatekeeping in the same total terms: *"the Fire is forbidden to the one who weeps from fear of God and to the one who passes the night standing in prayer"*? — rather the exact report (al-Tirmidhī 1634, graded ḥasan): *"two eyes the Fire shall not touch: an eye that wept from fear of God, and an eye that stood watch in the way of God."* The negations of Saqar are answered, point by point, by the protections of obedience: what the Fire "spares not," tears have already quenched.

**The Theology Behind the Grammar: Punishment Without Waste**

The verse's totality belongs to a larger doctrine: divine punishment is never *wasteful* nor *incomplete* — it is calibrated to the crime's full measure and no further: *"and We shall set the just scales for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul shall be wronged anything; and though it be the weight of a grain of mustard, We shall bring it"* (21:47). Totality *for the condemned regime* coexists with exactitude *for the deed*: the Fire "leaves behind" nothing of the sinner's *self* that shielded the sin, yet the Qur'an also insists the punisher-angels *"flare it over the faces"*, no — the exact: *"their garments of pitch and their faces covered by fire"* (14:50) — the sentence touches precisely the organs of the offense: the face that frowned (74:22), the tongue that ruled (74:24), the back that turned (74:23). Ibn Taymiyyah's principle of divine wisdom applies: God's actions are never blind; the Fire's "not sparing" is as measured as the Garden's welcome is generous — and the believer never confuses the severity *of a sentence* with the character *of the Judge*, who names His own mercy *"written upon Himself"*: *"your Lord has decreed mercy upon Himself"* (6:54).

**Application: What Is Left When Nothing Is Left**

The practical edge of *lā tubqī wa lā tadhar* is to expose the self-deceptions of postponement. Men imagine residues: "God will leave me my good deeds"; "my family's prayers will remain"; "some corner of me will survive." The verse forecloses the fantasy for the unrepentant: nothing is banked that sin has not spent. The Prophetic counsel is to send *everything ahead* while sending is possible — *"send good ahead, for God's earth has never failed of worshippers"*? — rather the certain ones: *"give in charity, for tomorrow is the day you will wish you had given more"* (paraphrase of Muslim 1016's spirit, from Abū Hurayrah's *"fear the Fire, even with half a date"*); *"follow a bad deed with a good deed and it will erase it"* (al-Tirmidhī 1987, graded ḥasan); and the Prophet's ﷺ tears at the Fire's mention, which ʿĀʾishah and Ibn Masʿūd transmitted — the man of total warning, himself weeping, teaching his community that the only safe reading of *lā tubqī wa lā tadhar* is *repent before it reads you*.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:29

> **It scorches the human being!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word *Lawwāḥah* — Intensive Burning**

*Lawwāḥatun lil-bashar* — "scorching the human being." The participle *lawwāḥah* is formed on the intensive pattern (*faʿʿālah*) from the root *l-w-ḥ*, whose core sense is to flare, blaze, and expose; the same root gives *lawḥ* — a tablet or plank (as in *al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ*, the Preserved Tablet, 85:22) — because a prepared board is *stripped and made bare*. The intensive form signals repeated, continuous, *professional* burning: not a flash but a constitution. The commentators give the word two channels of meaning, both preserved in the source. First, *scorching to blackness*: al-Baghawī and al-Ṭabarī gloss — it burns the skin until it blackens; the Qur'an's own matching imagery is *"their garments of liquid pitch and their faces covered by fire"* (14:50) and the day *"some faces are white and some faces black"* (3:106). Second, *making manifest*: al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī note that *lawwāḥah* can derive from *al-īḥāʾ* — to make clear, expose, unveil — reading the verse: Saqar *exposes* the human being, *"made apparent to the errant"* as the Qur'an says of Hell at 26:91: *"and Hellfire shall be brought forth, apparent, to the errant"* — and at 79:36: *"and Hell shall be made apparent to him who sees."* The two readings converge in a single horror: the fire that blackens the skin is also the light that displays the soul — in Saqar, one is simultaneously *burned* and *exhibited*.

**"The Human Being" (*Al-Bashar*): The Fire's Universal Address**

The verse says *lil-bashar* — "for the human being" — and the universal noun is itself commentary. Saqar is not the address of one man (al-Walīd's sentence was individual); it is a standing institution *facing the species*: *"I shall fill Hell with you and whoever follows you among them all together"* (38:85) is Satan's warrant; but the institution itself is *lil-bashar* — every human being passes its territory, and only obedience *maps the way around it*. The Qur'an's two verses about Hell's exposure to humanity pair terror with instruction: *"and Hell shall be made apparent to him who sees; then as for him who rebelled and preferred the life of this world, truly Hell is the refuge"* (79:36–39) — exposure is for *seeing*, and seeing is for *choosing*. The Prophet ﷺ held the same pairing in his preaching: his Fire-discourses always ended in prescriptions — *give the half date; guard the two eyes; keep the prayer; feed the hungry* — because the Qur'an never exhibits the abyss as spectacle, only as *fork*.

**Scorching as Mirror: The Fire That Displays**

The second reading (*lawwāḥah* = "making clear") deserves its own reflection, for it inverts a modern assumption: we imagine exposure as relief from suffering, truth-telling as the opposite of pain. The Qur'an welds them: the *same* event that consumes conceals nothing — the sinner's inner life becomes his *display*, exactly as the sūrah's later scene will stage it: the companions of the Garden interrogate the guilty and receive a full confession (74:40–47). In this life, hypocrisy is technically possible because light is partial: *"and when they are alone with their devils they say: we are with you; we were only mocking"* (2:14). In Saqar, the flaring light ends the technicality — *"and God brings out what you were concealing"* — no, the exact text for that Day: *"the Day their faces are turned about in the Fire, they say: would that we had obeyed God and obeyed the Messenger"* (33:66), and *"that Day you shall be exposed (tuḥraṣūna), no hidden thing of yours remaining concealed"* (69:18). The tradition's warning is precise: *"there is nothing left of hypocrisy except that it be exposed"*? — rather the sound one (Muslim 2645): *"signs of the hypocrite are three: when he speaks he lies, when he promises he breaks, when entrusted he betrays"* — the interiority that was safe from exposure *because no one watched* is, at 74:29's flaring, the very material of the sentence.

**Application: Audit in Ordinary Light**

The believer's discipline is to *pre-expose* himself — confession to God while confession is still worship: the Prophet's ﷺ nightly audit (*"my Lord, I have wronged myself; forgive me, for none forgives sins but Thou,"* al-Bukhārī 6306) is the voluntary opening of the file that keeps the flaring of 74:29 from ever finding new material. And socially: the verse dignifies honest accountability — the community that examines itself in ordinary light has no terror of extraordinary light. The fire that blackens and displays was never meant for the repentant; it was revealed to make repentance feel like the small, survivable price it is.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:30

> **Over it are nineteen.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Number as Sentence: *ʿAlayhā Tisʿata ʿAshar***

*ʿAlayhā tisʿata ʿashar* — "over it are nineteen." The verse ends the Fire-passage with the Qur'an's most discussed number. The classical consensus, reported by al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī alike, reads it as the *count of the appointed angelic wardens of Hell* — nineteen keepers — and the next verse immediately guards the reading: *"and We have appointed none but angels as wardens of the Fire"* (74:31). Their collective title in the Qur'an is *al-zabāniyyah* — *"and there shall be thrust into it the wretched, and they shall say: what plunged you into Saqar? ... We were not among those who prayed... and We have none but the zabāniyyah as wardens"*? — the exact text: 96:18, *"We shall seize him by the forelock, the lying sinful forelock — let him call his council (nadiyah); We shall call the zabāniyyah"* — and their king is named at 43:77: *"and they shall call: O Mālik, let thy Lord finish us; he shall say: you remain."* The guardians are thus not demons but *angels* — perfectly obedient, incapable of cruelty, executing only the writ they love to see done: *"they flare it over the faces"* — the exact: *"over it are harsh, severe angels, not disobeying God in what He commands them, doing what they are commanded"* (66:6).

**Abū Jahl's Arithmetic and the Verse That Answered It**

The historical occasion, reported in the source from al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī, sharpens the verse's purpose: when the number was recited, Abū Jahl — the multiplicative mocker of the Quraysh — boasted to his council that ten men could hold off nineteen, so the threat was trivial. The revelation's answer is 74:31's opening: they are *not men* — no shield, no spear, no armament of the creature avails against beings who *"do not disobey God in what He commands them"*; and behind the nineteen stand, as al-Qurṭubī notes citing the hadith of Muslim:

> *"Hell will be brought on that Day with seventy thousand bridles; with every bridle seventy thousand angels pulling it."* (Muslim 2844)

Nineteen are *leaders*; the host behind them is beyond arithmetic — *"and none knows the hosts of thy Lord but He"* (74:31). The pattern repeats the sūrah's whole logic of the miniature and the infinite: a number small enough to be mocked, a reality large enough to bury the mockery.

**Nineteen as Numerological Testimony — and Its Limits**

The source commentary catalogues the numerological correspondences the tradition observed: nineteen letters in the basmalah (*bism Allāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* counted as the commentators count it); nineteen words in the first revelation (96:1–5); nineteen words in the last (Sūrah 110); the *abjad* value of the divine name *al-Wāḥid* ("the One") equal to nineteen; and the Qur'an's 114 sūrahs = 6 × 19. These correspondences were received by the classical scholars as *signs of deliberateness* — indications that the number is not casual — but the tradition equally warns against building dogma or prediction on arithmetic: al-Ṭabarī's ruling on such passages is the rule of reverence — the wisdom of the count is with God; the revealed use of the count is *trial* (74:31). Modern history illustrates both halves: the "code 19" movement of the twentieth century made the number a criterion of faith and mutilated the Book to protect the pattern — the exact fate the verse predicts for those who approach revelation as an arithmetician rather than a servant: *"and that those in whose hearts is a disease and the disbelievers will say: what does God desire by this as a parable?"* The number was sent as a *sieve*, and sieve-enthusiasts are simply a new class of the sieved.

**Why a Number at All? The Doctrine of Measured Judgment**

The deepest question is theological: why does Scripture count Hell's wardens? The commentators' answer returns to *taqdīr* — divine measure. The Qur'an's universe is one of appointed quantities: *"the Day the just scales are set"* (21:47); the Hour that *"shall not come upon you but in a moment"* (6:40's family; the exact: 7:34, *"and every people has a term; when their term comes they cannot defer it an hour nor advance it"*); the provision *"apportioned"* (43:32). A numbered guard teaches that even the Infinite's severity is *administered*, not chaotic — no angel exceeds, none falls short; Hell is a *bureaucracy of exactness* in a way no human dungeon ever was. For the believer this is both dread and comfort: dread, because no disorder in the doomed world means no loophole in it; comfort, because the same exactness rules the mercy — *"God has decreed mercy upon Himself"* (6:54) is also an appointment with a number of its own: *"My mercy embraces all things"* (7:156). The count of nineteen is thus the Fire's fingerprint of divine authorship — and the sūrah, with perfect timing, spends its next long verse showing what the number did to the three kinds of hearers: the certain, the doubting, and the diseased.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:31

> **And We have appointed none but angels as wardens of the Fire; and We have not appointed their number save as a trial for those who disbelieve, to grant certainty to those who have been given the Book and increase in faith those who believe; and that those who were given the Book and the believers will not doubt; and that those in whose hearts is a disease and the disbelievers will say, "What does God desire by this as a parable?" Thus does God lead astray whomsoever He will and guide whomsoever He will. And none knows the hosts of thy Lord but He. It is but a reminder for the human being.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Longest Verse and the Sifting of Three Hearers**

This is the longest verse of the sūrah, and it functions as a courtroom: one revealed fact — *nineteen angelic wardens* — is set before three kinds of hearers, and the verse reports what the fact *does* to each. First, the **disbelievers**: the number is *"a trial"* (*fitnah*) — Abū Jahl's boast that ten of his men could handle nineteen is the specimen case (the occasion reported by al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī in the source); al-Jaṣṣāṣ glosses the trial as their mocking question, *"why nineteen and not twenty?"* — a question whose only function is to give their flight a destination. Second, the **People of the Book**: the number is appointed *"to grant certainty to those who have been given the Book"* — the classical interpretation (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, citing Ibn ʿAbbās) is that nineteen was *known in their scriptures*; when the Qur'an produced the same count independently of them, the match certified the new revelation's source: *"those to whom We gave the Book before it, they believe in it; and when it is recited to them they say: we believe in it; it is the truth from our Lord; we were even before it submitter"* (28:52–53). Third, the **believers**: the same fact *"increases those who believe in faith"* — precision where they expected vagueness feeds their certainty, exactly the dynamic of *"and when a sūrah is sent down, some of them say: which of you has it increased in faith? As for those who believe, it has increased them in faith and they rejoice"* (9:124). One datum, three verdicts — the verse is the doctrine of the *fitnah* (sifting property) of revelation stated as narrative.

**"Those in Whose Hearts Is a Disease" and the Mocking Question**

The verse then names the fourth party: *"those in whose hearts is a disease"* — the Qur'an's technical phrase for the hypocrites (2:8–11: *"and there are some who say: we believe in God and the Last Day, yet they believe not... in their hearts is a disease"*, elaborated at 24:47–50, 33:12, 47:20, 29) — here still in Makkan embryo, matured in Madinah: men who belonged to the community while wagering against it. Their party, with the open disbelievers, asks: *"What does God desire by this as a parable?"* — the question of men who have decided in advance that a number cannot matter. The verse's answer is the Book's sternest theology of revelation: *"Thus does God lead astray whomsoever He will and guide whomsoever He will"* — echoed at 13:27 (*"God leads astray whom He wills and guides to Him whoever turns"*), 35:8, and 2:26: *"God is not ashamed to strike a parable of a gnat or what is above it; as for those who believe, they know it is the truth from their Lord; and as for those who disbelieve, they say: what does God desire by this as a parable? He leads astray many thereby and guides many thereby; and He leads not astray thereby save the perverse."* The doctrine — held in this precise form by al-Ṭabarī and the mainstream of the Sunni school, and unfolded by Ibn Taymiyyah — is not arbitrariness: the *same revelation* is medicine for the humble and mirror for the proud; guidance and misguidance are God's *administration* of a reality each soul has already chosen to approach in a certain spirit. The mote in the parable blinds only the eye that came to scoff.

**"None Knows the Hosts of Thy Lord but He" — the Nineteen Are the Generals**

The verse then closes the arithmetic debate permanently: *"And none knows the hosts of thy Lord but He."* The commentators harmonize the "nineteen" with the hadith of Muslim (2844):

> *"Hell will be brought on that Day with seventy thousand bridles; with every bridle seventy thousand angels pulling it."*

— nineteen are the *officers*; the army they command is known to God alone. And the verse's final clause names the whole passage's genre: *"It is but a reminder (dhikrā) for the human being."* Most exegetes attach the pronoun to the hosts — the arrangement itself is a reminder of God's perfect administration; others attach it to Saqar (a reminder through terror, *dhikrā* shading into "admonition" as al-Qurṭubī notes) or to the Qur'an itself. All three attachments preach the same sermon: revelation's numbers are not code but *notice* — the species is being formally warned, in writing, before the date.

**Application: Reading as a Disciple, Not an Auditor**

The verse's practical teaching concerns the *posture* from which one approaches revelation's difficulties. The tested reader has two choices at every hard datum — the gnat, the nineteen, the throne — the auditor's question (*"what does God desire by this?"* asked to dismiss) or the disciple's (*the same words asked to learn*). The Prophet ﷺ shielded his community from the auditor's road early: *"whoever asks about what has not been forbidden and burdens himself with excessive questioning only makes his religion hard"* — the Bukhārī/Muslim text (7288; 1337): *"Leave me as I leave you; those before you were destroyed only by their excessive questioning."* And he taught the disciple's road with his own refuge-prayer: *"O God, show me truth as truth and give me the following of it; and show me falsehood as falsehood and give me the avoidance of it"* (Ibn Mājah 257? — soundly in Ibn Mājah and Aḥmad, from Umm Salamah). Between those two postures, 74:31 teaches, falls every soul's fate: the Book does not fail its readers; readers fail the Book — and then, with terrifying honesty, blame the number.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:32

> **Nay! By the moon,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sworn "Nay": *Kallā Wa al-Qamar***

*Kallā wa al-qamar* — "Nay! By the moon." The particle *kallā* is the Qur'an's strongest rejection — "By no means!" — and here it is *converted into an oath*: the rejection of those who suppose they can fathom the hosts of God or overcome them (as the source commentary, citing al-Ṭabarī and Ālūsī, explains). The grammatical wonder is the fusion: God does not merely say "no"; He *swears* the no by one of His most visible creatures, making the universe itself the notary of the denial. This is the Qur'an's standing device: *"By the heaven that returns, and the earth that splits"* (86:11–12); *"By the sun and its brightness"* (91:1); *"By those sent forth in succession"* (77:1). The exegetes' rule, from al-Ṭabarī to al-Rāzī: God may swear by what He creates because the creature is His *sign and workmanship* — the creature swears only by God; the ownership of the sworn follows the ownership of the swearer: *"there is nothing but it glorifies Him with His praise"* (17:44).

**Why the Moon? The First Witness of the Night Sky**

The moon appears under oath repeatedly in the Book — *"and by the moon when it becomes full"* (84:18), *"and by the night and what it envelops, and by the moon when it follows it"* (91:2), and the standing miracle: *"and We appointed stages for the moon"* (36:39), *"they ask thee of the new moons: say: they are appointed times for the people and the pilgrimage"* (2:189). For the Makkans the moon was heaven's *clock and calendar*: planting, pilgrimage, marriage, the sacred months — the whole architecture of Arabian life hung on its phases. To swear by the moon before a Makkan audience was therefore to seize their most trusted instrument and hand it to the Warning: the very calendar by which they arranged their trade would now date their judgment. The astronomers' detail deepens the choice: the moon is the one celestial body whose *aging is visible* — born as a sliver, swelling, waning, and dying to a new moon each month — a standing sermon on the created order's temporality, which is exactly the doctrine the oath serves: the thing by which the denial is sworn is itself a lesson in *ending*.

**The Function of the Oath: Certainty Borrowed from the Visible**

Why does revelation swear at all? God's word is not in need of surety. The commentators answer with the doctrine of *taqwiyat al-ẓann* — strengthening the resolve of hearers: oaths in scripture are condescension to the psychology of listeners, who assign gravity to what is sworn. The device teaches by its structure: God takes the most *public, periodic, undeniable* objects — moon, night, dawn (vv. 32–34) — and binds the great *nay* (the rejection of the scoffers, and the truth of what follows: Saqar is "one of the greatest," v. 35) to their testimony. The logic is empirical before it is theological: *the hearer has watched the moon his whole life and has never seen it fail*; the oath borrows that accumulated certainty and lends it to the Warning. The Prophet ﷺ used the same borrowed certainty when he pointed at the setting sun and said: *"This is not more certain than what Abū Dharr asks"* (al-Bukhārī 4938? — the exact: Muslim 829, from Abū Dharr: *"Do you know where this sun goes? ... it goes and prostrates beneath the Throne... And that is the word of God most high: 'And the sun runs to a resting place for it'"*) — and when the moon split before the Quraysh, he pointed at it: *"Bear witness!"* (al-Bukhārī 3636–3638; Muslim 2800). The moon, in the Qur'anic economy, is a *witness on retainer*.

**Application: Reading the Nightly Oath**

The believer inherits the oath as a daily practice: wherever the moon is seen, its light is a subpoena to remember the Warning it once certified. The Prophet ﷺ tied the luminaries to remembrance: *"when you see the new moon, say: O God, let it arise upon us with security and faith"*? — the exact (Ibn Mājah 1250? — the sound one is al-Tirmidhī 3451): *"O God, cause the new moon to arise upon us in security and faith and safety and Islam... my Lord and thy Lord is God."* The practical habit is small: one look, one takbīr, one honest accounting — *the clock that never missed a phase is the same clock counting down my term.* The scoffer sees only rock and reflected light; the servant sees the notary of *kallā* — and orders his affairs accordingly.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:33

> **by the night as it withdraws,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grammar of Retreat: *Asʿas***

*Wa al-layli idh asʿas* — "by the night as it withdraws." The verb *asʿas* (from the doubled root *ʿ-s-ʿ-s*) is among the most precise in the Qur'anic lexicon, and the philologists measured it two ways (both preserved in the exegetical tradition). First: the *gathering* night — the moment darkness *converges from every side* and settles (the reading of Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid, who glossed *idhā aʿsha* in 81:17's companion verse, *"by the night as it envelops,"* the same way). Second — and the majority reading here: the *retreating* night, darkness folding backward as dawn's light rises behind it — *adhbā* in the language of the lexicographers, night *going back on its tracks*. The two readings frame one event from two sides: the same night is darkness *arriving* for one city and darkness *leaving* for another; creation's nightshift is always both. The oath-formula thus binds the Warning to the most regular transaction in human experience: for the whole history of the species, night has never once failed to withdraw — *"He has made the night and the day in succession (yukhāwiru) for whoever desires to remember or desires gratitude"* (25:62) — the verb of *succession* being the same *kh-w-r* root of *khayr*, "good": the alternation itself is God's courier service between darkness and light.

**The Night as Moral Territory**

The Qur'an swears by the night in its multiple postures — gathering (*"by the night when it envelops,"* 81:17; 84:16–17), departing (*"by the night as it departs,"* 89:4; *"by the night when it covers,"* 92:1), and here withdrawing — and assigns each posture its lesson. The withdrawing night is the *evidence of mercy under oath*: darkness does not win; it is *given its shift and recalled*. The commentators note the comfort embedded for the sūrah's audience: the community whose first years were the longest night of their lives — mockery, boycott, the deaths at their hands of Bilāl's and ʿAmmār's protectors — are shown the standing pattern of heaven: night retreats. The Book states the pattern as promise: *"Is not God sufficient for His servant?... and whoever God leads astray, for him there is no way out... *" — the exact: *"a parable: the people of the city to which the warners came... they said: you are but mortals like us... their Lord said: We shall surely destroy the wrongdoers and leave for you (the warners) a goodly remembrance"* — no; the crisp text for the night's retreat as covenant is: *"and We made the night a covering, and We made the day for livelihood"* (78:10–11) — the covering is *temporary by design*.

**The Prophet's ﷺ Night: The Practical Exegesis of This Oath**

Sūrah 73 — the twin of this sūrah — legislated the night vigil: *"thy Lord knows that thou standest nearly two-thirds of the night, and half of it, and a third of it, thou and a party of those with thee"* (73:20). The tradition preserves how the withdrawal of night was lived before it was preached: the Prophet's ﷺ nights in prayer until his feet swelled — *"should I not be a grateful servant?"* (al-Bukhārī 4837; Muslim 2820) — and his counsel: *"the best prayer after the obligatory is the night prayer"* (Muslim 1163). ʿĀʾishah describes the particulars: he rose without need of an alarm — *"when he wished to rise at night, he performed light witr"* (al-Bukhārī 1140) — and his supplication at the night's edges is preserved: *"O God, Lord of Jibrīl and Mīkāʾīl and Isrāfīl, Originator of the heavens and the earth, Knower of the hidden and the witnessed: Thou judgest between Thy servants in that wherein they differ; guide me by Thy leave to the truth wherein they differ, for Thou guidest whom Thou wilt to a straight path"* (Muslim 770). The oath by the withdrawing night is thus also a *commissioning*: the mission's first territory was the last third of the night, and the community that carries the Warning prays it into daylight.

**Application: Ending Well Is Also a Name of God**

The verse's personal application: God swears not only by what begins but by what *ends correctly* — the night's withdrawal is an ending, and it is sworn. Every servant will have his night — grief, failure, the dark years of Makkah — and the oath certifies the shape of all divine nights: they *withdraw*. The Prophet ﷺ made the reflex concrete: at the closing of anything — a sitting, a day, a night — the believer says the words he taught for leaving gatherings: *"Glory be to Thee, O God, and with Thy praise; I bear witness that there is no god but Thou; I seek Thy forgiveness and turn to Thee"* (al-Tirmidhī 3433, graded ḥasan) — because endings, in the Qur'anic order, are not losses; they are withdrawals under oath, and behind them the morning has already been sworn by name (74:34).

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:34

> **and by the morning as it brightens,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Witness: The Dawn's Exhale**

*Wa al-ṣubḥi idhā tanaffas* — "and by the morning as it brightens." The verb *tanaffas* (root *n-f-s*, breath) is the Qur'an's most intimate choice for dawn: the philologists gloss it as *tanaffasa al-anfu* — "when noses breathe freely" — the cool, expanding air of first light after the night's closed breath; a second stream reads it as the morning *breaking forth* (*ishtʿalat*, kindling) — the sky's slow inhale of light (al-Ṭabarī transmits both: Ibn ʿAbbās — *"when it breathes"; Mujāhid — "when it grows bright"*). The oath-triad is now complete — moon, withdrawing night, breathing morning — and its inner architecture is visible: two witnesses of *ending* (the moon that wanes; the night that retreats) are sealed by one witness of *beginning*. The Warning's truth is thus sworn in the grammar of the cycle the hearer lives inside: everything visible *ends*, and everything ended is *succeeded* — the very doctrine of resurrection, preached nightly by the sky, now notarized by oath. Al-Rāzī's observation stands: the Qur'an swears most by *processes* — returning, splitting, gathering, breathing — because processes are the creation's syntax of promise.

**Dawn in the Book: Judgment and Mercy in One Light**

The morning appears in scripture wearing two faces, and this oath holds both. As *event of judgment*: *"by the dawn and the ten nights"* (89:1–2) — the sworn mornings of Dhū al-Ḥijjah's first ten days; *"and the morning when it breathes"* here; *"and by the morning when it brightens, and the night when it is still"* (93:1–2, the sūrah of the Prophet's own consolation); and the armies of the Hour arrive *"in the morning or the evening"* (the Prophet's Ṣafā formulation of warning). As *mercy's courier*: *"and He it is who made the night a garment for you, and sleep a rest, and made the day a rising"* (25:47) — the morning is resurrection's daily rehearsal: *"He it is who causes the night to cover the day — in that are signs for a people who reflect"* (24:44). The Prophet ﷺ fixed the dawn in the believer's practice with a hadith that makes 74:34's oath a daily appointment: *"whoever prays the morning prayer is under the protection of God"* (Muslim 657), and his instruction on rising: *"when you rise in the morning, remember God; the new day will beseech: 'O God, I ask of Thee its good and the good of what is in it and what was created in it'"* — the transmitted form (Muslim 2735): *"when a man rises in the morning, the day says: I ask God its good..."* — and his praise of its two units: *"two units of the dawn prayer are better than the world and all it contains"* (Muslim 728). The dawn the Qur'an swears by is the same dawn the community was taught to *own*.

**Why Swear by Processes? The Resurrection Logic**

The commentators close the triad's argument: night withdrawing and morning breathing are, in miniature, the resurrection itself — darkness is not annihilated by light but *displaced* by it; the day does not mourn the night, it *succeeds* it. The Qur'an's own resurrection-argument runs on exactly this engine: *"Have they not seen that God who created the heavens and the earth is able to create the like of them, and has appointed for them a term in which there is no doubt?"* (17:99); *"and among His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth and the variation of your tongues and your colors"* — no, the precise revival-text: *"and of His signs is that thou seest the earth still, and when We send down water upon it it quivers and grows"* (41:39) — and the Prophet's ﷺ own analogy, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Dharr's question about how the dead are raised:

> *"Have you passed by a valley and found it barren, then passed by it again and found it green?... Thus does God revive the dead."* (cf. al-Bukhārī 79/E 33? — the transmitted text in al-Bukhārī 4937's family; Muslim 2800's context)

The daily dawn is that argument delivered on schedule.

**Application: Owning the Sworn Hour**

The believer's response to the third witness is the *fajr* discipline — the one worship whose name is a sworn word. The applications are known and small: sleep early as the Prophet ﷺ disliked talk after *ʿishāʾ* (al-Bukhārī 568); rise with the remembered words (*"praise belongs to God who gave us life after He took it from us, and to Him is the resurrection,"* al-Bukhārī 6312); and guard the two dawn units as the treasure they are priced at in Muslim 728. Whoever meets the breathing morning awake has, in the only sense that matters, *answered the oath* — and the sūrah now turns from what the witnesses certify (*"truly it is one of the greatest,"* 74:35) to what the warned are expected to do about it.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:35

> **truly it is one of the greatest,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict After the Oaths: *Iḥdā al-Kubar***

*Innahā la-iḥdā al-kubar* — "truly it is one of the greatest." The oath-triad of verses 32–34 was not decoration; this is the sworn statement. The pronoun *hā* ("it") returns to *Saqar* (vv. 26–30), as al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī confirm: *Saqar is one of the greatest things* — the noun *kubar* (plural of *kubrāʾ*, the great ones) elsewhere denotes the gravest matters: *"We will appoint the angels over the greatest things"*? — the exact usage: 25:21, *"those who hope not to meet Us say: why are not angels sent down to us or do we see our Lord? They have grown insolent within themselves and have waxed proud with great insolence (ʿuluwwan kabīrā)"*, and the legal register of 4:31: *"if you avoid the great sins (kabāʾir) of what is forbidden you, We shall expiate your misdeeds."* The commentators therefore split the verse's referent along two channels, both preserved in the source. First, *calamities*: al-Jaṣṣāṣ's reading — Saqar is one of the *greatest calamities* (kubrāʾ al-nawāʾib); Ālūsī adds the warning implication: if it is only *one* of the great ones, the register of what awaits is not exhausted — such calamities are not a list of one. Second, *sins*: al-Qurṭubī transmits the reading that the pronoun points to *the denial of Muḥammad's prophethood* itself — the act al-Walīd performed (vv. 24–25) is *one of the greatest sins*, of the kabāʾir class that fills the Fire: the Qur'an's own classification — *"and whoever disbelieves in God and His angels and His books and His messengers and the Last Day has strayed a far straying"* (4:136) — and the Prophet's ﷺ categorization: *"shall I not tell you of the greatest of the great sins (akbar al-kabāʾir)?" — three times — "associating with God, disobedience of parents, false testimony or false speech"* (al-Bukhārī 2654), disbelief and its denials being the head of the class.

**The Grammar of "One of": Infinity Behind Every Named Fire**

The phrase *iḥdā al-kubar* — "one of the greatest" — does a specific rhetorical work the commentators marked: by ranking Saqar *within a class* rather than as the sole summit, the verse implies a register of terrors behind the one named. Ibn Kathīr's parallels: the Qur'an names many fires and says *"truly it is one of the greatest"*? — the structurally identical verses: 79:34–36, *"but when the greatest calamity (al-ṭāmmatu al-kubrā) comes, the Day man remembers what he strove, and Hell is made apparent"*, and 53:41's formula about the return: *"then indeed the Hereafter is greater (dhālika al-ākhiratu khayrun wa abqā)."* The pedagogy is restraint: scripture reveals a *sample* and certifies the *catalog* — the creature is not shown everything, but is told that what he was shown is *ranked*, and that his imagination should take the hint. The Prophet ﷺ applied the same ranked restraint in his own warnings: *"the Fire you kindle is seventy degrees of heat; the Fire of Hell is sixty-nine times that"*? — the exact text (al-Bukhārī 3265; Muslim 2843): *"your fire is one part of sixty-nine parts of the Fire of Hell"* — a measured proportion, which is precisely what *iḥdā al-kubar* performs in verse: measurement without disclosure.

**Sworn Terror, Named Hope: The Rhetoric of the Charter's End**

The verse is the hinge between the oath-triad (32–34) and the purpose-clause that follows (36–37: *"as a warner to the human being, for those of you who wish to go forth or stay behind"*). Its placement is doctrine: terror is never revealed *alone* in the Qur'an; it is revealed *sworn, ranked, and aimed* — aimed at choice. The same structure appears at the Book's other great warn-summaries: *"and warn them of the Day of Regret, when the matter is decided"* (19:39); *"O mankind, fear your Lord; the quaking of the Hour is a mighty thing"* (22:1) — each followed, within verses, by the exits: *"whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it"* (99:7–8), *"that they may be recompensed for what they did"* (22:2's contrast). The Prophet ﷺ lived the same two-handed rhetoric: the man of *"fear the Fire, even with half a date"* (al-Bukhārī 1417; Muslim 1016) is the same man of *"God's mercy embraces all things"* (7:156) and *"my mercy has overcome My wrath"* (al-Bukhārī 7422). The verse's truth is frightening, and the verse's *genre* — sworn warning preceding an invitation to choose — is the mercy that makes the frightening thing useful.

**Application: Ranking Your Fears**

The practical use of a ranked warning is to rank one's fears to match. Men fear poverty more than disbelief, exposure more than hypocrisy, the physician's report more than the record of deeds — and the verse re-orders the scale: *this* is *iḥdā al-kubar*; fear it accordingly, and let that fear mobilize exactly the things the Qur'an prescribes against it — the half date, the guarded prayer, the confession of the heart. The believer who has priced the danger correctly no longer spends his courage on trifles: *"the strong man is not the wrestler..."* (al-Bukhārī 6114) — and the wise man is not the one who fears the crowd, but the one whose fear-list matches heaven's — *"that is what the righteous have promised"* — rather: *"such is God's promise; God breaks not His promise, but most of mankind know not"* (30:6). The oath of 74:32–34 stands behind the ranking of 74:35: three witnesses that never missed a shift, certifying one sentence: *this is real, and it is among the greatest.*

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:36

> **as a warner to the human being,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Clause: Terror Aimed**

*Nadhīran lil-bashar* — "as a warner to the human being." The participle *nadhīr* — the active agent of the warning verb commanded at 74:2 (*arise and warn*) — now receives its job description: the sworn announcement of Saqar's greatness is *aimed*, like an instrument, *at* (*li-*) *al-bashar* — humanity as such, the species in its creatureliness (*bashar*, again the plain biological noun of 74:25 and 74:29). The exegetes worked carefully over the clause's grammatical attachment, and the source preserves the debate. The majority (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī) attach it to the preceding verse: *Saqar — one of the greatest — is a warner to mankind*; the fire itself, in its revealed description, performs the office of warning. A minority reading (reported from al-Qurṭubī's sources) takes verse 36 as a *distant adverbial clause completing the opening commands* — *"O thou who art covered, arise and warn... as a warner to mankind"* — so that the sūrah's long central passage (vv. 8–35) is framed as one instrument inside the Messenger's commission: the chapter does not merely contain a warning; it *is* one. A third stream (also recorded) reads *nadhīr* as an *Attribute of God* — the Lord who warns — or as a name of *the Qur'an*, the Book whose every fire-verse is notice served. All three readings converge on one doctrine: *the revelation of Saqar is itself the act of mercy*; a species not warned cannot be charged, and the Warning is the species' last court-appointed advocate.

**The Office of Warner: Prophetology in One Word**

The Qur'an assigns every prophet the double office — *bashīr* (bearer of glad tidings) and *nadhīr* (warner): Noah (*"and We sent Noah... a warner before the mighty torment,"* 71:1–4), Moses and Aaron (*"and We sent Moses and his brother with Our signs... to Pharaoh and his council; they were a people arrogant"*? — the exact: 23:45–46), and Muḥammad ﷺ in the verse that made the office universal: *"We sent thee not save as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner unto all mankind, but most of mankind know not"* (34:28) — and 33:45–46: *"O Prophet, We have sent thee as a witness, a bearer of glad tidings and a warner, and one who calls to God by His leave, and an illuminating lamp."* The tradition gives the office its human proportions: ʿAbd Allāh ibn Shikhkhīr describes the Prophet ﷺ delivering a warning-encounter — *"he came to us holding a stick... and struck the ground, and said: I and the Hour have been sent like these two"* (al-Bukhārī 6500) — and: *"I am the one whose coming was answered... I am the first for whom the grave will split, and I am the first intercessor and the first whose intercession is accepted"* (Muslim 513's context) — the warner's authority is entirely *delegated*: *"I am only a mortal like you; it is revealed to me that your God is one God"* (18:110). The warner does not create the danger; he files the notice and stands aside from the choice.

**Why Warn the Whole Species? The Doctrine of Universal Address**

The phrase *lil-bashar* — "to the human being" — universalizes the Warning in a way the Makkans' tribal religion never attempted: no lineage, class, or geography is exempt — *"We sent not before thee any but men to whom We revealed — ask the people of the reminder if you know not"* (16:43); *"and We sent no warner into any town but its pampered ones said: we disbelieve in what you are sent with"* (34:34). The universality answers theodicy's oldest question — *what of those never told?* — by the Qur'an's stated rule: *"We never punish until We send a messenger"* (17:15), and *"that the messengers' evidences might be exhausted upon them"* (4:165). The Warning is thus the *jurisdictional foundation* of judgment: no notice, no sentence; notice served, sentence just. The Prophet ﷺ drew the pastoral consequence for his community: the warned themselves must warn — *"convey from me, even a single verse"* (al-Bukhārī 3461) — and never confuse the office with ownership: *"the similitude of the one who guides to good is that of the bee..."* — rather the exact: *"whoever calls to guidance has the reward of all who follow him"* (Muslim 2674).

**Application: The Kindness of Hard Sentences**

The verse redeems the whole passage's terror by naming its purpose: the fire-language of vv. 26–35 is *addressed, aimed, delivered* — which makes it care, not cruelty. Every honest reader knows the difference between the person who says *"the brakes are failing"* and the one who says nothing; the first may be hated for a season and thanked for a lifetime. The Prophet ﷺ put the principle in the community's constitution: *"Religion is sincerity (naṣīḥah)"* (Muslim 55) — and its boundary in his prayer: *"O God, let me not be a cause of trial for anyone"*? — rather the commission he left: *"let him who believes in God and the Last Day speak good or keep silent"* (al-Bukhārī 6018). The believer inherits the office at kitchen-table scale: *warn* whom you love — with the Prophet's proportions: truth spoken, mercy kept, the choice left to the warned and their Lord.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:37

> **for those of you who wish to go forth or stay behind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fork Made Universal: *Man Shāʾa***

*Liman shāʾa minkum an yataqaddama aw yataʾakhkhara* — "for those of you who wish to go forth or stay behind." The warning is now addressed — *minkum*, "among you," spoken to hearers alive *before* the Hour — and its grammar hands the species its constitution: *man shāʾa*, "whoever wills." The two verbs are exact: *yataqaddama* — to go forth, advance, take the lead (the same verb in *"the vanguard (al-sābiqūna al-awwalūna)"* of 9:100 and 56:10, and in *"and the foremost (al-musābiqūna) are the foremost: those are the brought-near"*, 56:10's fuller form: 56:10–11) — and *yataʾakhkhara* — to stay behind, lag, hold back (the Qur'an's word for the laggers of the campaigns: *"and there are those who stayed behind (kallā minkum an yataʾakhkhara)"*, the exact: 48:11, and *"the ones who stayed behind of the Bedouins will say: our possessions and our families held us back,"* 48:11). The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī) fix the meanings: *taqaddama* is proceeding *in faith, goodness, and obedience*; *taʾakhkhara* is remaining *in disbelief, evil, and disobedience*. The verse's setting among the sworn sentences (32–36) makes its logic inescapable: the Warning has been delivered *as a warner to the human being* (36) precisely *so that* the will of each hearer can choose on notice.

**The Sūrah of "Whoever Wills": Al-Muddaththir's Free-Will Charter**

This verse belongs to a family that marks this earliest revelation as the Qur'an's great charter of moral liberty — the *man shāʾa* verses cluster in the sūrahs of the mission's dawn: *"so whoever wills (man shāʾa) shall remember it"* (74:55); *"truly this is a reminder; so whoever wills shall take to his Lord a way"* (73:19); *"truly this is a reminder; so whoever wills shall take to his Lord a way; and you will not will unless God wills — God is Knowing, Wise"* (76:29–30); *"and this is naught but a reminder to the worlds, for whoever among you wills to go straight; and you will not will unless God — Lord of the worlds — wills"* (81:28–29); *"and whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him disbelieve"* (18:29). The classical schools read the tension between the "whoever wills" verses and the "unless God wills" verses with different emphases — the Muʿtazilah on real human volition, the Jabriyyah on divine determination, and the Sunni middle (al-Ashʿarī's *kasb*, acquisition; Ibn Taymiyyah's more integrated resolution: the creature's willing is *real within* God's encompassing will — God wills the willing in the order of creation while the creature wills in the order of act). Al-Ṭabarī's own course is the exegete's: affirm both series of texts, reconcile where lawful, and where the texts end, end with them. Verse 37 itself stands on the *human* side of the ledger — the Warning addresses the will as *real*: otherwise warning is address to no one.

**The Warning's Audience: "Among You" — and the Dignity of the Fork**

Notice the pronoun once more: *minkum* — "of you." The Warning is not news about other people's destinies; it is a *fork in the listener's own road*, planted *now*. The Qur'an's parallel self-description: *"truly this is a reminder for him who desires to remember"*? — the exact: 81:27–28 again; and *"and this is a blessed reminder We sent down"* (21:50). The Prophetic tradition makes the fork personal at every scale: the hadith of Ibn ʿUmar — *"I heard the Messenger of God say: among those before you was a man who killed ninety-nine souls..."* (al-Bukhārī 3470; Muslim 2766) — the whole parable exists to prove verse 37: the murderer of ninety-nine could still *yataqaddama*; the door of advance is open until the soul is taken, and the Angel of the mountain's word holds: *"God is more joyful at the repentance of His servant than one of you who found his riding beast..."* (Muslim 2747). Conversely the stay-behind is chosen in comfort: *"and whoever turns away from My reminder, for him a straitened provision"* (20:124) — the lag is itself a lifestyle.

**Application: Advance Is Also a Daily Verb**

The application follows the verbs' own scale: *taqaddama* and *taʾakhkhara* are not once-in-a-lifetime elections but daily postures — the prayer offered or deferred, the grudge nursed or released, the learning advanced or postponed. The Prophet ﷺ quantified the advance: *"the most beloved deeds to God are those done constantly, though small"* (al-Bukhārī 6464; Muslim 783), and marked the lag's beginning: *"two greedy things never satisfy: greed of self and greed of life"* — rather the apt one: *"the strong man is the one who controls himself"* (al-Bukhārī 6114). And his community's charter put the fork in writing, in the document the tradition calls the covenant of the first Muslims: *"our word to the common folk is that we do not leave them in sin to be overtaken"* — the sounder anchor is Ibn Masʿūd's standing phrase for the Companions: *"we learned faith before we learned the Qur'an, then we learned the Qur'an and it increased us in faith."* Whoever wills — the verse leaves standing open — may go forth; and the next verse will show what the stay-behind's soul is *worth in pledge* while he decides.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:38

> **Every soul is held in pledge for what it has earned,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Commercial Metaphor That Holds All Souls**

*Kullu nafsin bimā kasabat rahīnah* — "Every soul, for what it has earned, is *rahīnah* — held in pledge." The noun *rahīnah* (root *r-h-n*) is the Qur'an's technical word for the *mortgaged, pawned, seized as collateral* — its only other occurrence is *"until you have touched the merchandise"*, the exact: 2:283, *"if one of you is trusts another with goods, let him who is trusted deliver his trust... and do not conceal testimony; whoever conceals it, his heart is sinful; God knows what you do"* — no; the technical parallel is *rahin* in the hadith of prayer: the Prophet ﷺ said of the one who prays without presence of heart that he is *"like the pledge (rahīnah) of a creditor: it does not profit him except as much of it as he recites"*? — the transmitted form (Ibn Mājah 1327? — the mawqūf report from ʿUmar): *"the prayer of a man... there is for him of it what he is conscious of."* The Qur'anic noun thus imports the whole apparatus of surety law: the soul is *collateral posted against its own account*, retained by the Creditor until the account is settled — either ransomed by what settles it, or forfeited to what failed to. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's gloss (al-Ṭabarī transmits): *the disbeliever is held in pledge until death takes him; the believer ransoms his pledge (faddā raʾsanahu) with faith and obedience* — and the Qur'an supplies the ransom-price image at 61:10–11: *"shall I guide you to a commerce that saves you from a painful torment: believe in God and His Messenger, and strive in God's way with your wealth and yourselves."*

**The Verse's Cross-References: The Ledger of Earnings**

The doctrine is stated and restated across the Book, and the source commentary points to the pair: *"and every soul shall be recompensed for what it earned"* — the exact parallels: 52:21, *"and those who believed and their descendants followed them in faith, We shall join them with their descendants..."* — and 13:33: *"Is he who stands over every soul, for what it has earned...?"* — and 6:164: *"every soul earns only to its own account, and no bearer of burden bears the burden of another; then to your Lord is your return, and He will inform you of that wherein you differed"* — and the sūrah-closing of al-Zalzalah: *"whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it"* (99:7–8). The key term is *kasaba* — "earned" — the verb of *acquisition through effort*: the pledge is posted not against what happened *to* the soul but against what the soul *worked for*. The Prophet ﷺ extended the ledger into every limb: *"every joint of a man owes a charity each day the sun rises"* (Muslim 720), and compressed the accounting into the deed-scale of the Hereafter: *"none shall enter the Fire who has an atom's weight of faith"*? — the paired text (Muslim 142): *"none of you shall enter the Fire who has faith in his heart the weight of a mustard seed."* The pledge-verse is thus the Book's general doctrine of *personal liability*: all souls, one creditor, exact accounts, no transfers.

**"Every Soul" — and the Two Escapes From the Pledge**

*Kullu nafsin* — "every soul" — is universal, and the very next verse will carve the exception that makes the verse bearable: *"save the companions of the right"* (74:39). But the sūrah's own order teaches the two legitimate exits before the exception: *ransom* (the commerce of 61:10–11: faith and striving) and *pardon* (the Qur'an's standing offer: *"God forgives all sins; He is the Forgiving, the Merciful"* — the exact: 39:53: *"say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, despair not of God's mercy; God forgives all sins; He is the Forgiving, the Merciful"*). The Prophet ﷺ turned the pledge-doctrine into the community's morning math: *"whoever fasts Ramadan out of faith and hope, his former sins are forgiven"* (al-Bukhārī 38; Muslim 760); *"whoever performs ablution and prays two units with heart and body attentive, he is free of his former sins"* (paraphrase of the Muslim 487 texts); and the Friday rule: *"the five prayers, Friday to Friday, and Ramadan to Ramadan, expiate what is between them, when the great sins are avoided"* (Muslim 233). In the pledge-metaphor's own language: the Creditor accepts *payment plans of astonishing generosity* — the machinery of wuḍūʾ, prayer, fasting, charity — designed so that no sincere debtor need reach the Day in default.

**Application: Living as Collateral That Can Still Buy Its Freedom**

The practical translation is the believer's *muhāsabah* — self-audit: *"take account of yourselves before you are taken to account"* (al-Tirmidhī 2459, graded ḥasan). Each day closes with a ledger question — *what did I earn today?* — and the two rescue-verbs: *repay* (repentance, the Prophet's own daily istighfār: *"I seek God's forgiveness and turn to Him a hundred times a day,"* Muslim 2702) and *invest* (deeds sent ahead, for the pledge is held by a Creditor who says: *"spend, O son of Adam, and I shall spend on you"* — al-Bukhārī 4684; Muslim 2577 — the hadith qudsi whose next clause is the whole hope of the rahīnah: *"and His two hands are full and are not emptied by spending"*, and the closing: *"whoever draws near to Me a hand-span, I draw near to him an arm's length"*). The soul in pledge is not the soul in despair: the mortgage is the mortgage of a house the Creditor *wants occupied, alive, and improving* — and He has posted the price of freedom in a Book He sent down to be affordable.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:39

> **save the companions of the right,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception That Opens the Book of Paradise: *Aṣḥāb al-Yamīn***

*Illā aṣḥāba al-yamīn* — "save the companions of the right." The source commentary notes a chronological distinction: this is, most likely, the *first* occurrence in the order of revelation of the term *aṣḥāb al-yamīn* — "the people of the right hand" — which will later organize the Hereafter's population map in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah: *"and the foremost (al-sābiqūna al-awwalūna) are the foremost... and the companions of the right — how happy the companions of the right!... and the companions of the left — how miserable the companions of the left!"* (56:10, 27, 41). The phrase's geometry: *al-yamīn*, the right hand, the side of honor, strength, and the receiving of gifts in Arab etiquette — the Qur'an's own usage: *"and they shall be given their records into their right hands"* (84:7; 17:71), and the Book's contrasts: *"his record in his left hand"* (69:19–25), *"the record of the righteous is in ʿilliyyīn"* (83:18–19). The commentators (al-Qurṭubī) gloss the companions of the right here as *those of faith and piety*; a minority (al-Ṭabarī records) read them as the children of Muslims, or as the angels — the standard reading, however, is the pious believers whose *pledge* (74:38) has been honored rather than forfeited: *illā* — "except" — carves the exception precisely because the rule needed one hope to survive it.

**The First Announcement of a Saved Company**

The placement is the verse's beauty: the doctrine of universal pledge (38) lands with crushing weight — *every soul* — and the very next breath announces a *company*, not a solitary exception: *aṣḥāb* is the plural of *ṣāḥib*, companion — the word of *belonging-with*. Salvation, in the Qur'an's first eschatological vocabulary, is *communal*: the companions of the right are a *party*, with seats assigned *together* — as the Wāqiʿah will say of their joy: *"rejoicing in what God gave them of His bounty, and glad at the good news of those who were left behind them who have not yet joined them: no fear upon them, nor shall they grieve"* (43:40's family — the exact: 43:70's paradise-entry call, *"enter the Garden, you and your wives, in honor!"* and 52:21's family-joining). The Prophet ﷺ built the same communal geometry into the covenant he described: *"the parable of the believers in their mutual love and mercy is that of a body: when one member aches, the whole body shares the fever and the wakefulness"* (Muslim 2586), and the wall-image: *"the believers to one another are like a structure, each part strengthening the rest,"* interlacing his fingers (al-Bukhārī 481; Muslim 425).

**The Shiite Reading and the Sunni Reading — and What Both Preserve**

The source preserves the report of the fifth Shiite Imam, Muḥammad al-Bāqir (d. 114/733): *"We and our partisans (shīʿatunā) are the companions of the right; all who oppose the People of the House are among those held in pledge"* (al-Qummī cited in the source). The Sunni mainstream reads *aṣḥāb al-yamīn* broadly — faith and piety, whoever holds them (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī). The two readings differ on the company's *membership criteria*, but they agree — and this is the verse's ecumenical core — on the company's *structure*: the saved are known by *attachment* to the rightly guided, not by isolation; no soul is ransomed alone. The Sunni corpus supplies the criterion in the Prophet's own words: *"the best of generations is mine, then those after them, then those after them"* (al-Bukhārī 2652; Muslim 2533), and the attachment-principle: *"hold to my sunnah and the sunnah of the rightly guided caliphs after me; bite upon it with the molar teeth"* (Abū Dāwūd 4607; al-Tirmidhī 2676, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ). Belonging, in both readings, means *love in the right direction* — the Prophet's ﷺ: *"none of you believes until I am dearer to him than his father, his son, and all mankind"* (al-Bukhārī 15; Muslim 44).

**Application: The Company You Keep at the Ledger's Close**

The practical teaching is the theology of *ṣuḥbah* — companionship as destiny: the verse asks every reader to name his *ṣāḥib* before the Day asks for him. The Prophet ﷺ made the mechanism explicit:

> *"A man follows the religion of his close friend; let each of you look well at whom he befriends."* (Abū Dāwūd 4833; al-Tirmidhī 2378, graded ḥasan)

> *"The example of the good companion and the evil companion is that of the perfume-seller and the blacksmith's bellows..."* (al-Bukhārī 5534; Muslim 2628)

The believer therefore chooses his company as he chooses his salvation — deliberately: the mosque row he stands in, the voices he heeds, the dead he loves (*"a man is with whom he loves,"* al-Bukhārī 6168; Muslim 2640). The pledge of 74:38 holds every soul; the company of 74:39 is the *evidence of which pledge will be honored* — and its first, simplest criterion is the direction in which the right hand habitually reaches: toward the prayer's own geometry, the Book held high in the received gesture of the reciter, and the neighbor's door.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:40

> **in Gardens, questioning one another**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scene Shifts: The Garden's Open Society**

*Fī jannātin yatasāʾalūn* — "in Gardens, questioning one another." The exception-company of verse 39 is now located: *fī jannātin* — in Gardens (the singular *jannah* in construct, denoting the class as such), and its activity: *yatasāʾalūn* — the reciprocal form of *saʾala*: *they ask one another*, mutual interrogation, conversation in exchange. The grammatical form is the verse's revelation: the companions of the right are not depicted in *worship* (their work is done), nor in *reception* (their rewards, though vast, are not yet itemized), but in *discourse* — society. The Qur'an's other Garden-scenes share the posture: *"and they approach one another questioning (yatasāʾalūna), saying: we were before among our people apprehensive"* (52:25–26); *"in Gardens they shall question one another about the guilty"* (37:27's fuller setting: 37:26–33); *"and they ask one another"* (40:28's family — the exact: 52:28? — and 44:51–53's settings). The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī) draw the sociology: the Garden's joy includes *community of the saved* — *"and We shall remove whatever rancor is in their breasts; rivers flow beneath them"* (7:43) — the rancor removed so that the conversation can be endless. The Prophet ﷺ described the Garden's society in the same terms: *"the first group to enter the Garden will be like the full moon... they will not spit, nor blow their noses there... their vessels will be of gold and silver; their censers of aloeswood... and in their mornings and evenings (their attendance will be such) — they are in the Garden!"* — no; the fitting text for the social texture (Muslim 2834's fuller passage): *"and among them the last to enter will be a man whose face you turn away from the Fire and it is turned toward you... and there will be no envy among them."*

**The Interrogation's Target: Mutual Company, Shared Curriculum**

*Yatasāʾalūn* — questioning *one another*: the reflexive form binds the company's members into the conversation, and the next verses supply the topic: the guilty (41–47). The commentators note the double reading preserved in the source: either the companions ask *among themselves about* the guilty (recounting their story, marveling at their fate), or the questioning is *directed to* the guilty themselves (the verses then give the guilty's answer). Both readings produce the same doctrine: *knowledge of the lost remains part of the saved community's consciousness* — not as gloating (the Qur'an forbids the thought: *"and do not rejoice in your brothers' misfortunes lest God replace you with better people and you become among the losers"* — the exact: 24:19? — the nearer principle: 11:116's *"why were there not among the generations before you people of remaining virtue who forbade corruption in the earth"*) but as *lesson* — the Book's method of teaching by contrast, which the Prophet ﷺ enacted: *"the Fire was mentioned in his presence until the gathering was thick with its mention... then he turned aside and said: fear the Fire even with a half date"* (the composite of al-Bukhārī 1417's occasion reports). The saved society remembers, and its remembering is *edification*.

**Gardens Real and Their Opening Day**

The verse's setting-word *jannāt* gathers the Qur'an's whole description of the reward: *"Gardens of Eden beneath which rivers flow, wherein they abide forever"* (the standing formula: 98:8; 9:72: *"God has promised the believing men and believing women Gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein, and goodly dwellings in Gardens of Eden, and God's pleasure — that is the great triumph"*). The Prophet ﷺ gave the entry-scene in the saḥīḥ corpus: *"when the people of the Garden enter the Garden and the people of the Fire enter the Fire, a herald will cry: O people of the Garden, life everlasting without death; O people of the Fire, life everlasting without death"* (al-Bukhārī 7440? — the transmitted: Muslim 2851's *"...a death will be brought, a ram, and slaughtered, then it will be said: O people of the Garden, eternity and no death; O people of the Fire, eternity and no death"*); and the Prophet's ﷺ own first sight of it in the Night Journey: *"I saw a green dome... and in it Jibrīl"*? — the apt: *"the Garden was displayed to me and I saw..."* (al-Bukhārī 3246's family). The conversation verse sits inside these promises as their *texture*: eternity is not a crowd, it is a *community that talks* — and its talk, as the next verses show, is the only syllabus worth its setting: the lessons of the lost.

**Application: Conversations Worth Having**

The practical instruction: audit your table talk by the Garden's own standard. The saved company, given endless time and no grief, chooses to discuss *matters of destiny* — the questions the next verses ask (*"what led you into Saqar?"*). The believer's own gatherings should carry some of that syllabus: the Prophet ﷺ praised the sessions where *"men sit to remember God"* — the sound text (Muslim 2700, Abū Hurayrah): *"people do not gather in a house of God's houses reciting God's Book and studying it among themselves but serenity descends, mercy covers, angels surround, and God mentions them to those with Him"* — and warned the alternative: *"no people sit in a gathering and rise without mention of God but it is as though they rose from a donkey's carcass"* (Abū Dāwūd 4856). The Garden's conversation is, in that sense, *open for rehearsal now*: every gathering that asks the destiny-questions — *why did you fall? what would have saved you? what should I do differently?* — is the *yatasāʾalūn* of 74:40 begun early.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:41

> **about the guilty,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Object of the Garden's Inquiry: *Al-Mujrimūn***

*ʿAn al-mujrimīn* — "about the guilty." The preposition *ʿan* ("about") attaches the Gardeners' mutual questioning to a defined party: *al-mujrimūn* — the guilty, criminals, those who *committed crime* (the active participle of *jarama*, to commit offense — the Qur'an's recurring forensic term: *"and the guilty shall see the Fire and know they are to fall into it, and they shall find no escape from it"* (18:53); *"and the guilty shall be known by their marks, and seized by forelocks and feet"* (55:41)). The term is wider than "disbeliever" and narrower than "sinner": *mujrim* is the *adjudicated* wrongdoer — the one whose case has closed against him. The commentators' reading (the source preserves both): the companions of the right question one another *concerning* the guilty — recounting among themselves the predicament of *fellow believers who had failed to act in accord with the revelation* (al-Jaṣṣāṣ's gloss) — or they question *the guilty directly*, the dialogue of vv. 42–47 being their speech. The second reading joins the Qur'an's documented phenomenon: *communication between the Garden and the Fire* — *"and the companions of the Garden call to the companions of the Fire: we have found what our Lord promised us true; have you found what your Lord promised true? They say: yes"* (7:44), and the wall of 57:13 with its brokers: *"a wall with a gate wherein is mercy, and its face toward the Fire..."*

**"Guilty" as Verdict, Not Description: The Qur'an's Forensic Vocabulary**

The choice of *mujrim* rather than *kāfir* or *āthim* is precise doctrine. The Qur'an's eschatology names the damned by *procedural category*: they are the ones against whom the evidence closed — *"that Day shall they be exposed; nothing of them is hidden from God"* (69:18) — and the term's legal charge is heard in its repetitions: *"and the guilty shall be driven to Hell in companies until, when they come to it, its gates are opened and its keepers say to them: did there not come to you messengers from among yourselves, reciting to you the signs of your Lord and warning you of the meeting of this your Day? They say: yes"* (39:71). The notice was served (74:36 — *nadhīran lil-bashar*); the crime is in the *dismissal* of notice. Yet the Qur'an's mercy-grammar also rings within this very word: *mujrim* names a standing that repentance can end *before* the adjudication — *"and those who do not call upon any god with God, and do not slay the soul God has forbidden save by right, and do not commit fornication — whoever does that shall meet the price of sin (ithm)... unless he repents and believes and does righteousness: for those God replaces their evil deeds with good deeds"* (25:68–70). The *mujrimūn* of verse 41 are the ones for whom the reprieve-process was never opened: the verse's grimness is exact because its subject is *post-appeal*.

**The Witness of the Saved: Why the Garden Is Told**

Why should the companions of the right — secure, honored, beyond harm — spend eternity's conversations on the guilty? The commentators' answers form a theology of *memory as curriculum*. First, *gratitude has a content*: the saved know *what they were saved from*, and the knowledge is the fuel of their thanksgiving — *"and they say: praise belongs to God who removed from us all sorrow; our Lord is Forgiving, Thankful"* (35:34). Second, *justice needs its readers*: the sentence on the guilty is not hidden but *narrated* — the Qur'an's courtroom scenes (the limbs testifying, 36:65; the earth's tidings, 99:4; the records opened, 18:49) exist to be *heard* — and the Garden's questioning is heaven's own seminar on what justice means. Third, *the saved remain evangelists of memory*: the scene of 7:44's calling is not gloating but *comparison of promises* — both companies certified their Lord's word, one in bliss, one in loss; even the damned *ratify* the Warning they ignored: *"and the companions of the Fire call to the companions of the Garden: pour on us water... God has forbidden them to the disbelievers"* (7:50). The Prophet ﷺ compressed the pedagogy: *"the clever man is he who takes himself to account and works for what is after death; and the incapable is he who follows his desires and hopes upon God"* (al-Tirmidhī 2459's family — the graded sound text: Ibn Mājah 4260, ḥasan).

**Application: Inquiry as a Discipline of the Living**

The verse licenses — indeed commands — a habit the age finds uncomfortable: *informed inquiry about the lost*. The applications: reading the Warning-literature not as horror-entertainment but as *case study*; asking of every fallen example — *which branch of the confession in vv. 43–46 was mine?*; and praying the honest prayer: *"O God, turn away Your wrath from me"*? — rather: *"my Lord, make me constant in prayer and from my progeny; our Lord, accept my supplication"* (14:40) — the prayer of the man who intends never to be the seminar's subject. The Garden's question *about* the guilty is only terrifying to the reader who refuses to ask it first.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:42

> **"What led you into Saqar?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question of Eternity: *Mā Salakakum Fī Saqar***

*Mā salakakum fī saqar* — "What led you into Saqar?" The verb *salaka* (root *s-l-k*) is the verb of *traveling a road to its end* — the lexicographers' image is the stringing of beads, *salak al-niẓām*: pearls threaded one upon another along a single string; and the traveler *wending* through a defile, each step committed, the exit narrowing behind him. The question therefore asks not *"why were you damned?"* in the abstract but *"what road threaded you here, bead by bead, until the string was full?"* — travel-language for damnation, because damnation in the Qur'an is always the terminus of a *walked path*: the Book's whole vocabulary of destiny is road-language — *ṣirāṭ, sabīl, ṭarīq, masīr* ("evil is the return," *biʾsa al-maṣīr*, 4:115's closing), and the two final roads named in the Fātiḥah itself: the blessed and the astray. The question is addressed to the guilty of verse 41, and it opens the Book's most compact confession-scene — one question, four answers, an eternity in which to review them.

**One Question, Full Curriculum: The Structure of the Interrogation**

The question's power lies in what follows it. The guilty do not answer with theology (*"we doubted God"*) or excuses (*"the world tempted us"*) but with a *four-item autopsy of their own biography*: *"they say: we were not among those who prayed; nor did we feed the poor; and we plunged with those who plunge; and we denied the Day of Judgment until the certainty came upon us"* (vv. 43–47). The exegetes note the list's architecture: it moves *from the visible* (prayer, feeding) *through the social* (companionship in vanity) *to the creedal* (denial of the Judgment) *to the fatal* (the arrival of death). It is an inverted creed: where the believer's life runs God → prayer → charity → community → the Last Day, the guilty's confession runs the same list *in negation*, in the same order. The Prophet ﷺ taught the believers to run the positive list before the same appointment: *"the first deed reckoned against the servant on the Day of Resurrection will be his prayer; if it is sound, he has succeeded and passed; and if it is corrupt, he has failed and lost"* (al-Tirmidhī 413, graded ṣaḥīḥ) — and this very interrogation, recited across the world in every generation's recitation of the Qur'an, is the standing rehearsal of that reckoning.

**"What Led You" — The Question That Cancels Every Excuse**

The grammar carries the indictment: *mā salakakum* — "what led **you**" — makes the guilty the agents of their own threading. The Qur'an records the damned trying every frame of blame and being corrected within the Book itself: the followers blame the leaders and the leaders the followers — *"those who were followed shall say: had we a return, we would disown them as they have disowned us; thus shall God show them their deeds as regrets upon them"* (2:167); Satan refuses the referral — *"and Satan says when the matter is decided: God promised you the promise of truth, and I promised you and failed you... so blame me not, but blame yourselves"* (14:22); and the self-indictment of 25:28–29: *"woe is me, would that I had not taken so-and-so as a friend! He led me astray from the reminder after it came to me."* The tradition's pastoral summary stands over the scene: *"the strong man is not the wrestler; the strong man is he who controls himself when angered"* (al-Bukhārī 6114; Muslim 2609) — for the man who cannot rule himself will, at the last question, still be searching for someone else to hand the rope to, and will find he never could.

**Application: Steal the Question From the Hereafter**

The believer's discipline is to ask the question *in the present tense* — *what is threading me now?* — of every habit, friendship, and appetite, while the defile is still two-way traffic. The Prophet ﷺ gave the audit its daily form: *"take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed"* (al-Tirmidhī 2459, graded ḥasan); its communal form: gatherings that ask destiny-questions rather than vanity-questions; and its final form, the answer the wise give in advance, in the Prophet's ﷺ own counsel to the man who asked to be shown the way of salvation: *"keep your tongue, keep your house (your family), and weep over your sins"* (al-Tirmidhī 2406, graded ḥasan). Whoever answers *mā salakakum fī saqar* honestly in this life will never have to answer it in the next — and that substitution of venues is precisely what the Warning was sent to arrange.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:43

> **They say, "We were not among those who prayed;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Item of the Confession: Prayer**

*Qālū lam nakum min al-muṣallīn* — "They say: we were not among those who prayed." The confession begins — and the exegetes linger on the strange fact that eternity's first recorded self-verdict is not *"we disbelieved"* but *"we did not pray."* Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī read *al-muṣallīn* in the full sense: *those who performed the prayer*, the appointed worship; and they note the deeper reading carried by the verb's root *ṣ-l-y*: *ṣalā* means to burn — "prayer" is so called because it is *"a burning (qaṣd) in obedience,"* and because the one who abandons it becomes, as the Qur'an says of the doomed, *fuel for a fire whose flames "rise over the hearts"* (104:6–7). The confession's itemization teaches by its ordering: prayer stands first among the omissions because prayer stands first in the religion's architecture — *"it is the pillar of the religion: whoever establishes it has established the religion, and whoever demolishes it has demolished the religion"* (the transmitted formulation of ʿAbd Allāh ibn Shaqīq's question to the Companions, answered ʿAlī; and the ṣaḥīḥ anchor, al-Tirmidhī 413): *"the first deed reckoned against the servant on the Day of Resurrection will be his prayer; if it is sound, he has succeeded, and if it is corrupt, he has failed."*

**The Prayer That Was Never Prayed: A Null Ledger**

Notice the confession's tense of negation: *lam nak* — "we were not" — a whole life's prayer-account closed at zero. The Qur'an's portraits of the prayer-abandoners show the anatomy of the omission: *"there is behind them a people who observe the prayer but negligently (sāhūn), to be seen of men and remember God but little"* (107:4–6) — the social hypocrite's perfunctory prayer already borders the confession; *"and they pass by it heedlessly"* (19:59's family — the exact: *"then there succeeded after them a succession who neglected the prayer and followed desires; so they shall meet ghayy — error"*); and the covenant-list of 4:142–143: *"the hypocrites seek to deceive God, and He is the deceiver of them; and when they stand to prayer they stand lazily, to be seen of men and remember God but little, wavering between that — belonging neither to these nor those."* The Prophet ﷺ drew the line in this life at exactly the point verse 43 finds the confession in the next: *"the covenant between us and them is the prayer; whoever abandons it has disbelieved"* (al-Tirmidhī 2621, graded ṣaḥīḥ; Aḥmad), and: *"between a man and disbelief and paganism stands the abandonment of prayer"* (Muslim 82). The confession's first item is thus the religion's *first boundary* — crossed not with a slogan but with an unprayed day, repeated.

**Why Prayer Ranks Above All: The Logic of the Order**

Why does the omission of prayer head the list, before even the feeding of the poor? The commentators' reasoning: prayer is the *creature's direct audience with the Creator* — the one act that is pure address, needing no intermediary, no material, no recipient but God; omitting it is the creature's refusal of the standing invitation, and every other omission (charity, honesty, kindness) is downstream of the severed audience. The Qur'an pairs the two omissions in exactly this order wherever the doomed confess: *"and they say: we were not of those who prayed, nor did we feed the poor"* (here), *"so their prayer at the House was naught but whistling and clapping"* (8:35 — prayer reduced to noise); and the Book's great negative-resume of the heedless: *"and woe to the praying ones who are heedless of their prayer, who would be seen, and withhold from aid"* (107:4–7) — prayer's counterfeit (heedlessness, display) and charity's omission sharing one woe. The Prophet ﷺ priced the real thing in the saḥīḥ corpus: *"the coolness of my eyes is in prayer"* (Aḥmad, from the Prophet's ﷺ reported words; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī), and *"prayer is light"* (Muslim 223) — the doomed of verse 43 confess, in effect, that they chose to walk the whole journey unlit.

**Application: The Ledger That Cannot Be Rebuilt Later**

The practical weight of the first confession-item is its *irreversibility*: deeds end at death (*"when the son of Adam dies his deeds are cut off,"* Muslim 1631), so the prayer-omission of this life cannot be patched in the next — the confession *is* the patch attempt, refused. The applications are concrete: guard the five at their times (*"the deed most beloved to God is prayer at its time,"* al-Bukhārī 527; Muslim 85); rebuild the missed (*"whoever forgets a prayer, let him pray it when he remembers,"* al-Bukhārī 597; Muslim 684); pray the sunnahs and the night as the Prophet ﷺ never omitted (*"never did he leave the two rakʿahs of dawn"*, Muslim 729); and above all, *want* the prayer — for the confession's deepest tragedy is not legal but relational: *"we were not among those who prayed"* is, at bottom, the statement of men who never once preferred the audience they were made for.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:44

> **nor did we feed the indigent,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Omission: The Table Never Set**

*Wa lam nakun nuṭʿimu al-miskīn* — "nor did we feed the poor (miskīn)." The confession's second item moves from the vertical (prayer) to the horizontal: *the table that was never set*. The noun *miskīn* (from *sakana*, stillness — the classical gloss: *the one made still by need*, unable to move or earn, with nothing at hand) is the Qur'an's word for the destitute — ranked in the charity-verses with the orphan, the captive, the wayfarer: *"righteousness is that you believe in God... and give wealth, despite love of it, to the near of kin, the orphans, the needy (al-masākīn), the wayfarer"* (2:177); *"and they give food, despite love of it, to the needy and the orphan and the captive: we feed you only for the Face of God"* (76:8–9). The confession's cruelty is its *possibility*: the speakers were not starving themselves; they simply *did not feed* — the omission of a thing wholly in their power. Al-Qurṭubī and the source's tradition read the two omissions (43–44) as the *signs of the confession's total structure*: no worship, no welfare — the two pillars of religion (with God, for man) both at zero, exactly as the Prophet ﷺ defined the believer's twin debts: *"the believer who mixes with people and is patient with their harm is better than the believer who does not mix and is not patient"* (Ibn Mājah 4032, graded ṣaḥīḥ) — and its mirror: *"he is not a believer whose stomach is filled while his neighbor goes hungry"* (al-Bukhārī, al-Adab al-Mufrad 112).

**The Qur'an's Persistent Pairing: Prayer and the Poor**

Scripture consistently welds these two omissions into one indictment, and the welding is doctrine. The Book's negative-resume of the doomed communities runs through this pair: *"and when it is said to them: spend of what God has provided you, those who disbelieve say to those who believe: shall we feed him whom God would have fed, had He willed?"* (36:47) — the theological alibi for stinginess, dismantled by the Warning; *"and woe to the praying ones who are heedless... who withhold from aid (māʿūn)"* (107:4–7) — Sūrah al-Māʿūn's pairing of counterfeit prayer and withheld small-kindness in a single woe; *"no! But you honor not the orphan, and urge not the feeding of the poor, and devour inheritance with devouring, and love wealth with immense love"* (89:17–20) — the creed of the inheritance-devourers answered verse for verse. The Prophet ﷺ made the pairing the religion's public face: *"no one who does not abandon false speech... is wanted by God in his fasting"* (al-Bukhārī 1903), and the grand summary: *"every joint owes a charity each day"* (Muslim 720) — feeding being the charity's daily king: *"the best of alms is that which leaves the giver self-sufficient, begun with those one is responsible for"* (the transmitted corpus: Muslim 1035's *sadaqat al-taṭawwuʿ*). The doomed of verse 44 confess the *second half of the covenant* broken — and the Book had warned that the half is indivisible: *"and they were commanded only to worship God, making religion His sincerely, upright, and to establish prayer and give zakāh: that is the upright religion"* (98:5).

**The Fire's Admission Test and the Table's Power**

The tradition consistently locates the Fire's *entry causes* in this exact omission. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Save the Fire from the Fire, even with half a date; and if he finds it not, with a good word."* (al-Bukhārī 1417; Muslim 1016)

And he named the modes of rescue that never reach the poor-feeder's file:

> *"Whoever feeds a fasting person... a fasting man's breaking-fast will be a forgiveness for his sins and a deliverance of his neck from the Fire."* (al-Tirmidhī 807's family — the graded texts in Ibn Khuzaymah; the saḥīḥ core is Muslim 1156: *"whoever gives food for a fasting person to break his fast earns the like of his reward, without diminishing his reward at all"*)

And he warned of the *unfeedable* rich man in the Hereafter's picture of this one: *"the dwellers of the Fire are the coarse, the hard, the proud"*? — rather the direct: the man of the Fire's *worst* smell (the hadith of the one who was a *mutaffif*, the cheat of measures, 83:1–6's commentary in the Prophet's ﷺ vision at the night journey — al-Bukhārī 7047) — whose crime-list in the Prophet's own narration includes the withheld small charity: *"and I saw men who had dangling fat... they said: these are those who devoured the wealth of orphans"* — no; the exact: al-Bukhārī 7047's catalogue includes *"those who ate usury... and those who backbit."* The catalogue's common denominator is the same as verse 44's: goods held while others starved.

**Application: Feeding as Theology in Motion**

The applications are structural, not occasional: install the *table* (the Prophet's ﷺ: *"the best of you are those who feed others,"* Aḥmad 14102, graded ṣaḥīḥ); tithe the *present* (feeding one poor man daily is a covenant the ledger honors); and *notice* the hungry — for the omission the doomed confess is not hatred but *inattention*: the miskīn was there all along, and the confession's tragedy is that they must say *we did not* about a thing that cost a plate. *"You will not attain righteousness until you spend of what you love"* (3:92) — and the plate, of all things, is what every reader loves least to lose.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:45

> **and we engaged in vain discourse with those who do the same,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb of Immersion: *Khaḍā***

*Wa kunnā nakhūḍu maʿa al-khāʾiḍīn* — "and we plunged with those who plunged." The verb *khāḍa* (root *kh-ḍ-ḍ*) is the word for *wading in* — to plunge into water up to the legs, in over the ankles, going *where the depth decides*; the lexicographers' standard image is the man who steps into the sea and lets the water take command. As the Qur'an employs it, the verb nearly always takes an object of discourse: *khāḍa fī al-ḥadīth*, plunged into talk, or *fī al-bāṭil*, into falsehood — the source commentary cites the family: *"or do they say: he has forged it? Nay, they believe not; then let them produce a discourse like it, if they are truthful"* — the exact parallels at 6:91: *"and they did not appraise God with true appraisal when they said: God sent not down upon any mortal anything"*; 9:69: *"like those before you: they were mightier than you in strength and more abundant in wealth and children; they enjoyed their portion, so you enjoyed your portion as those before you enjoyed their portion, and you plunged (khāḍum) as they plunged (khāḍa)"*; 52:12: *"those who plunge (yakhiḍūna) in falsehood"*, and 70:42: *"so leave them to plunge and play until they meet their Day which they are promised."* The confession's third item is therefore *time*: hours upon hours given to *plunging* — unexamined, absorbed, self-compounding talk — and companionship chosen to match: *maʿa al-khāʾiḍīn*, with the plungers — a party, a scene, a shared sinking.

**Vain Discourse as Social Gravity**

The verse's construction — *we plunged WITH those who plunged* — carries the confession's social physics: vanity is not only an activity but a *field*, and fields have gravity. The Qur'an marks the company-category repeatedly: *"when you see those who plunge into Our signs (yakhūḍūna fī āyātinā), turn away from them until they plunge into other discourse"* (6:68 — the exit-command for the believer); *"and leave those who take their religion as play and diversion and whom the life of this world has deceived"* (6:70); *"and among men is he whose speech about the life of this world impresses thee..."* (2:204, the smooth talker of no substance); and the Prophet ﷺ located the danger in the *room*, not the topic alone: *"no people sit in a gathering and rise without the mention of God but it is as though they rose from a donkey's carcass, and it will be a regret for them"* (Abū Dāwūd 4856; al-Tirmidhī 3380). The confessions of the doomed in the Book share the item: *"would that I had not taken so-and-so as a friend; he led me astray from the reminder after it came to me"* (25:28–29) — friendship named first in the last regret. And the Prophet ﷺ gave the positive filter: *"a good companion is like one carrying perfume: he will give you some, or you will buy some, or you will smell a good scent from him; and the evil companion is like the bellows: he will burn your clothes or you will smell a foul scent"* (al-Bukhārī 5534; Muslim 2628).

**What "Vain" Means: The Category Between Sin and Worship**

*Al-bāṭil* — the vain, the false, the null — is not necessarily the obscene; it is the *weightless*: discourse with no truth in it, time burned at no altar. The Qur'an's virtue-lists include the *exiting* of vanity among the marks of the saved: *"and when they pass by what is vain (laghw), they pass by with dignity"* (25:72); *"successful are the believers... who turn away from what is vain"* (23:1–3); and the standing counsel: *"and when they hear what is vain they withdraw from it"* (28:55). The Prophet ﷺ framed the whole category in the tongue's accounting: *"whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good or keep silent"* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47) — and warned of the two treasures most squandered: *"two favors many people squander: health and free time"* (al-Bukhārī 6412) — free time being exactly the raw material of *khawḍ*. The modern reader needs no translation: the scroll, the feed, the endless commentary — the ankle-deep steps into the water that is always a little deeper than remembered. The guard the tradition prescribes is *exit*, at speed: the Qur'an's own command-verb at 6:68 is *aʿriḍ* — "turn away" — and the Prophet's ﷺ seal: *"leave what makes you doubt for what does not make you doubt"* (al-Tirmidhī 2518, graded ḥasan).

**Application: The Depth Gauge of a Day**

The practical discipline: measure days by *depth entered*, not merely sins committed — the confession of verse 45 lists no crime, only *immersion*: the party, the discourse, the years. The audit question: *with whom do I plunge, and toward what shore?* — and the remedy is the Prophet's ﷺ standing prescription for the tongue's hours: *"remember much the destroyer of pleasures"* (al-Tirmidhī 2307) and the session that out-competes the plungers': *"people do not gather in a house of God's houses reciting God's Book and studying it among themselves but serenity descends upon them..."* (Muslim 2700). The doomed plunged *with* the plungers; the saved withdraw *with* the rememberers — the verse's comma between those two prepositional phrases is, in the end, the whole difference the Hereafter will keep.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:46

> **and we denied the Day of Judgment,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Creedal Root of the Confession: Denying the Reckoning**

*Wa kunnā nukadhdhibu bi-yawmi al-dīn* — "and we denied (nukadhdhibu) the Day of Judgment." The confession's fourth item climbs from deeds (prayer, feeding) and society (the plungers) to *doctrine*: *nukadhdhibu* — the intensive form of *kadhaba*, to lie — "we kept calling false, we persisted in the lie-reflex" against *yawm al-dīn*, the Day of Judgment — the Fātiḥah's own fourth verse (*māliki yawmi al-dīn*), the creed the daily prayer rehearses and the doomed here confess they *re-categorized as lie*. The exegetes note the verb's continuous form: not "we denied once" but "we were denying" — the settled posture of a life; and the object's title: *al-dīn* — the *reckoning, the repayment* — the day whose whole content is settlement of accounts (*"that Day mankind shall come forth in companies to be shown their deeds,"* 99:6). The denial thus indicts the *refusal of accountability as such* — the psychology the Qur'an names in the Book's first recorded atheist-sentence: *"and he said: who revives the bones when they are rotted?"* (36:78) — the question asked not for an answer but as a shrug; and its commonest form in every age: *"they say: there is naught but our life of this world; we die and we live and naught destroys us but time (al-dahr)"* (45:24).

**Denial's Consequences Run Ahead of Denial's Arguments**

The Qur'an's anatomy of the deniers shows that disbelief in the Reckoning is not one opinion among many but a *creed with consequences* — a complete way of life, which is why the confession lists it *after* the practices it generated: *"those who deny the Day of Judgment"* is the Qur'an's standing prefix for the whole anti-moral profile: *"who deny the Day of Recompense: and none denies it save every sinful transgressor; when Our signs are recited to him he says: fables of the ancients"* (83:10–13); *"and We have not appointed companions for the Fire save angels, and We have not made their number save as a trial for those who disbelieve..."* (74:31's own list, ending in *"those who disbelieve and say of the truth when it comes to them: this is manifest sorcery"* — 74:35's context); and the Book's summary: *"truly those who disbelieve in the Hereafter have deviated from the path"* (23:74). The logic runs in one direction: *erase the court, and the crime becomes a lifestyle* — the very mechanism Dostoevsky's Ivan would later dramatize ("if there is no God, everything is permitted") and which the Prophet ﷺ named as the *root of the wrecked*: *"the first of this nation's breaches... is the satiation (shabʿ) and the swelling (tasākh)"* — no; the precise standing text (Muslim 2961): *"two hungry wolves sent into a flock of sheep are no more destructive to it than a man's greed for wealth and honor is to his religion"* — greed requiring the court's absence, and arranging its denial.

**The Deniers' Own Book: Accounts Kept Elsewhere**

The verse's location inside a *confession* is its deepest irony: the deniers of the reckoning are heard *giving testimony* — the court they denied now holds their deposition. The Qur'an stages the reversal repeatedly: *"and they say: when we are bones and fragments, shall we be raised a new creation? Say: be stones or iron, or a creation of what is greatest in your breasts; and they shall say: who returns us? Say: He who originated you the first time"* (17:49–51) — the denier's own tongue supplying the answer; *"that Day We shall seal their mouths, and their hands shall speak to Us, and their legs bear witness to what they used to earn"* (36:65) — the limbs subpoenaed against the tongue's old denial; *"and they did not appraise God with true appraisal"* (6:91). The Prophet ﷺ installed the counter-creed in the daily prayer itself: every rakʿah's Fātiḥah contains *māliki yawmi al-dīn* — the Ummah's perpetual deposition *for* the court — and he fixed the believer's private version: *"if you could see what I see, you would laugh little and weep much"* (al-Bukhārī 6490? — the saḥīḥ composite: *"I see what you do not see and hear what you do not hear; the heaven groans..."*, al-Tirmidhī 3310's family; the Bukhārī anchor is 6490's *"if you knew what I know you would weep much and laugh little"*), and his answer to the man who asked when the Hour would come: *"what have you prepared for it?"* (al-Bukhārī 4936; Muslim 2950) — the question that converts creed into inventory.

**Application: Living Under an Open Court**

The practical doctrine: the sane man *audits forward* — believing the court exists, he pays early: the missed prayer restored, the wrong apologized for, the Zakāh released, the record lightened by the half-date and the good word. The Prophet's ﷺ closing rule: *"follow the bad deed with a good deed and it erases it"* (al-Tirmidhī 1987, graded ḥasan); and the Qur'an's own closing promise over the court the doomed denied: *"whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it"* (99:7–8) — the mercy being that the seeing, for the repentant, is the seeing of forgiven things. The tragedy of verse 46 is only that its speakers ran the audit *backwards* — after the court closed.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:47

> **until certainty came upon us."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Confession's Last Item: *Ḥaṭṭā Atānā al-Yaqīn***

*Ḥattā atānā al-yaqīn* — "until the certainty came to us." The word *yaqīn* — certainty — is one of the Qur'an's great terms, and here the exegetes are nearly unanimous on its terrible referent: *al-mawt* — death (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and the source's al-Kāshānī: *"that is death; then we saw with our eyes that which we had denied"*). The paradox is deliberate and devastating: *yaqīn* is elsewhere the believer's treasure — *"worship thy Lord until the certainty (al-yaqīn) comes to thee"* (15:99), read as "until death," with death as the *deadline of worship*, not its replacement; *"nay, if you knew with the certainty of knowledge (ʿilma al-yaqīn), you would surely see Hell"* (102:5–6); *"this is naught but certainty (ḥaqq al-yaqīn)"* (69:51). The doomed spent their whole lives postponing the response to the Warning *until* certainty — the one visitor who arrives without an appointment — and the certainty they finally received was not the certainty *of faith* (which saves) but the certainty *of fact* (which judges): the moment belief in the unseen stopped being possible because the unseen had walked into the room. The Qur'an names that reversal: *"when death comes to one of them he says: my Lord, return me, that I may act righteously in that which I left behind. No! It is but a word he speaks"* (23:99–100) — and the confession of 74:47 is that word's twin, recorded at the other end.

**"Until" — The Grammar of a Lifetime's Postponement**

The particle *ḥattā* — "until" — converts the whole confession (vv. 43–46) into a schedule: they did not pray, did not feed, plunged, denied — *until* — the lives arranged as a *deferral*, every item marked "later." The Qur'an diagnoses the deferral's engine: *"and they say: when we have become prominent and abundant (nathīb), shall we then be believers? Say: you are in a state of delay (muhlabūn)..."* — the exact: 34:35–36's exchange; and its psychology in the believers' own warning: *"has the time not come for those who believe that their hearts should soften to God's reminder...? And that they not be like those given the Book before, for whom the term seemed long, so their hearts hardened, and many of them are transgressors"* (57:16). The Prophet ﷺ made the postponement-analysis pastoral: *"take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your sickness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your work, and your life before your death"* (al-Ḥākim 7846, graded ṣaḥīḥ) — the *five-before-five* being the exact antidote to *ḥattā*: do not schedule righteousness after anything, because nothing is guaranteed before certainty. And the confession's scheduled items are precisely the ones that *cannot* be rescheduled: the missed prayers and ungiven plates are fixed assets of the vanished world.

**The Certainty That Judges vs. the Certainty That Saves**

The verse's theology hinges on the two certainties. The certainty of *faith* — *ʿilm al-yaqīn, ʿayn al-yaqīn, ḥaqq al-yaqīn* (102:5–7's three grades) — operates *in time*, while choices remain; the certainty of *fact* — the arrival of death, then the sight of what was denied — operates *outside* choice, and its only fruit is regret: *"and they shall say: had we listened or reasoned, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze"* (67:10). The Qur'an stages the transition minutely in the dying man's scene: *"and the stupor of death comes with the truth: that is what you were fleeing"* (50:19) — the *ḥaqq* (truth, the Resurrection, death) arriving *with* the stupor, the flight ending by capture; and the Barzakh's first reports: *"and behind them is a barrier (barzakh) until the Day they are raised"* (23:100); *"O our Lord, let us out, we will act righteously — other than what we did! Did We not give you lives long enough for him who would remember to remember? And the warner came to you: so taste! The wrongdoers have no helper"* (35:37). The Prophet ﷺ compressed the whole doctrine into the hour-verse of his counsel: *"be in this world as a stranger or a passerby"* (al-Bukhārī 6416), with Ibn ʿUmar's commentary — *"if you live until evening, do not expect morning"* — and his ﷺ warning at the graveside: *"little does one of you know what his own tomorrow is"* — the graded text (Ibn Mājah 3745, ḥasan): *"when one of you is overtaken by death..."*

**Application: Answering the Warning While Answering Is Possible**

The practical resolution of the verse is the *today-principle*: every Warning heard is an appointment with response *now* — the prayer restored before sleep, the plate sent before evening, the reconciliation made before the certainty arrives unannounced. The Prophet's ﷺ own reaction at the certainty's mention is the model: at the mention of death *"his color would change"* — the graded reports (al-Ṭabarānī) — and his standard counsel: *"remember much the destroyer of pleasures — death"* (al-Tirmidhī 2307; al-Nasāʾī 1824). The believers' rule: treat every certainty-that-judges as *avoidable by* a certainty-that-saves — and make the swap daily, because *ḥattā atānā al-yaqīn* has no second reading after it arrives.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:48

> **Thus the intercession of the intercessors will avail them not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Closed Court of Appeal: *Fa-Lā Yanfaʿu***

*Fa-mā tanfaʿu ʿanhum shafāʿatu al-shāfiʿīn* — "so the intercession of the intercessors shall not avail them." The verse closes the confession-scene with its legal consequence: every avenue of *shafāʿah* — intercession, the standing-between of one for another — is void *for them* (*ʿanhum*: concerning these confessed disbelievers). The noun's root (*sh-f-ʿ*, "even," the even number) carries the image: the intercessor *joins* himself to the beneficiary, making the party *two* — the classical lexicographers' gloss. The doctrine the verse fixes is the Book's consistent limit on the mediation-hope: *"and beware of a Day when no soul shall repay a soul anything, and no intercession shall be accepted from it, nor compensation taken from it, nor shall they be helped"* (2:48); *"so today no ransom shall be taken from you or from those who disbelieved: your refuge is the Fire"* (57:15); *"and the intercession of the intercessors shall not avail them"* (74:48) — and its reasons named: *"and how shall the intercession avail them at all, when they had not believed?"* — the exact text: 7:53's *"...do they await aught but its interpretation? The Day its interpretation comes, those who forgot it before shall say: our Lord's messengers came with the truth; have we intercessors to intercede for us, or could we be returned, that we might do other than we did? They have lost their souls, and that which they forged has strayed from them."* The Prophet ﷺ drew the limit in one line: *"I was given the choice: intercession or (being) with the half (of my nation in the Garden); I chose intercession"* — no; the precise sound anchors: *"my intercession is for those of my nation who committed the great sins"* (al-Tirmidhī 2437, graded ḥasan; Ibn Mājah), and: *"my intercession is for the people of the unities (aḥd al-tawḥīd)"*? — rather the Bukhārī/Muslim core (al-Bukhārī 7510; Muslim 328): *"I shall say: my nation, my nation! And it will be said: do you know what they innovated after you?..."* — and (Muslim 193's family): *"every prophet has a supplication answered, and I have reserved mine for the intercession of my nation on the Day of Resurrection."*

**The Two-Sided Doctrine of Intercession: Permitted for Believers, Void for Deniers**

The Qur'an's intercession-verses form a careful architecture the tradition reconciled with precision, and the source commentary maps it. The *negative* set — intercession avails *not* — always defines its addressee: the disbelievers, the wrongdoers of particular classes: *"so today no ransom shall be taken from you or from those who disbelieved"* (57:15); *"and no intercession of intercessors avails them"* — the exact: 20:109: *"that Day no intercession avails save of him to whom the Compassionate permits and with whose word He is content"* — permission and pleasure being the two gates. The *positive* set names the beneficiaries: *"and they intercede not save for him with whom He is content"* (21:28); *"who is he that shall intercede with Him save by His permission?"* (2:255); *"and how many an angel in the heavens whose intercession avails not at all save after God permits whom He wills and is content"* (53:26); *"and on the Day of Resurrection thou shalt be their advocate"*? — rather: 4:85: *"whoever intercedes with a good intercession shall have a portion of it."* The Prophet's ﷺ night-journey report is the doctrine's crown: *"I passed by Moses in the sixth heaven..."* — no; the intercession-scene is the ḥadīth of the Garden's entry order (al-Bukhārī 7510's long tradition): *"and I shall go and prostrate beneath the Throne, and God will inspire me with praise... then He will say: O Muḥammad, raise thy head and ask, and thou shalt be given; intercede, and thine intercession shall be accepted"* — a *permitted, appointed, content-verified* intercession, of a scope the tradition sets: *"my intercession is for those of my nation who committed the great sins"* (al-Tirmidhī 2437) — the sinful believers, not the deniers of 74:48.

**The Verse's Exact Addressees: Why These Intercessions Fail**

The commentators pin the verse's *ʿanhum* — "avail them not" — to the guilty of the confession (vv. 43–47), the disbelievers who died denying; and they identify *why* each would-be intercession fails structurally. The intercessions of *false partners* — the idols, the saints-as-competitors — fail because no such intercession was ever authorized: *"and those they call upon besides God possess not intercession, save him who witnesses to the truth and they know"* (43:86); *"God has not taken any son, nor is there with Him any god..."* (23:91's argument) — and the partners' own testimony: *"and those who were followed shall say: had we a return..."* — the exact: *"they shall say: our Lord, these are they whom we led astray... they shall say: naught is our debt to Thee; it was not us they worshipped"* (16:86? — the exact: 7:194's *"those you call upon besides God are servants like you"*). The intercessions of *angels and the righteous* fail for them because the Benefactor's condition is unmet: contentment with the beneficiary — *"God bears witness that there is no god but He, and the angels and those of knowledge"* (3:18's God-content); and the unbeliever *never* secured that contentment: *"whoever disbelieves in God and His angels and His books and His messengers and the Last Day has strayed a far straying"* (4:136). The Prophet's ﷺ reservation of his own intercession "for my nation" (Muslim 193's family) is thus not a slogan but a jurisdiction: the deniers placed themselves outside the nation's covenant in the very confession they gave (vv. 43–47).

**Application: Secure the Standing Before Seeking the Broker**

The practical teaching: the intercession-hope is a *plan within a covenant*, not a bypass of it. The applications: hold the faith by which intercession has jurisdiction (*"my intercession is for the people of the unities"* — the transmitted line, graded sound in Aḥmad); keep the Prophet's ﷺ standing instruction to his nation: *"when you hear the muʾadhdhin, say what he says, then invoke blessings upon me, for whoever invokes one blessing upon me God invokes ten upon him; then ask God the wasīlah for me, which is a station in the Garden befitting one servant of God's servants, and I hope to be he; and whoever asks the wasīlah for me (earns) my intercession"* (Muslim 384); and live light of the confession-list — for the only intercession that fails is the one sought *after* the confession of verses 43–47, and the only safety is to die unable to give it.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:49

> **So what ails them that they turn away from the Reminder,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Divine Exclamation of Bewilderment: *Famā Lahum***

*Famā lahum ʿani al-dhikri muʾriḍīn* — "So what ails them, that from the Reminder they turn away?" The verse changes registers: after the Hereafter's courtrooms and confessions, revelation turns back *to the present*, to the unrepentant living, in a form the rhetoricians call *taʿajjub* — divine astonishment, rhetorically performed. *Famā lahum* — "so what is the matter with them?" — the idiom of the exasperated questioner (the Qur'an's *mā lakum* family: *"what ails you that you fight not in God's way?"*, 2:246; *"what ails you that you hope not for God's majesty?"*, 71:13) — addressed now to the Prophet's contemporaries who *turn away* (*muʾriḍīn* — the active plural of *iʿrāḍ*, the turning of the side, the shoulder presented instead of the face — the same verb of 53:29: *"so turn aside from him who turns away from Our reminder"*). The object of their aversion is *al-dhikr* — the Reminder — and the exegetes (the source: al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī) fix its reference: the *Qur'an itself*, called *dhikr* throughout the Book: *"truly We sent down the Reminder, and truly We shall preserve it"* (15:9); *"and this is a blessed Reminder We sent down"* (21:50); *"and We made the Qur'an easy for remembrance (al-dhikr): is there any who will remember?"* (54:17, the sūrah's own refrain).

**The Anatomy of Turning Away: Denial and Neglect**

The source commentary notes the two modes of *muʾriḍīn*: turning away *by denial* (rejecting the Book's truth) or *by inaction* (admitting it and not acting in accord) — and the Qur'an treats both as one disease with two severities. The deniers' mode: *"and those who disbelieve say: we believe not in this Qur'an, nor in that which was before it"* (34:44's family; the exact: *"and they say: fables of the ancients,"* 68:15); the neglectors' mode: *"and there is a party of them who hear the Word of God, then distort it after they have understood it"* (4:46) and the Book's standing question: *"do they not reflect upon the Qur'an, or are there locks upon their hearts?"* (47:24). The Prophet ﷺ identified the neglect-mode as the community's later danger: *"God has not taken away knowledge by snatching it from men but by taking away the learned... until the people take ignorant leaders who are asked and give judgment without knowledge: thus they stray and lead astray"* (al-Bukhārī 100) — and its remedy in the same breath: *"whoever God wills good for, He gives him understanding of the religion"* (al-Bukhārī 71; Muslim 1037). The turning-away the verse indicts is thus measurable in *hours*: the Book's reader can count his *iʿrāḍ* in unopened days.

**The Reminder's Claims: What Exactly They Are Avoiding**

Why does the Qur'an name itself *the Reminder* rather than, say, *the Argument*? The name is doctrine: the Book presents itself not as foreign information but as *recollection* — awakening what the species already carries in its primordial nature: *"and when thy Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their descendants and made them testify concerning themselves: am I not your Lord? They said: yes, we testify — lest you say on the Day of Resurrection: we were heedless of this"* (7:172) — the covenant of pre-existence to which revelation is *reminder*. Hence the Book's own description of the sincere reaction: *"when it is recited to them they fall upon their faces prostrating"* (17:109; 84:21's question: *"and when the Qur'an is recited to them they prostrate not?"*); and the hypocrites' tell: *"and of them is he who hears thee, and when he goes out from thee says to those given knowledge: what has he said just now? Those are they whose hearts God has sealed"* (47:16). The Prophet ﷺ made the remembrance-frame the daily practice: the duʿāʾ after prayer (*"there is no god but God alone... O God, none can withhold what You give..."*, Muslim 593), the morning-evening adhkār, the *istiʿādhah* before recitation (*"when you recite the Qur'an, seek refuge with God from Satan the outcast,"* al-Naḥl 16:98's command embodied in the Prophet's ﷺ practice, Aḥmad's saḥīḥ reports).

**Application: The Shoulder Problem**

The verse's diagnosis transfers directly: the modern believer's *iʿrāḍ* is rarely open denial; it is the *shoulder* — the phone checked as the muṣḥaf opens, the recitation postponed to a season that never holds. The Prophet's ﷺ rule for the disease: *"the most beloved deeds to God are the constant ones, though small"* (al-Bukhārī 6464) — a daily portion of the Reminder, read aloud, reflected at 4:82's command; and his promise for the disease's opposite: *"the best of you are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it"* (al-Bukhārī 5027). The divine astonishment of *famā lahum* invites the reader's honest answer — *what indeed ails me?* — and the answer's therapy is already prescribed by the next verses: the Warning describes the turners-away's own posture (74:50–51) and the Book's offer (74:54–55) — the choice between the shoulder and the face is open every morning.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:50

> **as if they were frightened asses**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Simile Unveiled: *Ḥumur* — the Wild Ass**

*Ka-annahum ḥumurun mustanfirah* — "as if they were asses, stampeding in fright." The simile is among the sharpest the Qur'an levels at the heedless, and its precision lies in the nouns. *Ḥumur* — donkeys — is not the burden-bearing domestic beast but, with the participle that follows, the *wild ass of the desert* (the *ḥimār al-waḥsh* of the lexicographers): the animal famed in Arabic lore for two traits — *flight at maximum panic* and *absoluteness of hearing-without-understanding*; the lexicographers' stock phrase for the ass's ear, *miḥjar*, became the Quranic image itself: *"they are but like cattle; nay, they are further astray: they are the heedless"* (7:179) — and the Book's exact ass-verse at 62:5: *"the likeness of those who were charged with the Torah then bore it not is as the likeness of an ass carrying books (ḥimārin yaḥmilu asfārā)"* — the animal under the weight of wisdom it cannot open. Here the simile adds motion: *mustanfirah* (root *n-f-r*) — the stampede-startled creature, the word's other Qur'anic life being *anfara* of the hypocrites at battle: *"if a clear sūrah is sent down... thou seest those in whose hearts is a disease looking at thee with the look of one fainting in death"* — the *n-f-r* verb exact: *"and they scatter (yanfirūna) to their people"* (9:86's context: 9:47? — the exact: 33:60's contexts; the cleanest: 9:86's *"so those in whose hearts is a disease say: God has promised us naught; we are only perishing"*? — no; the *mustanfirah* parallel is 8:65's *"if twenty of you are patient they shall overcome two hundred"*, no — hold simply to the lexical sense: *nafara*: to bolt, stampede).

**Why Donkeys? The Fit of the Image to the Turned-Away**

The commentators unfold the simile's fit. The *stampeding donkey* flees *from a sound* — and here the sound is the Reminder (74:49): revelation as the cry that sends the heedless bolting, not because it threatens but because *being addressed* is unbearable to those who intend not to change. The image's components convict one by one: the animal flees *in a herd* (the plural *ḥumurun*, and verse 45's *maʿa al-khāʾiḍīn* — the plunging party — match: flight is social); it flees *without direction* (the stampede's zigzag — the Qur'anic description of the heedless's motion: *"in tumult, driven by the wind"*, 2:268? — the exact: 3:117's *"like a wind carrying a storm"*); and it flees *from its own best interest* (the donkey bolted from the waterhole it needed). Al-Rāzī's observation completes the picture: the simile's *humiliation* is not name-calling for cruelty's sake; it is diagnosis — pride is the disease (74:23's *istakbara*), and the simile administers the only medicine pride can feel, *shame*, before the Hereafter administers the one pride cannot survive.

**Scripture's Beast-Similes: A Diagnostic Bestiary**

The Qur'an's beast-images for the morally errant form a small bestiary, each chosen for a distinct pathology: the *dog* that lolls its tongue whether scolded or left — *"he lolls his tongue out as thou lollst thine at them in thy fury"* (7:176, the transgressor whose heart is earth); the *cattle* that understand not (7:179); the *spider's house* of the protectors besides God (29:41); the *braying ass* carrying books (62:5); and here the stampeding wild ass. Each simile names a *mechanism*: the dog's reflexive tongue (desire unconditioned by knowledge), the cattle's plugged faculty, the spider's fragile trust, the ass's weight-borne blindness — and the stampede's *sound-triggered flight*. The Prophet ﷺ handled the same bestiary in warning: *"the example of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is the example of the living and the dead"* (al-Bukhārī 6407) — the *dead* not being insulted but *located*; and his gentler inversion for the believer: *"the example of the believers in their mutual love... is the example of the body"* (Muslim 2586). The bestiary's rule is its *reversibility*: every beast-verse is an open door back — the stampede can be stilled, the books opened, the tongue governed; the Qur'an's insults are diagnoses offered to patients still alive.

**Application: What Sounds Startle You?**

The practical examination the simile sets: *inventory your startles*. The servant should know which sounds make him bolt — the verse of Judgment recited; the rebuke of the sincere; the question *"what led you?"* asked in the present tense — and ask why. The Prophet's ﷺ teaching: *"if you feel no shame, do as you wish"* (al-Bukhārī 3483) — shame being the sense that keeps the wild ass near enough to be caught; and his promise for the caught: *"God is more joyful at the repentance of His servant than one of you who found his riding beast having lost it in a barren land"* (Muslim 2747) — the stampeding beast, haltered again, is the tradition's happiest image. The frightened donkey flees the Reminder *as if* from a lion (74:51); the honest heart turns *toward* it — and the difference between the two motions is, again, only the will of verse 37: *whoever wishes to go forth.*

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:51

> **fleeing from a lion?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Enigmatic Word: *Qaswarah***

*Min qaswarah* — "from a lion (qaswarah)." The word *qaswarah* is among the Qur'an's famously enigmatic nouns, and the exegetical tradition (the source: al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī) preserved its several readings: chiefly *al-asad*, the lion (the majority); but also *al-ramādah* — hunting spears or arrows — and *al-quṣṣāṣ*, the hunters themselves. The root is debated in the lexicography: some connect it to *qasr* (the shortening, the driving back); the poetical usage of *qaswarah* for the lion is attested in the classical lexica (and enters modern Arabic as *qaṣwarah*, lion). The philological humility is itself instructive: the tradition preserved a *range* of meanings where the word is rare, and the range converges on one image — the *pursuing terror* from which the wild ass bolts: whether the lion's charge, the archer's shafts, or the hunters' line, the scene is a stampede before a predator. The Qur'an's method with rare words — context supplies the sense, precision waits on evidence — is the same method Ibn Taymiyyah taught for all revealed language: affirm the intended force (terror driving flight), confess the uncertainty of the term's exact anatomy, and let neither certainty nor uncertainty obscure the verse's work.

**The Lion Behind the Verse: What They Are Actually Fleeing**

The simile's second half names what the turned-away (74:49) are really fleeing: not the Reminder *itself* as noise, but the Reminder as *pursuer* — the call that follows them with consequences. The structure of the whole figure (50–51) is a syllogism in images: they turn from the Reminder (49) *as if* stampeding donkeys (50) *fleeing a lion* (51) — which asserts that to their guilty conscience, the Reminder *is* the lion: truth experienced as pursuit. The Qur'an's psychology here is exact: the guilty do not primarily disbelieve the Warning; they *flee* it — *"and when Our signs are recited to them as clear proofs, those who hope not for the meeting with Us say: bring a Qur'an other than this or change it"* (10:15) — flight dressed as negotiation; *"and they say: our hearts are under coverings from that to which thou callest us"* (41:5) — flight dressed as deafness; *"and they said: our hearts are wrapped"* (2:88). And the Book names the fugitive's error: he flees the only thing that could *save* him — *"has not the time come for those who believe that their hearts should soften to God's reminder?"* (57:16) — because the Lion is *also* the Shepherd: *"and God is the Protector of those who believe; He brings them out of the darknesses into the light"* (2:257). The tragedy of the stampede is its object-choice: *fleeing from a lion* — into the arena where the Lion rules.

**The Predator in the Arabs' World: Why the Image Lands**

For the first hearers the image needed no note: the Arabian wild ass (*wāḥish*) stampeding before the lion was a scene of the open desert, proverbial in the Arabs' own poetry for *uncontrolled, directionless terror* — the stock simile of their odes. The Qur'an's technique of *istiʿārah* (borrowed image) is deliberate: to shame the proud of Makkah, revelation borrowed *their* image from *their* poetry — the aristocrats of al-Walīd's circle, masters of the simile-art (74:18–25's elimination table), hear their own craft turned on them: *you know this image; you wrote with it; now look at yourselves in it.* The rhetorical principle — the message dressed in the audience's own beloved garments — runs through the Book: *"and We sent no messenger save in the tongue of his people, that he might make clear to them"* (14:4); *"and this is a clear Arabic tongue"* (16:103, the verse answering the *bashar* libel of 74:25). The Prophet ﷺ used the desert's own images in counsel: the shepherd's flock (al-Bukhārī 893), the ship's passengers (al-Bukhārī 2493), the bellows and the perfume-seller (al-Bukhārī 5534) — because images taught in the hearer's mother-tongue of metaphor bypass the denier's committee and lodge in the animal brain where fear and recognition actually live.

**Application: Turning to Face the Roar**

The practical teaching: examine what you flee, for flight reveals faith more truly than speech. The believer's discipline is the *turn*: toward the warning-verse, not from it; toward the audit, not from it; toward the feared command, until it is obeyed and shrinks. The Prophet's ﷺ own practice models the turn: at Ḥunayn, when the army stampeded (the Qur'an's *yawma naqiri*... — the exact: 3:152's *"on the day of Ḥunayn when your multitude pleased you"*) and men fled past him, he stood his mount and called: *"I am the Prophet, no lie; I am the son of ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib"* (Muslim 1776) — the man of the Reminder refusing the stampede. And his teaching for the smaller terrors: *"O God, I seek refuge in Thee from anxiety and grief, from incapacity and laziness, from cowardice and miserliness..."* (al-Bukhārī 6369) — the named fears facing the named Lord. The wild ass cannot learn; the servant can — and the verse's roar is aimed not at his destruction but at his halt.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:52

> **Rather, every man among them desires to be given pages unfolded.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demand: *Ṣuḥufan Munashsharah***

*Bal yurīdu kullu imriʾim minhum an yuʾtā ṣuḥufan munashsharah* — "Rather, every man among them desires to be given pages unfolded." The verse names the demand beneath the flight: not humility but *terms*. *Ṣuḥuf* — pages, scrolls — is the Qur'an's word for the scriptures given to former prophets: *"the pages (ṣuḥuf) of Abraham and Moses"* (87:18–19), *"and truly this is in the first pages (al-ṣuḥuf al-ūlā)"* (80:13); *munashsharah* — unfolded, spread open (from *nashara*, to spread, unfold — the same root in *"and when the pages are spread (nushirat)"*, 81:10, the Hour's own bookkeeping image). The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī) fix the demand's content: *each* man (*kullu imriʾim minhum* — every single one) wants a revelation *addressed to him personally* — his own delivered scroll, his own signed notice — as if the public Book addressed to all were insufficient for the individual's belief. The historical demand, reported in the source from al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī, had a second, more cynical layer: some Quraysh said that if Muḥammad were truly a messenger, he would bring a book *granting each of them exemption and protection from the Fire* — personal insurance documents from heaven. The demand thus bundles the two eternal appetites: *special treatment* (a revelation for me) and *immunity* (a covenant excusing me). The Qur'an's answer stands in its own record of these demands: *"and they say: why was there not sent down upon him gold, or were there not brought with it angels?"* (17:93's list, from 17:90–93: *"and they say: we shall not believe thee until thou makest a spring to gush from the earth for us, or hast a garden of palms and grapes and cause rivers to gush forth in its midst abundantly... or thou ascendest into heaven, and we shall not believe thy ascent until thou sendest down upon us a book that we may read"*).

**The Recorded Demands and the Prophetic Answers**

The asbāb and the tradition preserve the Quraysh's specific petitions, and the Prophet's ﷺ answer is the Sunnah's standing commentary on verse 52. Ibn ʿAbbās reports (al-Bukhārī 4953; Muslim 2805's family):

> *"The Quraysh said to the Prophet ﷺ: ask your Lord to make the hills gold for us... and to send an angel to testify with you... and he said: I am only a servant — so if you wish, I shall ask, and if He answers, and then you disbelieve, the torment will come upon you..."*

And the Book's own reply to the *pages-for-me* clause is its doctrine of universal address: *"and what ails this Messenger that he eats food and walks in the markets? Why was there not sent down to him an angel to be a warner with him?"* (25:7) — answered: *"and nothing prevented mankind from believing when guidance came to them save that they said: has God sent a mortal as messenger?"* (17:94) — the mortal medium being the *test's* very design; and *"and they say: if only we had been given the like of what was given to the messengers"* — no; the crisp text: 6:158's rule: *"the Day some of thy Lord's signs come, its faith shall profit no soul that believed not before, nor earned good in its faith."* The waiting-for-a-personal-scroll strategy is therefore *dead on arrival* by the Book's own legislation: signs after the deadline justify no one.

**Why a Shared Book Instead of Private Scrolls? The Design of Address**

The deeper theology: why does God address the species *together*? The commentators' answers assemble the design. First, *equality before the text*: a private scroll per man would stratify the species by revelation-count, and the Book's premise is one covenant, one address — *"O mankind, We created you from a male and a female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another"* (49:13); the shared Book makes the prophetic address the one thing every soul owns equally. Second, *the test of response without compulsion*: a personal, undeniable scroll to each man would compel assent and end the fork of 74:37 (*"whoever wishes to go forth or stay behind"*) — and the Book states the principle: *"and had thy Lord willed, all who are in the earth would have believed together; wouldst thou then compel mankind to become believers?"* (10:99). Third, *the record's public unity*: the shared scripture can be preserved (*"truly We sent down the Reminder, and truly We shall preserve it,"* 15:9) in a way a billion private exemptions cannot — the muṣḥaf in every mosque being the standing proof of the design's wisdom. The Prophet ﷺ embodied the shared-Book principle when ʿUthmān's standard codification went out to the cities: one text, every household — the anti-scroll decision of the third caliph (al-Bukhārī 4987's report of the standardized copies), which the tradition celebrates as among the Ummah's greatest services.

**Application: The Modern Demand for Personal Terms**

The demand of verse 52 is not extinct; it has been updated. The modern heart still negotiates for personal terms: *"give me certainty calibrated to my comfort — a religion with my name on the exemption, my sins pre-forgiven, my lifestyle grandfathered."* The Book's answer is the same as it was: the open public text, addressed to all, sufficient for whoever *wills to go forth* (74:37) — *"and We made the Qur'an easy for remembrance: is there any who will remember?"* (54:17). The Prophet's ﷺ rule for the demanded-certainty syndrome: *"the Hour will not come until the sun rises from its west; when it rises and the people see it, all will believe together — that is when no soul's faith will profit it that did not believe before"* (al-Bukhārī 4636's family) — the standing warning that demand-certainty is worthless certainty. The applications: stop negotiating terms and start *reading the terms* — the shared pages are already unfolded (81:10's image of the Hour being the last unfolding); and note the verse's *kullu* — "every man among them" — the demand was universal, and so is the Book: it answers every man at once, which is either its glory or, to the demander, its inconvenience.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:53

> **Nay! But they fear not the Hereafter.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Diagnosis Beneath the Demand: *Kallā***

*Kallā bal lā yakhāfūna al-ākhirah* — "Nay! But they fear not the Hereafter." The particle *kallā* — the Qur'an's emphatic refusal, the "By no means!" that rejected al-Walīd's greed at 74:16 and opened the oath-triad at 74:32 — now rejects the demand for personal pages: *no — that is not the real obstacle.* The verse then names the obstacle: *lā yakhāfūna al-ākhirah* — "they fear not the Hereafter" — the absence of *khawf*, reverent fear, the engine missing from the machine. The source commentary notes the exegetes' precision with *kallā*: it can carry "truly/indeed" as well as "nay" (al-Qurṭubī's note that the alternative rendering fits v. 54 better than v. 53), but either way the structure is diagnosis: the demand for unfolded pages (52) is not *evidence of doubt* but *evidence of fearlessness* — men who believed the Warning would not gamble on exemption-scrolls; men who fear nothing gamble in everything. The Qur'an's repeated equation: *"those who fear their Lord unseen"* (67:12) are the believers' definition; its negative twin: *"have you seen him who takes his desire as his god...?* — with the added phrase of the near verse: *"and thou seest them coming to it with eagerness"* — the fearlessness that is not bravery but * numbness*.

**Fear as Faculty: What the Hereafter-Fear Is For**

The verse teaches, by negation, that *khawf* is not a weakness to be cured but a faculty to be aimed. The Qur'an's positive doctrine of fear is dense: *"so fear not them, and fear Me, if you are believers"* (3:175) — the relocation of fear from creatures to Creator being the whole of *taqwā*'s psychology; *"and of mankind is he who says: we believe in God, and if he is harmed in God's way he holds the trial of mankind as God's torment"* — the fear-displacement failure of 2:214's family; and the Book's grand summary of the two fears: *"whoever fears the standing before his Lord and restrains the soul from desire, then the Garden is the refuge"* (79:40–41). The Prophet ﷺ taught the faculty's calibration: *"I know a sentence which, if a man says it seeking God's Face, death will not come until he has seen his place in the Garden"*? — rather the standing texts: *"hell-fire is forbidden to the one who weeps from fear of God"* (al-Tirmidhī 1634, graded ḥasan); *"whoever is put to trial by fear, God will secure him on the Day he meets Him"*? — the graded report; and his own weeping in prayer (ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Shikhkhīr's description, Abū Dāwūd 4849): *"he would weep until his beard was wet... as though from fear."* The fear the doomed lacked was not cowardice but *accurate accounting*: *"is one who creates like one who creates not? Will you not remember?"* (16:17) — and its fear-consequence: *"so fear God as much as you can (mā istaṭaʿtum)"* (64:16).

**The Hereafter as the Fear That Organizes Life**

Why name the Hereafter as the missing fear? Because it is the *organizing* fear — the one that re-weights every other decision. The Qur'an's logic of the Akhirah-fear is causal, not decorative: *"that House of the Hereafter We appoint for those who desire not exaltation in the earth nor corruption; the outcome belongs to the reverent"* (28:83) — the fear of the standing (*"whoever fears the standing before his Lord"*) producing precisely the conduct the doomed of vv. 43–47 lacked: the prayer kept, the plate sent, the plunge refused. The Prophet ﷺ tied the fear directly to the deeds: *"the keen man is he who takes himself to account and works for what is after death"* (Ibn Mājah 4260, graded ḥasan), and to the nights: *"two eyes the Fire shall not touch: an eye that wept from fear of God and an eye that stood watch in the night"* (al-Tirmidhī 1634). And he mapped the fear's social yield: the feared Day is why the believer dares today — *"when the affair becomes grave, turn to prayer"*? — rather the Ḥudhayfah tradition (Muslim 2961? — the composite): the man who slept through every trial *until the Fitnahs*, saying *"I am your storehouse, O people, and I know what will avert it from you: the prayer"* — fear of the uncontrolled Day producing the controlled night.

**Application: Where Is Your Fear Deposited?**

The practical audit: *what do you actually fear?* — not what you confess fearing, but what changes your behavior: the physician's call, the creditor's letter, the boss's summons, the crowd's opinion. The verse relocates the deposit: into the Hereafter — the one fear that improves every other decision instead of corrupting it. The Prophet's ﷺ prescription for the re-deposit: *"remember much the destroyer of pleasures"* (al-Tirmidhī 2307); visit the graves (*"I had forbidden you to visit graves; now visit them, for they remind of the Hereafter,"* Muslim 976); and pray the prayer of the fearful (*"O God, I seek refuge with Thee from the torment of the Fire and the trial of the Fire... and I seek refuge with Thee from every evil,"* Muslim 590's tashahhud-prayer). The man who fears the standing fears nothing else enough to sin for it — which is the entire moral engineering of the verse, and the difference between the demanders of 74:52 and the rememberers of 74:55.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:54

> **Yea! Truly it is a reminder.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second *Kallā*: From "Nay" to "Indeed"**

*Kallā inna al-dhikrā* — "Yea! Truly it is a reminder." The particle *kallā* returns (after 74:53) wearing its other face: the source commentary preserves the exegetes' note (al-Qurṭubī's school) that *kallā* may be rendered "truly" or "indeed" as well as "nay," and that here — opening an affirmation (*inna al-dhikrā*) — the "indeed" reading suits: *rather — indeed — it is a reminder.* The pronoun's referent is the Qur'an: the Reminder *par excellence* (Ṭabarī's reading in the source: *al-dhikr* = the Qur'an). The verse thus performs the Book's self-declaration: man demanded private scrolls (52); God answers that the *one public text* is precisely the appropriate gift — not because it withholds, but because its genre is *reminder*, and reminder is the species' true need. The Qur'an's self-names cluster here: *dhikr* (Reminder — 15:9; 21:50; 38:1 *"by the Qur'an of the Reminder"*), *tadhkirah* (Admonition — 74:31's closing: *innā hiya illā dhikrā lil-bashar*; 73:19: *"truly this is a reminder"*), *Qurʾān* (the Recitation — the thing read *aloud*, 17:106: *"and a Qur'an We have divided, that thou recitest it to mankind unhurried"*).

**Why a Reminder? The Doctrine of the Primordial Covenant**

The choice of *reminder* over *proof* encodes the Qur'an's anthropology: the Book does not deliver information to blank minds; it *awakens* knowledge already deposited — the covenant of 7:172 (*"am I not your Lord? They said: yes"* — testified *"from their loins"*, before embodiment), and the *fiṭrah* of 30:30: *"so set thy face to the religion as a ḥanīf — the fiṭrah of God upon which He created mankind; no change in God's creation — that is the upright religion."* Reminder (dhikr) works on prior content: it re-presents what the heart once knew and buried — which is why its recitation *moves* the sincere (*"when they hear what was sent down to the Messenger thou seest their eyes overflow with tears because of the truth they recognize,"* 5:83) and merely irritates the buried (*"and when they hear what was sent down to the Messenger, thou seest their eyes overflow..."* — no; the irritated: 47:16's *"what has he said just now?"*). The Prophet ﷺ operationalized the reminder-logic in his call: he did not demand invention but *return* — *"I have been sent to perfect the noble traits of character"* (al-Bayhaqī's Ṣaḥīḥ — the graded corpus), and to the Makkans: *"I call you to one word"* (Muslim 22's tawḥīd hadith from Muʿādh at the Hijrah dispatch: *"let the first you call them to be the testimony..."*). The Reminder's claim is therefore checkable by *resonance*: the reader who feels the jolt of recognition at Judgment-verses is feeling 7:172's deposit stir — evidence no private scroll could improve.

**A Reminder Needs No New Miracle — It Needs Attention**

The verse quietly answers the demands-culture of 74:52: reminder-genre truth does not work by demonstration but by *attention*; new pages, new springs, gold hills (17:90–93's list) would add nothing to a text whose office is to wake the sleeper — the alarm clock's power is in the sleeper's *staying awake*, not the clock's redesign. The Qur'an states the attention-principle as command: *"so when the Qur'an is recited, listen to it and be silent, that you may receive mercy"* (7:204); *"and when the Qur'an is recited to them they prostrate not?"* (84:21 — the recited miracle refused at the *posture* level); *"do they not reflect upon the Qur'an, or are there locks upon their hearts?"* (47:24). The Prophet ﷺ modeled the receiving posture: *"the example of the believer who recites the Qur'an is like the citron..."* (al-Bukhārī 5427); *"whoever recites a letter of God's Book has a good deed, and the good deed is tenfold"* (al-Tirmidhī 2910, graded ṣaḥīḥ); and his weeping at its recitation (ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd's report, al-Bukhārī 4822 — the night-recitation scene: *"and I heard him weeping"*). The applications are the practices the Ummah inherited: listening when the Book is recited (7:204), reciting daily portions (*the Prophet's ﷺ practice: a portion each day, Muslim 502's Ṣalāmah ibn ʿAmr report*), and the tafsīr-habit (*"recite the Qur'an and seek its meanings"*, the transmitted counsel) — because a reminder unattended is not a failed reminder; it is a postponed one (54:17's refrain ending at the door).

**Application: From Demand to Devotion**

The verse's practical conversion: trade the demander's posture (52) for the disciple's. The demander audits the Book; the disciple is audited *by* it — *"and when they hear vain discourse they withdraw from it, saying: we have our deeds and you have your deeds"* (28:55) — no; the disciple's text is: *"and the Book is sent down upon thee — blessed is He"* — rather the operative one: *"and this is a blessed Reminder: will you then deny it?"* (21:50). The believer's daily reservation of the Reminder — some verses, some silence, some tears — is the practical proof that 74:54's answer was heard: no private scroll is coming; the public one is *here*, and its office, warning and remembering, is already in session.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:55

> **So let whosoever will, remember it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Invitation Re-Issued: *Man Shāʾa Dhakarahu***

*Faman shāʾa dhakarahu* — "so whoever wills shall remember it." The verse is the invitation-side of the Warning: after the diagnoses (49–53) and the reminder's self-description (54), the door is held open in the grammar of *man shāʾa* — "whoever wills" — the signature construction of this sūrah-cluster's free-will charter (74:37: *"whoever wishes to go forth or stay behind"*; 73:19: *"so whoever wills shall take to his Lord a way"*; 76:29; 81:28). The verb's object generates the verse's two readings (the source preserves both): *dhakarahu* — "remember *it*" — the Reminder, the Qur'an: remembering *in the fullest Qur'anic sense*, which includes *acting in accord with the Word of God* (al-Ṭabarī's school: *dhikr* as heedful response, not mere recall); or "remember *Him*" — the pronoun shifting to God: *"so whoever wills shall remember Him."* Both readings converge on one practice: the attentive, lived remembrance that the whole sūrah was sent to elicit. The Qur'an's family of invitation-verses ends this way everywhere: *"and We made the Qur'an easy for remembrance: is there any who will remember?"* (54:17's refrain, repeated four times); *"so remind — thou art only a reminder; thou art not a warder over them — save him who turns away and disbelieves"* (88:21–23); *"so remind, if the reminder profits"* (87:9).

**Remembrance as Worship: The Vocabulary of *Dhikr***

The verse's verb *dhakara* names one of Islam's five great devotional registers — *dhikr*, the remembrance of God in tongue and heart. The Qur'an's doctrine of dhikr is expansive: *"those who believe and whose hearts find rest in God's remembrance; is it not in God's remembrance that hearts find rest?"* (13:28); *"remember Me, and I shall remember you"* (2:152 — the reciprocal covenant); *"and remember thy Lord much and glorify at evening and dawn"* (3:41); *"and men whom neither trade nor selling diverts from God's remembrance, and the prayer, and the giving of zakāh"* (24:37 — the merchant's dhikr). The Prophet ﷺ quantified and located it: *"two words light upon the tongue, beloved to the Compassionate, heavy on the scale: subḥāna Allāhi wa bi-ḥamdihī..."* (al-Bukhārī 6682; Muslim 2694); *"the example of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is the example of the living and the dead"* (al-Bukhārī 6407); *"the best of deeds is the remembrance of God"* — ʿUthmān's report with the Prophet's ﷺ answer when asked which deed is best: *"that you die with your tongue moist with God's remembrance"*? — rather the graded corpus (al-Tirmidhī 3377, ḥasan): *"shall I not tell you of the best of your deeds... the remembrance of God."* The believer's day is thus punctuated by the verse's verb: the morning-evening adhkār, the post-prayer formulas (Muslim 593's tasbīḥ-thirty-three), the *istiʿādhah* before recitation, the duas of entering and leaving — a life built as a *session of remembering it*.

**Remembering That Includes Acting**

The first reading — remember *it*, the Qur'an — carries the stricter application the exegetes pressed: Qur'anic *dhikr* is obedience-bearing memory; the Book's own pairs make this explicit: *"and this is a blessed Reminder We sent down; will you then deny it?"* (21:50) — denial being the remembered text's opposite; *"and remind, for the reminder profits the believers"* (51:55) — profit being its test; *"has not the time come... that their hearts should soften to God's reminder?"* (57:16) — softening, not storage. The Prophet ﷺ indicted memory-without-motion: *"the Qur'an will come as an intercessor for its companions on the Day of Resurrection"* (Muslim 804) — *companions*, not mere reciters; and warned of its counterfeit: *"the Qur'an's carrier who treats it as worn-out skins... whoever learns the Qur'an then sleeps upon it, forgetting, comes on the Day of Resurrection with a bridle of fire"*? — the transmitted corpus (Abū Dāwūd 1479's family): *"the example of the believer who recites the Qur'an is like the citron..."* and (Ibn Ḥibbān's graded text): *"most of the hypocrites of my nation are its Qur'an-reciters"* — memorization deployed against the Book's own content. The applications: recite *with* understanding (the tafsīr-discipline), recite *into* obedience (the one-change rule: each session leaves one command acted on), and recite *in company* (Muslim 2700's gathering-promise) — so that *dhakarahu* means what the doomed of vv. 43–47 never let it mean.

**Application: The Open Invitation Has a Closing Time**

The verse's tenderness is in its open door — *whoever wills* — and the sūrah's honesty is in the next verse: the will itself is hemmed inside the divine will (*"and they remember not save that God wills,"* 74:56). The practical posture between the two verses is the believer's *tawakkul-worked-out*: act on the invitation now, as if all depended on the will; thank God for the willing, knowing it all came from Him. The Prophet's ﷺ synthesis is his dua: *"O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm upon Thy religion"* (al-Tirmidhī 3522, graded ṣaḥīḥ) — the will praying for its own stability to the One who holds it. *Faman shāʾa dhakarahu* — the invitation stands, addressed to the will, tonight: remember it.

## Sūrah al-Muddaththir 74:56

> **And they remember not, save as God wills. He is most worthy of reverence and most worthy of granting forgiveness.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Closure: *Mā Yadhkurūna Illā An Yashāʾa Allāh***

*Wa mā yadhkurūna illā an yashāʾa Allāh* — "and they remember not, save that God wills." The sūrah's last doctrinal word locks the human invitation of 74:55 inside the divine will: *man shall remember only as God wills* — the "unless God wills" clause that everywhere completes the "whoever wills" clauses (76:30: *"and you will not will unless God wills — God is Knowing, Wise"*; 81:29: *"and you will not will unless God, Lord of the worlds, wills"*). The source commentary connects it back to 74:31: *"thus does God lead astray whomsoever He will and guide whomsoever He will"* — the sūrah opening and closing on the same hinge. The classical schools read the pair of verses with their characteristic emphases: the Muʿtazilah stressing the real freedom of 55 and reading 56 as God's *not willing good for the deserving obstinate* (the divine abandoning of those who first abandoned — 6:110's *"We shall turn their hearts away as they believed not the first time"*); the Sunni mainstream (al-Ashʿarī, al-Ṭabarī) affirming both texts: the creature *will* is real in the order of act; the divine *will* is absolute and prior in the order of creation — and Ibn Taymiyyah's integration: God wills the willing itself as the means (*sabab*) by which the remembered heart is guided; no contradiction afflicts the servant who prays. The Prophet ﷺ taught the synthesis as daily liturgy: *"O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm upon Thy religion"* (al-Tirmidhī 2140/3522, graded ṣaḥīḥ); *"none of you should say: had I only done such-and-such; rather say: God decreed and what He willed He did, for 'if' opens the devil's work"* (Muslim 2664); and the prayer of the willing-but-dependent: *"O God, there is no life but the life of the Hereafter, so forgive the Helpers and the Muhājirah"* — rather his standing counsel to ʿAbbās's petition (al-Tirmidhī 3551, ḥasan): *"O God, make for me from Thy mercy an opening..."*

**The Two Divine Perfections: *Aḥl al-Taqwā* and *Aḥl al-Maghfirah***

*Huwa aḥlu al-taqwā wa aḥlu al-maghfirah* — "He is most worthy of reverence and most worthy of granting forgiveness." The sūrah ends by naming two claims God holds upon the creature: *aḥl al-taqwā* — He *deserves* to be feared and revered (*taqwā*: the shielding awe that governs conduct — the Qur'an's master-virtue: *"O mankind, reverence your Lord who created you from one soul,"* 4:1; *"the noblest of you with God is the most reverent,"* 49:13) — and *aḥl al-maghfirah* — He *deserves* to be the Forgiver, the One whose pardoning is His due (*maghfirah* from *maghfara*, to cover — God as the Concealer of His servants' sins, as the slave's covering is the first image of the sūrah and God's covering its last: the covering of fear answered by the covering of forgiveness). The Prophet ﷺ transmitted the pairing as divine speech — the source cites it from al-Ājurrī and al-Qurṭubī:

> *"God says: I am most worthy of being reverenced; so let none be made a god beside Me. And whoever fears associating another with Me, it is fitting that I forgive him."* (al-Ājurrī; al-Ṭabarānī)

And the Book states the two perfections as the Hereafter's own verdict on the two companies: *"that is God's grace which He gives whom He wills, and God is of great grace and knowledge"* — the exact closing-pair: 5:118's *"...You are the Guardian of all things"*, and 39:53's grand offer: *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, despair not of God's mercy; God forgives all sins; He is the Forgiving, the Merciful"* — the forgiveness-claim being the door out of every warning this sūrah issued.

**The Sūrah's Perfect Ring: From Covering to Covering**

The chapter's architecture closes in a circle the commentators loved to trace: it opened with a man *covering* himself (*muddaththir*) in fear before overwhelming holiness; it ends with God *covering* (maghfirah) the sins of those who revere Him. Between the two coverings stands the whole program: arise, warn, magnify, purify, shun, give, endure (1–7); the trumpet and the day of difficulty (8–10); the anatomy of al-Walīd's rejection (11–25); Saqar and the nineteen (26–31); the oaths and the free choice (32–37); the pledge and the saved company (38–39); the Garden's interrogation and the doomed confession (40–48); the turners-away and the Reminder (49–55) — and the ring's clasp: reverence deserved, forgiveness offered. The Prophet ﷺ lived the clasp in his own breath: *"by God, I seek God's forgiveness and turn to Him more than seventy times a day"* (al-Bukhārī 6307) — the most forgiven man alive, the most repentant; and his final season's cry — *"say: O God, forgive me, have mercy on me, join me with the Highest Company"*? — the saḥīḥ text (Muslim 918): *"la ilāha illa Allāh, inna lil-mawti la-sakarāt"* — the reminder and the certainty in one mouth.

**Application: Holding Both Perfections to the End**

The practical legacy of the verse is the *both-hands* faith: the servant holds reverence in one hand (fear that obeys) and hope of forgiveness in the other (trust that returns) — and never drops either. The Prophet's ﷺ instruction to Ibn ʿAbbās is the verse's instruction: *"If you ask, ask God; and if you seek help, seek it from God; and know that if the nation gathered to benefit you, they could not benefit you except with what God has written for you..."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516, graded ṣaḥīḥ) — and his synthesis of fear and hope: *"none of you should die except while hoping in God"* (Muslim 2877). The sūrah that began with a trembling man under a cloth ends with the trembling itself dignified — turned toward a Lord worthy of the trembling and worthier of the forgiveness that answers it: *wa dhkur ism rabbika wa tabattal ilayhi tabtīlā* (73:8, the twin sūrah's counsel) — and the last word of al-Muddaththir's arc, for every reader, is the first word of its next breath: *remember.*
