# Sūrah al-Layl (Chapter 92) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Night and Its Answer**

Sūrah al-Layl, "The Night," is the ninety-second chapter of the Qur'an in the order of the written muṣḥaf and, by the reckoning of most historians of revelation, one of the earliest Makkan sūrahs — frequently counted around the ninth in the chronological order of descent, revealed in the first years of the prophetic mission when the Qur'an was still forging, in short, oath-driven sūrahs, the vocabulary with which it would confront the human conscience for the next two decades. Its name, like that of its immediate predecessor Sūrah al-Shams ("The Sun"), is taken from the first oath by which God swears within it: *wa-l-layli idhā yaghshā* — "By the night as it enshrouds." It consists of twenty-one verses, one of the most architecturally compact structures in the entire Qur'an: a set of cosmic oaths, a single declaration that those oaths exist to confirm, two portraits of the human being drawn in perfectly mirrored strokes, a statement of God's sovereignty over guidance and over both worlds, and finally two destinies — a Fire and a satisfaction — described in terms so spare that every word carries a load.

**The Companion of Sūrah al-Shams**

The sūrah is best read as the twin of the chapter that precedes it. Sūrah al-Shams (91) swore by the sun, the moon, the day, and the night, and then by the human soul and its Maker, concluding with the great thesis: *qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā wa-qad khāba man dassāhā* — "He succeeds who purifies it, and he fails who corrupts it" (91:9–10). Al-Layl takes up the same thesis and recasts it. Where al-Shams spoke of the soul's purification and corruption as an inner, vertical affair between the soul and its Lord, al-Layl projects the same polarity outward into human action: giving and withholding, reverence and arrogance, affirmation and denial. The vocabulary is deliberately continuous between the two sūrahs — *tazakkā* ("to purify oneself") returns in al-Layl's verse 18, and the pair of opposites "success/wretchedness" from al-Shams returns in al-Layl as the pair *al-ashqā* ("the most wretched") and *al-atqā* ("the most reverent"). The classical reciters and commentators noted that the two sūrahs function as one discourse in two movements: al-Shams establishes the law of the soul, and al-Layl demonstrates it in the marketplace of human conduct. A similar kinship binds it to Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā (93), which likewise opens with the day and the night and likewise ends on a promise of divine satisfaction — *wa-la-sawfa yuʿṭīka rabbuka fa-tarḍā* — an echo this sūrah answers with its own final word, *wa-la-sawfa yardā*, "and surely he shall be content."

**The Polarity of Creation and the Polarity of Conduct**

The sūrah's governing idea, announced in its introduction of oaths, is polarity. The created order runs in pairs: night and day, concealment and disclosure, male and female. Nothing in the visible cosmos stands still or stands alone; everything alternates, complements, answers. Against that backdrop of universal pairing, the sūrah then reveals a second, more consequential duality that runs not through the heavens but through the human will: *inna saʿyakum la-shattā* — "truly your endeavors are diverse" (92:4). Every reader of the sūrah is meant to notice the logic: if the physical world is structured by an inescapable binary of light and dark, the moral world is structured by an equally inescapable binary of two courses — one that leads to ease and one to hardship — and there is no third road. Some maintain that the sūrah is from the Madinan period, while others see it as part Makkan and part Madinan, a judgment connected to certain reports about its latter verses; but its language, its oath-structure, and its concision place its heart squarely in the Makkan idiom.

**The Ḥadīth That Made This Sūrah Famous**

Al-Layl holds a distinctive place in the Prophetic tradition because the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ himself chose its central verses to settle one of the deepest questions a believer can ask — the question of destiny and works. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib:

> *"We were with the Prophet ﷺ in a funeral procession at Baqīʿ al-Gharqad. He came and sat down, and we sat around him, and he had a stick with him. He lowered his head and began scraping the ground with his stick, then said: 'There is none among you, no soul in existence, but that his seat has been written — in the Garden or in the Fire.' They said: 'O Messenger of God, should we then not rely upon what has been written for us and abandon works?' He said: 'Work! For everyone is eased unto that for which he was created.' Then he recited: 'As for one who gives and is reverent, and attests to what is most beautiful, We shall ease his way unto ease. And as for one who is miserly and deems himself beyond need, and denies what is most beautiful, We shall ease his way unto hardship.'"* (al-Bukhārī 4945; Muslim 2647)

This single incident — the Prophet ﷺ seated among the graves, stick tracing the earth, reciting verses 5 through 10 of this sūrah — has made al-Layl for fourteen centuries the Qur'an's own appointed witness in the discussion of divine decree and human responsibility. The sūrah does not resolve the mystery by dissolving one side of it; it holds both in one breath: seats are written, and yet "work!" — because the writing shows itself precisely in the easing, and the easing shows itself precisely in the works.

**Its Place in Recitation and Learning**

Reports on the specific excellences of reciting individual sūrahs — including a narration transmitted in the tafsīr literature from Ubayy ibn Kaʿb promising divine sufficiency to the reciter of al-Layl — are generally graded very weak by the masters of ḥadīth criticism, and the sober scholars do not build doctrine upon them. What is authentic is the sūrah's constant presence in the earliest community's life: its third verse was so memorably recited in the variant reading transmitted from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd — *wa-l-dhakari wa-l-unthā*, "By the male and the female" — that it occasioned exchanges between the Companions recorded in the Ṣaḥīḥ of al-Bukhārī itself, and its middle verses were the Prophet's own chosen proof-text for the problem of destiny. The sūrah thus belongs to the stratum of the Qur'an the first Muslims knew by heart before they knew much else: short enough for a shepherd to carry entire, deep enough for ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib to spend a graveside lesson upon.

**The Structure of What Follows**

The movement of the sūrah may be set out plainly. Verses 1–3 are the oaths: by the night when it enshrouds, by the day when it discloses, by Him Who created the male and the female. Verse 4 is the oath's answer: your endeavors are diverse. Verses 5–7 draw the first portrait — the one who gives, is reverent, and attests to the most beautiful — and pronounce his recompense: We shall ease him unto ease. Verses 8–11 draw the exact inverse — miserly, self-sufficient, denying — with its inverse recompense: We shall ease him unto hardship, and his wealth shall not avail him when he perishes. Verses 12–13 interpose the ground on which both portraits stand: guidance is God's to give, and both worlds are God's to dispose of. Verses 14–16 issue the warning of the raging Fire and define its only permanent inhabitant: the most wretched, who denies and turns away. Verses 17–21 complete the mirror with the one who is removed far from it: the most reverent, who gives his wealth to purify, owing no favor to anyone, seeking only the Face of his Lord, the Most High — and whose end is the sūrah's last word: contentment.

**Method of This Commentary**

The commentary that follows treats each of the sūrah's twenty-one verses as an independent, fully developed discourse, in strict canonical order, exactly as the workflow mandates. Where the source commentary groups material — as it does for verses 15–17 and 19–21, and as it attaches a single occasion-story across verses 5–10 — the verses are here separated and each receives its own complete treatment, with the shared reports and arguments distributed to the verse that properly carries them. Every verse is examined through its Arabic vocabulary and roots, its cross-references within the Qur'an (quoted in translation), the authenticated Prophetic reports that attach to it with their collections identified, the readings of the classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, and others — its occasions of revelation, its implications for creed and conduct, and its bearing on modern life. Nothing has been compressed because the chapter is short; in the logic of this sūrah, the small is where the decisive things are decided.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:1

> **By the night as it enshrouds,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath Sworn in the Dark**

The sūrah opens with a sworn word. In the Arabic of the Qur'an, an oath formula — *wa-l-layli idhā yaghshā* — consists of the particle *wāw* ("by"), the object sworn by, and a qualifying clause that fixes the object at a particular moment of its being. Here God swears by the night, but not by the night as an abstraction: by the night specifically *idhā yaghshā*, "when it enshrouds," "at the moment it covers." The verb *ghashiya*, from the root *gh-sh-y*, means to come upon something and envelop it, the way a wave comes over a swimmer or sleep comes over the eyes; the Qur'an elsewhere uses it of sleep's gentle takeover of consciousness (*yu-ghashshīkumu l-nuʿās*, "He causes slumber to enshroud you," 8:11) and of the overwhelming event of the Hereafter (*idhā yaghshā*, "when it overwhelms," 53:54). The night sworn by, then, is the night in its active verb — the night arriving, spreading, and laying itself over creation as a covering is laid over a body.

**The Deliberately Unnamed Object**

The commentators immediately notice what the verse withholds: the verb *yaghshā* has no stated object. The night enshrouds — *what?* Classical exegesis filled the silence with a spectrum of answers, each faithful to the wording. Al-Ṭabarī gathers the early positions: the night when it covers the sun, as it does at its setting; the night when it covers the heavens; the night when it covers the earth and everything upon it. Al-Qurṭubī presents the same range, and the later tradition, including Ibn Kathīr, leans toward the widest reading — that the omitted object is deliberately universal, because the night does not enshroud one thing only; it enshrouds all things at once. The grammarians call this a *maḥdhūf*, a suppressed element, and the rule they state about it is worth pausing over: when the Qur'an deletes the object of a transitive verb, the deletion itself generalizes the meaning. Said plainly, the verse withholds the object in order to let every reader supply every object. The night enshrouds the sun, the mountains, the cities, the workers and their works, the sins and the prayers, the waking and the sleeping. A stated object would have limited the oath; the silence makes it absolute. The same device gave Sūrah al-Shams its verse *wa-l-layli idhā yaghlāhā*, "by the night when it covers *her*" — where the pronoun points back to the sun just mentioned; here, with no antecedent in sight, the covering is simply total.

**Why God Swears by Created Things**

The jurists of language in the tafsīr tradition ask a prior question: what is accomplished when God swears? God's plain statement requires no oath, since His word is in itself the highest verification. The answer the scholars give is pedagogical, not theological in the sense of need. Al-Zamakhsharī and after him al-Rāzī explain that oaths in the Qur'an serve three ends: to alert the listener that what follows is of moment; to honor the thing sworn by, thereby teaching the servant to see it as a sign rather than mere furniture of the world; and to display, in the thing sworn by, the evidence for the thing sworn to. Every one of these applies here. The oath tells the listener: wake, this matters. It honors the night, which human beings mostly resent, sleep through, or abuse, and re-appoints it as one of the great signs (*āyāt*) of God, joining the long register the Qur'an keeps of night-and-day passages — *inna fī khalqi s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-khtilāfi l-layli wa-n-nahāri la-āyātin li-uli l-albāb*, "Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the night and the day are signs for those of understanding" (3:190). And it displays the evidence in advance of the verdict: the sūrah will shortly declare that human striving divides into two courses, and nothing in the visible cosmos exhibits this law of pairs more constantly, more democratically, more inescapably, than the daily conquest of the earth by darkness and its daily recovery by light.

**The Night as Veil of Deeds**

There is a further layer the moral commentators draw out. The night does not merely cover bodies and landscapes; it covers deeds. In the moral vocabulary of the Qur'an, darkness is the element of what is hidden — *ẓulumāt* is the word the Qur'an uses for the layers of ignorance, disbelief, and concealed corruption — and the night is darkness made into a time. What people do at night is, for most of human history, what no ledger of men has recorded: the secret charity, the hidden sin, the private prayer, the sleepless anxiety, the plotted fraud. The verse's enshrouding night is thus read, in the ethical tradition, as the covering under which the true self comes out. The Prophet ﷺ gave this idea its permanent formulation in the ḥadīth of the two recorded angels and the one witnessing night, and the Qur'an itself returns to it repeatedly: *wa-huwa maʿakum ayna mā kuntum*, "and He is with you wherever you are" (57:4), and *yasta khfūna mina n-nāsi wa-lā yastakhfūna mina Llāhi wa-huwa maʿahum idh yubayyitūna mā lā yarḍā mina l-qawl*, "They conceal themselves from the people but cannot conceal themselves from God, while He is with them when they plot by night what He is not pleased with in word" (4:108). The oath by the enshrouding night quietly announces the sūrah's central concern: it is in what is covered that human beings are most truly decided. The modern condition has only sharpened the point. An entire economy of anonymous, night-time, screen-lit action now exists — transactions, messages, viewings, decisions — visible to no neighbor and recorded by no public ledger; the night of al-Layl has become, for billions, a permanent interior room. The verse, fourteen centuries on, still swears by it.

**The Pastoral Weight of the Image**

To the first audience of the Qur'an the image carried a weight that no modern reader should miss. The Makkan night was not decorative darkness; it was the hour of danger and of decision — the hour of clandestine worship when open worship was forbidden, of secret migration plans, of the Prophet's own vigils, and of the desert's absolute exposure. A sūrah revealed into such a time, opening by swearing by the night *as it enshrouds*, speaks first to people for whom darkness is real: real threat, real poverty, real concealment of a faith that could cost them their trade and their clan-protection. The oath dignifies their hidden condition: the very darkness that hides them from their persecutors is the thing God has sworn by. There is a pastoral logic here that recurs throughout the early Makkan revelation — God naming, in His own speech, the concrete textures of the believers' suffering, thereby telling them it has not gone unseen. The night will lift; the day is sworn by in the very next verse. But first, the enshrouding.

**A Rhetoric That Withholds Before It Declares**

Finally, the verse's form enacts its meaning. An oath always postpones its content: the listener who hears *by the night as it enshrouds* must hold the question "then what?" in suspension while the next oaths pile up. The sūrah will stack three oaths before it releases the declaration they were built to carry. That withheld-object grammar in the first verse is thus the small model of the sūrah's whole method: meaning arrives here through staged disclosure, exactly as the day arrives after the night. The enshrouding is not the last word; it is only the first word, sworn.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:2

> **by the day as it discloses;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of the Great Alternation**

If the first verse swore by the night in the act of covering, the second swears by the day in the act of uncovering. The verb is *tajallā*, from the root *j-l-y*, whose core meaning is to polish, to clear, to make manifest: one *jallā* a mirror by removing what dims it, a truth *tajallā* when it becomes evident. The day, accordingly, is not sworn by as a mere block of hours but as an event of disclosure — the moment the covering of the first verse is lifted and what lay beneath it stands exposed. The pair of verses thus forms one figure in two strokes: a world concealed, a world unveiled; and the two together constitute the "alternation" (*ikhtilāf*) that the Qur'an so often cites as a standing proof — *wa-jāʿala l-layla wa-n-nahāra āyātayn*, "and We made the night and the day two signs" (17:12).

**A Sworn Pair Repeated Across the Book**

The oath by the alternation of night and day is one of the Qur'an's recurring sworn pairs, and this sūrah is one member of a whole constellation. The commentators catalogue the passages: *wa-l-layli idh yubrid wa-n-nahāri idhā tajallā* is echoed in the pair of 74:33–34 (*wa-l-layli idh adbara wa-ṣ-ṣubḥi idhā asfara*), in the oaths of 81:17–18 (*wa-l-layli idhā ʿasʿasa wa-ṣ-ṣubḥi idhā tanaffasa*), in 84:16–17 (*fa-lā uqsimu bi-sh-shafaqi wa-l-layli wa-mā wasaqa*), in 89:1–4 (*wa-l-fajri wa-layālin ʿashr*), and in al-Shams's own opening (91:1–4). Al-Ṭabarī, listing these and others at the head of this sūrah, draws the structural lesson: God repeats the oath by the alternation because the alternation is the one sign that every generation of human beings, in every climate and every age, has actually witnessed without mediation — no instrument, no travel, no scholarship required. The sun sets and rises upon every human being equally; it is the proof that meets everyone where he stands, and the Book swears by it to people who possessed no other observatory. That God returns to this pair of oaths in sūrah after sūrah is itself a lesson in divine pedagogy: the greatest arguments deserve the plainest witnesses.

**The Grammar of Disclosure**

The symmetry between the two verses is exact in form and deliberate in meaning. *Idhā yaghshā* and *idhā tajallā* are both conditional time-clauses — "at the moment it enshrouds," "at the moment it discloses" — and the choice of this construction in both cases fixes each oath to the instant of transition rather than the steady state. It is not "by the night" and "by the day" in general; it is by the night *in the act of falling*, by the day *in the act of breaking*. The Qur'an elsewhere isolates that same transitional instant and names it as the hour of greatest sign-character: *fāliqu l-iṣbāḥi wa-jaʿala l-layla sakanan wa-sh-shamsa wa-l-qamara ḥusbānā*, "The splitter of the dawn, Who made the night a repose and the sun and the moon a reckoning" (6:96); and *yūliqu l-layla fī n-nahāri wa-yūliqu n-nahāra fī l-layl*, "He merges the night into the day and merges the day into the night" (35:13). The transition is where design is most visible, because it happens twice a day on schedule, forever, for everyone, and no power on earth can hurry it or delay it. Modern humanity has electrically colonized the night and filled it with artificial day, yet no city on earth can legislate a dawn. The oath keeps its force precisely because the thing sworn by remains un-owned by the one who hears it.

**Disclosure as the Element of Accountability**

The moral commentators read the pair as more than cosmology. Night, they observe, is the element of the hidden, and day is the element of the manifest; and the two together therefore constitute the complete field in which human deeds are performed. Whatever a person conceals, the day eventually discloses — the scheme ripens into consequence, the secret leaks into rumor, the hidden intention surfaces in the pattern of a life. The Qur'an states the principle flatly elsewhere: *yawma tublā s-sarāʾir*, "the Day when the hidden things are tested" (86:9), and *yaʿlamu mā yusirrūna wa-mā yuʿlinūn*, "He knows what they conceal and what they declare" (2:77). The day sworn by here is thus read as the pledge that disclosure is built into the structure of time itself: the cosmos runs on a cycle whose second half is, every twenty-four hours, the unveiling of the first. A person who spends his night hiding from God is still living inside a universe whose default setting is morning. This is one of the sūrah's quiet arguments for the Hereafter drawn entirely from observation: if time itself is made of concealment followed by disclosure, the great concealment of this world — its unpunished wrongs, its unrewarded goodness — argues for, not against, a final disclosure in which the books are opened. The logic is the same as 86:9, and the sūrah will return to it in verse 11, where the hoarder's wealth fails him at the hour of perishing: the day always comes.

**Two Times, Two Postures**

The spiritual writers of the tafsīr tradition, al-Qurṭubī among them in his customary sobriety, permit a further reading: the two times correspond to two postures of the soul. The night is the hour of intimate worship — the *qiyām al-layl* the Prophet ﷺ kept until his feet swelled, the third-of-the-night descent of mercy described in the ḥadīth of the two Ṣaḥīḥs: *"Our Lord descends each night to the lowest heaven when the last third of the night remains, and says: Who calls upon Me, that I may answer him?"* (al-Bukhārī 1145; Muslim 758). The day is the hour of testimony — of honest trade, public justice, visible kindness, the face-to-face obligations of the market and the street. A complete servant, in this reading, belongs to both oaths: covered with God in the night, disclosed before people in the day, and the same heart in both. Those who invert the order — performing for people in the dark hours of privacy, hiding from people's scrutiny what they display in worship — have broken the sūrah's pair. It is a diagnosis of remarkable durability: the modern language of "authenticity" is largely an attempt to say what this verse says in four Arabic words — that what one is in the covering and what one is in the disclosure should be one person.

**The Oath Completes Its Cadence**

With the second verse, the sūrah's first movement has half its music. One pair has been sworn — darkness and light, concealment and manifestation — and the ear now expects the structure to continue, because the Qur'an's oaths rarely come singly. The alternation has been established as the cosmos's law; the next verse will carry that law one step further, from time into life itself, and then the sūrah will deliver the verdict all three oaths were assembled to certify.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:3

> **by Him Who created the male and the female,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Sign to the Maker of Signs**

The oath-sequence now performs a deliberate ascent. The first two verses swore by created events — the night falling, the day breaking. The third verse swears by the Creator of such things, expressed through one of His most universal acts: the making of human life in pairs. The Arabic is *wa-mā khalaqa dh-dhakara wa-l-unthā*, and its pivot is the word *mā*, which the grammarians and commentators have parsed in two principal ways. The first, favored by a group of the early authorities and reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, takes *mā* as the relative particle in its sense of "he who": *By Him Who created the male and the female.* The second takes *mā* as the *maṣdariyyah*, the nominalizing particle: *By the creation of the male and the female* — the act of creating sworn by, rather than the Creator. Both readings are grammatically sound, and the tradition kept both alive.

**The Reading of Ibn Masʿūd and Its Lesson**

A third element belongs to this verse's history: the famous variant reading transmitted from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd, *wa-l-dhakari wa-l-unthā*, "By the male and the female," which drops the verb of creation altogether and swears directly by the two. This reading is historically authenticated in the highest degree — it is discussed in narratives recorded by al-Bukhārī himself, including the well-known exchange concerning ʿAlqamah and Abū al-Dardāʾ, in which the Syrian Companion asks whether the recitation of "the one who was sent among them" — Ibn Masʿūd — still survives, and the words *wa-l-dhakari wa-l-unthā* are the test-case cited (al-Bukhārī 3762; cf. 4944). The canonical recitation retained in the ʿUthmānic muṣḥaf is nonetheless *wa-mā khalaqa*, and the scholars draw from the episode a principle that governs all Qur'anic recitation: the preservation of the Book is not the preservation of every word ever uttered around it, but of the agreed codex. What matters for the commentary is the convergence of all three forms of the verse upon a single point — the oath is aimed at the *pairing* of human life, whether the pair is sworn by directly, through its act of being made, or through its Maker.

**Why the Pair, and Not the Individual**

The question the commentators press is why, of all divine acts, this one was chosen to close the oath-series. Several answers accumulate. First, the male-and-female pairing extends into the realm of living beings the same law of polarity the first two oaths displayed in time: night/day, concealment/disclosure, male/female. The universe, the sūrah is assembling its evidence verse by verse, is built of complementary opposites; anyone who has watched that pair generate every generation of every species has seen the law at work in his own ancestry. Second, as the source notes, the creation of human beings in pairs is presented elsewhere in the Qur'an as a sign bearing symbolic meaning: *wa-khalaqnākum azwājā*, "and We created you in pairs" (78:8); *wa-annahū khalaqa z-zawjayni dh-dhakara wa-l-unthā*, "and that He creates the two kinds, the male and the female" (53:45); and the great charter of 4:1 — *khalaqakum min nafsin wāḥidatin wa-khalaqa minhā zawjahā*, "He created you from a single soul, and created from it its mate" (echoed at 7:189 and 39:6). The single soul divided into mates, and the mates re-united in every marriage and every child, is the cosmos's most intimate demonstration that unity and multiplicity are not rivals.

**An Oath That Abolishes Rank Between the Sexes**

The most consequential reading of the verse is the one the source singles out, because the sūrah's argument requires it. The oath names male and female together, in one breath, as the joint object of one creative act — and the sūrah then proceeds to spend its remaining eighteen verses determining human destinies by criteria in which sex never once appears. The ends of human beings, this sūrah teaches, are not decided by gender but by reverence: by giving or withholding, by affirming or denying. The verse thereby lays the grammatical foundation for the Qur'an's explicit declaration elsewhere: *yā ayyuhā n-nāsu innā khalaqnākum min dhakarin wa-unthā wa-jaʿalnākum shuʿūban wa-qabāʾila li-taʿārafū inna akramakum ʿinda Llāhi atqākum*, "O mankind! Truly We created you from a male and a female, and We made you peoples and tribes that you may come to know one another. Surely the most noble of you before God are the most reverent of you" (49:13). Read together, the two passages state a complete anthropology in miniature: creation distributes difference — sexes, peoples, tribes — for the sake of mutual knowledge, not rank; and the only distinction that survives into the Hereafter is *taqwā*, which is equally available to every soul. The sūrah will prove the thesis dramatically: its portraits of the wretched and the reverent are drawn without any specification of sex, wealth, or lineage, and its historical exemplars in the reports of revelation include a slave from Abyssinia and the Prophet's closest male Companion — the same Fire threatening both, the same contentment promised to both.

**The Creator Sworn By His Own Handiwork's Deepest Pattern**

On the reading *by Him Who created*, the verse also accomplishes a quiet theological correction of the first two oaths' possible misreading. One who hears "by the night, by the day" might settle into a kind of nature-reverence; the third oath re-anchors every oath in the One who makes what is sworn by. The Qur'an's consistent rule is that oaths by created things glorify their Maker, and the Prophet ﷺ enforced that rule practically: he forbade swearing by fathers or by created beings and directed oaths to God alone — *"Whoever swears, let him swear by God or be silent"* (al-Bukhārī 2679; Muslim 1646). That God Himself swears by His creation while forbidding the servant to do so is one of the asymmetries the scholars note with care: for God, the oath is a display of signs; for the servant, swearing by anything other than God risks assigning it a sacred rank it does not hold. The verse's middle reading — *by the creation of the male and the female* — stands between the two positions as a reminder that the act of making is itself an object of wonder: the embryology the Qur'an will describe elsewhere in detail (23:12–14; 22:5) was, at the moment of this sūrah's revelation, a sealed book to every human being on earth, and yet the simple named fact — that every one of you comes from a male and a female, and neither sex alone can produce you — was already enough to swear by.

**The Pair as the Cell of Society**

Finally, the social horizon of the oath should not be missed. The male and the female are not merely two biological facts; they are the founding cell of every human society — the household from which clan, city, and civilization propagate. A sūrah about the ultimate ends of human striving, opening its evidence with the oath by the pair, quietly asserts that the destinies it will describe are worked out not by solitary souls in the abstract but by paired, social, generational beings: people who marry, inherit, provide, abandon, give, and withhold inside households. The entire moral portrait-gallery that follows — the giver and the miser, the reverent and the self-sufficient — will consist of people embedded in precisely these relations. The oath chose the family because the verdict will be rendered within it.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:4

> **truly your endeavors are diverse.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict the Oaths Were Built to Carry**

This verse is the *jawāb al-qasam* — the answer of the oath — the declaration for which three sworn sentences have been assembling their weight. In the grammar of Qur'anic oaths, everything before this point existed in order to make this point land: the night sworn by, the day sworn by, the Maker of the pair sworn by, and then, with the double emphasis of *inna* and the lām of certitude, the declaration — *inna saʿyakum la-shattā*: truly, your endeavors are diverse. The structure itself teaches a method: before the Qur'an states its verdict about human beings, it first marshals the visible universe as witness, so that the listener receives the moral claim not as a bare assertion but as a conclusion resting on sworn evidence.

**The Word Saʿy: Striving with the Whole Body**

The verse's subject is *saʿy*, a word of precise physical origin. From the root *s-y-ʿ*, it denotes rapid, effortful movement — the quickened walk of someone with somewhere urgent to be. It is the word the Arabs used for hurried travel on a serious errand, and the Sharīʿah kept that bodily connotation: the ritual traversing between Ṣafā and Marwah in the pilgrimage is called *saʿy*, seven lengths of effortful walking commemorating Hagar's desperate running between the two hills in search of water for her infant. When the Qur'an elsewhere uses *saʿy* of human effort, it likewise means work pursued with urgency: *wa-an laysa li-l-insāni illā mā saʿā*, "and that man shall have nothing but what he strives for" (53:39); and *wa-man arāda l-ākhirata wa-saʿā lahā saʿyahā*, "and whoever wills the Hereafter and strives for it as it ought to be striven for" (17:19). The commentators therefore note the pointed choice of word here. The sūrah does not say "your works (*aʿmālukum*) are diverse," still less "your wishes are diverse." It says your *saʿy* — your committed, effortful, body-enlisted striving — is diverse. As the source glosses, *saʿy* in this context functions as a synonym for doing and acting (*ʿamal*), with the added charge of earnestness: human beings are not contemplating their ends but running toward them. The verse takes every reader's daily scramble — the job, the trade, the scheme, the career, the household's upkeep — and names it with the word of Hagar's desert running, thereby asking what destination all that running has actually chosen.

**The Word Shattā: Scattered Ends**

The predicate is *shattā*, the broken plural of *shatīt*, from the root *sh-t-t*, whose sense is dispersion, separation, scattering — a people *shattā* are scattered in different directions, and the same root gives the verb *tashattata*, to become dispersed. The word is therefore stronger than "different." Different opinions can sit together in one room; *shattā* ends point in directions that separate their holders from one another. The commentators draw out the theological import: the diversity in question is not the harmless diversity of crafts and temperaments but the forked diversity of ultimate destination. Al-Shawkānī, following the earlier tradition, states the point crisply: people work either for redemption or for perdition, and there is no in-between. The binary the sūrah is building — ease and hardship, the Fire and the Face — is announced here in a single scattered-plural word: your strivings do not merely differ; they diverge, and divergence ends in arrival at different places.

**The Logic of the Transition: From Cosmic Pairs to Moral Forks**

The movement from verse 3 to verse 4 contains the sūrah's whole argument in compressed form, and it deserves to be unfolded. The oaths displayed a universe of polarities — night/day, concealment/disclosure, male/female — and every one of those polarities is a polarity of *complement*: the pair completes one order. The verdict then discloses a second kind of polarity within human striving, but this one is a polarity of *contradiction*: two strivings that cannot both arrive, because they travel opposite roads. The same Maker Who ordered the cosmos by complementary pairs has ordered the moral universe by an exclusive fork. The logic pressed on the listener is plain: you live inside a creation whose every great feature comes in twos; do not imagine you will be the exception to that law in the one domain where the two are eternally different. The verse thereby refutes in advance every version of the claim — fashionable in every century, including this one — that ultimately all roads arrive at the same destination. The Qur'an grants that the roads are many; it denies they converge. *Shattā* is the word that carries the denial.

**The Emphatic Particles and Their Audience**

The double emphasis — *inna* plus the lām of certitude upon *la-shattā* — is itself a commentary on the original audience. Certitude-markers in Arabic rhetoric are prescribed for the resistant listener, not the convinced one; one does not swear and emphasize to a man already agreeing. The emphatic form therefore diagnoses the sūrah's addressees: they lived in a society that believed effort and destiny were decided by lineage, tribal honor, wealth, and the patronage of gods who could be managed with sacrifices — a world, in short, in which the decisive diversity of human ends was being attributed to everything except the direction of one's own striving. Into that world the verse drops its correction, doubly emphasized: the decisive scatter is in the *saʿy*, and it is *yours* — the second-person plural address (*saʿyakum*, "your striving") makes every hearer individually implicated. No clan, no ancestry, no accumulated night of merely inherited religion is under discussion; the plural pronoun gathers the rich Qurashī merchant and the Abyssinian slave into one identical "you," the same one that verse 13 of Sūrah al-Ḥujurāt will later address.

**A Mirror for Modern Striving**

The verse lands with undiminished force in the modern world, which has multiplied the modes of *saʿy* beyond anything Makkah could imagine while leaving the question of destination unexamined. The contemporary economy glorifies effort itself — the hustle, the grind, the optimization — and measures human worth in outputs, while remaining officially silent about what all the running is *for*. Al-Layl's fourth verse performs its ancient surgery on this condition with two words: the issue was never whether you strive — everyone strives, the sūrah takes that for granted — but that strivings are *shattā*, divergent, and divergence is decided by direction. Two people can perform identical external actions — the same hours worked, the same money moved — and exercise diametrically opposite *saʿy*, because one's running is toward the ease of verse 7 and the other's toward the hardship of verse 10. The verse thus relocates destiny from the visible metric of activity to the invisible one of orientation, and it does so before it has introduced either road. Everything that follows — the two portraits, the two destinies — is the unpacking of the single word *shattā*.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:5

> **As for one who gives and is reverent,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Portrait Opens with an Open Hand**

With this verse the sūrah begins to draw its two portraits, and the first stroke of the first portrait is an act of giving: *fa-ammā man aʿṭā*, "as for one who gives." The verb *aʿṭā* — from the root *ʿ-ṭ-w*, to hand over, to bestow — is left without a stated object, in the same deliberate grammar of openness the sūrah employed in verse 1. The earliest commentators accordingly widened it in every direction: giving of wealth in charity; giving of help, intercession, and presence; giving of one's person in service; and, in the reading the source preserves, giving God His proper due — the obedience and worship that are His right. Al-Ṭabarī gathers these senses and concludes that the word covers every kind of giving in obedience to God, since He did not restrict it to any one of them. The effect of the unspecified object is again generalizing: the portrait is of a person whose default posture toward existence is *outward* — hands that release rather than retain. Everything else in the portrait will follow from that first orientation, and its inverse — the clenched hand of verse 8 — will be the first stroke of the opposite face.

**What the Three Qualities Have in Common**

The portrait lists three qualities in sequence: giving, reverence, attestation. The commentators observe that the three are not parallel items in a list but a causal structure. Giving is the outer deed; reverence (*taqwā*) is the inner guard that keeps the deed honest; attestation of the most beautiful is the belief that makes both possible at all. Each needs the other two: giving without reverence becomes display, reverence without attestation becomes anxiety, attestation without giving becomes a claim. The Prophet ﷺ gave the three their integrated definition in the famous ḥadīth of Gabriel, where religion is taught as *islām* (deeds of the limbs, including the giving of zakāh), *īmān* (attestation of the unseen), and *iḥsān* (worshipping God as though one sees Him — the perfection of reverence) — Muslim 8. Al-Layl's three qualities map onto that same triad in compressed Makkan form, and the sūrah's promise of ease in verse 7 is accordingly pronounced not over any one of them alone but over the complete person they compose.

**Taqwā: The Careful Walker on a Dangerous Path**

The second quality, *wa-ttaqā* — "and he is reverent, he guards himself" — is built on the root *w-q-y*, whose physical sense is to place a shield between oneself and what harms. The classical definition handed down through the generations makes the image concrete: *taqwā* is that a servant places between himself and what he fears of God's anger a barrier of obedience. The Companions' own explanation of the verse *ittaqu Llāha ḥaqqa tuqātih* (3:102) — reported through Ibn Masʿūd and others — spells it out as the full program: that He be obeyed and not disobeyed, remembered and not forgotten, thanked and not disbelieved. The most enduring analogy in the tradition is the one the early authorities drew from the word's own imagery: the one with *taqwā* is like a man walking through a thorn-strewn field, gathering his garment about him and placing each foot with care, or — in its everyday modern translation — a driver negotiating a dangerous road in poor visibility, whose alertness, speed-control, and refusal of distraction are precisely what deliver him through. Awareness, in both images, does not paralyze movement; it makes movement survivable. That is the sūrah's point about the reverent giver: his caution is not a separate religious activity performed alongside his generosity; it is the manner of the generosity itself — giving with attention to source, to intention, to recipient, and to the One watching.

**The Occasion of Revelation: Abū Bakr's Money**

The tafsīr tradition attaches the portrait in our verse directly to a historical man. The dominant report, cited by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī among others, holds that verses 5–10 were revealed concerning Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, whose giving in the Makkan period became proverbial: he spent his fortune ransoming tortured slaves — Bilāl, ʿĀmir ibn Fuhayrah, Umm ʿUbayyis, al-Nahdiyyah and her daughter, and others — buying their bodies out of Meccan cruelty and setting them free. The later verses 17–21 of this same sūrah will be tied, in equally strong reports, to that same ministry of emancipation. But the scholars also record a second, broader occasion preserved by al-Wāḥidī and others: the verses describe a *type*, and the Companions who embodied the type — Abū Bakr foremost among them — are its named instances rather than its exclusive referents. Ibn Kathīr states the governing hermeneutic rule in exactly this connection: *al-ʿibrah bi-ʿumūm al-lafẓ lā bi-khuṣūṣ al-sabab* — "the lesson is taken from the generality of the wording, not from the particularity of the occasion." The verse was revealed about a man who gave away his fortune to buy freedom for the helpless, and it stands, for all time, over every hand that gives and guards itself while giving.

**The Ḥadīth That Recited This Verse at a Graveside**

It was precisely this verse and the five that follow it that the Prophet ﷺ chose to recite in the incident of Baqīʿ al-Gharqad already quoted in the introduction — the graveside lesson on destiny and works recorded by al-Bukhārī (4945) and Muslim (2647) from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib. The Companions' question there is the hinge of the whole Islamic debate about decree: if seats in the Garden and the Fire are already written, why work? The Prophet's answer — *"Work! For everyone is eased unto that for which he was created"* — was followed immediately by the recitation of our passage, establishing forever that these six verses are the Qur'an's appointed resolution of the question. Their logic, as the ḥadīth applies it, is this: the writing of destinies is not a cancellation of striving but the hidden order *within* striving. God does not write the end and then dispense with the means; He writes the end *with* the means, so that the man who finds himself giving, guarding, and attesting has, in those very acts, the evidence of which ease is being prepared for him. Al-Zamakhsharī, citing the last clause of the ḥadīth at this verse, states the mechanism: God "makes it amiable to him and grants him success until obedience becomes the easiest and simplest of things for him," as in His saying: *fa-man yuridi Llāhu an yahdiyahu yashraḥ ṣadrahū li-l-islām*, "Whomsoever God wishes to guide, He expands his breast for submission" (6:125). The portrait's three qualities are thus simultaneously the servant's acts and the signs of divine facilitation at work — the theme verse 7 will make explicit.

**The Sequence Teaches**

One last observation belongs to this verse: the order *aʿṭā wa-ttaqā* — he gave *and* he was reverent. The deed is named before the guard, because the Qur'an consistently presents the testable action first and the inner state as its attesting context; the Bedouins who claimed belief were told *qul lammā tuʾminū wa-lākin qūlū aslamnā*, "Say: you have not yet believed; rather say: we have submitted" (49:14) until the claim proved itself in deeds. The giver comes before the reverent in the wording because giving is the reverent man's visible signature.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:6

> **and attests to what is most beautiful,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Quality: Belief as the Engine of the Other Two**

The portrait now names what holds giving and reverence together: *wa-ṣaddaqa bi-l-ḥusnā* — "and he attests to what is most beautiful." The verb *ṣaddaqa*, from *ṣ-d-q*, is the intensive form of *ṣadaqa*, to be truthful: it means to hold something true, to ratify it, to give one's full assent to a claim — the same root that gives *taṣdīq*, the confirmation the prophets give one another, and *ṣidq*, the integrity of a person whose inside matches his outside. To *ṣaddaqa bi-l-ḥusnā* is therefore not a passive opinion but an act of ratification: the servant has examined the most beautiful claim reality offers and has set his seal to it. The commentators emphasize that this third quality is, in the order of causation, the first: no one gives what costs him and guards himself against his own appetites unless he has first ratified something beyond the visible. Belief is the engine; the two preceding verses showed the engine's output.

**What Is "The Most Beautiful"?**

The word *al-ḥusnā* — the feminine superlative of *ḥasan*, "beautiful, good, excellent" — is left, like the objects of verses 1 and 5, without an explicit referent, and the tradition filled the silence with a garden of readings, each anchored somewhere in the Qur'an. The majority position, reported through Ibn ʿAbbās and favored by al-Shawkānī and others: *al-ḥusnā* is *lā ilāha illā Llāh* — the testimony that there is no god but God — so that attesting to what is most beautiful is attesting to the most beautiful of all statements, the word the Qur'an calls the *kalimat aṭ-ṭayyibah*, the good word, in 14:24–25. A second great reading, held by al-Zamakhsharī among others: it is simply belief in God — attestation of His existence, His oneness, and His right to be worshipped. A third, transmitted by Ibn Kathīr, al-Shawkānī, and al-Zamakhsharī: *al-ḥusnā* is Paradise — the most beautiful reward — anchored in the phrase with which the Qur'an names the recompense of the Hereafter, *ḥusna thawābi l-ākhirah*: *fa-āṭāhumu Llāhu thawāba d-dunyā wa-ḥusna thawābi l-ākhirah*, "So God granted them the reward of this world and the most beautiful reward of the Hereafter" (3:148; cf. 3:195). To attest to *al-ḥusnā* in this sense is to believe that such a reward exists and is real — that behind the veil of the visible stands a recompense worthy of the Beautiful. And a fourth reading, which the source commends as perfectly coherent with the text: *al-ḥusnā* points to God Himself, for the Qur'an repeatedly declares *wa-lillāhi l-asmāʾu l-ḥusnā* — "Unto Him belong the Most Beautiful Names" (7:180; 17:110; 20:8; 59:24) — so that attesting to what is most beautiful is attesting to the Most Beautiful: to His Names, His promise, and His reality.

**Why the Readings Do Not Compete**

The classical way with such a spread of readings is worth exhibiting in miniature, because it is a model of how the tradition thinks. Al-Ṭabarī's method, applied throughout his immense tafsīr, is to ask whether the reported meanings contradict or accumulate. Here they accumulate. The oneness of God, the reality of Paradise, and the beauty of God's own Names are not rival referents of *al-ḥusnā* but concentric ones: the testimony *lā ilāha illā Llāh* is the linguistic vessel; the most beautiful reward is what the testimony secures; the Most Beautiful Himself is what the testimony is *about*. A servant who attests to the one, believes the reward, and knows the Named has attested to *al-ḥusnā* in all its senses at once. The Qur'an's choice of the superlative with its article — *the* most beautiful — performs the same generalizing work as the suppressed objects elsewhere in the sūrah: whatever is most beautiful in existence, this is the one who ratifies it, and whatever falls short of most beautiful, this is not where he has placed his weight.

**Attestation Explains the Open Hand**

With the three referents on the table, the verse explains the psychology of generosity itself, which is why the sūrah positioned it where it did. A man parts with wealth he has labored for only when he believes in a better economy than the visible one. If the only reward is the reward of this world, rationality is hoarding; the miser of verses 8–9 is, in fact, the perfectly logical man *given* his creed of denial — which is exactly why his denial is named alongside his miserliness. The giver, by contrast, is rational in the opposite economy: he has ratified *al-ḥusnā*, and a ratified unseen reward converts charity from loss into investment. The Qur'an states this arithmetic repeatedly: *mā ʿindakum yanfadu wa-mā ʿinda Llāhi bāq*, "what is with you is exhausted, and what is with God remains" (16:96); *man dhā lladhī yuqriḍu Llāha qarḍan ḥasanan fa-yuḍāʿifahū lahū aḍʿāfan kathīrah*, "Who is he that will lend God a good loan, that He may multiply it for him manifold?" (2:245). Ibn Kathīr reads verse 6 together with verse 5 in exactly this light: the attestation is what makes the giving *giving* rather than expenditure. It is also what separates Islamic generosity from mere philanthropy of temperament: the attestation fixes the recipient of ultimate credit — God — and thereby protects the deed from collapsing into social transaction, a protection the sūrah will legislate explicitly in verses 19–20.

**A Creed With Consequences Built In**

The verse finally asserts something about the nature of belief in the Qur'an's anthropology: attestation is not a private interior state that leaves the limbs unemployed. The intensive form *ṣaddaqa* is used in the Qur'an of faith that issues in action — *wa-lammā yaʾtihim rasūlun min ʿindi Llāhi muṣaddiqun limā maʿahum*, and of the prophets' mutual confirmation, and it is the verb chosen for Abū Bakr's epithet *al-Ṣiddīq*, the man of total ratification, whose title he earned precisely because his attestation translated instantly into action on the morning of every test. The sūrah's first portrait is thus internally consistent down to its grammar: a giving hand, a guarding soul, and a ratifying heart — the heart last in word order because it is the foundation laid beneath the building after the building is already visible to the onlooker. What the onlooker sees is a man who gives and refrains; what the verse reveals is the belief that pays for both.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:7

> **We shall ease his way unto ease.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reward Matches the Deed in Kind**

The verdict on the first portrait is now pronounced, and its wording is one of the Qur'an's great sentences: *fa-sanuyassiruhu li-l-yusrā* — "We shall ease his way unto ease." The verb *nuyassiruhu* is from *y-s-r*, ease, and the same root gives the sūrah's noun *al-yusrā*, "the state/course of ease," so that the sentence says, in a compressed form impossible to reproduce in any translation: We will make easy for him the easy. The commentators observe the justice of the construction: the reward matches the deed in kind. This man made things easy for others — he gave where others withheld, and his reverence kept him from making life hard for the creatures around him — and so God makes the easy easy for him. The Prophet ﷺ stated this law of correspondence as a general principle: *"Whoever relieves a believer of one of the hardships of this world, God will relieve him of one of the hardships of the Day of Resurrection; whoever makes things easy for one in difficulty, God will make things easy for him in this world and the Hereafter"* (Muslim 2699, from Abū Hurayrah). The verse is that principle raised to its highest power: not one hardship relieved for one, but the entire road made easy.

**What Al-Yusrā Is**

The noun *al-yusrā* is the feminine superlative/elative of ease, and the commentators identified its referents along the same converging lines they used for *al-ḥusnā*. The earliest cluster of reports, gathered by al-Ṭabarī from Ibn ʿAbbās and the generation after him, reads *al-yusrā* as the good outcome — for good — meaning that God will facilitate for him the deeds of goodness in this life, so that obedience becomes his natural element. Ibn Kathīr states this plainly: "We will make easy for him the path to the good in this world" — and the path to Paradise in the next, since the two are one road with two horizons. Al-Qurṭubī gathers the range: *al-yusrā* is obedience, or Paradise, or both together; and al-Zamakhsharī, as quoted in the source, develops the mechanism: God makes obedience amiable to him and grants him success (*tawfīq*) until obedience becomes "the easiest and simplest of things for him," citing *fa-man yuridi Llāhu an yahdiyahu yashraḥ ṣadrahū li-l-islām*, "Whomsoever God wishes to guide, He expands his breast for submission" (6:125). The word the tradition settles on for this divine action is *tawfīq* — the aligning of the servant's capacity with the good he is called to, a help that does not coerce but clears.

**Ease as the Qur'an's Own Self-Description of the Religion**

The promise also resonates with the Qur'an's repeated self-description. This sūrah's verse is the companion of 87:8, *wa-nuyassiruka li-l-yusrā* — "and We shall ease you unto ease" — addressed to the Prophet ﷺ himself, and the two verses together teach that what was given to him by way of prophetic ease is given to his followers by way of servanthood's ease. The Book states the principle of the whole religion in one line: *yurīdu Llāhu bikumu l-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu l-ʿusr*, "God desires ease for you, and He does not desire hardship for you" (2:185); and the practical corollary in another: *wa-man yattaqi Llāha yajʿal lahū min amrihī yusrā*, "And whosoever reverences God, He will appoint ease for his affair" (65:4). Read together, these three verses form a syllogism the sūrah is building deliberately: the religion is founded on ease (2:185); reverence is the appointed cause of ease (65:4); therefore the reverent giver is being carried, verse by verse, toward the ease he was promised from the beginning (92:7). The Prophet ﷺ ratified the same principle in practice and word: *"Religion is easy, and no one overburdens himself in religion without being overcome by it"* (al-Bukhārī 39). Ease, in this vocabulary, is not laxity; it is the state of a road whose obstacles have been removed by the One who owns the road.

**The Psychology of the Eased Road**

The commentators' insistence that *al-yusrā* begins *in this world* deserves a pause, because it is where the verse touches experience. Every person has observed the phenomenon the verse describes: certain people find good easy. Prayer comes to them without negotiation with themselves; generosity costs them no visible struggle; restraint from the forbidden feels, to them, like relief rather than deprivation. Others fight the same battles daily and lose them. The verse does not deny effort — the man of verses 5–6 gave, guarded, and attested — but it describes what grace does to effort over time: it lowers the gradient. Al-Ghazālī's *Iḥyāʾ* explains the same phenomenon by habit and divine habit together: the repeated act, done for God's sake, gradually re-centers the appetite until the soul's inclination and the Law's demand coincide, and from that moment the servant runs downhill. The modern science of habit formation describes, in its own secular grammar, exactly the mechanism the tradition calls *tawfīq*-assisted practice: cues, repetitions, and rewards rewire the path of least resistance. The verse's claim is that this rewiring, in the case of the reverent giver, is not merely psychological but providential — that God Himself is the agent of the easing: *sanuyassiruhu*, "We shall ease him." The difference matters: a self-made discipline can be lost in a crisis; an ease granted from above is the servant's in every season.

**The Eschatological Horizon**

At the far end of the road, *al-yusrā* is the Hereafter's ease: the lightened reckoning, the gentle passage, the Garden. The Qur'an elsewhere names the two final experiences with the same root's family: *fa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahū bi-yamīnihī fa-sawfa yuḥāsabu ḥisāban yasīrā*, "As for him who is given his book in his right hand, he shall be reckoned with an easy reckoning" (84:7–8); and its opposite: *wa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahū warāʾa ẓahrihī fa-sawfa yadʿū thubūran wa-yaṣlā saʿīrā*, "but as for him who is given his book behind his back, he shall cry for perdition and enter the blazing fire" (84:10–12). The sūrah's own ending supplies the content of that ease: contentment (*yardā*, v. 21). Ease, at its terminus, is not a location but a state — the soul made fully easy, because fully satisfied. The sūrah thus encloses its first portrait in a single word's family: from *yassara* in the verdict to *yusrā* in the destination, and from there to *riḍā* in the last verse, where the eased soul rests.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:8

> **And as for one who is miserly and deems himself beyond need,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inverse Portrait Begins with a Closed Hand**

The sūrah now turns the canvas over. The second portrait is drawn stroke for stroke against the first, and its first stroke is the closed hand: *wa-ammā man bakhila*, "and as for one who is miserly." The verb *bakhila* — from *b-kh-l* — is the exact antonym of *aʿṭā*: where the first man's defining motion was release, this man's is retention. The Qur'an uses the word and its family for the specific moral disease of wealth-withholding: *alladhīna yabkhalūna wa-yaʾmurūna n-nāsa bi-l-bukhl*, "those who are miserly and command people to be miserly" (4:37); and *wa-lā yaḥsabanna lladhīna yabkhalūna bimā ātāhumu Llāhu min faḍlihī huwa khayran lahum bal huwa sharrun lahum*, "And let not those who are miserly with what God has given them of His bounty suppose it is good for them; rather it is evil for them" (3:180). The sūrah's parallelism is exact in one more respect: as *aʿṭā* in verse 5 was left without an object and therefore widened to every kind of giving, *bakhila* here is objectless and therefore widened to every kind of withholding — of wealth, of help, of intercession, of kindness, of the self. The miser is not merely the man who refuses money; he is the man whose fundamental orientation toward existence is to keep.

**The Second Stroke: The Illusion of Self-Sufficiency**

The portrait's second quality is *wa-staghnā* — "and he deems himself beyond need." The verb is the tenth form of *gh-n-y*, to be wealthy, to be without want; the tenth form carries the sense of *regarding oneself* as such, and the translation "deems himself beyond need" preserves precisely that reflexive arrogance. The source's note on the word is decisive: literally it means "self-sufficient," but "beyond need" better carries the negative charge of viewing oneself as self-sufficient *vis-à-vis God*. The Qur'an's own diagnosis of the type is 96:6–7, *kallā inna l-insāna la-yaṭghā an raʾāhu staghnā* — "Nay! Truly man transgresses, because he sees himself beyond need" — a verse the commentators cite here almost unanimously. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both develop the linkage: wealth produces the feeling of sufficiency, the feeling of sufficiency produces transgression, and transgression — *ṭughyān*, the same word used of Pharaoh — is the assertion of a sovereignty that belongs to God alone. The sūrah's pairing of miserliness with self-sufficiency therefore names a complete spiritual posture: the closed hand is the body-language of the closed creed.

**The Inversion of the Proper Axis**

The source draws out the verse's structural theology with unusual precision, and it deserves the fullest statement. The proper balance of the human position between Heaven and earth is two-directional: *upward* obedience and dependence upon God, *downward* generosity and responsibility toward the creatures. This sūrah's first portrait kept both directions right — reverence upward, giving downward. The second portrait inverts both: toward what is above him, the man claims sufficiency (*istaghnā*), refusing the dependence of a servant; toward what is below him, he is miserly (*bukhl*), refusing the generosity of a steward. He has reversed his place in the order of things: defiant toward his Superior, withholding toward his dependents. The commentators observe that the inversion is self-reinforcing — the man who will not admit need of God has no reason to admit obligation to people, and the man who gives nothing to people will find it natural to give nothing to God — and that is why the two qualities are conjoined in one verse rather than distributed. They are two faces of one error: the self enthroned as the sufficient one.

**A Diagnosis That Has Only Deepened**

The modern world offers the verse an audience the Makkan original could not have imagined. *Istighnā* — self-sufficiency as a creed — is arguably the founding assumption of secular modernity: the autonomous individual, the self-made fortune, the species that expects to engineer its own salvation from disease, scarcity, and death. The sūrah does not deny human competence; it denies the *theology* of competence, the quiet conviction that because one's needs are met, one is without a Lord. Its diagnostic sequence — need-met, then sufficiency-claimed, then transgression — can be tested against whole biographies and whole corporations: the point at which success stopped being received and started being owned is, in the sūrah's vocabulary, the point where *istaghnā* entered. Conversely, the verse's antidote is legible in its mirror: the reverent giver of verse 5 is the man who remains consciously *in need* — of God above, of the obligation to people below — and whose giving is the daily enacted confession of that need. The ḥadīth tradition makes the confession verbal as well: *"O God, I am Your servant… I ask You by every Name that is Yours… that You make the Qur'an the spring of my heart"* and, above all, the Prophet's own standing supplication recorded by Muslim: *"O Living, O Everlasting, by Your mercy I seek help; set right for me all my affair, and do not entrust me to myself even for the blink of an eye"* (from the long ḥadīth of Abū Saʿīd, in the collections; cf. al-Ḥākim). The servant's entire posture is the denial of *istaghnā* — never, not for the blink of an eye, left to his own sufficiency.

**The Pair, Together, Is the Creed**

One final point of structure. In the first portrait, the third quality (attestation of the most beautiful) explained the first two. In this portrait too, the two named qualities await their third: the denial of verse 9. Miserliness and self-sufficiency are not yet the complete man — they are the disposition; the denial is the doctrine. The sūrah draws the body of the second portrait in this verse and reserves its creed for the next, preserving the pedagogical order of the first portrait exactly: deed and posture first, underlying belief after. The symmetry is one more sign — in a sūrah entirely about symmetries — that both portraits are drawn by the same hand and measured by the same scale.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:9

> **and denies what is most beautiful,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Creed of the Closed Hand**

The third stroke of the second portrait supplies its doctrine: *wa-kadhdhaba bi-l-ḥusnā*, "and he denies what is most beautiful." The verb *kadhdhaba* — the intensive form of *k-dh-b*, to lie, to declare false — is the exact antonym of *ṣaddaqa* in verse 6: where the first man ratified, this man falsifies. And the object is the identical object: *al-ḥusnā*, the most beautiful. The two portraits have now been drawn with perfect mirror-symmetry on all three of their qualities — giving against miserliness, reverence against self-sufficiency, ratification against denial — and the sūrah's argument reaches its sharpest point: the same "most beautiful" that stands before every human being is met by one sort of soul with attestation and by the other with denial. The object does not change; the encounter does.

**The Meanings of Al-Ḥusnā, Reversed**

The referents the tradition identified for *al-ḥusnā* in verse 6 apply here in exactly the same range, now taken in the mode of rejection. Denying *al-ḥusnā* means, on the majority reading reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, rejecting the testimony *lā ilāha illā Llāh* — refusing the most beautiful of statements. On al-Zamakhsharī's reading it means refusing belief in God Himself — living as though the Most Beautiful does not exist or does not matter. On the reading cited by Ibn Kathīr and al-Shawkānī it means denying Paradise — rejecting the reality of the most beautiful reward, the *ḥusna* of 3:148 and 3:195, so that nothing remains on the far side of death to be hoped for. And on the reading the source finds most comprehensive, it means denying God Himself, of Whom the Most Beautiful Names are the revealed signature — *wa-lillāhi l-asmāʾu l-ḥusnā* (7:180; 17:110; 20:8; 59:24). Al-Ṭabarī's method again treats these as accumulating rather than competing: the denier of the testimony, the denier of the reward, and the denier of the Named are converging figures, because each denial entails the others. The Qur'an elsewhere names the same convergence from the opposite side: those who disbelieve in God, His angels, His Books, and His messengers are each disbelieving in the whole by disbelieving in any part (4:150–151).

**Why Denial Is Named After Generosity and Before Verdict**

The ordering of the three qualities in this portrait carries the sūrah's moral psychology in full. Denial is named third, after miserliness and self-sufficiency, and the commentators read that position as diagnostic rather than accidental. Takdhīb — the declaration that the most beautiful is false — rarely begins as a purely intellectual event in the Qur'an's anthropology; it ripens out of a posture already adopted. The man who has closed his hand to the creatures and closed his need toward the Creator has constructed a life in which the most beautiful claim — that there is a God, a reckoning, and a reward — arrives as an intrusion and an indictment. Denial is therefore the creed the closed life requires, and the sūrah presents it where it appears in experience: last in order of arrival, first in order of destruction. The Qur'an states the same sequence in other passages, attributing the sealed heart to accumulated refusal: *kallā bal rāna ʿalā qulūbihim mā kānū yaksibūn*, "Nay! Rather what they used to earn has rusted over their hearts" (83:14); and *fa-lammā zāghū azāgha Llāhu qulūbahum*, "When they deviated, God made their hearts deviate" (61:5). None of this removes responsibility — the first refusal was free — but it explains why denial so often appears, biographically, as the terminus of a road that began with withheld kindness and imagined sufficiency.

**The Logic: A World Without the Most Beautiful Is a World of the Miser**

The verse's deepest logic is the one already glimpsed in verse 8: if *al-ḥusnā* is denied, miserliness becomes the only rational economics. The man who believes in nothing beyond the visible holds in his hands the only treasure that exists; of course he clutches it. The man who believes in a reward with God holds, in every coin he gives, an instrument of exchange into the most beautiful economy; of course he spends. The two men in the two portraits can be made, in thought-experiment, to swap creeds while keeping their characters — and the sūrah implies that their characters would not survive the swap. Generosity and attestation stand or fall together; denial and hoarding stand or fall together. This is why the Qur'an consistently refuses to separate creed from conduct in its final accounting, and why this sūrah — the shortest complete treatise on the subject in the Book — will in verse 11 add the demonstration: the hoard itself fails its owner at the hour it was hoarded for.

**Takdhīb as a Standing Qur'anic Charge**

It is worth noting the weight the word *takdhīb* carries across the Makkan revelation as a whole. Sūrah al-Māʿūn (107) opens with the same root: *a-raʾayta lladhī yukadhdhibu bi-d-dīn*, "Have you seen him who denies the religion?" — and immediately identifies the denier by his conduct: he repulses the orphan and withholds the food of the poor, the same two marks of the closed hand. Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn (83) builds its entire portrait of the defrauder on the same foundation: *wa-mā yuʿallimūhā illā kullu muʿtadin athīm… idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā qāla asāṭīru l-awwalīn*, and it concludes with the declaration *wa-mā yukadhdhibu bihī illā kullu muʿtadin athīm*, "and none denies it but every sinful transgressor" (83:12). The pattern is constant: the Book reads denial off the hands before it hears it off the tongue. Al-Layl's ninth verse is that school of reading in its most compressed form — three words completing a portrait whose first two qualities had already made the third inevitable.

**What the Denier Actually Denies**

A final observation, theological rather than polemical. To deny *al-ḥusnā* is, in the strictest sense, to call beauty itself a lie — to assert that the deepest truth of things is not the Beautiful, that the Names which adorn the Throne are not real, that the reward promised to the good is a story, and that the testimony which has carried every prophet is false. The sūrah does not here argue with the denier; the oaths of the first three verses were that argument, marshaled from the structure of the cosmos. It simply records the denial as the third quality of the second portrait, and proceeds — in the very next verse — to pronounce upon it the verdict the oaths were assembled to certify.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:10

> **We shall ease his way unto hardship;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Chilling Sentence in the Sūrah**

The verdict on the second portrait is pronounced in words exactly parallel to the first portrait's promise, and exactly terrifying: *fa-sanuyassiruhu li-l-ʿusrā* — "We shall ease his way unto hardship." The verb is the same verb of easing, *nuyassir*; the destination alone is changed — *al-ʿusrā*, the hard, the difficult, the state and course of hardship, from *ʿ-s-r*, the root of *ʿusr*, difficulty, which gives the Qur'an its great pair in Sūrah al-Sharḥ: *inna maʿa l-ʿusri yusrā*, "indeed, with hardship comes ease" (94:6). Here the two are inverted: not ease after hardship, but ease *into* hardship — the road downhill, made smooth.

**The Paradox of the Eased Hardship**

The commentators pause over the paradox, because the verse intends it. How is hardship "eased"? Al-Ṭabarī gathers the early readings: *al-ʿusrā* is glossed by many as evil — the works of evil and the road to them — and the easing is understood as God's opening of that road to the one who has chosen it. Al-Zamakhsharī states it with characteristic directness: God makes the paths of evil smooth for him, facilitating his descent as He facilitated the other man's ascent. Ibn Kathīr summarizes the consensus reading: as the first man was eased into the good by *tawfīq*, this man is eased into the evil by *khidhlān* — that solemn theological term for the withdrawal of divine assistance, the leaving of the servant to the consequences of his own chosen direction. The source's gloss adds the dynamic dimension: when people disbelieve and commit evil deeds, God opens the way for them to commit further evil, and it becomes increasingly difficult to turn back — the reciprocity of the turned-away road, answering the reciprocity of the turned-toward one in verse 7.

**The Mechanism of the Slippery Road**

What the tradition means by this easing is described, in sober terms, as both psychological fact and divine act. Psychologically, it is the law of habit read in the negative: every repeated refusal lowers the threshold of the next one. The first withheld charity costs a struggle; the tenth costs nothing; the man of the fiftieth withholding no longer notices the poor man at all. The heart that said its first "no" to the most beautiful did so against the grain of its own creation — *fiṭrah* — but each subsequent denial wears the groove deeper, until the grain itself seems reversed. The Qur'an describes this hardening process in a series of precise images: the rust of 83:14 (*rāna ʿalā qulūbihim*), the seals of 2:7 (*khatama Llāhu ʿalā qulūbihim*), the narrowing chest of 6:125's counterpart — *wa-man yurid an yuḍillahū yajʿal ṣadrahū ḍayyiqan ḥarajan*, "and whomever He wills to send astray, He makes his breast narrow and constricted" (6:125). Theologically, the easing is God's act — the sūrah says *We* shall ease him — because nothing in creation moves except by His permitting and creating; but the servant's choice is the cause, and the tradition guards both truths in one sentence. This is precisely the balance the graveside ḥadīth of ʿAlī taught when the Prophet ﷺ recited these very verses: seats are written, *and* "Work! For everyone is eased unto that for which he was created" (al-Bukhārī 4945; Muslim 2647). Muslim also records from Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh the same formula stated as a universal law: *"Everyone is eased unto that for which he was created"* (Muslim 2649). The easing of our verse is that law's dark instance — facilitation descending where facilitation ought to ascend.

**Why the Verse Is a Mercy Even So**

The severest classical commentators note — and the observation must not be lost — that the verse is still, in its way, a warning and therefore a mercy. It is pronounced *before* the road has fully closed, inside a sūrah that will spend its final seven verses offering the way out. The easing into hardship is described in the future tense over a type still alive and still choosing; the sūrah's own last portrait will show the type's escape — a man who once stood where the miser stands and then gave, purified, and sought the Face. Nothing in the doctrine of the eased road licenses despair, because the door of repentance stands open for as long as the servant can turn: *qul yā ʿibādiya lladhīna asrafū ʿalā anfusihim lā taqnaṭū min raḥmati Llāh*, "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against yourselves, do not despair of God's mercy; truly God forgives sins altogether" (39:53). What the verse abolishes is not hope but presumption — the comfortable belief that one can drift in God's direction while walking away from Him. The eased road of hardship is the shape that drifting takes: downhill, smoothed, and gaining speed.

**The Modern Road, Smoothly Paved**

No generation has ever lacked the material of this verse, but the present one has industrialized it. Every age has its smooth descents; ours is distinguished by the engineering of frictionlessness — feeds that keep scrolling, transactions that keep closing, choices that keep defaulting — each designed by a thousand skilled hands to make the next step in any chosen direction cost less than the last. The sūrah's word for a life built on frictionlessness in the wrong direction is *taysīr li-l-ʿusrā*: the terrifying dignity of being *helped* downhill. Its counter-word is equally available: the same sūrah's verse 7 — ease into ease — is built one deliberate uphill act at a time, each one lightening the next, until the good becomes, as al-Zamakhsharī said of the first portrait's owner, "the easiest and simplest of things." Both roads are eased; the verse's only question is which easing a person is presently inside.

**The Symmetry, Completed**

With this verdict the two portraits stand complete and perfectly balanced: three qualities each, one promise each, pronounced in identical grammar with exchanged destinations. The sūrah has now said everything essential about the fork of verse 4 — the scattered strivings have been shown scattering toward exactly two arrivals. What remains is for the sūrah to demonstrate that the stakes are real (verse 11), to declare the ground on which both roads stand (verses 12–13), and to show the two arrivals themselves (verses 14–21). The portraits are finished; the landscape of their destinations is next.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:11

> **and his wealth shall not avail him when he perishes.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demonstration: The Hoard Fails at the Hour It Was Hoarded For**

The portraits and their verdicts are complete; the sūrah now appends the demonstration that seals the argument against the second man. *Wa-mā yughnī ʿanhu māluhu idhā taraddā* — "and his wealth shall not avail him when he perishes." The verb *yughnī* is from *gh-n-y* — the very root of *istaghnā* in verse 8 — so the sentence puns on the man's own creed with surgical economy: he deemed himself beyond need (*staghnā*), and his wealth shall not suffice him (*lā yughnī ʿanhu*) in the one hour sufficiency was for. The word-play is noted by the rhetoricians of the tafsīr tradition as one of the sūrah's quiet master-strokes: the man of imagined sufficiency is met at the end by the insufficiency of the very instrument of his imagination.

**Taraddā: The Falling Man**

The verse's other key word is *taraddā*, from *r-d-y*, whose concrete sense is to fall down, to perish — and, in its most vivid Qur'anic usage, to fall into a pit or abyss. The same root gives the Qur'an's name for Hell in Sūrah al-Qāriʿah: *fa-ummuhū hāwiyah* — "his mother is the Abyss" (101:9) — and the classical commentators divide on which fall the verse means. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī both preserve the two readings side by side: *idhā taraddā* may mean when he dies — the fall every human being makes out of this world — or when he falls into the Fire, the second and final fall. Al-Qurṭubī cites both and adds the grammatical observation that the verb's form suits both, since death itself is described in the Qur'an as a fall into what follows: *kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu l-mawt*, "every soul shall taste death" (3:185). The doubled meaning is the verse's intent: the hoard fails at the first fall, and it fails again at the second.

**The Grammar of Futility**

The opening words *mā yughnī ʿanhu* admit the two parsings the source records. Read as a negation — "his wealth shall not avail him" — the sentence is a flat declaration of futility. Read as an interrogation — "what shall his wealth avail him when he perishes?" — it becomes a rhetorical question expecting the answer "nothing." Both parsings are classical, and both carry the same verdict; the interrogative reading has the additional force of making the listener supply the nothingness himself. The Qur'an elsewhere states the same futility in a series of scenes that amount to a dossier on this verse. The hoarder's own soliloquy is recorded in Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah: *mā aghnā ʿannī māliyah, halaka ʿannī sulṭāniyah* — "My wealth has not availed me; my power has perished from me" (69:28–29). The would-be ransom is refused in Sūrah al-Maʿārij: *yubaṣṣarūnahum yawaddu l-mujrimu law yaftadī min ʿadhābi yawmiʾidhin bi-banīhi wa-ṣāḥibatihī wa-akhīhi*, "the guilty one would love to ransom himself from the punishment of that Day by his children, his spouse, and his brother" (70:11–13) — and the refusal follows. Sūrah al-Masad pronounces the verdict over a specific man and his fortune: *mā aghnā ʿanhu māluhū wa-mā kasab*, "his wealth availed him not, nor what he earned" (111:2). And the general law is stated in the sūrah of the family of ʿImrān: *inna lladhīna kafarū lan tughniya ʿanhum amwāluhum wa-lā awlāduhum mina Llāhi shayʾā*, "as for those who disbelieve, neither their wealth nor their children shall avail them against God at all" (3:10; cf. 3:91, 10:54). Al-Layl's eleven words are the thesis of which those passages are the proofs.

**Why Wealth Fails Precisely Then**

The logic the commentators extract is worth stating plainly, because it converts the verse from a threat into an explanation. Wealth is an instrument of this world's economy; death is the border where that economy's currency ceases to be legal tender. Everything a person owns derives its value from a system — markets, laws, bodies, time — that death dissolves in a single instant. The hoarder's error, in the sūrah's analysis, was not that he valued wealth but that he misdated its usefulness: he stored value in the only instrument that cannot cross the border, when the border was the one certainty of his calendar. The Prophet ﷺ gave the principle its positive formulation in the ḥadīth every Muslim child learns: *"When the son of Adam dies, his works are cut off except three: an ongoing charity (ṣadaqah jāriyah), or knowledge by which people benefit, or a righteous child who prays for him"* (Muslim 1631, from Abū Hurayrah). Note the structure of the exception: the only wealth that survives death is wealth that was *converted into deed before death* — the ongoing charity being precisely the giver's act of verse 5 performed in advance of the fall. The miser and the giver hold identical assets; one converted them before the border, the other carried them to it in kind. The verse's *mā yughnī ʿanhu* is the customs declaration at that border.

**Inheritance as the Unwitting Almsgiving**

The commentators add one more observation, dark in its irony: the miser's wealth does not even rest with him at the fall — it passes, by the law of inheritance the Qur'an itself legislates (4:11–12), to heirs whose prayers may or may not reach him and whose spending he can no longer direct. He who withheld from the poor in life finds his hoard dispersed, often within hours of his death, among the claimants of the estate. The great early teacher Ḥasan al-Baṣrī is reported to have summarized the economics of this verse in a single line preserved in the ascetic literature: the miser is a courier who carries another's treasure through a dangerous road and delivers it, at the journey's end, to someone else. The sūrah's verdict is that the road ends exactly there.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Argument**

Structurally, verse 11 closes the book on the two portraits. The sūrah has announced the fork (v. 4), drawn both travelers (vv. 5–10), and now demonstrated that the second traveler's chosen instrument of salvation is the one instrument that demonstrably fails. Nothing remains but for the sūrah to state the ground-rules of the road itself — which it does in the next two verses, turning from the travelers to the Owner of the road. The shift of voice that follows is one of the sūrah's great rhetorical movements: from description of human types to declaration of divine prerogatives.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:12

> **Truly Ours it is to give guidance,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ground-Rule of the Road**

The sūrah has shown two roads and two travelers; it now states the law under which both roads exist: *inna ʿalaynā la-l-hudā* — "Truly Ours it is to give guidance." The sentence is emphatic twice over — the *inna* and the lām of certitude — and its preposition is the verse's theology in miniature: guidance is not merely *with* God or *from* God; it is *upon* God (*ʿalaynā*), incumbent in the sense of undertaken, promised, and guaranteed by His own wisdom and grace. The Qur'an states the same prerogative elsewhere with the same grammar of obligation-upon-Himself: *wa-ʿalā Llāhi qaṣdu s-sabīl*, "And it is for God to show the way" (16:9), which al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī all cite here as the parallel of record. The verse thus performs a double work: it honors the servant by assuring him that guidance has been provided and cannot be absent from any age or any land, and it humbles him by reserving the authorship of that guidance to its Owner.

**The Two Readings of "Upon Us Is Guidance"**

The commentators divide the verse's meaning along two great lines, both preserved in the sources the annotations cite. The first, transmitted from the early authorities and favored by al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr among others, reads the verse as a promise of *bayān* — explanation: upon God is to make clear the guidance from the misguidance, the lawful from the prohibited, the road from the ravine. On this reading, *inna ʿalaynā la-l-hudā* is the sūrah's compressed statement of the prophetic enterprise itself: God has undertaken to explain, and the explanation has in fact arrived — in messengers, in books, and finally in this Book. The second reading, also cited by Ibn Kathīr from a group of the commentators, is more mystical in register: the verse means that whoever travels the path of guidance arrives at God — the road, once taken, is guaranteed to terminate at its Maker. On this reading the promise is to the walker rather than about the road: take the guided path and the destination is underwritten. Al-Qurṭubī records both without forcing a choice, and the later tradition has generally followed him, since the two readings accumulate: God undertakes to show the way, and He undertakes to be found at its end.

**Why This Verse Stands Exactly Here**

The placement of the verse is part of the argument. It comes immediately after the demonstration that the hoard fails (v. 11) and immediately before the declaration that both worlds are God's (v. 13), and the sequence is a syllogism addressed to the two travelers of the portraits. To the miser: your instrument has failed you, and the only provision that does not fail — guidance — is not purchasable, because it is dispensed by prerogative, not by price; you cannot buy what God gives by His own undertaking, which is why the attestation of verse 6 was free and the reward of verse 7 was pure grace. To the giver: the guidance by which you gave, guarded, and attested was itself the first gift — *ʿalaynā*, upon Us — and the ease promised you in verse 7 is therefore continuous with it: the One who undertook to guide has undertaken to ease. The verse thereby answers, in advance, the deepest worry of both portraits — the miser's fear that guidance is a commodity he has failed to acquire, and the giver's fear that his guidance was his own achievement. Neither is true. Guidance is God's undertaking, and the servants' part is reception.

**The Doctrine of the Prerogative**

The verse also states, in the context of creed, one of the Qur'an's most repeated boundary-lines: guidance, in its full sense of creating guidance in a heart, belongs to God alone, and the Prophet ﷺ himself — the best of guided and the guide of the guided — is excluded from it in that sense. *Innaka lā tahdī man aḥbabta wa-lākinna Llāha yahdī man yashāʾ*, "Truly you do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills" (28:56); and *laysa ʿalayka hudāhum wa-lākinna Llāha yahdī man yashāʾ*, "Not upon you is their guidance; rather, God guides whom He wills" (2:272). The commentators reconcile these with *wa-innaka la-tahdī ilā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm*, "and truly you guide unto a straight path" (42:52) by distinguishing the two guidances: guidance of showing (*hidāyat al-bayān*), which belongs to messengers and heirs of messengers, and guidance of granting success (*hidāyat al-tawfīq*), which belongs to God alone — *ʿalaynā*, upon Us, in the grammar of this verse. The distinction is the theological backbone of the entire sūrah: the portraits of verses 5–10 describe precisely the two guidances at work — the shown road in the preached message, and the eased road in the facilitated heart.

**The Modern Lesson: Guidance Cannot Be Manufactured**

For the contemporary reader the verse's lesson is liberating in a specific and urgent way. The modern condition is characterized by an unprecedented surplus of information and an unprecedented famine of guidance — everyone can access every claim, and almost no one can settle which claim is true. The markets of the age sell substitutes: algorithms that recommend, influencers that persuade, ideologies that recruit. The sūrah's verse announces that the real article is not in any of these jurisdictions: guidance is God's own undertaking, dispensed on His terms, to the heart that positions itself where He has promised to provide it — before His Book, in His worship, along the road of His messengers. The practical corollary the tradition draws is simple: ask the Owner. The greatest daily enactment of the verse is the prayer every Muslim recites a minimum of seventeen times a day — *ihdinā ṣ-ṣirāṭa l-mustaqīm*, "Guide us to the straight path" (1:6) — which is, in light of this verse, the servant addressing the undertaking to the Undertaker: You have made guidance upon Yourself, so fulfill it for us. Al-Fātiḥah asks; al-Layl explains why the asking is well-founded.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:13

> **and truly unto Us belong the Hereafter and this world.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ownership of Both Worlds**

The ground-rule of guidance is now completed by the ground-rule of ownership: *wa-inna lanā la-l-ākhirata wa-l-ūlā* — "and truly unto Us belong the Hereafter and this world." The verse is emphatic again twice over, and its preposition — *lanā*, "to Us belong" — is the preposition of exclusive possession. The Qur'an states the same thesis in almost identical words elsewhere: *wa-inna lanā la-l-ākhirata wa-l-ūlā* is the twin of *fa-lillāhi l-ākhiratu wa-l-ūlā*, "Yet unto God belong the Hereafter and this world" (53:25), which the commentators cite here as the verse's own scriptural witness. The pairing of the two declarations across Sūrah al-Najm and Sūrah al-Layl — one Makkan sūrah explaining the other — is among the instances the early scholars gathered under the heading of the Qur'an's self-interpretation.

**Why Both Worlds Are Named, and Not One**

The verse's double object is its entire message, and it cuts in two directions at once. Against the man of this world — the miser of the second portrait, who has staked everything on *al-ūlā* — the verse declares that the world he has staked himself upon is not his, and never was: it belongs to the One of Whom he has deemed himself beyond need. Against the despair of the persecuted believer — the original audience of this Makkan sūrah, stripped of property and protection for their confession — the verse declares that the Hereafter they were promised is equally owned by their Lord, and the One Who owns both can transfer the treasures of one into the other at will. Al-Qurṭubī develops this second reading by citing the verse the source annotation brings forward: *man kāna yurīdu thawāba d-dunyā fa-ʿinda Llāhi thawābu d-dunyā wa-l-ākhirah*, "Whosoever desires the reward of this world — with God is the reward of this world and the Hereafter" (4:134): both treasuries are in one hand, and the servant has been addressing his petitions to a split treasury of his own imagination. The same commentators note the mirror-case: the ownership extends to punishment as well as reward, citing *fa-akhadhahumu Llāhu nakāla l-ākhirati wa-l-ūlā*, "So God seized him with a punishment exemplary in the Hereafter and in this world" (79:25) — Pharaoh's double seizure being the negative proof of the double ownership.

**The Theology of the Single Treasury**

Al-Shawkānī, in the source's annotation, states the verse's comprehensive reading in one sentence: everything is God's, and He does with it as He wills. From that sentence the whole practical theology of the verse unfolds. If both worlds belong to one Owner, then the servant's strategy of dealing with reality simplifies radically: there is only one Court to petition, one Giver to thank, one Owner to obey. The polytheism of Makkah had divided the world's management among a hundred intermediaries, each requiring its own fee; the modern world has replaced the intermediaries with their secular equivalents — markets, networks, institutions, algorithms — each likewise requiring its own fee of devotion. The verse abolishes the entire pantheon, ancient or contemporary, with three words: both worlds are owned here. Every transaction the servant performs in either world is, in the final accounting, a transaction with a single Counterparty.

**The Logic of Seeking**

From the ownership the commentators derive the logic of seeking that the next section of the sūrah will dramatize. He who seeks a reward seeks it from its owner; he who seeks it from other than its owner seeks from one who owns nothing — the Qur'an's own description of false intercessors: *wa-yaʿbudūna min dūni Llāhi mā lā yaḍurruhum wa-lā yanfaʿuhum*, "and they worship, besides God, what neither harms them nor benefits them" (10:18); and more sharply, *hāʾulāʾi shufāʿāʾukum ʿinda Llāh*, "are these your intercessors with God?" (10:18). The miser of verse 8 was, in this light, a man who sought sufficiency from *al-ūlā* — from the world — as though it were an independent proprietor; the verse answers him that *al-ūlā* is property, not proprietor. The giver of verse 5 was the man who understood the ownership: he moved his wealth out of the world of change into the custody of the Owner of both worlds, which is precisely what the Qur'an calls lending God a good loan (2:245). The two portraits, seen from the vantage of this verse, differ on one point only: the accuracy of their map of ownership.

**The Verse's Comfort for the Persecuted**

One pastoral function of the verse deserves particular emphasis, because it belonged to the hour of its revelation. The first Muslims of Makkah were being offered a brutal bargain: apostatize and keep your standing, or confess and lose everything in *this* world. The verse answered the bargain before it could seduce anyone: the world with which they threaten you is not theirs to give or withhold — *inna lanā la-l-ākhirata wa-l-ūlā*. The threat of confiscation loses its sting the moment the threatened man knows both the world being confiscated and the world being promised belong to the same Lord Who made the promise. This is the same logic by which the sorcerers of Pharaoh, in Sūrah Ṭā Hā, absorbed the tyrant's ultimatum: *lan nuʾthiraka ʿalā mā jāʾanā mina l-bayyināt*, "we shall never prefer you over what has come to us of the clear proofs" (20:72) — they had seen, as the verse says, that both worlds were elsewhere owned. Al-Layl's thirteenth verse planted that same sight into the hearts of the Makkan believers one revelation at a time.

**The Pair, as the Hinge of the Sūrah**

Structurally, verses 12 and 13 together form the hinge on which the sūrah turns from its first half (the two travelers, vv. 4–11) to its second half (the two arrivals, vv. 14–21). The travelers have been described; the ground-rules have now been stated — guidance is God's undertaking, and both worlds are God's property. Only one question remains: what, under these ground-rules, awaits each traveler at the end of his road? The sūrah answers by turning, in the next verse, directly to its listeners with the first first-person address of the chapter — *fa-andhartukum*, "Thus have I warned *you*" — and the warning it delivers is the Fire itself.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:14

> **Thus have I warned you of a raging Fire,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First-Person Turn: The Warner Speaks**

The sūrah now shifts voice for the first time. Everything up to this point has been declaration — oaths, portraits, verdicts, ground-rules — delivered in the third person or the royal "We." With *fa-andhartukum* the address turns direct: "Thus have I warned *you* of a raging Fire." The verb *andhara* — from *n-dh-r*, the root of *nadhr*, vow, and *nadhīr*, warner — is the Qur'an's technical term for the prophetic warning of danger ahead, and its appearance here marks the moment the sūrah stops describing and starts addressing. The first-person singular — *I* have warned you — is understood by the commentators in the voices the tradition allows it: God Himself speaking directly to the hearers, or the Prophet ﷺ speaking by divine commission, since he is the appointed *nadhīr* — *innī lakum nadhīrun mubīn*, "I am for you a plain warner" (11:25; 67:26). On either voice, the verse's form is identical: warning, delivered personally, named Fire.

**Indhār: Warning as an Act of Mercy**

The theology of warning is worth pausing over, because the modern temperament misreads it. A warning is not the infliction of fear for its own sake; it is fear deployed in the service of escape. The man who shouts at a child wandering toward a well is not cruel — he is performing the one act of speech that can still save. The Qur'an's entire prophetic institution is built on this logic: messengers are sent before punishment, not after — *wa-mā kunnā muʿadhdhibīna ḥattā nabʿatha rasūlā*, "and We never punish until We have sent a messenger" (17:15). The verse's *fa-andhartukum* therefore carries, inside its severity, its own proof of mercy: the Fire being warned of is a Fire that can still be avoided, by exactly the two portraits' respective choices just displayed. A warning to the already-doomed would be mockery; a warning to the still-free is grace. Ibn Kathīr and the traditionists note that the Qur'an pairs the two prophetic offices in the same breath — *bashīran wa-nadhīrā*, "a bearer of good news and a warner" (2:119; 5:19; 48:8) — because a messenger of good news without warning is a deceiver, and a warner without good news is a torturer. Al-Layl supplies both: this verse is the nadhīr's office; the sūrah's final verse, *wa-la-sawfa yardā*, is the bashīr's.

**Nāran Talaẓẓā: The Fire That Blazes**

The Fire is described with a single participle, and the commentators work it fully. *Talaẓẓā* is from *l-ẓ-y*, the root of *laẓā* — blaze, flame — the same word the Qur'an uses as a proper name of Hell in Sūrah al-Maʿārij: *kallā innahā laẓā, naz-zāʿatan li-sh-shawā*, "Nay! Truly it is Laẓā, the remover of the skin of the head" (70:15–16). A fire *talaẓẓā* is a fire at full blaze — not embers, not smoke, not the fire that can be fed and managed, but the flame in its pure rage, licking upward, consuming what it touches. The Qur'an's dossier of this fire is extensive, and the commentators here gather its companions: the crushing fire of Sūrah al-Humazah — *kallā la-yunbadhanna fī l-ḥuṭamah, wa-mā adrāka mā l-ḥuṭamah, nāru Llāhi l-mūqadah*, "Nay! He will surely be thrown into the Crusher. And what will tell you what the Crusher is? The fire of God, kindled" (104:4–6); the fire of Sūrah al-Masad, *sa-yaṣlā nāran dhāta lahab*, "he shall enter a fire of flame" (111:3); the fire whose fuel is men and stones of 2:24 and 66:6. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both treat *talaẓẓā* as the comprehensive descriptor: this is fire in the absolute, the fire of which every earthly fire is, as the ḥadīth tradition phrases it, one part of seventy parts (al-Bukhārī 3265; Muslim 2843 — *"Your fires are one part of seventy parts of the fire of Gehenna"*).

**Why the Warning Comes Before the Description of Its Inhabitant**

The verse's position is deliberate: it announces the Fire first, in the rawest possible terms, and only afterward — in the next two verses — defines who enters it permanently. The order is pedagogically exact. Had the definition come first, the listener might have checked himself against it before feeling the danger; a man told "only the most wretched enter" tends to assume he is not the most wretched, and to relax. By announcing the blaze first — *I have warned YOU* — the sūrah ensures that every hearer stands, for one verse, inside the warning personally, before the criteria are supplied. The effect is the effect of the fire alarm before the report of which floor is burning: everyone moves first, inquires afterward. The classical preachers of Islam built their entire art on this verse's order — *takhwīf* before *tafsīl*, the fright before the footnotes — and the great tafsīr tradition preserved the balance by supplying, immediately, the criteria that prevent the fright from becoming despair.

**The Modern Reader and the Unfashionable Fire**

The contemporary encounter with this verse deserves honesty. The modern mind has largely abolished hell from its imaginative geography, and where it retains the word, it keeps it as a metaphor — for trauma, for addiction, for war. The sūrah's fire is none of these accommodations. It is *nāran talaẓẓā* — a named, warned-of, blazing reality, presented here as the destination of one of the two roads every striving human being is presently walking. The commentators' counsel to the modern reader is not to soften the verse but to remember what it is doing: it is a *warning*, which means it is addressed to the still-alive, the still-free, the still-able-to-choose. Every recitation of the verse is, in the tradition's understanding, an instance of the mercy it seems to contradict — one more delivery of the shout at the wandering child. The one truly severe reading of the verse, in the classical view, belongs to the man who hears it, understands it, and files it away as metaphor for someone else. The warning was second-person from its first syllable: *fa-andhartukum* — I have warned *you*.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:15

> **which none shall enter, save the most wretched,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Generated a Science**

Few verses in the short sūrahs have provoked more sustained discussion than this one: *lā yaṣlāhā illā l-ashqā* — "which none shall enter, save the most wretched." The verb *yaṣlā* is from *ṣ-l-y*, whose concrete sense is to warm oneself at a fire, and thence to enter it, be roasted by it, abide in it; the same root gives the Qur'an's repeated phrase *ṣalā nār* for entering Hell (as in 4:10, 88:4, 111:3). The noun *al-ashqā* is the superlative of *shaqī* — wretched, miserable, damned — from *sh-q-y*, the opposite of *saʿādah*, felicity. The apparent form of the sentence — none enters it *except* the most wretched — became, within a century of the revelation, one of the battle-verses of Islamic theology, because the Khawārij and the Muʿtazilah cited it against the sinful believers, and the Sunnī tradition answered them from the verse's own wording. The discussion deserves full statement, because the verse intends it.

**The Question the Verse Poses**

Taken in strict isolation, the verse seems to say that only *the most* wretched — the extremest of mankind — enter the Fire, which would appear to exclude ordinary sinners, and perhaps ordinary disbelievers, from it. The Khawārij and after them certain Muʿtazilī theologians wielded the verse precisely so: since no one enters the Fire but the most wretched, and the grave sinner among the Muslims is (they held) precisely the most wretched, his abode must be the Fire forever — every major sinner, in their system, having thereby become the superlative case. The Sunnī response, formulated by the early authorities and consolidated in the great tafsīrs, does not deny the verse's wording; it reads the wording to its full depth.

**The Classical Resolution: Eternal Entry Is What the Verse Describes**

Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both record the resolution that became standard. The key is the verb *ṣalā* in its full sense — entering the Fire *and abiding in it*, the complete immersion. The sinful believer, in the Sunnī doctrine fixed by the ḥadīth of intercession, may enter the Fire but does not *abide* in it: he is extracted, by the mercy of God and the intercession of the intercessors, for as little as the weight of a mustard seed of faith. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record the Prophet ﷺ saying: *"There will come out of the Fire whoever said 'lā ilāha illā Llāh' and has in his heart the weight of a barley grain of good, and there will come out of the Fire whoever said 'lā ilāha illā Llāh' and has in his heart the weight of a wheat grain of good, and there will come out of the Fire whoever said 'lā ilāha illā Llāh' and has in his heart the weight of a speck of good"* (al-Bukhārī 44; Muslim 193, from Anas). And the comprehensive statement: *"My intercession is for the people of the major sins among my community"* (al-Tirmidhī 2435; Abū Dāwūd 4739; Ibn Mājah 4310, graded ṣaḥīḥ). The one for whom the Fire is *ṣalā* in the absolute sense — entry without exit — is therefore the superlative case: the completely wretched, the man whose wretchedness has no remainder of faith to weigh against it. He is the *ashqā*, and the verse defines him in the next words.

**The Rhetorical Reading: The Superlative for the Extreme of Each Kind**

The commentators add a second, complementary resolution from the science of rhetoric. The Arab usage of the superlative in such positions names the extreme of a kind in order to characterize the kind at its decisive instance — the same figure by which a poet praises "the bravest" to characterize the tribe's bravery in its flower. On this reading, *al-ashqā* is not a single individual ranked above all others but the type of wretchedness in its completed form: wretchedness that has denied and turned away completely. Al-Ṭabarī inclines toward harmonizing the readings rather than choosing: the verse describes the Fire's *permanent* population, and the permanent population is defined by the completed type. Both resolutions converge on one doctrine: the Fire's eternity belongs to the completed denier; the Fire's temporariness is the mercy extended, by intercession and grace, to everyone else who entered it bearing any weight of faith. The verse, far from supporting the Khārijite conclusion, is one of the proofs against it — because it defines the Fire's only permanent inhabitants by *denial and turning away*, qualities no sinful believer possesses in their completed form.

**The Source's Occasion-Story and Its Limits**

The source records here the occasion-story several commentators attach to the sūrah's latter half: the Madinan Muslim whose date-palm overhung a poor man's yard, who snatched fallen dates from the poor man's children's mouths, and who refused the Prophet's offer of a palm in Paradise for it — while a second man bought the tree at the exorbitant price of forty palms and gave it to the poor family. On that report, verses 15–16 describe the refuser and verses 17–21 the buyer, and the story is preserved by al-Qurṭubī and al-Thaʿlabī. The commentators themselves register the chronological difficulty the source notes: if al-Layl is an early Makkan sūrah, the story — set in Madinah, among an established Muslim community with property law in operation — cannot be its original occasion, and the more general reading (a condemnation of those who rejected the Prophet ﷺ and their conduct) stands. Ibn Kathīr's hermeneutic rule, already cited at verse 5, governs: the generality of the wording prevails over the particularity of any one occasion. The story is preserved here because the tradition preserved it — and because it illustrates, with unforgettable vividness, exactly the two hands this sūrah draws: the hand that snatches dates from children's mouths, and the hand that pays forty trees to put them back.

**Wretchedness as a Position, Not a Fate**

One doctrinal note closes the verse's treatment. The word *ashqā* — most wretched — is, in the Qur'an's usage, a description of achieved position, not of arbitrary assignment. The Qur'an nowhere teaches that God created souls *for* wretchedness by bare fiat; it teaches that wretchedness is assembled, act by act, out of the qualities this very sūrah has catalogued: withholding, self-sufficiency, denial. The man of verses 8–9 *manufactured* the man of verse 15. That is the whole consolation and the whole terror of the verse in one observation: the Fire's only permanent inhabitant is the one who spent a lifetime constructing himself into the position, and the construction was visible, in this world, at every stage. Verse 16 will read out the construction's blueprint.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:16

> **who denies and turns away.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Blueprint of the Most Wretched**

The sūrah now defines its superlative in two words: *alladhī kadhdhaba wa-tawallā* — "who denies and turns away." The verse is the caption under the portrait, and every word of it has been prepared for. *Kadhdhaba* — he denied, he declared false — is the identical verb of verse 9, *wa-kadhdhaba bi-l-ḥusnā*: the most wretched man is defined first by the second portrait's third quality, the denial of the most beautiful. *Tawallā* — he turned away, withdrew, disengaged — is from *w-l-y*, the root of *walāyah* (nearness, allegiance) and *tawallī* (turning one's back), and it names the physical and moral act of withdrawal from the truth one has denied: away from the message, away from its bearer, away from its obligations. The two words together constitute the completed type: denial in the heart, withdrawal in the feet.

**Denial and Turning Away: Why Both Are Named**

The commentators press the conjunction, because the Qur'an elsewhere names each alone. Why is the permanent inhabitant of the Fire defined by *both*? The answer the tradition gives is that each quality completes the danger of the other. There exists a denial that has not yet turned away — the man who rejects the truth in his heart but remains in its vicinity, listening, present, exposed to its evidence; such a man retains, in his very proximity, the possibility of reversal, and the history of the first Muslim generation supplies the instances: ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb walked out with the sword of denial and arrived home with the sword sheathed in belief. And there exists a turning away without articulated denial — the drift of the distracted, who have not so much rejected the truth as stopped facing it; such people retain, in their un-denied hearts, the seed the message can still reach. The *ashqā* is the man in whom the two have closed ranks: he has denied *and* he has turned. His denial justifies his turning, and his turning protects his denial from being disturbed by further evidence; the circuit is closed. The Qur'an describes the same closure elsewhere in its most clinical terms: *wa-qālū qulūbunā fī akinnatin mimmā tadʿūnā ilayh*, "and they say: our hearts are within coverings from that to which you call us" (41:5) — the covering being the denial, the turning being the cover.

**The Mirror of the Atqā**

The symmetry of the sūrah requires the reader to set this caption beside its counterpart, which the sūrah will supply in verses 17–21. The *ashqā* is defined by *kadhdhaba wa-tawallā* — denied and turned away. The *atqā*, as the verses will show, is defined by the inverse pair: he attested (the continuation of v. 6's *ṣaddaqa*) and he turned toward — in the act of giving his wealth to purify himself, of seeking the Face of his Lord. Between the two captions lies the entire space of human freedom: every soul is, at every hour, engaged in one of the two motions — a turning-toward or a turning-away, however small. The Ḥanafī-era scholars of creed stated the point as doctrine: faith and disbelief, in the Qur'an's anthropology, are ultimately directions before they are doctrines. The sūrah's two-word caption is that doctrine in its Qur'anic ore.

**Wretchedness Earned, Not Allocated**

The theological consequence of the caption is the one the tradition insists upon against every reading that would make wretchedness a matter of divine allocation alone. The verse defines the *ashqā* by his own verbs: *he* denied, *he* turned away. No destiny is cited in the definition; the definition is entirely biographical. This is the sūrah's final word on the graveside question of the ḥadīth of ʿAlī: yes, seats are written — but the writing the servant is shown is the transcript of his own verbs. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the same tradition: *"Work! For everyone is eased unto that for which he was created"* — and the sūrah supplies, in this verse, the work by which the wretched seat is earned. No one arrives at *kadhdhaba wa-tawallā* except through a history of smaller denials and smaller turnings-away, each one free, each one recorded. The verse's severity is therefore inseparable from its justice: it does not describe a man condemned for what he could not help; it describes, in two participles, a career.

**The Warning's Door Left Open**

One last observation of grammar and mercy. Both verbs are past tense over a type, not over a named individual — the same generalizing device noted throughout the sūrah. The caption describes the *completed* denier-and-turner-away; it does not name any living person as completed. Every living denier is still one verb short of the caption — he has denied, but he has not yet finished turning away; or he has turned away, but has not yet finished denying, since the evidence still reaches him. The sūrah's own final portrait, verses 17–21, exists precisely to demonstrate that the caption can be escaped: a man who once stood inside its two verbs and then walked out of them. That is why the warning of verse 14 still stands in the present tense over every reciter: the Fire has been warned, the blueprint of its permanent inhabitant has been read out, and the blueprint is, at every stage before completion, reversible.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:17

> **And the most reverent shall be removed from it,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Portrait, Now Promoted**

The Fire's permanent population has been defined; the sūrah now turns to the Fire's opposite — not merely exemption from it, but removal from it: *wa-sa-yujannabuhā l-atqā*, "and the most reverent shall be removed from it." The verb *yujannabuhā* is the second form of *j-n-b*, the root of *jānib*, side: it means to be set aside, kept far, placed at a distance from a thing. The sūrah could have said the atqā "shall not enter it"; it says something more emphatic — he shall be *kept far* from it, the distance itself guaranteed by the same divine "We" that eased the two roads. The prefix *sa-* carries the promise into certainty: this is not a hope but a scheduled event.

**Al-Atqā: The Superlative and Its Question**

*Al-atqā* is the superlative of *taqwā* — the most reverent, the most God-fearing, the utmost in the quality verse 5 made the second mark of the first portrait. And immediately the exegetical question arises, the mirror-image of the question posed by *al-ashqā* in verse 15: if only the *most* reverent is removed from the Fire, what becomes of ordinary believers — the vast middle of the community, reverent but not maximally so? The answer the tradition developed is the exact counterpart of its answer to verse 15. Al-Qurṭubī records it, and Ibn Kathīr states it plainly: *al-atqā* here is used in the sense of *al-muttaqī* — the one who has taqwā — and the superlative form carries the type at its decisive instance, as is the way of Arabic in such positions. The *atqā* of this verse is every soul who guarded himself against *shirk* — against associating partners with God — for it is shirk that is the one unforgiven if unretracted, the one quality that delivers a soul permanently to the Fire: *inna Llāha lā yaghfiru an yushraka bihī wa-yaghfiru mā dūna dhālika liman yashāʾ*, "Truly God does not forgive that partners be associated with Him, and He forgives what is less than that for whom He wills" (4:48, 4:116). Whoever kept his taqwā intact at that height — whatever his standing in the lesser degrees — is inside the promise of removal.

**The Abū Bakr Occasion and the Rule of Generality**

The occasion-reports attached to this verse and the four that follow it are the strongest in the sūrah's latter half, and they are almost unanimous in naming a single man: Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq. Ibn Kathīr gathers them: the verses were revealed concerning Abū Bakr's ministry of purchasing weak, tortured slaves and freeing them for God's sake. His father, Abū Quḥāfah — not yet a Muslim at the time — watched his son spending a fortune on the helpless and counseled him in the terms the tradition preserves: "My son, if you insist on freeing slaves, why do you not buy strong ones who could defend you and stand by you?" And Abū Bakr answered with the sentence the commentators quote as the creed of the entire passage: *"Father, it is not my defense that I seek."* — I am not buying these people for what they can do for me; I am buying them for what God will do. Upon that ministry, the reports continue, verses 17–21 were revealed, extolling the giver who owes no one a favor and seeks only the Face of his Lord. The tradition also preserves the occasion-story of the date-palm, discussed at verse 15, as a rival account; and Ibn Kathīr's rule governs both: *al-ʿibrah bi-ʿumūm al-lafẓ lā bi-khuṣūṣ al-sabab* — the lesson is taken from the generality of the wording, not the particularity of the occasion. The verse was revealed about the man who bought slaves to free them, and it stands over every soul who has ever spent himself in purity of intention. Abū Bakr is the verse's named instance; the wording is the believers' inheritance.

**The Logic of Removal**

The verse's central word — removal, being set at a distance — carries a theological logic the commentators unfold. Exemption can be passive (a man simply does not go where the danger is), but removal is active: someone removes him. The passive construction of *yujannabuhā* conceals its Agent by the same grammar the Qur'an uses elsewhere for divine acts of protection, and the tradition hears the Agent in the hiding: it is God Who removes the atqā from the Fire, as it was God Who eased him into ease in verse 7. The symmetry of the two easings now completes itself across the sūrah: as the man of denial was eased *into* hardship by the withdrawal of help (*khidhlān*), the man of reverence is eased *away from* the Fire by the extension of help (*tawfīq*). Both verdicts were pronounced in the sūrah's first half; the second half is now showing them executed.

**Taḥrīq al-Asbāb: Distance Earned in This World**

The spiritual writers add the practical corollary: the distance from the Fire that the verse promises at the end is assembled out of distances adopted at the beginning. Every act of taqwā in this world is, in the imagery of the tradition, a step *away from* — away from the forbidden, away from the doubtful, away from the excessive, away from the self's immediate demand. The servant who spent his life placing distances between himself and what angers God is, at the end, placed at a distance from what punishes for it. The ḥadīth tradition supplies the maxim: *"The Fire is veiled by desires, and the Garden is veiled by hardships"* (al-Bukhārī 6487; Muslim 2822) — meaning that the Fire is reached by passing through the indulgence of appetite, and the Garden by passing through the discipline of it. The *atqā* is the man who refused, all his life, to pay the price of the first veil; the verse announces that the second veil's reward now includes removal from the Fire itself.

**The Verse as Consolation for the Ordinary Believer**

It belongs to the mercy of this verse's wording to note how the tradition read it as consolation rather than exclusion. A superficial reading of *the most reverent shall be removed from it* could breed despair in the ordinary soul — "I am not the most reverent; the promise is not mine." The commentators' unanimous move in the opposite direction — the superlative names the type, and the type is everyone who kept shirk out of his heart — converts the verse into one of the great assurances of the short sūrahs. The ordinary believer, staggering under his own shortcomings, recites this verse and hears: the Fire's permanent claim on souls is limited to the completed denier; you, with your mixed record and your intact confession, are on the removal list. The doctrine has its proofs elsewhere in overwhelming number — the ḥadīths of intercession, the verse *wa-ākhiru daʿwāhum ani l-ḥamdu lillāhi rabbil-ʿālamīn*, the countless promises of forgiveness for the monotheist — but al-Layl's seventeenth verse states it in six Arabic words, and the tradition has recited it as a consolation ever since.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:18

> **who gives his wealth to purify,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Atqā's First Mark: Purifying Giving**

The sūrah now draws the atqā's portrait in the same number of strokes as the ashqā's caption, and the first stroke is — as in the first portrait of verse 5 — an open hand: *alladhī yuʾtī mālahū yatazakkā*, "who gives his wealth to purify." But the verse adds what verse 5 left implicit: the purpose of the giving. The word *yatazakkā* is the verse's theology in a single verb, and the tradition works it thoroughly.

**Yatazakkā: The Root of Zakāh at Work**

*Yatazakkā* is the fifth form of the root *z-k-w*, whose twin senses — growth and purification — are the Qur'an's deepest economics. The same root gives *zakāh*, the obligatory alms, and the Qur'an uses the verb forms of the root for both of its senses at once: *khudh min amwālihim ṣadaqatan tuṭahhiruhum wa-tuzakkīhim bihā*, "Take from their wealth a charity to purify them and sanctify them thereby" (9:103). To give *yatazakkā* is therefore to give in order to be purified — and the commentators specify the double object of the purification. One purifies *oneself*: the soul is cleansed of miserliness, of the love of the world, of the delusion of self-sufficiency — the very three diseases of the second portrait. And one purifies *one's wealth*: whatever attaches to property of others' rights, of the poor's share, of the stain of acquisition, is discharged in the giving. Al-Qurṭubī develops both readings; Ibn Kathīr emphasizes the first — the purification of the self from shirk and from lowliness — and the tradition holds both, because the root holds both.

**Why Purification Is the Purpose, Not the Price**

The verse's ordering is doctrinally exact, and the commentators note it against every misunderstanding: the atqā gives his wealth *in order to* purify — he does not pay his wealth *in order to be* pure. The difference is the difference between medicine and purchase. The man of verse 5 gave as the expression of an attestation already made; the atqā of verse 18 gives as the instrument of a purification being performed. The Qur'an elsewhere states the same theology of giving-as-medicine: *mā ʿindakum yanfadu wa-mā ʿinda Llāhi bāq* (16:96); *lan tanālū l-birra ḥattā tunfiqū mimmā tuḥibbūn*, "You shall not attain righteousness until you spend of what you love" (3:92); and the great verse of the hypocrites' opposite: *wa-mā ātaytum min zakātin turīdūna wajha Llāhi fa-ulāʾika humu l-muḍʿifūn*, "And what you give in zakāh, desiring the Face of God — those are the multipliers" (30:39). In all of these, the giving is not a price paid to God — God owns both worlds, as verse 13 just declared — but a surgery performed on the giver's own attachment. The wealth is the scalpel; the self is the patient.

**The Abū Bakr Portfolio: What the Purifying Looked Like**

The occasion-reports give the portrait its historical flesh, and the flesh is unforgettable. The traditionists preserve the names: Bilāl ibn Rabāḥ, the Abyssinian slave of Umayyah ibn Khalaf, tortured in the noon heat with a rock on his chest and saying only *Aḥad! Aḥad!* — "One! One!"; ʿĀmir ibn Fuhayrah; Umm ʿUbayyis; al-Nahdiyyah and her daughter; Zanīrah, blinded by her torture and restored. Abū Bakr bought them — one, two, and groups at a time, spending his considerable merchant fortune — and freed them. When the idolaters mocked the purchase of the weak and useless, the answer came in these verses; when his own father counseled the purchase of the strong and useful, the answer came in Abū Bakr's own words, already quoted at verse 17: *"It is not my defense that I seek."* The ḥadīth literature records the Prophet ﷺ's own testimony to the man these verses named: *"No one's wealth ever benefited me as Abū Bakr's wealth benefited me"* (al-Tirmidhī 3661; Ibn Mājah 94, from Abū Hurayrah, graded ṣaḥīḥ). And Bilāl — the slave whose price included, in one report, a pound of gold — became Islam's first muʾadhdhin, the voice raised five times daily over the community's prayer until the end of time, and one of the Prophet's own treasurers. The man the idolaters weighed in gold as chattel, the sūrah weighed as *al-atqā*.

**The Logic of Purification by Loss**

The verse contains, in its single verb, the refutation of the miser's entire economics. The miser of verse 8 believed wealth was kept by keeping; the atqā demonstrates that wealth is kept by releasing. The logic is the logic of every living system the Qur'an points to: the seed that is hoarded rots in the granary; the seed that is buried — released — multiplies. *Mathalu lladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum fī sabīli Llāhi ka-mathali ḥabbatin anbatat sabʿa sanābila fī kulli sunbulatin miʾatu ḥabbah*, "The likeness of those who spend their wealth in God's way is as the likeness of a grain that sprouts seven ears, in every ear a hundred grains" (2:261). Zakāh legislates the same logic as law: the obligatory portion — a quarter of a tenth on hoarded wealth, a tenth on rain-fed harvest — is declared by the Lawgiver not a tax but a *growth*, because the portion released is the portion that multiplies in the only ledger that survives the fall of verse 11. The atqā's giving is zakāh in its superlative form: not the minimum due but the maximum possible, given not because it is owed but because it purifies.

**The Modern Application: Purification from the Love of Money**

The verse's contemporary address is precise. The modern economy has made wealth abstract — digits on screens, positions in portfolios — and abstraction has removed from wealth the last friction that once attended its handling. The love of money, which the prophetic tradition identified as the root of idolatry in every age — *"Wretched is the slave of the dinar, wretched is the slave of the dirham"* (al-Bukhārī 2886) — now operates on minds that never handle money at all. The atqā's verse prescribes the ancient cure in the modern condition: the only demonstrated treatment for the love of wealth is the repeated, deliberate, worshipful release of it. Every act of giving performed *yatazakkā* — consciously, for purification, toward the Face — is one session of the cure. The sūrah does not ask the modern reader to buy tortured slaves; it asks him to notice that the hands of his own second portrait are still closed, and to remember that the same God Who praised the open hand in Abū Bakr's Makkah is watching the same hands now.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:19

> **not recompensing any for a favor thereby;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Clean Ledger**

The portrait's second stroke removes every creaturely motive from the giving: *wa-mā li-aḥadin ʿindahu min niʿmatin tujzā* — "not recompensing any for a favor thereby." The Arabic is a negation of maximum strength: *min* before *niʿmatin* is the *min* of intensification in negative contexts, so that the sentence means, literally, "there is not, with him, for anyone, any favor at all to be recompensed." The commentators parse the sense in two directions that converge. Backward-looking: he is not paying anyone back — the giving is not the discharge of a social debt, not the repayment of a kindness received, not the settlement of an account. Forward-looking: he is not buying anyone forward — the giving carries no expectation of future return, no cultivation of obligation, no entry in any ledger but God's. Both readings are recorded in the source's annotation, and both are the verse's intent: the atqā's hand moves in one direction only — outward, toward God — with no return circuit through the creatures.

**The Occasion: Bilāl and the Slander of Motive**

The reports of revelation attach this verse and the next two to a specific slander and its divine refutation, and the story is one of the noblest in the tradition of *asbāb al-nuzūl*. Al-Wāḥidī preserves it in his collection of occasions, and the tafsīrs transmit it in substance: when Bilāl — slave of ʿAbd Allāh ibn Judʿān in one account, of Umayyah ibn Khalaf in the torture-narratives — embraced Islam, the idolaters took him into the blazing desert, laid the rock upon him, and tortured him while he said only *Aḥad! Aḥad!* The Prophet ﷺ came upon him and said, *"The One will save you!"* and then informed Abū Bakr: *"Bilāl is being tortured for the sake of God!"* Abū Bakr went with a pound of gold — in other reports with five ūqiyyahs, in others with nine — and bought him. And when the idolaters saw the purchase, they did what slanderers do: they invented a motive. "Abū Bakr has done this only because he owes Bilāl a favor" — some old kindness, some prior obligation, some worldly account being settled. Upon that slander, the reports state, verses 19–21 were revealed: *not recompensing any for a favor thereby* — the slandered motive named, examined, and dismissed by revelation itself, and the true motive installed in the next verse. The incident gives the verse its permanent edge: whenever a pure act of worship is met with a cynical accounting of motives — "he only gave because he owed him," "she only helped because she wanted something" — this verse is the revealed answer, and it is addressed first to the slandered giver: your ledger is clean, and God has said so.

**The Sociology of the Unpaid Favor**

The verse's social teaching reaches far beyond its occasion. Every society runs on economies of reciprocal obligation — the favor given now against the favor owed later, the gift that creates a debt, the charity that purchases gratitude or status or silence. The Qur'an does not forbid these economies in ordinary commerce; it forbids them at the point where giving meets God. The atqā's giving is the one transaction in human life from which the counterparty — society — is entirely excluded. The commentators draw out the psychology with care: the moment a giver expects return, the gift becomes a loan, the recipient becomes a debtor, and the giver becomes a creditor — and the creditor's heart is the very opposite of the purified heart verse 18 described. Gratitude extracted by expectation is no purification; it is commerce wearing the robe of charity. The Prophet ﷺ policed this boundary in his own legislation: he forbade the publicizing of gifts in ways that humiliate the recipient, and he taught the formula that dissolves every social debt the instant it threatens to form — the ḥadīth's counsel that whoever has a favor done to him and says *"Jazāka Llāhu khayran"* — "May God reward you with good" — has discharged the thanks, transferring the account from the social ledger to the divine one (al-Tirmidhī 2035, from Usāmah ibn Zayd, graded ṣaḥīḥ). The atqā's verse is that teaching's theological foundation: the ledger belongs to God, and the giver who keeps it clean has given as the sūrah's first portrait gave — *aʿṭā*, without object, without account, without return.

**Against the Philanthropy of the Visible**

The modern world has constructed an entire architecture of visible giving — naming rights, donor walls, publicized campaigns, the philanthropy that is announced in the same breath it is performed. Nothing in the verse condemns public charity as such — the Qur'an praises both the hidden and the manifest gift (*in tubdū ṣ-ṣadaqāti fa-niʿimmā hiya*, "if you disclose the alms, it is good," 2:271) — but the verse condemns the *ledger* beneath the visibility: the gift whose real counterparty is the public's admiration rather than God's Face. The atqā's test is internal and exact: could this gift survive being anonymous? If the giving would stop once the recognition stopped, the favor was being recompensed all along — to the crowd. The tradition's maxim for the case is the ḥadīth of the seven whom God shades on the Day when there is no shade but His, among them *"a man who gave charity so secretly that his left hand did not know what his right hand spent"* (al-Bukhārī 1423; Muslim 1031). Al-Layl's nineteenth verse is that secret man's charter.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:20

> **save for seeking the Face of his Lord, the Most High,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One Motive Left Standing**

The sūrah now installs the true motive in the place the slander tried to occupy: *illā btighāʾa wajhi rabbihī l-aʿlā* — "save for seeking the Face of his Lord, the Most High." The grammarians classify the exception as an *istithnāʾ munqaṭiʿ* — a disjunctive exception: what follows *illā* is not of the genus of what was negated. The verse said: he recompenses no favor of any creature — *except* that he seeks the Face of his Lord; and the seeking of God's Face is no favor of any creature. The effect of the construction is to leave exactly one motive in the field after every creaturely motive has been swept out: no repayment backward, no purchase forward, no audience in the world at all — only the Face. The Qur'an elsewhere performs the same sweep with the same word, and the repetition across the Book is deliberate: *wa-mā tunfiqūna llā btighāʾa wajhi Llāh*, "and you spend not except seeking the Face of God" (2:272); *wa-ṣbir nafsaka maʿa lladhīna yadʿūna rabbahum bi-l-ghadāti wa-l-ʿashiyyi yurīdūna wajhah*, "and keep yourself patient with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His Face" (18:28); and *wa-mā ātaytum min zakātin turīdūna wajha Llāh*, "and what you give in zakāh, desiring the Face of God" (30:39). Al-Layl's verse is the type of which these are the instances.

**The Face: What the Word Carries**

The word *wajh* — Face — is the verse's theology, and the tradition handles it with its characteristic balance. At the level of reference, the commentators are agreed on what the phrase denotes: seeking the Face of God is seeking God Himself — His pleasure, His regard, His nearness, the reward that consists in Him rather than in anything created. Ibn Kathīr states the standard gloss: the atqā acts seeking thereby the reward of God's Face — that is, for God alone, with no partner in the intention. At the level of creed, the phrase belongs to the great Qur'anic verses of the Face — *kullu man ʿalayhā fānin wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika dhū l-jalāli wa-l-ikrām*, "all that is upon her perishes, and there remains the Face of your Lord, full of majesty and honor" (55:26–27); *fa-aynama tuwallū fa-thamma wajhu Llāh*, "wherever you turn, there is the Face of God" (2:115) — and the tradition's position on them is the position on all such verses: affirmed as revealed, without reduction to metaphor on one side and without corporeal imagining on the other. The servant's part in the verse is not to anatomize the Face but to seek it — and the seeking, the commentators note, is what transforms the deed: the identical dirham, given for a man's praise and given for God's Face, is two different deeds in the ledger, as different as the two motives.

**Al-Aʿlā: The Most High as the Address of the Seeker**

The verse's closing Name is itself instruction: *rabbihī l-aʿlā* — his Lord, the Most High. The superlative of *ʿ-u-l-w*, height and exaltation, is the same Name that opens Sūrah al-Aʿlā — *sabbisma rabbika l-aʿlā*, "Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High" (87:1) — and its presence here completes the portrait's orientation. The atqā's seeking has direction: upward. The sūrah has spent its whole argument establishing directions — the ease that leads up, the hardship that leads down, the turning-toward and the turning-away — and it closes its portrait of the saved man with the destination named by the Name of height. The Name also performs a quiet refutation of the second portrait: the miser deemed himself beyond need of what is above him; the atqā seeks precisely the Most High. Self-sufficiency toward the Exalted and seeking of the Exalted are the two fundamental postures of the soul, and the sūrah leaves no third.

**Ikhlas: The Doctrine the Verse Legislates**

From this verse the tradition derives much of its doctrine of *ikhlāṣ* — sincerity, the purification of the motive until one Face alone remains. The ḥadīth literature builds the doctrine in detail: *"Actions are only by intentions, and every man shall have only what he intended"* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907) — the first sentence of Islam's most famous ḥadīth, establishing that the deed's worth is the motive's worth; and the three first to be judged on the Day — the reciter, the giver, the fighter — each condemned for having performed a real act of religion for a false audience (Muslim 1905, from Abū Hurayrah). Al-Layl's twentieth verse is the positive pole of that ḥadīth's negative pole: the act of religion performed for the one true Audience. The Sufi masters made the verse the station of their entire path — the sought is the Face, and everything short of the Face is the idol of the intention — and the jurists made it the condition of their entire law: no act of worship is valid without the intention directed to God. Between the jurist's minimum and the mystic's maximum, the verse stands as the common charter: *illā btighāʾa wajhi rabbihī l-aʿlā*.

**The Freedom of the Single Audience**

One practical consequence deserves the final word on this verse, because it is the atqā's earthly reward before the Hereafter's. The man who seeks many audiences is enslaved to all of them: he must monitor each, please each, adjust himself to each, and he is never finished, because the audiences multiply and contradict. The man who seeks one Face is free of all the others — not by contempt for people but by the simple arithmetic of the heart: the ledger has one entry, and the entry is closed. This is the liberty Abū Bakr tasted when he answered his father *"it is not my defense that I seek"* — and it is the liberty the verse offers every reciter: to be, in one's deepest act, unmonitored by the crowd, unindebted to the powerful, unmarketable by the age, answerable to the Most High alone. The slanderers of verse 19 had their economy; the atqā has his Face.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:21

> **and surely he shall be content.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's Last Word: Satisfaction**

The entire chapter — its oaths, its fork, its two portraits, its two verdicts, its Fire and its Face — arrives at last at this single sentence: *wa-la-sawfa yardā* — "and surely he shall be content." The particles are emphatic to the limit of Arabic's power: the lām of certitude, and *sawfa*, the future of assured realization. The verb *yardā* is from *r-ḍ-w*, satisfaction, pleasure, contentment — the state of the soul that wants nothing more because it has what it was made for. The sūrah that opened with the night's enshrouding closes with the daylight of a satisfied soul, and the last word of its argument is not a reward but a state of heart: the atqā shall be *content*.

**The Promise Answered Across the Sūrahs**

The verse is one of a pair of promises that the short Makkan sūrahs exchange with one another, and the pair is worth assembling. Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā, revealed to the Prophet ﷺ himself in his hour of consolation, ends its ascending promises with the same grammar and the same verb: *wa-la-sawfa yuʿṭīka rabbuka fa-tarḍā*, "and your Lord shall give to you, and you shall be content" (93:5). Al-Layl returns the promise to every atqā of every age, in the identical construction: *wa-la-sawfa yardā*. And Sūrah al-Fajr, al-Layl's near-neighbor in the Makkan constellation, closes the same arc from the other side: *yā ayyatuhā n-nafsu l-muṭmaʾinnah, irjiʿī ilā rabbiki rāḍiyatan marḍiyyah*, "O soul at peace! Return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing" (89:27–28) — the soul that is *rāḍiyah* (content, satisfied) and *marḍiyyah* (the object of God's own satisfaction). Al-Layl's last verse is that address's promise in advance: the contentment the soul will arrive with is the contentment it is here assured of. The three sūrahs together — al-Fajr, al-Layl, al-Ḍuḥā — form the Qur'an's short treatise on satisfaction, and al-Layl supplies its legal clause.

**What the Contentment Contains**

The commentators unfold the verse's content in ascending degrees. The first degree is the contentment with the reward: the Garden, whose satisfaction is described in the Qur'an as complete — *fīhā mā tashtahīhi l-anfusu wa-taladhdhu l-aʿyun*, "therein is whatever souls desire and eyes delight in" (43:71). The second degree is the contentment beyond the Garden: the pleasure of God Himself, which the ḥadīth declares greater than every created reward. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī the Prophet ﷺ saying:

> *"God will say: O people of the Garden! They will say: At Your service, our Lord, and all good is in Your hand. He will say: Are you content? They will say: And why should we not be content, O Lord, when You have given us what You have not given to any of Your creation? He will say: Shall I not give you what is better than that? They will say: O Lord, what is better than that? He will say: I shall bestow upon you My pleasure (riḍwān), and I shall never be displeased with you thereafter."* (al-Bukhārī 6549; Muslim 2829)

The verse's *yardā* is heard by the tradition over that entire scene: the contentment is contentment because it is sealed with the divine pleasure that is never withdrawn. And the third degree, which the verse's wording quietly contains, is the contentment of the atqā *in this world* before the next — the contentment of the man whose motive is already single, whose ledger is already clean, whose Face is already sought. The commentators who read the sūrah's promise as beginning before death read it truly: the man of verses 17–20 already tastes his verse 21, because the heart that seeks one Face is, in the seeking, already at rest from a hundred others.

**The Symmetry, Completed and Judged**

The sūrah ends where it began: in perfect symmetry, now with the verdict attached. Set the two completed figures against one another, as the sūrah has drawn them. The ashqā: denied the most beautiful, and turned away — and his wealth availed him nothing when he perished, and the Fire is his abode. The atqā: attested to the most beautiful, and turned toward — giving his wealth to purify, owing no favor to any creature, seeking only the Face of his Lord the Most High — and he is removed far from the Fire, and he shall be content. Every element of the first portrait has its exact counterpart in the second; every verdict of the first has its exact counterpart in the second. The sūrah thereby demonstrates, in twenty-one verses, the thesis its oaths swore to: *inna saʿyakum la-shattā* — your endeavors are diverse, and the diversity is not a difference of degree but a difference of direction, and the directions arrive. Nothing in the visible universe contradicted the oaths, and nothing in the moral universe contradicts the verdict.

**The Final Exhortation**

The tradition's way with a sūrah's final verse is to ask what it leaves the reciter doing, and al-Layl's final verse leaves its reciter at the only fork the sūrah has not closed: the fork of verse 4, still open, in every reciter's own *saʿy*. The night still enshrouds and the day still discloses; the male and the female are still being created; the strivings are still scattering toward the two arrivals. The atqā's portrait is not a biography of one Makkan century but a standing specification — givable by anyone, in any economy, to any recipient, in any age where a hand can open and a motive can be purified. The ashqā's caption is likewise standing: every small denial and every small turning-away is a stroke of it, and the strokes accumulate only until they are stopped. Between the caption and the portrait lies the whole of a human life, and the sūrah's last word — *yardā*, he shall be content — stands at the portrait's end as the price-tag of the one choice the age cannot price: to give, to purify, to owe nothing to the crowd, and to seek, in all of it, the Face of the Lord, the Most High. The night of al-Layl, for the soul that chooses it so, ends in that Face — and surely he shall be content.
