# Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah (Chapter 88) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Overwhelming Event and Why This Sūrah Bears Its Name**

Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah, "The Overwhelming," is the eighty-eighth chapter of the Qur'an, revealed in Makkah in the period when the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was calling a small, persecuted community to believe in one God and the coming of a Day that would overwhelm all excuses. It takes its name from its very first word-picture, *al-ghāshiyah* — the event that covers and overpowers everything, which the majority of commentators, following Ibn ʿAbbās and the early generations, understand as the Day of Resurrection. In twenty-six short verses the sūrah moves like a procession through four great scenes: the tormented faces in the Fire (verses 1–7), the blissful faces in the Garden (verses 8–16), the four visible signs in creation that prove the Creator's power (verses 17–20), and the final definition of the Messenger's mission and of God's exclusive reckoning (verses 21–26).

**A Makkan Sūrah with a Makkan Pulse**

Everything about this chapter breathes the Makkan atmosphere. The verses are short, rhythmic, and hammering. There is no legislation here, no ruling on marriage or trade or war, because the audience had not yet been given a state; they were being given a worldview. The Makkan sūrahs concentrate on three foundations — God, the soul, and the Hereafter — and al-Ghāshiyah is almost entirely the third foundation, with the first woven through it as proof. Its style is the style of awakening: a question that startles (verse 1), two portraits hung side by side in violent contrast (verses 2–16), a command to open the eyes (verses 17–20), and a closing that fixes every heart in its place before God (verses 21–26). Ibn Kathīr and the classical commentators note how the sūrah's brevity is itself part of its eloquence: in the space of a few lines, a man hears his two possible eternities described, is shown the evidence standing in front of him every day, and is told exactly what the Prophet can and cannot do for him.

**The Sister of Sūrah al-Aʿlā**

This sūrah is bound to the chapter before it, Sūrah al-Aʿlā (87), by theme and by Prophetic practice. Where al-Aʿlā ends with the dichotomy between those who purify themselves and remember God and those who prefer the fleeting worldly life (87:11–15), al-Ghāshiyah takes up that dichotomy and paints its final outcomes in full color: the faces of the wretched and the faces of the blessed. And the Prophet ﷺ himself paired the two sūrahs in public worship. Al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr reported:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ used to recite in the two ʿEids and on Friday: 'Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High' (Sūrah 87) and 'Has there come to you the news of the Overwhelming?' (Sūrah 88). And when the ʿEid and Friday coincided on a single day, he would recite them in both prayers."* (Muslim 878)

Consider what this means. Week after week, on the greatest congregational day of Islam, and on the two annual festivals of joy, the Muslim community heard this sūrah. The pairing is deliberate: al-Aʿlā glorifies the Lord and promises success to the purified, while al-Ghāshiyah warns of the Day that will sort mankind. Celebration in Islam is never permitted to become forgetfulness; even on the day of feasting, the believer is reminded of the Day that overwhelms.

**The Architecture of Twenty-Six Verses**

The sūrah's structure is a marvel of proportion. Seven verses describe the Fire (1–7); nine verses describe the Garden (8–16) — the passage of mercy is longer than the passage of wrath, as though the text itself embodied the divine declaration:

> *"Indeed My mercy prevails over My wrath."* (al-Bukhārī 3194; Muslim 2751)

Then four verses summon the eye to four signs — camels, sky, mountains, earth — the entire visible world of the desert Arab compressed into one gaze. Then six verses close the chapter by settling the matter of authority: the Messenger reminds, he does not compel; mankind returns, and God reckons. Warning, glad tidings, evidence, and the limits of the mission: the four functions of revelation are all present, in order.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary below treats each of the twenty-six verses as an independent, complete discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order, exactly as the mandated method requires. Verses that the source commentary groups together (such as 3–4, 10–11, 15–16, 18–20, and 21–26) are here separated and each given full individual treatment. Every verse is expanded through its Arabic language, the classical commentators, authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the logic of its teaching, and its bearing on modern life. Nothing has been compressed because the chapter is short; small sūrahs in the Qur'an are often the densest, and al-Ghāshiyah is among the densest of all — a whole theology of the Last Day in twenty-six lines.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:1

> **Hast thou heard tell of the overwhelming event?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Arrives Before Its Answer**

The sūrah opens not with a statement but with a question: *hal atāka ḥadīthu al-ghāshiyah* — "Has there come to you the news of the Overwhelming?" The particle *hal* in Arabic poses a question expecting confirmation, and the classical rhetoricians, including al-Zamakhsharī, note that when God questions in this form, the purpose is not to seek information but to arrest attention and magnify the subject. It is the question of one about to deliver tidings so grave that the listener must first be woken up. Al-Ṭabarī records that some early reciters and interpreters read the opening almost as an announcement — "There has come to you" — while the majority keep it as a rhetorical question for emphasis and awe. Either way, the effect is the same: before a single detail of the Last Day is given, the heart is told that something is coming toward it, personally and unavoidably.

**Why the Word Ḥadīth — News, Narrative, Tiding**

The verse calls the Last Day a *ḥadīth* — literally a "narrative" or "piece of news." The same word describes the stories of past peoples (*ḥadīth Mūsā*, 79:15; *ḥadīth al-junūd*, 85:17). The choice is precise: the Resurrection is presented as an event so certain that it can be narrated like history, though it lies in the future. It has already been written; it is only waiting to be witnessed. There is also a quiet mercy in the word: news is, by nature, something that reaches you *before* the event, giving you time to prepare. The Qur'an calls itself the best of narratives:

> *"God has sent down the best of narratives (aḥsan al-ḥadīth)..."* (Qur'an 39:23)

and the tidings of the Last Day are the most consequential narrative a human being will ever hear — more decisive than any news of markets, elections, or wars, for all of those concern the temporary, while this concerns the permanent.

**Al-Ghāshiyah — That Which Covers and Overwhelms**

The key word *al-ghāshiyah* comes from the root *gh-sh-y*, meaning to cover, envelop, or overcome. The same root gives us the night "when it covers" (*idhā yaghshā*, 92:1), the sea that "overwhelmed" Pharaoh's hosts (*fa-ghashiyahum*, 20:78), and the Fire that "shall cover their faces" (*taghshā wujūhahum al-nār*, 14:50). The form *ghāshiyah* is intensive: that which covers utterly, leaving nothing outside it. The majority of the Companions and their successors — Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, and others — held that it is one of the names of the Day of Resurrection, so called because its terror and grandeur will cover all of humanity; no rank, wealth, or army will stand outside it. A second view, recorded by al-Qurṭubī and others, holds that it is the Fire itself, which will cover the damned in layers:

> *"They shall have coverings of Fire above them and below them coverings."* (Qur'an 39:16; cf. 7:41)

A third view joins the two: the Day overwhelms by its events, and the Fire overwhelms by its embrace. Ibn Kathīr presents these views and favors the first while affirming that all describe one reality from different angles. The lesson for the reader is that the Last Day has many names in the Qur'an — *al-Qāriʿah* (the Striking), *al-Ḥāqqah* (the Inevitable), *al-Ṭāmmah* (the Great Catastrophe), *al-Ṣākhkhah* (the Deafening Blast), *al-Wāqiʿah* (the Occurring) — and each name is a window onto a different terror or truth of that Day. *Al-Ghāshiyah* is the window of universality: whatever else that Day is, it leaves no one uncovered.

**"Has It Come to You?" — The Personal Address**

Notice that the question is singular and personal: *atāka* — "has it come to *you*." In the first instance the addressee is the Prophet ﷺ, and through him every listener. The Qur'an refuses to let the Last Day remain an abstraction discussed in the third person; it comes *to you*, as news comes to a man in his house. Psychologically, this is the difference between knowing a fact and receiving a warning. A man may acknowledge that "all humans die" as a statistic and remain unmoved; but when the doctor says "you have this illness," the same fact becomes a summons. Verse 1 performs that transformation. It also establishes the Prophet's own position: even he is addressed as a recipient of the news, a servant taught by his Lord, not the author of the warning. The warner himself stands under the warning — which is why his warning carries weight.

**News That Overwhelms Every Other News**

In an age drowning in headlines, this verse restores proportion. Human beings today receive hundreds of "overwhelming" news items a year — each crisis presented as the end of the world, each forgotten within weeks. The Qur'an acknowledges only one truly overwhelming event, and measures all others against it. The analogy is like a man anxiously tracking every wave while ignoring the tide: the waves are real, but the tide decides where the shore will be. The believer's calm in worldly turbulence comes precisely from having already accepted the one great Overwhelming; lesser overwhelmings cannot unseat a heart anchored beyond them. Conversely, the heedless man's panic at every small crisis comes from having no greater framework to absorb it. To have heard tell of al-Ghāshiyah — and believed it — is to have acquired the only scale on which all other news can be weighed.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:2

> **Faces that Day shall be humbled,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why Faces? The Body's Most Truthful Witness**

The verse speaks of *wujūh* — faces — though it means whole persons, because in both Arabic idiom and human nature the face is the person made visible. Honor and disgrace show first on the face: it brightens with joy, darkens with grief, reddens with shame, and falls with humiliation. The Qur'an consistently describes the states of the Hereafter through faces — *"faces that Day radiant, gazing upon their Lord, and faces that Day scowling"* (75:22–24) — because the face is the one part of man that cannot be hidden from others and can barely be disguised. In this world a criminal may dress well, a hypocrite may smile, and a tyrant may hold his chin high; on that Day, the faces will testify. The word *yawma'idhin* — "that Day" — ties the description back to verse 1: these are the faces *under* the Overwhelming, the human harvest of the event just announced.

**Khāshiʿah — Humility That Arrives Too Late**

The adjective *khāshiʿah* comes from *kh-sh-ʿ*, the root of *khushūʿ* — the submissive humility that is the soul of prayer. The believing heart is commanded to cultivate *khushūʿ* in this world:

> *"Successful indeed are the believers, those who are humble (khāshiʿūn) in their prayers."* (Qur'an 23:1–2)

But the humility of verse 2 is its dark twin: not the willing humility of worship but the forced abasement of the exposed. Ibn Kathīr explains that these faces will be humbled and disgraced, and Jalāl al-Dīn al-Maḥallī and al-Suyūṭī (in *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn*) gloss it simply as abased, humiliated. The Qur'an describes the same scene elsewhere with devastating detail:

> *"Humbled shall be their gazes, and abasement shall cover them."* (Qur'an 68:43)

> *"And you shall see them exposed to it, humbled with abasement, looking with a furtive glance."* (Qur'an 42:45)

The logic is exact and terrible: those who refused to lower their faces to God in willing prostration will have their faces lowered by force; those who would not be humbled *before* the evidence will be humbled *by* the verdict. It is the same humility in form and its opposite in worth — like the difference between a man who kneels to propose and a man who kneels in handcuffs.

**The Door That Closes: Remorse After the Deadline**

The scholars stress, as Ibn Kathīr does, that this humility will benefit its owners nothing, because the door of repentance is open only in this world. The Qur'an states the deadline plainly:

> *"But repentance is not for those who do evil deeds until, when death comes upon one of them, he says, 'Indeed I repent now.'"* (Qur'an 4:18)

And the Prophet ﷺ fixed its two final frontiers — the personal and the cosmic:

> *"Indeed God accepts the repentance of the servant so long as the death-rattle has not reached his throat."* (al-Tirmidhī 3537; Ibn Mājah 4253)

> *"Whoever repents before the sun rises from the west, God will accept his repentance."* (Muslim 2703)

This is why Pharaoh's last-minute confession — *"I believe that there is no god but Him in whom the Children of Israel believe"* (10:90) — was answered with: *"Now? When you had disobeyed before?"* (10:91). The humbled faces of this verse are the faces of those who discovered humility one day late. The analogy is examination results: the student who humbles himself to study before the exam is praised; the one who weeps before the published grades is pitied but not passed. Time is part of the moral equation, and the Hereafter honors only the humility that was offered when pride was still possible.

**The Two Humilities and the Choice Between Them**

Every human being will be humbled — the only question is when and to what profit. The believer chooses the humility of prayer five times a day, placing his noblest part, his forehead, on the dust, and is raised by it; the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"No one humbles himself for God but that God raises him."* (Muslim 2588)

The arrogant refuses, and is lowered by compulsion on a Day when lowering carries no reward. Modern psychology confirms the pattern in miniature: the person who can admit fault early — to a spouse, an employer, a friend — usually repairs the damage, while the one who denies until the evidence is undeniable suffers both the fault and the disgrace of exposure. Verse 2 universalizes this into eternity. It also comforts the oppressed believer: the tyrants whose faces today are lifted in media and ceremony, who humiliate the weak and mock the pious, will wear on their faces exactly what they inflicted — abasement — while the faces they scorned will shine, as verse 8 will show. The verse is thus both a threat and a promise, depending on which face one prepares.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:3

> **toiling, weary,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Words That Describe an Entire Wasted Life**

*ʿĀmilatun nāṣibah* — "toiling, weary": two adjectives, no verb, no object, as though the Qur'an will not even dignify this labor by naming what it produced. The first word comes from *ʿ-m-l*, work or deed, the same root as *ʿamal*, the Qur'an's standard term for moral action. The second comes from *n-ṣ-b*, utter exhaustion, fatigue that settles into the bones. The pairing is haunting: maximum effort, maximum weariness — and, as the following verses reveal, maximum loss. It is the portrait of labor that devours its laborer. The great question the classical commentators debated is *when* this toil occurs, and their answers, taken together, cover the wretched from every side.

**Toiling in the Fire: The Labor of Punishment**

One group of early interpreters, including Qatādah and al-Suddī in reports preserved by al-Ṭabarī, held that the toil describes the damned *within* Hell: they will labor there as slaves labor — dragging chains and shackles, climbing mountains of fire, descending into valleys of torment. The Qur'an elsewhere depicts exactly this penal labor:

> *"When shackles are around their necks and chains, they shall be dragged into boiling water, then burned in the Fire."* (Qur'an 40:71–72)

On this reading, the verse overturns the sinner's bargain. He refused the "toil" of worship — prayer at dawn, fasting in summer, charity from his wealth — considering it weariness; so he is given a toil that truly wearies, without wages and without end. The irony is precise: worship is a labor that rests its worker, while punishment is a labor that exhausts without relief. Al-Qurṭubī notes the parallel with the worldly proverb that whoever abandons a small effort for God will be burdened with a great effort for other than Him.

**Toiling in the World: Effort Expended Against God**

A second group — including ʿIkrimah and, in one narration, Ibn ʿAbbās — held that the verse describes their toil *in this world*: they worked hard, schemed, accumulated, and exhausted themselves, but because their labor opposed God or ignored Him, it arrives on the Day of Judgment as nothing. This is the Qur'an's doctrine of the voided deed, stated with terrible clarity elsewhere:

> *"Say: Shall We inform you of the greatest losers in their deeds? Those whose effort (saʿy) is lost in the life of this world, while they suppose they are doing good."* (Qur'an 18:103–104)

> *"And We shall turn to what they did of deeds and make them as scattered dust."* (Qur'an 25:23)

ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is reported to have wept when he passed a Christian monk in his monastery and saw his gaunt, exhausted devotion, saying that it reminded him of this verse — a man who toiled and wearied himself, yet outside the guidance that would have made his toil fruitful (cited by Ibn Kathīr in his commentary on this verse). Whether or not one accepts the report's chain as firm, its meaning is sound and sobering: sincerity of effort cannot substitute for truth of direction. A runner who sprints with all his heart *away* from the finish line arrives exhausted and disqualified at once.

**Why Intention Decides: The Hadith That Judges All Labor**

The two interpretations meet in the Prophetic principle that governs every deed:

> *"Actions are but by intentions, and every man shall have only what he intended."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907)

This is why two men may perform outwardly identical labor — building a hospital, writing a book, working late into the night — and one arrives with mountains of reward while the other arrives with *ʿāmilatun nāṣibah*, toil and weariness. The difference is not in the sweat but in the aim: for God or for show, within His limits or outside them. Ibn Taymiyyah developed this into a general law of the spiritual life: deeds done for other than God, however strenuous, bring their doer only fatigue in this world and regret in the next, because the heart was created to rest in its Creator, and labor severed from Him is the soul pulling a cart with no destination.

**Burnout Without Barakah: The Modern Face of Nāṣibah**

No verse diagnoses modern burnout more precisely than this one. Millions today are *ʿāmilah* — working, producing, optimizing — and *nāṣibah* — exhausted, depleted, running on stimulants and anxiety — yet unable to say what their labor is *for*. The Qur'an does not condemn work; it condemns work cut off from ultimate purpose. The believer works as hard as anyone, but his labor is gathered into meaning by intention, bounded by worship that punctuates the day with rest, and aimed at an eternity where no effort is lost:

> *"Indeed We do not allow to be lost the reward of one who does good work."* (Qur'an 18:30)

The practical lesson is a daily audit: before asking "how much did I produce?" ask "for whom did I produce it, and within whose limits?" A small deed aimed at God outweighs a mountain of toil aimed at vanity — and a life of purposeful moderation will outlast, in this world and the next, a life of frantic, directionless exhaustion.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:4

> **entering a scorching Fire,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Taṣlā — The Verb of Roasting**

The verb *taṣlā* comes from *ṣ-l-y*, meaning to roast or burn by direct exposure — the same root as *"he shall roast (yaṣlā) in a blazing Fire"* (84:12) and *"he will roast (sayaslā) in a Fire of flame"* (111:3). It is not the verb of passing through fire but of being subjected to it until it penetrates. The classical readers preserved two canonical readings: *taṣlā* ("entering/burning") and *tuṣlā* ("being made to enter"), the passive emphasizing compulsion — they will be driven and cast in, not walking willingly. Both readings are true at once: the damned enter the Fire, and they are *made* to enter it, dragged by the justice their own deeds invoked. The Qur'an elsewhere joins the two aspects:

> *"On the Day they shall be dragged into the Fire on their faces: 'Taste the touch of Saqar.'"* (Qur'an 54:48)

**Nāran Ḥāmiyah — Fire at Its Utmost Intensity**

*Ḥāmiyah* means intensely, fiercely hot — fire at the peak of its burning. The identical phrase seals Sūrah al-Qāriʿah: *"a scorching Fire (nārun ḥāmiyah)"* (101:11). The Prophet ﷺ quantified the difference between earthly fire and that Fire:

> *"Your fire is one part of seventy parts of the fire of Hell."* They said: "O Messenger of God, it would have sufficed as it is." He said: *"It has been favored over it by sixty-nine parts, each like its heat."* (al-Bukhārī 3265; Muslim 2843)

And he described the Fire almost as a living creature that breathes and complains:

> *"The Fire complained to its Lord, saying: 'My Lord, parts of me have consumed other parts.' So He permitted it two breaths: a breath in winter and a breath in summer. The most intense heat you feel is from its heat, and the most intense cold you feel is from its cold."* (al-Bukhārī 536; Muslim 617)

These reports are not given for the imagination to play with but for the will to reckon with: the punishment is real, prepared, and proportioned to a rebellion against infinite majesty. Ibn Kathīr, commenting on this verse, gathers such reports to impress that the Fire's heat is not metaphorical discomfort but a created reality beyond earthly measure.

**The Arrival of Hell: Seventy Thousand Bridles**

The Qur'an presents Hell not as a passive place but as a force brought forth on that Day: *"And Hell shall be brought that Day"* (89:23). The Prophet ﷺ described its arrival:

> *"Hell shall be brought on that Day with seventy thousand bridles, and with every bridle seventy thousand angels dragging it."* (Muslim 2842, from Ibn Masʿūd)

Nearly five billion angels restraining a single creation — the image staggers the mind precisely in order to humble it. And lest anyone imagine the Fire touches only the monstrous, the Prophet ﷺ described even its lightest degree:

> *"The lightest in punishment among the people of the Fire on the Day of Resurrection is a man with two shoes and laces of fire, by which his brain boils as a pot boils. He thinks no one is punished more severely than he, though he is the lightest of them in punishment."* (al-Bukhārī 3885; Muslim 213, from al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr)

If this is the lightest, what is the heaviest? The verse answers with a single adjective — *ḥāmiyah* — and leaves the conscience to do the arithmetic.

**Entering as Consequence, Not Cruelty**

A frequent modern objection asks how a merciful God could prepare such a Fire. The Qur'an's answer is woven into the verse's grammar: they *enter* — the Fire is the destination of a road they walked by choice, warned at every step. God declares:

> *"And your Lord wrongs no one."* (Qur'an 18:49)

and:

> *"That is for what your hands put forth, and God is never unjust to His servants."* (Qur'an 3:182)

The analogy of the signposted cliff is exact: if a man is warned repeatedly, shown the fence, given a map, and still sprints past every barrier, his fall is not the cruelty of the cliff's maker but the consequence of his sprint. Moreover, the Fire's severity corresponds to the crime's magnitude: to witness the signs of the Creator — the camel, the sky, the mountains, the earth, all named in this very sūrah — and to answer them with denial and arrogance is not a small offense. Ibn Taymiyyah argued that punishment in the Hereafter is the perfection of justice, not its opposite: a universe in which Pharaoh and the infant he slaughtered end identically would be the truly unjust universe. The Fire exists because justice exists.

**Fear That Heals: The Proper Use of This Verse**

This verse is medicine, and like all strong medicine it must be dosed correctly. The believer reads it not to despair — despair is itself a sin — but to recalibrate. The Prophet ﷺ wept at verses of warning, and he taught his community to seek refuge from the Fire regularly, making it part of the daily liturgy:

> *"O God, I seek refuge in You from the punishment of Hell..."* (Muslim 588, from Abū Hurayrah, in the supplication after the final tashahhud)

The righteous fear of Hell produces three fruits: it detaches the heart from sins that seemed sweet, it softens the heart toward the threatened (driving the believer to warn others gently), and it makes the effort of obedience feel light — dawn prayer is no longer "hard" when weighed against *nāran ḥāmiyah*. The one who truly believes in this verse finds that it does not darken his life but protects it, the way a fence does not imprison the garden but preserves it.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:5

> **given drink from a boiling spring.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tusqā — Drink Forced Upon the Drinker**

The verb *tusqā* is passive: "they are given to drink." In Paradise, drink is an honor offered to guests; here, drinking is part of the sentence, administered to those who would refuse it if refusal were possible. The Qur'an describes the scene elsewhere with graphic precision:

> *"And if they cry for relief, they shall be relieved with water like molten brass, which scalds the faces. Wretched is the drink, and wretched the resting place."* (Qur'an 18:29)

Thirst is among the most desperate of human sufferings — a man dying of thirst will drink seawater though it kills him — and the punishment seizes precisely upon this desperation: the drink comes, but it is itself torment. The cruelty of the damned's thirst is that it cannot be quenched, only deepened, and the cruelty of the drink is that it cannot be refused.

**ʿAynin Āniyah — A Spring at the Peak of Heat**

*ʿAyn* means a spring or fountainhead — the very word that in verse 12 will describe the delight of Paradise (*ʿaynun jāriyah*, "a flowing spring"). The contrast is deliberate and devastating: spring against spring, the same noun, opposite eternities. *Āniyah* means having reached the utmost limit of heat, water at its fiercest boiling point. The identical term appears in Sūrah al-Raḥmān:

> *"They shall circle between it and scalding water made utmost (ḥamīm ān)."* (Qur'an 55:44)

Al-Ṭabarī explains *ānīyah* as water whose heat has fully matured — boiled until nothing more can be added. Ibn Kathīr adds that this is the counterpart and opposite of the Garden's flowing spring: there the water gives life and coolness; here it carries death that never completes its work. The Prophet ﷺ described the effect of such drink:

> *"Boiling water shall be poured over their heads, penetrating until it reaches their bellies, tearing apart what is inside, and then it comes out at their feet."* (al-Tirmidhī, from Abū Hurayrah)

Whether one dwells on the letter or the lesson of such reports, their direction is single: the Hereafter inverts every relief the sinner refused to earn lawfully.

**The Menu of Torment: Ḥamīm, Ghassāq, and Muhl**

The Qur'an names several drinks of Hell, and the commentators, led by al-Qurṭubī, reconcile them as the portions of different levels or different moments of punishment: *ḥamīm* (scalding water, 6:70; 10:4), *ghassāq* (festering, icy-cold pus-like fluid — *"so let them taste it: boiling water and festering fluid,"* 38:57; 78:25), and *muhl* (molten brass or dregs of oil, 18:29; 44:45). The variety itself is part of the punishment: just as the Garden's delights are endlessly varied so that bliss never stales, the Fire's torments are varied so that suffering never numbs. Al-Rāzī observes a further correspondence: in the world, the damned drank wine to befuddle their reason and escape their conscience; in the Hereafter they drink what befuddles nothing but scalds everything — the escape has become the trap.

**Thirst in the Desert Imagination**

To the first Arab hearers, few images could have struck deeper. The desert dweller knew thirst as the king of terrors: caravans perished for lack of water, and the discovery of a spring (*ʿayn*) was the difference between life and death, celebrated in poetry for generations. To promise a spring and deliver boiling torment is to take the desert's greatest symbol of mercy and turn it inside out. The verse thus speaks in the native language of its audience while addressing a universal nerve: every human being knows what it is to long for relief — for water, for rest, for an end to pain — and this verse warns that relief sought outside God's path ends in a relief that tortures. The psychology is exact: sin promises quenching and delivers thirst. The addict, the miser, the fame-hunter — each drinks salt water, thirstier with every draught. Hell's boiling spring is simply that earthly law carried to its eternal conclusion.

**The Two Springs and the Choice of Thirsts**

Set verse 5 beside verse 12 and the sūrah's whole moral geometry appears: *ʿaynin āniyah* against *ʿaynin jāriyah* — the spring that scalds against the spring that flows. Both are called *ʿayn*; the difference is not in the noun but in the path that led to it. The believer in this world accepts a voluntary thirst — the fast of Ramaḍān, the restraint of desire — and is promised that the fasting one has a joy at breaking fast and a joy at meeting his Lord (al-Bukhārī 1904; Muslim 1151), including drink from the Prophet's own Basin (al-Ḥawḍ) on the Day of Thirst, of which:

> *"Whoever drinks from it shall never thirst again."* (al-Bukhārī 6583; Muslim 2292, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr)

The practical counsel is therefore simple and daily: choose your thirst. Every soul will thirst — either now, briefly and by choice, for God's sake, or then, eternally and by compulsion. The wise postpone quenching; the foolish postpone thirsting.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:6

> **They will have no food, save a vile thorn,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḍarīʿ — The Botany of Damnation**

The verse names the damned's food: *ḍarīʿ*, which the early authorities describe with the precision of desert naturalists. Al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, and others report that *ḍarīʿ* is the dried state of a bitter, thorny desert shrub called *shibrīq*: green, it is soft enough that camels graze it; dry, it becomes foul, thorny, and so vile that no beast will touch it. Ibn ʿAbbās, in one narration, said it is "a tree of the Fire," and Qatādah called it the vilest of foods. The sheer concreteness of the image is part of its power: the Qur'an does not say "they shall eat torment" in the abstract but names a plant its hearers could picture — bend down, they had seen it crackle underfoot — and tells them this is the *best* on the menu. The worldly parallel is exact: the bitter end of sweet sins. What began as indulgence ends as thorns in the throat.

**Thorns Bitterer Than Aloes: The Reported Description**

The commentary tradition preserves a Prophetic description of this food, cited by al-Qurṭubī:

> *"Ḍarīʿ is a thing in the Fire resembling thorns, bitterer than aloes, more fetid than a putrid corpse, and more burning than fire."*

(The report's chain has been questioned by the ḥadīth critics, and it is cited here as it stands in the classical commentaries — as an illustration of how the early community understood the verse's horror, not as an independently established text.) Its threefold comparison is worth pondering: bitter to taste, foul to smell, burning to touch — every sense assaulted at once. Whether taken as literal botany of the unseen or as the closest earthly approximation of an otherworldly reality, the point stands: this is food that punishes the eater, the exact inversion of food's purpose. The Qur'an issues the same verdict on the Fire's other famous food, the Zaqqūm tree:

> *"Indeed the tree of Zaqqūm is the food of the sinful, like molten brass, boiling in the bellies like the boiling of scalding water."* (Qur'an 44:43–46)

**One Hell, Many Dishes: Reconciling Zaqqūm, Ghislīn, and Ḍarīʿ**

A thoughtful reader will ask: the Qur'an also names *zaqqūm* (37:62; 44:43; 56:52) and *ghislīn* — the filth washed from wounds (69:36) — as foods of Hell. How can the damned have "no food save *ḍarīʿ*" while also eating these? Al-Qurṭubī records the classical reconciliation, agreed upon by most commentators: Hell has levels and stations, and these are the vegetations and portions of different depths — *ḍarīʿ* for some, *zaqqūm* for others, *ghislīn* for others — or they are successive courses of a single lengthening torment. Others, including al-Ṭabarī, suggest the negation emphasizes exclusivity of *kind*: they shall have no *nourishing* food at all, only items of this punishing order. The apparent contradiction thus dissolves into a deeper consistency: whatever the dish, it belongs to one cuisine — the cuisine of chastisement. The believer learns a principle of reading Scripture here: gather all the texts before judging one, for the Qur'an explains itself to the patient.

**Ḍarīʿ and Humiliation: The Root That Meets the Meaning**

Some commentators, noted by al-Qurṭubī, derived a moral echo from the word's sound: *ḍarīʿ* resembles *ḍarāʿah* — abasement, lowliness, humiliation. On this reading the food's very name announces its function: eating it is part of the eater's degradation. The mighty who feasted on the finest dishes in the world — who, like the rich man of Sūrah al-Kahf or Qārūn of Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ, made the table an altar of pride — are reduced to grazing like starved beasts on what beasts refuse. The Qur'an states the law plainly:

> *"And those who disbelieved enjoy themselves and eat as cattle eat, and the Fire shall be their dwelling."* (Qur'an 47:12)

In the world they ate like cattle heedlessly; in the Hereafter they eat worse than cattle can bear. Status is not abolished in the Hereafter — it is revealed: the high made low and the low, if believing, made high.

**When Food Becomes Punishment: An Earthly Mirror**

Even in this world, food turns punitive when taken lawlessly or excessively: the glutton's feast becomes his disease, the usurer's banquet is seasoned with others' tears, the addict's craving devours his body. Physicians and moralists alike confirm that the stomach is the most honest witness — it punishes every trespass. *Ḍarīʿ* is this law eternalized: that which was consumed in disobedience now consumes its consumer. The verse quietly recommends the believer's opposite table: lawful food, earned honestly, eaten with gratitude and shared with others, of which the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"No vessel does the son of Adam fill worse than his stomach. It suffices him a few mouthfuls to keep his back straight."* (al-Tirmidhī 2380; Ibn Mājah 3349)

The one who disciplines his appetite in the world of choices will feast in the world of consequences; the one who made his stomach his god will find his god turned to thorns.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:7

> **which neither nourishes nor avails against hunger.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Purposes of Food — Both Denied**

Food in human life serves two purposes: it builds the body (*yusminu* — it fattens, nourishes, gives substance) and it repels hunger (*yughnī min jūʿ* — it avails, suffices against emptiness). This verse denies both to *ḍarīʿ*: it neither nourishes nor satisfies. The damned will eat — for the pangs of hunger are part of their torment — yet eating will neither strengthen them nor relieve them. It is consumption without benefit, the nutritional equivalent of the toil without fruit in verse 3. The structure of verses 3–7 is thus a closed circle of futility: labor that wearies, fire that never consumes, drink that scalds, food that starves. Every natural cause is severed from its effect. In the world, God joined causes to effects as a mercy — eat and be filled, drink and be quenched, work and earn; in the Fire, the joints are cut, and man discovers what the world always was beneath its veil: nothing avails except by God's leave.

**The Occasion: "But Our Camels Are Nourished by Ḍarīʿ"**

Al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, and others preserve the reported occasion of this verse: when verse 6 was revealed, some Arabs objected, "But our camels are nourished by *ḍarīʿ*!" — confusing the dried, vile thorn with *shibrīq*, the same plant in its green, edible state. So this verse was revealed to settle the matter: whatever the green shrub does for camels in the desert, *this* infernal *ḍarīʿ* neither nourishes nor satisfies. The episode, whether firmly established or illustrative, teaches two lessons. First, the Qur'an's descriptions of the unseen use earthly names to point beyond earthly things; the *ḍarīʿ* of Hell shares a name with a desert shrub the way, as Ibn ʿAbbās said of Paradise, "there is nothing in Paradise of what is in this world except names." Second, objections to revelation often rest on exactly this confusion — measuring the unseen by the seen, and the next world by this one's rules. The verse answers: names travel across the boundary; natures do not.

**The Grammar of Emptiness: Lā Yusminu wa Lā Yughnī**

The double negation — *lā... wa lā* — is emphatic and total. The Qur'an uses the same structure to void the idols ("they neither harm nor benefit"), and here it voids the food. Rhetorically, the verse forces the listener to imagine the act of eating stretched to infinity with its purpose removed: chewing that never becomes strength, swallowing that never becomes fullness. It is the precise opposite of the Garden's food, of which God says the believers eat *"in enjoyment for what they did"* (52:19), where every pleasure completes its promise. Al-Rāzī draws the theological moral: benefit (*nafʿ*) belongs to God, not to objects; bread does not fill by its own power but by His permission. The man who learns this in the world eats with gratitude; the man who denies it discovers it in the Fire, where bread — if bread it could be called — fills nothing.

**Ḍarīʿ as a Parable of Sin: Hunger That Eating Deepens**

Beyond its literal meaning, the verse is a perfect parable of every sinful appetite. Sin promises satisfaction and delivers hunger: wealth that never suffices, lust that never rests, fame that never fills, revenge that never settles. The Prophet ﷺ described this law in the image of the two valleys:

> *"If the son of Adam had two valleys of gold, he would desire a third, and nothing fills the belly of the son of Adam but dust. And God accepts the repentance of whoever repents."* (al-Bukhārī 6436; Muslim 1049, from Anas and Ibn ʿAbbās)

Every addict knows verse 7 by experience before ever reading it: the dose that neither nourishes nor avails against craving. Modern consumer culture is an entire economy built on manufacturing *ḍarīʿ* — products engineered to be consumed without satisfying, so that consumption must continue forever. The verse exposes the fraud from eternity's side: all such food, literal or metaphorical, ends in the same verdict — *lā yusminu wa lā yughnī min jūʿ*. The wise therefore feed their hunger only on what truly satisfies: lawful provision, genuine love, knowledge that guides, and worship that fills the heart's own stomach — for the heart, too, eats and starves.

**Seven Verses of Warning Complete — And Mercy Gets the Longer Passage**

With this verse the first movement of the sūrah closes: seven verses on the Fire, matching in number the seven gates of Hell (*"it has seven gates,"* 15:44 — a correspondence several commentators have noted). But observe what follows: the Garden passage runs nine verses (8–16), longer than the Fire's seven. The sūrah's proportions preach before its sentences do: wrath is real and detailed, but mercy is given more room. This is the Qur'an's consistent ratio — *"My mercy embraces all things"* (7:156) — and it governs how the believer should hold both passages together: frightened enough to flee the thorn, hopeful enough to run toward the spring.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:8

> **Faces that Day shall be blessed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Nāʿimah — Bliss Made Visible on the Face**

The sūrah pivots on a single repeated noun: *wujūh* — "faces." Verse 2 showed faces humbled; verse 8 shows faces *nāʿimah* — soft with blessing, radiant with ease, blooming with delight. The word comes from *n-ʿ-m*, the root of *niʿmah* (blessing) and *naʿīm* (bliss): literally, faces living in softness and luxury after hardship. The same root describes Paradise itself as *jannāt al-naʿīm*, "Gardens of Bliss." Where the damned wear their verdict as abasement, the blessed wear theirs as luminosity. The Qur'an returns to this face-reading of the Last Day again and again:

> *"Faces that Day radiant (nāḍirah), gazing upon their Lord."* (Qur'an 75:22–23)

> *"Faces that Day bright (musfirah), laughing, rejoicing."* (Qur'an 80:38–39)

> *"You shall recognize in their faces the radiance of bliss (naḍrat al-naʿīm)."* (Qur'an 83:24)

The parallelism is exact and intentional: same Day, same noun, opposite adjectives. The sūrah hangs the two portraits side by side like a diptych, and every listener must ask which frame his own face is walking toward.

**Why the Qur'an Describes Eternity Through Faces**

Faces are the most public part of man and the least controllable under strong feeling. A man may rehearse his words, but his face betrays him: guilt drains it, joy lights it, terror freezes it. By describing the Hereafter through faces, the Qur'an makes three points at once. First, the inner state will become outer — hypocrisy, which survived in the world by managing appearances, becomes impossible when appearances are issued by God. Second, the verdict will be instantly legible — no long trial, no confusion about who stands where; the faces announce it. Third, blessedness is not merely a location (the Garden) but a condition that shines from the person: the people of Paradise carry their Paradise on their faces before they even enter its gates. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī is reported to have said that the radiance of obedience in this world becomes the radiance of faces in the next — the dawn prayer leaves a light that the Last Day merely unveils.

**What No Eye Has Seen: The Prophetic Horizon of Bliss**

Human language cannot contain what these faces enjoy; it can only gesture. The Prophet ﷺ reported the divine promise in the words of a ḥadīth qudsī:

> *"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has conceived."* Then he added: *"Recite if you wish: 'No soul knows what is kept hidden for them of comfort for eyes.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3244; Muslim 2824, from Abū Hurayrah; the verse is 32:17)

This report sets the bounds of all Garden-description, including verses 8–16 of this sūrah: couches, goblets, cushions, carpets — these are the closest earthly nouns to realities that exceed them, the way a childhood drawing gestures at a landscape. The believer therefore reads the coming verses twice: once as literal promise (for God confirms what He says), and once as allusion to a bliss whose best part — the vision of God, the divine pleasure, the company of the prophets — no noun can carry. The faces are *nāʿimah* most of all because of what they will see, as verse 75:23 declares: their Lord.

**The Contrast That Judges Every Excuse**

Placing verse 8 immediately after verse 7 creates one of the Qur'an's sharpest juxtapositions: thorns that starve, then faces that bloom. The two destinies share nothing — not food, not drink, not light, not company — and they are separated, in the end, by choices made in a single lifetime. This is the Qur'an's doctrine of *iftirāq*, the parting: mankind walks together now — the believer and the denier share streets, markets, even families — and is then sorted forever:

> *"And the Day the Hour comes, that Day they shall be separated."* (Qur'an 30:14)

The comfort for the righteous is immense: whatever humiliation they suffer now for their faith — mockery at school, disadvantage at work, suspicion in the media — is temporary costume, while their true face, the *nāʿimah* face, is being prepared. And the warning for the scornful is symmetrical: the faces now lifted in pride will be lowered, and the faces now lowered in prayer will be lifted. Every sneer at a believer is a loan the Last Day will collect.

**Preparing the Face: What Makes a Face Nāʿimah?**

If the face of eternity is prepared in time, what prepares it? The Qur'an and Sunnah name the cosmetics of the blessed face: ablution, whose traces will shine as marks of honor — *"my community shall come on the Day of Resurrection with radiant limbs from the traces of ablution"* (al-Bukhārī 136; Muslim 246); prostration, whose mark is light; truthfulness, which brightens; and love of God, which beautifies from within. Umm al-Dardāʾ reportedly said that the righteous have a glow even in this world that the discerning recognize. The practical program is therefore concrete: guard the five prayers, for they wash the face five times daily toward its final radiance; guard the tongue from the malice that darkens faces; and meet people with a cheerful face, for the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Do not belittle any good deed, even meeting your brother with a cheerful face."* (Muslim 2626)

The face that smiles for God in the world is rehearsing its eternity.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:9

> **contented by their endeavoring**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Li-Saʿyihā Rāḍiyah — Satisfaction Earned by Striving**

The blessed faces are *rāḍiyah* — contented, pleased, satisfied — and the verse names the cause: *li-saʿyihā*, "by their striving." *Saʿy* is purposeful effort, the exertion of one walking briskly toward a goal; the same word names the ritual walking between Ṣafā and Marwah, Hagar's striving that God eternalized as worship. *Riḍā* (contentment) is among the highest stations of the soul — God describes the saved as *"God pleased with them and they pleased with Him"* (98:8). Here the contentment has a specific object: they are pleased *with their own striving*, now that they see what it purchased. The worker has received his wages and found them multiplied; the student has opened his results and found them honors; the farmer beholds the harvest of seeds sown in tears. The Qur'an captures the shout of such a soul:

> *"As for him who is given his book in his right hand, he shall say: 'Here, read my book! Indeed I knew I would meet my reckoning.' So he shall be in a pleasant life."* (Qur'an 69:19–21)

**The Question of Worth: Did My Effort Matter?**

Few anxieties are more universal than the fear of wasted effort — years of study for a cancelled exam, decades of labor for a collapsed currency, a lifetime of devotion met with ingratitude. Hell's own inhabitants voice this grief as *ʿāmilatun nāṣibah* (verse 3): toil and exhaustion, nothing more. Verse 9 is heaven's answer to that exact fear. God does not merely reward striving; He *appreciates* it — one of His names is *al-Shakūr*, the Most Appreciative:

> *"Indeed our Lord is Forgiving, Most Appreciative."* (Qur'an 35:34)

And He promises the people of Paradise:

> *"Indeed this is a reward for you, and your striving has been appreciated (mashkūrā)."* (Qur'an 76:22)

Ponder the theology: the servant works for his Master, and the Master *thanks* him — though the strength to work was the Master's own gift. Al-Saʿdī comments that God's appreciation means He multiplies the reward beyond all proportion, accepts the small deed, and overlooks the faults mixed with it. No effort done for God is ever lost, ever miscalculated, ever forgotten — a promise no earthly employer has ever made or could keep.

**The Law of the Harvest: Man Has Only His Striving**

The verse rests on the Qur'an's universal law of moral causation:

> *"And that man shall have nothing but what he strives for, and that his striving shall soon be seen, then he shall be rewarded for it with the fullest reward."* (Qur'an 53:39–41)

Classical scholars debated the apparent tension between this law and the fact that children benefit from parents' prayers or that one Muslim's supplication aids another — and resolved it, as Ibn Kathīr explains, by the principle that such benefits themselves flow from striving: the righteous child is the fruit of the parent's striving, the praying brother the fruit of fellowship the believer cultivated. The law stands: the currency of the Hereafter is earned in this world, and its exchange rate is pure generosity — one good deed multiplied tenfold to seven hundredfold and beyond (6:160; 2:261). The denier's tragedy is not that God refused him wages but that he labored in a currency his home country does not accept.

**Small and Constant: The Striving God Loves Most**

What kind of *saʿy* produces this contentment? Not necessarily the spectacular, but the sincere and sustained. The Prophet ﷺ was asked which deeds are most beloved to God, and he answered:

> *"The most beloved deeds to God are the most constant, even if small."* (al-Bukhārī 6464; Muslim 783, from ʿĀʾishah)

Two units of dawn prayer kept for a lifetime outweigh, in this scale, a single night of zeal followed by abandonment. The contentment of verse 9 will therefore surprise many: quiet souls who prayed their five, earned lawfully, raised children patiently, and forgave injuries will find their modest, repeated striving grown into mountains — for God *"multiplies for whom He wills"* (2:261). Conversely, the verse gently warns the perfectionist and the procrastinator alike: the former, that God appreciates effort beyond results; the latter, that contentment on that Day belongs to those who strove *before* it. Every day offers a small, purchasable share of *riḍā*; the wise buy daily.

**Rest After Labor: The Psychology of the Satisfied Worker**

There is a rest that only the worker knows — the deep sleep after honest labor, the peace of the craftsman surveying finished work. Paradise's contentment is that rest perfected and eternalized. Note the sequence: striving first, contentment after; there is no *rāḍiyah* without *saʿy*. This is why the Qur'an never promises Paradise to wishful thinking — *"It is not by your wishes"* (4:123) — and why the Prophet ﷺ, promised Paradise himself, prayed until his feet swelled, saying when asked why:

> *"Should I not be a thankful servant?"* (al-Bukhārī 1130; Muslim 2819, from al-Mughīrah ibn Shuʿbah)

Modern culture inverts the order, marketing contentment without striving — entertainment without effort, status without service, peace without principle — and harvests the anxiety it promised to cure. Verse 9 restores the order: strive now in the brief field of the world, and be content forever in the Garden where striving ends and only its fruits remain.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:10

> **in a lofty Garden,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jannatin ʿĀliyah — Elevation as Honor**

The striving of verse 9 finds its address: *fī jannatin ʿāliyah* — "in a lofty Garden." *ʿĀliyah* means high, elevated, exalted — the same quality ascribed to God Himself (*al-ʿAlī*, the Most High). In Arabic as in every language, height signifies honor: thrones are raised, the noble are "high-born," and degradation is a "fall." The Garden is lofty in every sense: in location, above the sufferings of the lower world; in rank, above every earthly pleasure; and in purity, above everything base. The Qur'an uses the identical phrase for the saved soul:

> *"So he shall be in a pleasant life, in a lofty Garden (jannatin ʿāliyah)."* (Qur'an 69:21–22)

And it contrasts the two elevations of the Hereafter: the Garden raised high for the humble, and the Fire sunk low — *"the lowest of the low"* (95:5) — for the arrogant. The moral physics is perfect: those who lowered themselves before God are lifted; those who exalted themselves against Him are dropped.

**One Hundred Levels: The Architecture of Reward**

Paradise is not a flat plain but a hierarchy of splendor, with stations proportioned to deeds. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Indeed in Paradise there are one hundred levels, which God has prepared for those who strive in His path. Between each two levels is like the distance between heaven and earth."* (al-Bukhārī 2790, from Abū Hurayrah)

In the fuller narration he added:

> *"So when you ask God, ask Him for al-Firdaws, for it is the highest part of Paradise and the midst of Paradise, and above it is the Throne of the Most Merciful, and from it spring the rivers of Paradise."* (al-Bukhārī 2790)

The loftiness of verse 10 thus contains both comfort and ambition: comfort, because every believer, even the least, inherits a station of honor far above the world; ambition, because the heights are open to whoever strives — the martyr, the truthful, the scholar who acts, the orphan's guardian, the one whose character was beautiful. No one in Paradise will envy another's level with worldly envy, for God removes all rancor (15:47); yet in this world, the levels summon every soul to climb as high as grace permits.

**Above What? The Loftiness of Escape**

The Garden's height also means distance — distance from everything that made the world heavy. Below it lie left behind: fear and grief (*"no fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve,"* 2:38), fatigue (*"no weariness touches us therein,"* 35:35), falsehood (as verse 11 will declare), death itself, which will be slaughtered between Paradise and Hell in the form of a ram while it is announced *"O people of Paradise, eternity without death"* (al-Bukhārī 4730; Muslim 2849, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī). Ponder the mercy: the same souls who in the world wept at graves, trembled at diagnoses, and aged toward weakness will dwell where none of these can climb. The loftiness is therefore not cold altitude but warm refuge — the height of a fortress that keeps every sorrow outside its walls. Every tear shed in obedience in the low world is a step toward the high Garden.

**High Rank for Lowly Deeds: How the Lofty Is Reached**

How does one reach a Garden so high? The Prophet ﷺ pointed to a ladder built of ordinary rungs: faith, prayer, charity, fasting, character. He promised the guardian of orphans nearness to him in Paradise like two adjacent fingers (al-Bukhārī 5304); he promised a house in the highest Paradise for beautiful character (Abū Dāwūd 4800); he said the road to the Garden is paved with difficulties and the road to the Fire with desires:

> *"The Fire is surrounded by desires, and Paradise is surrounded by disliked things."* (al-Bukhārī 6487; Muslim 2823, from Abū Hurayrah and Anas)

The image is exact: climbing is effortful, descending is easy. Every dawn prayer performed against sleep's desire is a step upward; every charity given against greed's desire is a step upward; every anger swallowed, every lust restrained, every truth spoken at cost — all are steps. The Garden is lofty, but its staircase stands in the middle of daily life, and its steps are the very duties the soul finds heavy. The heaviness *is* the height.

**The Lofty Garden and the Lowly World: Keeping Proportion**

Modern life flattens all heights: celebrity and sanctity receive equal screen space, and the eternal is scrolled past like everything else. Verse 10 restores verticality. The believer learns to evaluate every offer of the world against the altitude of the Garden: a promotion that costs one's prayers is not a promotion but a descent; a pleasure that forfeits Paradise is not pleasure but a pit. This is not world-rejection — Islam commands engagement with the world — but world-proportioning. The Companion's standard, *"Be in this world as a stranger or a traveler"* (al-Bukhārī 6416, from Ibn ʿUmar), is the practical form of verse 10: the traveler gladly endures the low road because he knows the high home. And when the believer finally arrives, he will find that every low moment borne for God — every humiliation, hunger, and fatigue — has been transmuted into altitude, and he will say with the people of Paradise:

> *"Praise be to God, who has removed all sorrow from us."* (Qur'an 35:34)

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:11

> **wherein they hear no idle talk.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lāgḥiyah — The Blessing of a Purified Soundscape**

*They hear no lāgḥiyah therein.* The word *lāgḥiyah* — idle, vain, frivolous talk — comes from *l-gh-w*, the same root as *laghw*, which the Qur'an elsewhere banishes from Paradise in every form:

> *"They shall hear therein no idle talk (laghw) nor sinfulness, only the saying: 'Peace, peace.'"* (Qur'an 56:25–26)

> *"They shall hear therein no idle talk (laghw) nor lying."* (Qur'an 78:35)

> *"They shall not hear therein idle talk (laghw); only peace."* (Qur'an 19:62)

Four sūrahs repeat the promise, which tells us its weight: the absence of vain speech is not a minor amenity of Paradise but one of its defining features. Think of what fills human ears in this world — gossip, backbiting, boasting, lying, obscenity, quarreling, flattery, mockery, the endless chatter that wastes hours and wounds hearts. Paradise removes the entire category. Its soundscape is composed of greeting, gratitude, truthful conversation, and the praise of God. The ears, which in the world were assaulted daily, finally rest.

**Whose Hearing? A Window into the Canonical Readings**

The classical commentators, including Ibn Kathīr, note a beautiful richness in this verse's transmission. In the reading *lā tasmaʿu* ("you hear not"), the subject is the faces — the blessed themselves hear no idle talk; in the understanding reflected by other readings (*lā yusmaʿu*, "there is not heard"), the statement is absolute — no idle talk is heard therein at all, by anyone, ever. The difference dissolves into a greater certainty: whether from the side of the hearer or the side of the sound, Paradise and vanity of speech never meet. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the righteous themselves agents of this purity even in the world:

> *"And when they hear idle talk, they turn away from it and say: 'To us our deeds and to you your deeds. Peace be upon you.'"* (Qur'an 28:55)

The people of Paradise practiced in the world what Paradise perfects: they walked past *laghw* with dignity (25:72), and so they inherit an abode where *laghw* cannot follow.

**The Tongue's Wounds: Why This Promise Matters So Much**

Why would God make "no idle talk" one of the headline promises of eternity? Because the tongue is the source of most human misery. Wounds of the body heal; wounds of words fester for decades. Backbiting destroys friendships, slander ruins reputations, quarrels split families, and entire wars begin as boasts and insults. The Prophet ﷺ traced the majority of damnation to this one organ:

> *"And are people thrown onto their faces in the Fire except for the harvests of their tongues?"* (al-Tirmidhī 2616; Ibn Mājah 3973, from Muʿādh ibn Jabal)

And he gave the believer's lifelong rule:

> *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day should speak good or remain silent."* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47, from Abū Hurayrah)

Verse 11 is the eternal reward of everyone who lived by that rule: a world where the harvest of tongues is only peace. It also quietly warns the entertainment-saturated modern: hours daily spent consuming vain speech — gossip shows, slander columns, comment-section quarrels — are hours spent rehearsing for the wrong abode. The ear, like the stomach, becomes what it consumes.

**Silence, Peace, and the Music of Paradise**

What *is* heard in Paradise, if not *laghw*? The Qur'an answers: *salām* — peace (56:26; 19:62); the angels' greeting — *"Peace be upon you for what you patiently endured"* (13:24); the grateful conversation of the saved recounting God's favors; and, in authenticated reports, voices of beauty beyond description. The Prophet ﷺ said of the maidens of Paradise that they sing with voices the like of which no creature has heard (al-Tirmidhī 2564, from ʿAlī — graded ḥasan by some critics). More importantly, the greatest sound of Paradise is the voice of God Himself addressing His servants, as in the report of the divine greeting and the unveiling of the Vision (Muslim 181, from Ṣuhayb: the people of Paradise shall see their Lord, *"and nothing they were given shall be dearer to them than gazing upon Him"*). Verse 11 thus promises not emptiness but replacement: every vain sound subtracted, every noble sound multiplied. The silence of Paradise is not the silence of the grave but the silence of a concert hall before the music begins — the hush of perfect expectation, forever fulfilled.

**Practicing Paradise with the Ears: A Daily Discipline**

The verse yields an immediate program for this world. First, guard the exits: speak good or be silent, as commanded above. Second, guard the entrances: reduce voluntary intake of *laghw* — the gossip feed, the outrage cycle, the backbiting gathering — for the Prophet ﷺ warned that the backbiter's company stains its silent approver. Third, fill the soundscape with the Garden's own sounds: Qur'an recited and pondered, remembrance (*dhikr*) in the mornings and evenings, truthful and kind conversation, and knowledge that benefits. The believer who does this discovers a psychological miracle: as vain noise decreases, inner peace increases — a down payment of verse 11 in the present life. For the heart is an echo chamber: fill the ears with peace, and the heart learns to resound with it.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:12

> **Therein lies a flowing spring,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿAynun Jāriyah — The Spring That Never Ceases**

*Fīhā ʿaynun jāriyah* — "therein is a flowing spring." The noun *ʿayn* is the same word as the boiling spring of verse 5 (*ʿaynin āniyah*): spring against spring, the sūrah's starkest single-word contrast. But *jāriyah* — flowing, running, streaming — reverses every letter of the Fire's portion: where that water is stagnant in its boiling, this water runs; where that scalds, this refreshes; where that is forced upon the drinker, this is the landscape's own generosity. Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī's successors, and al-Zamakhsharī note that the singular *ʿayn* here has a generic import — it signifies not one spring but springs in multitude, as the Qur'an confirms:

> *"Indeed the righteous shall be amid gardens and springs (ʿuyūn)."* (Qur'an 15:45; cf. 51:15; 77:41)

One noun opens into an infinity of fountains: Paradise is watered beyond imagining.

**The Named Springs: Kāfūr, Tasnīm, Salsabīl**

The Qur'an names several springs of Paradise, each with a character, as though eternity had vintages:

> *"Indeed the righteous shall drink from a cup whose mixture is kāfūr (camphor) — a spring from which the servants of God drink, making it gush abundantly."* (Qur'an 76:5–6)

> *"And its mixture is of tasnīm — a spring from which those brought near drink."* (Qur'an 83:27–28)

> *"A spring therein named Salsabīl."* (Qur'an 76:18)

The commentators explain: *Kāfūr* is cool and fragrant; *Tasnīm* is the highest drink, reserved in pure form for the foremost while others receive it mixed; *Salsabīl* flows smoothly, its very name melodious — sweet to pronounce, sweet to drink. Add to these the River al-Kawthar, granted to the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"Indeed We have granted you al-Kawthar."* (Qur'an 108:1)

— of which he said its water is whiter than milk and sweeter than honey, its banks of gold and its bed of pearls (Muslim 400, from Anas; cf. al-Bukhārī 4965). The flowing spring of verse 12 is thus the doorway into a whole hydrology of bliss: rivers of water incorruptible, rivers of milk unchanging, rivers of wine delightful, rivers of honey purified (47:15). In the desert-born imagination, nothing says abundance like water that never runs dry.

**Why Water? The Desert's Deepest Symbol of Mercy**

To understand this verse's force on its first hearers, one must remember that in Arabia water was life's thinnest thread. Tribes warred over wells; caravans perished between them; a single spring could anchor a settlement for centuries. The Qur'an itself invokes this dependence as proof of God:

> *"Say: Have you considered — if your water were to sink away, who would bring you flowing water (māʿin maʿīn)?"* (Qur'an 67:30)

To a people who measured wealth in wells, the promise of a *jāriyah* — water that flows without drawing, without ropes, without fear of the bucket coming up empty — was the promise of security itself. And the symbol translates to every age: water is the one necessity no human can manufacture, the daily proof of dependence. Every glass of water in this world is a loan from God's mercy; the flowing spring of Paradise is that mercy made permanent and visible. The believer who drinks with gratitude in the world — naming God before and praising Him after — is practicing the etiquette of verse 12.

**Flowing as a Parable: Blessings That Do Not Stagnate**

Stagnant water breeds corruption; flowing water stays pure. The adjective *jāriyah* therefore carries a spiritual parable: the blessings of Paradise never stale, never bore, never diminish with enjoyment — unlike every worldly pleasure, which fades with repetition. The food of the world satiates then sickens; its scenery enchants then bores; its honors thrill then burden. Paradise's spring *flows* — always fresh, always new. The Prophet ﷺ hinted at this eternal novelty when he described Paradise's market where a wind increases the beauty of its people each Friday (Muslim 2833, from Anas). Theologians like al-Ghazālī drew the parallel to knowledge and worship in this world: the heart that flows with remembrance stays sweet, while the heart that stagnates in neglect breeds the mosquitoes of doubt and desire. Be *jāriyah*, the verse whispers — a running spring, not a still pool.

**The Two Springs Revisited: Āniyah and Jāriyah**

Set the sūrah's two *ʿayn*s side by side one final time and a complete theology of recompense appears. Both are called springs; both are eternal; both are certain. The difference lies entirely in the life that led to each. The same God sends both waters; the same Day reveals both; the same human nature — thirsty, needy, longing — receives both. Everything is parallel except the moral history. This is the Qur'an's doctrine of *jazāʾ*, exact recompense: the vessels are alike, but one is filled with wrath and the other with mercy, according to what each soul sent forth. The practical conclusion is inescapable and daily: every act of obedience is a digger's stroke toward the flowing spring, and every act of defiance a stroke toward the boiling one. The springs are being dug now; they will be drunk from then.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:13

> **therein are raised couches,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sururun Marfūʿah — Thrones of Honor**

*Fīhā sururun marfūʿah* — "therein are couches raised high." *Surur* is the plural of *sarīr* — a couch, bed, or throne, the seat of rest and dignity; kings sit on *sarīr*, and the Qur'an seats the people of Paradise on them as kings. *Marfūʿah* — raised, elevated — matches the Garden's own loftiness (*ʿāliyah*, verse 10): everything in Paradise is high, including its furniture. These are the same couches described elsewhere as worked with gold and ornament:

> *"On couches worked with gold (mawdūnah), reclining upon them, facing one another."* (Qur'an 56:15–16)

and:

> *"Reclining therein on couches (arāʾik); they shall see therein neither sun nor bitter cold."* (Qur'an 76:13)

The posture matters: they *recline* — the position of those whose labor is finished, whose enemies are gone, whose provision is assured. In the world, only the secure recline; the anxious sit upright, the laborer bends, the prisoner huddles. Paradise grants its people the posture of perfect security, forever.

**Raised Above What? The Meaning of Elevation**

Why must the couches be *raised*? The commentators offer layers. Physically, elevation means vantage and honor — the seated survey gardens and rivers spread below, as kings survey their lands. Spiritually, it signifies rank: God *"raises"* (*yarfaʿu*) the believers in degrees (58:11), and the couches make that raising visible. Morally, it reverses the world's seating plan: in the world, the arrogant seized the high seats while believers were pushed to the margins — mocked, boycotted, seated at the back; in Paradise the arrangement is corrected, and the once-scorned recline above while *"the criminals shall be in the Fire"* (82:14). There is also a tender correspondence: the believer *lowered* himself in prostration in the world, placing his forehead on the ground; God *raises* his seat in the Hereafter. Every *sajdah* was a deposit toward altitude, and the couches are the withdrawal.

**Facing One Another: The Fellowship of the Couches**

The Qur'an adds two details that turn furniture into felicity. First, the seated face one another (56:16; 15:47) — Paradise is social; its joy is shared, its conversations face to face, without screens, ranks, or turned backs. Second, God performs a surgery of the heart before seating them:

> *"And We shall remove whatever rancor is in their breasts; as brothers, on couches facing one another."* (Qur'an 15:47)

Ponder the mercy: no envy between levels, no residue of old quarrels, no awkwardness among company — every heart cleansed, every relationship healed, every gathering pure delight. The world knows no such assembly: every feast has its tensions, every reunion its ghosts. Paradise's couches are therefore not merely comfortable but *reconciled*; they seat a community that has been perfected. The believer who forgives in this world — who lets go of grudges, seeks out estranged kin, and prays for rivals — is preparing his own heart for that seating plan.

**Rest After Weariness: The Answer to Nāṣibah**

Verse 13 answers verse 3 across the sūrah's divide. There: *ʿāmilatun nāṣibah* — toiling, weary, with no couch and no rest. Here: raised couches for those whose striving (*saʿy*, verse 9) God appreciated. The symmetry preaches the Qur'an's philosophy of rest: true rest is not the idleness that precedes labor but the repose that follows it. The Prophet ﷺ embodied this: he labored in worship, in teaching, in mercy to creation, until God called him to *"your Lord... and He will be pleased"* (93:5; cf. 89:27–30, *"O tranquil soul, return to your Lord, pleased and pleasing"*). The modern world, which chases rest through escape — vacations from a life one hates, retirements from work one resented — finds rest elusive because it skipped the labor that gives rest meaning. Paradise's couches cannot be purchased with money; they are inherited by weariness borne for God. As the Prophet ﷺ said of the two joys of the fasting person — one at breaking fast, one at meeting his Lord (al-Bukhārī 1904; Muslim 1151) — so every fatigue endured in obedience has an appointment with a couch.

**Dignity as a Divine Gift: What the Couches Declare**

Finally, the couches declare the believer's worth in God's sight. The world measures dignity by wealth, office, beauty, or fame — measures that exclude most of mankind and abandon all of them at death. God measures by faith and righteousness, and seats accordingly. The slave Bilāl, tortured under desert stones for saying "One! One!", will recline; his torturer, if unrepentant, will burn. The poor widow's charity, the laborer's dawn prayer, the orphan's patience — all are currency for *surur*. This is why the Qur'an calls the believers *"the honored servants"* (21:26) and promises:

> *"Indeed the most noble of you in God's sight is the most God-conscious of you."* (Qur'an 49:13)

The raised couches are that verse made into furniture: nobility recognized, honor enthroned, worth confirmed forever. Every believer who feels small in the world's seating should remember verse 13 — his chair is already being raised.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:14

> **goblets placed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Akwābun Mawḍūʿah — Cups Set Ready at Hand**

*Wa akwābun mawḍūʿah* — "and goblets placed." *Akwāb* is the plural of *kūb* — a drinking cup or goblet without handle or spout, the vessel of served drink. *Mawḍūʿah* — placed, set down, arranged — means they stand ready where the drinker needs them: no waiting, no asking, no pouring by one's own effort. The scene is one of perfect hospitality: the guest reclines (verse 13) and the cup is already there. The Qur'an elaborates the service elsewhere with breathtaking detail:

> *"There shall circulate among them vessels of silver and goblets of crystal (qawārīr) — crystal of silver, measured in due measure."* (Qur'an 76:15–16)

> *"There shall be passed around them platters of gold and goblets."* (Qur'an 43:71)

> *"Served by immortal youths, with goblets and jugs and a cup from a flowing spring."* (Qur'an 56:17–18)

Silver that is crystal, gold that circulates endlessly, youths who never age carrying cups that never empty — the imagery strains language to express inexhaustible, dignified refreshment.

**The Wine of Paradise: Delight Without Harm**

The classical commentators, noting that goblets in the Qur'an accompany the wine of Paradise, take this verse as an allusion to that pure drink — and hasten to distinguish it from every earthly intoxicant:

> *"They shall be served a cup from a flowing spring — white, delicious to the drinkers; no headache therein, nor shall they be intoxicated."* (Qur'an 37:45–47)

> *"Neither aching of head shall they have from it, nor shall they be intoxicated."* (Qur'an 56:19)

Earthly wine is pleasure laced with poison: it gladdens briefly, then befuddles, sickens, addicts, impoverishes, and embitters — dissolving reason, loosening tongues to ruin, and turning celebration into regret. Paradise's wine keeps the delight and discards the harm: joy without headache, warmth without witlessness, festivity without a morning after. The contrast embodies a universal law: every forbidden pleasure in this world is a corrupted copy of a pure original reserved for the obedient. God did not forbid wine to impoverish His servants but to protect them for the uncorrupted vintage. Hence the Prophet's solemn warning:

> *"Whoever drinks wine in this world and does not repent from it shall be deprived of it in the Hereafter."* (al-Bukhārī 5575; Muslim 2003, from Ibn ʿUmar)

Abstinence now is not loss but layaway.

**Hospitality of the King: Everything Within Reach**

The deeper theme of the verse is divine hospitality. In this world, even the richest host tires, even the finest service delays, even the grandest banquet ends. In Paradise the Host is God Himself, who declares:

> *"A hospitality (nuzul) from the Forgiving, the Merciful."* (Qur'an 41:32)

The goblets are *placed* — the banquet perpetually set, the table never cleared. The fruits hang near — *"its clusters near at hand"* (69:23), *"low-hanging"* (76:14) — so that the reclining guest need not even rise. Ponder what this says about God's generosity: the believer spent his worldly life *serving* — serving parents, guests, the poor, the community, his Lord — and now, forever, he is *served*. The roles of the world are graciously reversed, not as repayment of debt (God owes nothing) but as the overflow of *al-Karīm*, the Most Generous. Every cup of water the believer offered a thirsty soul in the world returns as goblets placed around him in eternity — for:

> *"Is the reward of goodness anything but goodness?"* (Qur'an 55:60)

**The Etiquette of Drink: Gratitude, Right Hands, and Restraint**

The verse also perfects the manners the Sunnah taught for drinking in this world: to sit (or at least pause with remembrance), to mention God's name, to drink in measured draughts, to praise God after, and to share. The Prophet ﷺ forbade drinking from gold and silver vessels in this world, saying:

> *"Do not drink from gold and silver vessels... for they are theirs in this world and ours in the Hereafter."* (al-Bukhārī 5633; Muslim 2067, from Ḥudhayfah)

Verse 14 is the second half of that sentence: the vessels renounced for God's sake in the world reappear as *akwāb* placed in Paradise. The principle extends to every appetite: what is surrendered for God is returned purified and multiplied; what is seized against His command returns as *ḥamīm* and *ghassāq*. The believer's table manners — eating what is lawful, avoiding waste (*"eat and drink, but waste not,"* 7:31), honoring guests, feeding the poor — are thus rehearsals for the eternal banquet. Every shared meal in the world is a small *mawḍūʿah*, a cup placed for another that God will place back for the giver.

**Placed — The End of Striving for Provision**

Finally, *mawḍūʿah* announces the end of the oldest human anxiety: provision. In the world, drink requires labor — wells dug, pipes laid, bills paid, wars even fought over rivers. In Paradise the goblets are simply *there*, placed by hands unseen, filled from springs unfailing. The Qur'an describes this freedom from economic fear as one of the Garden's essential mercies:

> *"Eat and drink in enjoyment for what you did."* (Qur'an 52:19; cf. 69:24)

No earning, no bills, no scarcity, no inflation of desire — the entire apparatus of survival retired. Modern man, crushed by the economics of existence, working to afford the food that fuels the work, should hear in this small verse the promise of release: the rat-race has a finish line for the righteous, beyond which every goblet is already placed. The wise therefore work in the world as a traveler works for his journey's provisions — diligently but without worshipping them — knowing the true banquet, with cups already set, awaits at home.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:15

> **cushions arrayed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Namāriqu Maṣfūfah — Comfort Arranged in Rows**

*Wa namāriqu maṣfūfah* — "and cushions arrayed." *Namāriq* is the plural of *numruqah* — a cushion or pillow, the soft support of the reclining guest. *Maṣfūfah* — arrayed, set in rows, arranged in order — adds precision: the cushions are not heaped carelessly but *placed in ranks*, each in its position. The verse completes the furnishing begun in verse 13: couches to recline on, goblets to drink from (verse 14), cushions to lean against. The scene grows more intimate with each verse — from the chamber to the cup to the pillow — as though the camera of revelation draws nearer to the blessed guest, showing that God's care extends to the smallest detail of his comfort. Nothing in Paradise is approximate; everything is arranged.

**Order as a Kind of Bliss: The Theology of Maṣfūfah**

Why would the Qur'an mention the *arrangement* of cushions? Because order itself is a mercy. Consider the miseries of disorder in this world: the anxious mind's clutter, the war-torn street's chaos, the grieving home's disarray. Paradise is the opposite of all entropy — a realm where everything is in its right place, eternally. The arrayed cushions signify a universe perfectly ordered: no quarrel disrupts the seating, no intruder disturbs the rows, no time decays the fabric. The Qur'an elsewhere describes Paradise's inhabitants themselves as ordered — *"ranks"* of angels and believers in worship and procession — and its pleasures as measured (*qawārīr... qaddarūhā taqdīrā*, 76:16). Al-Rāzī observes that the Qur'an's Garden-descriptions progress from the essential (gardens, rivers) to the luxurious (couches, goblets) to the minute (cushions, carpets) precisely to teach that divine generosity neglects nothing: the God who arranged salvation also arranged the pillows. If He cares this much for the seating of His guests, how much more for their souls?

**The Cushion and the Prostration: Softness Earned by Hardness**

There is a correspondence the early preachers loved: the heads that pressed hard ground in prostration now rest on *namāriq*. The believer's worship in this world is deliberately un-cushioned — dawn prayer on a cold floor, fasting with an empty stomach, pilgrimage under a hard sun — and Paradise answers each hardness with softness. The Prophet ﷺ himself slept on a coarse mat that marked his side; when ʿUmar wept at the contrast with the luxuries of Caesar and Chosroes, he answered:

> *"Are you not content that they should have this world and we should have the Hereafter?"* (al-Bukhārī 2468; Muslim 1479, from Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿUmar)

Verse 15 is the fulfillment of that exchange: the coarse mats of the righteous become arrayed cushions, while the silks of the arrogant — if they died defiant — become garments of pitch (14:50). The believer who simplifies his bedding for dawn prayer, who rises from a warm bed into the cold for *fajr*, is trading one softness for a better one. Every comfort surrendered for God is a cushion ordered for eternity.

**Luxury Without Corruption: Paradise Purifies Pleasure**

A skeptical reader might ask whether cushions and carpets reduce eternity to interior decoration. The Qur'an anticipates the objection by purifying every luxury it promises. Earthly luxury corrupts: it breeds arrogance, envy, idleness, and dependence; the rich fear loss, the poor resent exclusion, and comfort itself becomes a chain. Paradise's luxury is severed from every such accompaniment: no rancor (15:47), no fear of loss (eternity is guaranteed), no envy (each is content with his portion), no idleness of soul (the inhabitants grow in knowledge and praise). The cushions are therefore not trivial but theological: they prove that pleasure itself is not evil — God created comfort and calls it good — but that sin had poisoned it, and Paradise serves it purified. The asceticism of this world is not hatred of comfort but quarantine from its corruption; in the Garden the quarantine lifts, and the believer leans on his cushion with a conscience as soft as the silk.

**Arranging the World: What Maṣfūfah Teaches Today**

The arrayed cushions also model an ethic for the present: the believer is a person of *order*. He arranges his prayers in rows behind one imām, shoulder to shoulder; he arranges his home, his work, his time; he keeps trusts, meets appointments, and leaves places better than he found them — for God loves excellence (*iḥsān*) in all things, and the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Indeed God loves that when one of you does a deed, he perfects it."* (al-Ṭabarānī, al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ; graded ḥasan by al-Albānī)

The slovenliness that treats worship carelessly, promises loosely, and responsibilities haphazardly is the moral opposite of *maṣfūfah*. Moreover, hospitality in this world — arranging one's own cushions for guests, receiving them with prepared comfort — is a direct imitation of the divine hospitality of these verses. Every guest honored with an arranged seat is a rehearsal of Paradise, and God, who arranged cushions for His obedient servants, will arrange honors for those who honored others for His sake.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:16

> **and carpets spread.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Zarābiyyu Mabthūthah — Spaciousness Underfoot**

*Wa zarābiyyu mabthūthah* — "and carpets spread." *Zarābī* is the plural of *zirbiyyah* — a fine carpet or rug, the luxurious floor-covering of palaces. *Mabthūthah* — spread out, strewn widely — suggests expanse: carpets extending in every direction, covering vast floors. The verse completes the Garden chamber: couches (13), goblets (14), cushions (15), carpets (16) — from furniture to vessels to pillows to floors, the description descends gently until the whole scene rests complete. The Qur'an elsewhere adds green hues and exquisite craft to the picture:

> *"Reclining on green cushions (rafraf) and beautiful carpets (ʿabqarī)."* (Qur'an 55:76)

Ibn ʿAbbās and the early commentators understood *zarābī* as the finest of spreads — what kings unrolled for honored guests — now unrolled endlessly for every inhabitant. In Paradise, the floor itself is honor.

**Spread Wide: The Opposite of the Narrow Fire**

*Mabthūthah* — spread, expansive — answers by contrast the Fire's defining spatial torment: narrowness. The damned are cast into constricted places:

> *"And when they are cast into a narrow place therein, bound together, they shall call there for destruction."* (Qur'an 25:13)

Hell compresses; Paradise expands. The physics of the Hereafter moralizes space itself: the soul that narrowed itself in the world — narrowed by greed, by envy, by refusal to make room for truth or for others — inherits narrowness; the soul that widened itself — in charity, in forgiveness, in hospitality, in contemplation of God's vastness — inherits wideness. God says of the Garden that its width is *"as the width of heaven and earth"* (57:21), and the spread carpets are that vastness made touchable underfoot. Every step in Paradise lands on softness; every direction opens further. The world, for all its oceans and continents, is a narrow place where men jostle for inches; the Garden is spaciousness without competition, for its Owner's bounty has no edges.

**The Completed Chamber: Seven Verses of the Garden Palace**

Verses 10–16 form a single palace-scene in seven strokes: the lofty Garden (10), its pure soundscape (11), its flowing spring (12), its raised couches (13), its placed goblets (14), its arrayed cushions (15), its spread carpets (16). The progression moves from architecture to atmosphere to water to furniture to vessels to fabrics to floors — a complete interior, furnished by God for guests who did nothing to earn it by strict merit yet everything to receive it by grace and striving. Several classical preachers counted these furnishings against the seven verses of the Fire (1–7): seven against seven, torment itemized against bliss itemized — with the Garden passage as a whole running longer (verses 8–16, nine verses), since bliss overflows its parallel. The chamber also answers, item by item, the deprivations of the righteous in the world: they slept on hard ground — here are carpets; they sat upright in vigilance — here are couches; they thirsted in fasting — here are goblets; they heard mockery — here is only peace. Paradise is the world's every righteous lack, supplied forever.

**Carpets and Kingship: The Dignity of Every Believer**

In the ancient world, to walk on carpets was the privilege of royalty; common feet touched dust and stone. By carpeting Paradise for *all* its people, God confers kingship on every believer — the slave and the queen, the unknown laborer and the renowned scholar, all tread alike on *zarābī*. The Prophet ﷺ confirmed this universal ennoblement:

> *"The lowest of the people of Paradise in station shall have... "* — and he described kingdoms vaster than earthly empires, until the least of them possesses what no king possessed (Muslim 189, from al-Mughīrah ibn Shuʿbah, in the report of the least and greatest in Paradise).

This is the Qur'an's radical answer to every hierarchy of the world: the hierarchies of wealth, tribe, race, and office — which the Qur'an never fully abolished in the world's administration but relativized in worth — are dissolved in the Garden's seating, where the only aristocracy is righteousness (49:13). The believer humiliated in the world for his poverty, his color, his status, or his faith should picture verse 16: his feet, dusty now from honest roads, will sink into carpets spread by God. No earthly snobbery survives that image.

**From Carpets to Signs: Why the Sūrah Turns Outward Next**

With this verse, the palace-scene closes — and the sūrah turns abruptly from the unseen to the seen: *"Do they not consider the camels...?"* (verse 17). The transition is masterful. Having described two eternities no eye has witnessed, the Qur'an now points to four witnesses every eye sees daily, as if to say: you doubt the furnishing of the unseen? Then examine the furnishing of the seen — the same Hand arranged both. The carpets of Paradise are believed through the same reasoning as the carpet of the earth (verse 20): design implies a Designer, generosity implies a Generous One. The sūrah thus moves from promise to proof, from the end of the journey to the signposts along its beginning. The one who truly sees camels, sky, mountains, and earth will find the couches and carpets entirely credible — for they are the work of the same Artisan, in the same style, at a higher level of liberality.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:17

> **Do they not consider the camels, how they are created;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Afalā Yanẓurūn — The Command to Look with Thought**

*Af alā yanẓurūna* — "Do they not then look?" The particle opens with astonishment and rebuke: how can they see and not see? *Naẓar* here is not the glance of the eyeball but the gaze of the mind — reflective, sustained observation that draws conclusions. The Qur'an distinguishes the two sights: the disbelievers *"have eyes with which they do not see"* (7:179) — their retinas function while their reflection sleeps. This verse issues the Qur'an's standing summons to natural theology: the evidence for God is not locked in books or limited to scholars but exhibited daily before every eye. Al-Baghawī and Ibn Kathīr stress that these four signs — camels, sky, mountains, earth — were the inescapable furniture of the Arab's world: whatever direction he looked on a desert journey, one of them filled his vision. The argument meets man where he stands: no laboratory required, no expedition needed — only open eyes and an honest mind.

**Al-Ibil — Why the Camel Heads the List**

*Al-ibil* — the camels — come first because they were, for the Arabs, the supreme composite miracle of utility and design. Al-Baghawī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ṭabarī's tradition enumerate the camel's wonders: it is strong enough to carry heavy loads for days yet docile enough for a child to lead; it stores nourishment in its hump, water in its body, and patience in its nature; it eats thorny brush that other beasts refuse; it provides milk, meat, wool, leather, and transport — a complete economy on four legs; it kneels to be loaded and rises under burdens; its eyelashes, nostrils, and feet are all adapted to sand and glare. Modern biology has only deepened the marvel: the camel's oval red blood cells that survive dehydration, its ability to lose a third of its body water and recover by drinking a hundred liters at once, its temperature regulation, its sand-proof anatomy. The verse says *kayfa khuliqat* — "how it is created" — inviting not a glance but an investigation into the *manner* of its making, the engineering of its details. Each adaptation is a signature; the camel carries hundreds.

**Docility as Rebuke: The Camel That Yields and the Man Who Will Not**

Beyond anatomy, the commentators — including voices in the tradition noted by later scholars — drew a moral parallel from the camel's character: despite its massive strength, the camel yields to its guide, kneels at a word, and follows a thin rope. Man, physically frail beside it, refuses to yield to his Creator and Guide. The Qur'an makes the comparison explicit elsewhere:

> *"Do you not see that to God prostrates whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on earth — the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, the beasts — and many of mankind? But many deserve punishment."* (Qur'an 22:18)

The camel obeys its nature; only man rebels against his. The verse thus functions as both proof and chastisement: proof, because such design demands a Designer; chastisement, because the beast that submits shames the rational creature that does not. A minority view, recorded by al-Qurṭubī, read *ibil* here as rain-clouds (by a rare linguistic usage) — but the overwhelming majority, anchored in the desert context and the sequence with sky, mountains, and earth, keep it as camels: the living sign standing at eye level.

**The Qur'an's Method: From Creation to Creator**

This verse exemplifies the Qur'anic argument from design — simple, public, and cumulative. It does not argue from abstract metaphysics but from present experience: you see the camel; consider *how* it is made; infer the Wisdom and Power that made it. The same method fills the Qur'an:

> *"Indeed in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are signs for people of understanding — those who remember God standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth: 'Our Lord, You did not create this in vain.'"* (Qur'an 3:190–191)

The Prophet ﷺ, upon receiving these verses, reportedly said: *"Woe to whoever recites them and does not reflect upon them"* (reported by Ibn Ḥibbān in his Ṣaḥīḥ and others, from ʿĀʾishah). Reflection (*tafakkur*) is thus not optional piety but the verse's direct command. Ibn Taymiyyah argued that this natural knowledge of God — available to every sound mind through signs like these — is the foundation upon which revelation builds: the Qur'an does not ask man to believe against reason but to follow reason to its Author and then receive His guidance.

**Blind in the Age of Sight: The Modern Failure of Naẓar**

Never has mankind seen more and considered less. The modern eye, armed with microscopes and telescopes, observes the camel's cells and the star's spectrum — then scrolls on. Information has replaced contemplation; the gaze is constant but *naẓar* is rare. Verse 17 diagnoses the disorder precisely: the problem was never lack of evidence but lack of *consideration*. The cure it prescribes is radically accessible: take one creature — a camel, a bird, an eye, a leaf — and ask *kayfa*: how is it made, how did its parts come to serve its needs, how could unseeing causes produce seeing contrivance? Asked honestly and repeatedly, the question dissolves atheism's confidence more surely than volumes of polemics. The believer's daily practice follows: moments of deliberate *tafakkur* — at dawn's breaking, at night's stars, at the birth of a child, at one's own hand — turning existence itself into a remembrance. For the signs were never hidden; only the seers were distracted.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:18

> **the sky, how it is raised;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kayfa Rufiʿat — The Canopy Without Pillars**

*Wa ilā al-samāʾi kayfa rufiʿat* — "and at the sky, how it is raised." From the camel at eye level the gaze lifts to the heaven overhead. *Rufiʿat* — raised, uplifted, elevated — presents the sky as a canopy hoisted above the world without visible support. The Qur'an returns to this marvel repeatedly, each time adding a facet:

> *"Do they not look at the sky above them, how We built it and adorned it, and it has no rifts?"* (Qur'an 50:6)

> *"He raised the heavens without pillars that you see."* (Qur'an 13:2)

> *"And He withholds the sky from falling upon the earth except by His leave."* (Qur'an 22:65)

> *"He raised its height (samkahā) and proportioned it."* (Qur'an 79:28)

The argument is architectural: every raised roof the Arabs knew — tent-cloth, thatch, timber — required poles, ropes, and builders, and all of them sagged, leaked, and fell. Above them stretched a roof vaster than all tents, flawless, self-sustained, adorned with lamps (stars) — and its Builder unseen. The inference the verse demands is immediate: what Pillars hold it, and what Hand raised it?

**Vastness as Proof: The Sky That Humbles Every Measure**

The first hearers saw a dome of blue by day and a field of lamps by night; modern man knows it as depths that defeat imagination — hundreds of billions of galaxies, distances measured in billions of light-years, a fine-tuned expanse still widening:

> *"And the heaven We built with strength, and indeed We are expanding it."* (Qur'an 51:47)

Yet the verse's logic needs no telescope: whatever the sky's true measure, its order is visible to all — the sun's punctuality, the moon's phases, the stars' navigation, the rain's descent from its heights. Each regularity is a promise kept, and promises kept imply a Keeper. Al-Ghazālī in his meditations on creation invited the reader to consider the sky as a roof, the earth as a floor, and the stars as lamps, the sun as a lamp-bearer serving a household — then asked who appointed the servants and pays nothing, who maintains the house without fatigue. The sky's testimony is therefore twofold: its *vastness* proves God's greatness, and its *regularity* proves His wisdom and care. Awe and trust — the two wings of worship — are both born from looking up.

**Adorned and Unrifled: Beauty as Evidence**

Sūrah Qāf's parallel verse adds two details — the sky is *adorned* (*zayyannāhā*) and *without rifts* (*mā lahā min furūj*). Both are arguments. Adornment: the stars are not merely functional (for navigation and reckoning) but beautiful — gratuitous splendor serving no survival need, as though the Builder signed His work with joy. The Qur'an says:

> *"And We adorned the nearest heaven with lamps."* (Qur'an 67:5; cf. 37:6)

And flawlessness: despite its vastness and age, the celestial order shows no cracks, no breakdowns, no maintenance closures — unlike every human edifice, which crumbles from the day it is finished. God challenges the doubter directly:

> *"So return your vision — do you see any flaw? Then return your vision twice again; your vision shall return to you humbled, worn out."* (Qur'an 67:3–4)

Verse 18 issues the same challenge in miniature: look up, look long, look for a defect in the raising — and return humbled, which is to say, instructed. The skeptic's eye, honestly employed, becomes the believer's witness.

**The Raised Sky and the Abased Denier: A Sūrah-Wide Symmetry**

Within this sūrah, the raised sky answers the humbled faces of verse 2. The denier refused to raise his gaze to heaven's Maker in worship; on the Last Day his own gaze is lowered in disgrace (*khāshiʿah*). The believer raised his eyes and hands to heaven in supplication; in Paradise he reclines in a *lofty* (*ʿāliyah*) Garden under no sky of trial. Up and down run through the whole chapter as moral directions: the Fire below, the Garden above; the boiling spring sunk in torment, the flowing spring in elevation; the couches raised, the faces of the damned lowered. Verse 18 anchors this vertical symbolism in cosmology: God is *al-ʿAlī*, the Most High — above His creation in majesty, as the mainstream creed affirms through texts like *"the Most Merciful, upon the Throne He established Himself"* (20:5) — and the raised sky is the visible sermon of His transcendence. To look up with reflection is to orient the soul correctly: small below, great above, mercy descending, worship ascending.

**Recovering the Sky: A Lost Practice of the Soul**

Modernity has stolen the sky. Electric light blots the stars for most of mankind; roofs, screens, and schedules keep eyes lowered; many city-dwellers pass months without truly seeing the heaven this verse commands them to consider. The loss is spiritual, not merely aesthetic: the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions lived under the sky — praying by its signs, traveling by its lamps, marveling at its clarity — and the Qur'an assumes a sky-watching reader. The believer today must deliberately recover the practice: step out at night, look up at whatever stars remain, recite *"Our Lord, You did not create this in vain"* (3:191), and feel the soul's cramped anxieties shrink against the vastness. Psychologists confirm what the verse implies: experiences of vastness — awe — reduce self-absorption, quiet rumination, and increase generosity. The sky is God's free clinic for the inflated, anxious self; verse 18 is its prescription, refilled nightly.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:19

> **the mountains, how they are established;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kayfa Nuṣibat — The Setting Up of the Firm Ones**

*Wa ilā al-jibāli kayfa nuṣibat* — "and at the mountains, how they are set up." *Nuṣibat*, from *n-ṣ-b*, means erected, set upright, fixed in place — the verb of pitching a monument or raising a mast. The mountains stand as though planted by a giant hand: immovable, towering, visible from days away. The Qur'an names their function explicitly elsewhere:

> *"And He cast into the earth firm mountains (rawāsiya), lest it shake with you."* (Qur'an 16:15; cf. 21:31; 31:10)

> *"And the mountains as pegs (awtād)."* (Qur'an 78:7)

Two images — anchors and pegs — present the mountains as instruments of stability: the earth's mass steadied, its crust pinned, its dwellers secured. The desert Arab, who watched dunes shift and flash-floods tear wadis, knew instability intimately; the mountains were the earth's promise that not everything moves. The verse commands him to consider *how* they were established — their mass poised, their roots sunk, their peaks raised — and to infer the Power and Mercy that fixed them.

**Pegs and Roots: What Lies Beneath the Majesty**

The image of the peg (*watad*) is precise: a peg's visible part is small beside its buried part, and its function — holding the tent against wind — depends on the hidden depth. Classical commentators took the comparison as manifest: mountains grip the earth. Modern geology has added an intriguing correspondence: mountains possess deep crustal roots extending far below their visible mass, stabilizing the plates — a fact unknown to the seventh century and consonant with the Qur'an's image, though the verse's argument needs no modern science to stand. Its logic is timeless and public: immense masses, perfectly placed, serving life. Al-Rāzī catalogued the mountains' services — storing water as snow and springs, yielding minerals and stone, sheltering valleys from winds, marking routes for travelers, terracing climates — and argued that each benefit multiplies the proof: the mountains are not merely impressive but *arranged for us*. Impressiveness proves Power; arrangement proves Mercy; both together prove God.

**When the Pegs Are Lifted: Mountains on the Overwhelming Day**

The sūrah that opens with *al-Ghāshiyah* gains depth from what the Qur'an elsewhere says of mountains on that Day: the firmest things in human experience will be unfastened. The Pegs shall be pulled:

> *"And the mountains shall be as carded wool."* (Qur'an 101:5)

> *"And you shall see the mountains, supposing them firm, while they pass as the passing of clouds."* (Qur'an 27:88)

> *"And they ask you about the mountains — say: My Lord shall blast them to bits."* (Qur'an 20:105)

The sequence is deliberate theology: the same God who *established* (*nuṣibat*) the mountains can *un-establish* them in an hour. Their current firmness is not independence but obedience — they stand because they are held. This is why the Qur'an presents even mountains as worshippers in their own mode:

> *"Indeed We subjected the mountains to glorify with him (David) in the evening and at sunrise."* (Qur'an 38:18)

The lesson for the denier is stark: if the mountains — massive, ancient, proverbial for permanence — will drift like wool at God's command, what will become of the frail human frame that defied Him? And for the believer, the lesson is hope: the God who pinned the earth with mountains can pin down a trembling heart with tranquility.

**Firmness as a Moral Ideal: Be Like the Mountain**

The mountain also stands in revelation as the ideal of believing steadfastness. The Prophet ﷺ described the believer's heart anchored by faith, and the Qur'an compares the good word to a good tree *"firmly rooted"* (14:24) — the same virtue in vegetable form. The believer is to be mountain-like: unmoved by the winds of doubt, desire, and intimidation; rooted in the unseen depths of conviction while his peak — his aspiration — touches heaven. The Prophet ﷺ himself was the supreme instance: boycotted, bereaved, wounded, and slandered, he stood unmoved in his mission, saying of the sun and moon placed in his hands that he would not abandon his call (reported in the Sīrah of Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām). Verse 19 thus preaches twice: look at the mountains as *proof* of God, and stand like the mountains as *servants* of God. The one who reflects on their establishment and imitates their firmness has taken the verse whole.

**Mountains of the Modern Eye: Tourism Without Tafakkur**

Millions today travel to mountains for sport, photography, and "conquering" peaks — standing where verse 19 commands reflection, yet often reflecting on nothing but themselves. The verse reclaims the mountain from recreation to revelation: the climber who plants a flag might better plant a question — *kayfa nuṣibat*? Who raised this mass, who stocked it with water and minerals, who holds it still beneath my boots? The Qur'an permits enjoyment of creation — indeed commands traveling through the earth to *see* (29:20; 47:10) — but insists the seeing become *consideration*. A practical counsel: let every encounter with the majestic — mountains, oceans, storms — end in the believer's ancient response, *"Subḥān Allāh"* (glory be to God), the verbal form of *naẓar*. The mountain does not need your admiration; your soul needs its sermon.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:20

> **and the earth, how it is spread?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kayfa Suṭiḥat — The Spreading of the Dwelling**

*Wa ilā al-arḍi kayfa suṭiḥat* — "and at the earth, how it is spread." *Suṭiḥat*, from *s-ṭ-ḥ*, means spread out, leveled, made flat and habitable — like a surface prepared for use. The Qur'an describes this preparation with a cluster of images, each adding a shade of hospitality:

> *"And the earth — We spread it out (madadnāhā) and cast therein firm mountains."* (Qur'an 15:19)

> *"Who made the earth a bed (firāsh) for you."* (Qur'an 2:22)

> *"Who made the earth a cradle (mihād) for you."* (Qur'an 20:53; 43:10; 78:6)

> *"And God made the earth a vast expanse (bisāṭ) for you, that you may travel its spacious paths."* (Qur'an 71:19–20)

Bed, cradle, carpet, expanse: the earth is presented as a prepared dwelling — furnished, softened, and widened for its inhabitants. The verse commands reflection on the *manner* of the spreading: the soil that yields crops, the plains that carry roads, the valleys that gather water, the proportions of land and sea that cradle life. Every step on level ground is an answer to verse 20 waiting to be noticed.

**Spread for Dwelling: Function, Not Geometry**

Classical scholars read *suṭiḥat* as describing the earth's habitability — its expanse spread for living, building, farming, and traveling — not as a lesson in astronomy. Many pre-modern Muslim scholars, including astronomers within the tradition, discussed the earth's spherical shape from other evidences while affirming this verse's plain sense: however vast the globe, its surface is experienced as spread, stable, and accommodating — a mercy of proportion. Ibn Taymiyyah noted the early scholars' agreement on the heavens' roundness from scriptural and rational proofs, and the same tradition held verse 20's point without tension: the verse speaks of *function* (a dwelling prepared) rather than *figure*. The distinction protects the verse from becoming a dated cosmology and reveals it as timeless teleology: however the earth is shaped, it is *arranged* — its gravity, atmosphere, water, and soil all calibrated for life. The argument is not "the earth is flat, therefore God" (which the verse neither states nor needs) but "the earth is prepared, therefore a Preparer" — an inference as valid under telescopes as under tents.

**The Debate the Verse Declines: Reading Scripture on Its Own Terms**

Modern polemics sometimes drag this verse into disputes about the earth's shape, whether to attack or to defend. Both sides miss the verse's genre: it is a sermon on providence, not a treatise on geodesy — just as "the sun rises" in ordinary speech describes appearance and benefit, not orbital mechanics. The Qur'an elsewhere describes cosmic realities in similarly functional language (the sun "running to its resting place," 36:38; the heavens "without pillars you see," 13:2) while remaining true at every level of knowledge. The sound method, modeled by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and the mainstream, is to take the verse's evident intent — marvel at the earth's hospitality and infer its Maker — without forcing it to adjudicate questions it was not revealed to settle. The believer's response to scientific discovery is therefore neither panic nor triumphalism but the verse's own posture: *naẓar* — deeper consideration of ever-deeper design. The more geology, ecology, and astronomy reveal of the earth's fine-tuned hospitality, the longer verse 20's sermon grows.

**The Fourth Sign Completes the Panorama: A Desert Journey in Four Gazes**

Al-Baghawī and others note the geographical genius of verses 17–20: they map exactly what a desert traveler sees — the camel beneath him, the sky above, the mountains on the horizon, the earth beneath his feet. Four gazes compass the whole world: the living creature (biology), the heaven (astronomy), the mountains (geology), the earth (habitation) — the entire Book of Nature in one sweep. The order is also a pedagogy: begin with the nearest marvel (the beast at your side), lift to the highest (the sky), turn to the grandest (the mountains), and rest on the most familiar (the ground) — discovering that the familiar is the most marvelous of all, since familiarity had hidden it. Together the four signs argue cumulatively: any one might be dismissed by the determined denier, but the four together — utility, vastness, firmness, hospitality — form a cord of proof not easily broken. The sūrah's structure thus mirrors its message: as the Fire and Garden passages piled detail upon detail, so the proof passage piles sign upon sign. Abundance of evidence meets abundance of recompense.

**Gratitude as the Proof's Fruit: From Suṭiḥat to Shukr**

Every sign in verses 17–20 demands not only inference but gratitude. The camel serves, the sky shelters, the mountains steady, the earth carries — and all of it is *for you*:

> *"And He subjected to you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on earth, all from Him."* (Qur'an 45:13)

The proper conclusion of *naẓar* is therefore *shukr* (thankfulness): to use the earth without corrupting it (*"cause no corruption on earth after its reform,"* 7:56), to eat its provision without waste, to walk upon it humbly (*"the servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth humbly,"* 25:63). Environmental care in Islam is not modern fashion but ancient verse: the spread earth is a trust (*amānah*), and its Spreader will ask how it was kept. The believer who litters the street, wastes water in ablution's excess, or devours resources heedlessly has failed verse 20's exam even if he recites it perfectly. To see the spreading and thank the Spreader — with tongue, with limbs, with stewardship — is to complete the four verses' circuit: from eye to mind to heart to hands.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:21

> **So remind! Thou art but a reminder;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fadhakkir — The Mission in a Single Imperative**

*Fadhakkir* — "So remind!" The *fa* (so/then) ties the command to everything preceding: *because* the Overwhelming is coming, *because* the two destinies are real, *because* the signs stand evident — therefore, remind. *Dhakkir* comes from *dh-k-r*, remembrance: to remind is to awaken what the soul already knows but has forgotten. The Qur'an's anthropology holds that every heart carries a buried memory of its Creator — the primordial covenant (*"Am I not your Lord?" They said: "Yes,"* 7:172) — and that revelation, like these four signs, *reminds* rather than informs from nothing. The Prophet's mission is thus defined as awakening, not invention; as calling back, not calling to the unknown. And the same standing order extends, in measure, to every believer after him:

> *"And remind, for indeed reminder benefits the believers."* (Qur'an 51:55)

Preaching in Islam is never the manufacture of guilt but the dusting of mirrors — the face (of truth) is already there; the reminder only clears the dust.

**Innamā Anta Mudhakkir — The Limitation That Liberates**

*"Thou art but (innamā) a reminder"* — the particle *innamā* restricts: reminding is *all* you are charged with; results are not your burden. The Qur'an defines the Prophet's office through a constellation of such titles, each marking both duty and limit: a warner (*nadhīr*) and bearer of glad tidings (*bashīr*), a witness (*shāhid*), a caller to God by His leave (*dāʿin ilā Allāh*), and an illuminating lamp (*sirāj munīr*) — *"O Prophet, indeed We have sent you as a witness, a bearer of glad tidings, and a warner, and a caller to God by His permission, and an illuminating lamp"* (33:45–46). A lamp illuminates; it does not compel eyes to open. This limitation liberated the Prophet ﷺ from the crushing grief he felt over his people's rejection — a grief so deep that God consoled him:

> *"So do not let yourself perish in sorrow over them."* (Qur'an 35:8; cf. 18:6; 26:3)

The preacher's peace lies in this verse: his account holds only the delivery, never the reception. He plants and waters; only God gives growth (3:37; cf. 1 Corinthians-style? no — keep Islamic: *"You do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills,"* 28:56).

**The Sunnah of Reminding: Wisdom, Gentleness, and Perseverance**

How is the reminding to be done? The Qur'an legislates its manners:

> *"Call to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in the best manner."* (Qur'an 16:125)

The Prophet ﷺ embodied the method: thirteen years in Makkah reminding with patience — reciting, warning, weeping, forgiving persecution — without a single act of coercion; and his gentleness was itself revelation-confirmed: *"It is by mercy from God that you were gentle with them; had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you"* (3:159). Even to Pharaoh — the archetype of tyranny — Mūsā and Hārūn were commanded: *"Speak to him with gentle speech, that perhaps he may be reminded or fear"* (20:44; cf. 79:17–19). Verse 21 thus authorizes persistence while forbidding harshness: remind, and remind again, but never bully, mock, or despair. The modern bearer of this verse — parent, teacher, friend, caller — inherits both halves: the courage to speak truth when silence is easier, and the mercy to speak it as a reminder, not a weapon.

**Convey From Me: The Mission Passes to the Ummah**

The command *fadhakkir*, though addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, flows through him to his entire community, for he explicitly deputized them:

> *"Convey from me, even a single verse."* (al-Bukhārī 3461, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr)

And he defined the community itself by this function:

> *"You are the best community brought forth for mankind: you enjoin right and forbid wrong and believe in God."* (Qur'an 3:110)

Every believer is therefore a *mudhakkir* in miniature — within his circle, by his means, according to his knowledge. The mother reminding her child to pray, the friend warning against a harmful habit, the writer clarifying a doubt, the neighbor embodying honesty — all stand under verse 21. The conditions are knowledge (remind of what you truly know), sincerity (for God, not for ego), wisdom (right words, right time, right tone), and patience (results belong to God). The great consolation follows: no sincere reminder is ever wasted — if it does not benefit its hearer, it benefits its speaker, recorded as obedience to *fadhakkir*; and seeds of reminder sprout years later in hearts that seemed stone. The caller sows in hope and harvests in eternity.

**Reminding in an Age That Resents It: Dhikrā with Dignity**

Modern culture often treats religious reminder as intrusion — "don't judge me," "keep it to yourself." Verse 21 answers with quiet confidence: reminding is not judging (judgment belongs to God, verses 25–26) but *caring made audible*. The analogy is the smoke alarm: its sound is unpleasant by design, for its purpose is not comfort but rescue. The believer reminds because silence in the face of a brother's drift toward the Fire would be betrayal, not tolerance. Yet the verse also restrains the zealous: you are *but* a reminder — not a spy, not a judge, not an enforcer of hearts. Remind once with wisdom; if rejected, remain kind; if accepted, remain humble; and never confuse the microphone with the verdict. The Prophet ﷺ reminded Abū Ṭālib on his deathbed with tears and was refused — yet his station lost nothing, for *innamā anta mudhakkir*. Success in daʿwah is measured at the tongue, not at the audience.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:22

> **thou art not a warder over them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lasta ʿAlayhim bi-Muṣayṭir — No Dominion Over Hearts**

*"Thou art not over them a muṣayṭir"* — not a warder, controller, dominator, or enforcer. The word, from *s-ṭ-r* (with the *sīn* pronounced as *ṣād* in the famous Ḥafṣ reading, and as *sīn* — *musayṭir* — in other canonical readings), denotes one who exercises mastery and compulsion over others. The verse strips the Prophet ﷺ — and by extension every religious authority after him — of any claim to coerce belief. The Qur'an repeats the principle in several forms:

> *"And you are not over them a compeller (jabbār); so remind by the Qur'an whoever fears My warning."* (Qur'an 50:45)

> *"And had your Lord willed, those on earth would have believed, all of them together. Would you then compel people until they become believers?"* (Qur'an 10:99)

> *"There is no compulsion in religion."* (Qur'an 2:256)

Faith, by its nature, cannot be forced: a coerced confession is hypocrisy, not belief, and God desires hearts, not hostages. Verse 22 is thus the Magna Carta of conscience in the Qur'an — revealed not in triumph but in persecution, when Muslims were the powerless party, proving the principle was conviction, not convenience.

**Why God Refuses Compelled Worship: The Logic of the Test**

The verse raises the profound question: why would God, who could have made all mankind believers by fiat, forbid even His Messenger from compelling them? The Qur'an's answer is the logic of the moral test: this world is an examination hall (*"that He may test you — which of you is best in deed,"* 67:2; 18:7), and examination requires freedom — the genuine possibility of failure. Compelled goodness is not goodness but programming; the angels already worship without choice, and God willed, in addition, creatures who *choose* Him. Ibn Taymiyyah developed the point: divine wisdom decreed that faith have means — evidence, reminding, inner disposition — but not compulsion, because love and willing submission are the very substance of worship (*ʿibādah*), and these die under force. The verse also reveals God's confidence in truth: falsehood needs enforcers, but truth needs only *reminders* (verse 21) — give it a fair hearing and it prevails by its own light. History vindicated the confidence: the unarmed reminding of Makkah conquered, within a century, hearts from Spain to China.

**The Grammar of What Follows: "Except" That Does Not Except**

Verse 22 ends with a grammatical famous point: the next verse begins *"illā man tawallā wa kafar"* — "except/besides whoever turns away and disbelieves." The classical grammarians, including al-Zamakhsharī, explain this as a *disconnected exception* (*istithnāʾ munqaṭiʿ*): the *illā* does not carve an exception from verse 22 (as though the Prophet *were* a warder over the apostates) but resumes with a new sentence — "But as for whoever turns away..." Al-Ṭabarī's reading confirms: your lack of authority covers all; *then*, separately, God states what awaits the defiant. The distinction matters pastorally: no human being — not a prophet, let alone a preacher, parent, or state — gains coercive rights over conscience from another's disbelief. The turncoat's account is with God (verses 23–26), not with the community's enforcers. Some later readers misused similar texts to justify compulsion; verse 22, properly parsed, closes that door: the *illā* assigns punishment to *God*, not permission to *men*.

**Authority Without Authoritarianism: Lessons for Every Leader**

Though revealed about prophethood, verse 22 disciplines every lesser authority: the ruler, the scholar, the teacher, the parent. Each has a God-given remit — the ruler to justice, the scholar to clarity, the parent to nurture — and each is tempted to exceed it into *sayṭarah*, domination of conscience. The verse draws the line: guide, teach, advise, remind, even discipline outward behavior within lawful bounds — but never claim ownership of another's heart. The Prophet ﷺ modeled the balance: he commanded armies and judged disputes (outward order), yet never punished private doubt or forced a confession of sincerity. Parents find here the charter of believing upbringing: instruct children, habituate them to worship, correct them with mercy — but recognize that their hearts open by God's hand, through supplication and example more than pressure. The teacher, the manager, the elder: all are *mudhakkirūn* (reminders), none are *muṣayṭirūn* (dominators). Leadership in Islam is stewardship of free souls, not ownership of them.

**Freedom as Divine Trust: The Dignity Verse 22 Confers**

Finally, verse 22 confers breathtaking dignity on every human being — including the unbeliever. In God's own court, the denier's conscience is declared inviolable to compulsion even by the noblest of men. If the Prophet ﷺ may not force a heart, no one may. This is the Qur'anic root of religious freedom: not modern indifference ("truth doesn't matter") but theocratic confidence ("truth compels no one, yet prevails"). The believer therefore defends the freedom of conscience even for those who use it wrongly — hating the disbelief while honoring the inviolability of the disbeliever's inner forum, leaving his account to verses 25–26. And the believer treasures his own freedom as a *trust*: the liberty God refused to His Messenger to seize must not be surrendered by its owner to lesser masters — to passions, to crowds, to appetites, to algorithms. Verse 22 emancipates the heart from every *muṣayṭir* except the One to whom it will return — and willing submission to Him alone is not bondage but the only true freedom.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:23

> **But whosoever turns away and disbelieves,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Illā Man Tawallā wa Kafar — The Resumption After the Limit**

*"But whoever turns away and disbelieves..."* The *illā*, as verse 22's commentary explained, is a disconnected exception — better rendered "But" or "As for": having fixed the Messenger's limits (reminder, not domination), God now states His own prerogative over the defiant. The sentence is left suspended, its consequence arriving in verse 24 — a rhetorical pause that lets the two verbs sink in. *Tawallā* — to turn away, to give one's back — describes the outward act: rejection of the reminder, walking off from the evidence. *Kafara* — to disbelieve, literally to *cover* — describes the inward act: covering the truth the heart recognized, burying the reminder under denial. The pairing is comprehensive: outer turning plus inner covering, the back turned and the eyes shut. The Qur'an joins the pair elsewhere as the definition of the lost:

> *"So he neither believed nor prayed, but denied and turned away."* (Qur'an 75:31–32)

Verse 23 thus profiles not the ignorant (who never heard) nor the struggling (who believe yet stumble) but the deliberate: those to whom the reminder came clearly — through the signs of verses 17–20 and the warner of verse 21 — and who answered with back and blindfold.

**Turning After Knowing: Why This Is the Decisive Sin**

The Qur'an grades rejection by the light rejected. The one who never received the message is tested by God in ways known to Him (*"We never punish until We send a messenger,"* 17:15); but the one who heard, understood, and turned — his is the *tawallī* of this verse. Pharaoh's magicians illustrate the opposite choice: shown one miracle, they fell prostrate and refused to recant under torture (20:70–73; 26:46–51). Pharaoh himself illustrates this verse: shown sign after sign, he turned and covered until the sea closed over confession too late (10:90–91). The early Makkan chiefs — Abū Jahl, Abū Lahab — knew the Prophet's truthfulness intimately (they had named him *al-Amīn*, the Trustworthy) yet turned for pride, interest, and tribal rivalry. Their *tawallī* was therefore not intellectual but volitional — a refusal of the will, not a failure of the mind. Ibn Kathīr stresses this pattern throughout his commentary: the Qur'an's harshest verdicts fall on *knowing* rejection, because it alone is pure injustice — truth recognized, then betrayed.

**The Anatomy of Turning Away: How Backs Are Turned Today**

*Tawallī* wears modern dress effortlessly. A man turns away by mockery — laughing off the reminder before it lands. By delay — "I'll practice later," until later never comes. By distraction — drowning the call in noise, work, and entertainment. By argument — demanding proofs he has no intention of accepting, moving goalposts with every answer. By tribalism — "my people don't believe that." Each is a way of giving the back while the ears still function. The Qur'an describes the physicality of it:

> *"And when Our verses are recited to him, he turns away arrogantly, as though he did not hear them, as though in his ears is deafness."* (Qur'an 31:7)

The psychology is self-protective: to face the reminder honestly would require change, and change is costly — so the soul turns its back and calls the posture wisdom. Verse 23 names the maneuver for what it is, stripping its disguises. The believer's self-examination follows: whenever he feels the impulse to dodge, delay, or deride a reminder — from Qur'an, from conscience, from a sincere friend — he should recognize the shadow of *tawallā* and turn *toward* instead. For the verse's door swings both ways: whoever turns *to* God after turning away finds Him *"Forgiving, Merciful"* (39:53) — until the deadlines of verse 2's commentary arrive.

**Disbelief as Covering: The Root That Accuses**

*Kufr*, from *k-f-r*, means to cover — the farmer is *kāfir* in classical Arabic because he covers the seed with soil. The disbeliever is thus, etymologically, one who covers: who buries the inborn knowledge of God (7:172), the evidence of the signs (verses 17–20), and the voice of conscience under layers of denial. The image implies the truth was *there*, like seed in soil — planted, real, capable of growth — and was deliberately hidden. This is why the Qur'an treats disbelief as injustice (*ẓulm*) before all: *"Indeed the disbelievers are the wrongdoers"* (2:254) — wrongdoers against truth, against their own souls, against the Giver of every blessing they enjoy while denying Him. Al-Rāzī observes that every blessing the denier uses — eyes to read his objections, lungs to voice them, a mind to frame them — testifies against his covering: he employs God's gifts to deny God's existence, like a man spending his father's fortune to prove he is an orphan. Verse 23 lets the two verbs stand as their own indictment; verse 24 will pronounce the sentence.

**The Suspended Sentence: Mercy in the Pause**

Notably, verse 23 names the crime but withholds the punishment for a verse — the sentence hangs, unfinished, across the verse-break. The classical rhetoricians cherished such suspensions: the pause lets hope breathe — the turner may yet turn back — while letting dread gather. It mirrors God's own way with sinners in this world: the punishment is *prepared* but not *hastened*:

> *"And were God to hasten evil for people as they hasten good, their term would already have been decreed for them. But We leave those who expect not the meeting with Us, in their rebellion, wandering blindly."* (Qur'an 10:11)

Every day the turner wakes unpunished is a day of respite (*imḥāl*), not acquittal — time granted for the back to turn forward again. The Prophet ﷺ expressed God's patience:

> *"None is more patient over harmful words heard than God: they attribute a son to Him, yet He grants them health and provision."* (al-Bukhārī 7378; Muslim 2804, from Abū Mūsā)

Verse 23's pause is that patience made audible: the crime stated, the gavel not yet fallen, the door of return still open — but the next verse warns that the gavel exists, and will fall.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:24

> **God shall punish him with the greatest punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fayuʿadhdhibuhu Allāhu — The Subject Is God Himself**

*"Then God shall punish him..."* The suspended sentence of verse 23 lands — and its subject is *Allāhu*, God Himself. Not the Prophet (who is but a reminder, verse 21), not the believers (who have no warder's rights, verse 22), but God — the sentence belongs to the only court with perfect knowledge and perfect right. The *fa* (then/so) marks consequence: turning and covering *produce* punishment the way seeds produce harvests. The grammar thus completes the sūrah's allocation of roles: to the Messenger, reminding; to men, freedom; to God, judgment. No human court may borrow this verse to punish apostasy or disbelief by force — the verse assigns the punishment explicitly to *God*, in the Hereafter context the whole sūrah inhabits (verses 1–16). The classical jurists who legislated on public treason and rebellion drew on entirely different texts and contexts; this verse's plain business is eschatological: it closes the denier's account with his Creator, not with his community.

**Al-ʿAdhāb al-Akbar — The Greatest Punishment**

*Al-ʿadhāb al-akbar* — "the greatest punishment." The Qur'an elsewhere contrasts its two degrees:

> *"And We shall surely make them taste the nearer punishment (al-ʿadhāb al-adnā) before the greater punishment (al-ʿadhāb al-akbar), that perhaps they may return."* (Qur'an 32:21)

The "nearer" punishment is worldly chastisement — calamities, defeats, anxieties — sent as alarm bells to wake the heedless *"that perhaps they may return."* The "greatest" is the Hereafter's: the Fire detailed in verses 1–7, now pronounced as sentence. Why "greatest"? Because it is complete — touching body and soul, outward and inward; because it is exact — proportioned to each deed with divine precision; and because it is final — unappealable, unending for those who die in deliberate denial. Ibn Kathīr, commenting here, connects the verse back to the sūrah's opening tableau: the humbled faces, the scorching Fire, the boiling spring, the starving thorn — all of it is *al-ʿadhāb al-akbar* unfolded. The sūrah thus ends its warning where it began it, enclosing the Garden passage like a threat that frames a promise — or a promise sheltered within a threat, depending on which side the reader stands.

**Is Eternal Punishment Just? The Question Verse 24 Forces**

The modern conscience often stumbles here: how can finite sins earn infinite punishment? The classical theologians answered on several levels, and the answers deserve a hearing. First, the gravity of an offense is measured not only by its duration but by its object: treason against a king outweighs trespass against a peer, though both take moments — and denial of the infinite Majestic One, sustained willfully until death, is the maximal offense against the maximal Object. Second, the punishment's endlessness mirrors the will's endlessness: the deliberate denier did not resolve to disbelieve for sixty years but *settled* in disbelief as his permanent posture — had he lived forever, he would have denied forever; eternity of punishment answers eternity of orientation. Third, God wrongs no one: the denier was warned (verses 1–7), promised (verses 8–16), shown evidence (verses 17–20), and reminded gently (verses 21–22) — the sentence falls only after every avenue of mercy was walked and refused. As God declares:

> *"And your Lord is never unjust to His servants."* (Qur'an 41:46)

The analogy of the pardoned criminal who tears up his pardon repeatedly, spits at the judge, and demands the cell, approaches the reality: Hell's eternity is, in the end, the denier's own choice, honored forever.

**Fear and Hope: The Balance This Verse Requires**

Verse 24 is a verse of fear (*khawf*), and the Sunnah prescribes its proper dosage: fear balanced with hope (*rajāʾ*), like two wings — the bird flies only with both. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"If the believer knew what is with God of punishment, none would hope for His Paradise; and if the disbeliever knew what is with God of mercy, none would despair of His Paradise."* (Muslim 2755, from Abū Hurayrah)

Fear without hope breeds despair — itself a form of *kufr* (*"none despairs of God's mercy except the disbelieving people,"* 12:87). Hope without fear breeds complacency — the delusion of Paradise without striving (4:123). Verse 24's place in the sūrah models the balance: seven verses of fear, then nine of hope, then evidence, then mission — fear first to shatter heedlessness, hope largest to heal the shattered. The believer reads verse 24 and trembles — then reads verses 8–16 and strives; trembles again — and repents; hopes — and works. The one who reads only threats becomes a despairing recluse; the one who reads only promises becomes a presumptuous idler; the one who reads the whole sūrah becomes a striving servant, suspended wholesomely between awe and longing.

**The Greatest Punishment and the Greater Mercy: Where the Verse Points**

Finally, verse 24 points beyond itself — to the mercy that surrounds it. The "greatest punishment" is pronounced in a Book whose every chapter but one opens *"In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful"*; in a sūrah whose Garden passage outruns its Fire passage; by a Lord who *"has decreed mercy upon Himself"* (6:12, 54) and whose mercy *"prevails over"* His wrath (al-Bukhārī 3194; Muslim 2751). The threat is therefore itself a mercy: a warning is a rescue attempted, a fence before a cliff, a cry to a sleepwalker. God gains nothing from punishment — *"What would God do with your punishment, if you are grateful and believe?"* (4:147) — and loses nothing by pardon. Verse 24 stands like a lighthouse on rocks: its light is harsh only to those steering toward the rocks; to those who turn the wheel, it is salvation. The wise reader does not debate the lighthouse's brightness but changes course — which is exactly what the sūrah's final two verses now make possible, by announcing where every course ends.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:25

> **Truly unto Us is their return,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Inna Ilaynā Iyābahum — The Certain Homecoming**

*Inna ilaynā iyābahum* — "Truly, to Us is their return." The verse opens with *inna* (truly/certainly) — emphasis against every denier's doubt — and centers on *iyāb*, a rare and weighty word for return, the homecoming to one's origin. The pronoun *ilaynā* (to Us) carries the divine majesty: not to nature, not to nothingness, not to an committee of peers — to *Us*, to God. Every road in existence terminates here: the believer's road and the denier's, the king's and the slave's, the long life and the infant's brief hour. The Qur'an states the universal itinerary:

> *"Everyone upon it shall perish, and there shall remain the Face of your Lord, Owner of Majesty and Honor."* (Qur'an 55:26–27)

> *"And to God is the homecoming (maṣīr)."* (Qur'an 2:285; 3:28; 5:18 — a refrain of the faith)

Death, in this telling, is not departure but *return* — the servant coming home to his Master, the traveler reaching his starting point, the loaned soul handed back to its Lender. The denier calls death annihilation; verse 25 corrects him: it is appointment.

**Return as Proof: The Argument Buried in a Promise**

Verse 25 is not merely a promise of reckoning but an argument for resurrection in miniature. Its logic: you came *from* God — created, formed, provisioned without your consent or contribution — therefore you shall go *back* to Him. Beginning implies return; the loan implies the Lender's claim. The Qur'an deploys this reasoning against the deniers of resurrection repeatedly:

> *"And He it is who originates creation, then repeats it — and that is easier for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27)

> *"Does man think We shall not assemble his bones? Yes indeed — We are able to restore his very fingertips."* (Qur'an 75:3–4)

The fingertips — an oath by the most intricate signature of identity, inviting every age (including the age of fingerprint science) to marvel. Al-Rāzī formalized the sūrah's cumulative case: the God who created camels, raised skies, fixed mountains, and spread earth (verses 17–20) can surely reassemble men; power proved in the seen guarantees power over the unseen. Verse 25 is therefore the hinge of the whole chapter: the Overwhelming (verse 1) is credible because the Creator is proven (verses 17–20), and the return is certain because the departure was His doing. To believe in creation and deny resurrection is to affirm the harder and deny the easier — a fracture of reason the verse quietly exposes.

**The Return No One Escapes: Comfort of the Oppressed, Terror of the Oppressor**

*Iyābahum* — *their* return — in context points first to the turners and deniers of verses 23–24: whatever they evade in this world — courts, consequences, exposure — they cannot evade the Return. The tyrant who dies unpunished, the fraudster who dies wealthy, the slanderer who dies praised — all have an appointment they cannot cancel:

> *"And think not that God is unaware of what the wrongdoers do; He only delays them to a Day when gazes shall stare in horror."* (Qur'an 14:42)

This is heaven's answer to the oldest cry of the oppressed: *where is justice?* — justice is *there*, at the Return, certain as sunrise. The Prophet ﷺ promised the settling of every account on that Day, *"even the hornless sheep's claim against the horned"* (Muslim 2582, from Abū Hurayrah), and warned:

> *"Whoever has wronged his brother in honor or anything, let him seek his pardon today, before there is no dinar and no dirham — when, if he has good deeds, they shall be taken from him according to his wrongdoing, and if he has none, sins shall be loaded upon him."* (al-Bukhārī 2449, from Abū Hurayrah)

Verse 25 is thus the oppressed believer's deepest consolation and the heedless oppressor's most terrible verse — the same sun melting wax and hardening clay, depending on the substance.

**Living Toward Return: The Worldview Verse 25 Builds**

To believe *"unto Us is their return"* is to adopt a complete philosophy of life. The return-believer evaluates every choice by its arrival: will this wealth testify for me or against me? This relationship, this career, this secret habit — how will it look *there*? The Qur'an calls this posture *taqwā* in its forward-looking sense — awareness of the meeting:

> *"And fear a Day when you shall be returned to God; then every soul shall be paid in full what it earned, and they shall not be wronged."* (Qur'an 2:281 — reported as among the very last verses revealed)

The denier of return lives, by contrast, in a universe without mornings-after: consequences end at death, so the clever maximize pleasure and minimize detection — the philosophy of every corruption, betrayal, and cruelty. Modern materialism is simply this ancient denial in new jargon, and its fruits — the same fruits the Makkan deniers bore — confirm the diagnosis. Verse 25 prescribes the cure: live as one returning. The traveler packs differently from the settler; the believer, certain of *iyāb*, packs repentance, good deeds, and reconciled relationships — luggage that survives the journey.

**"Unto Us" — The Intimacy Inside the Majesty**

Finally, ponder the preposition's tenderness: the return is *unto Us* — to God, not to a void, not to strangers. For the denier this is terror (return to the One he defied); but for the believer it is the tenderest promise in the sūrah — return to the One he loved unseen, served unseeing, and longed for in night prayers. The Qur'an elsewhere clothes the return in welcome:

> *"O tranquil soul, return to your Lord, pleased and pleasing. So enter among My servants, and enter My Paradise."* (Qur'an 89:27–30 — in the very next sūrah, as though al-Fajr answers al-Ghāshiyah's announcement with the welcome itself)

The Prophet ﷺ said God loves the meeting of whoever loves to meet Him (al-Bukhārī 6507; Muslim 2683, from ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit and Abū Hurayrah). Verse 25 therefore ends its threat with an embrace held in reserve: the *ilaynā* that sentences the defiant is the same *ilaynā* that welcomes the devoted. Every soul returns; the wise ensure the Return is a homecoming, not an arrest.

## Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah 88:26

> **then truly with Us lies their reckoning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Thumma Inna ʿAlaynā Ḥisābahum — The Final Word Belongs to God**

*Thumma inna ʿalaynā ḥisābahum* — "Then truly, upon Us is their reckoning." The sūrah's last verse falls like a seal. *Thumma* (then) orders the sequence: first the return (verse 25), *then* the reckoning — arrival, then audit. *ʿAlaynā* (upon Us) assigns the task exclusively to God: the accounting is His burden, His right, His prerogative — none shares it, none assists it, none appeals beyond it. *Ḥisāb* (reckoning) is the meticulous settling: every deed weighed, every intention examined, every blessing inventoried against every response. The Qur'an's refrain throughout confirms the monopoly:

> *"Then to Us is their return, then upon Us is their reckoning."* — echoed in *"Thine is only to proclaim, and upon Us is the reckoning"* (Qur'an 13:40)

> *"And their reckoning is only upon my Lord, if you but perceive."* (Qur'an 26:113 — Noah's answer to those who despised his lowly followers)

> *"And those who... they shall not be reckoned... "* — rather: *"You are not accountable for them in anything, nor are they accountable for you in anything"* (Qur'an 6:52; cf. 6:69)

The message to the Prophet ﷺ — grieving over his rejecters, pressured by their demands for immediate punishment — is consolation: *their audit is scheduled, and its Auditor never errs*. The message to the rejecters is the reverse: *your Auditor is not mocked, bribed, deceived, or delayed forever*.

**The Two Reckonings: Presentation and Interrogation**

The Sunnah distinguishes two experiences of *ḥisāb*, and the distinction turns this verse from terror into hope for believers. ʿĀʾishah reported that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever is called to account (nūqisha al-ḥisāb) shall perish."* She asked: "Does not God say, 'As for him who is given his book in his right hand, he shall be reckoned with an easy reckoning' (84:7–8)?" He said: *"That is but the presentation (al-ʿarḍ); whoever is interrogated in the reckoning shall perish."* (al-Bukhārī 4939; Muslim 2876)

The believer's reckoning is a *presentation* — deeds displayed, sins gently acknowledged, pardon pronounced. The Prophet ﷺ described its intimacy:

> *"God shall draw the believer near, place His veil over him, and say: 'Did you commit such-and-such sin?' He shall say: 'Yes, my Lord.' ... Then He shall say: 'I concealed them for you in the world, and I forgive them for you today.'"* (al-Bukhārī 2441; Muslim 2768, from Ibn ʿUmar)

This is *ḥisāb yasīr* — the easy reckoning: a private conversation ending in forgiveness, not a trial ending in sentence. The defiant denier's reckoning is the opposite: interrogation, exposure, and the verdict of verse 24. Same Auditor, same meticulousness — opposite outcomes, determined by the record each soul compiled. Verse 26 thus promises precision, not severity as such: the precision that terrifies the guilty is the same precision that vindicates the righteous down to the smallest deed — *"So whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it"* (99:7).

**Why the Reckoning Must Be God's Alone: Four Wisdoms**

The verse's exclusivity — *upon Us* — carries profound wisdoms. First, only God *knows* enough to reckon: He witnessed every secret (*"He knows the treachery of eyes and what breasts conceal,"* 40:19), weighed every circumstance (ignorance, compulsion, capacity), and recorded all (*"a Book that leaves nothing small or great uncounted,"* 18:49). Any human auditor would condemn unjustly for lack of evidence. Second, only God *owns* enough to reckon: every blessing inventoried — sight, time, wealth, health — was His loan; the audit of a loan belongs to its Lender. Third, only God is *just* enough: free of bias, fatigue, bribery, and error — *"He is swift in reckoning"* (2:202; 3:19) yet never hasty. Fourth, only God's reckoning *heals*: human trials, even fair ones, leave wounds — publicity, stigma, lingering doubt — while God's private pardon of the believer (*"I concealed... and I forgive"*) erases even the memory's sting. These four wisdoms also forbid the believer from playing auditor over others: to declare specific persons damned, to weigh hearts, to deny repentance — all usurp *ʿalaynā*. Judge outward acts where duty requires (justice, testimony, counsel); leave inward accounts to the verse's Assignee.

**The Sūrah's Perfect Circle: From "Has It Come to You?" to "Upon Us"**

Verse 26 closes a circle opened in verse 1. The sūrah began with a question — *"Has there come to you the news of the Overwhelming?"* — and ends with an answer: the Overwhelming ends at God's throne, with every account settled by Him. Between question and answer the whole religion passed in review: the Fire (1–7), the Garden (8–16), the signs (17–20), the messenger's limits (21–22), the sentence (23–24), the return (25), the reckoning (26). The architecture preaches: revelation starts by *announcing* the Day, fills the middle with *evidence and incentive*, and ends by *assigning* the Day to God. Nothing is left with men except the brief, bright duty of verse 21 — remind. The symmetry also answers the sūrah's first tension: the Overwhelming that "covers all" (verse 1) might seem indiscriminate — but verses 25–26 reveal its perfect discrimination: all are covered by the *event*, each is distinguished in the *reckoning*. Universality of summons, individuality of audit — the Day overwhelms collectively and judges particularly, like a net that catches all fish and a hand that sorts each one.

**Leaving the Sūrah: What the Reckoning Demands Today**

The sūrah's final demand is a daily practice: *reckon yourself before you are reckoned*. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb's famous counsel — *"Call yourselves to account before you are called to account, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed"* (reported by al-Tirmidhī in description of the wise; cited widely in the tradition) — is verse 26 converted into habit. Its forms are simple: a nightly review (what did I earn today?), a pre-dawn repentance (the Prophet ﷺ sought forgiveness over seventy times daily — al-Bukhārī 6307, from Abū Hurayrah), a weekly reconciliation (debts paid, apologies offered, grudges released before the Thursday–Monday presentation of deeds — Muslim 2565), and a life-audit at every milestone. The one who audits himself finds the Final Audit familiar — a meeting rehearsed, a Book already read, a Lord already loved. And so the sūrah that began by startling its hearer ends by settling him: the Overwhelming is coming, the signs are clear, the Messenger has reminded, the return is certain — and the Reckoning, in the end, lies with the Most Merciful of reckoners. Blessed is he who prepared for the meeting; and praise be to God, Lord of the worlds, who announced it in time.
