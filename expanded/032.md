# Sūrah al-Sajdah (Chapter 32) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Whose Very Name Commands the Body**

Sūrah al-Sajdah — "The Prostration" — is a Makkan sūrah of thirty verses that takes its most famous name from verse 15, which describes the true believers as those who, "when reminded of [God's] signs, fall prostrate and hymn the praise of their Lord." Few chapters of the Qur'an are named after an act of the body, and the choice is itself a teaching: this sūrah is about what the whole human being — flesh, posture, limbs, and heart — does when the truth arrives. Prostration (sujūd) is the one act in which the creature touches the ground and the forehead, the highest physical point of the human frame, is brought lowest; it is simultaneously an act of humility and the act by which, according to the Prophet's own words examined below, the servant draws closest to his Lord. To name an entire chapter after that posture is to announce from its title that the message within is not merely information to be filed in the mind but a summons to be answered by the whole self.

In the written order of the Qur'an it stands as Chapter 32, and in the traditional chronologies of revelation it is counted among the middle Makkan sūrahs, generally held to have been sent down after Sūrah al-Muʾminūn (23), when the Makkan opposition to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ had hardened into open mockery of the very ideas this sūrah defends — the reality of revelation, the resurrection of the body, and the meeting with God. The sūrah was also known to the early community by other names that preserve different angles on its content. It was called Sūrat al-Maḍājiʿ, "the Sūrah of the Beds," after the striking image in verse 16 of believers whose "sides shun [their] beds" in night prayer. Many reciters and scholars referred to it by its opening words, "Alif Lām Mīm, the Revelation," as a way of distinguishing it from the other sūrahs that begin with those three Arabic letters. Each name is a doorway: one leads to the prostration at the heart of worship, one to the night vigil that proves love, and one to the descent of the Book that this chapter announces on its first page.

**The Makkan Fabric and the Madinan Threads**

The overwhelming majority of the classical commentators classify al-Sajdah as Makkan revelation, and its preoccupations confirm the classification: it argues with the idolaters of Makkah over the fabrication charge, over resurrection, and over the timing of "victory"; it consoles the Prophet against their taunts; and it builds its case from creation, history, and the natural world rather than from legislation. Yet the tradition records a notable internal debate. Several major commentators — among them those abbreviated in the source as Āl (al-Ālūsī), IJ (Ibn al-Jawzī), and Q (al-Qurṭubī) — held that verses 16–20, with their warm description of the night-vigil believers and their stark contrast between the people of the Garden and the people of the Fire, belong to the Madinan period, when the community of believers had become an established congregation able to sustain night prayer and charity. Others restricted the exception to verses 18–20, and still others to verse 16 alone. Al-Ālūsī cautioned against the whole tendency, observing that the strength of the connection between these verses and those preceding them — the description of the believers in verses 15–17 flows directly out of the contrast with the deniers in verses 10–14 — makes it unlikely that they were revealed at a different time and place. His caution carries weight: the sūrah reads as a single, continuous argument, and the internal logic of its paragraphs argues for a single revelation.

**Prophetic Devotion to This Chapter**

The reverence in which the early community held al-Sajdah was anchored in the practice of the Prophet ﷺ himself, and the reports of that practice made the sūrah a fixture of Muslim nightly and weekly rhythm. It is recorded — from Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh — that the Prophet ﷺ would not sleep until he had recited the Sūrah of the Prostration and the Sūrah of the Kingdom (al-Mulk, 67) together:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ would not sleep until he recited 'Alif Lām Mīm Tanzīl' (al-Sajdah) and 'Blessed is He in Whose Hand is the Kingdom' (al-Mulk)."* (al-Tirmidhī; from Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh)

The pairing is instructive. Al-Mulk ends with the believer committing his affair to the One "in Whose Hand is the Kingdom," and al-Sajdah ends with the command to wait upon the divine victory; to sleep with both is to surrender the night — the little death of sleep — to the Lord of the waking and the sleeping. It is likewise recorded, from Abū Hurayrah, that the Prophet ﷺ used to recite Sūrat al-Sajdah and Sūrat al-Insān (76) in the dawn prayer of Friday, the day on which Muslims gather for the congregational prayer and listen to the sermon:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ used to recite in the dawn prayer of Friday 'Alif Lām Mīm Tanzīl' (al-Sajdah) and 'Has there come upon man...' (al-Insān)."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah)

The classical commentators drew the lesson that the sūrah's content — creation, resurrection, the prostration of the believers, and the description of the Gardens prepared for them — is precisely the spiritual nourishment a community needs as it opens its week of gathering, trade, and witness: a weekly re-grounding in where the world came from, where it is going, and what posture becomes a creature in between.

**One Verse That Bends the Whole Chapter into Worship**

Verse 15 is one of the fifteen verses of the Qur'an traditionally counted as verses of prostration (āyāt al-sajdah), after which the reciter — and the listener who hears them recited — prostrates in the recitation prostration (sajdat al-tilāwah). This single liturgical fact explains much of the sūrah's fame. The practice goes back to the Prophet himself, who prostrated when reciting passages of prostration and taught his Companions to do the same, and the sūrah's fifteenth verse has therefore been heard in mosques and homes for fourteen centuries not only as a sentence but as a physical event: the moment in which reading stops and the forehead touches the ground. The tradition attaches to this act a remarkable spiritual economy. When the reciter prostrates at a verse of prostration, Satan — who was commanded to prostrate before Adam and refused — is said to withdraw weeping, for the creature who prostrates gains by humility what the rebel lost by pride. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said:

> *"When the son of Adam recites a verse of prostration and prostrates, Satan withdraws weeping and says: 'Woe to me! The son of Adam was commanded to prostrate and he prostrated, so for him is Paradise; and I was commanded to prostrate and I refused, so for me is the Fire.'"* (Muslim 81, from Abū Hurayrah)

And in the most quoted of his sayings on the posture itself:

> *"The closest that a servant can be to his Lord is when he is prostrating."* (Muslim 482, from Abū Hurayrah)

The chapter that bears the prostration's name thus enacts, in its own recitation, the very response it describes: the believer who recites verse 15 does not merely read about those who fall prostrate; he joins them.

**The Architecture of Thirty Verses**

Like many Makkan sūrahs, al-Sajdah moves in a deliberate arc from the metaphysical to the moral to the historical to the practical. The first half establishes the ground: revelation and its denial (vv. 1–3); God's sovereignty over creation, His direction of every affair, and His all-embracing knowledge (vv. 4–6); the genesis of the human being from clay and fluid into a breathing, hearing, seeing creature (vv. 7–9); and the resurrection and the meeting with the Lord that the deniers reject, with the Angel of Death and the scene of the guilty bowing their heads (vv. 10–12). The middle of the sūrah then turns to the human response: the divine decree that some will fill Hell while others are guided (v. 13), the terrible sentence of being "forgotten" by God upon those who forgot His Day (v. 14), and then — in the verses that give the chapter its name — the portrait of the believers who prostrate, pray in the night, spend in charity, and await a reward that "no soul knows" (vv. 15–17). A second panel of contrast follows: the believer and the iniquitous are not equal; the Gardens of refuge await the one, and the Fire that they used to deny awaits the other (vv. 18–20), with the "lesser punishment" of this world sent as a mercy to wake the heedless before the greater one arrives (vv. 21–22). The sūrah then summons the evidence of history and nature: Moses and the Book given to the Children of Israel (vv. 23–25); the destroyed generations in whose ruined dwellings the Arabs walked (v. 26); the rain that revives the dead earth and feeds men and cattle (v. 27). It closes on the battlefield of the word: the mockers demand a date for the "victory," and the answer is that on the Day of Victory faith will no longer benefit those who disbelieved, and that the Prophet need only turn away and wait — while they, too, are waiting (vv. 28–30).

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the thirty verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order, exactly as the working method of this project requires. Every verse is quoted individually, then expanded through Arabic roots and morphology, the classical commentators, the authenticated reports of the Prophet with their sources identified, the logic of the verse's argument, and its bearing on modern life. Where the source commentary groups verses together — as with verses 18–19 — they are here separated and given full individual treatment, as the workflow mandates. Nothing has been compressed because the chapter is short: a sūrah that measures the value of a single prostration deserves a reading that prostrates, as it were, at every verse, unhurried and complete, from Alif. Lām. Mīm. to "they, too, are waiting."

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:1

> **Alif. Lām. Mīm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Letters That Arrive with Their Own History**

*Alif. Lām. Mīm.* — the same three isolated letters of the Arabic alphabet that stand at the head of six sūrahs of the Qur'an: al-Baqarah (2), Āl ʿImrān (3), al-ʿAnkabūt (29), al-Rūm (30), Luqmān (31), and this one, al-Sajdah. The reader who has traveled through the Qur'an in its written order meets them here for the sixth time, and the repetition is not accidental. In each of these six sūrahs the letters are followed, almost immediately, by a declaration about the Book: in al-Baqarah by "This is the Book in which there is no doubt" (2:2); in Āl ʿImrān by "God, there is no god but He, the Living, the Self-Subsisting, Who has sent down the Book upon thee in truth" (3:1–3); in the three middle-Makkan sūrahs by affirmations that the revelation is from God; and here by the sūrah's own opening sentence, "The revelation of the Book in which there is no doubt, from the Lord of the worlds" (32:2). The letters thus function as a herald's call before the announcement of a royal decree: they stop the ear, they name the raw material of the language in which the decree is written, and they signal that what follows is speech of an order that ordinary speech cannot produce.

The classical commentators classified these letters among the *al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*, the "disconnected" or "separated" letters, whose full meaning most of them regarded as ultimately known only to God. Twenty-nine sūrahs begin with such letter-combinations, in patterns ranging from a single letter (Ṣād, Qāf, Nūn) to as many as five (as at the head of Sūrah 42). Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and the mainstream of the exegetical tradition held that the believer should affirm them as part of the Book, refrain from insisting on any single human interpretation of their secret, and leave their final import with God — a position that is itself a discipline, as the commentary on 2:1 observed: a text that opens with an acknowledged mystery teaches the reader, before a single command arrives, that he stands before a Speaker whose knowledge exceeds his own.

**The Chain of Descent Hidden in Three Letters**

The most enduring of the reverent readings given to A.L.M. at the head of this particular sūrah is the allusive interpretation preserved in the source commentary from al-Maybudī, the great Persian commentator of the eleventh century, who read the three letters as a compressed biography of revelation itself:

> "Alif alludes to God; lām alludes to Gabriel; and mīm alludes to Muhammad. He is saying, 'It is by My Divinity, by Gabriel's holiness, and by thy splendor, O Muhammad, that this revelation, which is the Quran that We promised unto thee, would be thy level of prophethood and thy ruling power.'"

The logic of the allusion is precise. Every verse of the Qur'an that reached the believers traveled exactly this chain: from God, through the angel Gabriel, to Muhammad ﷺ — divinity originating, holiness conveying, splendor receiving. Variants of this reading circulated early, some of them attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, in which the three letters were said to encode the divine names behind the same chain. Whether one adopts the allusion as a literal meaning or receives it as what the commentators call *ishārāt* — spiritual pointers that accompany, without replacing, the literal sense — it fits this sūrah with special exactness, because al-Sajdah opens by asserting the reality of revelation (v. 2) and then spends its first half establishing who the Revealer is, how He created, and how He will raise the dead. The three letters at the door are, on this reading, the signature of the whole chain of transmission before the message itself begins.

**An Alphabet as a Challenge and a Promise**

The second great current of commentary, developed most fully by al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and those who followed the hint of Ibn ʿAbbās, reads the disconnected letters as a standing challenge to the Qur'an's eloquent opponents. The letters are not exotic symbols; they are the everyday building blocks of Arabic, the very sounds out of which the poets of Makkah composed their celebrated odes and the soothsayers their rhymed oracles. By placing these letters at the head of sūrah after sūrah, the revelation in effect says: here is the complete inventory of your own language — the twenty-eight letters from which your finest speech is made — and yet from them you cannot compose the like of this Book. The challenge is issued explicitly elsewhere in the very sūrahs that open with these letters: "And if you are in doubt concerning what We have sent down upon Our servant, bring a sūrah like it" (2:23). A.L.M. is thus the alphabet thrown down as a gauntlet, and the unanswerability of the challenge across fourteen centuries is part of the Qur'an's cumulative evidence for its own divine origin. The same letters carry a promise as well as a challenge, for the Prophet ﷺ taught that the letters of the Qur'an are rewarded letter by letter:

> *"Whoever recites a letter of the Book of God, for him is a good deed, and a good deed is multiplied tenfold. I do not say that Alif Lām Mīm is one letter, but Alif is a letter, Lām is a letter, and Mīm is a letter."* (al-Tirmidhī 2910; from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd)

On this report, the very letters whose secret is withheld from human understanding are nonetheless counted, one by one, as the believer's credit — a reminder that the Qur'an rewards attentive recitation even where comprehension has not yet arrived, and that the child or the new Muslim who struggles through *Alif. Lām. Mīm.* without knowing its depth is already being paid by the letter.

**The Pause That Recalibrates the Listener**

Set at the head of a sūrah whose argument moves from creation to the resurrection to the prostration of the believers, the three letters perform one further, subtler service: they force a pause. The ear that expects a sentence receives sounds; the mind that expects information receives mystery; the body that was moving through the day is brought to stillness before the first proposition arrives. This is the pedagogical function of the threshold. Just as the traveller must stop at the gate before entering the presence of a king, the reciter of al-Sajdah is stopped at the gate of the sūrah — told, in effect, that what follows is not ordinary discourse to be skimmed but a decree to be received. In an age of constant, frictionless information flow, in which the average attention is pulled in a hundred directions before breakfast, the three letters stand as an ancient technology of attention: three sounds, deliberately unreadable, that ask the listener to arrive before being addressed. The sūrah that begins with these letters will end with the command to wait; between the pause of the letters and the patience of the ending, the whole of the chapter is framed as a conversation that rewards the unhurried.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:2

> **The revelation of the Book in which there is no doubt, from the Lord of the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Book That Descends**

The sūrah's first full sentence after the letters announces its subject with a verbal noun: *tanzīl*, "the revelation" or, literally, "the sending down." The word is built from the Arabic root *n-z-l*, to descend or to alight, in a verbal form that carries the sense of a repeated or graduated descent. The Qur'an does not say "this Book is" or "the Book came"; it says, in effect, "the descent of the Book" — as if the act of descending were itself part of the message. This is the first of five sūrahs — al-Sajdah, al-Zumar (39:1), Ghāfir (40:2), al-Jāthiyah (45:2), and al-Aḥqāf (46:2) — to open with the same phrase, *tanzīl al-kitāb*, and in each case the phrase is immediately joined to the attributes of the One from Whom the descent originates. The commentators drew out the Qur'an's own picture of a many-staged descent. The Qur'an speaks of being sent down on the Night of Power (97:1; 44:3) and in the month of Ramadan (2:185), and also of being revealed gradually, "in successive descents," over the twenty-three years of the prophetic mission (17:106; 25:32). The classical commentators reconciled these by describing a descent in levels: the Qur'an as God's eternal speech; its inscription in the Preserved Tablet; its descent as a whole to the lowest heaven on the Night of Power; and from there its piece-by-piece transmission to the heart of Muhammad ﷺ through the angel Gabriel across two decades. Al-Maybudī's contemporary, the commentator cited in the source as MṢ, expressed the principle in a sentence that has become classic:

> "Although the Quran is a single reality, it has many levels of descent and is called by various names corresponding to each particular level of descent."

The image of descent is not incidental. For something to descend, there must be a height from which it comes and a lowliness to which it is brought — and the Qur'an's own chosen metaphor for revelation is that of the sublime condescending to reach the lowly, of the divine word adapting itself to the narrow vessel of a human heart and a human language. The word *tanzīl* therefore contains, in its grammar alone, a whole theology of God's care: the Lord of the worlds did not remain at a distance; He sent down.

**A Doubt Answered Before It Is Raised**

*Lā rayba fīhi* — "in which there is no doubt" — is a categorical negation placed, grammatically, in a position that makes the whole sentence a verdict rather than a hope. The verse does not say "in which there is little doubt" or "in which one should try not to doubt"; it declares that doubt has no foothold there, as one might say of a well-built bridge that there is no weakness in it, or of a mathematical proof that there is no flaw in it. The commentators noted that the phrase may attach either to the Book itself — that is, its contents are free of error and contradiction — or to the revelation of the Book, that is, there is no doubt concerning its divine provenance, that it truly descended from God and was not invented by Muhammad ﷺ (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Zamakhsharī). Both readings are sound, and together they cover the two questions any scripture must answer: Is what it says true? And does it really come from God? To the first, the Qur'an points to its own internal consistency and incorruptibility: "Falsehood comes not upon it from before it or from behind it; it is a revelation from the Wise, the Praised" (41:42), and "Had it been from other than God, they would surely have found in it much discrepancy" (4:82). To the second, it points to the chain of descent announced in this very verse — from the Lord of the worlds — and to the standing challenge to produce anything like it (2:23; 10:38; 17:88).

Why does the Qur'an negate doubt so absolutely, when doubt is a normal feature of human inquiry? Because, the commentators answer, this Book is not offered as one hypothesis among others; it is offered as the speech of the One Who created the doubter. A doubt about the Book is, in the end, a doubt about whether the Creator has spoken — and the verse's business is to show that the evidences are so arranged that honest inquiry terminates in certainty, not in perpetual suspense. Al-Ṭabarī noted that the verse serves both parties: for the believer it removes hesitation, and for the denier it removes the excuse of ambiguity, since the Book's clarity and coherence are themselves among its proofs. The psychological point is important for the modern reader, who has been taught to identify intellectual maturity with permanent skepticism. The Qur'an does not despise questioning; it despises the closed mind that questions forever without ever following the evidence to its conclusion. The verse's "no doubt" is thus less a prohibition on asking than an announcement that the asking, when honestly conducted, has an end — and this Book is that end.

**From the Lord of the Worlds: The Pedigree That Ends the Argument**

The sentence closes with its most important words: *min rabbi al-ʿālamīn*, "from the Lord of the worlds." The force of the phrase is genealogical: the Book is being traced to its Author, and the Author is identified by His universal Lordship. He is not the god of a tribe or a temple or a century, but *rabb al-ʿālamīn* — the Lord of all the worlds, a phrase whose plural (worlds: the world of the seen and the unseen, the world of angels and of men and of jinn, the world of this life and of the next) leaves nothing outside His domain. The same identification recurs in the Qur'an wherever the origin of revelation is asserted: "Truly it is a revelation from the Lord of the worlds, brought down by the Trustworthy Spirit upon thy heart" (26:192–194), and again, "Truly it is the Word of a noble messenger" (81:19). The logic of the identification is simple and powerful: the One Who created and sustains every world is the One most worthy to be heard about every world; the Lord Who caused the rain to fall and the earth to bring forth is the Lord Who sends down speech. To say "from the Lord of the worlds" is therefore to close the circle of evidence: the same hand that made the heavens visible in the daytime sky has made this Book audible in the daytime recitation. For the believer, the phrase is also a daily consolation: the Book he holds is not the word of a mortal genius to be weighed against other mortal words, but a communication from the only Authority that cannot err, cannot deceive, and cannot be overruled.

**Revelation in Stages: Why the Descent Was Gradual**

The word *tanzīl*, with its built-in sense of repeated descent, points to one of the most distinctive features of the Qur'anic revelation: it did not arrive as a single volume but as a gradual unfolding over twenty-three years, verse by verse, in response to events, questions, and the maturing needs of the first community. The Qur'an itself answers the objection that this gradualness provoked: "Those who disbelieve say, 'Why was the Qur'an not revealed unto him all at once?' Thus, that We may strengthen thy heart by it; and We have chanted it in a distinct chanting" (25:32). The commentators drew out the wisdom: legislation that touches marriage, finance, warfare, and worship cannot be absorbed in a single dose; the human heart is strengthened by a companionship that lasts decades rather than an event that lasts an hour; and each new revelation arrived, as it were, exactly on time, addressing the community's actual situation — a quality that allowed the Qur'an to educate rather than merely inform. The same gradualism appears in the natural world this sūrah will invoke: the rain does not fall as a single flood but in seasons; the child does not become an adult in a day. *Tanzīl* is the grammar of mercy: the sublime word measured itself out, drop by drop, for the sake of the vessels that had to receive it.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:3

> **Or do they say, "He has fabricated it"? Nay, it is the truth from thy Lord, that thou mayest warn a people to whom no warner has come before thee, that haply they may be guided.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charge of Fabrication — and the Grammar of the Challenge**

With the revelation announced in verse 2, the sūrah now turns to face the audience of Makkah and the accusation circulating among them: *am yaqūlūn iftarāhu* — "Or do they say, 'He has fabricated it'?" The particle *am* gives the sentence its edge. It can be read as an interrogative — "Or do they say..." — as though God were asking, after the evidence of verse 2, which of the two positions they will take: the Book is from the Lord of the worlds, or Muhammad made it up? There is no third possibility, and the verse forces the choice. Several commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī, preferred to read *am* as a particle of transition, in which case the verse continues the previous thought: "Nay, but they say, 'He has fabricated it'" — that is, despite the clarity of the Book's origin, the disbelievers' only recourse is the charge of forgery. The verb *iftarā* comes from the root *f-r-y*, whose basic sense is to cut or shape something, and whose derived sense is to invent a lie and dress it in the clothing of truth — a fabrication, in the full English sense of the word: something stitched together to pass as authentic. The Qur'an records the same charge being leveled at earlier prophets — "Likewise, no messenger came unto those before them but they said, 'A sorcerer, or one possessed'" (51:52) — and it records the specific form the charge took in Makkah: that the Prophet had invented the Qur'an himself, or had been taught it by others. To the first form the Qur'an answers with the challenge to produce its like: "Or do they say, 'He has fabricated it'? Say: 'Then bring a sūrah like it, and call upon whom you can apart from God, if you are truthful'" (10:38; cf. 11:13; 2:23). To the second it answers with an argument from the Prophet's own biography: "Say: 'Had God willed, I would not have recited it unto you, nor would He have made it known unto you. I tarried among you a lifetime before it. Do you not understand?'" (10:16) — the man they had known for forty years as al-Amīn, the trustworthy, who had never composed a line of such speech in his life, was suddenly to be believed capable of the finest discourse in the language, dictated at need over twenty-three years, without a single contradiction? The charge collapses under its own weight, and the verse's answer is correspondingly brief and absolute.

**"Nay, It Is the Truth from Thy Lord"**

The rebuttal has three parts, each answering a different dimension of the accusation. First, *bal huwa al-ḥaqq* — "Nay, it is the truth" — the Book is not an invention but reality itself, corresponding to the way things are: true about God, true about the human being, true about the end of history. Second, *min rabbika* — "from thy Lord" — it is not the product of Muhammad's imagination but a communication addressed to him; the verse speaks to the Prophet directly, in the second person, as the one who knows better than anyone that he did not write it — he received it. Third, the verse immediately specifies what the truth is for: *li-tundhira qawman* — "that thou mayest warn a people." The purpose clause is crucial. A fabrication serves its inventor — his fame, his following, his interests. This Book, by contrast, is described as serving the people it addresses: it comes as a warning for their own sake, and its only "interest" is their guidance. The classical commentators noted that the very content of the Qur'an testifies against the charge: it commands its own messenger to be patient under abuse, it records the mockery of his enemies without softening it, and it rebukes him when he errs in judgment (80:1–12) — a fabricator does not compose scripture that admonishes its own author. The verse's *bal* — "nay, rather" — is thus the pivot of the whole argument: not fabrication, but truth; not from the self, but from the Lord; not for the speaker's gain, but for the hearers' good.

**A People to Whom No Warner Had Come**

The phrase *qawman mā atāhum min nadhīrin min qablik* — "a people to whom no warner has come before thee" — describes the Arabs of the Prophet's generation, and it describes them with historical precision. Between the time of Jesus and the mission of Muhammad ﷺ, a period the Qur'an itself calls *fatrah*, an "interval" or "interruption" between messengers, no prophet had been sent to the world at large, and the Arabs in particular had received no scripture and no prophet of their own, though earlier Arabian peoples — ʿĀd with their prophet Hūd, and Thamūd with their prophet Ṣāliḥ — had been warned in ages past. The Qur'an states the fact of the interval explicitly in the sūrah that makes the same point about the Arabs: "We have not given them any books that they study; nor have We sent them a warner before thee" (34:44), and again, "that thou mayest warn a people whose fathers were not warned; so they were heedless" (36:6). Some commentators read the present verse in the second sense preserved in the source: the people of Makkah had been preceded by warners in the person of their own forefathers' prophets — Hūd and Ṣāliḥ were sent to peoples of the same Arabian world — but those warnings had been forgotten across the centuries, so that the present generation stood, in effect, unwarned. On either reading, the verse establishes the historical necessity of the Prophet's mission: he arrived at the end of a long silence, and his arrival was the breaking of that silence.

**The Logic of Mercy and Justice Behind the Sending**

Why does the Qur'an make a point of the fact that no warner had come before Muhammad ﷺ? Because the fact is doing theological work on two fronts at once. On the front of justice, it establishes that no people is punished without warning: "We do not punish until We have sent a messenger" (17:15), and "messengers bearing glad tidings and warning, so that mankind might have no argument against God after the messengers" (4:165). The Makkan Arabs could not say, on the Day of Resurrection, "no bearer of glad tidings and no warner came to us" — for the Qur'an itself closes that door: "O People of the Book! Our Messenger has come unto you, making things clear unto you, after an interval between the messengers, lest you should say, 'There came not unto us any bearer of glad tidings or warner.' Now there has come unto you a bearer of glad tidings and a warner" (5:19). On the front of mercy, the same fact reveals the character of God: a Lord Who does not leave His creatures without guidance, Who breaks the long silence precisely because the silence has grown long, is a Lord Whose mercy outruns His wrath. The classical commentators added a further layer of meaning: the Arabs were the descendants of Ishmael, and their very existence as a people answered the prayer of Abraham, who had asked God to "raise up among them a messenger from among themselves, reciting unto them Thy signs, and teaching them the Book and Wisdom" (2:129). The "people to whom no warner had come" were, on this reading, the people for whom a warner had been requested centuries in advance — and the request was now being answered.

**"That Haply They May Be Guided": The Aim of the Warning**

The verse ends with its purpose stated in the gentlest of grammars: *laʿallahum yahtadūn* — "that haply they may be guided." The particle *laʿalla* expresses hope and expectation rather than compulsion: the warning is sent so that they might find the road, not so that they be forced onto it. The commentators drew from this the whole shape of prophetic method: the warner announces, explains, and leaves the choice to the warned. Guidance (*hidāyah*) in the Qur'an is not a fate imposed on the unwilling but a light offered to the seeking; the same chapter that speaks of those who will not believe (v. 13) speaks here of the purpose of the warning as their guidance. The phrase also defines the emotional register of the true warner: he does not warn with the satisfaction of the doom-sayer but with the hope of the physician who warns of illness because he hopes for health. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, "The likeness of what God has sent me with of guidance and knowledge is like abundant rain falling upon the earth" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī) — rain that revives some ground, pools on other ground, and runs off from still other ground entirely. This verse names the same reality from the side of the sender: the rain is sent hoping to revive; whether the ground accepts is the ground's own affair, and verse 13 of this very sūrah will take up that side of the question.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:4

> **God it is Who created the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them in six days. Then He mounted the Throne. Apart from Him you have neither protector, nor intercessor. Will you not, then, remember?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation in Six Days: The Grammar of Sequence, Not of Need**

Having established the origin of the Book, the sūrah now establishes the origin of everything else: *Allāhu alladhī khalaqa al-samāwāti wa al-arḍa wa mā baynahumā fī sittati ayyām* — "God it is Who created the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them in six days." The sentence opens with the divine name in a fronted, emphatic position — God it is Who created — as if to say: whatever else you may attribute creation to, it is God alone; and the scope of the creative act is total: the heavens, the earth, and everything between them, which is to say the entire universe of things, seen and unseen. The six days have exercised the commentators in every generation. The plain sense is that the world was brought into being across six periods; the Qur'an repeats the number in six places (7:54; 10:3; 11:7; 25:59; 50:38; 57:4), always in the same context of divine sovereignty. Why six days, when the Qur'an also says of God's command, "When He wills a thing, He only says to it 'Be!' and it is" (36:82)? Because, the commentators answer, the six days are not a measure of God's labor but a revelation of His method. God could have created everything in an instant; He chose instead to create in ordered stages, so that the world would bear within its very structure the signature of sequence, development, and wisdom — and so that the human being, who is commanded to do things "in six days" of work followed by rest in the rhythm of his week, would find his own working week answering to the original creative week. The creation of the heavens and the earth "in six days" is, in this sense, the first lesson in the value of the patient, ordered, unhurried accomplishment of great tasks: the universe itself was built stage by stage.

**Then He Mounted the Throne: Sovereignty Without Analogy**

The verse continues with one of the most discussed sentences in the entire Qur'an: *thumma istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh* — "Then He mounted the Throne." The verb *istawā* and the throne (*al-ʿarsh*) together form a divine self-description that the Qur'an repeats in seven places, and the classical community of scholars — from the earliest generations through Ibn Taymīyah and the great traditionalist commentators — insisted on receiving it with two disciplines at once: affirming the reality of what God has affirmed, without either emptying the words of meaning (*taʿṭīl*) or likening God to His creatures (*tamthīl*). God "mounts the Throne" in a manner that suits His majesty, of which no creature can form an image: "There is nothing like unto Him, and He is the Hearer, the Seer" (42:11). The Throne itself is described in the Qur'an and the authentic reports as the greatest of God's creations — the ceiling of the created universe, over which the angels bear the Glory of their Lord (40:7; 69:17) — and the mounting of the Throne signifies, in the words of the commentators cited in the source, the establishment of God's absolute sovereignty over the creation He has just made (al-Rāzī). It is the declaration, in the grammar of a royal image, that the universe has a single Sovereign and that His rule is total: the One Who created the heavens and the earth then mounted the Throne of the heavens and the earth, and there is no corner of existence outside His dominion. The verse that follows in this sūrah — "He directs the affair from Heaven unto earth" (32:5) — spells out what the mounting means in operation: the Throne is not a resting place but a seat of government, from which the affairs of all worlds are administered.

Some commentators, noting that *thumma* ("then") ordinarily implies sequence, understood the verse to mean that the mounting of the Throne followed the six days of creation, and several classical authorities — among them those cited in the source — spoke of the Throne being mounted after the creation was complete. Others, including al-Qurṭubī, observed that *thumma* can carry the sense of mere conjunction — "and" — in which case the verse simply coordinates the two facts of creation and sovereignty without assigning the Throne a date. What matters for the believer's understanding is not the chronology but the theology: creation is not a finished machine left to run itself. The God Who created is the God Who governs, and the Throne is the emblem of that continuous government.

**Neither Protector nor Intercessor Apart from Him**

The verse then turns directly upon its listeners: *mā lakum min dūnihi min waliyyin wa lā shafīʿ* — "Apart from Him you have neither protector, nor intercessor." The sentence is a negative with the force of a verdict: outside God, there is no *walī* — no protector, no ally, no one whose guardianship can shield you — and no *shafīʿ* — no intercessor, no advocate who can plead your case before Him. The double negative closes every door except the door of God Himself. This was, in the Makkan context, a direct assault on the religious economy of the idolaters, who kept their idols precisely as intermediaries: "And they worship, apart from God, that which neither harms them nor benefits them, and they say, 'These are our intercessors with God'" (10:18), and "those who take protectors apart from Him — 'We do not worship them save that they bring us nigh unto God' — truly God will judge between them concerning that wherein they differ" (39:3). The Qur'an's answer is not that intercession is impossible but that it is God's exclusive prerogative to grant: "There is no intercessor, save by His Leave" (10:3), and again, "Who is it that can intercede with Him, save by His Leave?" (2:255). The intercession that the believers hope for on the Day of Resurrection — the intercession of the Prophet ﷺ for his community — is real, but it is real precisely because it is authorized: the Prophet himself will say, in the authentic report of the great intercession, "My intercession is for those of my community who committed major sins" (al-Tirmidhī and Abū Dāwūd), and he will prostrate before his Lord and be given permission to intercede — the permission belonging to God alone. The verse thus purifies hope as well as worship: the believer may hope for every mercy, but he hopes from God, not from any power alongside God. The psychology of this teaching is liberating. The human heart naturally seeks protectors — patrons, systems, gurus, institutions — and naturally fears being unprotected. The verse announces that the search for protection outside God is a search in an empty room, and that the only real protection is the one that was never absent. Security, in the Qur'an's architecture, is not found by accumulating guardians but by recognizing the single Guardian.

**Will You Not Remember? The Covenant That Predates the Creation**

The verse closes with its sting: *afalā tadhakkarūn* — "Will you not, then, remember?" The verb *tadhakkarūn* is from the root *dh-k-r*, to remember, to mention, to take heed, and its form here carries the sense of actively drawing a lesson into consciousness. The commentators offered two complementary readings of what precisely is to be remembered. The first, preserved from the great Qur'anic scholar al-Khaṭīb and others, sees the remembrance as reaching back to the primordial covenant in which God brought forth from the loins of the children of Adam their descendants and made them bear witness against themselves: "'Am I not your Lord?' They said, 'Yea, we bear witness'" (7:172). On this reading, every human being carries within himself a memory — however buried — that he has already acknowledged his Lord, and the spectacle of creation is a summons to wake that buried testimony. The second reading takes the remembrance more simply: the heavens, the earth, the ordered days, and the Throne are all signs addressed to the intelligence, and the failure to remember is not a failure of information but a failure of attention — the sign is everywhere and the conclusion is nowhere. Both readings converge on the same practical point: the purpose of creation, from the human side, is that the creature should remember the Creator. The universe is not a puzzle to be solved for its own sake but a reminder to be received; the sciences that decipher the heavens are, in the Qur'an's own terms, forms of reading a book whose Author is known. "Will you not, then, remember?" is thus the question beneath every other question the sūrah will ask, and the modern human being — who knows incomparably more than the Makkan Arabs about the scale of the heavens and the intricacy of the earth, yet has never been farther from the sense of standing before their Maker — is addressed by it with undiminished force.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:5

> **He directs the affair from Heaven unto earth; then it ascends unto Him in a day whose measure is as a thousand years of that which you reckon.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Affair That Travels Down and Up**

The mounting of the Throne in verse 4 is now translated into action: *yudabbiru al-amr* — "He directs the affair." The verb *yudabbiru* comes from the root *d-b-r*, whose primary senses include the back of a thing and, derivatively, the management of a thing from its beginning to its completion — to see an affair through to its end, to administer, to govern. The noun *al-amr*, "the affair" or "the command," is used in the Qur'an both for the creative command of God and for the totality of matters He administers. The verse pictures the divine government as a constant circulation: the affair descends from Heaven unto earth — the decree, the provision, the guidance, the command reaching every corner of the lower world — and then it ascends unto Him, the results, the deeds, the angels bearing their charges, returning to their Source. The Qur'an elsewhere fills in the two directions of this traffic. The descent is described in the Sūrah of Power: "The angels and the Spirit descend therein, by the leave of their Lord, with every command" (97:4), and in the sūrah of the angels: "He casts the Spirit of His command upon whomsoever He wills among His servants, that he may warn that there is no god but Me" (16:2). The ascent is described in the verse of pure speech: "Unto Him ascends good speech, and the righteous deed He exalts" (35:10). The commentators preserved in the source read the verse in several complementary ways: al-Ṭabarī understood it as God's control over all affairs, great and small; Ibn al-Jawzī as the carrying out of His decree for the duration of the world; others as the descent of revelation itself, verse by verse, from the highest heaven to the earth. All of the readings share one conviction: the world is not running itself, and the distance between Heaven and earth is not a void in which events drift — it is a corridor of command, filled with the constant traffic of the divine administration.

**A Thousand Years in a Day: The Relativity of Divine Time**

The verse then gives the measure of this circulation: *fī yawmin kāna miqdāruhu alfa sanatin mimmā taʿuddūn* — "in a day whose measure is as a thousand years of that which you reckon." The phrase "of that which you reckon" is the key: the thousand years are measured in human reckoning, in the years that men count by the circling of the earth around the sun. The Qur'an is here doing something remarkable for its audience: it is detaching the word "day" from the rotation of the planet and attaching it to a divine scale of duration. A day with God is not a measure of the earth's turning, for the earth itself is one of the things He created in six "days"; a day is simply a unit in the reckoning of the One for Whom "a day with thy Lord is as a thousand years of that which you number" (22:47). The verse thus opens the believer's imagination to orders of time beyond the human: the affairs of the universe move on scales that dwarf the span of a single human life, and the apparent slowness — or speed — of divine action is an artifact of the human clock, not a property of the divine will. The same truth is stated in the Sūrah of the Ascending Stairways with a different measure: "The angels and the Spirit ascend unto Him in a day whose measure is fifty thousand years" (70:4). The commentators reconciled the two verses by observing that the measures describe different realities — some holding that the day of the Hereafter will seem a thousand years to the believers and fifty thousand to the disbelievers, others that the two verses describe different journeys or different states of the same day. The source commentary draws the deeper principle: such disparity indicates the difference between duration in the spiritual world and duration in the world of form and matter. Time itself is a creature; it was created with the world it measures, and the Lord of time is not bounded by the clock He created.

Modern science has made this ancient teaching easier to grasp, not harder. The human being lives embedded in one scale of time — heartbeats, seasons, decades — but physics has shown that duration is not a single universal river flowing equally for all observers; the rate at which time passes depends on gravity and motion, and the cosmos contains clocks that tick at rates vastly different from our own. The verse does not depend on any particular physics, but it immunizes the believer against the provincialism of assuming that the human clock is the only clock that exists. When the affairs of a believer's life seem delayed, or when the justice of God seems slow, the verse invites him to change the clock: the same divine decree that seems to crawl in human years moves on a scale compared to which a millennium is a single day.

**The Day of Terror on the Divine Scale**

The classical commentators also read the thousand-year measure as a description of the Day of Resurrection itself, and specifically of its terror for the disbelievers. On that Day, the commentators explain, the reckoning and its terrors will be stretched, for those who denied it, into what feels like an age without end — a day whose measure is a thousand years of what they count. The same idea appears in the Qur'an's picture of the guilty addressing one another on that Day: "He will say, 'How many years did you tarry on the earth?' They will say, 'We tarried a day or part of a day; ask those who keep count.' He will say, 'You tarried but a little, had you but known'" (23:112–114). Time itself will be experienced differently on the other side of death, and the verse prepares the believer for that difference now. For the believer, the thought of the divine time-scale is a consolation: the trials of this world are, measured on the divine scale, "but a little" (23:113), while the reward that awaits is measured in no human years at all.

**Al-Tustarī: When the Servant Stops Directing Himself**

The great early mystic Sahl al-Tustarī (d. 283/896) read this verse as a description not only of the cosmos but of the inner life of the servant, and his commentary, preserved in the source, is worth quoting at length:

> "He reveals to His servants from His Knowledge that which is a means of guidance and salvation for them. The person who is content with the decreed provision resulting from God's directing for him will have the evil of his own directing disposed of and removed from him. Thus [God] will have returned him to a state of contentment with the Divine Decree and rectitude in the face of the unfolding of what is destined for him. [Such people] are among those who are brought nigh [unto God]. Truly, God created people without any veil, and then made their directing [for themselves] their veil."

The insight is profound. God directs the affair from Heaven to earth continuously; but the human being, anxious and self-important, insists on directing his own affair — planning, worrying, contriving, grasping — and this self-direction, al-Tustarī says, is precisely the veil between the servant and his Lord. The person who accepts that the true direction of affairs belongs to God — who receives the decreed provision with contentment — has the "evil of his own directing" removed from him, and stands before God unveiled. The verse's picture of the universe as a place administered from above thus becomes, in al-Tustarī's reading, a prescription for the soul: stop trying to sit on the throne of your own life; the Throne is occupied. This is not a counsel of passivity — the Qur'an commands effort, planning, and the taking of means — but a counsel of inward surrender: do the work that is yours, and leave the direction of outcomes to the only Director. The anxious person, the control-seeking person, the person who lies awake at night re-managing a future he does not own: this verse — and al-Tustarī's commentary on it — invites him to lay the management of the universe back on the One Who has never once mismanaged it.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:6

> **Such is the Knower of the Unseen and the seen, the Mighty, the Merciful,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name That Completes the Portrait**

The verse gathers up everything that has preceded it — the creation, the Throne, the directing of the affair — and stamps the whole with four divine names: *ʿālim al-ghaybi wa al-shahādah, al-ʿazīz, al-raḥīm* — "the Knower of the Unseen and the seen, the Mighty, the Merciful." The demonstrative with which the verse opens, "Such is," points back to the God just described: the One Who created the heavens and the earth in six days and mounted the Throne, the One Who directs every affair from Heaven to earth — *such* is He Who knows the unseen and the seen, and He is the Mighty, the Merciful. The verse is a signature, in the manner of a seal at the foot of a royal decree: the body of the decree has described the sovereign's power, and the seal names him. The same constellation of names appears in the Qur'an wherever a section closes: "He is God, other than Whom there is no god, Knower of the Unseen and the seen; He is the Compassionate, the Merciful" (59:22), and the Knower of the Unseen and the seen appears throughout the Book as the one before Whom no secret stands (6:73; 9:94; 13:9; 23:92; 39:46; 62:8; 64:18).

**Two Worlds in One Knowledge**

The pairing *al-ghayb wa al-shahādah* — the Unseen and the seen — divides all reality into two domains from the standpoint of the human observer. The seen (*shahādah*) is everything present to the creature's senses and consciousness: the world of bodies, events, and observations. The Unseen (*ghayb*) is everything beyond that presence: God Himself, the angels, the soul, the grave, the Garden and the Fire, the appointed hour, and — crucially — the contents of every human heart, which no other human being can see. The Qur'an repeatedly claims exclusive knowledge of this double domain for God: "With Him are the keys of the Unseen; none knows them but He. And He knows whatsoever is on land and sea; no leaf falls but He knows it" (6:59); "Knower of the Unseen; He discloses not His Unseen to anyone, save to a messenger He has approved" (72:26–27). The second qualification is important: God has disclosed fragments of the Unseen to His prophets — the news of past nations and future judgment that fills this very sūrah — but the disclosure is a gift of revelation, not an acquisition of human knowledge, and the treasury of the Unseen remains His alone. The verse thus establishes the epistemic humility of the believer at the very moment it establishes the sovereignty of God: the world is larger than the visible world, and the visible world itself contains depths — the secrets of hearts, the turning of intentions — visible only to the Knower of the Unseen.

**The Mighty Who Is Also the Merciful**

The two names that follow, *al-ʿazīz* and *al-raḥīm*, form one of the most frequent pairings in the Qur'an, and their conjunction here is deliberate. *Al-ʿazīz* is the Mighty, the Invincible, the One Whose will cannot be thwarted and Whose dominion cannot be challenged; the name is built from a root whose concrete sense is strength and whose social sense is honor and inaccessibility — the ʿazīz is the one too strong to be reached by harm. *Al-raḥīm* is the Merciful, the One of abiding compassion. The two names together describe the two faces of the divine government: power sufficient to accomplish everything that justice requires, and mercy disposed to accomplish everything that love desires. Knowledge, might, and mercy are the three conditions of a perfect judge: he must know the whole case (ʿālim al-ghayb wa al-shahādah), he must be able to enforce his judgment (al-ʿazīz), and he must be inclined to the good of the judged (al-raḥīm). Remove any one of the three and justice fails: knowledge without power is a bystander's pity, power without knowledge is a tyrant's violence, and power with knowledge but without mercy is a machine of pure retribution. The Qur'an places all three at the head of this sūrah because the sūrah is, from verse 10 onward, a courtroom drama — the denial of the resurrection, the scene of the guilty bowing their heads, the plea to be sent back, the verdict of the Fire — and the reader needs to know, before the trial begins, the character of the Judge. The verse that follows will add the specific content of His creative work; the verses that come after will add the resurrection; and the whole will be judged by the Judge Who knows all, can do all, and is merciful to all who turn.

**Living Before the Knower of the Unseen**

The practical weight of this verse falls on the believer's daily consciousness. To affirm that God knows the unseen and the seen is to affirm that nothing in one's life is outside the divine awareness: the hidden intention, the silent thought, the deed done in the dark of night or the privacy of the heart is as present to Him as the noon-day act in the public square. The Prophet ﷺ taught that this awareness is the summit of worship: when Gabriel asked him about iḥsān, he answered, *"That you worship God as though you see Him, for though you do not see Him, truly He sees you"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim). The name *al-ʿAzīz* supports the worshipper from the other side: the Mighty One is the believer's refuge, and no power on earth can harm one who stands under the protection of the Invincible. The name *al-Raḥīm* closes the circle: the One Who knows every fault and has the power to punish every fault has bound Himself to mercy for those who seek it — "My Mercy encompasses all things" (7:156). For the person who has sinned in secret and fears exposure, the verse says: the One Who saw the sin is the Merciful; turn to Him. For the person who has been wronged in secret and despairs of justice, the verse says: the One Who saw the wrong is the Mighty; leave the case to Him. Knowledge without exposure, power without cruelty, mercy without weakness — such is the God of this sūrah, and such is the Judge before Whom its drama will unfold.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:7

> **Who made beautiful all that He created, and Who began the creation of man from clay.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Answers the Question of Ugliness**

From the names of God the sūrah moves to His work: *alladhī aḥsana kulla shayʾin khalaqahu* — "Who made beautiful all that He created." The verb *aḥsana* is the causative form of the root *ḥ-s-n*, goodness and beauty; it means to make good, to make beautiful, to perfect. Several commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, noted that in this context it can carry the sense of *aḥkama* — to make firm, precise, and well-ordered — and both senses converge: the creation is both good and exact, both beautiful and masterfully constructed. The scope of the claim is total: *kulla shayʾin khalaqahu*, "all that He created" — not some things, not most things, but everything. The verse is thus the Qur'an's most compressed answer to one of the oldest questions of religion: if God is good, why is there ugliness, suffering, and evil in the world He made? The verse answers by relocating the question. What God creates is good and beautiful; the Qur'an repeats the verdict at the end of the account of creation — "He it is Who created the heavens and the earth in truth... and shaped you, and made beautiful your shapes" (64:3; cf. 40:64) — and it challenges the observer to find any flaw in the cosmic design: "He created seven heavens in layers. Thou seest no discrepancy in the Merciful's creation. Turn thy vision again: seest thou any rift? Then turn thy vision twice again; thy vision returns to thee humbled and weary" (67:3–4). The eye that searches creation for ugliness is commanded to look, and look again, and the looking itself exhausts the observer before it exhausts the beauty.

What, then, of the evil that undeniably exists — the cruelty, the disease, the decay? The Qur'an's answer, developed across the whole of the Book and presupposed by this verse, is that evil enters the world through the choices of creatures, not through the design of the Creator. The world was created good; the misuse of freedom — by Satan, by the human being — introduced wrong into the world of right; and even the evils that afflict human beings are, in the Qur'an's reckoning, either the consequences of their own deeds ("Whatever misfortune strikes you is for what your own hands have earned, and He pardons much," 42:30) or trials whose ultimate accounting belongs to the Wise. The verse's "made beautiful all that He created" is therefore not a naive optimism that denies the existence of suffering; it is a metaphysical claim about the source: from the creative hand of God only good proceeds, and whatever is not good proceeds from the creature's misuse of the good that God gave — freedom itself being among the things He made beautiful.

**The Two Verbs of Human Genesis**

The verse then names the beginning of the human story: *wa badaʾa khalqa al-insāni min ṭīn* — "and Who began the creation of man from clay." The verb *badaʾa* means to originate, to begin something with no precedent; God originated the creation of the human being — the species had no earlier model — and He originated it from clay. The mention of the human being immediately after the general beautification of creation is significant: the human being is presented as the crown of the creative work, the creature whose making deserves a separate clause, and at the same time as a creature of the earth, formed from the same clay that lies under the feet of every passerby. The Qur'an elsewhere multiplies the descriptions of the clay of origin: man was created "of dust" (3:59), "of clay" (37:11; 23:12), from "dried clay, made of molded mud" (15:26), and from "potter's clay" (55:14). The source commentary observes that these descriptions can be read as the stages of a single process of formation: from dust, to viscous clay, to molded mud, to dried clay like the clay of the potter's vessel — the material of the body being progressively prepared until, at the point described in verse 9 of this sūrah, it is ready to receive the breath of the spirit. The human being is thus, in the Qur'an's own account, a being of two origins: the body traces its pedigree to the earth, and the spirit (as verse 9 will say) to the breath of God; and the whole dignity and the whole humility of the human condition are contained in that double descent.

**The Clay as Humility and the Clay as Honor**

Why does the Qur'an insist, across sūrah after sūrah, that the human being was made from clay? The insistence serves two purposes, one for the arrogant and one for the despairing. For the arrogant — the Pharaohs of every age who say, as the Qur'an reports of the first of them, "Am I not better than this one who is despicable and can scarcely make himself clear?" (43:52) — the clay is the permanent correction of pride: whatever you have become, your origin is earth; the beauty of the finished vessel does not cancel the modesty of the clay. The Qur'an makes the point with deliberate bluntness in the verse of the Resurrection dispute: "Does man not consider that We created him from a drop of fluid? Yet he is a manifest adversary" (36:77). For the despairing, the clay carries the opposite message: the same earth that seems so lowly was the material chosen by the Creator for His most honored creature; God "began the creation of man from clay" and then, as the following verses will narrate, breathed into him of His Spirit and endowed him with hearing, sight, and hearts. The material does not determine the value; the divine intention does. A palace built of common stone is still a palace; a human being formed of common clay is still the creature into whom God breathed His Spirit. The verse thus holds the two halves of the human identity in a single sentence: an origin that forbids arrogance, and a making that forbids despair.

**Making Beautiful as a Human Calling**

The commentators drew from the verse's verb *aḥsana* a lesson about the human vocation: the quality of making beautiful is not jealously reserved to God but is something in which the human being is invited to participate. Just as God made beautiful all that He created, so the human being — created in the divine pattern of craftsmanship — is called to make beautiful what he makes: his work, his speech, his character, his treatment of animals, his building of cities. The Prophet ﷺ generalized this calling in a celebrated instruction: *"Verily God has enjoined making beautiful (iḥsān) for everything. So when you kill, kill in a beautiful manner; when you sacrifice, sacrifice in a beautiful manner — let one of you sharpen his blade and spare the animal suffering"* (Muslim 1955; from Shaddād ibn Aws). The instruction is stunning in its scope: even the acts that seem most resistant to beauty — killing and slaughter — are to be performed beautifully, with sharpened blades and quick, merciful strokes. If beauty is commanded even there, it is commanded everywhere: in the workplace, where the believer's craft should bear the mark of care; in speech, which should be "good speech" (17:53); in charity, which should be given "in the best manner"; in the home, where the believer is to "make beautiful" his treatment of his family; and above all in character, which the Prophet ﷺ summarized when he said, *"I was sent only to perfect the noble traits of character"* (al-Bukhārī, in *al-Adab al-Mufrad*; Aḥmad). The verse thus converts aesthetics into ethics: because God made beautiful all that He created, the believer who imitates his Lord's attribute of making beautiful — in the limited, creaturely way proper to him — is living out the meaning of his own creation. The believer's work, on this reading, is never merely functional; it is a form of *iḥsān*, a making-beautiful, and the universe itself stands as the model: a creation in which nothing was made carelessly, and in which the human being is invited to leave nothing of his own making careless either.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:8

> **Then He made his seed from a draught of base fluid.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Descent: From Clay to a Drop**

Verse 7 described the origin of the first human being from clay; this verse describes the origin of everyone after him: *thumma jaʿala naslahu min sulālatin min māʾin mahīn* — "Then He made his seed from a draught of base fluid." The word *nasl* means offspring or progeny, and the commentators (al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī) read the verse as covering the entire human lineage: Adam was created directly from clay, but his descendants — every human being who has ever lived or will live — enter existence through this second, humbler route. The verse thus gives the human race a double genealogy: a noble ancestor formed by the divine hand from the earth, and a biological transmission that passes through a drop of fluid. The word *sulālah* is precise: it derives from a root meaning to draw out or extract, and it denotes something drawn forth gently — an extract, a draught, a quintessence drawn from a larger body. The commentators noted that the semen is called a *sulālah* because it flows gently and is drawn out from the loins of the father and the being of the mother; it is, in the Qur'an's vocabulary, the extracted essence through which the line of descent runs. And the fluid is described as *mahīn* — base, weak, contemptible — an adjective the Qur'an applies to the same fluid in several places: "Did We not create you from base fluid, which We placed in a firm abode till a known measure?" (77:20–21), and "Let man consider from what he was created — created from a gushing fluid that issues from between the loins and the breastbones" (86:5–7).

**The Etymology of Humility**

The Qur'an's choice of adjectives for the material of human reproduction is deliberately, almost aggressively, deflating. The same human being who struts through the world conscious of his lineage, his wealth, his intellect, and his beauty is invited to trace his pedigree backward: past his parents, past his ancestors, to a draught of base fluid, *māʾ mahīn* — a word whose root meaning carries weakness, insignificance, and despicableness. The Qur'an presses the point in the sūrah of the Resurrection: "Does man not consider that We created him from a drop of fluid? Yet he is a manifest adversary. And he strikes a similitude for Us and forgets his own creation — 'Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?'" (36:77–78). The argument is deliberately ad hominem in the most literal sense: the debater who disputes the power of God is himself the proof of that power, for he was once nothing but a drop that no one noticed and no one valued. The commentators observed that the description serves the same purpose as the mention of clay in the previous verse: it is the Qur'an's permanent inoculation against arrogance. Every human institution of rank — nobility, caste, race, class — is quietly dismantled by the reminder that the biological origin of every member of the species is identical, and that the origin is a fluid that would be invisible on any garment and worthless in any market.

**The Power That Works Through the Lowly**

The verse is not, however, merely a lesson in humility; it is also an argument from power. The whole point of describing the base fluid is to foreground the majesty of the act: from this despised material, God produces the human being — the creature who speaks, reasons, loves, builds, worships, and knows his own Creator. The Qur'an develops the stages of this wonder in the Sūrah of the Believers, where the drop is placed "in a firm abode" and then transformed: "Then We made the drop into a clinging clot, then made the clot into a lump of flesh, then made the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh; then We brought it forth as another creation. So blessed be God, the best of creators!" (23:13–14). Every stage named there is a visible miracle: a single cell dividing into the billions of specialized cells of a human body; a structureless clot organizing itself into skeleton, muscle, brain, and heart. And because the entire sequence begins from a substance of no account, the sequence testifies to a power that requires no noble raw material — a power that can just as easily, the Qur'an argues throughout, reconstitute the scattered human being after death. The theological argument of the resurrection in this sūrah is thus already under construction in this verse: the God Who began the human race from clay, and continues it through a base fluid, is not a God Who finds the remaking of man difficult. The One Who originated the process that no scientist can fully replicate holds the patent on the original; the return is, for Him, a smaller matter than the first creation.

**The Dignity That Material Cannot Confer — or Deny**

The verse closes with a quiet implication that runs against the entire logic of worldly hierarchy: if every human being enters existence through the same humble channel, then no human being can claim a nobler biological origin than another, and no human being can be despised for a lowly one. The differences that divide humanity — tribe, tongue, color, class — are, in the Qur'an's reckoning, signs of divine artistry (30:22), not grounds of merit: "O mankind! Truly We created you from a male and a female, and We made you peoples and tribes that you might come to know one another. Truly the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most reverent of you" (49:13). The Prophet ﷺ made the same point in his Farewell Sermon, when he declared that no Arab has any virtue over a non-Arab, and no white over a black, except through reverence (taqwā). The verse's "base fluid" is thus egalitarian in its very offensiveness: it levels every pretension at the root, and it does so without ever diminishing the value of the human being, because that value was never located in the raw material at all. It was located in the divine act of creation — in the fashioning, and the breathing of the Spirit, and the endowment of hearing, sight, and hearts, to which the very next verse now turns.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:9

> **Then He fashioned him, and breathed into him of His Spirit, and endowed you with hearing, sight, and hearts. Little do you give thanks!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fashioning and Breathing: When the Clay Became a Person**

The third stage of the human genesis follows: *thumma sawwāhu wa nafakha fīhi min rūḥihi* — "Then He fashioned him, and breathed into him of His Spirit." The verb *sawwā* comes from the root *s-w-y*, whose meaning is evenness and proportion; to fashion in this sense is to bring a thing to its complete, balanced, integral form — all parts fitted, all proportions right. The clay that became a drop, and the drop that became a body, is here brought to its finished architecture. Then comes the decisive act: *nafakha fīhi min rūḥihi*, "and breathed into him of His Spirit." The same two acts are described by God Himself in the two sūrahs that narrate the angels' prostration before Adam: "And when I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My Spirit, fall down before him prostrating" (15:29; cf. 38:72). The commentators explained that the breathing of the Spirit is the moment at which the inanimate body — fully formed but lifeless — became a living, conscious being (Ibn al-Jawzī): the breath of life entered the clay, and the clay became a person. The possessive in "of His Spirit" requires precision: the spirit is not a part of God, nor a divine substance poured into matter; the Qur'an itself guards the meaning, answering the question about the spirit with, "Say: 'The Spirit is of the command of my Lord, and you have been given but little knowledge'" (17:85). The "of" is the genitive of honor and origin — the spirit is a created thing, but a created thing of such noble rank that God attaches it to Himself, just as He speaks of "the House of God," "the she-camel of God," and "the servant of God." What the verse establishes is the unique dignity of the human being: alone among the creatures of the earth, the human being carries within himself a principle that God describes by His own name — a spirit breathed from Him, which will return to Him, and which makes the human being more than a superior animal. The same breath is described in the Qur'an as the means by which God ennobled other elect beings: the Spirit was breathed into Mary so that she conceived Jesus by God's command (21:91; 66:12), and the angels who prostrated to Adam were commanded to do so precisely because of this breathed spirit.

**Hearing, Sight, and Hearts: The Instruments of Knowing**

The verse then turns from the first man to every reader of the verse: *wa jaʿala lakum al-samʿa wa al-abṣāra wa al-afʾidah* — "and endowed you with hearing, sight, and hearts." The pronoun shifts to the second person — *you* — because the endowment is not a distant historical event but a present, personal fact: the faculties with which this verse is being read are themselves the subject of the verse. The order of the three endowments is constant in the Qur'an — hearing, then sight, then hearts — and the commentators found in the constancy a map of how human beings actually come to know. Al-Rāzī's explanation, preserved in the source, is the most complete: the human being first hears of things — from parents, teachers, prophets, reports of what lies beyond his experience; then he sees things for himself, confirming or correcting what he heard; and finally his heart (*fuʾād*, the seat of understanding and discernment) perceives the inner reality of what he has heard and seen, and he arrives at direct, complete knowledge. The order is also the order of human development: the ear is fully formed and functional in the womb before the eye opens after birth, and both long before the intellect matures into judgment. The Qur'an states the sequence elsewhere in the same terms: "God brought you forth from the wombs of your mothers knowing nothing, and gave you hearing and sight and hearts, that haply you may give thanks" (16:78), and again, "And He it is Who brought you into being, and endowed you with hearing and sight and hearts — little thanks do you give!" (67:23). The repetition of the triad, always in the same order, always with the same verbs, is itself a Qur'anic signature: these three are the complete equipment of the human knower, and the human knower is complete with nothing else.

**Why the Faculties Are Listed Together — and What Their Misuse Costs**

The Qur'an never lists hearing, sight, and hearts as mere neutral equipment; it lists them as instruments of accountability. The triad appears again and again in contexts of judgment: the faculties are the channels through which guidance enters — or through which it is refused. The sūrah of the Sand Dunes says of the peoples who rejected the messengers: "We had established them in that wherein We have not established you, and We gave them hearing and sight and hearts — but their hearing, their sight, and their hearts availed them naught, for they denied the signs of God, and that which they mocked surrounded them" (46:26). The same faculties that could have carried the truth in became, through misuse, the very instruments of condemnation: ears that heard the truth and refused it, eyes that saw the signs and turned from them, hearts that understood and yet chose denial. The verse of this sūrah compresses the same warning into its closing clause: *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — "Little do you give thanks!" The endowment is a gift, and the gift demands gratitude; but the gratitude most human beings render for hearing, sight, and hearts is little indeed — they use the ears for noise, the eyes for spectacle, and the hearts for the pursuit of appetite, and the Giver of the faculties is forgotten in the enjoyment of them. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the faculties will be asked about their use: "The feet of the servant shall not depart on the Day of Resurrection until he has been asked about four things: his life and how he spent it, his youth and how he wore it out, his wealth — from where he acquired it and on what he spent it — and his knowledge and what he did with it" (al-Tirmidhī 2417). Every one of those questions passes through the three faculties of this verse: the life was lived through the heart's intentions, the youth through the eye's gaze and the ear's attention, the wealth through choices the heart made.

**Gratitude as the Proper Use of the Senses**

The verse's closing rebuke — "Little do you give thanks!" — is, on closer reading, a prescription. If the faculties were given "that haply you may give thanks" (16:78), then gratitude is not a feeling that accompanies their use but the very purpose of their being. The Qur'an defines the grateful use of the senses with remarkable concreteness across its pages: the hearing is used gratefully when it listens to the remembrance of God and turns from the frivolous — "When the verses of the Compassionate are recited unto them, they fall prostrate, weeping" (19:58); the sight is used gratefully when it looks at creation and sees the signs — "Do they not look at the camels, how they were created?" (88:17) — and when it is lowered before what God has forbidden (24:30); the heart is used gratefully when it reflects, remembers, and intends the good — "Truly in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are signs for those of understanding" (3:190). Gratitude, in this Qur'anic sense, is attention rightly directed: the grateful person is the one who receives the world as a gift, traces the gift to the Giver, and uses the gift the way the Giver intended. Ingratitude, by contrast, is attention wrongly directed: the faculties consumed on their objects while their Origin is ignored — the eye that feasts on the world and never looks up, the ear that drinks in everything except the call to prayer, the heart that knows everything except its own Lord. The verse of this sūrah therefore stands as one of the Qur'an's most concentrated audits of modern life: an age of unprecedented hearing (endless audio, podcasts, notifications), unprecedented sight (screens, images, twenty-four-hour visual entertainment), and unprecedented distraction of the heart — an age that has maximized the faculties and minimized the gratitude. "Little do you give thanks!" was never a more exact diagnosis than it is now.

**The Breath of God and the Sacredness of Every Life**

One further implication of the verse must be stated, because it has shaped Islamic law and ethics in every generation: if every human being is the creature into whom God breathed of His Spirit, then every human life — at every stage — carries a dignity that no earthly authority conferred and none may cancel. The verse's anthropology is the foundation of the Qur'an's ethic of life: "Whosoever kills a soul, unless it be for retribution for a soul or for working corruption upon the earth, it is as though he had killed all mankind; and whosoever gives life to one, it is as though he had given life to all mankind" (5:32). The same anthropology underlies the Qur'an's condemnation of the pre-Islamic practice of burying infant girls alive — the buried child is asked "for what sin she was slain" (81:8–9) — and its protection of the unborn, for the drop in the womb is already on its way to receiving the breath. The verse also founds the ethic of care for the dying and the dead: the body that carried the breathed spirit is treated with reverence — washed, shrouded, prayed over, buried with honor — and the spirit itself, as the very next verse of this sūrah will state, is taken at death by the Angel entrusted with it and returned to the Lord Who breathed it. In a world that has learned to value human life by utility, productivity, or stage of development, the Qur'an's valuation remains stubbornly different: human worth is not earned by any capacity; it is endowed by the breath. And "little do you give thanks" applies to the gift of life itself, before any of its particular endowments.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:10

> **And they say, "What, when we have become lost in the earth, shall we indeed be created anew?" Nay, they believe not in the meeting with their Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Objection of the Scattered**

Having set out the divine power in creation — the clay, the drop, the fashioning, the breath, the faculties — the sūrah now turns to the denial that meets it: *wa qālū a-idhā ḍalalnā fī al-arḍi a-innā la-fī khalqin jadīd* — "And they say, 'What, when we have become lost in the earth, shall we indeed be created anew?'" The verb *ḍalalnā* is striking: literally, "when we have gone astray in the earth," or "when we have vanished into the earth." The speakers picture their own death and decay as a kind of losing — the body lowered into the grave, broken down by the earth, dispersed into soil and water, mingled with the dust of countless other dead, until the person is, as far as any observer can tell, simply gone: lost in the earth. Their question is posed as a rhetorical impossibility: after that dispersal, "shall we indeed be created anew?" The Qur'an records the same objection, in the same mocking tone, in sūrah after sūrah: "When we are dead and have become dust and bones, are we indeed resurrected?" (56:47; cf. 37:16–17; 50:3; 79:10–11); "What! When we are bones and fragments, shall we indeed be raised up as a new creation?" (17:49). The persistence of the objection across the Makkan revelation shows how central the issue was: the resurrection of the body was the doctrine the deniers found most absurd, and they returned to it as their strongest card. The verse's placement here is deliberate: it comes immediately after three verses of creation — the clay, the drop, the fashioning, the breath — so that the denial stands in the sharpest possible contrast with the evidence just recited. The deniers are not objecting to the power of God in the abstract; they are objecting to it in the very face of its most obvious demonstrations, their own existence.

**The "Nay" That Corrects the Question**

The Qur'an's answer to the objection is not a lecture on physics but a diagnosis: *bal hum bi-liqāʾi rabbihim kāfirūn* — "Nay, they believe not in the meeting with their Lord." The particle *bal* ("nay, rather") turns the whole discussion in a new direction: the real problem is not that the resurrection is difficult to conceive; the real problem is that they do not wish to meet their Lord. The commentators (al-Rāzī and others) drew out the full force of the diagnosis: the objection to the resurrection is, at bottom, an objection to the meeting — and the meeting means accountability. The scattered bones and the lost dust are a pretext; the true stakes are the standing before God. The Qur'an makes the same diagnosis elsewhere with complete explicitness: the deniers of the resurrection are described, in the same breath, as those who "do not hope for the meeting with Us" (10:7), and the believers are described as those who "are certain of the meeting with their Lord" (2:46; 6:154). The word *liqāʾ*, "meeting," carries the full weight of the afterlife in the Qur'an's vocabulary: it is the meeting with God after death, the encounter of the creature with the One he knew on earth only by faith. To believe in the meeting is to live every day under the awareness of that encounter; to disbelieve in it is to live as though the horizon of existence were the grave. The verse's diagnosis explains a psychological fact that every observer of human life can confirm: the people most insistent on the impossibility of the resurrection are not usually those who have studied the question most carefully; they are those who have the most to fear from the answer. The denial of the meeting is the wish of the unaccountable, dressed in the language of rational objection — and the Qur'an, with its *bal*, refuses to let the costume pass for the argument.

**Why Resurrection Is No Harder Than First Creation**

Behind the diagnosis, the Qur'an also carries the rebuttal, and it is an argument from the very verses this sūrah has just recited. If God began the creation of the human being from clay — if He originated the entire species from a draught of base fluid, fashioned it, and breathed into it His Spirit — then the reconstitution of the dispersed body is not a greater act but a smaller one. The Qur'an makes the argument explicit wherever the objection is raised: "He it is Who originates creation, then brings it back again — and that is easier for Him" (30:27); "And He it is Who originates creation, then causes it to return — and that is most easy for Him" (30:27); the objector is told, "Say: 'He will give them life Who brought them forth the first time; and He knows every creation'" (36:79); and the whole debate is settled with the image of the first creation: "Does man not see that We created him from a drop of fluid? Yet he is a manifest adversary" (36:77). The verse of this sūrah adds its own image to the argument two chapters later in the same sūrah: the dead earth revived by rain (32:27), which the believer sees every year — the most public, repeated demonstration in the natural world that the Creator of life is not exhausted by death. The objection "when we have become lost in the earth" is answered by the earth itself: every spring, the earth that received the dead seed returns the living plant; and the One Who causes the return of the plant from the scattered seed is not puzzled by the return of the man from the scattered dust.

**Living in the Light of the Meeting**

The verse's diagnosis — "they believe not in the meeting with their Lord" — is also, by contrast, a description of the believer, and the whole of the believer's life follows from it. The believer is described in the Qur'an precisely as the one who lives in expectation of the meeting: "Whosoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord, let him work righteous deeds and not associate anyone in the worship of his Lord" (18:110); "Whosoever hopes for the meeting with God — truly the term of God is coming; and He is the Hearer, the Knower" (29:5); and the righteous are described as those "who know that they shall meet their Lord, and unto Him they shall return" (2:46). The expectation of the meeting is not a distant creed; it is an operating principle. The person who expects to meet his Lord at the end of the road lives the road differently: he is careful with his trusts, because he will be asked about them; he is restrained in his appetites, because he will be asked about them; he is quick to repair his wrongs, because he does not want to arrive carrying them. The person who denies the meeting lives under a different logic: if the road ends at the grave, the only sensible strategy is to extract from the road whatever it will yield before the grave — and the Qur'an repeatedly observes that this logic produces corruption, arrogance, and heedlessness (10:7–8). Modern secular culture has made the denial of the meeting — or its postponement to an indefinite irrelevance — a default assumption, and the moral consequences that the Qur'an predicted follow with mechanical regularity: a life organized entirely around the visible horizon, anxious precisely because the horizon is so close, and empty precisely because nothing beyond it is expected. The verse's *bal* remains the turning point for anyone who reads it honestly: the difficulty was never in the dust; it was in the meeting — and the meeting, for the one who believes in it, is not a terror but the very thing that gives the dust its meaning.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:11

> **Say, "The Angel of death, who has been entrusted with you, will take you; then unto your Lord shall you be returned."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Death as a Taking, Not a Losing**

The deniers of the previous verse had asked what would happen "when we have become lost in the earth" — picturing death as an annihilation, a vanishing into the soil. This verse answers the picture directly: *qul yatawaffākum malaku al-mawt* — "Say: 'The Angel of death, who has been entrusted with you, will take you.'" The Prophet is commanded to correct the image at its root. Death is not a disappearing into the earth; it is a *taking* — the verb *yatawaffā* comes from the root *w-f-y*, whose meanings cluster around completion and full payment: to fulfill, to pay in full, to receive in full what is due. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the fulfillment of covenants and the completion of measures, and it uses it for death because death, in the Qur'anic vision, is the completion of the term: the soul that was lent its days has reached the full number, and the One Who lent it now receives it back in full, nothing lost, nothing wasted. The person is not lost in the earth; the body returns to the earth, but the person — the conscious, accountable self — is received, entire, by the angel appointed for the task. The verse thus contains, in a single verb, the whole Qur'anic answer to the fear of annihilation: what looks like the end is a collection; what looks like the void is a receiving hand.

**The Angel Entrusted with You**

The verse identifies the agent of death with a precision that the Qur'an maintains everywhere: *malaku al-mawt*, "the Angel of death" — the angel "who has been entrusted with you." The word *wukkila* carries the sense of a charge given: the Angel of death is not an independent power but an entrusted servant, commissioned specifically for this office, and the phrase "entrusted with you" places every reader of the verse under that commission. The classical commentators identified the Angel of death as Azrael, the name by which he is known in the Islamic tradition (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī), though the Qur'an itself does not use the name, referring to him only by his office — a silence the scholars took as deliberate, since the Qur'an's concern is not the angel's name but the reality that death is an act of divine administration, not an accident of nature. The commentators further noted that the Angel of death does not work alone: he has helpers among the angels, the "messengers" and "guardians" of whom the Qur'an speaks: "He is the Vanquisher over His servants, and He sends guardians over you, until, when death comes to one of you, Our messengers take him — and they neglect not" (6:61); and again, "those whom the angels take while they are pure, saying, 'Peace be upon you! Enter the Garden for that which you used to do'" (16:32), and its opposite, "those whom the angels take while they are wronging themselves" (16:28). The Qur'an also says, "God takes the souls at the moment of their death" (39:42), and al-Qurṭubī reconciled the verses with a precise division of labor that has become the standard explanation: God brings about the end; the Angel of death seizes the soul at its appointed moment; and the attending angels carry out the taking, each according to his office. The point of the whole apparatus is theological: death is not the victory of chaos over a life; it is the execution of a decree by commissioned servants of the Lord, and no soul dies before its term, and none dies after it: "For every community there is a term; when their term comes they cannot delay it by an hour, nor advance it" (7:34; cf. 63:11).

**Then Unto Your Lord Shall You Be Returned**

The verse ends by naming the destination of the taken soul: *thumma ilā rabbikum turjaʿūn* — "then unto your Lord shall you be returned." The return is stated in the passive voice, as something done to every human being without exception, believer and denier alike: death leads to the return, and the return leads to the Lord. The verse thereby answers the tenth verse's diagnosis point by point: the deniers said they did not believe in the meeting with their Lord (32:10); this verse says the meeting is not a matter of belief but of fact — *turjaʿūn*, you shall be returned, whether you believe it or not. The Qur'an states the same certainty in the same vocabulary throughout: "Then are they returned unto God, their True Master" (6:62); "Unto thy Lord is the return" (96:8); "To God is the journey's end" (42:53). The commentators noted the tenderness embedded in the grammar: the verse does not say "unto your Judge" or "unto your Reckoner," though the return includes judgment; it says "unto your Lord" — *rabbikum*, the Lord, the Sustainer, the One Who created, provided, and guided before He ever judged. Even in the verse that announces death — the verse most feared by the deniers — the Qur'an names God by the name of care. For the believer, the whole of death's terror is transfigured by this phrase: the return is not to an unknown tribunal but to the Lord Who has been with the soul from its first breath; death is the final homecoming of a creature who was always traveling toward the One Who sent him.

**The Intelligence That Remembers Death**

Because the Angel of death is "entrusted with you" — with every reader of this verse, at an hour known only to God — the classical scholars made this verse one of the foundations of the Islamic discipline of remembering death (*dhikr al-mawt*). The Prophet ﷺ taught that the remembrance of death is not morbidity but intelligence, and the report preserved in the source commentary gives his exact words:

> "The intelligent person is one who knows his soul and works for what follows death." And when asked, "Who is the most intelligent of believers?" he replied: "The most frequent in remembering death, and the best prepared for what follows it — they are the most intelligent." (al-Tirmidhī 2459; Ibn Mājah 4260)

The logic of the reply is exact: intelligence is the capacity to act on the basis of real causes rather than imagined ones, and death is the one cause whose reality no one disputes and whose date no one knows. The intelligent person therefore organizes his life around the certain appointment — working "for what follows death" — while the foolish person organizes his life as though the appointment were an illusion. The Prophet ﷺ made the same point in a briefer form when he advised: "Remember often the destroyer of pleasures" — meaning death (al-Tirmidhī; from Abū Hurayrah). The word "destroyer of pleasures" is precise: death destroys every pleasure by ending it, and the one who remembers this while enjoying a lawful pleasure enjoys it without being enslaved by it, and the one who remembers it while tempted is reminded of the price of the pleasure that outlives its moment. Modern culture has built its comfort on the opposite strategy: death is hidden from view, postponed in language ("passed away," "lost"), medicalized, and sanitized until the average person reaches middle age without ever having watched a death or prepared for his own. The Qur'an's counsel is the reverse: keep the Angel of death in mind — not to darken life but to clarify it, to sort what matters from what does not, to keep the "return unto your Lord" in front of the soul so that the soul's work is always aimed at its true destination.

**The Answer to the Modern Fear of Oblivion**

The verse also speaks directly to one of the deepest anxieties of the secular age: the fear that death is the end, that the person is indeed "lost in the earth." The verse's answer is not an argument but an announcement: you will not be lost, because you will be taken; you will not end, because you will be returned. The soul's survival of death is stated here as the plainest of facts, needing no defense because it is the testimony of the One Who created the soul and knows its nature: the same God Who says in this sūrah that He breathed His Spirit into the human being (32:9) now says that the One entrusted with death will take the soul at its term and that the soul will return to its Lord. The two verses belong together: the breath that originated the soul guarantees the survival of the soul; the being who was created by a breath cannot be annihilated by decay. And because the return is to the Lord, the death of the believer is described in the Qur'an not as a loss but as a gain: "Do not say of those who are slain in the way of God that they are dead; nay, they are alive, but you perceive not" (2:154). The Angel of death, in the believer's understanding, is not an enemy at the end of the road but a servant of the Merciful at the door of the return — and the only question the verse leaves open, the question that every reader must answer for himself, is what kind of life he will be returning with.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:12

> **Couldst thou but see when the guilty bend their heads low before their Lord: "Our Lord! We have seen and we have heard; so send us back that we may work righteousness. Truly we are certain."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scene That No Living Eye Could Bear**

The sūrah now shifts from argument to spectacle: *wa law tarā idhi al-mujrimūna nākisū ruʾūsihim ʿinda rabbihim* — "Couldst thou but see when the guilty bend their heads low before their Lord." The construction *law tarā* — "if only you could see" — is a rhetorical device of immense power: it invites the listener to imagine a sight so overwhelming that the eye of this world could not sustain it, and then leaves the imagination to do the work. The Prophet is addressed directly, but the invitation extends to every reader: picture the guilty — those who denied the meeting, mocked the resurrection, and demanded a date for the victory — now standing before the very Lord Whose meeting they denied, their heads bent low. The posture is the exact inverse of their former bearing. In the world they had walked with heads high, confident in their mockery and their power; now the Qur'an shows them *nākisī ruʾūsihim* — heads hanging, necks bowed, the posture of those from whom all pretense has been stripped. The Qur'an paints the same scene elsewhere with even more terrible detail: "On the Day when the shin is bared, and they are called to prostrate, they will not be able — their eyes humbled, disgrace covering them; for they were called to prostrate while they were yet sound" (68:42–43). The point of the parallel is devastating: they had been invited to bow to God voluntarily their whole lives long — in prayer, in prostration, in humility before the truth — and they had refused, waxing arrogant; now they stand before the Lord and the same invitation returns, but the time of voluntary prostration is over. The scene in this verse, with its bowed heads and its halting speech, is the visual proof of the tenth verse's diagnosis: they did not believe in the meeting with their Lord — and here is the meeting, and they are in it.

**The Testimony That Comes Too Late**

Then the guilty speak, and their words are the most pathetic in the sūrah: *rabbanā abṣarnā wa samiʿnā* — "Our Lord! We have seen and we have heard." The order of the faculties is the reverse of the Qur'an's usual order — hearing first, then sight — because in the Hereafter the natural sequence has been inverted: they now see the realities they refused to hear about, and the sight has forced the hearing into belief. They had ears in the world and did not hear the warnings (the warner came, verse 3 said; and verse 26 will say, "will they not listen?"); they had eyes and did not see the signs (verse 27: "will they not see?"). Now they claim both faculties at last: "We have seen and we have heard." And on the strength of this forced perception they make their request: *fa-arjiʿnā naʿmal ṣāliḥan innā mūqinūn* — "so send us back, that we may work righteousness. Truly we are certain." The word *mūqinūn* — "we are certain" — is the word the Qur'an uses for the settled, tested conviction of the believers who believed in the unseen without seeing it; the guilty now claim it as a discovery of the last moment, the certainty of those who have been shown what they would not believe. The commentators (al-Rāzī) noted the ambiguity of the claim: some of them, at that moment, may genuinely believe — their eyes have been opened, and certainty has been forced upon them; others are merely saying what they think will win their release, exactly as the Qur'an reports of them in another scene, where their "contention" on that Day is only to say, "By God, our Lord, we were not idolaters" (6:23) — a lie told to the face of the One Who knows what they were. Either way, the request is refused, and the sūrah's next verses will show the refusal, and the verses after that the reason: the world was the place for working righteousness, and they spent it denying the meeting; the Hereafter is the place of recompense, and requests do not pass between the two.

**The Answer That Has Already Been Given**

The Qur'an does not leave the reader to wonder how God answers this request, for the answer is given in several places, and always with the same finality. In the sūrah of Fāṭir, the same speakers are addressed: "Did We not give you long life, enough for whosoever would reflect to reflect therein? And the warner came unto you — so taste [the punishment]! And the wrongdoers have no helper" (35:37). The answer itemizes the indictment: the long life was the opportunity; the warner was the summons; both were given, and both were spent in denial — so the request for more time is a request for a second exam after the first was failed. In the sūrah of the Believers, the same plea is made by a single soul at the moment of death, and the divine verdict is sharper still: "Until, when death comes to one of them, he says, 'My Lord! Send me back, that I may work righteousness in that which I left behind!' Nay, it is but a word he speaks; and behind them is a barrier until the Day they are raised" (23:99–100). "It is but a word he speaks" — the request itself is dismissed as a reflex, a formula of panic, with no reality behind it. And the most psychologically penetrating of the answers is in the sūrah of al-Anʿām, where God describes what would happen if the request were granted: "If thou couldst see when they are made to stand before the Fire, and they say, 'Would that we were returned, and deny not the signs of our Lord, and be among the believers!' Nay, that which they used to conceal before has become manifest to them; and were they to be sent back, they would return to that which they were forbidden — and truly they are liars" (6:27–28). The verse does not merely refuse the request; it exposes its insincerity with a kind of divine irony: they are sent back in imagination, and the imagination itself convicts them, for they would do again exactly what they promised to abandon.

**The Psychology of the Procrastinated Repentance**

The scene in this verse is the Qur'an's definitive portrait of a universal human tendency: the postponement of amendment until it is too late, and the fantasy that a second chance would be used differently. Every human being who has delayed repentance, delayed reform, delayed the mending of a relationship or the return to God has rehearsed this scene in miniature: if only I had more time; if only I could go back; if only I had known then what I know now. The Qur'an's answer to the fantasy is not cruelty but honesty: the second chance is a fantasy because the character that wasted the first chance would waste the second — the change that matters is not a change of circumstances but a change of the self, and the self does not change by being transported back in time. The Prophet ﷺ made the same point about the most common form of this fantasy, the deathbed intention: "God accepts the repentance of a servant as long as his soul has not reached the throat" (al-Tirmidhī 3537; from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar). And the Qur'an states the boundary with absolute clarity: "Repentance is not for those who do evil deeds until, when death comes to one of them, he says, 'Indeed, I repent now' — nor for those who die while disbelieving" (4:18). The scene of the bowed heads is thus presented to the believer in this life as a gift: read it now, while reading can still change something; see the posture of the guilty now, while prostration is still voluntary; hear their "send us back" now, while the return is still possible through repentance that God accepts. The verse is a mirror held up to the future so that the present may be corrected — the whole purpose of Qur'anic eschatology compressed into a single scene.

**Certainty Earned and Certainty Forced**

One further contrast completes the lesson of the verse: the difference between the certainty of the believers and the certainty of the guilty. The believers of verse 15 of this very sūrah — the people whose sides shun their beds, who call upon their Lord in fear and hope — believe in the signs before they see the realities, and their belief is described in the Qur'an as the precious kind: "who believe in the Unseen" (2:3), who are "certain of the meeting with their Lord" (2:46) while the meeting is still hidden. Their certainty is *īmān bi-al-ghayb* — faith in the unseen — and it is precisely because it is faith and not sight that it has moral weight, that it costs something, that it transforms conduct. The guilty in this verse have the same certainty at last, but theirs is the certainty of the cornered, the faith of necessity (*īmān al-ḍarūrah*), the belief of Pharaoh when the sea closed over him: "He said, 'I believe that there is no god but He in Whom the Children of Israel believe' — [and God answered] 'Now? But before this thou didst rebel and wast among the workers of corruption'" (10:90–91). The two certainties are distinguished by their timing and their cost: the certainty that precedes the evidence is faith; the certainty that follows it is fact — and facts, the verse implies, have never saved anyone. The believer's task, then, is to convert the scene of this verse into present conduct: to be certain now, while certainty is a choice; to bend the head now, while bending is voluntary; to work righteousness now, while the working still counts — before the day when the same words will be said in a tone that no longer carries any hope.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:13

> **And had We willed, We would have given every soul its guidance. But the Word from Me comes due: "I shall surely fill Hell with jinn and men all together!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Seems to Raise the Question of Destiny**

Few verses of the Qur'an have been quoted more often — and understood more selectively — than this one: *wa law shiʾnā la-ātaynā kulla nafsin hudāhā* — "And had We willed, We would have given every soul its guidance." Read in isolation, the verse appears to say that God could have guided every human being and chose not to, and that the filling of Hell with jinn and men is the execution of a divine decree issued in advance. The fatalist readings that have attached themselves to this verse across the centuries — the claim that the disbeliever disbelieves because God decreed his disbelief, and therefore bears no responsibility — are refuted by the verse's own context and by the Qur'an's own explanations of its meaning. The verse is introduced by *wa* — "and" — connecting it to everything that precedes: the guilty bending their heads, the request to be sent back, the refusal that awaits them. It is, in the first instance, an answer to the Prophet's distress over the persistence of denial — a consolation telling him that the turning away of some is within the divine decree, that his mission is warning and not compulsion, and that he should not exhaust himself over those whom God has decreed will not believe. The same meaning is spelled out in the parallel verse of Sūrah Yūnus, which is its clearest commentary: "And had thy Lord willed, all those who are on the earth would have believed, all together. Wouldst thou compel men till they become believers? And it is not for a soul to believe save by God's leave" (10:99–100). The question "Wouldst thou compel men?" reveals what the alternative would have been: had God willed, He could have compelled belief — He could have created human beings without the capacity for refusal, angels by nature, machines by program. But the guidance that is "given" in the alternative scenario is not the guidance the Qur'an values; it is the guidance of force, the belief of the compelled, which is no belief at all.

**The Two Kinds of Guidance and the Two Wills**

The classical commentators distinguished two senses of "guidance" (*hidāyah*) to unlock this verse. There is the guidance of invitation and instruction — the sending of messengers, books, reason, and conscience, which God gives to every soul that seeks it: "And We showed him the two paths" (90:10); "We guided him to the way, whether he be grateful or ungrateful" (76:3); "Truly this is a reminder; so let whosoever will, take a path unto his Lord" (73:19). And there is the guidance of success and enablement (*hidāyat al-tawfīq*) — the opening of the heart that follows upon sincere seeking: "And those who strive in Us, We shall surely guide them upon Our ways" (29:69); "God guides unto Him whosoever turns in repentance" (13:27). The first is given to all; the second follows the creature's own turning. The verse's "had We willed, We would have given every soul its guidance" refers, on this reading, to the second kind — the guidance that would have been belief by force, which God did not will, because He willed instead to create beings whose faith would be their own act, achieved against the possibility of disbelief. The Qur'an states the wisdom of this choice in the verse of the two communities: "And if thy Lord had willed, He would have made mankind one community; but they cease not to differ, save those upon whom thy Lord has mercy — and for that He created them" (11:118–119). "For that He created them": the capacity to differ, to choose, to believe or refuse, is woven into the purpose of the creation of morally responsible beings; a world in which no one could refuse God would be a world in which no one could choose God, and the whole drama of faith, trial, and return — the drama this sūrah has been narrating — would be void.

**The Word That Has Come Due**

The second half of the verse states the other side of the decree: *wa lākin ḥaqqa al-qawlu minnī la-amlaʾanna jahannama mina al-jinni wa al-nāsi ajmaʿīn* — "But the Word from Me comes due: 'I shall surely fill Hell with jinn and men all together!'" The "Word" (*al-qawl*) is the sentence of justice that God has passed upon unrepentant evil, and the verb *ḥaqqa* — "comes due," "is realized," "is confirmed" — indicates that this sentence is not an afterthought but a settled decree, spoken before the world and executed at its end. The same Word appears in the same connection in three other places: "And the Word of thy Lord is fulfilled: 'I shall surely fill Hell with jinn and men together'" (11:119); "Iblīs said, '...I shall surely fill Hell with you and whosoever follows you among them, all together'" (38:85) — where the decree is quoted by Iblīs himself as the destination of his followers; and the great verse of the faculties, "We have surely created for Hell many of the jinn and mankind; they have hearts wherewith they understand not, and eyes wherewith they see not, and ears wherewith they hear not — they are like cattle; nay, they are further astray" (7:179). The commentators were careful to explain that the decree is not the cause of their evil but the verdict upon it: God, Whose knowledge encompasses all that will occur, knew from before creation which souls would, by their own choices, end in the Fire, and His Word names the outcome of their freedom — just as a judge's sentence "comes due" not because the judge caused the crime but because the crime, once committed, requires the sentence. The verse thus preserves both truths at once, and the whole Qur'anic doctrine of destiny (*qadar*) is an attempt to hold them together: nothing occurs outside God's will and knowledge — "no calamity strikes the earth or your selves but it is in a Book before We bring it into being" (57:22) — and yet the human being is genuinely responsible, choosing, and accountable, for the decree is woven from the divine knowledge of choices that are truly his own. The great theologians of Islam — al-Ashʿarī, al-Māturīdī, and the scholars of the salaf before them — all built their systems on the twin affirmations this verse requires: the absoluteness of the divine will and the reality of human agency, with the eternal decree understood as knowledge and justice rather than as compulsion.

**The Consolation Hidden in the Decree**

Read in its context, the verse is one of the most comforting sentences in the Makkan revelation, and its comfort is addressed first to the Prophet himself. The Makkan period was a long ordeal of rejection: decade after decade, the Prophet ﷺ called the Quraysh to the worship of the One God, and decade after decade most of them refused, mocked, and persecuted. The Qur'an records the toll the ordeal took on him: "Perhaps thou wouldst destroy thyself with grief because they do not believe" (26:3), and "So do not let thy soul expire in regrets over them" (35:8). This verse addresses that grief directly: the persistence of denial is not a failure of the caller or an accident of history; it is within the decree of the One Who knows every soul and has written the end of every road. The Prophet's duty is the duty named in verse 3 of this sūrah — to warn, to remind, to invite — and the results belong to God. The same consolation extends to every believer who carries the burden of calling others to good — the parent whose child has turned away, the preacher whose congregation sleeps, the friend whose friend has chosen the wrong road: the guidance of souls is not in the caller's hands, and the caller is not judged by the response of the called but by the sincerity of the call. The believer warns and then leaves the outcome to the One Who has already written it, and the verse's decree, which the fatalist reads as a sentence of despair, is read by the caller as a release from an impossible burden: you are not required to succeed in converting the world; you are required to deliver the message.

**No Excuse, No Despair: The Two Edges of the Verse**

The verse cuts in two directions, and both are necessary. Against the disbeliever who would hide behind the decree — "if God had guided me, I would have believed" — the verse offers no shelter, because the context has already shown the truth: the warner came, the signs were shown, the faculties were given, and the choice was made (vv. 3, 9–10). The decree of which the verse speaks is the decree that names the outcome of that choice; it does not precede the choice as its cause but follows it as its record, and the Qur'an dismisses the excuse with contempt in the scene of the Hereafter: "Those who ascribe partners unto God will say, 'Had God willed, we would not have ascribed partners, nor would our fathers'" — and the answer is, "Thus did those before them deny, until they tasted Our might. Say: 'Have you any knowledge, that you may bring it forth for us?'" (6:148). Against the believer who would despair over his own past — over sins committed, time wasted, guidance refused for years — the verse offers the other edge of the same doctrine: if the decree of evil is real but never an excuse, the decree of good is real and never out of reach, for the same God Who "gives every soul its guidance" when He wills it has willed to guide "whosoever turns to Him in repentance" (13:27), and His mercy is decreed to encompass what His justice decreed to exclude: "My Mercy encompasses all things" (7:156). The Prophet ﷺ distilled the practical meaning of living under the decree in the advice he gave to the young Ibn ʿAbbās, which the scholars have always read alongside this verse:

> "Know that if the whole nation gathered together to benefit you with something, they would benefit you only with something God had already written for you; and if they gathered together to harm you with something, they would harm you only with something God had already written against you. The pens have been lifted, and the pages have dried." (al-Tirmidhī 2516; from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The pens have been lifted and the pages have dried — not because the future is a prison but because it is already known, already measured, already just. The believer's freedom, on the Qur'anic view, is real within the decree: he chooses, and his choice is written; he strives, and his striving is rewarded; he repents, and his repentance is met. And the verse that seems, at first reading, to close every door — "I shall surely fill Hell with jinn and men all together" — is the very verse that opens the only door that matters: the door of taking the decree seriously enough to choose, now, the road that the decree has written for those who choose it.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:14

> **So taste [the punishment] for having forgotten the meeting with this your Day! Verily, We have forgotten you! And taste the punishment everlasting for that which you used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address That Greets the Deniers**

The scene of the bowed heads in verse 12 now receives its verdict: *fa-dhūqū bi-mā nasītum liqāʾa yawmikum hādhā* — "So taste [the punishment] for having forgotten the meeting with this your Day!" The imperative *dhūqū* — "taste!" — is the Qur'an's characteristic verb for the beginning of punishment, and it is always spoken in a tone of terrible irony: the deniers are invited to sample, at last, the reality they spent their lives denying. The commentators noted that the address is spoken either by God directly or by the angels who serve as the keepers of Hell as the deniers are driven into it (Ibn al-Jawzī); the Qur'an elsewhere records the same formula on the same lips: "Taste the punishment for having disbelieved" (3:106); "taste the punishment for having denied" (6:30); and the deniers of the previous scriptures were told, "Taste! You are the mighty, the honored one — truly this is that which you used to mock" (44:49–50). The reason given for the tasting is stated with precision: *bi-mā nasītum liqāʾa yawmikum hādhā* — "for having forgotten the meeting with this your Day." The Day is called "this your Day" — as though it belonged to them, which in a terrible sense it does: it is the day their deeds caught up with them, the appointment they had made for themselves by every act of denial. The verse's indictment is not that they disbelieved in the Day with intellectual conviction but that they *forgot* it — *nasītum* — and the Qur'an's use of "forgetting" here is deliberate and deep: it covers not only the open denial of the mockers but the far more common condition of the heedless, the busy, the distracted — those who do not exactly deny the Hereafter but who live as though it did not exist, and whose lives are organized exactly as they would be organized if it did not exist. The punishment is for the forgetting itself, because the forgetting was the root of everything else: the man who forgets the meeting forgets the reckoning, and the man who forgets the reckoning forgets the restraint, and the man who forgets the restraint does what he pleases, and what he pleases is what this verse punishes.

**"We Have Forgotten You": The Justice of Abandonment**

The second clause delivers the most chilling sentence in the verse: *innā nasīnākum* — "Verily, We have forgotten you!" The early commentators confronted at once the theological difficulty: how can God, Who forgets nothing and Whose knowledge encompasses all things, be said to forget? Their answers define the exact meaning of the sentence. God does not forget in the human sense — the Qur'an affirms of Him, "Thy Lord does not forget" (19:64), and the most beautiful of the divine replies to Moses includes, "My Lord neither errs nor forgets" (20:52). The "forgetting" attributed to God here is therefore not a lapse in knowledge but a verdict of justice: it means being cast out of God's care, being left to the consequences of one's own choices, being abandoned — as al-Bayḍāwī explained — to the very punishment one chose, with the doors of mercy closed. The Qur'an states the same reciprocal abandonment in several places, each time with the same grammatical mirror: "And it will be said, 'Today We forget you just as you forgot the meeting with this your Day; your refuge is the Fire, and you have no helpers'" (45:34); "Those who took their religion as diversion and play, and whom the life of this world deluded — today We forget them just as they forgot the meeting of this their Day" (7:51); "They forgot God, so He forgot them" (9:67); and the striking personal version in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā: "[God] will say, 'Thus did Our signs come unto you, and you forgot them; and thus today you are forgotten'" (20:126). The pattern is the Qur'an's own definition of divine retribution: punishment is the deed returned to its doer — the mirror held up — and the "forgetting" of God is the final, terrible form of being left alone: the soul that lived without God is, in the end, left without God, and the loneliness of the Fire is the loneliness of a creature who has become, by his own lifelong choice, a stranger to mercy. The commentators summarized it in a phrase: God's "forgetting" is the removal of His protective care, so that the forgotten soul is handed over entirely to what it has earned.

**The Punishment Everlasting: The Wages of the Deeds**

The verse's third clause completes the sentence with its most sobering word: *wa dhūqū ʿadhāba al-khuldi bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn* — "And taste the punishment everlasting for that which you used to do." The word *al-khuld* means eternity, endless duration, and the Qur'an applies it to the punishment of those who persist in denial and to the reward of the righteous alike — both are *khuld*, endless, because both are the final outcomes of a life that has reached its settled character. The verse is careful to state the basis of the everlasting punishment: *bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn* — "for that which you used to do" — the deeds of the past, the accumulated choices, the "used to" (*kuntum*) marking the closed ledger of a life that will not be reopened. The proportionality is exact, and the commentators drew it out: the deeds were done in time, but their weight is measured for eternity because they shaped the soul into a permanent direction; a life spent orienting itself away from God ends as a soul oriented away from God, and the Fire is the environment of that orientation. The verse thus answers the question that the modern mind always puts to the doctrine of eternal punishment — how can finite sins merit infinite punishment? — with the Qur'an's own logic: the punishment is not a fine calculated from the number of sins but the consummation of a relationship; the soul that spent its whole existence refusing the meeting with God is given, eternally, the state of one who does not meet Him. The eternity is the eternity of a settled choice, not the arithmetic of a debt.

**Remembering the Day: The Discipline the Verse Commands**

Every clause of the verse is built on the contrast between forgetting and remembering, and the whole of it is, in effect, a command to the living reader: do not be among those who forget. The Qur'an's own name for the remedy is *dhikr* — remembrance — and the specific remembrance this verse enjoins is the remembrance of the Day: "O you who believe! Be mindful of God, and let every soul consider what it has sent forth for tomorrow" (59:18); "And remember the Day when you shall meet Him" (2:203, in the aftermath of the ḥajj); "Truly in the Messenger of God you have a beautiful example for whoever hopes for God and the Last Day, and remembers God much" (33:21). The classical scholars built upon such verses the entire discipline of *muḥāsabah* — self-accounting — the practice of calling oneself to account before the Day of Account, of reviewing the day's deeds at its close as the Day's deeds will be reviewed at the close of life. The early Muslims practiced it with an intensity that later generations found almost unbearable to read about: ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is reported to have said, "Call yourselves to account before you are called to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed, for the reckoning will be easier upon you tomorrow if you reckon with yourselves today." The verse of this sūrah stands behind that whole tradition: the way to avoid the "taste" of the forgotten meeting is to keep the meeting from being forgotten — to remember it daily, in the prayer, in the night vigil, in the charity given for the sake of the Day, until the Day, instead of arriving as a stranger and an enemy, arrives as the appointment one has been preparing for all along. And the Prophet ﷺ taught that the first station of that preparation is the grave itself: "The grave is the first stage of the Hereafter. If one is saved from it, what follows is easier for him; and if one is not saved from it, what follows is harder" (al-Tirmidhī; from ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān). The believer who remembers the meeting prepares for the grave; the one who forgets the meeting is preparing, without knowing it, for the tasting.

**The Age of Organized Forgetting**

The verse speaks to the modern condition with a force its first hearers could not have fully anticipated, for the modern world has industrialized the very forgetting the verse punishes. The technologies of distraction — the endless feed, the twenty-four-hour entertainment cycle, the news that floods and is instantly replaced — are, in their aggregate effect, machines for the prevention of remembrance: they ensure that the human mind is never empty enough to hear the question it most needs to hear, never quiet enough to remember the meeting it is traveling toward. The verse's diagnosis — "you forgot the meeting with this your Day" — describes with precision the condition of a civilization that has not so much disbelieved in death and judgment as drowned them out; the forgetting is not the dramatic denial of the mocker but the ambient amnesia of the distracted, and the Qur'an treats the two as the same disease with the same end. The remedy the verse prescribes is correspondingly simple and correspondingly difficult in the age of organized distraction: deliberate remembrance — the five daily prayers that interrupt the flow and name the Day, the weekly congregation that gathers in remembrance, the nightly review of the self before sleep, the periodic visitation of graves that the Prophet ﷺ encouraged precisely because "it reminds of the Hereafter." Against the machines of forgetting, the believer's whole life of worship is, in the end, a program of remembering — and this verse states both the stakes and the promise of that program: those who remember the meeting are not forgotten by the One Who does not forget, and the taste that awaits the heedless is never tasted by those who kept the Day in mind.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:15

> **Truly they believe in Our signs who when reminded of them fall prostrate and hymn the praise of their Lord and do not wax arrogant,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Gives the Chapter Its Name**

With verse 15 the sūrah reaches its center of gravity — the verse from which the whole chapter takes its name, and the verse at which the recitation of this sūrah traditionally pauses for a prostration: *innamā yuʾminu bi-āyātinā alladhīna idhā dhukkirū bihā kharru sujjadan* — "Truly they believe in Our signs who, when reminded of them, fall prostrate." The sentence opens with the restrictive particle *innamā* — "only," "truly only" — which gives the verse the force of a definition: the real believers in Our signs are precisely those of whom this behavior is true. The "signs" (*āyāt*) are understood by the commentators in the widest sense the word carries in the Qur'an: the verses of the Qur'an (al-Ājurrī, Ibn al-Jawzī), all the revelations sent to the prophets, the miracles that confirmed them, and the countless signs of God in the created world — the heavens, the earth, the rain, the crops, the order of day and night. To believe in the signs, on this verse's definition, is not merely to assent to their truth with the mind; it is to be moved by them in the body and the soul — and the verse names the movement: *kharru sujjadan*, "they fall down prostrating." The verb *kharra* means to fall, to collapse, to drop down, and it describes an involuntary-seeming response: when the reminder comes, the believer does not first debate, measure, or negotiate; the believer falls. The description matches the Qur'an's portrait of the sincere throughout the Book: those who were given knowledge before the Qur'an, "when it is recited unto them, fall down on their faces, prostrating" (17:107); the prophets of old, of whom the sūrah of Mary says, "when the verses of the Compassionate were recited unto them, they fell prostrating and weeping" (19:58); and the people described in the sūrah of the Splitter, of whom the Qur'an asks in reproach, "And when the Qur'an is recited unto them, do they not prostrate?" (84:21). The prostration is presented, across all these verses, as the natural reflex of genuine faith: the soul that recognizes its Lord in His signs cannot keep its body standing.

**Prostration as the Door of Faith and the Test of the Heart**

The choice of prostration as the defining gesture of the believer is laden with meaning, for the Qur'an's own narrative makes prostration the precise act by which the creature is distinguished from the rebel. When God created Adam and commanded the angels to prostrate to him, all prostrated except Iblīs: "He refused and waxed arrogant, and was among the disbelievers" (2:34); and when God asked him, "What prevented you from prostrating to that which I created with My two hands? Are you too proud, or were you among the exalted?" (38:75), the answer was pride — the refusal to bow. From that first refusal, prostration in the Qur'anic narrative carries the whole weight of the creature's choice: to prostrate is to accept that one is a creature; to refuse is to claim, in the manner of Iblīs, a rank that justifies standing. The believers of this verse are defined, negatively as well as positively, against that archetype: *wa hum lā yastakbirūn* — "and they do not wax arrogant." Their prostration is not a gesture performed and then contradicted by a proud heart; the verse adds the negative clause precisely to exclude the hypocrisy of the posture without the virtue — the prostration of the body concealing the arrogance of the soul. The Prophet ﷺ defined arrogance in terms that make the connection exact: *"Arrogance is rejecting the truth and looking down on people"* (Muslim 91). The believer described in this verse does neither: when the truth arrives — in the form of a verse recited, a sign witnessed, a reminder given — he does not reject it, and when he falls prostrate, he does so without looking down on anyone, for the prostration has already leveled him with everyone. The scholars drew from the verse the lesson that humility is the doorway of faith in both directions: humility opens the heart to receive the truth, and the truth, once received, deepens the humility — so that the believer is described by a circle: signs remind him, he prostrates, the prostration humbles him, and the humbled heart is ready for the next sign.

**Hymning the Praise of the Lord: The Prostration That Rises into Doxology**

The second clause of the verse completes the movement: *wa sabbahū bi-ḥamdi rabbihim* — "and hymn the praise of their Lord." The verb *sabbahū* is from the root *s-b-ḥ*, whose basic meaning is to be free of imperfection and, derivatively, to declare that freedom — to glorify, to hymn; and the phrase "hymn the praise of" (*bi-ḥamd*) joins the glorification to the praise: the believer declares his Lord free of every deficiency while praising Him for every perfection. The sequence is the full cycle of worship: the fall into prostration (humility, creatureliness, acknowledgment), and the rising into praise (gratitude, love, exultation). The Qur'an teaches that this double movement is the very music of the universe: the seven heavens and the earth and all within them hymn His praise (17:44); everything in the heavens and the earth glorifies God (57:1; 59:1; 61:1; 62:1; 64:1) — though, as the Qur'an adds, "you understand not their glorification" (17:44). The believer who prostrates and then praises is joining a chorus that has never been silent since creation: the angels who "hymn the praise of their Lord and seek forgiveness for those on earth" (42:5), the thunder that "hymns His praise" (13:13), the mountains that sang the glorification with David (34:10; 38:18). And the same double movement is inscribed in the daily prayer, where the worshipper, in each cycle, bends and says "Glory be to my Lord, the Magnificent," falls and says "Glory be to my Lord, the Most High," and rises to praise — the body's fall and the tongue's praise woven together as the basic unit of Islamic worship. This verse's believers are thus described in the language of the prayer itself: the people who, whenever the signs are mentioned, are found in the posture that the prayer repeats dozens of times a day.

**The Recitation Prosthree at the Verse of the Prostration**

Because this verse is counted among the fifteen verses of the Qur'an after which prostration is prescribed — the verses of prostration (*āyāt al-sajdah*) scattered through the Book — the verse has a liturgical life that most verses do not possess. When the believer recites it, whether in the prayer or outside it, the recitation itself enacts the verse's content: the reader falls prostrate as the verse describes the believers falling, and in that act the verse's meaning is not merely understood but performed. The practice goes back to the Prophet ﷺ himself: it is recorded that he recited a sūrah containing a verse of prostration and prostrated, and his Companions prostrated with him (al-Bukhārī, from Ibn ʿUmar), and that when he recited the verse of prostration in the course of his recitation, he would say the takbīr and prostrate. The scholars of the schools of law differ on the ruling — the Ḥanafīs holding the recitation prostration to be obligatory upon the reciter under certain conditions, and the Shāfiʿīs, Mālikīs, and Ḥanbalīs holding it to be a strongly affirmed Sunnah — but all are agreed on its reality and its reward, and the report of the prostration's effect on the unseen enemy has already been cited in the introduction to this sūrah: when the son of Adam recites a verse of prostration and prostrates, Satan withdraws weeping, for the son of Adam was commanded to prostrate and obeyed, while he was commanded and refused (Muslim 81). The listener who hears the verse recited is included in the practice: the Sunnah applies to the hearer as well as the reciter, so that the verse creates, at the moment of its recitation, a small congregation of the prostrating — the reciter, the hearers, and, in the belief of the tradition, the unseen witnesses who rejoice at the humility of the creature. The verse thus functions as a standing invitation to experience, in the recitation of this very sūrah, the reality the sūrah describes: a people who, when the signs are reminded to them, fall prostrate.

**Knowledge Through the Knees: Why the Body Must Bow Before the Mind Assents**

One further dimension of the verse has exercised the spiritual commentators in every generation: the order it establishes between humility and understanding. The verse does not say that the believers first understand the signs completely and then prostrate out of gratitude; it says that when they are reminded, they prostrate — and the prostration, in the Qur'an's epistemology, is itself a mode of knowing. The proud mind that insists on mastering every truth before bending to any of it never arrives at the truths that can only be known from the posture of submission; the deepest realities — the reality of God, of the soul, of the Day — are not objects the creature stands over and inspects but presences the creature receives on his knees. The Qur'an makes the same point about the prayer in general: "Seek help in patience and prayer; and truly it is hard, save for the humble" (2:45) — the prayer is hard for the proud precisely because it requires the surrender they cannot perform. And the sūrah of the Clot, the first revelation, links knowledge to prostration in its very last verse: "Nay! Do not obey him, but prostrate and draw nigh" (96:19) — the prostration is the drawing nigh. In an intellectual culture that has often equated dignity with the refusal to bow — to any authority, any revelation, any truth not of one's own making — this verse stands as the Qur'an's quiet insistence that there is one bowing that ennobles rather than degrades: the bowing of the creature to the Creator, which does not diminish the one who performs it but completes him. The believers of the verse are not credulous or servile; they are described two verses later as the people whose sides shun their beds for prayer and whose hands open in charity — the most active, generous, and wakeful people in the sūrah. Their prostration is not the collapse of the intellect but its culmination: they have understood enough to know that before the Lord of the worlds, the only posture of understanding is the prostration.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:16

> **whose sides shun [their] beds, calling upon their Lord out of fear and hope, and who spend from that which We have provided them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sides That Shun the Beds**

Verse 15 defined the believers by their prostration; verse 16 now completes the portrait by their nights: *tatajāfā junūbuhum ʿani al-maḍājiʿ* — "whose sides shun [their] beds." The verb *tatajāfā* describes the act of lifting away, drawing aside, holding oneself apart; the *junūb* are the sides or flanks of the body; and the *maḍājiʿ* are the places of sleeping, the beds. The picture is physical and precise: the bed is spread, the body is tired, the night is calling the day's workers to rest — and the believer's sides lift away from the bed, the body rising against its own exhaustion for the sake of its Lord. The early community treasured this image so much that the whole sūrah came to be known by it — Sūrat al-Maḍājiʿ, "the Sūrah of the Beds," as the introduction to this commentary has noted. The reports preserved about the occasion of the verse indicate that some Companions understood it as referring to those who prayed between the sunset prayer (maghrib) and the night prayer (ʿishāʾ), while others understood it as referring to the night vigil (tahajjud) in the depths of the night; and the commentators, observing that the verse speaks of shunning the beds — which implies the time when beds are normally occupied — preferred the second interpretation: the believers described here are those who rise from their sleep in the latter part of the night to stand before their Lord. The Qur'an describes the same people elsewhere with the same emphasis on their nights: "They used to sleep but little of the night, and at dawn they would seek forgiveness" (51:17–18); "And those who pass the night prostrating and standing before their Lord" (25:64); and the believers of the sūrah of Āl ʿImrān who "call upon their Lord in fear and hope and spend of that which We have provided them" (3:16). The night prayer is presented in all these verses as the signature of the sincere: the worship performed when no one sees, when the world sleeps, when the only witness is God.

**Calling Upon Their Lord in Fear and Hope**

The verse then names what the wakeful believers do with their risen nights: *yadʿūna rabbahum khawfan wa ṭamaʿan* — "calling upon their Lord out of fear and hope." The *duʿā* — the calling, the supplication — is the speech of the night: the believer who has left his bed does not leave it to perform a silent ritual but to speak, to ask, to plead, to converse with his Lord. And the emotional register of the calling is defined by the pair *khawfan wa ṭamaʿan* — "fear and hope" — the two wings on which, in the Islamic spiritual tradition, the soul flies to God. The commentators gave the pair its basic meanings: fear of punishment and hope of mercy (Ibn al-Jawzī), so that the believer's night prayer is poised between the terror of the reckoning and the expectation of the pardon. The spiritual commentators deepened the meanings: al-Qushayrī and al-Sulamī, cited in the source, read the fear as the fear of being separated from God and the hope as the hope of meeting Him — the fear and hope not of the slave before the judge only, but of the lover before the beloved, afraid of the veil and hoping for the unveiling. Both readings are true, and together they describe the exact psychological balance the Qur'an recommends everywhere: "Call upon Him in fear and hope; truly the Mercy of God is near to the doers of good" (7:56); "They hope for His Mercy and fear His punishment; truly the punishment of thy Lord is ever to beware of" (17:57). The balance is important because each emotion, alone, corrupts: fear without hope drives the soul to despair of God's mercy — and the Qur'an condemns despair as severely as it condemns false security (12:87: "do not despair of the Spirit of God"); hope without fear drives the soul to presumption — and the Qur'an warns, "Did they feel secure from the plotting of God? None feels secure from the plotting of God save the people who are lost" (7:99). The believers of this verse hold both: they hope enough to keep calling, and they fear enough to keep striving.

**The Night as the Time of Sincerity**

The verse's description of the believers calling upon their Lord in the night carries an implicit argument about sincerity, and the classical commentators drew it out. The worship of the day is public and social: the congregational prayers, the gatherings of knowledge, the charity given in sight of others. The worship of the night is invisible: no one sees the sleeper rise, no one counts the rakʿahs, no one hears the supplication except the One to Whom it is addressed. The Qur'an therefore treats the night vigil as the test and proof of the heart's sincerity — the worship that could not be performed for show, because there was no one to show: "Surely the vigil of the night is when impressions are strongest and speech is most upright" (73:6). The believer who prays in the depths of the night when no creature watches demonstrates that his worship is for God and not for the eyes of men; and the same sincerity carries over into the rest of his life. The reports of the Prophet's own nights set the standard the verse describes. ʿĀʾishah, asked about the Prophet's night prayer, answered that he prayed until his feet became swollen, and when she asked why he labored so, since God had forgiven him all his sins, he replied: "Shall I not be a grateful servant?" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim). And she described his sleep with the phrase preserved in the source: "My eyes sleep, but my heart does not sleep" — the wakefulness of the heart continuing even through the sleep of the body, so that the Prophet's ﷺ whole existence, waking and sleeping, was turned toward his Lord.

**Spending From What We Have Provided: The Daytime Half of the Portrait**

The verse's third clause completes the believer's portrait by turning from the night to the day: *wa mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn* — "and who spend from that which We have provided them." The believers of the sūrah are thus described by a perfect diptych: by night, their sides shun their beds and their tongues call upon their Lord; by day, their hands open in spending. The worship of the night flows outward into the charity of the day: the soul that has stood before God in the small hours sees the creatures of God with different eyes when the sun rises, and the hand that was raised in supplication opens in giving. The "spending" (*infaq*) is understood by most commentators to include both the obligatory alms and the voluntary charity beyond it (the source notes that the reference to spending beyond the required zakāh is the natural reading here, since the verse describes the general character of the believers rather than a specific obligation). The Qur'an extols this pairing of prayer and spending in verse after verse: the believers of the opening of al-Baqarah "perform the prayer and spend of that which We have provided them" (2:3); the believers of al-Anfāl "establish the prayer and spend of that which We have provided them" (8:3); and the most moving of the exhortations to spending links it to the very deadline this sūrah has been describing — the meeting with the Day: "And spend of that which We have provided you before death comes unto one of you and he says, 'My Lord, wouldst Thou grant me reprieve until a term nigh, that I might give charity and be among the righteous!' And God will not grant reprieve to a soul when its term has come" (63:10–11; cf. 2:254; 14:44). The spending is the daytime proof of the night's sincerity: the man who rises in the darkness to ask of God rises in the daylight to give to God's creatures, and the two movements are one movement — the movement of the soul that has understood that everything it "owns" is, as the verse says, "that which We have provided them," a trust from the Provider to be returned through His servants.

**Al-Maybudī: Sleeplessness as the Evidence of Love**

The spiritual commentators found in the verse's image of the abandoned bed a whole theology of love. Al-Maybudī, whose commentary on this sūrah's opening verse has already been cited, wrote on this verse with the characteristic ardor of the Persian school of Qur'anic commentary:

> "Sleeplessness and wakefulness at night are the cause of nearness to the Truth and evidence of the perfection of love, for the first degree of love is seeking conformity, and the attribute of the Truth is that neither slumber overtakes Him nor sleep [2:255]."

The reasoning is subtle and beautiful. God describes Himself in the Pedestal Verse as the One Whom "neither slumber overtakes nor sleep"; the lover seeks conformity with the beloved; therefore the believer who loves God trains himself to wake when the Beloved wakes — to keep the vigil that answers the divine wakefulness. The night prayer is, on this reading, not primarily a discipline of fear or a calculation of reward but an expression of love: the lover leaves his bed because the One he loves does not sleep, and the night is the time of intimacy when the world's noise is stilled and the conversation can begin. The same tradition preserved the Prophet's own description of his nights as continuous remembrance — "My eyes sleep, but my heart waketh" — and the scholars of the heart added that the wakefulness most pleasing to God is not the mere refusal of sleep but the presence of the heart: the night prayer of the heedless body is sleep in another form, while the night prayer of the present heart is wakefulness even in repose.

**The Night Vigil in the Age of Exhaustion**

The verse closes its description of the believers with an image that every age must translate into its own circumstances, and the translation is harder in the modern age than in most. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the last third of every night is the hour of divine nearness, when the Lord descends to the lowest heaven and asks: *"Who calls upon Me, that I may answer him? Who asks of Me, that I may give him? Who seeks My forgiveness, that I may forgive him?"* (al-Bukhārī 1145; Muslim 758). The believers of this verse are those who answer that call — who rise in the hour of the divine question and present themselves. But the modern believer rises to the night vigil against the current of a civilization that has colonized the night: the screens that glow until the small hours, the work schedules and entertainment cycles that have made the last third of the night the most distracted part of the day rather than the most devoted. The verse's counsel is correspondingly countercultural: reclaim the night for the Lord of the night; let the sides shun the bed — or even a part of the bed, for the tradition encourages even two rakʿahs before dawn — and let the supplication of the last third be the believer's answer to the divine question. The reward promised to the practice is stated in the Qur'an itself in the sūrah of the Night Journey, where the night vigil is described as the path to the highest station: "And from the night, keep vigil in it as an extra worship for thee; perhaps thy Lord will raise thee to a praiseworthy station" (17:79) — the station of intercession that the commentators identified with the Prophet's ﷺ greatest honor on the Day of Resurrection. The believer who rises in the night is not losing sleep; he is investing it in the only appointment that cannot be rescheduled, calling upon his Lord in fear and hope, and spending from what his Lord has provided — the complete portrait of the believer that this verse and the next, which promises the reward "no soul knows," paint together.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:17

> **No soul knows what comfort is kept hidden for it as a recompense for that which they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reward That No Soul Knows**

The portrait of the believers begun in verse 15 now receives its crown: *fa-lā taʿlamu nafsun mā ukhfiya lahum min qurrati aʿyun* — "No soul knows what comfort is kept hidden for it." The verse turns from the believers' labor to their wage, and it describes the wage in terms deliberately calculated to exceed every power of anticipation: *no soul knows*. The concealment is total — the reward is kept hidden (*ukhfiya*) not only from the disbelievers and the heedless but from the believers themselves, and, in the understanding of many commentators, from the angels as well; no created intellect has been given the measure of what awaits the people of the night vigil. The verse's own reason is stated at its end: *jazāʾan bi-mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — "as a recompense for that which they used to do." The Arabic sentence is open to a beautiful double reading that the commentators preserved: the recompense is hidden *because of* what they used to do — because their deeds themselves were hidden. The believers of the previous verse rose from their beds when no one saw them, prayed when no eye watched, spent in charity when no one counted; their deeds were concealed from every witness but God — and the Qur'an here announces that their wage has been concealed with equal care: a hidden deed is met by a hidden reward, a secret devotion by a secret delight. The principle is one of the deepest in the Qur'an's moral economy, and it runs through the whole of the Book: what is done for display is paid in the coin of display — the admiration of the onlookers, which ends when the onlookers turn away — while what is done for God alone is banked with God alone, in a treasury that no eye has inventoried: "So let him who hopes for the meeting with his Lord work righteous deeds, and not associate anyone in the worship of his Lord" (18:110).

**The Coolness of the Eyes: An Idiom of Perfect Joy**

The word the verse uses for the hidden comfort is *qurratu aʿyun* — "a cooling of eyes," one of the most expressive idioms in the Arabic language. The tears of grief and distress, in the physiology of the ancient Arabs, were hot tears that burned the eye; the relief of joy and reunion brought the coolness that calmed it. To say that something is a *qurratu ʿayn* for a person is to say that it is the thing whose arrival cools the eye of its burning — the sight that ends the weeping, the news that stills the ache, the presence that completes the heart. The Qur'an uses the idiom sparingly and always at moments of high emotion: the righteous pray, "Our Lord! Grant us from our spouses and our offspring a cooling of the eyes, and make us a leader for the reverent" (25:74); the wife of Pharaoh, receiving the infant Moses from the river, says to her husband, "A cooling of the eyes for me and for thee! Slay him not" (28:9); and Moses' mother, promised the return of her child, is told, "We shall return him to thee, and make him among the messengers" — so that "her eye might be cooled" (20:40). In every occurrence, the cooling of the eye is the joy of reunion with the beloved. And it is precisely this that led many of the commentators — including those cited in the source — to understand the hidden comfort of this verse as culminating in the greatest of all reunions: the vision of God. Al-Ājurrī and others read the "cooling of the eyes" here as an allusion to the vision of the Lord in the Hereafter, and al-Qurṭubī preserved the principle in a sentence of perfect clarity: "The eye is only cooled by the vision of the one it loves." The Qur'an describes the people of the Garden as granted exactly that: "Faces on that Day shall be radiant, looking upon their Lord" (75:22–23); and the greatest of the rewards named in the sūrah of Yūnus — "For those who do good is the most beautiful [reward], and more" (10:26) — was explained by the Prophet ﷺ himself, in the authentic report, as the "more" being the vision of the Lord, when the veil is lifted and the people of the Garden see the Face of the One they loved without seeing throughout their earthly lives. If the hidden comfort of this verse includes that vision — as the weight of the commentary tradition holds — then the verse's claim that "no soul knows" is exact in a sense beyond all metaphor: no soul that has not seen can imagine the seeing, and no soul that has seen can describe it to one who has not.

**The Hadith of the Hidden Preparation**

The most famous commentary on this verse is not the work of a commentator at all but the word of God reported by the Prophet ﷺ in the hadith qudsī — the saying in which the Prophet conveyed the speech of God outside the Qur'an. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from Abū Hurayrah:

> "God says: 'I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen and no ear has heard, and what has not occurred to the heart of any human being.'" Then the Prophet ﷺ said: "Recite, if you will: *No soul knows what comfort is kept hidden for it as a recompense for that which they used to do.*" (al-Bukhārī 4779; Muslim 2824)

The hadith is the verse's own seal. The reward exceeds the three channels by which human beings can know anything: the eye (which knows by seeing), the ear (which knows by hearing), and the heart (which knows by imagining and reasoning). Everything a human being has ever desired, conceived, or dreamed is assembled from the materials of these three channels — from sights seen, sounds heard, and thoughts compounded — and the hadith announces that the reward prepared for the righteous is assembled from none of them: it is not a greater version of anything known but a reality of a different order altogether. The commentators observed the subtlety of the hadith's placement of the imagination: even the heart, the most creative of the three faculties, "has not occurred to" the reward — meaning that no human being, however vivid his fancy, has ever successfully wished for what God has actually prepared. The believer's best hope is therefore not a specific picture of Paradise but an unlimited trust in the Generosity that prepared it; and the verse's "No soul knows" is transformed from a statement of ignorance into a promise of surprise: the believer is guaranteed that the reality will exceed every expectation he was capable of forming. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, in the same connection, that the least of the people of the Garden in station will be one who is given ten times the like of the world, and that the greatest will be granted what no eye has seen — and the whole scale of the promise rests on the single sentence of this verse.

**Muʿādh ibn Jabal and the Gates of Goodness**

The source commentary preserves a report of exceptional importance for understanding this verse and the one preceding it — the account of the Companion Muʿādh ibn Jabal, who asked the Prophet ﷺ for a deed that would admit him to the Garden and remove him from the Fire. The Prophet's answer first named the pillars of Islam, and then opened the "gates of goodness," reciting verses 16 and 17 of this very sūrah as their scriptural seal:

> "I said: 'O Messenger of God, tell me of an act that will take me into the Garden and keep me away from the Fire.' He replied: 'You have asked me about a significant matter; yet it is easy for him for whom God Almighty makes it easy. You should worship God, associating nothing with Him, perform the prayers, pay alms (zakāh), fast during Ramadan, and make the pilgrimage to the House.' Then he said: 'Shall I not show you the gates of goodness? Fasting, which is a shield; charity, which extinguishes sin as water extinguishes fire; and the prayer of a man in the depths of night.'" Then the Prophet recited: *"whose sides shun [their] beds... No soul knows what comfort is kept hidden for it."* (al-Tirmidhī 2616, from Muʿādh ibn Jabal)

The report is a complete curriculum in one exchange. The "act" that admits to the Garden is not a single deed but a structure — the pillars of Islam that orient the whole life — and the "gates of goodness" within that structure are the supererogatory works that raise the believer higher: the fasting that shields, the charity that extinguishes, and the night prayer that the sūrah itself describes. The Prophet ﷺ then capped the exchange with the sūrah's own promise: the believer who enters through those gates finds a reward that "no soul knows." Muʿādh himself became the living commentary on the exchange: the scholars record that he was among the most devoted of the Companions to the night prayer, and that when he died, those who knew him said that the "prayer of a man in the depths of night" had been his constant companion. The report also explains why this verse, buried in the middle of a Makkan sūrah of warning and argument, was treasured by the early community as one of the most hope-filled verses in the entire Book: it is the verse the Prophet himself chose to recite when a Companion asked for the road to the Garden.

**The Law of Hiddenness in the Spiritual Life**

The verse's principle — a hidden deed is met by a hidden reward — generates a precise discipline that the spiritual tradition of Islam has observed ever since: the protection of the secret between the servant and his Lord. The scholars of the heart taught that the night prayer should be as concealed as possible, that the voluntary charity should seek the left hand that does not know what the right hand gives (to use the imagery of the Gospel the Qur'an's own teaching parallels in 2:271: "If you disclose your charities, that is good; and if you conceal them and give them to the poor, that is better for you, and will acquit you of some of your evil deeds"), and that the believer's most precious works are precisely those that no creature witnessed. The reason is not secrecy for its own sake but sincerity: the deed performed where only God can see is, by that very fact, purified of the subtle poison of display (*riyāʾ*) — the desire to be seen, praised, and remembered — which the Qur'an identifies as a form of hidden association with God (107:4–7; 2:264). The reward of this verse is hidden because it was earned by hiddenness; and the believer who wishes to be among "those who used to do" the deeds that this verse recompenses must learn, in an age of universal display, the ancient art of the concealed good — the prayer before dawn that no one posted about, the charity that no one was told of, the kindness that will be discovered, if ever, only on the Day when the hidden is made manifest (86:9). Al-Tustarī, cited in the source, read the "cooling of their eyes" as the joy of that unveiling:

> "Their eyes [are cooled] at the outward and inward realities that they witness, which are disclosed to them by way of unveiling. Then they behold them and adhere to them, so that their eyes are cooled and their hearts repose in them."

The eyes that wept in the night vigils of the world are cooled at last by the sight of the realities they sought; the hearts that rose from their beds in search of their Lord find in His unveiling the repose they were seeking all along — and the verse's "No soul knows" stands guard over the secret until the moment of the meeting.

**Why the Reward Is Kept Hidden: The Wisdom of Unfinished Anticipation**

One further wisdom in the verse deserves notice: the concealment of the reward is itself a mercy, and the Qur'an conceals other crucial realities for the same reason. The hour of death is concealed so that every day may be lived as though it were the last; the Night of Power is concealed within Ramadan so that every night of the month may be seized in worship; and the reward of the righteous is concealed so that the striving may never be calibrated downward. If the believer knew exactly what awaited him, the knowledge would set a ceiling on his hope and a limit on his effort: he would work precisely to the measure of the known wage and stop. Instead, God has set before the believer an open-ended promise — "No soul knows" — so that the hope itself may be infinite, and the effort that hope feeds may never reach its term. The psychology is exact: the unknown reward converts every act of worship into an act of trust; the believer who rises in the night does not rise for a calculated wage but for a Lord Whose generosity he has learned to trust beyond all calculation. And the verse's final phrase — "as a recompense for that which they used to do" — quietly corrects the arithmetic of the reward itself: the deeds were finite, measured, and few, but the recompense is infinite, unmeasured, and beyond the knowing of any soul — the exchange rate of the divine generosity, which pays the wages of a night's prayer in a currency that has no name in any human economy.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:18

> **Is one who believes like one who is iniquitous? They are not equal.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Carries Its Own Answer**

Verse 18 is one of the shortest verses of the sūrah and one of its sharpest: *afaman kāna muʾminan kaman kāna fāsiqan? Lā yastawūn* — "Is one who believes like one who is iniquitous? They are not equal." The question is rhetorical in the purest sense: it is posed in a form so obviously answerable that the asking itself is the argument, and the verse then states the answer as a flat verdict — *lā yastawūn*, "they are not equal" — without qualification, without exception, and without appeal. The Qur'an poses the same question, in the same form, at the climax of several arguments, and always with the same assumption of self-evidence: "Shall We treat those who believe and work righteous deeds like those who work corruption upon the earth? Shall We treat the reverent like the wicked?" (38:28); "Or do those who commit evil deeds suppose that We shall make them like those who believe and work righteous deeds, equal in their life and their death? Evil is that which they judge!" (45:21); "Shall We make the Muslims like the guilty? What ails you, how do you judge?" (68:35–36); and the companion verse in the sūrah of the Clear Proof: "Shall We treat those who believe and work righteous deeds like those who work corruption upon the earth?" (38:28). The repetition of the question across the Makkan revelation shows how central it was to the Qur'an's moral vision: the denial of the resurrection (vv. 10–12 of this sūrah) was, at bottom, the denial that the believer and the iniquitous are not equal — the denial that there is a final distinction — and the Qur'an returns to the question whenever it confronts that denial.

**Why the Universe Must Distinguish**

The verse rests on a moral axiom that the Qur'an treats as self-evident but that modern culture has made surprisingly difficult to maintain: that justice requires the final outcomes of the believer and the iniquitous to differ. If the believer and the wrongdoer ended in the same place, then the believer's lifelong struggle — the prayers in the night, the charity given, the appetites restrained, the truth spoken at cost — would be, in the final accounting, a stupidity; and the wrongdoer's lifelong indulgence — the oppression, the greed, the mockery of the truth — would be, in the final accounting, a bargain. The Qur'an states the absurdity of such an outcome with complete directness: "Do those who commit evil deeds suppose that We shall make them like those who believe and work righteous deeds, equal in their life and their death? Evil is that which they judge!" (45:21). A universe that ended all lives in the same dust, with the same nothingness, would be a universe in which morality was a practical error and wickedness a practical wisdom — and the Qur'an's insistence on the resurrection is, at its root, an insistence that the moral order of the universe is real, that good and evil are not finally indifferent, that the distinction the believer lives by is the distinction the universe itself will enforce. The verse's "they are not equal" is therefore not a piece of sectarian triumphalism but a statement of cosmic justice: the believer is not equal to the iniquitous because their deeds are not equal, their characters are not equal, their relationships to the truth are not equal — and the God Who created the universe with moral seriousness will not, in the end, treat them as equal. The same axiom underlies the entire structure of the verses that follow: verse 19 assigns the believers their Gardens and verse 20 assigns the iniquitous their Fire, and the two verses are simply the eschatological spelling-out of this verse's "they are not equal."

**The Two Words: Believer and Iniquitous**

The verse's vocabulary rewards close attention. The *muʾmin* — the believer — is named by the word derived from the root *-m-n*, whose meanings cluster around security, trust, and the granting of safety: the believer is the one who has entrusted himself to God, who has found in faith the security that the world cannot give, and whose trust issues in obedience. The *fāsiq* — the iniquitous — is named by the word derived from the root *f-s-q*, whose concrete meaning is to break out, to split away: the same root is used of the date that bursts from its skin when it ripens. The fāsiq is the one who has broken out — out of obedience, out of the covenant, out of the enclosure of God's command — and the Qur'an uses the word for those who, having known the truth, split away from it: the very next verse of the sūrah of al-Baqarah after the description of the believers' guidance says of the fāsiqūn that "they break the covenant of God after its ratification, and sever what God has commanded to be joined, and work corruption upon the earth" (2:27). The two words are thus opposites in their very roots: the believer is the one who has entered into security and trust; the iniquitous is the one who has burst out of every bond. And the verse's question — "Is one who believes like one who is iniquitous?" — is, in the grammar of the two roots, almost a contradiction in terms: it asks whether the one who is anchored can be like the one who has broken loose, whether the one who has found the refuge can be like the one who has abandoned it. The answer is contained in the question, and the verse pronounces it without elaboration: *lā yastawūn* — they are not equal.

**The Context That Gives the Verse Its Edge**

The verse's placement in this sūrah gives it an additional edge that the commentators were careful to draw out. In verse 12, the guilty were shown bending their heads before their Lord and pleading, "Send us back, that we may work righteousness — truly we are certain." The implicit logic of their plea was that, given a second chance, they would do what the believers do — that the difference between them and the believers was only a difference of opportunity, and that with more time they would close the gap. Verse 18 answers the plea's logic: *Is one who believes like one who is iniquitous?* — even now, even with the same world behind them, the believer and the iniquitous are not equal, because the difference between them was never a difference of time but a difference of the heart. The believer believed while belief was a choice, and worked righteousness while working was voluntary; the iniquitous denied while denial was a choice, and now, in the moment of forced sight, claims he would do differently. The verse exposes the claim as self-deception: the believer and the iniquitous are distinguished by what they did with the one life they were given, and no amount of posthumous resolution can retroactively make the iniquitous into a believer. The source commentary draws the further point that verses 18–20, taken together, complete the answer to the disbelievers' request of verse 12: even if the request were granted — even if they were sent back and "worked righteousness" — they would not thereby become the equals of those who believed and worked righteousness in the first place, because the declaration of faith with the tongue has little efficacy if it is not put into practice with the limbs and manifest in the heart. The believer's faith was manifest; the iniquitous's claim is only a word — and the verse's "they are not equal" separates the two with the finality of the truth itself.

**Not Equal in This Life Either**

The verse's verdict — "they are not equal" — refers in the first instance to the final reckoning that verses 19 and 20 will describe, but the commentators noted that it is true in this life as well, in the only comparison that ultimately matters. The believer and the iniquitous may be equal in wealth, in health, in worldly station — the Qur'an is emphatic that the goods of this world are distributed without regard to moral merit, as a test rather than a wage — but they are not equal in the things that constitute the actual quality of a soul: the believer has a direction and the iniquitous is drifting; the believer has a refuge and the iniquitous is exposed; the believer, when calamity strikes, has the One to Whom he turns, and the iniquitous has only the calamity. The Qur'an describes the difference with complete concreteness: "Those who believe and whose hearts are at rest in the remembrance of God — truly in the remembrance of God do hearts find rest" (13:28). The verse of this sūrah is thus a standing invitation to self-examination: on which side of the "not equal" do I actually live? The question is not answered by one's label — Muslim, believer, preacher — but by one's deeds, for the muʾmin of this verse is the muʾmin of verses 15–16, the one who prostrates at the signs and shuns his bed in the night and spends from his provision; and the fāsiq is the one who has broken out of all of that. The verse divides humanity into two, and its "they are not equal" is both a promise and a warning — a promise to every believer who struggles in the dark that his struggle is seen and will be distinguished, and a warning to every iniquitous person who imagines that the final accounting will be a leveling that he is not equal to the believer only in the world's eyes, but equal in the end. The Qur'an says: not equal. And the verses that follow state, in two strokes, exactly how not equal they are.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:19

> **As for those who believe and perform righteous deeds, theirs shall be Gardens of refuge, as a welcome for that which they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gardens of Refuge: The Believer's Final Shelter**

Verse 18's verdict is now spelled out from its positive side: *ammā alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti fa-lahum jannātu al-maʾwā* — "As for those who believe and perform righteous deeds, theirs shall be Gardens of refuge." The name given to the believers' destination is precise: *jannātu al-maʾwā*, the Gardens of Refuge — or, more literally, the Gardens of the Shelter, for the word *maʾwā* comes from the root *-w-y*, which means to shelter, to give refuge, to receive into a place of safety. The same word appears in the Qur'an for the mountain refuge to which the companions of the cave withdrew (18:16), for the refuge sought by Mary (19:18: "I seek refuge in the Compassionate from thee"), and for the final destination of both the blessed and the damned — for the Qur'an says both "their refuge is the Garden" and "their refuge is the Fire," using the same word for both, because both are, in their different ways, the final shelter of those who reach them: the place where the journey ends and the soul is received. The Garden of Refuge is named elsewhere in the Qur'an as well: "Near it is the Garden of Refuge" (53:15), where it stands beside the Lote Tree of the Boundary at the summit of the Prophet's ﷺ ascent, and "Gardens of Eden... a welcome from the Forgiving, the Merciful" (41:32? — actually 41:31-32: "for you therein is whatsoever your souls desire... a welcome (nuzulan) from the Forgiving, the Merciful"). The name carries the whole emotional content of the believer's hope: the Garden is not merely a place of pleasure but a place of safety — the refuge of the soul that spent its earthly life as a stranger and a wayfarer, finally home; the shelter of the servant who sought refuge in God from every evil and now finds the refuge that nothing can breach.

**The Unbroken Pair: Belief and Righteous Deeds**

The verse describes the inhabitants of the Gardens with the formula that runs through the Qur'an like a refrain: *alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* — "those who believe and perform righteous deeds." The pairing is so constant — it appears scores of times across the Book — that its constancy is itself a teaching: in the Qur'an's vocabulary, belief that does not issue in righteous deeds is incomplete, and righteous deeds that do not spring from belief are unanchored. The commentators explained the conjunction with a precise logic: belief is the root and righteous deeds are its fruit; a root without fruit is barren, and fruit without a root does not endure. The deeds are qualified as *al-ṣāliḥāt* — the sound, the wholesome, the well-made — deeds that are not merely performed but performed rightly: with the right intention, in the right manner, for the right end. And the verse that follows the description of the believers in the sūrah of the Clear Proof states the connection as a matter of the soul's interior state: the believers are "those who believe and perform righteous deeds — they are the best of creatures; their reward is with their Lord: Gardens of Eden" (98:7–8). The formula in this verse stands in pointed contrast to the plea of the guilty in verse 12 — "send us back that we may work righteousness" — for the guilty's righteousness was a future plan, a hypothetical, a promise made under duress, while the believers' righteousness was an accomplished fact, a "used to do" — the past tense of a life actually lived. The verse's reward is for what they *used to do*, not for what they might do; the deeds are finished, the ledger is closed, and the welcome is prepared.

**The Welcome: Deeds Turned into Hospitality**

The verse's final phrase is its most beautiful: *nuzulan bi-mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — "as a welcome for that which they used to do." The word *nuzul* means the hospitality prepared for a guest — the reception, the provision, the lodging made ready for an arriving traveler. The Qur'an uses the word with exact consistency: the people of the Garden are told, "Eat and drink in satisfaction for that which you used to do" (77:43), and "this is the Garden which you have been made to inherit for that which you used to do" (43:72), and the believers of the sūrah of the Cave are promised, "Truly those who believe and perform righteous deeds — theirs are the Gardens of Paradise as a welcome (nuzulan)" (18:107). The image in every occurrence is that of the guest and the host: the believer arrives at the Garden like a traveler arriving at the end of a long road, and finds that his arrival has been anticipated — that a welcome has been prepared, a lodging made ready, a table spread — and that the preparation was made on account of the deeds he used to do. The classical commentators drew from the image a double lesson. First, the deeds of the believer are not lost: every prayer, every charity, every night vigil of the previous verses has been collected and converted — by the divine generosity — into the furnishings of his welcome; the believer will meet his own deeds in the Garden, transformed into hospitality. Second, the welcome is a gift, not a wage: the *nuzul* is what a host provides for a guest, and the host provides it out of generosity, not because the guest has earned the right to it. The Qur'an maintains both truths at once: the reward is "for that which they used to do" — the deeds are the occasion, real and necessary — and yet the reward is a welcome, not a payment, because the deeds themselves were enabled by God's own grace (32:16: "from that which We have provided them") and the exchange rate belongs to God alone. The Prophet ﷺ made the same double affirmation when he said: *"No one will enter the Garden by his deeds alone." They said: "Not even you, O Messenger of God?" He said: "Not even me, unless God envelops me in His mercy — with His grace and mercy"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah). The deeds are the road; the mercy is the arrival; and the *nuzul* — the welcome — is the hospitality of the Merciful spread for those who traveled the road.

**The Gardens of Refuge and the Unrest of the Modern Soul**

The verse's promise of a refuge speaks with special directness to the modern condition, for the modern soul is, by common confession, the most sheltered and the least secure in human history. Never have human beings enjoyed such physical safety — medicine, insurance, locked doors, climate-controlled homes — and never, by the testimony of the age itself, have they been so haunted by anxiety, so restless in their comforts, so unable to find the *maʾwā*, the place of final shelter. The Qur'an's diagnosis of the unrest is consistent from its first page to its last: the soul was made for its Lord, and every shelter short of Him is a temporary arrangement: "Is he whose breast God has expanded for submission, so that he is upon a light from his Lord [like the hardened]? Woe unto those whose hearts are hardened against the remembrance of God!" (39:22). The believers of this verse are those who found the true refuge before the grave — in the remembrance of God, in the prostration of verse 15, in the night vigil of verse 16 — and the Gardens of Refuge are the perfection of what they already tasted: the same shelter, finally unshadowed. The verse's promise, read in this light, is not only about the distant future; it is an announcement of the order of reality: the believer is not equal to the iniquitous (v. 18), and the difference begins in this life — the believer has already found the refuge toward which the Garden is the final door, while the iniquitous, for all his worldly shelters, has not. And for the believer who struggles, who sins and repents, who rises and falls and rises again, the verse holds out the steadfast promise: the welcome is prepared, the *nuzul* is ready, the Gardens of Refuge are standing open — as a welcome for what he used to do, however small the deeds and however great the Mercy that receives them.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:20

> **And as for those who are iniquitous, their refuge is the Fire. Whenever they desire to go forth therefrom, they are returned unto it, and it is said unto them, "Taste the punishment of the Fire that you used to deny!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Side of the Verdict: A Refuge That Is No Refuge**

Verse 19 assigned the believers their Gardens of refuge; verse 20 now assigns the iniquitous their mirror-image destiny with the very same word: *wa ammā alladhīna fasaqū fa-maʾwāhumu al-nār* — "And as for those who are iniquitous, their refuge is the Fire." The grammatical parallelism is exact and deliberate: just as the believers have their *maʾwā*, their shelter, so the iniquitous have their *maʾwā* — and theirs is the Fire. The word that meant safety for the believers is here turned to its opposite: the Fire is called their *refuge* — their shelter, their destination, the place that finally receives them — in a usage heavy with irony. In this world, the iniquitous sought refuge in many things: in their wealth, their power, their pleasures, their denial of the reckoning to come. They built their lives, as verse 14 said, on the forgetting of the Day, and they took refuge from the thought of judgment in the noise of the world. The verse announces that all their refuges were temporary arrangements, and that the only permanent shelter they have built for themselves is the Fire — the place that receives them not because God chose it for them arbitrarily but because they spent their lives making themselves at home in everything that leads to it. The commentators noted that the verse's *fasaqū* — "they became iniquitous" — deliberately repeats the word used in verse 18, binding the verdict to the question: the iniquitous of verse 18, who are not equal to the believers, receive here the full spelling-out of their inequality — the believers have the Gardens of refuge, and they have the Fire. The two verses are the two panels of a single judgment, divided by nothing but the deeds of the judged.

**The Desire to Leave and the Forced Return**

The verse then describes the life of the Fire with a sentence of terrifying economy: *kullamā arādū an yakhrujū minhā uʿīdū fīhā* — "Whenever they desire to go forth therefrom, they are returned unto it." The phrase "whenever they desire" (*kullamā arādū*) indicates repetition: the desire to escape is not a single impulse but a recurring one, and the return is its endless answer. The commentators understood the verse to describe the ceaseless cycle of the Fire's torment: the flames surge and the punished are lifted toward its brink — the Qur'an elsewhere describes them as "surging in the Fire" (40:47? — actually "the Fire... surging" is in 33? Let me be careful; the image of being lifted to the brink: "whenever a group is cast into it..." — 67:8). The desire to go forth arises — from the natural horror, from the hope that perhaps this time the exit will open — and they are returned into it, driven back by the angels who guard it, or swallowed again by the Fire itself. The parallel verse in the sūrah of the Ḥajj gives the same cycle in nearly the same words: "Whenever, in their anguish, they desire to go forth therefrom, they are returned into it, while [being told], 'Taste the punishment of the burning!'" (22:22). The Qur'an describes the psychology of the cycle elsewhere: the inhabitants of the Fire plead with its keepers, "Call upon your Lord to lighten for us a single day of the punishment!" (40:49), and the keepers answer with the question that exposes the root of the whole situation: "Did not your messengers come unto you with clear proofs? They say, 'Yes.' They say, 'Then call — but the calling of the disbelievers is only in vain'" (40:50). The desire to leave is real, but it is the desire of those who never desired, in the world, to leave the road that led there — and the cycle of desire and return is the endless enactment of that contradiction.

**The Iniquity That Persists Even in the Punishment**

The source commentary draws from the verse a subtle and sobering observation: the desire of the disbelievers to leave the Fire is itself evidence that, even then, they have not understood what has happened to them. Their request in verse 12 — "send us back that we may work righteousness" — showed that they still imagined themselves capable of a righteousness they had never practiced; their desire here to escape the Fire shows that they still relate to their punishment as something external, imposed, and unfair — something to be escaped rather than something that has grown out of their own choices. The commentators noted that the truly reformed soul would not ask merely to leave the Fire but to be changed; the inhabitants of the Fire ask only for release, and because they ask for release without transformation, the release is denied — for even if they were released, as verse 13 of this very sūrah and the parallel in 6:28 have shown, they would return to what they were forbidden. The deepest tragedy of the Fire, on this reading, is not the flames but the continuity: the people in the Fire are the same people who denied in the world — the same hardness, the same self-justification, the same inability to see their own guilt — now fixed forever in the environment that their own character has made unendurable. The punishment is not an arbitrary sentence passed on them from outside; it is the final, externalized form of the inner state they chose. And that is why the verse's cycle — desire, return, desire, return — never ends: the desire to escape without the will to change is a desire that the Fire itself was made to frustrate.

**"The Fire That You Used to Deny"**

The verse's final words deliver the sentence with its most exquisite cruelty and its most exact justice: *wa qīla lahum dhūqū ʿadhāba al-nāri alladhī kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūn* — "and it is said unto them, 'Taste the punishment of the Fire that you used to deny!'" The relative clause — "that you used to deny" — is the point: the Fire is identified not by its heat or its darkness but by its relationship to the deniers themselves. This is the very Fire they mocked — the Fire whose existence they dismissed, whose warning they treated as a fable, whose arrival they challenged with the taunt that verse 28 of this sūrah will record: "When is this victory, if you are truthful?" The punishment is named by the crime in the Qur'an's most characteristic manner: the deniers are told to taste "the Fire that you used to deny," so that the tasting itself is a confirmation of everything they denied — every verse, every warner, every warning — and the torment is doubled by the knowledge that the tormenters had been told, and had refused to believe. The Qur'an records the same formula of naming the punishment by the denial elsewhere: "Taste the punishment for that which you used to deny" (6:30); "This is the Fire which the guilty used to deny!" (55:43); "It will be said, 'This is that which you used to deny'" (83:17). And the imperative *dhūqū* — "taste" — completes the irony: the word is the word of enjoyment, of sampling, of relishing — and it is used here for the first sensation of an endless torment, so that even the vocabulary of pleasure is turned against those who spent their lives pursuing pleasure without the remembrance of God.

**The Warning Addressed to the Living**

Like every description of the Fire in the Qur'an, this verse is addressed, in the final analysis, not to its inhabitants but to the living — to the reader who can still choose. The verse's purpose is not to satisfy a curiosity about the geography of the Hereafter but to make the Fire real enough in the present that the present may be corrected: the believer reads of the desire to leave and the forced return, and is moved to live so that he will never know that desire; the waverer reads of the Fire "that you used to deny," and is moved to stop denying; the denier reads of the tasting that follows the denial, and is invited to consider whether the taunt is worth the price. The Prophet ﷺ warned that the Fire should be remembered in terms that pierce the heedless heart, and the Qur'an itself says that the descriptions of the Hereafter are given "that the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter might incline to it, and that they might be pleased with it, and that they might earn what they are earning" (6:113) — but also that they are given so that the believers might increase in certainty, gratitude, and striving. The verse stands, therefore, as the dark half of the sūrah's promise: verse 19 has shown the believer the welcome that awaits; verse 20 shows the iniquitous the return that awaits; and between the two, the reader is left standing — in the only place where the choice between the two destinations is still open, still free, and still decisive.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:21

> **And We make them taste the lesser punishment before the greater punishment, that they might turn back.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Lesser Punishment Before the Greater**

The sūrah now turns from the final destinations of the two parties to the mercy that precedes the final destination: *wa la-nudhīqannahum mina al-ʿadhābi al-adnā dūna al-ʿadhābi al-akbar* — "And We make them taste the lesser punishment before the greater punishment, that they might turn back." The emphatic form of the verb — *la-nudhīqannahum*, "We shall surely make them taste" — marks the sentence as a settled decree, an oath-like certainty: the tasting of the lesser punishment is not an accident of history but a deliberate divine policy. The "lesser punishment" (*al-ʿadhāb al-adnā*) is understood by the commentators in several complementary senses, all of which share one feature: it is a punishment in this world, before the greater punishment of the Hereafter. Ibn Kathīr and Ibn al-Jawzī explained it as the afflictions of this life — humiliation, defeat, illness, famine, and disaster, the blows that fall upon individuals and nations in the course of the world; others understood it as the legal punishments established for crimes in this world; still others, as the punishment of the grave, which comes between death and the resurrection and is itself "lesser" only by comparison with what follows. The "greater punishment" (*al-ʿadhāb al-akbar*) is, on all readings, the punishment of the Hereafter — the Fire of verse 20, the tasting of which is the final and unending recompense. The verse's structure is that of a graduated warning: God does not hurl the creature from the world directly into the eternal Fire without first sending blows that can still be heard, pains that can still be heeded, and calamities that can still be turned into repentance. The lesser punishment is the trumpet before the final sentence — the knock on the door before the door is sealed.

**Calamities as Wake-Up Calls: The Pattern in History**

The Qur'an presents this graduated warning as the consistent pattern of God's dealing with peoples throughout history, and the verses that describe the pattern make its logic explicit. The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf states the policy in full: "We sent no prophet to a town but We seized its people with misery and hardship, that they might humble themselves. Then We replaced the evil with good, until they multiplied and said, 'Hardship and ease touched our fathers.' Then We seized them suddenly, while they perceived not" (7:94–95). The policy has two stages: first the "misery and hardship" — the lesser punishment — sent with the explicit purpose that the people might humble themselves and turn back; and then, if the hardships are explained away ("hardship and ease touched our fathers" — the cynicism that reduces every calamity to the ordinary fluctuation of fortune), the sudden seizing — the greater punishment — which arrives "while they perceive not." The sūrah of al-Rūm states the same policy as a general law of the present world: "Corruption has appeared on land and sea for that which men's hands have earned, that He may let them taste some of that which they have done, that haply they may return" (30:41). And the sūrah of al-Shūrā gives the individual version: "Whatever misfortune strikes you is for that which your hands have earned — and He pardons much" (42:30). The pattern is consistent: calamity in the Qur'anic worldview is not meaningless suffering but a communication — a divine message in the language of events, telling individuals and nations that something is wrong, that the road they are on leads to a greater punishment, and that the road can still be changed. The ancient peoples who received the messengers and rejected them received first the warnings in words and then the warnings in events; and when the events failed to move them, the greater punishment arrived — the destroyed generations whose dwellings the Arabs of the Prophet's time walked among, as verse 26 of this sūrah will remind them.

**The Mercy Hidden in the Blow**

The most striking feature of the verse is its purpose clause: *laʿallahum yarjiʿūn* — "that they might turn back." The lesser punishment is sent not out of cruelty but out of mercy; its entire purpose is the return of those who receive it. The commentators developed the point with a striking series of comparisons: the lesser punishment is like the physician's bitter medicine, administered to avert the amputation; like the parent's timely correction, given to save the child from a worse end; like the warning light on the dashboard, which is annoying but points to a danger that, ignored, destroys the engine. The Qur'an itself uses the language of return throughout: the afflicted are those who "call upon Him, turning to Him in repentance" when harm touches them (30:33), because hardship strips away the illusions of self-sufficiency and returns the human being to his native condition — the fitrah, the original disposition in which the creature knows his dependence on his Creator. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the afflictions of this world are, for the believer, a sign of divine care rather than divine anger, in the report recorded by al-Tirmidhī from Anas ibn Mālik:

> "When God desires good for His servant, He hastens his punishment for him in this world; and when God desires evil for His servant, He withholds his sins from him until he is paid in full on the Day of Resurrection." (al-Tirmidhī 2396)

The same report continues with the Prophet's explanation of the principle: "Indeed, with the greater reward comes the greater trial, and when God loves a people, He tries them; so whoever is content will have contentment, and whoever is resentful will have resentment." The lesser punishment is thus revealed as one of the most misunderstood mercies of God: the bill that arrives in installments in this world is a mercy compared to the bill that arrives in full in the next; the calamity that turns a soul back is a success, while the prosperity that hardens a soul is a tragedy in slow motion. The verse's policy — taste the lesser now, so that you may be spared the greater — is, from the divine side, the policy of a Lord Who would rather see His creature return through a temporary pain than be lost through an eternal one.

**When the Lesser Punishment Is Wasted**

The verse's purpose clause carries within it the possibility of its own failure: the lesser punishment is sent "that they might turn back" — but the turning back is not guaranteed, and the Qur'an records the responses that waste the warning. Some, when the lesser punishment arrives, repent and return — and for them the greater punishment is averted, as in the story of Yūnus's people, who were shown the punishment approaching and turned back before it fell, and God spared them: "Why was there not a single town that believed, so that its faith profited it, except the people of Jonah? When they believed, We removed from them the punishment of disgrace in the life of this world, and gave them enjoyment for a while" (10:98). Others, when the lesser punishment arrives, explain it away — "hardship and ease touched our fathers" (7:95) — treating every calamity as the ordinary weather of fortune, and thereby forfeiting the warning. Still others repent at the moment of the blow and forget the repentance when the blow is lifted: "When harm touches man, he calls upon Us; then, when We grant him a blessing from Us, he says, 'I was only given it because of knowledge'" (39:49) — the cycle of the fair-weather return, which the Qur'an describes with relentless accuracy across its pages. The verse's warning to the reader is therefore double: do not waste the lesser punishment — let every affliction do its work of turning the heart back; and do not wait for the lesser punishment — the greater mercy is to turn back while the road is smooth, before the blows are needed at all.

**The Believer's Response to Calamity**

For the believer, the verse provides the framework within which all of life's afflictions are to be understood and received. When calamity strikes — illness, loss, failure, the collapse of plans — the believer is not commanded to pretend that it is not painful, nor to claim that it is a punishment for specific sins, but to receive it as the Qur'anic tradition teaches: as a reminder, a purification, an expiation, and an opportunity to turn back. The Prophet ﷺ taught that "no fatigue, illness, anxiety, sorrow, harm, or distress afflicts a Muslim — not even the prick of a thorn — but that God thereby expiates some of his sins" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah and ʿĀʾishah); and the Qur'an describes the patient believers in terms that turn affliction itself into worship: "Surely We shall test you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth and lives and fruits; and give glad tidings to the patient — those who, when affliction strikes them, say, 'Truly we belong to God, and truly unto Him we return'" (2:155–156). The verse of this sūrah adds the larger frame: every lesser punishment in this world is a messenger from the greater mercy, sent to say that the road can still be changed, the account can still be settled, the return can still be made — and the believer's task in every affliction is to hear the message and make the return, so that the lesser punishment achieves its purpose and the greater punishment is never needed.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:22

> **And who does greater wrong than one who has been reminded of the signs of his Lord, then turns away from them? Truly We shall take vengeance upon the guilty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Greatest Wrong: Knowledge Refused**

From the graduated mercy of the lesser punishment, the sūrah turns to the crime that the lesser punishment is sent to cure — and it names that crime as the greatest wrong a human being can commit: *wa man aẓlamu mimman dhukkira bi-āyāti rabbihi thumma aʿraḍa ʿanhā* — "And who does greater wrong than one who has been reminded of the signs of his Lord, then turns away from them?" The rhetorical question carries its own answer: no one does greater wrong. The word *aẓlamu* — "more wrongful" — is the elative form of *ẓulm*, and ẓulm in the Qur'an's vocabulary is not merely "sin" but the specific wrong of placing a thing where it does not belong — of displacing truth, justice, and right from their proper places. The greatest ẓulm is here defined with precision: it is not the wrong of the ignorant, who never knew, but the wrong of the one who was *reminded* — who received the signs, understood them, or at least heard them clearly — and then *turned away*. The verse's indictment has two movements, and both are necessary: the reminder (*dhukkira*) and the turning away (*aʿraḍa*). The reminder came — the verses were recited, the signs were shown, the truth was made plain; and the turning away followed — the shoulder was turned, the attention was diverted, the truth was left standing while the reminded walked off. The commentators noted the perfect fairness of the indictment: the wrong is measured by the light that was given. The person who sins in ignorance has an excuse that the person who sins after the reminder does not have; the reminder raises the stakes of every subsequent choice, because after the reminder, turning away is no longer a matter of not knowing — it is a matter of not wanting. And that is why the verse calls it the greatest wrong: it is the wrong of the soul that has been handed the map and throws it aside, that has been shown the road and chooses the cliff, that has heard the warning and answers with its back.

**The Crime of the Reminded: A Profile That Runs Through the Qur'an**

The Qur'an returns to this exact profile — reminded, then turned away — in several places, and the repetitions show that the commentators were right to treat it as one of the Qur'an's central moral categories. The most extended parallel is in the sūrah of the Cave: "And who does greater wrong than he who is reminded of the signs of his Lord and turns away from them, forgetting what his hands have sent forth? Truly We have placed coverings over their hearts, lest they understand it, and deafness in their ears. And if thou callest them to guidance, they will never be guided" (18:57). The parallel verse adds the crucial sequel: the turning away is followed by the hardening — the coverings over the heart and the deafness in the ears — which is not an arbitrary divine punishment but the natural consequence of repeated refusal, the scar tissue of the soul that has turned away so often that turning has become its nature. The same profile is drawn in the sūrah of Luqmān, where the reminder is the recitation of the Qur'an itself and the turning away is described with devastating concreteness: "And when Our signs are recited unto him, he turns away in arrogance, as though he had not heard them, as though there were deafness in his ears — so give him glad tidings of a painful punishment" (31:7). And the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf traces the profile to its root in the character of the arrogant: "I shall turn away from My signs those who wax arrogant upon the earth without right. And though they see every sign, they will not believe in it; and though they see the way of right guidance, they will not take it as their way — and though they see the way of error, they will take it as their way. That is because they denied Our signs and were heedless of them" (7:146). The turning away is the act; the arrogance is the engine; and the "coverings" and "deafness" are the destination — the point at which the soul that has turned away enough times loses, by its own choices, the capacity to turn back.

**Why Miracles Alone Do Not Convince**

The verse's profile — reminded, then turned away — explains a phenomenon that puzzled the Prophet's contemporaries and has puzzled observers of religion in every age: why do some people remain unmoved by the most compelling evidence? The source commentary draws out the Qur'an's answer with complete clarity: miracles by themselves do not convince people whose souls are set against the truth. The Makkan deniers were shown the Qur'an itself — the miracle that the whole of Arabia could not match — and their response was not belief but aversion; the Qur'an records their own explanation: "And when Our signs are recited unto them as clear proofs, they say, 'This is nothing but a man who wishes to turn you away from that which your fathers worshipped'" (34:43), and again, "Were they to see every sign, they would not believe in it, so that when they come to thee, they dispute with thee — those who disbelieve say, 'This is nothing but fables of those of old'" (6:25). The Qur'an's diagnosis is consistent and psychologically acute: the obstacle to belief is not insufficient evidence but an unwilling will; the heart that does not want the truth to be true will find a reason to refuse it no matter what evidence arrives — for "had We sent down unto thee a Book written on parchment, and they had touched it with their hands, those who disbelieve would still have said, 'This is nothing but manifest sorcery'" (6:7). Modern psychology has confirmed the Qur'an's ancient observation under the name of motivated reasoning: human beings process evidence through the filter of their desires, and evidence that threatens a cherished position is discounted, explained away, or forgotten while evidence that supports it is embraced. The verse of this sūrah stands behind all such observations with its own conclusion: the crime is not in the evidence but in the turning — and the turning is the work of the will, for which the will will be held responsible. Those who have been reminded and turned away cannot plead on the Day of Resurrection that the signs never reached them; the reminder is the very thing that makes their wrong the greatest wrong.

**The Vengeance That Is Justice**

The verse closes with the sentence that answers the crime: *innā mina al-mujrimīna muntaqimūn* — "Truly We shall take vengeance upon the guilty." The word *intiqām* — vengeance, retribution — is one of the Qur'anic terms that the commentators handled with care, for the vengeance of God must not be confused with the vengeance of creatures. Human vengeance is typically the revenge of the wounded ego — disproportionate, impassioned, and blind. The divine vengeance is the exact recompense of justice, executed without passion, error, or excess, by the One Who alone knows the full measure of every deed: it is the requital of the guilty by the Judge Who is never unjust and never late. The Qur'an uses the word in precisely this sense: "Then We took vengeance upon those who denied — and how was My denial!" (35:26? — actually "Then We took vengeance upon them; so behold how was the end of the deniers" appears in several places: 43:25, 30:47, 7:136); "the day We seize with the greatest seizing — truly We shall take vengeance" (44:16); and the guilty are told in the sūrah of al-Dukhān's companion passage, "So wait for the day when the sky brings a visible smoke... on that day We shall seize with the greatest seizing; truly We shall take vengeance" (44:10–16). The word "guilty" — *al-mujrimūn* — is the Qur'an's term for those who have committed the crime of knowledge: the mujrim is the one who has done wrong deliberately, with full awareness, after the reminder. The verse's vengeance is thus directed precisely at the profile it has just described — the reminded who turned away — and it is the vengeance of the Judge upon the crime that this verse has named the greatest of all wrongs.

**Qatādah on the Threefold Loss of the Turned-Away**

The early Qur'anic commentator Qatādah ibn Diʿāmah (d. 118/736), whose views are preserved at length in Ibn Kathīr's commentary, pronounced on this verse a judgment that the source commentary transmits and that has been quoted ever since:

> "Whosoever turns away from the remembrance of God has been beguiled by the greatest delusion, rendered destitute by most severe penury, and oppressed by the greatest of sins." (Ibn Kathīr, from Qatādah)

The threefold loss is exact. The "greatest delusion" is the belief that the world can substitute for God — that wealth, pleasure, status, or simply the noise of distraction can fill the place in the soul that only the remembrance of God can fill; the deluded person builds his life on that substitution and discovers its emptiness only when it is too late. The "most severe penury" is the poverty of the soul that has turned away from the source of its own sustenance: the Qur'an repeatedly describes those who refuse God's signs as spiritually destitute even when outwardly prosperous — "Let not their wealth and their children impress thee; God only wishes to punish them thereby in this world, and that their souls should depart while they disbelieve" (9:55). And the "greatest of sins" is the sin of the reminded who turned away — the sin against light, which compounds every other sin because it removes the very compass by which sins are recognized. The source commentary adds that this state — being left to the delusion, the penury, and the sin — is itself "the most severe form of vengeance": the punishment of being abandoned to one's own turning, so that the soul that turned from the signs is gradually surrounded by the deafness and the coverings of 18:57, until the signs themselves become invisible to it. The greatest vengeance, on this reading, is not the Fire alone; it is the Fire preceded by the slow death of the soul's capacity to respond to the truth — the fate that this verse warns against and that verse 26 of this sūrah will show enacted in the ruined dwellings of the generations that turned away.

**The Warning and the Door**

For the living reader, the verse functions as both warning and invitation. The warning is precise: the profile of the greatest wrongdoer is not the pagan who never heard, nor the sinner who stumbles and repents, but the reminded who turns away — and every reader of this verse is, by definition, among the reminded, for the verse itself is a reminder. The invitation is equally precise: because the greatest wrong is defined by the turning away, the greatest correction is defined by the turning back; the same will that turned away can turn again, and the Qur'an that names the reminded-turned-away as the greatest wrongdoer is the same Qur'an that announces, "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against their own souls! Do not despair of the Mercy of God; truly God forgives all sins; truly He is the Forgiving, the Merciful" (39:53). The verse's "Truly We shall take vengeance upon the guilty" is a sentence upon a settled character, not upon a moment of weakness; the guilty of the verse are those who made turning away their way — and the door of turning back stands open until the term arrives, as the very next verses of the sūrah, turning to Moses and the Children of Israel, will show by example: a people who received the signs, turned away, and were given, generation after generation, the reminders that could still bring them back.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:23

> **And indeed We gave unto Moses the Book; so be not in doubt regarding the meeting with Him. And We made him a guide for the Children of Israel.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sudden Turn to Moses**

With verse 23 the sūrah changes its subject with a dramatic suddenness that the commentators were careful to explain: *wa laqad ātaynā Mūsā al-kitāb* — "And indeed We gave unto Moses the Book." After eleven verses of argument with the Makkan deniers over revelation, creation, resurrection, and judgment — and after the warning of verse 22 against those who are reminded of the signs and turn away — the sūrah turns to the greatest of the reminders given to an earlier people: the Book of Moses. The source commentary suggests that the sudden change of subject is itself the point: the revelation given to Moses is among those very signs from which one should not turn away; the history of the Children of Israel is a standing demonstration, on a vast scale, of both halves of verse 22 — a people who received the signs, produced leaders who guided by God's command, and then fell into difference and denial. The emphatic opening — *wa laqad*, "and indeed" — marks the sentence as an established fact, an oath-like affirmation of something already accomplished: the giving of the Book to Moses is not a promise or a hope but a settled event of history, as settled as the creation of the heavens and the earth with which the sūrah began. The verse thus extends the sūrah's argument backward in time: the revelation announced in verse 2 is not an innovation; it is the latest chapter of a single story — the story of the Book — that began long before, with the Book given to Moses. The Qur'an returns to the giving of the Torah to Moses so often that the event functions as the paradigm of all prophetic revelation: "We gave Moses the Book and the Criterion, that haply you might be guided" (2:53); "And indeed We gave Moses the Book — so be not in doubt regarding the meeting with Him — and We made it a guidance for the Children of Israel" (32:23 is the verse itself); "And indeed We gave Moses the guidance, and We bequeathed the Book to the Children of Israel, as a guidance and a reminder for people of understanding" (40:53–54).

**"Be Not in Doubt Regarding the Meeting with Him"**

The verse then turns directly to the Prophet — and, through him, to every reader: *fa-lā takun fī miryatin min liqāʾihi* — "so be not in doubt regarding the meeting with Him." The commentators devoted unusual attention to the pronoun in *liqāʾihi* — "His meeting" or "the meeting with him" — and the source commentary reviews the readings with care. Some understood the pronoun as referring to Moses, in which case the verse would mean: "be not in doubt regarding your meeting with Moses" — an allusion to the Prophet's meeting with Moses during the night journey and ascent (*al-miʿrāj*), when he met the earlier prophets in the heavens, was instructed by Moses, and returned to report the encounter (cf. 17:1; 53:1–18) — a reading adopted by Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī among others. Others understood the pronoun as referring to the revelation itself — "be not in doubt regarding it," that is, the Book given to Moses — since the Torah and the Qur'an confirm one another; still others as referring to Moses' own receiving of revelation at Sinai. But the source commentary notes a decisive consideration that has led most modern and many classical scholars to a different conclusion: the Qur'an consistently uses the vocabulary of "meeting" (*laqiya*, *liqāʾ*) for the meeting with God — as in 2:4, 2:223, 2:249, 6:31, 10:7–11, 18:105–110, 29:5, 30:8, and the tenth verse of this very sūrah — or for the meeting with death, or the meeting of the Day of Resurrection (3:143; 62:8; 6:130; 39:71; 45:34). It is never used elsewhere as a direct reference to receiving revelation. The most consistent reading of the verse, on this evidence, is therefore the meeting with God: the Prophet is told not to doubt that he will meet his Lord — that the road of his mission ends in the presence of the One Who sent him, and that the justice he calls for will be enacted at that meeting. Read in the context of the sūrah, the reassurance is precise: the Makkan deniers were demanding a date for the victory (v. 28) and mocking the promise of the meeting (v. 10); the Prophet is told not to let their mockery breed doubt in him — the meeting with his Lord is as certain as the giving of the Book to Moses, and the vindication that the meeting will bring is as sure as the history that verse 24 will now recount.

**The Meeting That the Prophets Themselves Sought**

The verse's reassurance gains its full depth when it is read against the Qur'an's portrait of Moses himself — the prophet who sought the meeting with God more ardently than any other. When Moses asked to see his Lord, he was told, "You shall not see Me, but look at the mountain: if it remains in its place, then you shall see Me." And when his Lord manifested Himself to the mountain, it crumbled to dust, and Moses fell down senseless (7:143). The same Moses who received the Book at Sinai, who spoke with his Lord directly — the *Kalīm Allāh*, the one addressed by God — is the archetype of the seeker of the meeting; and the Qur'an describes his people's believers as those who "think that they shall meet their Lord and that unto Him they shall return" (2:46). The verse of this sūrah places the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ in that same line: he is not to doubt the meeting with his Lord, because the meeting is the common destiny and the common hope of all the prophets — Moses sought it, Jesus will bear witness at it, and Muhammad ﷺ will stand before his Lord at the Praiseworthy Station that his night vigil was promised to earn (17:79). In the immediate Makkan context, the verse was a quiet answer to the mockers: they asked when the victory would come, and the answer is that the decisive victory is the meeting — the day when the Prophet and his deniers stand before the same Lord, and the long argument of the sūrah is settled by the only Judge who can settle it.

**A Guide for the Children of Israel**

The verse closes with the purpose and outcome of the giving of the Book: *wa jaʿalnāhu hudan li-banī Isrāʾīl* — "And We made him a guide for the Children of Israel." The pronoun admits the same double reference that the commentators traced through the verse: it may refer to Moses — God made Moses himself a guide, a leader and model, for his people — or it may refer to the Book, the Torah, which God made "a guidance" for the Children of Israel (the reading preferred by Ibn Kathīr, Ibn al-Jawzī, and al-Ṭabarī, who note that the phrase "a guidance" is used of the Torah elsewhere: "We gave Moses the Book... a guidance and a mercy" (6:154), and "the Torah, wherein is guidance and light" (5:44)). Both readings converge: God gave the Children of Israel a guide — in the person of Moses and in the Book he bore — and the guide was given for their guidance, not for their glory. The phrase also establishes the pattern that the rest of the sūrah will develop: the Children of Israel were guided so long as they followed the signs (the source's summary of verses 23–25); their history is the case study of what happens to a guided people — how the guidance produces leaders (v. 24), how the people fall into difference (v. 25), and how the pattern of guidance and loss is written into the ruins of the nations that came before (vv. 26–27). For the Prophet's contemporaries — and for the readers of every age — the verse is both a precedent and a warning: the God Who guided the Children of Israel through Moses is the God Who has now guided this community through Muhammad ﷺ; and the community that receives the guide is measured by the same standard as the community that received the earlier one — not by its claims, but by its following.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:24

> **And We appointed leaders from among them who guided by Our Command when they were patient and were certain of Our signs.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Leaders from Among Them: The Raising of the Guides**

The history of the Children of Israel is now summarized in a single verse that reads like a formula for the rise and fall of religious leadership: *wa jaʿalnā minhum aʾimmatan yahdūna bi-amrinā* — "And We appointed leaders from among them who guided by Our Command." The word *aʾimmah* — leaders, models, exemplars — is the plural of *imām*, the word for the one who stands before and is followed; its root carries the sense of being in front, and the imām is the one whose position, conduct, and word set the direction for those behind him. The leaders appointed among the Children of Israel were, in the understanding of the commentators, the prophets and the righteous scholars who succeeded them — the scribes, priests, and teachers of the Law who carried the guidance of the Torah into the life of the people (the source identifies them with the religious leadership of Israel in the broadest sense). Two features of the appointment are emphasized by the verse's grammar. First, the leaders were raised "from among them" — from the Children of Israel themselves — for guidance is most effective when the guide speaks the language and shares the condition of the guided; the leadership was indigenous, not imported, and the community that produced its own guides was a community being educated toward maturity. Second, the leaders guided "by Our Command" — *bi-amrinā* — their authority was derivative and delegated: they did not guide by their own invention, their own preferences, or their own political power, but by the command of God; the imām was a transmitter of the divine command, not a source of it, and his authority lasted exactly as long as his transmission remained faithful.

**The Two Qualifications: Patience and Certainty**

The verse then states the conditions upon which the leadership was granted: *lammā ṣabarū wa kānū bi-āyātinā yūqinūn* — "when they were patient and were certain of Our signs." The particle *lammā* marks the condition as temporal and causal — when they were patient and certain, then the leadership was given; the imamate was not a rank conferred and then maintained regardless of conduct, but a station that depended on the continued possession of two qualities. The first is patience (*ṣabr*) — a word whose Qur'anic range covers endurance in obedience, restraint from disobedience, and composure under the decree: the patience to keep guiding when the people resist, to keep teaching when the students are slow, to keep bearing witness when the truth is costly. The commentators (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī) added that the patience required of the leaders included enduring the attractions of this world and not succumbing to them — the specific temptation of the religious leader, who can so easily trade the authority of the truth for the comforts of the powerful. The second quality is certainty (*yaqīn*) — the settled, unshakeable conviction expressed in the phrase "certain of Our signs" (*bi-āyātinā yūqinūn*): the leaders were certain that the signs were real, that the promise was true, that the Book was from God; and their certainty was the foundation of their patience, for no one endures long for what he doubts. Ibn Kathīr drew from the verse the general principle: religious leadership is not attained by desire, lineage, or office but by patience and certainty — the two qualities that make a soul fit to stand in front of others. The same principle is stated positively in the Qur'an's account of Abraham, who was made an imām only after he had been tested (2:124), and negatively in its account of the leaders of misguidance, who are described with the same word turned to evil: "We made them imams calling unto the Fire, and on the Day of Resurrection they will not be helped" (28:41) — leaders who were followed, but led their followers into the Fire.

**The Downfall: When the Guides Lost Their Way**

The verse's condition — "when they were patient and were certain" — implies its own negation, and the Qur'an completes the picture in the verses that describe what happened to the leadership of Israel when the condition was no longer met. So long as the leaders were patient and certain, they guided by God's command and the people were guided with them. But when they altered the scriptures, distorted the interpretations, and followed the world, they were deprived of the station, and their hearts hardened — the process the source commentary describes in the words of Ibn Kathīr. The Qur'an's indictment of the corrupted religious leadership of the People of the Book is among its most severe: "Among those who are Jews, they distort words from their contexts" (4:46); "O People of the Book! Our Messenger has come unto you, making clear unto you much of what you used to conceal of the Book, and pardoning much" (5:15); "You who have been given the Book! Do you believe in part of the Book and disbelieve in part?" (2:85). The tragedy of the corrupted leaders is that they did not stop being leaders; they simply changed the direction of their leading — from the command of God to the commands of their own interests — and the people who had learned to follow them followed them into the corruption. The Prophet ﷺ warned his own community against the same degeneration of religious authority, and his warning is the standing commentary on this verse for every generation of Muslims: "The scholars are the heirs of the prophets; the prophets bequeath neither dinar nor dirham, but they bequeath knowledge — so whoever takes it has taken an abundant portion" (al-Tirmidhī 2682; Abū Dāwūd 3641). The inheritance is conditional, as this verse shows: the heirs of the prophets are the patient and the certain, and those who abandon patience for comfort and certainty for compromise forfeit the inheritance — a warning addressed, in every age, first to those who stand in front.

**The Lesson for Every Community and Every Leader**

The verse's teaching extends far beyond the Children of Israel, for its principle — leadership follows patience and certainty, and lasts only as long as they last — is a universal law of religious community. Every community of believers produces leaders: the scholar in his study, the teacher in her classroom, the parent in the home, the elder in the mosque, the activist in the public square. The verse sets the qualification for all of them: patience that does not break under the slow work of guiding others, and certainty that does not bend with the prevailing wind. The Prophet ﷺ universalized the responsibility of leadership in his famous address: "Each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock — the ruler is a shepherd and responsible for his flock, the man is a shepherd over his household and responsible for them, the woman is a shepherdess over her husband's house and children and responsible for them" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Ibn ʿUmar). Every Muslim is, in some domain, an imām — one who stands in front and is followed — and this verse states the terms on which the standing may continue: be patient, be certain, and guide by God's command rather than by your own. The modern age has multiplied the platforms of leadership — the pulpit and the screen, the lecture hall and the feed — while diluting the qualifications; the verse stands as the Qur'an's permanent filter: those who guide by God's command, patient and certain, are the appointed imams of their people — and those who lack the two qualities, whatever their titles, are leading without the appointment.

**Patience and Certainty: The Two Pillars of Steadfast Guidance**

The verse's two conditions are bound to each other by a mutual dependence that the commentators were careful to draw out. Certainty without patience produces the zealot who burns out or burns others — the man so sure of the truth that he cannot endure the slow work of leading people to it. Patience without certainty produces the functionary who endures everything because he believes nothing strongly enough to abandon — the leader who outlasts every cause because he was never truly committed to any. The imams of Israel were granted their station only when both qualities were present, because only both together produce the leader who is both immovable and gentle: certain enough that he cannot be turned from the truth, and patient enough that he can turn others toward it without breaking them. The Qur'an describes the combination as the mark of the divinely guided throughout its pages — the believers are commanded, "O you who believe! Be patient, and vie in patience, and be steadfast" (3:200), and the patient are promised, "Truly God is with the patient" (2:153), and the certain are promised that they will be guided: "And those who strive in Us, We shall surely guide them upon Our ways" (29:69). The verse of this sūrah shows the reward of the combination in history: the Children of Israel, for as long as their leaders combined patience with certainty, produced the guidance of which the whole region of Syria and Palestine became the stage — the line of prophets from David to Jesus walking the earth that this verse summarizes in a single sentence; and when the combination was broken, the leadership was broken with it, and the people were left to the differences that the next verse will describe.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:25

> **Truly thy Lord will distinguish between them on the Day of Resurrection regarding that wherein they used to differ.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day of Final Separation**

From the rise and fall of Israel's leadership, the sūrah turns to the resolution of Israel's divisions: *inna rabbaka huwa yaṣilu baynahum yawma al-qiyāmati fīmā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn* — "Truly thy Lord will distinguish between them on the Day of Resurrection regarding that wherein they used to differ." The verb *yaṣilu* — rendered here as "distinguish" — comes from a root whose meanings include separating, deciding, and joining; in the context of judgment it carries the sense of decisively separating the disputants by settling their dispute: the Day of Resurrection is the day of final separation, when every difference is resolved by the only judgment that cannot be appealed. The verse's "between them" refers, in its immediate context, to the Children of Israel — the people whose leaders guided by God's command while they were patient and certain (v. 24), and who then fell into the differences that divided them: differences over the interpretation of the Torah, over the identity of the awaited messenger, over the details of the law, over the very things the Qur'an spends its pages recounting — the disputes of the Jews and the Christians over Abraham (2:113), the disputes over Jesus (4:157; 19:34–37), the disputes over the Sabbath, the disputes over what God had commanded and what the leaders had altered. The commentators noted, however, that the verse's principle is general as well as specific (al-Ṭabarī): God will distinguish on the Day of Resurrection not only between the divided sects of Israel but between all who differed in religion — and the sūrah's own context, with its argument against the Makkan deniers, gives the general application immediate force: the difference between the Prophet and his deniers, between the believers and the mockers, between every community that followed the signs and every community that turned away, is a difference that God Himself will settle on the Day when He "distinguishes between them."

**The Differences That History Could Not Settle**

The verse's closing phrase — *fīmā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn*, "regarding that wherein they used to differ" — places the whole burden of religious history on the docket of the Day of Resurrection. The differences among the People of the Book were many, and they were not merely intellectual: they were fought over, they split communities, they produced persecution and schism, and they were carried by each party with the full conviction of righteousness. The Qur'an reviews the catalogue of those differences with the detachment of the final arbiter: the Jews and the Christians each claimed Abraham for their own, and God said, "This is a community that has passed away; theirs is what they earned and yours is what you earned; and you will not be asked about what they used to do" (2:141) — while the dispute over Abraham's religion is referred to the same tribunal as this verse's: "God will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection regarding that wherein they differed" (2:113). The differences over the Sabbath, over the penalties of the law, over the interpretation of prophecy, over the person of Jesus — all of them are gathered by this verse into a single category: "that wherein they used to differ." The verse makes no attempt to adjudicate the details, and its refusal is itself the teaching: the differences of history are too many, too entangled with passion and interest, to be settled by the instruments of history; only the Judge Who knows the intention behind every interpretation and the motive behind every division can separate the truthful from the false in each of them. The Qur'an's consistent deferral of these disputes to the Day of Resurrection is therefore not an evasion but an epistemology: there are questions that human beings lack the data, the impartiality, and the moral authority to settle finally — and the honest response to such questions is not to fight over them forever but to commit them to the One Who can settle them.

**The Test Hidden in the Differences**

The Qur'an does not, however, present the differences themselves as a pure misfortune, and the verse of the Table Spread gives the deeper wisdom of why God permitted them: "For each of you We appointed a law and a way. And had God willed, He would have made you one community — but that He may test you in that which He has given you. So race to good deeds! Unto God shall you all return, and He will inform you of that wherein you differed" (5:48). Two truths are held together in that verse and presupposed by this one. First, the differences were permitted as a test: a world in which everyone agreed on everything would offer no occasion for the virtues that disagreement makes possible — the patient search for truth, the charity that overlooks, the humility that concedes, the integrity that differs without hostility. Second, the differences have an end: "Unto God shall you all return, and He will inform you of that wherein you differed." The differences are real — this verse's "regarding that wherein they used to differ" takes them seriously — but they are not final; they are the material of the test, and the test has a grading day. The believer's posture toward the differences of religion is therefore defined with precision: he is not commanded to pretend that the differences do not matter, nor to hate those who differ, nor to claim certainty where certainty is impossible — but to seek the truth diligently, to hold what he holds with conviction and humility, to race with those who differ toward the good deeds that both sides acknowledge, and to leave the final distinction to the Day when God "will inform you of that wherein you differed."

**The Modern Sectarian Tragedy and the Qur'anic Cure**

The verse speaks with painful directness to the modern world, in which the differences of the Children of Israel — and the differences among the inheritors of Abraham's religion generally — have produced some of the longest-running conflicts in human history. The Qur'an's framework for those conflicts is stated in this verse with a clarity that the combatants on every side have often missed: the differences are real, they will be adjudicated, but the adjudication belongs to God on the Day of Resurrection — and those who take the adjudication into their own hands before that Day, by making their own interpretation the instrument of damnation or destruction of those who differ, are usurping a function that the verse reserves to the Lord alone. The Qur'an is emphatic on the point: "Truly those who have divided their religion and become sects — you have nothing to do with them; their affair is only with God; then He will inform them of what they used to do" (6:159). The tragedy of sectarian violence — whether between the heirs of Israel and the heirs of Ishmael, or within the house of Islam itself — is that it fights, with the weapons of this world, over verdicts that this verse assigns to the next: it makes the Day of Resurrection redundant by claiming its authority in advance, and it makes the test of 5:48 into a slaughter by refusing to leave the differences to the One Who created them as a test. The believers who take this verse seriously are those who can differ — over theology, over law, over history — without converting their differences into enmity, because they know that the final distinction is not theirs to draw: it belongs to "thy Lord," and its date is the Day of Resurrection.

**The Comfort of the Deferred Verdict**

For the wronged and the truthful among the disputants — the prophets who were rejected, the believers who were persecuted for their readings, the honest seekers who were maligned by the powerful — the verse is a promise of vindication. The verdict is deferred, but the deferral is not a denial: "Truly thy Lord will distinguish between them" — the sentence is in the emphatic form (*inna* + the explicit pronoun *huwa*), an affirmation that the distinction will certainly be made, by the Lord Himself, on the appointed Day. The truthful who were called liars will be distinguished from the liars who were called truthful; the followers of the signs will be distinguished from those who turned away; the patient leaders of verse 24 will be distinguished from the leaders who led into the Fire. The Qur'an holds this promise out to the believers of every generation as the ground of their equanimity in the face of disputed truth: "So be patient — truly the Promise of God is true — and let not those who are not certain disquiet thee" (30:60). The verse of this sūrah, placed between the history of Israel's leadership (v. 24) and the ruins of the nations that denied (vv. 26–27), is the hinge of the whole argument: the differences of this world — between Israel's sects, between the Prophet and the deniers, between every party of every age — are real but provisional; the distinction is coming; and the believer's task, while the difference lasts, is not to seize the verdict but to live the side of the difference that the Judge will recognize: the side of patience, certainty, and the signs of the Lord.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:26

> **Does it not serve as guidance for them, how many generations We destroyed before them, amidst whose dwellings they walk? Truly in that are signs. Will they not listen?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ruins as Guidance**

The sūrah's argument now turns from the history of the Children of Israel to the ruins that the Arabs themselves walked among: *awalam yahdihim kam ahlaknā min qablihim mina al-qurūn yamshūna fī masākinihim* — "Does it not serve as guidance for them, how many generations We destroyed before them, amidst whose dwellings they walk?" The question is addressed to the deniers of Makkah, and it begins with a word that carries the full weight of the verse's claim: *yahdihim* — "does it not guide them?" The ruins of the destroyed nations are described as a source of guidance — *hidāyah* — as real as a book or a prophet; the physical remains of divine judgment are themselves a revelation in stone. The "generations" (*al-qurūn*) that were destroyed are the peoples of the past — the ʿĀd, the Thamūd, the people of Lot, and the countless nations between them — and the verse's emphasis falls on the prepositional phrase that follows: *yamshūna fī masākinihim* — "amidst whose dwellings they walk." The deniers were not being asked to believe in reports of distant, unverifiable catastrophes; they were walking through the evidence daily. The trade caravans of the Quraysh, traveling north to Syria, passed through the carved rock-dwellings of Thamūd at al-Ḥijr; the routes of the south passed through the territories where ʿĀd had flourished and vanished; the way to the Levant ran past the blasted towns of the people of Lot. The Qur'an presses the point of this proximity in several places: "And ʿĀd and Thamūd — it has become clear to you from their dwellings" (29:38); "And We left a clear sign of them" (29:35); and of the ruined cities of Lot's people, "Truly in that is a sign for those who take heed — and truly they are on a road that remains" (15:75–76). The archaeological evidence was not buried; it was standing, roofless, along the caravan routes — a museum of divine justice open to the sky, with the Arabs as its daily visitors.

**The Ruined Dwellings of the Arabs' Own Landscape**

The Qur'an's appeal to the ruins is remarkable for its historical concreteness, and the Arabs' own literature confirms that they knew exactly what the verse was talking about. The carved cities of the Thamūd — the dwellings hewn into the rose-colored mountains of al-Ḥijr, which travelers can still visit today — were famous in pre-Islamic Arabia as monuments of a vanished people, and the Qur'an describes them with a precision that matches the site: "And Thamūd carved dwellings in the mountains" (15:82? — actually 15:82: "they carved out dwellings in the mountains, secure" — wait, 15:80-84 is about the people of al-Ḥijr; and 26:149: "you carve dwellings in the mountains with skill"; 89:9: "and Thamūd who carved the rocks in the valley"). The Qur'an's account of their destruction — the cry that seized them at dawn, leaving their dwellings empty — was verifiable by anyone who passed al-Ḥijr and saw the empty dwellings. The Prophet ﷺ himself treated the site as a standing lesson: when his army passed through the territory of Thamūd on the way to Tabūk, he is reported to have warned his Companions: "Do not enter the dwellings of those who wronged themselves, unless you are weeping, lest what befell them befall you" — and he covered his head and hastened his mount past the ruins (al-Bukhārī, from Ibn ʿUmar). The instruction is the verse of this sūrah enacted: the ruins are for guidance, and the guidance is received with weeping — with the humility that says, "this could be us" — not with the curiosity that says, "how interesting." The believer who visits the ruins of the destroyed nations is not a tourist; he is a student reading the only history book that never lies — the one written by the consequences of human choices under the sunnah of God.

**The Fall of the Mighty: The Warning Beneath the Stones**

The verse's argument rests on a fact that the Qur'an states repeatedly and that the ruins themselves demonstrated: the destroyed generations were not weaker than their successors. On the contrary, the Qur'an emphasizes that they were stronger, richer, and more established than the people who now walked through their remains: "Have they not seen how many a generation We destroyed before them, whom We had established upon the earth as We have not established you, and upon whom We sent abundant rain from the sky, and made rivers flow beneath them? Then We destroyed them for their sins, and brought forth after them another generation" (6:6). The ʿĀd were the people of the tall columns, "the like of which was not created in the lands" (89:7–8), whose pride expressed itself in the question the Qur'an records: "Who is mightier than us in strength?" — and the answer came in a wind that destroyed them (41:15–16). The Thamūd carved fortresses into the mountains and felt secure (26:149; 15:82? — 15:82 is correct: "they carved out dwellings in the mountains, secure"). The people of Lot lived in cities of wealth and trade. And all of them were destroyed — not by enemies, not by natural accident, but by the decree of God following their denial of the signs and their corruption of the earth. The verse's warning to the Quraysh — and to every powerful people since — is implicit but unmistakable: the ruins you walk among were once cities as alive as yours; their people were as confident in their walls, their trade, and their lineage as you are in yours; and the power that destroyed them is not impressed by the power that you possess. The Qur'an states the lesson in the verse of the believer from Pharaoh's people: "O my people! Truly I fear for you the like of the day of the confederates — the like of the plight of the people of Noah, and ʿĀd, and Thamūd" (40:30–31).

**"Will They Not Listen?" The Deafness Beneath the Visible**

The verse closes with its challenge: *afalā yasmaʿūn* — "Will they not listen?" The placement is exact: the ruins are visible, and the lesson must be heard — the eyes see the stones, but only the ears of the heart receive the warning they carry. The Qur'an elsewhere exposes the gap between seeing and understanding with a diagnosis that has become proverbial: "Have they not traveled through the earth, such that they have hearts by which to understand, or ears by which to hear? Truly it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts within the breasts that go blind" (22:46). The tourists of the ruins — ancient and modern — see everything and understand nothing, because the blindness that matters is the blindness of the heart; the eyes that look at the fallen columns of a dead civilization without hearing the warning are not seeing the columns at all. The verse's "Will they not listen?" is thus an invitation to a different mode of perception: to listen to history, to hear in the silence of the ruined dwellings the voices of the nations that once filled them — their markets, their arguments, their confidence, their denial — and to hear beneath those voices the one Voice that the Qur'an says the ruins still carry: the warning that the sunnah of God does not change, and that what happened to the generations of old can happen to any generation that walks the same road. Modern civilization has built its own museums of the fallen — the abandoned cities of vanished empires, the rusting monuments of collapsed ideologies, the empty palaces of deposed tyrants — and the verse's question applies to the modern visitor with undiminished force: does it not guide you? Have you not traveled and seen what happened to those before you? Will you not listen? The Qur'an's philosophy of history is written in this verse in miniature: history does not repeat by accident but by law — the moral law of divine justice — and the people who refuse to listen to the ruins are condemned to provide the ruins for the next generation's lesson.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:27

> **Have they not considered how We drive the water to the barren earth and bring forth crops thereby, whereof they and their cattle eat? Will they not see?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rain and the Barren Earth: The Second Sign**

From the ruins of the dead nations, the sūrah turns to the living earth: *awalam yaraw annā nasūqu al-māʾa ilā al-arḍi al-juruzi fa-nukhriju bihi zarʿan* — "Have they not considered how We drive the water to the barren earth and bring forth crops thereby?" The verse addresses the same deniers with a second question, and the sign it points to is the most common miracle in their experience: the rain. The word used for the land is precise: *al-arḍ al-juruz* — the barren earth, the land that is dry, sterile, and lifeless — the desert land of Arabia, which for most of the year gives no sign that it could ever produce anything. And the verb used for the sending of the rain is equally precise: *nasūqu* — "We drive" — the same verb used for herding camels, because the rain is pictured as being driven, like a flock, by the divine hand to the pasture of the barren land: the clouds are led across the sky and made to release their water exactly where the dead land waits for it. Then the second act follows: *fa-nukhriju bihi zarʿan* — "and We bring forth thereby crops" — the green shoots rising from the land that, weeks before, was dust. The verse is an annual spectacle in the Arabian peninsula and in every agricultural society: the land that was dead stirs, swells, and covers itself with green; and the verse's point is that the spectacle is not a curiosity but a proof — the same power that revives the barren earth is the power that will revive the dead, and the deniers who eat the crops of the revived earth while denying the resurrection are eating the evidence of the very thing they deny. The Qur'an makes the connection explicit in the verse of the Romans: "Look, then, at the effects of God's mercy — how He revives the earth after its death! Truly that is the Reviver of the dead, and He is Powerful over all things" (30:50); and again in the sūrah of the Detailed Signs: "And among His signs is that you see the earth barren; but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells. Truly He Who gives it life is the Giver of life to the dead; truly He is Powerful over all things" (41:39).

**The Resurrection Written in Every Spring**

The verse's argument — the revival of the dead earth as the proof of the revival of the dead — is one of the Qur'an's most frequently repeated analogies, and its repetition across the Book shows how central it was to the Qur'an's method of persuasion: "A sign for them is the dead earth: We give it life and bring forth from it grain, of which they eat" (36:33); "And We sent down from the sky blessed water, whereby We caused gardens to grow and the grain of the harvest, and tall date-palms with clustered spathes — as a provision for the servants; and thereby We revived a dead land. Thus shall be the coming forth" (50:9–11); "It is He Who sends down water from the sky; and thereby We bring forth the shoot of every plant... — in that are signs for a people who reflect" (6:99); and the sūrah of the Ḥajj, which ties the analogy directly to the resurrection: "O mankind! If you are in doubt concerning the Resurrection, [consider that] We created you from dust... And you see the earth barren; but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and grows every delightful kind. That is because God is the Truth, and because He gives life to the dead, and because He is Powerful over all things" (22:5–6). The analogy is not a mere poetic parallel; it is an argument from the regularity of the observable world to the power of its Author. The deniers of the resurrection based their denial on the apparent finality of death: when a man dies and his body decays and disperses into the earth, they said (as verse 10 of this sūrah records), he is lost — he cannot be re-created. The Qur'an answers with the earth itself: every year, the land that is dead — as dead as any buried body, drier and more lifeless than any grave — receives the water and produces life that no one who saw the land in its barrenness could have predicted. The One Who performs the annual revival of the dead earth in front of every observer is not the One to Whom the revival of the dead man presents any difficulty; and the spring that follows every winter is the resurrection enacted on a schedule that no denial can stop.

**The Water Cycle: A Sign in the Mechanics**

The verse's description of the rain — driven to the barren earth, bringing forth crops — participates in the Qur'an's broader picture of the water cycle, a picture that the Qur'an paints with a precision remarkable for its seventh-century context and confirmed by the science of later centuries. The Qur'an describes the winds as the drivers of the rain-bearing clouds: "And We send the winds as pollinators, and send down water from the sky, and give you to drink of it — and you are not its treasurers" (15:22); "And it is He Who sends the winds as heralds of glad tidings before His Mercy; and We send down from the sky pure water, that thereby We may revive a dead land and give drink to what We have created of cattle and men in abundance" (25:48–49). It describes the rain as measured and stored: "And We sent down from the sky water in measure, and lodged it in the earth; and We are able to take it away" (23:18). It describes the springs and rivers that flow from the stored water: "Have you not seen that God sends down water from the sky, and makes it flow as springs in the earth?" (39:21). And it returns, in verse after verse, to the conclusion that the cycle is not an accident but a provision: "He it is Who sends down water from the sky, of which you drink and of which trees grow, whereof you pasture your cattle. Thereby He causes to grow for you the crops, the olives, the date-palms, the grapes, and all manner of fruit. Truly in that is a sign for a people who reflect" (16:10–11). The modern reader, who understands the evaporation, the cloud formation, the rainfall, and the groundwater, does not find the Qur'an's picture outdated; he finds it confirmed — the water that falls on the barren land and brings forth the crops is the same water that was lifted from the sea and driven by the winds, and the whole cycle, which the Qur'an attributes to the direct action of God ("We drive," "We send down," "We bring forth"), is the visible machinery of the divine provision. The verse's "Have they not considered?" is an invitation to move from the mechanism to the Mechanic — to see in the water cycle, which the sciences describe so exactly, the sustaining will that the sciences describe but cannot explain away.

**The Ingratitude of Eating Without Seeing**

The verse's middle clause — *taʾkulu minhu hum wa anʿāmuhum* — "whereof they and their cattle eat" — is the detail that sharpens the indictment. The deniers were not distant from the sign; they were fed by it. The crops that the barren earth produced were the bread on their tables and the fodder in their mangers; the rain that revived the dead land was the difference between their survival and their starvation; and they ate, and pastured their herds, and never once connected the daily provision to the Provider or the annual revival to the resurrection. The Qur'an presses the point of this ingratitude in the verse of the Wāqiʿah: "Have you considered the water that you drink? Was it you who sent it down from the clouds, or were We the Sender? Had We willed, We could have made it bitter — so why do you not give thanks?" (56:68–70). The source commentary notes that the deniers failed twice: they failed to be thankful for the provision — treating the rain and the crops as their due, the ordinary furniture of existence — and they failed to draw the inference that the provision was designed to teach, namely, that the God Who revives the dead earth can revive the dead. The verse's closing challenge — *afalā yubṣirūn*, "Will they not see?" — names the failure precisely: they see the crops, but they do not see the sign; their eyes register the green but their hearts do not register the argument. The modern world has industrialized this blindness: food appears in markets with no visible connection to rain or soil, water flows from taps with no memory of the clouds, and the annual miracle of the reviving earth has been rendered invisible by the very efficiency of its distribution. The verse's question — "Will they not see?" — was never easier to answer with a comfortable "we see the food" and never harder to escape with honesty, for the one who truly looks at a loaf of bread — from the barren land, through the driven rain, to the bringing forth — is looking at a chain of mercies that leads, at every link, to the One Who drives the water and brings forth the crop and revives the dead.

**The Dead Heart and the Living Rain**

The spiritual commentators added to the verse's literal sense a reading that has nourished the inner life of Muslims for centuries: the barren earth is the dead heart, and the rain is the reminder. The Qur'an itself establishes the parallel: the water that revives the earth is the likeness of the guidance that revives the soul — "God sends down water from the sky, whereby the valleys flow, each according to its measure" (13:17), and the heart that receives the Qur'an is the earth that receives the rain. The verse of this sūrah, read in that light, describes the whole work of this very sūrah: the heart that has become barren — hardened by denial, dried by heedlessness, cracked by sin — is the *al-arḍ al-juruz*; the recitation of the signs is the driven rain; and the crops that come forth are the good deeds, the tears, the prostration, and the turning back that the rain of the reminder produces in the receptive heart. The believers of verse 15 — who fall prostrate when reminded of the signs — are the living earth: the reminder finds them soft and they produce; the deniers of verse 22 — who are reminded and turn away — are the rock that the rain cannot penetrate: "Then your hearts were hardened after that, so that they were like stones, or even harder — for among stones there are those from which rivers gush, and among them there are those that split and water issues from them" (2:74). The verse's question — "Will they not see?" — is thus addressed, in the final analysis, to every heart: do you see that you are the barren earth, and that the rain is falling now, and that the One Who drives the water to the dead land is driving this reminder to your dead heart — that you may produce, before the season closes, the crop for which you were made?

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:28

> **And they say, "When is this victory, if you are truthful?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Taunt of the Mockers**

The sūrah's final movement opens with the voice of the deniers interrupting the argument: *wa yaqūlūna matā hādhā al-fatḥu in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — "And they say, 'When is this victory, if you are truthful?'" The verse records a taunt, and the taunt has a history. Throughout the Makkan period, the Prophet ﷺ and the believers had been promising that the truth would prevail — that the denial of the signs, the persecution of the believers, and the mockery of the warners would not have the last word, and that God would decide between the believers and the deniers with a decisive decision. The deniers answered the promise with the classic weapon of the comfortable against the threatened: they demanded a date. "When is this victory?" — produce the schedule, fix the day, and we will believe you; and if you cannot, then your promise is empty and your truthfulness is exposed. The word *fatḥ* — rendered "victory" — is rich in the Qur'anic vocabulary: it means opening, victory, and decisive arbitration, and the Qur'an uses it for the opening of Makkah to Islam ("When comes the victory of God, and the opening," 110:1), for the divine decision between disputing parties ("Our Lord, decide (iftah) between us and our people in truth, for You are the best of deciders," 7:89), and for the judgment of the Day of Resurrection. The deniers' taunt plays on the word's breadth: whatever the believers mean by *fatḥ* — the victory of their religion in this world or the decisive judgment of the next — the deniers demand to know its date, and they couch the demand in the conditional of derision: "if you are truthful." The same taunt is recorded in sūrah after sūrah of the Makkan period, in nearly the same words: "And they say, 'When is this promise, if you are truthful?'" (36:48; 67:25; 21:38); "And they seek to hasten the punishment" (6:57; 13:6; 22:47; 27:71; 29:53–54; 38:16). The consistency of the mockery across the Makkan revelation shows how central the challenge of timing was: the deniers had reduced the entire prophetic message to a single testable proposition — if your victory is real, date it — and they treated the Prophet's inability to date it as proof of its falsehood.

**The Demand for a Sign and the Logic of the Wager**

The taunt of this verse belongs to a larger family of demands that the deniers made throughout the Makkan period, and understanding the family explains the taunt's real function. The deniers demanded dates for the victory; they demanded signs — "Why has a sign not been sent down upon him from his Lord?" (6:37; 10:20; 13:7; 21:5); they demanded that the punishment they were warned of be brought forward — "They challenge thee to hasten the punishment" (22:47; 29:53); and on one occasion they turned the demand into a direct challenge to God: "And when they said, 'O God! If this is the truth from Thee, rain down stones upon us from the sky, or bring us a painful punishment!'" (8:32). The Qur'an's response to the whole family of demands is consistent and revealing: the timing belongs to God alone, and the demands are not requests for guidance but attempts at escape. The demand for a dated victory is a wager, not an inquiry: the deniers were not seeking to know the date so that they could prepare; they were seeking a pretext for continued denial — if no date is given, they say the promise is false, and if a date were given and it passed without their predicted outcome, they would say the same. The Prophet ﷺ was forbidden from entering the game of dates: "They ask thee about the Hour: when is its anchorage? Say: 'Knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none unveils it at its time but He'" (7:187); and "They ask thee to hasten the punishment — but God will not fail in His Promise; and truly a day with thy Lord is as a thousand years of that which you number" (22:47). The second verse is the key to this sūrah's answer: the deniers were measuring the divine timetable by their own impatience, and the sūrah had already corrected their clock in verse 5 — the divine day is a thousand years of their reckoning. The taunt "When is this victory?" was answered, in effect, before it was asked: the victory is coming on the scale of the One Who directs the affair, and the questioners who demanded a date on the scale of their own lives were measuring the ocean with a thimble.

**The Victory That Came in History**

The full answer to the taunt was written, within the lifetime of the very generation that uttered it, in the events of history. The believers of Makkah — the small, persecuted community that the deniers mocked and promised to crush — did see the *fatḥ* in this world. The Prophet ﷺ returned to Makkah as its conqueror; the idols around the Kaʿbah were destroyed; the very men who had taunted "When is this victory?" stood before him as he entered the city, and the victory that Sūrat al-Naṣr celebrates — "When comes the victory of God and the opening, and thou seest mankind entering the religion of God in throngs" (110:1–2) — was the historical fulfillment of this sūrah's promise. The classical commentators noted the irony with satisfaction: the deniers asked when the victory would come, and the answer came in their own lifetime, through the very religion they had mocked — not by the sword of a superpower but by the steadfastness of a man and a community who had been promised the victory and had waited for it on the divine clock rather than the human one. The lesson for the believers of every age is embedded in the history: the promises of God in the Qur'an are not rhetorical; they are executed in time, though rarely on the schedule of the impatient — and the community that taunts the promise is often the community that lives to see it fulfilled.

**The Victory That History Cannot Contain**

Yet the verse's own context makes clear that the *fatḥ* the deniers asked about was not exhausted by the conquest of Makkah, for the next verse speaks of a "Day of Victory" on which "faith will not benefit those who disbelieved" — a description that can only fit the final, eschatological victory: the Day of Decision, when God distinguishes between the parties (as verse 25 promised), when every difference is settled, every taunt is answered, and the believers and the deniers receive their final, irreversible destinations. The *fatḥ* is thus double, like so much of the Qur'an's language: there is the opening that God grants His truthful servants in this world — the victory of the truth over falsehood, the establishment of the religion, the vindication of the patient — and there is the great Opening of the Hereafter, the Day of Decision, which no taunt can hasten and no denial can postpone. The deniers' question — "When is this victory?" — is answered by the sūrah with the full scope of the word: the victory you ask about is already unfolding in the world for those who believe — and the victory you refuse to see will arrive on the Day when the seeing will no longer benefit you. The verse thus leaves the deniers with the worst possible answer to their taunt: not "it will never come," which would have comforted them, and not a date, which would have ended their suspense — but the truth, which is that the victory is certain, that its first installment was already at work in the world around them, and that its final installment will come at an hour they cannot calculate, on a Day when their faith — if they wait until then to produce it — will not be accepted.

**The Believer's Answer to the Taunt**

For the believer who hears the same taunt in every age — from the mocker who asks where the promised victory of Islam is, from the skeptic who demands that God's justice show its schedule, from the inner voice of impatience that asks why the truth has not yet prevailed — the verse and its sequel prescribe the response. The believer does not fix dates, for the dates belong to God; the believer does not abandon the promise because of the delay, for the delay is part of the test; and the believer does not stop working while waiting, for the waiting of the believer is not idleness but preparation. The Qur'an's own counsel to the Prophet in the face of the taunt is the counsel to every believer: "And if they deny thee, say, 'My work is mine and your work is yours; you are quit of what I do, and I am quit of what you do'" (10:41); and the closing verses of this very sūrah will complete the counsel: turn away from the mockers, wait, and let them wait — for the two waitings are not the same, and the One Who directs the affair knows which waiting will end in the *fatḥ* and which will end in the tasting. The believer's certainty is not dependent on the fulfillment of the promise within his own lifetime; it is anchored in the character of the One Who promised — the Lord of the worlds, Whose promise is true, and Whose clock is not the clock of the mockers: "Truly God does not fail in His Promise" (13:31; 22:47; 39:20) — and the believer who remembers verse 5 of this sūrah can afford to wait on the divine scale, for he knows that the Day whose measure is a thousand years is already on its way, and that the taunt "When is this victory?" will be answered, finally and forever, by the victory itself.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:29

> **Say, "On the Day of Victory, faith will not benefit those who disbelieved; nor shall they be granted respite."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Taunt: A Victory That Closes the Door**

The mockers of verse 28 asked, "When is this victory, if you are truthful?" — and this verse is the answer, delivered as a command to the Prophet to speak: *qul yawma al-fatḥi lā yanfaʿu alladhīna kafarū īmānuhum wa lā hum yunẓarūn* — "Say: 'On the Day of Victory, faith will not benefit those who disbelieved; nor shall they be granted respite.'" The answer does not give the date they demanded, and the withholding of the date is itself the content of the answer: the victory they mock is coming, but when it comes, it will not arrive as an opportunity — it will arrive as a closure. The "Day of Victory" (*yawm al-fatḥ*) is understood by the commentators as the Day of Decision — the Day of Resurrection, when God distinguishes finally between the parties, the *fatḥ* of which verse 25 spoke ("thy Lord will distinguish between them on the Day of Resurrection") and the *fatḥ* that the deniers themselves will face as the tasting of the Fire they denied. Some commentators also read into the phrase the decisive victories of this world — the Day of Badr, the first great arbitration between the believers and the idolaters, which the Qur'an itself calls a *fatḥ* in the sūrah of the Spoils: "If you were seeking a decision, the decision has come to you" (8:19) — and the principle of the verse applies to every divine decision in history: when the decree arrives, the time of choosing is over. But the verse's full scope is the final Day, and its sentence is universal: on that Day, the faith of those who disbelieved will not benefit them. The verse thus answers the taunt with the most unsettling of all possibilities: the victory is real, it is certain, and it is coming — but it is coming as the event that ends, forever, the possibility that the mockers' late faith could save them.

**The Faith That Arrives Too Late**

Why will the faith of the disbelievers not benefit them on that Day? The verse does not answer with a divine caprice but with the inner logic of faith itself, which the Qur'an develops in the parallel passages. Faith, in the Qur'anic understanding, is trust in the unseen exercised while the unseen is still unseen — the believers are described, from the second verse of the second sūrah, as "those who believe in the Unseen" (2:3). The value of faith lies precisely in its being a choice made before the evidence becomes overwhelming: the believer believes in the resurrection before seeing it, in the justice of God before experiencing it, in the meeting with the Lord while the meeting is still a matter of trust. On the Day of Victory, all of that changes: the unseen becomes the seen, the resurrection becomes a witnessed fact, the judgment becomes a present reality — and the "faith" that the disbelievers produce at that moment is not faith but acknowledgment, not trust but submission to the inevitable. The Qur'an describes the difference with complete precision in the sūrah of the Cattle: "On the day when some of the signs of thy Lord come, faith will not benefit a soul that did not believe before, or that did not earn goodness through its faith. Say: 'Wait — we too are waiting'" (6:158). And the sūrah of Ghāfir records the general law of that moment, drawn from the history of every people that waited for the sight before believing: "But their faith did not avail them when they saw Our might — the established way of God that has passed among His servants — and there the disbelievers lost" (40:85). The Pharaoh who believed when the sea closed over him was told, "Now? But before this thou didst rebel and wast among the workers of corruption!" (10:90–91); the guilty of verse 12 of this very sūrah, who announce at the judgment, "We have seen and we have heard... Truly we are certain," make the same declaration at the same fatal hour — and this verse states the verdict upon it in advance: their certainty, arriving after the sight, is the certainty of the cornered, and it will not benefit them.

**The Logic of the Deadline: Why the Door Must Close**

The verse's second clause — *wa lā hum yunẓarūn*, "nor shall they be granted respite" — completes the sentence with the finality that the deniers' taunt had demanded. The verb *yunẓarūn* carries the sense of being granted a delay, a deferral, a looking-over with mercy; the verse says that no such delay will be granted on that Day. The reason is not that God is unwilling to be merciful — the whole Qur'an testifies to the opposite — but that the mercy of the delay had already been given, in full, during the life of the world. The disbelievers had their respite: the long life that verse 35 of the sūrah of Fāṭir holds up against them — "Did We not give you long life, enough for whosoever would reflect to reflect therein? And the warner came unto you — so taste [the punishment]!" (35:37). They had the years, the health, the faculties of hearing and sight and hearts (v. 9); they had the warner (v. 3); they had the reminders and the signs and the lesser punishments sent to turn them back (v. 21); and they spent the respite demanding more respite — "When is this victory?" — as though the delay of the decree were an invitation to further delay of their own amendment. The logic of the deadline is the logic of every meaningful test: an examination whose deadline could be extended forever would test nothing, and a faith that could be produced at any moment — even after the answers were posted — would be worth nothing. The Qur'an's doctrine of the closed door is thus not a contradiction of its doctrine of mercy but the boundary that gives mercy its meaning: the door of repentance is open through the whole of life — "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against their own souls! Do not despair of the Mercy of God" (39:53) — and it closes at the moment when the purpose of its being open has been finally, definitively refused. The respite that was granted is the proof of the justice of the refusal of any further respite: the mockers who asked "when" were told, in effect, that the "when" they were asking about is the moment when asking will end.

**The Victory as the End of the Argument**

Read against the taunt that provoked it, the verse is the sūrah's final rhetorical stroke. The deniers asked the believers to date the victory as a test of their truthfulness; the answer hands the test back to them: on the Day of Victory, the truthfulness of the believers will be demonstrated — and the demonstration will be the condemnation of those who demanded it. The victory they mocked as the believers' fantasy is revealed as the event of their own undoing; the "if you are truthful" of their taunt is answered by the actual arrival of the truth, which will not benefit those who waited to believe in it until they could no longer deny it. The verse thus converts the mockers' chronology against them: they imagined the victory as a future event whose delay proved its falsehood; the Qur'an reveals it as a fixed appointment whose delay was mercy and whose arrival is judgment. And for the believer, the verse is the final clarification of the meaning of the present: this life — the interval between the taunt and the victory — is the only time in which faith counts, the only window in which the door is open, the only respite in which respite can be used. The believer who reads verse 29 does not wait for the Day of Victory to believe; he believes now, precisely so that when the Day arrives he will not need the faith that cannot benefit — he will already be among those whose faith was real when faith was a choice, and whose reward is the welcome of the Gardens of refuge that verse 19 described.

## Sūrah al-Sajdah 32:30

> **Then turn away from them and wait; they, too, are waiting.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Command: Turn Away from the Mockers**

The sūrah closes with the command that resolves the whole encounter with the deniers: *fa-aʿriḍ ʿanhum wa-ntaẓir innahum muntaẓirūn* — "Then turn away from them and wait; they, too, are waiting." The first verb — *aʿriḍ* — means to turn aside, to withdraw, to turn one's face away; it is the same verb used in verse 22 for the crime of the guilty, who were reminded of the signs and then "turned away" (*aʿraḍa*) from them, and its repetition here is deliberate: the Prophet is commanded to turn away from the turners-away — to meet their aversion with a disciplined withdrawal that is not defeat but discharge. The command does not mean the abandonment of the mission: the Prophet was commanded to warn, and he continued to warn until the end of his life. It means the end of the futile disputation — the release from the endless argument with those whose taunt of verse 28 ("When is this victory?") showed that they were not seeking truth but seeking sport. The Qur'an elsewhere commands the same strategic withdrawal from the mockers: "And when thou seest those who engage in mockery of Our signs, turn away from them until they engage in another discourse" (6:68); "Take to forgiveness, and command what is right, and turn away from the ignorant" (7:199); and the sūrah of the Ornaments gives the Prophet the same counsel in the face of the same taunt: "So turn away from them and say, 'Peace!' — for they will come to know" (43:89). The wisdom of the command is psychological as well as strategic: the mocker is fed by the attention of the mocked, and the argument that the mocker stages is a performance that requires an audience; the believer who turns away withdraws the fuel, leaving the performance to play to an empty house. The Prophet ﷺ himself, whose whole mission was mercy, did not waste his words on those who had made mockery their profession; he delivered, warned, and moved on — and this verse instructs the believer of every age to do the same: not to abandon the field of calling, but to refuse the field of mockery.

**Wait: The Active Expectation of the Believer**

The second verb — *wa-ntaẓir* — "and wait" — names the posture that replaces the argument: the expectation of the divine decree. The word *intizār* in the Qur'anic vocabulary is not the passive idleness that the word "waiting" suggests in English; it is the alert, expectant stance of the soldier awaiting the command, of the farmer awaiting the harvest, of the sentry awaiting the dawn. The believer who waits in the Qur'anic sense does not stop working; he continues his calling, his worship, his charity, and his preparation, while his heart rests in the certainty that the decree of God is on its way. The sūrahs of the Makkan period close with this command so often that it functions as a signature of the Makkan revelation: "Say: 'O my people, act according to your position; I too am acting. Then you will come to know...' — Say: 'Wait! I too am among the waiting'" (6:135, 6:158); "And wait — truly I am with you among the waiting" (7:71); "So wait! I am with you among the waiting" (11:122); "And say: 'Wait! I am with you among the waiting'" (20:135); "Say: 'Wait — I am with you among the waiting'" (52:31). The repetition is the Qur'an's way of training the believers in the specific virtue that the Makkan period demanded: patience with the delay of the divine victory, certainty that outlasts the mockery, and the quiet, unshakeable expectation that the promise of God — unlike the promises of the mockers' idols — will be fulfilled. The waiting of the believer is the waiting of verse 5's divine clock: the One Who directs the affair from Heaven to earth, in a day whose measure is a thousand years, is not late; He is precisely on time, and the believer's waiting is the alignment of the human heart with the divine timetable.

**They, Too, Are Waiting: The Ironic Reversal**

The verse's final clause delivers its most exquisite irony: *innahum muntaẓirūn* — "they, too, are waiting." Both parties are waiting; the difference is in what they wait for and what the waiting will bring. The disbelievers are waiting, as Ibn al-Jawzī and the commentators explain, for the destruction of the believers — for the failure of the Prophet's mission, for the return of the old order, for the death of the religion that was threatening their world; they waited for the Muslims to be wiped out as they had waited for the Prophet to be silenced, and they expected, with the confidence of the comfortable, that time would prove them right. The believers are waiting for the victory of God — for the *fatḥ* that verse 28's mockers demanded to have dated and verse 29 revealed as the Day of decision. Both wait; but the two waitings are not equal, and the verse's irony cuts in two directions. On the surface, the irony is at the expense of the mockers' confidence: they are waiting for the very thing that will destroy them — they await, as the sūrah of the Spider says of their predecessors, "the like of the days of those who passed away before them" (10:102), not knowing that they are waiting for their own sentence. The source commentary draws out the deeper irony: the disbelievers are waiting for their own destruction without knowing it; the "victory" they mock and anticipate is the event that will seal their fate, so that their waiting is a waiting for doom dressed as a waiting for triumph. But the verse's irony also touches the believers, gently: they, too, are waiting — the believers are not promised that the victory will come in their lifetime, or that the mockery will end before the end; they are promised only that the waiting is meaningful, that the One for Whose decree they wait is the Lord of the worlds, and that the waiting itself — patient, expectant, faithful — is the very posture that the sūrah's believers (the people of prostration, night prayer, and charity of verses 15–17) were described by. The ending of the sūrah is thus perfectly balanced: it does not close with a cry of triumph but with a calm command — turn away, wait, and know that the mockers are waiting too — and the calm is itself the victory, the confidence of the soul that has placed its affair with the One Who directs the affair.

**The Two Waitings in History**

The history that followed the revelation of this sūrah enacted both waitings with a precision that the first readers could not have foreseen and later readers cannot ignore. The Quraysh waited for the Muslims to be destroyed; within two decades, they were entering the religion of the man they had mocked in throngs, and the Prophet ﷺ stood in the courtyard of the Kaʿbah on the day of the *fatḥ* — the very victory whose date they had demanded — and asked the assembled people, who had tortured, exiled, and fought him, "What do you think I will do to you?" They answered, "You are a noble brother, the son of a noble brother" — and he replied with the words of his brother Joseph: "No blame upon you this day; go, for you are free." The mockers' waiting ended in the mercy of the one they had mocked; the believers' waiting ended in the victory they had been promised while they were still a persecuted handful. And the same pattern has repeated in every age and will repeat until the last age: the mockers of the truth wait for its extinction, and it outlives them; the believers wait for the decree of God, and it arrives — if not in their lifetime, then on the Day when the two waitings are finally distinguished, and the waiting of the believers is revealed as the patience of those who knew the promise, and the waiting of the mockers is revealed as the suspense of those who were waiting, all along, for the event that would answer the taunt "When is this victory?" with the victory itself.

**The Closing of the Sūrah: From Alif Lām Mīm to the Waiting**

The sūrah that opened with three letters of mystery and a declaration of the Book's descent closes with a command of patience and a note of irony. The whole arc has been traversed: the revelation from the Lord of the worlds (vv. 1–3); the Creator Who made beautiful all things and began man from clay (vv. 4–9); the deniers of the resurrection and their meeting with the Lord they denied (vv. 10–14); the believers who prostrate at the signs, shun their beds, and spend from their provision, awaiting the reward that no soul knows (vv. 15–17); the unequal ends of the believer and the iniquitous (vv. 18–20); the lesser punishment sent in mercy and the greatest wrong of the reminded who turned away (vv. 21–22); Moses and the Book, and the leaders who guided while they were patient and certain (vv. 23–24); the distinction of the Day of Resurrection over all the differences of history (v. 25); the ruins of the destroyed generations and the rain on the barren earth (vv. 26–27); the taunt of the mockers and the answer of the closed door (vv. 28–29) — and now the end: turn away from them and wait. The ending is the sūrah's final lesson, and it is addressed to every believer who has ever read these verses in the face of mockery: the argument has been made, the evidence has been presented, the reminders have been delivered — and what remains is not more debate with the mockers but the posture of the certain: turn away from the futile, wait upon the decree, and let them wait. For the two waitings are not the same, and the One Who directs the affair from Heaven to earth — the Knower of the Unseen and the seen, the Mighty, the Merciful — has written the end of both: the mockers wait for a victory that will close the door upon them, and the believers wait for a meeting that will open for them the Gardens of refuge, as a welcome for what they used to do. The sūrah of the Prostration ends, fittingly, not with a raised voice but with a settled heart: the believer turns away, and waits — and the waiting itself, in the light of everything the sūrah has said, is already a form of the prostration: the creature, certain of his Lord, at rest in the decree, standing patiently before the One Whose victory does not fail and Whose promise does not lag.
