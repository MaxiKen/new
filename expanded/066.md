# Sūrah al-Taḥrīm (Chapter 66) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah That Began in the Prophet's Own Household**

Al-Taḥrīm, "The Prohibition," is the sixty-sixth chapter of the Qur'an, and its opening word gives it its name. The first verse addresses the Prophet ﷺ with a gentle yet searching question — *"O Prophet! Why do you forbid that which God has made lawful unto you?"* — and in that single line the sūrah announces the unusual ground on which it stands: it is revelation occasioned not by a battlefield, a treaty, or a public controversy, but by the private life of the Prophet's own home. For this reason the chapter has been known by several names. Al-Suyūṭī in his *al-Itqān* records that it is also called *al-Mutaḥḥaram*, "The Forbidden," and because it speaks directly into the Prophet's ﷺ domestic situation, some scholars styled it *Sūrat al-Nabī*, "The Sūrah of the Prophet." Its famous opening address — *limā tuḥarrimu*, "why do you forbid?" — likewise served some as a title in its own right.

The time and place of revelation are matters of broad agreement with a minor scholarly difference. The sūrah is Madinan, revealed in approximately the seventh year of the Hijrah (circa 628–629 CE), a period crowded with major events in the life of the young Muslim community. A minority of scholars — noted by Ālūsī — held that while the first nine verses are Madinan, the final three are Makkan; but the overwhelming view places the whole sūrah in Madinah, where its concerns — the Prophet's household, oaths and their expiation, the duty of protecting one's family, and the exemplary women of sacred history — all naturally belong. What matters most for understanding the sūrah is not its precise date but its occasion, for this is one of the clearest cases in the Qur'an where revelation descends upon the concrete, human texture of a single domestic moment and lifts it into universal instruction.

**A Household Incident That Became a Lesson for All Time**

The opening verses presuppose an event in which the Prophet ﷺ, out of consideration for the feelings of his wives, declared something forbidden to himself that God had not forbidden — and swore an oath upon it. The classical commentators record two principal accounts of what that "something" was, and both are worth setting down carefully, because the sūrah's meaning does not depend on choosing between them.

The first, preserved in the most authoritative collections, is the incident of the honey. ʿĀʾishah reported that the Prophet ﷺ used to spend time with Zaynab bint Jaḥsh and drink honey at her house; she and Ḥafṣah, moved by a shared possessiveness natural to wives, agreed that whichever of them he next visited would say, "I smell *maghāfīr* (a resin of unpleasant odor) on you." When the Prophet ﷺ heard this from Ḥafṣah, he replied that he had only drunk honey at Zaynab's house and declared that he would not drink it again — and it was in connection with this that the opening verses were revealed. This report stands in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The second account, transmitted by other authorities including al-Wāḥidī in his *Asbāb al-Nuzūl*, relates the incident to Māriyah the Copt, the bondwoman gifted to the Prophet ﷺ: that he was with her in Ḥafṣah's apartment on Ḥafṣah's allotted day, that Ḥafṣah was distressed, and that he swore to make her forbidden to himself and asked Ḥafṣah to keep the matter secret — which she then disclosed to ʿĀʾishah. Many scholars, including al-Ṭabarī, accepted the principle that the verse may apply to more than one concrete occasion, since its teaching is general: no one, not even the Prophet ﷺ, may render unlawful what God has rendered lawful.

What unites both accounts is the profound and comforting truth they together disclose. The Qur'an does not present its Prophet ﷺ as a figure beyond human feeling. He sought to please his wives; he made a hasty oath; and God — the Most Merciful — gently corrected him. The sūrah thereby performs, in its very first movement, one of the great functions of revelation: it shows that even the noblest of human beings remains human, and that the path of virtue includes the experience of being lovingly corrected.

**The Architecture of the Sūrah**

The sūrah's twelve verses unfold in a carefully balanced structure. Verses 1 through 5 concern the Prophet ﷺ and his wives, moving from the gentle question of the first verse, through the legislation of oath-expiation in the second, the delicate disclosure of the third, the address to the two wives in the fourth, and the warning of the fifth that God could replace them with better wives. Verses 6 through 9 then widen the lens from the Prophet's household to the whole believing community and, beyond it, to the disbelievers: the believers are commanded to protect themselves and their families from the Fire (verse 6), the disbelievers are told that their excuses will avail nothing on the Day of Reckoning (verse 7), the believers are summoned to sincere repentance and given the glad promise of light on the Day of Judgment (verse 8), and the Prophet ﷺ is commanded to strive against disbelievers and hypocrites (verse 9). The final three verses (10–12) close the sūrah with four portraits of women from sacred history — two examples for the disbelievers (the wives of Noah and Lot) and two examples for the believers (the wife of Pharaoh and Mary daughter of ʿImrān) — portraits whose whole point is that no one is saved by association and no one is condemned by environment, but each soul answers for itself.

This structure is not accidental. The sūrah begins with a concrete domestic problem and ends with the universal principle that resolves it: *individual responsibility*. The wives of the Prophet ﷺ are reminded, gently but unmistakably, that their nearness to him does not guarantee them anything before God — just as, in the other direction, the wife of Pharaoh attained Paradise despite her proximity to the worst of tyrants. The sūrah's movement is thus from the particular to the universal, from the private to the cosmic, from a honey pot or a household secret to the Fire and the Garden and the light that runs before the believers on the Day of Judgment.

**The Relation of This Sūrah to What Precedes It**

Al-Taḥrīm stands in a deliberate conversation with the sūrahs around it. The preceding chapter, al-Ṭalāq (Chapter 65), laid down in detail the laws of divorce and the treatment of women, closing with the reminder that God created the heavens and the earth in truth and that He encompasses all things in knowledge. Al-Taḥrīm now turns from the general law of marriage and divorce to the Prophet's own household, showing that the principles taught to all believers apply first and most searchingly to the Prophet ﷺ himself and to those nearest to him. And its closing verses reach back to al-Mumtaḥanah (Chapter 60), whose final verses speak of the believing women who made their own pledge to the Prophet ﷺ — for the four women of verses 10–12 are, in a sense, the archetypes of that pledge, both its triumph (Āsiyah and Mary) and its betrayal (the wives of Noah and Lot). The sūrah thus sits within the Qur'an's larger teaching that prophethood confers no spiritual advantage upon relatives, and that faith is the only currency that passes at the final reckoning.

**The Lasting Relevance of a Private Story**

A modern reader might ask why a fourteen-hundred-year-old household episode should occupy such a place in scripture. The answer is that the episode is the vehicle, not the message. The message is a set of truths that every generation needs to hear again: that only God legislates the lawful and the forbidden, and no human authority — not even the most beloved — may add to or subtract from that legislation; that oaths must not become prisons, and God has mercifully provided a way out of every hasty oath; that marriage is built on trust, discretion, and the guarding of confidence; that spouses are accountable for their own souls and cannot borrow righteousness from one another; that the family is the first place where faith must be taught and the Fire must be feared; that repentance is always open and sincere repentance is the surest road to Paradise; and that in the final analysis, each soul stands alone before its Lord, bearing only what it earned. These are not the concerns of one household in seventh-century Madinah; they are the permanent concerns of every household, in every age, that seeks to live under God's authority.

What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary, treating each of the twelve verses as a complete and independent discourse, drawing upon the Arabic language and its roots, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, Ibn ʿAbbās, and others), the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, and the living reasoning that connects this ancient text to the conditions of the modern human being.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:1

> **O Prophet! Why dost thou forbid that which God has made lawful unto thee, seeking the good pleasure of thy wives? And God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Rebuke That Begins in Tenderness**

The sūrah opens with a question addressed not to the believers at large but to the Prophet ﷺ himself, and the first thing to notice is the tone. God does not thunder; He asks. *"O Prophet! Why do you forbid that which God has made lawful unto you?"* The classical commentators lingered on this gentleness. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī both observe that the question is not the interrogation of a wrongdoer but the loving correction (*ʿitāb*) of one who is dear — the way a parent asks a beloved child, "Why did you do that?" not to humiliate but to awaken awareness. The very address by the noble title *yā ayyuhā al-nabiyy* — "O Prophet," rather than the more severe "O Messenger" — is itself a mercy, for the title of prophethood is here invoked precisely at the moment when the Prophet ﷺ is being asked to remember the full weight of his office. He who is sent to convey God's law must not, even in a private domestic matter, appear to alter that law.

The substance of the rebuke is profound. The Prophet ﷺ had declared something forbidden to himself — *tuḥarrimu*, from the root *ḥ-r-m*, the same root that yields *ḥarām*, the forbidden, and *ḥurumah*, sanctity — when God had made it lawful, *aḥalla Allāhu laka*, from *ḥ-l-l*, to loosen, to make lawful, whence *ḥalāl*. In the Islamic conception, the pair *ḥalāl* and *ḥarām* — lawful and forbidden — is not a matter of human taste, social convention, or even prophetic preference. It is the exclusive prerogative of the Lawgiver. The verse therefore does not merely correct a single domestic decision; it announces a fundamental principle of the faith: *the authority to permit and to prohibit belongs to God alone.* The Prophet ﷺ himself is made the vehicle of this lesson, so that no one after him can ever claim the right to forbid what God has permitted.

**The Two Accounts of the Occasion: Honey and the Bondwoman**

The occasion of revelation (*sabab al-nuzūl*) is recorded in two principal traditions, both of which the commentators preserve, and both of which converge on the same teaching. The first, and most widely authenticated, is preserved in both Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim from ʿĀʾishah, the Mother of the Believers:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ used to stay with Zaynab bint Jaḥsh and drink honey at her house. So Ḥafṣah and I agreed that whichever of us he visited would say: 'I find upon you the smell of maghāfīr — have you eaten maghāfīr?' So he visited one of them and she said that to him. He replied: 'No, rather I drank honey at Zaynab bint Jaḥsh's house, and I will never do it again.' Then this verse was revealed: 'O Prophet! Why do you forbid that which God has made lawful unto you...' up to '...if you two turn in repentance to God' (66:1–4) — addressed to ʿĀʾishah and Ḥafṣah — 'When the Prophet confided to one of his wives a matter...' (66:3), referring to his saying: 'Rather, I drank honey.'"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5267, 6691; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1474)

The second account, transmitted by al-Wāḥidī in his *Asbāb al-Nuzūl* and others, relates the verse to Māriyah the Copt: that the Prophet ﷺ was with her in Ḥafṣah's apartment on Ḥafṣah's day, that Ḥafṣah was grieved, and that he swore to make her forbidden to himself and bade Ḥafṣah keep the matter secret — which she then divulged to ʿĀʾishah. Al-Ṭabarī, who narrates both, concludes with the sound principle that a verse may be revealed upon more than one occasion or apply to more than one instance, since the command it teaches is general. What the two reports share is the essential human scene: the Prophet ﷺ, desiring to please his wives and to keep peace in his home, used the language of prohibition upon a lawful thing, and God intervened to restore the boundary that no creature may move.

**"Seeking the Good Pleasure of Your Wives": The Honor in a Human Motive**

The verse names the Prophet's motive with remarkable precision: *tabtaghī marḍāta azwājik* — "seeking the good pleasure of your wives." The word *marḍāh* comes from *riḍā*, contentment and approval, and the phrase is not condemnatory but descriptive. The Qur'an does not say that the Prophet ﷺ sinned or transgressed; it says he sought to please those he loved. There is a quiet tenderness in this, and an important reassurance for every married believer. The desire to please one's spouse is not itself a fault — it is a natural and even noble inclination. The fault, if any, lies only in allowing that inclination to override the law of God. The verse thus teaches a delicate art of balance: love your spouse, seek their contentment, honor their feelings — but never purchase their approval at the price of God's command.

This is a lesson modern life sorely needs. How often do people, to please a spouse, a friend, a employer, or a peer group, cross a boundary God has drawn — a small dishonest "white lie," a forbidden financial arrangement, a social indulgence, a compromised integrity? The verse's answer is that even the Prophet ﷺ, the most beloved of creation to God, was gently pulled back the moment his desire to please his wives threatened to blur a divine boundary. If he was not exempt, none of us is.

**The Deep Principle: Only God Makes Things Lawful or Forbidden**

Beneath the household scene lies one of the great theological principles of Islam, one that the Qur'an states repeatedly and emphatically. Only God has the right to declare what is lawful and what is forbidden, and no human being — prophet, saint, ruler, or scholar — may add to or subtract from that legislation. The Qur'an makes this explicit in a verse that reads almost as a commentary on this very incident:

> *"O you who believe! Do not forbid the good things which God has made lawful for you, and do not transgress. Indeed, God does not love the transgressors."* (Qur'an 5:87)

And again, in a verse addressed to the idolaters who presumed to legislate for themselves:

> *"And do not say, concerning the falsehood your tongues utter: 'This is lawful and this is forbidden,' forging lies against God. Indeed, those who forge lies against God will not succeed."* (Qur'an 16:116)

Ibn Taymiyyah built an entire dimension of his theology upon this principle: the *ḥalāl* is what God permitted, the *ḥarām* is what God forbade, and what He was silent about is an act of His indulgence, not a matter for human invention. To forbid the lawful is itself a form of transgression, and to permit the forbidden is its mirror. The incident of this sūrah thus becomes a permanent warning against two opposite errors that have troubled religious communities in every age: the error of *legalism*, which multiplies prohibitions God never made, and the error of *license*, which dissolves prohibitions God did make. Both errors move the boundary-stone that God set in place, and both are rebuked in the Prophet's own person here.

**The Mercy That Closes the Verse**

The verse does not end with the rebuke. It ends with two of the most consoling of the Divine Names: *wa Allāhu Ghafūrun Raḥīm* — "and God is Forgiving, Merciful." The commentators point out that the verse opens with a question, passes through a gentle correction, and then closes with forgiveness and mercy, as though to assure the Prophet ﷺ — and through him every believer — that even the slips of the sincere are met, not with wrath, but with the Lord's forgiveness. Al-Qurṭubī notes that *Ghafūr* is an intensive form, indicating a forgiveness that covers much and returns again and again, while *Raḥīm* denotes the mercy that endures. The placement of these Names here teaches that God's correction of His beloved servants is itself an act of mercy: He does not leave them in error; He guides them out of it, and He forgives.

There is a psychological wisdom here too. A rebuke that ends in mercy heals the very wound it opens; a correction wrapped in love invites change rather than resentment. God models for parents, teachers, and leaders the perfect manner of correction: name the fault honestly, but close with the door of forgiveness open. The Prophet ﷺ learned this lesson so completely that it became the texture of his own character — for he is described in the Qur'an itself as *"merciful to the believers"* (9:128), and he said of himself and his mission:

> *"I was not sent to curse, but I was sent as a mercy."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2599)

**Why the Prophet's Example Matters for the Ordinary Believer**

Finally, the verse contains a subtle and powerful logic that every believer should grasp. The Qur'an could have taught the principle "do not forbid the lawful" as an abstract command — and indeed it does so in 5:87. But it chose also to embed that command in the life of the Prophet ﷺ, in a moment of tender human frailty, so that the lesson would be carried not as a rule to memorize but as a story to feel. The result is that the Prophet's ﷺ humanity becomes, paradoxically, a source of comfort and guidance rather than embarrassment. If the Prophet ﷺ himself could be gently corrected over a honey pot or a household secret, then the ordinary believer's own slips — the hasty oath, the impulsive prohibition, the promise made in the heat of feeling — are not the end of the road. They are the very material out of which God's forgiveness and guidance are woven. The sūrah begins by teaching that the door of return is always open, and that the Most Merciful is never far from the one who turns back to Him.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:2

> **God has already ordained for you the absolution of your oaths. And God is your Master. He is the Knower, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God's Mercy Anticipates Human Weakness**

The second verse flows directly from the first. The Prophet ﷺ had, in the incident just recounted, declared something forbidden to himself and — in the fuller accounts — bound that declaration with an oath. The first verse gently corrected the act of forbidding the lawful; this second verse now addresses the oath itself, and it does so with a sentence of astonishing comfort: *qad faraḍa Allāhu lakum taḥillata aymānikum* — "God has already ordained for you the absolution of your oaths."

The key word is *taḥillah*, from the root *ḥ-l-l* — the very same root from which *ḥalāl* (lawful) is formed, and the precise opposite of the *taḥrīm* (prohibition) of the first verse. *Taḥillah* is the dissolving, the loosening, the releasing of an oath — the appointed way by which a sworn commitment may be lawfully undone. The verse thus sets two movements of mercy in parallel: just as the first verse loosened the false prohibition the Prophet ﷺ had placed upon the lawful, so this verse loosens the oath by which he had bound himself. What man ties in haste, God has already provided the means to untie.

The verb *faraḍa* — "He has ordained, made obligatory, prescribed" — is itself striking. Ordinarily, *farḍ* denotes a duty placed upon human beings. Here, however, God *ordains* a relief, a way out, an absolution. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī note this inversion with admiration: the same word that imposes obligation elsewhere here imposes mercy, as though God were saying, "I have made it incumbent upon My own generosity that you should have a way out of your oaths." It is as if the relief itself is a divine obligation — not an obligation upon man, but a promise God has undertaken. The believer is therefore never trapped by his own words; the Most Merciful has already written a door into every wall that a hasty oath might build.

**What Is the Absolution of an Oath? The Legislation of Expiation**

The "absolution" here refers to the *kaffārat al-yamīn*, the expiation of a broken oath, whose details the Qur'an has already given in full in Sūrat al-Māʾidah:

> *"God will not take you to task for what is unintentional in your oaths, but He will take you to task for the oaths you have solemnly sworn. Its expiation is the feeding of ten needy people with the average of what you feed your own families, or clothing them, or freeing a slave. And whoever cannot find that, then fasting for three days. That is the expiation of your oaths when you have sworn. And guard your oaths. Thus does God make clear to you His signs, that you may be grateful."* (Qur'an 5:89)

This verse is the constitution of oath-expiation in Islam, and it deserves careful reflection because it reveals the mercy and the justice of God working together. A believer who swears an oath and then wishes — for good reason — to act contrary to it has three graduated means of release. First and highest: feed ten poor people with the average of what one feeds one's own family, or clothe them — a real material transfer that heals, through generosity, the rupture that the broken oath represents. Second: free a slave, which in the seventh-century context was an act of liberation with deep social consequence. Third, for the one who lacks the means: fast three days, an act of self-discipline that trains the very soul that was too hasty with its tongue. The expiation is thus arranged in descending order of what one can bear, so that no one — rich or poor — is left without a way out.

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both explain the deep logic here. An oath is a solemn invocation of God's name upon a promise; when that promise is broken, the name of God has, in a sense, been taken lightly. The expiation restores the balance — it is not a "penalty" in the punitive sense, but a *restoration*, an act that mends the torn fabric and honors the name that was invoked. It is, in the Qur'an's own economy, the price at which a soul buys back its own uprightness. And notice the mercy in the very structure: the expiation is *graduated* and *proportionate*, never crushing, always possible.

**What Actually Happened in the Prophet's Own Case**

The commentators discuss how this verse applied to the Prophet ﷺ specifically. The Jalālayn and others record that when the verse came, the Prophet ﷺ freed a slave — or, according to another report, he simply acted upon the verse's teaching — in expiation of his oath. Al-Ṭabarī preserves the fuller discussion: some maintained that God's absolution of the oath in this verse was itself the release, so that no further expiation was needed, while others held that the Prophet ﷺ performed the expiation as a model for his community. The scholarly reconciliation is instructive: whatever the precise detail of the Prophet's own case, the community learned from this episode the general rule — *every Muslim who wishes to depart from a sworn oath has a lawful, merciful way to do so.* The Prophet's ﷺ humanity became, once again, the occasion for teaching mercy to all.

**"God Is Your Master": The True Direction of Servitude**

The verse then turns to a profound declaration: *wa Allāhu mawlākum* — "and God is your Master." The word *mawlā* is one of the richest in the Arabic language, carrying at once the senses of *master*, *protector*, *guardian*, *ally*, and *patron*. Al-Qurṭubī gathers these meanings and shows how each illuminates the context. God is the *Master* of the believers — and therefore it is to Him, not to human beings, that ultimate obedience is owed; the Prophet ﷺ, however beloved, is Himself God's servant, and the lesson of this sūrah is precisely that no created being stands between the believer and God's law. God is the *Protector* — and therefore the believer who finds himself entangled in a hasty oath need not despair, for his Master has provided the release. God is the *Patron* — the one who undertakes the believer's affairs, who has "ordained" the absolution as a mercy owed to His own servants.

There is a subtle correction of perspective here. The Prophet ﷺ had been moved, in the incident, by his desire to please his wives; the verse redirects attention upward, to the one Master whose good pleasure should govern all. This is not a devaluation of human relationships but their reordering: love for spouse, family, and all creatures is good and God-commanded, but it must always flow *within* the love and obedience owed to God, never against it. The word *mawlā* thus quietly restores the hierarchy that the first verse's gentle question had begun to reestablish.

**"The Knower, the Wise": Why God's Legislation Is Always Right**

The verse closes with two further Names: *al-ʿAlīm*, the Knower, and *al-Ḥakīm*, the Wise. Their placement is deliberate and full of meaning. Why close a verse about oath-expiation with the attributes of knowledge and wisdom? Al-Rāzī and others explain: because the whole point of the verse is that God's legislation — His dividing of lawful from forbidden, His provision of a way out of oaths — is grounded in perfect knowledge and perfect wisdom. God *knows* human weakness; He knows that people swear hastily, under emotion, in moments they later regret. And God is *wise*; He knows that a religion which left people forever trapped by their own hasty words would not be a mercy but a tyranny, and that a religion which took oaths too lightly would not teach the seriousness of invoking His name. The expiation of oaths sits at the precise point where knowledge and wisdom meet: it honors the sanctity of the oath while never allowing the oath to become a chain.

This is a truth with direct modern application. Human beings still swear — in anger, in grief, in desperation, in the passion of love — "I will never speak to him again," "I will never touch that again," "I swear I will never forgive." Such oaths, made in the heat of feeling, can become prisons that trap a person for years, souring relationships and hardening hearts. Islam's answer is both serious and merciful: take your words seriously, but know that God has provided a lawful way out — an expiation that turns the broken oath into an occasion of generosity and self-discipline. The believer is thus never condemned to live in the prison of his own past words; the Master, in His knowledge of human frailty and His wisdom in legislation, has already opened the door.

**The Reassuring Logic of a Merciful Law**

Let us step back and state plainly the logic of this verse, because it is a logic every reflective reader should savor. Human beings are creatures of emotion and speech; they make commitments in moments of feeling that they cannot always keep in moments of calm. A just and merciful God could have responded to this in two ways: by treating all such oaths as trivial and void — which would teach people to take their sacred words lightly; or by treating all such oaths as absolutely binding — which would turn human emotion into a lifelong cage. Islam's answer, revealed here, is the middle path of wisdom: oaths *are* binding, and God's name is not to be invoked lightly — but the law provides a proportionate, graduated release, so that seriousness and mercy are held together. This is the signature of divine legislation throughout the Qur'an: it never reduces human beings to their worst moment, and it never lets their worst moment pass without remedy. The Knower knows our weakness; the Wise has legislated for it; the Master has undertaken our relief.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:3

> **When the Prophet confided a certain matter to one of his wives, but she divulged it, and God showed it to him, he made known part of it and held back part of it. When he informed her of it, she said, "Who informed thee of this?" He replied, "The Knower, the Aware informed me."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Anatomy of a Broken Confidence**

The third verse opens a window into the delicate interior of the Prophet's household, and in doing so teaches lessons about trust, discretion, and the gracious handling of others' faults that reach far beyond that single home. The scene is intimate and entirely human. The Prophet ﷺ confided a private matter — *asarra* — to one of his wives. The word *asarra* comes from the root *s-r-r*, meaning what is hidden, secret, concealed; it is the same root from which *sirr* (a secret) and *surūr* (joy) both derive, a linguistic kinship the commentators note: a confidence shared between intimate companions is among the quiet joys of love. But here the trust was broken. The wife *nabbaʾat bihi* — she divulged it, made it known, carried the secret to another. The bond of confidence, once the most tender thread between the Prophet ﷺ and his wife, was snapped.

The classical commentators identify the parties precisely, following the authenticated report of ʿĀʾishah already cited under verse 1. The Prophet ﷺ had confided to Ḥafṣah the matter of the honey — his declaration that he would not drink it again — and Ḥafṣah disclosed it to ʿĀʾishah. In the alternative account, the secret concerned Māriyah, and it was likewise conveyed between the two wives. What matters for the teaching is not the specific content but the event itself: a confidence was entrusted, and it was betrayed. The Qur'an, which speaks of cosmic truths with unadorned majesty, does not hesitate to record this small domestic rupture, because in the divine economy no rupture of trust is small.

**"God Showed It to Him": The Lord Who Protects His Prophet's Trust**

The verse then states a remarkable fact: *fa-lammā nabbaʾat bihi wa aẓharahu Allāhu ʿalayhi* — "but she divulged it, and God showed it to him." God Himself informed His Prophet ﷺ of the disclosure. The commentators pause here over the tenderness of this divine intervention. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī explain that God did not leave His Prophet ﷺ in ignorance of what had been said behind his back; He informed him, so that he might address the matter with knowledge and not with suspicion. This is itself a lesson for every believer about the difference between suspicion and knowledge, and about trusting God's governance even in the most private corners of life: nothing is hidden from the One whose knowledge encompasses all, and He supports His servants in ways they do not always see.

But the verse immediately records something even more instructive: when the Prophet ﷺ learned of the disclosure, *ʿarrafa baʿḍahu wa aʿraḍa ʿan baʿḍ* — "he made known part of it and held back part of it." He confronted his wife with *some* of what had been disclosed, but deliberately — out of kindness, as the Jalālayn and al-Ṭabarī note — he passed over the rest. He did not lay bare everything he knew; he did not seek to humiliate her with the full catalogue of her breach. He mentioned enough to make the point and to allow her to recognize that he knew, but he withheld enough to preserve her dignity and to leave a door of return open. This is the etiquette of correction at its most refined: *speak only so much of another's fault as is necessary to guide, and conceal the rest.* It is a standard so high that the Prophet ﷺ, in the very moment of being wronged, embodied it.

**"Who Informed Thee of This?": The Reflex of Surprise**

The wife's response is recorded with disarming realism: *man anbaʾaka hādhā* — "who informed you of this?" She was astonished, and her astonishment itself betrays her — for she knew that no human being had carried the secret to the Prophet ﷺ, and yet he knew. The question is the natural reflex of one caught in a private betrayal, suddenly confronted with knowledge that could only have come from beyond. It is a small, vivid, and entirely believable human detail, and the Qur'an's willingness to preserve it is part of the text's power: revelation does not airbrush the human scene; it illuminates it.

The Prophet's answer is the summit of the verse: *nabbaʾaniya al-ʿAlīmu al-Khabīr* — "The Knower, the Aware informed me." He does not say "I guessed," or "so-and-so told me," or even "I reasoned it out." He attributes his knowledge directly to God, by two Names chosen with exact precision. *Al-ʿAlīm*, the Knower, denotes the totality of divine knowledge — nothing escapes it. *Al-Khabīr*, the Aware, denotes knowledge of the *inward* — the hidden, the secret, the unspoken; it is the attribute by which God knows what lies beneath the surface of every heart and every whispered conversation. Together the two Names answer the wife's question completely: no creature told the Prophet ﷺ; the One who knows all things and is aware of all secrets is the One who informed him. The verse thus becomes, at its climax, a quiet but profound declaration of one of God's essential attributes — His encompassing knowledge — taught not in abstract theology but in the concrete texture of a domestic scene.

**The Weight of a Secret: Why Confidence Is Sacred in Islam**

Beneath the narrative, the verse legislates a principle that the Islamic tradition has always held dear: *the secret of another person is a trust (amānah), and to divulge it is a breach of that trust.* The Prophet ﷺ made this explicit in more than one saying. From Jābir ibn ʿAbdillāh, he ﷺ is reported to have said:

> *"When a man relates something (to another) and then turns away, it is a trust."* (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4868; Sunan al-Tirmidhī 1959 — graded ḥasan)

The meaning is beautiful and precise: if a person shares a matter with you and then departs, that matter has become a deposit in your keeping, and to repeat it to others is to violate a trust. The scholars add that this applies even when no explicit instruction to secrecy was given; the very intimacy of the confidence makes it a trust by its nature. The Qur'an elevates the keeping of trusts to the level of a defining quality of the believers:

> *"Indeed, God commands you to render the trusts back to their owners..."* (Qur'an 4:58)

> *"And those who are keepers of their trusts and their covenant..."* (Qur'an 23:8; 70:32)

The sūrah's domestic scene thus becomes a universal ethic. Every marriage, every friendship, every workplace, every family rests on the quiet infrastructure of kept confidences. When a spouse, friend, or colleague entrusts you with a matter meant to remain between you, and you carry it to another, you have not merely "shared information" — you have broken the very fabric on which relationship stands. The verse teaches this, not by abstract command, but by showing even the Prophet's own household held to the same standard, and by showing how gently and how firmly the standard was restored.

**Discretion as a Kind of Charity**

There is a further, beautiful teaching folded into the Prophet's conduct here: his withholding of part of what he knew. The tradition developed this into a general virtue — *the concealment of the faults of others is a form of charity and mercy.* The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever conceals (the fault of) a Muslim, God will conceal (his fault) in this world and the Hereafter."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2580)

And in another report:

> *"Do not pursue the faults of the Muslims; for whoever pursues the faults of the Muslims, God will pursue his faults — and whoever God pursues, He will expose even if he is in the interior of his house."* (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4880; Sunan al-Tirmidhī 2032)

The logic is symmetrical and just: the measure you use for others will be used for you. The one who eagerly exposes others' secrets invites the exposure of his own; the one who conceals others' faults places himself under the shade of God's own concealment. The Prophet's ﷺ conduct in this verse — confronting enough to correct, but concealing enough to preserve dignity — is the perfect enacted example of this principle, and it teaches leaders, parents, spouses, and friends how to correct without destroying.

**The Marriage Lesson: Trust Is the Load-Bearing Wall**

For the married believer, this verse is especially searching, because it places the keeping of confidences at the very heart of the marital bond. The Qur'an describes spouses as *libās* — garments — for one another:

> *"They are a garment for you, and you are a garment for them."* (Qur'an 2:187)

A garment covers, protects, and conceals; it is the closest thing to the body and the thing that shields its wearer from exposure. The analogy implies that spouses are entrusted with each other's secrets and vulnerabilities precisely because they are closest. When that trust is broken — when a private matter shared in intimacy is broadcast beyond the home — the "garment" has been torn, and the one who should have been most protected is left exposed. This is why the scholars treat the divulging of marital confidences with particular gravity, and why the sūrah, which is in part a discourse on the Prophet's household, places this incident before us: to teach every married couple that their home is a sanctuary of trust, and that what is said within it should, as a rule, remain within it.

**Knowledge, Correction, and the Grace Not to Expose**

Let us draw the threads of this verse together. It teaches that God knows all secrets and supports His servants; that a confidence is a trust whose breach is a fault; that correction should be measured — enough to guide, never enough to humiliate; that the one corrected should be left a dignified way back; and that the measure we use for others' hidden faults is the measure God will use for ours. The verse's quiet domestic scene is thus a complete course in the ethics of trust, discretion, and gracious correction — and it accomplishes all of this while simultaneously teaching, through the Prophet's own words at its climax, the encompassing knowledge of *al-ʿAlīm al-Khabīr*, the Knower, the Aware, from whom no secret is hidden and by whom every trust is observed.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:4

> **If you both repent unto God . . . For your hearts did certainly incline, and if you aid one another against him, then truly God, He is his Protector, as are Gabriel and the righteous among the believers; and the angels support him withal.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Address to Two Wives, and a Door Left Open**

The fourth verse turns from the Prophet ﷺ to the two wives who were party to the secret — and the address is direct, in the dual form: *in tatūbā ilā Allāhi* — "if you both repent unto God." The commentators are unanimous that the "you both" are Ḥafṣah and ʿĀʾishah, the two Mothers of the Believers, who had been involved in the disclosure of the previous verse. The sentence is famously elliptical: the conditional "if you both repent" is left without its explicit conclusion, and the commentators supply the understood completion. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and the Jalālayn agree that the meaning is "it will be accepted of you both," or "it will be better for you both." The very elision is a mercy — the verse does not even need to finish the sentence, because the promise of God's acceptance of repentance is so certain that it can be left unspoken.

And note the tone once more. The two wives are not expelled, not cursed, not publicly shamed. They are invited — *invited* — to repent. The door of return is held open to them by the very Lord whose Prophet they had grieved. This is the consistent character of the Qur'an's address to erring believers: correction is always accompanied by the standing invitation to return. The God who says elsewhere, *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God's mercy; indeed God forgives all sins"* (39:53), here opens that same door to two women in the Prophet's own home.

**"Your Hearts Did Certainly Incline": The Diagnosis of the Inward State**

The verse then names the root of the matter with clinical precision: *fa-qad ṣāghat qulūbukumā* — "for your hearts did certainly incline." The verb *ṣāghat* is from the root *ṣ-w-gh* / *ṣ-y-gh*, which fundamentally means to incline, to lean, to swerve, to bend away from the straight. It is the same root used of a vessel tilting, or of a person leaning toward something. The Qur'an uses it here to describe the hearts of the two wives as having *leaned* — inclined away from what was right, toward what their jealousy and their desire to influence the Prophet's ﷺ conduct prompted them to do. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī explain the sense: their hearts deviated, swerved from the truth, inclined to that which was not fitting — namely, to the very act of causing the Prophet ﷺ to forbid what God had made lawful, and to the disclosure that followed.

The choice of the word "hearts" is essential. The verse does not merely say "you erred" or "you made a mistake"; it locates the error in the *heart*, the *qalb* — that organ which in the Qur'anic anthropology is not merely the seat of emotion but the very center of understanding, intention, and moral orientation. The lesson is profound: sin does not begin in the limbs but in the *inclination of the heart*. Long before the tongue speaks the forbidden word or the hand commits the forbidden act, the heart has already begun to lean. This is why the Qur'an so relentlessly directs attention inward, and why the Prophet ﷺ taught that the body contains a piece of flesh which, if sound, makes the whole body sound, and if corrupt, corrupts the whole body — "indeed it is the heart" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 52; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1599). The diagnosis of this verse is thus not a mere reproach but a profound piece of moral psychology: guard the heart, for from its inclination every outward slip is born.

**"If You Aid One Another Against Him": The Warning of Alliance**

The verse then contemplates a graver possibility: *wa in taẓāharā ʿalayhi* — "and if you aid one another against him." The verb *taẓāharā* is from the root *ẓ-h-r*, carrying the sense of mutual support and backing — two people leaning on one another against a third. The verse is warning the two wives against the dangerous path of *collusion*: of combining forces, each reinforcing the other, to press against the Prophet ﷺ and his mission. This is a warning of real gravity, because it describes a dynamic that appears in every human community: individuals who might each hesitate to err alone become emboldened when they find an ally; two people egging each other on can accomplish what neither would attempt singly. The Qur'an names this dynamic and warns against it even in the Prophet's own household, for no household is immune to it.

But observe what the verse does *not* say. It does not say "you have allied against him and are therefore lost." It says *if* you aid one another against him — it holds up the possibility as a warning, not a verdict, leaving the two wives entirely free to turn back. This is the Qur'an's consistent respect for human freedom and its consistent hope for human return: it warns against the road without ever sealing anyone upon it. The conditional structure is itself a mercy, a held-open door.

**The Comfort of the Guarantee: God, Gabriel, the Righteous, and the Angels**

The verse then delivers its most consoling sentence: *fa-inna Allāha huwa mawlāhu* — "then truly God, He is his Protector." Whatever the wives might do, however they might combine, the Prophet ﷺ stands under a protection that no human alliance can breach. God Himself is his *mawlā* — his Master, Protector, and Ally. And God does not stand alone in this protection; the verse enumerates a supporting company that rises in rank and power: *wa Jibrīlu wa ṣāliḥu al-muʾminīn* — "and Gabriel, and the righteous among the believers" — *wa al-malāʾikatu baʿda dhālika ẓahīr* — "and the angels, moreover, are his supporters."

The word *ẓahīr*, from the same root as *taẓāharā*, is the deliberate counterpoint to it. The wives might *taẓāharā* — ally against him — but the angels are his *ẓahīr* — his backing, his support, his reinforcement. The verse thus sets two alliances in opposition: a possible alliance of two mortal women, and the assured alliance of God, the greatest of the angels, the righteous of the believers, and the angelic host. The imbalance is almost humorous in its weight: against the whispered collusion of two jealous hearts stands the entire structure of divine support. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr explain the intended effect: the verse both *comforts* the Prophet ﷺ — reminding him that he is never alone — and *admonishes* the two wives — reminding them that opposition to God's Messenger is opposition to a cause whose backers are not of this world.

The specific mention of Gabriel is itself meaningful. Gabriel (*Jibrīl*) is the angel of revelation, the one who carried the Qur'an down to the Prophet's ﷺ heart; to name him here is to say that the very channel of revelation stands beside the Prophet ﷺ against any who would harm him. And "the righteous among the believers" (*ṣāliḥ al-muʾminīn*) has been variously understood. Many commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Ṭūsī, and others — take it as a reference to the best and most upright of the believers generally, or to the prophets of old, while some identify it specifically with the two great Companions who were fathers of the two wives: Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, father of ʿĀʾishah, and ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, father of Ḥafṣah — a reading with a pointed and tender edge, for it reminds the two wives that even their own righteous fathers stand on the side of God and His Messenger. Shiʿi commentaries add that "the righteous among the believers" refers to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, citing the report that the Prophet ﷺ took ʿAlī by the hand and declared him "the righteous among the believers." However these identifications are weighed, the central teaching is unshaken: the Prophet ﷺ is enveloped in a support that neither jealousy nor collusion nor any human power can overcome.

**Why This Verse Matters for Every Believer**

It would be a mistake to read this verse only as ancient household history. Its teachings are universal and permanent. First, it teaches the *hope of repentance*: no error of the heart is beyond the door of return, and God's acceptance of sincere repentance is so certain that the verse can leave it unspoken. Second, it teaches the *anatomy of sin*: error begins as an inclination of the heart, and therefore the heart must be guarded before the limbs. Third, it teaches the *danger of collusion*: souls that would hesitate alone are emboldened in alliance, and we must be vigilant against the alliances our own desires form. Fourth, it teaches the *independence of the righteous cause*: whoever stands with God and His Messenger stands with a support that outlasts every earthly coalition — a truth that has sustained believers through persecution, isolation, and apparent weakness in every generation.

And beneath all of these lies the most personal lesson, directed at every spouse and every believer: *do not make your spouse, or your friend, or your family, the ally of your sin against God, and do not fear that righteousness leaves anyone alone.* The two wives are not condemned; they are warned and invited back. The Prophet ﷺ is not left vulnerable; he is surrounded. The verse is, in its four short lines, a complete map of the believer's situation before God — warned against the heart's inclination, protected from every foe, and always within reach of the door of return.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:5

> **It may be that if he divorces you, his Lord would give him wives in your stead who are better than you, submitting, believing, devoutly obedient, penitent, worshipping, and given to wayfaring — previously married, and virgins.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Warning That Carries No Sting of Contempt**

The fifth verse continues the address to the Prophet's wives, and it is the most sobering verse of the opening passage. It reminds them that their position is not beyond replacement: *ʿasā rabbuhu in ṭallaqakunna an yubdilahu azwājan khayran minkunna* — "it may be that if he divorces you, his Lord would give him wives in your stead who are better than you." The commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr — are careful to note the tone. The word *ʿasā* ("it may be") softens the sentence into a possibility rather than a threat; the verse does not pronounce their divorce but holds it up as a possibility against which to measure their conduct. And the point is not to insult the Mothers of the Believers, whose rank in the community is immense, but to teach a universal truth that the sūrah will drive home fully in its final verses: *no one is indispensable to God, and no station — however elevated — is a guarantee of God's good pleasure.* The wives of the Prophet ﷺ, the most honored women of the community, are here told in the clearest terms that even their place is held by grace, not by entitlement.

This is a truth that every believer needs to hear, precisely because it cuts against the deepest human tendency — the tendency to grow secure in one's position and to imagine that one's nearness to what is holy confers a claim upon it. The sūrah's warning reaches far beyond that Madinan household. The scholar who imagines his learning guarantees his salvation, the worshipper who imagines his devotion obligates God, the leader who imagines his office protects him, the child of the righteous who imagines his lineage suffices — all are addressed by this verse. Nearness is a trust and a test, never a title of entitlement.

**The Seven Qualities of the Better Wives: A Portrait of the Ideal**

The verse then paints, in a single flowing list, the portrait of the women who would be "better" — and this portrait is itself a treasure, for it constitutes the Qur'an's own description of the ideal believing woman. The list flows as a cascade of seven qualities, each a distinct dimension of excellence:

*Muslimāt* — "submitting." The word derives from *islām*, and at its root from *s-l-m*, peace and soundness; the *muslimah* is the one who has surrendered herself to God, whose will is aligned with the divine will, at peace with her Lord. Al-Ṭabarī explains that *islām* here indicates humility and submission before God, the foundation upon which all the other qualities stand.

*Muʾmināt* — "believing." From *īmān*, from the root *a-m-n*, security and trust; the *muʾminah* is the one who has faith, whose heart has found security in God and who is entrusted and trustworthy. Faith is deeper than outward submission; it is the settled conviction of the heart that renders the whole person reliable.

*Qānitāt* — "devoutly obedient." From *q-n-t*, a root which in the Qur'an relates exclusively to obedience toward and worship of God — never to servility before human beings. The *qānitah* is constant, serene, and devoted in her worship, maintaining the inward posture of obedience through all the fluctuations of circumstance. The commentators note that this quality conveys constancy and tranquility together: not a fitful devotion but a settled, abiding one.

*Tāʾibāt* — "penitent." From *t-w-b*, to return; the *tāʾibah* is the one who continually turns back to God, who does not rest in her errors but returns from them, and whose life is a rhythm of turning. This is the quality the sūrah has already emphasized in verse 4 — the standing invitation to repent — now held up as a permanent attribute of the ideal believer.

*ʿĀbidāt* — "worshipping." From *ʿ-b-d*, servitude; the *ʿābidah* is the one devoted to worship, whose life is ordered around the service of her Lord. Where *qānitāt* emphasizes the inward constancy of obedience, *ʿābidāt* emphasizes the outward life of worship — prayer, fasting, remembrance — the body and time given over to God.

*Sāʾiḥāt* — "given to wayfaring." This is the most richly debated of the seven, and the source text itself flags the discussion. The root *s-y-ḥ* fundamentally means to travel, to journey, to roam. Al-Ṭabarī and the Jalālayn interpret it here as those given to *emigration* for the sake of religion — the women who left their homes and traveled for the sake of their faith, as the early Muslim women emigrated from Makkah to Madinah. Others interpret *sāʾiḥāt* as "given to fasting," on the basis that the traveler abstains and endures, and that fasting is a kind of journeying of the soul — an interpretation recorded by Iṣbahānī and others, who further extend it to the broader sense of preserving one's limbs from disobedience. The Study Quran's source wisely notes that the "fasting" interpretation may partly reflect later attempts to restrict women's roles, and that the more natural and comprehensive sense is the spiritual and literal wayfaring: the woman whose life is a journey toward God, unrooted from the merely worldly. The very breadth of the word is a gift — it names a believer who is never stationary in her growth, always traveling onward.

*Thayyibātin wa abkāran* — "previously married, and virgins." The verse closes with a note of completeness: the "better" wives would be drawn from both categories — those previously married and those never married — so that no category is excluded and no condition is privileged. It is as if to say: the abundance of God's provision is not limited to any single kind; He can give what He wills, from wherever He wills.

**The Grammar of the List: The Feminine Plural and Its Purpose**

The careful reader will note that all seven qualities are cast in the feminine plural — *muslimāt, muʾmināt, qānitāt, tāʾibāt, ʿābidāt, sāʾiḥāt* — and this is itself a point the commentators make. The verse is not only a warning to the Prophet's wives but simultaneously an *instruction to all believing women*, and indeed to all believers, about the qualities that constitute true excellence in God's sight. The list has been memorized and treasured across fourteen centuries as a charter of the believing woman's spiritual dignity: she is defined by her submission, her faith, her devotion, her repentance, her worship, and her wayfaring toward God — not by lineage, beauty, wealth, or any of the worldly measures that societies so often use to rank women. The Qur'an here, as throughout, measures human beings by their inward relationship to God and by the qualities of the soul, not by the accidents of birth or circumstance.

**The Modern Lesson: Dignity Without Entitlement**

This verse speaks with unusual directness to the modern age, and in two directions at once. On one hand, it dignifies women with a list of spiritual qualities that no society's stereotypes can diminish — submission to God, faith, devotion, repentance, worship, and the ceaseless journey of growth. On the other hand, it strips away every illusion of entitlement — reminding even the most honored that position before God is earned, not inherited, and held by grace, not by right. These two truths, held together, are the antidote to both of the modern age's characteristic spiritual diseases: the *belittlement* of persons (especially women) by worldly measures, and the *entitlement* by which people imagine that their identity, association, or privilege constitutes merit before God.

For the married believer, the verse carries a quieter, gentler lesson as well. It reminds spouses — and here the address, though to wives, applies in principle to both — that the bond of marriage is nourished by spiritual quality, not merely by position or habit; that a spouse should be a *companion in the journey to God*, and that the qualities that make a marriage thrive before God are precisely these seven — submission, faith, devotion, repentance, worship, and the shared wayfaring of two souls traveling together toward their Lord. A marriage in which both partners cultivate these qualities is a marriage rooted in what the Qur'an itself calls "better."

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:6

> **O you who believe! Shield yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is men and stones, over which are angels, stern and severe, who do not disobey God in what He commands of them and who do what they are commanded.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turning Point: From One Household to Every Household**

With the sixth verse the sūrah performs its great turning. The opening verses addressed the Prophet ﷺ and his wives in the particular intimacy of one Madinan household; now the address widens to embrace every believer: *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū* — "O you who believe!" The lesson learned in the Prophet's home — that nearness is no guarantee, that each soul must tend its own standing before God — is now generalized into a command that reaches every Muslim family until the end of time. The household of the Prophet ﷺ becomes the model of what every household must become: a place where the Fire is feared, and where each member helps the others to safety.

The command itself is weighty: *qū anfusakum wa ahlīkum nāran* — "shield yourselves and your families from a Fire." The verb *qū* is the imperative of *w-q-y*, to protect, to guard, to shield — the very root from which *taqwā* (God-consciousness, the vigilant care that shields against harm) and *wiqāyah* (a shield, protection) are derived. The verse thus commands believers to *do for themselves and their families* what *taqwā* does for the soul: to place a shield between their households and the Fire. This is the first and deepest duty of every Muslim — not wealth, not status, not worldly provision alone, but the spiritual protection of oneself and one's family.

**The Uniqueness of This Command: A Duty No One Can Delegate**

The source text makes a precise and important observation: this is *the only verse* in which believers are commanded to shield themselves and their families from the Fire. Elsewhere, the Qur'an shows believers *asking* God to shield them — as in the moving supplications recorded in 3:16 and 3:191, *"Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and shield us from the punishment of the Fire,"* and in 40:7, where the angels themselves pray for the believers. But here the matter is put in the imperative: you *yourselves* must act to shield your families. The distinction is the difference between petition and responsibility. Petition asks God to do something for us; responsibility is what God has commanded *us* to do. The believer is never told that shielding the family is God's business alone; it is a task entrusted to the believer's own hands, even as its success ultimately depends on God's grace.

This has an immediate and searching application. Many believers pray fervently for their families — "O Lord, guide my children, protect my spouse" — and such prayer is good and commanded. But this verse insists that prayer alone is not the assigned instrument. The parent is commanded to *act*: to teach, to model, to admonish, to set the home in order, to direct the family toward worship and away from the Fire. The duʿāʾ (supplication) and the duty (responsibility) are partners, not substitutes. The parent who prays but does not teach, or who teaches but does not model, has discharged only half the command.

**"Whose Fuel Is Men and Stones": The Reality of the Fire**

The verse describes the Fire with a phrase that has captured the imagination of commentators for fourteen centuries: *waqūduhā al-nāsu wa al-ḥijārah* — "whose fuel is men and stones." The word *waqūd* is the fuel, the kindling, the material that feeds a blaze. That human beings are the fuel of the Fire is a sobering image that recurs in the Qur'an, but the addition of "stones" has drawn particular attention. The commentators offer two principal explanations. The Jalālayn and others relate the stones to the *idols* the idolaters worshipped — for those idols were carved of stone or were stones themselves, and the Qur'an elsewhere warns that the worshipper and the worshipped will both be cast together into the Fire:

> *"Indeed, you and what you worship besides God are the fuel of Hell; to it you will come. Were these [idols] gods, they would not have entered it; but all of them will abide therein forever."* (Qur'an 21:98–99)

On this reading, the stones are a picture of poetic justice: the very objects on which a person pinned his hopes and worship will become, with him, the kindling of the Fire that consumes him. Other commentators simply note that *ḥijārah* are stones — the hardest, most resistant of materials — indicating a fire of such intensity that even stone burns, an image of utter, consuming heat beyond anything known on earth. Both readings converge on the same effect: the verse paints the Fire not as a distant abstraction but as a real, consuming reality, fed by real materials, awaiting real human beings — and thereby compels the believer to take the command to shield the family with utter seriousness.

**The Angels of the Fire: Sternness in Perfect Obedience**

The verse then describes the keepers of the Fire: *ʿalayhā malāʾikatun ghilāẓun shidād* — "over which are angels, stern and severe." The words *ghilāẓ* (stern, harsh) and *shidād* (severe, mighty) are chosen to convey a force that admits of no leniency and no escape — the guardians of Hell are not negotiable, not persuadable, not moved by appeal or tears. The Qur'an elsewhere names their chief and numbers them:

> *"Over it are nineteen (angels). And We have made the keepers of the Fire none but angels..."* (Qur'an 74:30–31)

And it describes their total unswerving in a parallel verse:

> *"O you who believe! Shield yourselves and your families from a Fire... over which are angels, stern and severe, who do not disobey God in what He commands them and do what they are commanded."* — with the near-identical phrasing at 66:6 and in the description of the angels' nature elsewhere.

But the verse immediately adds a phrase that is the key to the whole description: *lā yaʿṣūna Allāha mā amarahum wa yafʿalūna mā yuʾmarūn* — "who do not disobey God in what He commands them and who do what they are commanded." These stern angels are themselves perfectly obedient. The point is profound and often missed: the severity of the guardians of Hell is not cruelty or caprice; it is *the exact execution of a command.* They are not angry gods or sadistic spirits; they are servants — the most disciplined of servants — carrying out without the slightest deviation precisely what God has ordered. The Fire, and its stern keepers, are thus woven into the same fabric of perfect divine order as the rest of creation.

This teaches the believer something essential about the nature of the Hereafter's justice: it is not arbitrary. The punishment is administered by beings who cannot deviate from God's command, and therefore it is administered with a precision that admits no error — the Qur'an's repeated insistence that *"God wrongs no one"* (e.g., 3:108; 8:51) is made concrete in the very description of the Fire's keepers. The sternness is the sternness of exact justice; the severity is the severity of a command flawlessly fulfilled.

**The Logic of the Command: Why Family First?**

Why does the Qur'an join oneself and one's family in a single command — "shield *yourselves and your families*"? The answer lies in the deepest structure of human existence. The family is the first and most formative society; a human being's earliest and most enduring moral education happens within it. If the family is neglected, the individual is launched into the world unprotected, and the community inherits the consequences. If the family is tended, the individual and the society are both fortified. The family is therefore the *load-bearing wall* of the entire moral order — and this is why the Qur'an gives it such central place, and why the protection of the family is joined inseparably to the protection of the self.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the practical content of the command with characteristic clarity: shield yourselves by obedience and reverence to God, and shield your families by teaching them, admonishing them, and directing them to obedience and reverence. The command is thus a complete program of family life: the believer's household is to be a school of taqwā, where the fear and love of God are taught by word and modeled by example, where prayer is established, where the lawful is observed and the forbidden avoided, where each member is helped toward God rather than left to drift.

The Prophet ﷺ made this duty concrete in his own teaching. He said, in the famous hadith that every parent knows:

> *"Every one of you is a shepherd, and every one of you is responsible for his flock. The ruler is a shepherd responsible for his people; the man is a shepherd in his household and responsible for his family; the woman is a shepherdess in her husband's house and responsible for her flock..."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 893; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1829)

And in a saying that reads as a direct commentary on this verse, he ﷺ taught:

> *"Command your children to pray when they are seven years old..."* (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 495; Sunan al-Tirmidhī 407)

The image of the shepherd is exact. A shepherd does not drag his flock to pasture by force, nor does he abandon them to wander. He leads them, feeds them, guards them, and brings them back when they stray — and he is accountable for them. This is the model of family leadership the verse commands: not tyranny, not neglect, but the tender, vigilant, accountable care of a shepherd for his flock.

**The Modern Application: Protecting the Family in an Age of Fire**

The command of this verse has never been more urgent than it is now, because the "fuel" of the modern Fire — the things that consume souls — has multiplied beyond anything the earlier ages knew. The believer of every era has had to guard his family against dangers; the modern believer must guard against a torrent. The family today is surrounded by influences that compete for the hearts of its members: screens and feeds that shape desires, markets that commodify attention, social pressures that normalize what the faith forbids, and a pervasive culture that treats the family's role in moral formation as quaint or optional. In such an age, "shield yourselves and your families from a Fire" is not a metaphor for a distant future; it is a present, daily task.

And yet the tools the verse gives are the same tools that have always worked. The shield of *taqwā* — the vigilant, loving awareness of God — placed around the family through teaching, example, worship, and the careful tending of what enters the home. The parent who prays with the family, who speaks of God naturally and joyfully, who models honesty and kindness, who gently directs children toward what is good and away from what is harmful, who makes the home a place where faith is *lived* rather than merely asserted — that parent is doing precisely what this verse commands. The verse is, in the end, a charter of hope: it assures every believer that the protection of the family is not beyond reach, that the instruments of that protection have been provided, and that the One who commands the shielding is the One who rewards it — the same Lord who, as the sūrah will soon remind us, forgives and is Merciful.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:7

> **O you who disbelieve! Make no excuses this day! You are only requited for that which you used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse Spoken on the Far Side of Time**

The seventh verse performs a startling leap. The previous verse addressed the believers in the present world, commanding them to shield themselves and their families from the Fire. This verse addresses the disbelievers — but not here and now; rather, *on the Day of Resurrection*, as they stand before the Fire they denied. The address *yā ayyuhā alladhīna kafarū* — "O you who disbelieve!" — is, as the Jalālayn and al-Ṭabarī note, a direct address to the disbelievers *as they enter the Fire*, spoken across the threshold of the Hereafter. The word *al-yawm* — "this day" — fixes the scene: it is the Day of Resurrection, the Day when every claim will be tested and every excuse will fall away. The Qur'an here lets the reader overhear, as it were, the very words that will be spoken to the deniers on that final Day, so that the warning might reach them *now*, while there is still time to heed it.

This technique — of bringing the future into the present, of speaking the words of the Last Day to the ears of the living — is one of the Qur'an's most powerful devices. It collapses the distance between now and then, making the Hereafter not a remote speculation but an imminent reality. The disbeliever who reads this verse is hearing, in advance, the sentence that will be pronounced upon him if he persists. The believer who reads it is hearing the fate from which he prays to be shielded. For both, the effect is the same: the Hereafter becomes real.

**"Make No Excuses": The Collapse of Every Defense**

The command is *lā taʿtadhirū al-yawma* — "make no excuses this day." The word *iʿtidhār* is the offering of excuses, the making of justifications, the plea in self-defense. The verse commands the disbelievers to *stop* — not as an arbitrary silencing, but because their excuses have lost all purchase. The commentators explain the full meaning: on that Day, every false justification will be exposed as false, and there will be nothing left to say. The Qur'an paints this scene elsewhere with devastating clarity:

> *"And if you could but see when the wrongdoers are made to stand before their Lord, casting back the blame upon one another... And if you could but see when they are made to stand before the Fire, and they say: 'Would that we could be sent back! Then we would not deny the signs of our Lord, and we would be among the believers.'"* (Qur'an 6:27, 34:54; cf. 34:54)

And in the verse the source text cites as its twin, spoken in the same voice on the same Day:

> *"So this Day, you shall not be helped. Indeed, you have denied, so taste [the punishment]."* — and its exact parallel: *"O you who disbelieve! Make no excuses this day! You are only requited for that which you used to do"* (66:7) stands with *"Taste! Indeed, you are the mighty, the noble!"* and the verse *"Enter it, then be patient or be impatient — it is the same for you; you are only being requited for what you used to do"* (Qur'an 52:16).

The human being is a creature of excuses. We excuse our sins to ourselves, we excuse them to others, and we imagine we will excuse them to God. The Qur'an's verdict is that there is a Day when all of this collapses — when the excuses that seemed so plausible in the world, woven from self-deception and defended with argument, will be seen for what they always were. The verse thus delivers one of the Qur'an's most bracing truths: *self-deception has an expiration date.* It works only while the reality is hidden; when reality appears, it dies.

**"You Are Only Requited for What You Used to Do": The Perfect Justice of God**

The second half of the verse is the reason for the first: *innamā tujzawna mā kuntum taʿmalūn* — "you are only requited for that which you used to do." Here is the whole doctrine of divine justice in a single line. The requital is *exact* — *innamā*, "only," the word of restriction and precision. Nothing is added; nothing is taken away. The punishment corresponds precisely to the deeds. The Qur'an states this principle again and again, in a chorus of verses that the source text gathers:

> *"God created the heavens and the earth in truth, and so that every soul may be recompensed for that which it has earned; and they shall not be wronged."* (Qur'an 45:22)

> *"And whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it."* (Qur'an 99:8)

> *"This Day, every soul is recompensed for what it earned. No injustice this Day. Indeed, God is swift in account."* (Qur'an 40:17)

The emphasis on *justice* — on the exact correspondence between deed and requital — is the Qur'an's answer to every anxiety about the fairness of the final reckoning. God does not punish for nothing; He does not punish beyond measure; He does not punish one soul for the sin of another. Each soul answers for what *it* earned. The verse's "you are only requited for what *you* used to do" is the guarantee that the judgment is not arbitrary — it is the mirror held up to the life one actually lived.

**The Parallel Scene: When the Truth Silences the Deniers**

The source text draws attention to a beautiful and important point: when the disbelievers actually *see* the reality of their deeds on the Day of Resurrection, they will know the truth — and *therefore* they will not even try to make excuses. This is the profound meaning of the command "make no excuses": it is not that they are forbidden to speak, but that the truth will have rendered speech impossible. The Qur'an records this very scene:

> *"They will say: 'If only we had listened or reasoned, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze.' So they will confess their sin — and away with the companions of the Blaze!"* (Qur'an 67:10–11)

Notice the logic: on that Day, the deniers themselves will *confess*. The excuses they spent a lifetime constructing — the doubts they entertained, the arguments they rehearsed, the compromises they rationalized — will dissolve at the first sight of the reality, and they will admit what they denied. The command "make no excuses" is thus not an external silencing but the description of an internal collapse: the excuse-making faculty itself dies when the truth appears. This is why the Qur'an so relentlessly urges its hearers to *think now*, to *listen now*, to *reason now* — because there is a time when reasoning about the truth will no longer be possible, only the seeing of it.

**The Lesson for the Living: Deal with Your Excuses Now**

The verse's warning, though spoken to the disbelievers on the Last Day, is addressed — through the Qur'an's collapse of time — to every living reader. Its lesson is practical and urgent: *deal with your excuses now, while they can still be traded for repentance.* The same human habit of self-justification that the verse shows collapsing on the Last Day is at work in every one of us every day — the habit that turns our sins into "mistakes," our compromises into "pragmatism," our procrastination into "one day," our neglect of God into "not yet." The Qur'an's message is that this habit can be broken now, by the very act it refuses: the honest admission of the truth about oneself, which in the language of faith is called *repentance*.

And here the sūrah's own structure speaks. The verse is immediately followed — in the very next verse — by the call to the believers to repent with sincere repentance. The Qur'an places the fate of the excuse-maker and the invitation of the repenter side by side, as two doors: the door of excuses, which leads to the Day when excuses die; and the door of repentance, which leads to the Garden. The reader is left with the clearest possible choice: break your excuses now, in this world, where breaking them is called repentance and is met with forgiveness — or face their collapse on a Day when it will be too late. This is the verse's ultimate teaching: *the excuses you dissolve today become the forgiveness you meet tomorrow; the excuses you keep today become the silence of the Last Day.*

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:8

> **O you who believe! Repent unto God with sincere repentance. It may be that your Lord will absolve you of your evil deeds and cause you to enter Gardens with rivers running below — the Day when God will not disgrace the Prophet and those who believe with him, with their light spreading before them and on their right, while they say, "Our Lord, complete our light for us and forgive us. Truly Thou art Powerful over all things."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Door That Always Stands Open**

The eighth verse is the sūrah's great hinge of mercy. Having just spoken, in verse 7, of the disbelievers whose excuses collapse on the Last Day, the Qur'an now turns to the believers and holds open the opposite door: *tūbū ilā Allāhi tawbatan naṣūḥā* — "repent unto God with sincere repentance." The word *tūbū* is the imperative of *t-w-b*, to return, to turn back — the same root from which *tawbah* (repentance) and *al-Tawwāb* (the Ever-Relenting, one of God's beautiful names) both derive. Al-Ṭabarī notes that *tūbū ilā Allāh* can be read in two complementary senses: "turn *unto* God" — returning from sin to obedience — and "repent *to* God" — directing the act of return toward Him alone. Both senses meet in the same motion: the turning of the whole self from what it has been toward what it should be, aimed at God.

The verse thus answers the question that verse 7 implicitly raised. If the excuse-maker faces the collapse of his excuses, what is the alternative? The answer is *tawbah* — the honest, active, forward-moving admission of the truth about oneself, which is the living opposite of excuse-making. Excuses look backward to justify; repentance looks forward to change. Excuses protect the self from truth; repentance opens the self to truth. The Qur'an places them side by side so that no one can mistake the choice.

**Sincere Repentance: The Anatomy of True Return**

The qualifying word is *naṣūḥā* — "sincere" — and it is the crux of the verse. What makes repentance *sincere*? The classical scholars, gathering the understanding of the Companions and their successors, defined *tawbatan naṣūḥā* with a precision that has become standard. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī explain that sincere repentance is not a mere turning *away* from sin, but a turning *with true contrition* — a repentance so complete that one resolves never to return to the sin, and never does return. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is reported to have defined it memorably: it is the repentance in which one turns away from the sin and then *never returns to it, just as milk never returns to the udder*. The image is perfect — the extracted milk cannot be poured back; so the sincerely repented sin cannot be re-entered.

The scholars then laid out the conditions of sincere repentance, which may be summarized as three. First, *remorse* — genuine grief over the sin, not merely the discomfort of being caught. Second, *desistance* — the actual abandonment of the sin in the present. Third, *resolve* — the firm intention not to return to it in the future. And where the sin involved the right of another human being, a fourth condition is added: *restoration* — returning to the wronged person what is owed, whether a debt, a right, or an apology. This is the full anatomy of *tawbatan naṣūḥā*: remorse, desistance, resolve, and restoration. It is the Qur'an's answer to the cheap repentance that says "I'm sorry" with the lips while the heart clings to the sin — a repentance that is, in truth, only another excuse.

**"It May Be That Your Lord Will Absolve You": The Grammar of Hope**

The verse then opens the promise of forgiveness with a remarkable word: *ʿasā* — "it may be." *ʿasā rabbukum an yukaffira ʿankum sayyiʾātikum* — "it may be that your Lord will absolve you of your evil deeds." Why "it may be," when God's forgiveness is certain? The commentators explain the subtlety with care. The word *ʿasā* here does not express doubt about God — it expresses the *hope* of the servant, and it teaches a necessary attitude. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī note that when *ʿasā* is spoken by God or about God's acts, it conveys a promise whose fulfillment is sure, while simultaneously teaching the believer to *hope* rather than to *presume*. The believer is not to say "I have repented, therefore God *must* forgive me" — an attitude of entitlement that would itself corrupt the repentance — but rather "I have repented, and I *hope* for my Lord's forgiveness," an attitude of humility that keeps the door of return ever before the heart. Hope, not presumption: this is the precise spiritual posture the word *ʿasā* teaches.

The word *yukaffira* — "absolve" — is also rich. It is from the root *k-f-r*, which fundamentally means to cover, to conceal; and it is the direct opposite of the sin it forgives. The same root yields *kufr* (disbelief, the covering over of the truth) and *kaffārah* (expiation, the covering over of a fault). Here God is said to *cover over* the believer's evil deeds — to erase them, to shield them from consequence. The Qur'an links this absolution to faith and repentance in a great chorus of verses the source text gathers: absolution is promised to those who believe and repent (29:7; 47:2; 48:5; 64:9), to those who are God-fearing (8:29; 5:65; 65:5), to those who shun major sins (4:31), and to those who perform specific righteous deeds — charity in secret (2:271), striving in God's way (3:195), prayer and alms (5:12). In every case the teaching is the same: God's absolution is not arbitrary; it is His response to the servant's turning, and it is described in the most consoling terms the language possesses — the covering over, the erasing, the shielding from the consequences of one's own deeds.

**The Reward Beyond Forgiveness: Gardens with Rivers Running Below**

The absolution is then joined to its reward: *wa yudkhilakum jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — "and cause you to enter Gardens with rivers running below." The image is the Qur'an's most beloved description of Paradise, repeated dozens of times: gardens beneath which rivers flow. The phrase paints a picture of abundance, shade, coolness, and perpetual refreshment — the exact opposite of the Fire's consuming heat described two verses earlier. The believer is promised not merely the removal of punishment but the positive gift of Paradise — not just the erasing of evil deeds but the entering of eternal gardens. This is the Qur'an's consistent generosity: God's mercy is not a bare amnesty but a lavish welcome. Forgiveness and Paradise are two movements of one gift; the erasing of the past and the opening of the future.

The source text and the commentators note a further subtlety in this verse's picture of the Hereafter: the believers are described not only as forgiven and admitted, but as *accompanied* — the verse continues, "the Day when God will not disgrace the Prophet and those who believe with him." The believer's hope is not only personal; it is communal and it is centered on the Prophet ﷺ. On the Day of Judgment, the believer's deepest fear is not only for himself but that the Prophet ﷺ and his community might be disgraced before the assembled creation — and the verse assures the believers that this will not happen. God will honor His Prophet ﷺ and those who believed with him. This is a profound comfort: the believer's salvation is not a solitary matter but a place within a company — the company of the Prophet ﷺ and the faithful, honored together on the Day of Reckoning.

**The Light That Runs Before Them: The Believer's Radiance on the Last Day**

The verse then paints one of the most beautiful images in the Qur'an: *nūruhum yasʿā bayna aydīhim wa bi-aymānihim* — "with their light spreading before them and on their right." The light of the believers — the radiance their faith has stored up — runs before them and at their right hands on the Day of Judgment, illuminating their way through the darkness of that Day. The commentators (al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and others, drawing on the fuller description in Sūrat al-Ḥadīd 57:12–14) explain the scene in detail. On the Last Day, the believers are given a light in proportion to their faith and deeds; it runs before them, lighting their path across the bridge toward Paradise. The hypocrites, who lived among them with a faith that was only outward, find their light extinguished, and they cry out to the believers to wait for them — but they are told to return and seek a light elsewhere. The verse's image of light "before them and on their right" is thus not decorative; it is the precise geometry of salvation — the light that faith stored up, now released to guide its bearer home.

And in this moment of crossing, the believers speak the supplication the verse records: *"Rabbanā atmim lanā nūranā wa ghfir lanā"* — "Our Lord, complete our light for us and forgive us." Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī explain that this is the believers' prayer when they see the light of the hypocrites extinguished — a prayer of grateful humility in the very moment of being saved. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī adds a beautiful observation recorded by al-Rāzī: God *has already* completed their light for them, and yet they continue to call upon Him, seeking to draw nearer — for the believer's supplication never ceases, even in Paradise, since drawing near to God is an endless journey. The prayer "complete our light" is thus the prayer of the saved, spoken not from fear but from love — the believer who has been given everything still asks for more of God, because God Himself is the ultimate desire and no gift of His exhausts the longing for Him.

**"Truly Thou Art Powerful over All Things": The Foundation of the Hope**

The supplication closes with *"innaka ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr"* — "truly Thou art Powerful over all things." This closing is not a mere formula; it is the theological foundation of everything the verse has promised. Why may the believer hope for forgiveness, for Paradise, for the completion of light? Because God is *powerful over all things* — nothing stands beyond His capacity, no sin is too great for His forgiveness, no request too large for His power. The verse grounds its hope precisely here: the believer's confidence rests not on his own worthiness but on God's unlimited power joined to God's unlimited mercy. This is the deepest lesson of the verse — that hope in Islam is never presumption *about oneself* but always confidence *in God*. The one who repents sincerely does not say "I deserve forgiveness" but "my Lord is powerful over all things, and therefore I hope in His forgiveness." The verse's final phrase is thus the believer's true ground of standing: not the self, which sins, but the Lord, who is able to do all things.

**The Call to Repentance That Runs Through the Prophet's Life**

Finally, the verse's command — *repent unto God* — was the living texture of the Prophet's ﷺ own teaching, for he summoned his community to repentance at every turn. The source text records a hadith in this very connection:

> *"O people! Repent unto your Lord before you die, and draw yourselves nigh unto Him with righteous deeds before you become preoccupied."*

And the Prophet ﷺ, whose own sins God had forgiven, still said of himself:

> *"O people, repent to God, for I repent to Him a hundred times a day."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2702)

If the Prophet ﷺ — the most beloved of creation to God — turned to his Lord in repentance a hundred times each day, then repentance is not a mark of the sinner's shame but the constant posture of the believer's love. It is the turning of the heart to God that defines a life of faith, renewed a hundred times a day, and consummated at last in the Gardens where the rivers flow and the light runs before its bearers to the gate.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:9

> **O Prophet! Strive against the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be harsh with them. Their refuge is Hell. What an evil journey's end!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command That Is Both Firm and Discriminating**

The ninth verse returns to the direct address of the opening — *yā ayyuhā al-nabiyy* — "O Prophet!" — and delivers one of the sūrah's most forceful commands: *jāhid al-kuffāra wa al-munāfiqīna* — "strive against the disbelievers and the hypocrites" — *wa ughluẓ ʿalayhim* — "and be harsh with them." The verse is, as the source text notes, *identical* to verse 9:73, a fact of great significance: the Qur'an repeats this command word for word in two places, marking it as a permanent element of the Prophet's mission and of the community's duty after him.

The first word, *jāhid*, is the imperative of *jihād*, from the root *j-h-d*, which fundamentally means to strive, to exert, to struggle with utmost effort. It is essential to understand this root correctly, because no word in the Islamic vocabulary has been more misunderstood in the modern age. *Jihād* is not synonymous with "holy war"; it is the comprehensive *striving* of the believer in the way of God — a striving that includes, in its full range, the struggle against one's own lower self, the struggle to convey God's message with wisdom and argument, the struggle to uphold justice, and — when all peaceful means are blocked and the community is under attack — the armed defense of the faith. The verse's command to "strive against the disbelievers" is thus a command to *exert every lawful effort* to bring the truth to them and to defend the truth against their opposition. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both record the classical understanding: striving against the disbelievers is undertaken *with the sword as well as through preaching and calling them to God* — that is, the jihād of hand is the last resort, while the jihād of tongue, argument, and example is the permanent duty.

**Two Different Strivings for Two Different Foes**

The verse joins two objects in a single command but the tradition distinguishes their treatment with care, and this distinction is the key to understanding the verse's wisdom. The disbelievers — those who openly reject the faith — are striven against in the open field: by argument, by the call to God, and, where oppression makes it necessary, by the sword. The hypocrites — *al-munāfiqūn* — are an entirely different case. The hypocrite *appears* to be a Muslim; he is within the community, outwardly professing faith while inwardly working against it. He cannot be fought with the sword, because he outwardly belongs to the community and enjoys its protection. Against him, the jihād is of a different kind: the legal punishments for his transgressions when they become manifest, the exposure of his deceptions, the firm application of the law, and the refusal to be misled by his outward show. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī state this understanding explicitly: striving against the hypocrites is by applying the legal punishments (*ḥudūd*) for their transgressions and by dealing with them sternly in word and stance, since their threat is inward and deceptive rather than outward and military.

This distinction is a model of the Qur'an's realism. It recognizes that the enemies of a community of faith come in two kinds — the open enemy who can be met openly, and the hidden enemy who must be met with discernment, firmness, and law. The modern reader may translate this easily: every movement, institution, or nation faces both the open opponent and the internal underminer, and the two require different responses. The open opponent is met with argument and, where necessary, strength; the internal underminer is met with accountability, transparency, and the firm application of agreed rules. The verse teaches that both responses are part of a single *jihād* — a single comprehensive striving for the truth.

**"Be Harsh with Them": Firmness in Its Proper Place**

The command *wa ughluẓ ʿalayhim* — "and be harsh with them" — requires careful understanding, for taken alone it can be misread. The word *ughluẓ* is from *gh-l-ẓ*, meaning to be thick, coarse, severe, harsh. The commentators are careful to locate this harshness in its proper place. It is a harshness directed *at the opposition to the truth*, not a harshness of general disposition; it is a firmness of stance against those who actively wage war on God's message, not a cruelty toward people as such. The Qur'an itself, in the same breath of revelation, defines the Prophet's ﷺ fundamental character in the opposite terms:

> *"And by the mercy of God, you were gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you."* (Qur'an 3:159)

The reconciliation of these two verses is the reconciliation of two truths: the Prophet ﷺ was *mercy itself* to those open to guidance, and *firmness itself* against those who waged war on guidance. Mercy and firmness are not opposites in the Qur'anic vision; they are two faces of the same commitment to the truth. A physician is gentle with the patient who seeks healing and firm with the disease that destroys — the gentleness and the firmness are one single dedication to life. So the Prophet ﷺ was gentle with the seeker and firm with the enemy, and both were mercy.

The early community understood this balance perfectly. The same Prophet ﷺ who said "I was not sent to curse, but I was sent as a mercy" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2599) also stood firm, when firmness was required, against those who had made war on his community. The verse's command to "be harsh" is thus not a license for cruelty — the Qur'an explicitly forbids transgression in every form — but a command not to waver, not to be diluted by sentiment, not to mistake weakness for kindness when the truth itself is under attack. There are moments when firmness *is* the most merciful act, because it prevents a greater harm and protects what is precious. The verse teaches the believer to have the courage of that firmness when it is called for.

**"Their Refuge Is Hell": The Terminal Reality of the Unrepentant**

The verse closes with a sobering statement of consequence: *wa maʾwāhum jahannam* — "their refuge is Hell" — *wa biʾsa al-maṣīr* — "what an evil journey's end!" The word *maʾwā* means the place of resort, the dwelling, the final shelter to which one retreats. The image is devastating in its irony: those who strove against the truth will find their *shelter*, their *refuge*, their final place of rest — in Hell. The verse then seals it with the Qur'anic refrain of condemnation, *biʾsa al-maṣīr* — "what an evil journey's end!" — the same phrase that recurs throughout the Qur'an to mark the ultimate destination of the unrepentant deniers.

But here again the sūrah's larger architecture must be kept in view. The verse's condemnation of the disbelievers and hypocrites is immediately followed, in the very next verses, by the story of the wives of Noah and Lot and the wife of Pharaoh — and the whole point of those final portraits is that *the categories are not sealed.* The wife of Noah and the wife of Lot were, in effect, among the disbelievers and betrayers; the wife of Pharaoh, born into the household of the greatest tyrant, became among the most honored of the believers. The verse's "their refuge is Hell" describes the destination of those who *persist* in disbelief and hypocrisy to the end — not the fate of every person who ever wavered. The Qur'an never closes the door before the door has actually closed; as long as a soul lives, the possibility of turning remains. The harshness of this verse is thus held in balance, by the verses that follow, with the mercy that can transform the wife of Pharaoh into a model for all believers.

**The Jihād That Endures: A Clarification for the Modern Age**

Given the modern age's acute sensitivity to this word, it is worth stating the verse's permanent teaching with precision. *Jihād* in the Qur'an is, first and forever, the striving of the soul to live and die in submission to God — the struggle against one's own weaknesses, the effort to convey the truth with wisdom and beautiful exhortation, and the labor to establish justice and defend the community against oppression. The armed dimension of jihād is real but strictly conditioned: it is defensive, it is regulated by law, it forbids harm to non-combatants, and it is a means, never an end. The command of this verse, addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, was fulfilled in the Prophet's ﷺ own life as a striving that began with preaching and persuasion, endured persecution with patience, and turned to the sword only when the community's very survival required it. The believer of every age inherits the same comprehensive striving — against the open enemies of truth by argument and example, against the inner enemies by self-discipline and the law, and against the tyranny of the self by the constant *jihād* of the heart, which the Prophet ﷺ called "the greater jihād." The verse is thus not a summons to violence but a summons to *total, principled striving* in the way of God — a striving whose first battlefield is the self and whose ultimate aim is the truth.

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:10

> **God sets forth as an example for those who disbelieve the wife of Noah and the wife of Lot. They were under two of Our righteous servants; then they betrayed them, and they availed them naught against God. And it was said unto both, "Enter the Fire with those who enter."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn to Example: Four Women, One Lesson**

With the tenth verse, the sūrah enters its magnificent conclusion — the four portraits of women from sacred history that the source text rightly calls the key to the whole chapter. The device is *mathal* — *ḍaraba Allāhu mathalan* — "God sets forth an example," the Qur'anic method of teaching by vivid parable drawn from real history. The examples are arranged in two deliberate contrasts: two *disbelieving* women married to *righteous* prophets (the wives of Noah and Lot), and, in the verses to follow, two *believing* women placed in the households of *disbelief and tyranny* (the wife of Pharaoh, and Mary). The four portraits together constitute the sūrah's final and most searching argument, and their point is single and devastating: *no human being is saved by association, and no human being is condemned by environment.* Each soul stands alone before its Lord, bearing only what it earned.

This is the truth toward which the entire sūrah has been moving. The opening verses dealt with the Prophet's own wives — the most honored women of the community — and gently warned them that their nearness to the Prophet ﷺ was not a guarantee. Now, at the close, the sūrah generalizes that warning into universal law, using the most famous women of previous dispensations as its exhibits. The wife of Noah and the wife of Lot were married to two of the greatest prophets who ever lived — men who spoke directly with God's revelation and carried His message to nations — and yet their marriage to those prophets availed them nothing, because their own hearts were corrupt. The lesson is inescapable: *the righteousness of another does not benefit the one who works corruption.*

**The Two Betrayals: What "Betrayal" Means Here**

The verse states their crime in a single, weighty word: *fa-khānatāhum* — "then they betrayed them." The commentators are careful to explain precisely what this betrayal was and was not, and their care is itself instructive. The betrayal was *not* adultery — God's prophets are protected from such disgrace in their households, and the tradition is unanimous that no prophet's wife ever committed fornication, for that would be a dishonor that the divine protection of prophethood excludes. Rather, the betrayal was in *religion*: each wife betrayed her husband *in the matter of faith*, siding with his enemies and working against his mission.

For the wife of Noah, the betrayal was that she used to tell his people that he was a madman — she lent her voice to the mockery of the disbelievers, confirming them in their rejection of her own husband's message. For the wife of Lot, the betrayal was that she used to inform his people of the whereabouts of his guests — the angels who had come to him in the form of young men — so that the people of Sodom could pursue their wicked designs upon them. Both women, in other words, used the intimacy of the marriage bed and the home to serve the enemies of God's message. This is the full meaning of "betrayal" here: not the betrayal of a bed, but the far graver betrayal of a *calling* — the use of one's position of nearness to undermine the truth one's husband carried. It is the betrayal of the soul's own covenant, enacted through the betrayal of the closest human bond.

**"Under Two of Our Righteous Servants": The Irony of Privileged Proximity**

The verse frames the whole tragedy with a phrase of terrible irony: *kānatā taḥta ʿabdayni min ʿibādinā ṣāliḥayn* — "they were under two of Our righteous servants." The word *taḥta* — "under" — denotes both the marital relation (a wife is "under" her husband's care and authority) and the position of privilege (they were sheltered "under" the wing of prophethood). And the description of Noah and Lot as *ʿibādinā ṣāliḥayn* — "two of Our righteous servants" — is the Qur'an's own honorific for its prophets: God Himself calls them His righteous servants. The point of the phrase is to make the contrast as stark as possible. These two women enjoyed the closest possible proximity to righteousness — they lived with, ate with, and were married to two of the most righteous men in human history. They heard revelation discussed at their own hearth; they saw prophecy lived before their own eyes. And yet it availed them nothing, because the proximity was external and the corruption was internal.

Here is the sūrah's deepest lesson, and it is a hard one for every religious community to hear: *proximity to what is holy is not itself holiness.* A person may be married to a saint, born to a prophet, seated in the first row of the mosque, or descended from a noble lineage — and remain, in the soul, a stranger to all of it. The wife of Noah had the greatest preacher of his age under her own roof, and his preaching did not save her; the wife of Lot had the purest man of Sodom as her husband, and his purity did not cleanse her. Faith is not inherited, not borrowed, and not positional; it is a reality of the individual heart, and where it is absent, no external association can supply it.

**"They Availed Them Naught Against God": The Futility of Borrowed Protection**

The verse then states the consequence with finality: *fa-lam yughniyā ʿanhumā min Allāhi shayʾan* — "and they availed them naught against God." The husbands — the two righteous prophets — could not protect their wives from God's decree. When the punishment came — the flood for the people of Noah, the rain of stones for the people of Sodom — the wives were swept away with the disbelievers. No intercession from their prophet-husbands, no plea, no appeal to the marriage bond, could save them. The Qur'an records the fate of Lot's wife elsewhere with terrible brevity:

> *"So We saved him and his family, except his wife; she was among those who remained behind."* (Qur'an 7:83; 11:81; 26:170–71; 29:32)

And the Genesis tradition, which the Qur'an's narrative completes, preserves the image of her turning back toward the doomed city and being fixed in place — a pillar of salt — the very emblem of a heart that, though outwardly rescued, remained inwardly attached to what was being destroyed. The wife of Noah met her end in the flood, drowned with those who had rejected the ark. Both women had the means of salvation standing beside them — the ark, the command to flee — and both perished, because salvation is not a place but a heart.

**"Enter the Fire with Those Who Enter": The Sentence of the Betrayers**

The verse closes with the sentence pronounced upon them: *wa qīla adkhulā al-nāra maʿa al-dākhilīn* — "and it was said unto both, 'Enter the Fire with those who enter.'" The passive voice — "it was said" — is the Qur'an's characteristic way of conveying the divine command on the Last Day without ascribing it directly, and the sentence is exact in its justice. They entered the Fire *with those who enter* — not alone, not singled out, but in the company of the disbelievers with whom they had sided. They chose their company in this world — they aligned themselves with the enemies of the prophets — and in the next world they are given precisely the company they chose. The verse is a picture of the most exact of justices: the company you keep, in the deepest sense, is the company you choose, and the choice follows you.

**The Warning for Every Community of Faith**

Let us state plainly what this verse says to the believer of every age, because it is the sūrah's most uncomfortable and most necessary truth. *Your association with the righteous will not save you.* Being married to a good person does not make you good; being the child, sibling, or friend of a righteous person does not make you righteous; being a member of a community that carries the truth does not, by itself, make you a bearer of it. Each soul will stand before God alone, with only what it earned. The wife of Noah and the wife of Lot are the eternal witnesses to this truth — two women who had every external advantage and lost everything, because the one thing that mattered was absent: their own faith.

And yet the warning is not despair, because the very next verses — the portrait of the wife of Pharaoh and of Mary — will show the mirror image: that *no environment can condemn the one whose own heart is with God.* The two sides of the sūrah's lesson are inseparable. If association cannot save, neither can environment damn; if the daughter of the noblest house can fall, the wife of the worst tyrant can rise. The four portraits together teach the single, liberating, and terrifying truth at the heart of the Qur'an's doctrine of individual responsibility: *you are not your circumstances — neither saved by them nor doomed by them. You are what your own soul, before God, actually is.*

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:11

> **And God sets forth as an example for those who believe the wife of Pharaoh when she said, "My Lord, build for me a house near unto Thee in the Garden, deliver me from Pharaoh and his deeds, and deliver me from the wrongdoing people."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mirror Image: A Saint in the House of a Tyrant**

The eleventh verse turns the sūrah's argument on its head with a single, breathtaking portrait. Where the previous verse showed two women *married to prophets* who nevertheless perished in disbelief, this verse shows a woman *married to the greatest tyrant* who nevertheless attained the highest rank of faith. The wife of Pharaoh — known in the Islamic tradition by the beautiful name Āsiyah bint Muzāḥim — is held up as an example *for those who believe*, and her story is the perfect counterweight to the warning that came before. If the wives of Noah and Lot prove that *no association can save the corrupt heart*, the wife of Pharaoh proves that *no environment can corrupt the sincere heart.* The two truths together form the complete doctrine of individual responsibility: your circumstances neither save you nor condemn you; only your own soul does.

The contrast is as stark as the Qur'an could possibly make it. The wife of Noah lived in a prophet's household and heard the truth daily, yet disbelieved. The wife of Pharaoh lived in the household of a man who claimed to be God, surrounded by the most extreme idolatry and oppression ever recorded, yet believed. Pharaoh — *Firʿawn* — is in the Qur'an the very type of tyranny and self-deification:

> *"And Pharaoh said: 'O nobles, I know of no god for you other than myself.'"* (Qur'an 28:38)

> *"Then he gathered and called out, saying: 'I am your lord, most high.'"* (Qur'an 79:23–24)

To be the wife of such a man — to live under his roof, in his palace, amid his court, under his absolute power — was to live in the very furnace of disbelief. And yet it was precisely in that furnace that Āsiyah's faith was forged. Her example teaches the believer that the sincerity of the heart is not determined by the wickedness of the surroundings; the light of faith can burn in the darkest place, and often burns brightest there.

**The Supplication of Āsiyah: A Prayer That Became a Charter**

The verse records the words Āsiyah spoke — and they are among the most beautiful and most frequently recited supplications in the entire Qur'an. She said:

> *"My Lord, build for me a house near unto Thee in the Garden, deliver me from Pharaoh and his deeds, and deliver me from the wrongdoing people."* (Qur'an 66:11)

Every phrase of this prayer is a treasure, and the commentators linger over each. *Rabbi bni lī ʿindaka baytan fī al-jannah* — "My Lord, build for me a house *near unto Thee* in the Garden." Notice what she asks first: not deliverance from her tormentor, not the relief of her suffering, but a *house near God in Paradise*. Before asking to be saved *from* something, she asks to be brought *to* Something — and the Something she desires is proximity to her Lord. The commentators (al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr) note the spiritual priority this reveals: Āsiyah's heart was so filled with longing for God that even in the extremity of her torture, her first request was for His nearness. She asked for the *ḥaythiyyah* of Paradise — not merely Paradise, but a dwelling *in God's presence*, for the believer's ultimate desire is not the Garden's comforts but the Garden's Lord.

Then, only after this, comes the prayer for deliverance: *wa najjinī min Firʿawna wa ʿamalihi* — "deliver me from Pharaoh and his deeds." The order is instructive. She prays to be delivered not merely from Pharaoh the man, but from *his deeds* — from the system of disbelief, oppression, and corruption that he embodied. And then the final, widest phrase: *wa najjinī min al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn* — "and deliver me from the wrongdoing people" — extending the prayer beyond Pharaoh to the entire people who participated in the wrongdoing. The prayer thus moves in widening circles: from her own longing for God, to her personal deliverance, to the deliverance of all the oppressed from all the oppressors. It is a supplication of such beauty and depth that it has become a permanent part of the Muslim's treasury of prayer, recited by believers in every age who find themselves, in whatever form, "in the house of Pharaoh" and longing for the house of God.

**The Story Behind the Prayer: Faith Tested to the Limit**

The commentators — Ibn Kathīr, the Jalālayn, al-Rāzī, and others — fill in the story that the verse compresses. When Āsiyah believed in Moses — when she recognized the truth of his message and of his God — her faith was discovered, and Pharaoh subjected her to the most terrible tortures to force her to recant. The tradition describes how she was stretched out under the scorching sun, and how, as the torture reached its height, God opened a vision for her of her house in Paradise — and it was in that moment, seeing the reward that awaited her, that she uttered these words. She chose her Lord over her tormentor, and she was taken to Paradise. The tradition holds her up as one of the four women who attained perfection — the Prophet ﷺ himself said:

> *"The best of the women of the people of Paradise are Khadījah bint Khuwaylid, Fāṭimah bint Muḥammad, Maryam bint ʿImrān, and Āsiyah bint Muzāḥim, the wife of Pharaoh."* (Musnad Aḥmad; similar in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3411, with slightly varying wording)

This report — preserved in the most authoritative collections — fixes Āsiyah's rank permanently: she stands with Khadījah, Fāṭimah, and Mary as one of the four most perfect women who ever lived. And the extraordinary thing about her place on that list is her *origin*: three of the four were women of the Prophet's own household or the mother of Jesus, born into or brought into the circle of faith — but Āsiyah came from the very heart of the enemy's palace. She is the proof, carved into the tradition's own memory, that *faith has no geography and no genealogy; it is the free act of the individual soul, possible anywhere, to anyone.*

**The Deliverance of Āsiyah: What "Deliver Me" Means**

The verse's prayer for deliverance was answered in a manner the tradition records with wonder. Some commentators relate that Āsiyah was taken up to Paradise in body as well as soul, delivered from Pharaoh's hands by God's direct intervention before the worst could be completed. The Qur'an itself does not narrate her death; it preserves only her prayer and her rank, as though to say that the prayer *was* the deliverance — the moment she turned her heart wholly to God, she was already free, whatever her body endured. This is a truth of immense comfort to every believer who suffers in circumstances from which there seems no escape: *the freedom of the heart is not held captive by the bondage of the body.* Āsiyah, stretched upon the torture-rack of a tyrant, was freer than her tormentor on his throne, because her soul had reached its true home while his was already lost. Her prayer "deliver me" is thus answered in the deepest sense the moment it is sincerely made — for the one who has God has been delivered, even before the chains fall.

**The Modern Meaning: Faith Against the Current**

The wife of Pharaoh speaks with extraordinary directness to the modern believer, because her situation is in some ways the situation of every sincere believer in a secular age: she was a person of faith surrounded by a dominant culture that considered her faith absurd, even criminal, and that held absolute power over her life. And her example teaches precisely what such a believer needs to hear. First, *faith can survive and flourish in a hostile environment* — no surrounding culture, however powerful, can reach the sanctuary of a heart that belongs to God. Second, *the believer's first longing is for God Himself, not merely for relief* — the deepest freedom is not escape from difficulty but nearness to the Lord, and that nearness is available now, in any circumstance. Third, *the believer's prayer should widen outward* — from one's own deliverance to the deliverance of all the oppressed, for the heart that has known Pharaoh's house cannot rest while any of God's servants remain in it. And fourth, *no worldly power is absolute* — Pharaoh, the self-proclaimed god, could not hold a single sincere soul against her will, and the tyrants of every age discover the same limit: they can touch the body but never the believing heart.

**A Name to Live By**

Finally, Āsiyah's name itself — preserved by the tradition though not spoken in the verse — carries a fitting meaning, for it is related to the root conveying healing and consolation. She is remembered as the woman who, from within the worst house in history, reached the best house in eternity; who traded the palace of a false god for a dwelling near the true God; and whose single prayer, uttered in the extremity of suffering, became a lamp for every believer who has ever felt surrounded by the powers of disbelief. Her example stands at the end of the sūrah as the definitive answer to the warning of the wives of Noah and Lot: *where a sincere heart is, there is salvation — and no Pharaoh on earth can prevent it.*

## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:12

> **and Mary, the daughter of ʿImrān, who preserved her chastity. Then We breathed therein of Our Spirit, and she confirmed the Words of her Lord and His Books; and she was among the devoutly obedient.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Portrait and the Sūrah's Climax**

The twelfth verse completes the sūrah's gallery of women with the fourth and crowning portrait: Mary, the daughter of ʿImrān, the mother of Jesus. The verse continues directly from the eleventh — the grammatical construction, as the commentators note, means that Mary is likewise "set forth as an example for those who believe," a second exhibit of the believing woman alongside the wife of Pharaoh. The two believing women are thus placed together as a pair, mirroring the pair of disbelieving women at the start of the passage: two women lost despite their prophets, and two women saved despite their trials. And in this final portrait the sūrah reaches its summit, for Mary is not merely an example of faith under trial — she is the woman whom the Qur'an itself declares, in another passage, to have been *chosen and purified and preferred above all the women of the worlds*:

> *"And when the angels said: 'O Mary, indeed God has chosen you and purified you and preferred you above all the women of the worlds.'"* (Qur'an 3:42)

The progression of the four portraits is thus not random but ascending: from the two lost women, to the rescued wife of Pharaoh, to the woman chosen above all women. The sūrah closes on its highest note — the example of the woman whose faith, purity, and devotion reached the very summit of human excellence.

**"The Daughter of ʿImrān": The Lineage of Mary**

The verse names her *Maryam ibnata ʿImrān* — "Mary, the daughter of ʿImrān." The identification matters. ʿImrān is the name of Mary's father, whom the extra-biblical tradition knows as Joachim; the Qur'an gives his name in its third chapter, whose family narrative opens with the house of ʿImrān. The name itself — ʿImrān — is the Arabic form of the same name borne by Amram, the father of Moses, Aaron, and Miriam in the Hebrew tradition, and the Qur'an's use of these names links Mary's family to the great prophetic lineage of Israel. But the deeper point of naming her father is made by the commentators: Mary is identified by her *lineage*, and her lineage was one of the most honored in the world — and yet, as the sūrah has just spent its final verses establishing, *lineage itself is nothing before God.* What made Mary great was not that she was the daughter of ʿImrān, but that she was, in her own person, the woman who preserved her chastity, believed, and was devoutly obedient. The verse names her lineage precisely so that the contrast between lineage and personal merit may be complete: the noblest ancestry in the world is honored here, not for its own sake, but because it produced a soul whose own excellence confirmed it.

**"Who Preserved Her Chastity": The Integrity of a Consecrated Soul**

The verse's first description of Mary is *allatī aḥṣanat farjahā* — "who preserved her chastity." The phrase is freighted with meaning. The word *farj* denotes that which must be guarded, the private sanctity of the body; and *aḥṣanat*, from *ḥ-ṣ-n*, meaning to fortify, to protect, to keep inviolable — the same root from which *taḥṣīn* (fortification) and *ḥiṣn* (fortress) derive. Mary is described as the one who *fortified* her chastity — who kept her purity as an impregnable sanctuary, untouched by any violation. The phrase occurs elsewhere in the Qur'an in nearly identical form:

> *"And she who guarded her chastity (Mary) — so We breathed into her of Our Spirit, and made her and her son a sign for the worlds."* (Qur'an 21:91)

The commentators (al-Rāzī and others) note the historical weight of this description. When Mary returned with the infant Jesus, her people accused her — as the Qur'an records — of an unchaste act:

> *"Then she brought him to her people, carrying him. They said: 'O Mary, you have certainly done a thing unprecedented. O sister of Aaron, your father was not a wicked man, nor was your mother unchaste.'"* (Qur'an 19:27–28)

The verse's declaration that Mary "preserved her chastity" is thus, in part, the divine vindication of Mary against every accusation — God Himself bearing witness to her purity. But it is more than vindication; it is the statement of a *quality*. Mary's chastity was not merely a physical state but a spiritual achievement — the guarding of her whole being for God, the consecration of her purity to the divine will. This is the first dimension of her excellence: *integrity*, the fortress of the self kept inviolate.

**"Then We Breathed Therein of Our Spirit": The Miracle of Jesus**

The verse then states the miracle that defines Mary's place in sacred history: *fa-nafakhnā fīhi min rūḥinā* — "then We breathed therein of Our Spirit." This is the Qur'an's description of the conception of Jesus — without a father, by the direct creative command of God, a breath of the divine Spirit entering the guarded chastity of Mary and producing the Messiah. The commentators are careful and precise about what this means and does not mean, because the phrase has been the site of much theological discussion. Ibn Kathīr, the Jalālayn, and al-Ṭabarī all record that "Our Spirit" is understood by many to refer to the Angel Gabriel — for the angels are described in the Qur'an as made of spirit, and Gabriel is himself called "the Trustworthy Spirit" (al-Rūḥ al-Amīn, 26:193) and "the Holy Spirit" (Rūḥ al-Qudus). On this reading, God breathed into Mary through the agency of Gabriel, who is God's creature and messenger, and the breath was the means by which the creative word was conveyed. Other commentators read "Our Spirit" as referring to one of the spirits God has created — the spirit of Jesus himself, created by God's command — while the most literal reading takes it as the Spirit of God, the life-breath that God attributes to Himself as a mark of honor, just as He attributes the Kaʿbah to Himself as "My House" and the she-camel of Ṣāliḥ as "the she-camel of God."

All these readings agree on the essential point, which the Qur'an states elsewhere with absolute clarity:

> *"Indeed, the example of Jesus in the sight of God is like that of Adam: He created him from dust, then said to him, 'Be,' and he was."* (Qur'an 3:59)

> *"The Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, was only a messenger of God, and His word which He cast into Mary, and a spirit from Him."* (Qur'an 4:171)

The breath of the Spirit is thus the Qur'an's way of declaring the miracle — that Jesus was created by God's command without a father — while simultaneously guarding the absolute truth of God's unity. Mary remained a pure virgin, the conception was a divine act, and Jesus was God's servant and messenger, not a partner in divinity. The verse's phrase "of Our Spirit" is a mark of *honor* — God attributing the life-breath to Himself as He attributes what He honors — never a mark of *division* in the divine essence. This is the delicate theological precision the commentators preserve, and it is essential: the Qur'an honors Mary and Jesus to the highest degree while keeping the absolute oneness of God intact.

**"She Confirmed the Words of Her Lord and His Books": The Faith of Mary**

The verse then states the second dimension of Mary's excellence: *wa ṣaddaqat bi-kalimāti rabbihā wa kutubihi* — "and she confirmed the Words of her Lord and His Books." The word *ṣaddaqat*, from *ṣ-d-q*, means to affirm as true, to believe with complete conviction — the same root from which *ṣiddīq* (the intensely truthful, the one who confirms the truth totally) derives. Mary is described as the one who *confirmed* — who believed without hesitation — in the words of her Lord and in His revealed Books. Al-Rāzī and others explain that "the Words of her Lord" refers to the divine decree and the divine revelation, including the glad tidings brought to her by the angels and the miracle announced to her; and "His Books" refers to all the previous scriptures revealed before the Qur'an. Mary is thus described as a woman of *complete faith in revelation* — she believed in all that God had revealed, from the Torah to the glad tidings of her own son, without wavering or doubt.

This is the essential contrast with the wives of Noah and Lot, who had revelation in their own homes and disbelieved it. Mary had no prophet for a husband; she had only the word of God brought to her by the angel — and she believed completely. The verse thus completes the sūrah's argument with perfect symmetry: *faith is not a matter of external access but of internal assent.* The wives of Noah and Lot had every access to truth and refused it; Mary had a single angelic message and accepted it with her whole being. The difference was not in their circumstances but in their hearts.

**"Among the Devoutly Obedient": The Crown of the Portrait**

The verse closes with the phrase the sūrah itself has prepared us to recognize: *wa kānat min al-qānitīn* — "and she was among the devoutly obedient." The word *qānitīn* is from *q-n-t*, the root the sūrah used in verse 5 in describing the ideal believing women as *qānitāt* — and, as the commentary on that verse established, this root in the Qur'an relates *exclusively* to obedience toward and worship of God, never to servility before human beings. Mary is crowned with the very quality the sūrah held up as the summit of feminine excellence: constant, serene, settled devotion to God. The commentators note a further beautiful subtlety in the phrasing. The literal wording is "she was *among* the devoutly obedient" (*min al-qānitīn*) — using the masculine plural form, which in Arabic can denote a mixed or general category — and Ālūsī and others observe that this very phrase can also indicate *purity of lineage*: she came *from* the line of the devoutly obedient, the prophets and the righteous. Both meanings are true at once: Mary was herself among the devout, and she descended from the devout — her personal excellence crowned a lineage of excellence. The two senses together close the portrait perfectly: lineage *and* personal merit, in her case, converged — not because lineage confers merit, but because she added to the merit of her line the crowning merit of her own devotion.

**The Sūrah's Conclusion: Four Women and One Truth**

With this verse, Sūrat al-Taḥrīm closes, and its final argument stands complete. Four women — the wife of Noah, the wife of Lot, the wife of Pharaoh, and Mary — are set before the believer as two pairs of opposites, and their combined lesson is the sūrah's ultimate message. *The righteous marriage did not save the corrupt wives; the tyrannical marriage did not destroy the sincere wife; and the greatest purity and devotion could flower in a woman who stood alone, with only her faith, before God.* The four portraits together demolish every illusion of borrowed righteousness and every despair of inherited circumstance. No one is saved by being near the saved, and no one is lost by being near the lost. Each soul — prophet's wife or Pharaoh's wife, daughter of the noblest house or mother in the humblest town — stands before its Lord with only what its own heart contains.

And this is the answer to the sūrah's beginning. The chapter opened in the Prophet's own household, with a gentle question about a forbidden thing and a broken confidence; it closes with the four women of sacred history, the eternal examples of what the individual soul can be. The arc from the opening to the close is the arc of the whole Qur'an: from the particular to the universal, from the private to the eternal, from a honey pot in Madinah to the house near God in the Garden that Āsiyah asked for, and that every sincere soul may hope to reach. The sūrah's final word is not warning but example — not a threat but a portrait — for God's last teaching to the believer here is not "fear what you might become" but "behold what the human soul, turned wholly to its Lord, has been." The wife of Pharaoh and Mary are held up, at the end, as the living proof that it can be done — that in any house, in any age, a heart can belong to God alone, and that heart is what God will receive.
