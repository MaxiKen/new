# Sūrah Nūḥ (Chapter 71) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sūrah Named for Persistence, Not for the Flood**

Sūrah Nūḥ, "Chapter 71," takes its name from the prophet whose nine-and-a-half centuries of unrelenting preaching supply its entire subject matter. It is a short Makkan sūrah — twenty-eight verses, occupying the second third of the twenty-ninth juzʾ, standing between Sūrat al-Muḍammil and Sūrat al-Qamar in the received order of the written muṣḥaf. Yet its brevity is deceptive. What the chapter actually narrates is not a disaster but a *career*: the longest recorded preaching mission in sacred history, its methods, its refusal, and its verdict. Nothing in it describes the ark, the dimensions of the vessel, the depth of the water, or the relief of the survivors. The flood arrives in a single compressed verse — *"Because of their sins they were drowned, then made to enter a Fire"* (71:25) — and the sūrah immediately returns to what it really cares about: why a people who heard the truth for a thousand years moved further from it, and what a messenger is to do when his labours produce nothing visible.

That arrangement is deliberate, and the classical commentators noticed it. Where the book of Genesis devotes chapters to the architecture of the ark, the catalogue of animals, and the covenant of the rainbow, the Qur'an reserves its detail for the *argument*: the dialogue, the escalation of outreach, the anatomy of refusal, the final supplication. The story is not told to record history; it is told to produce guidance — a function the Qur'an states about the whole prophetic corpus:

> *"All that We recount unto thee of the stories of the messengers is that whereby We make firm thine heart. And herein there has come unto thee the truth, and an exhortation and a reminder for the believers."* (Qur'an 11:120)

"Whereby We make firm thy heart" — *nuṣabbitu bihi qalbaka*. The verb *th-b-t* means to fix, to anchor, to make something immovable after it had wobbled. The consolee here is the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ himself, threatened with despair by the disbelief of his own townspeople; and the medicine administered is the knowledge that Nūḥ, honoured above most of creation, preached for a millennium to a handful. Steadiness is the sūrah's first fruit, and it is offered first to the preacher before it is offered to the audience.

**Its Place Among the Four Prophet-Named Sūrahs and Among the Fragments**

Nūḥ is one of four sūrahs named after a figure known from the Torah — the others being Yūnus (Jonah), Yūsuf (Joseph), and Ibrāhīm (Abraham). This naming is itself an instruction: the Qur'an is willing to place an Israelite prophet's personal name on the cover of a chapter addressed to the Arabs of Makkah, because it regards the prophets as one chain rather than as foreign history. The Nūḥ narrative is distributed across that chain with a care that rewards tabulation. Seven passages carry it in the Qur'an's mid-length retellings: 7:59–64 (in the sequence of prophets who came after Hūd), 10:71–73 (with the confrontation at the court of Pharaoh appended), 11:25–48 (the longest account, including the dialogue about the drowned son), 21:76–77 (two verses compressing the whole rescue), 23:23–30 (the account framed by the command to build the ark and by the landing on al-Jūdī), 26:105–122 (the Nūḥ episode as the first of a series in which every people answers the messenger with the same formula), 29:14–15 (the nine hundred and fifty years), 37:75–82 (the salutation *"Peace upon Nūḥ in the worlds"*), 54:9–15 (the water "carrying him" and the ark as a thing of *"planks and nails"*), and 66:10 (where Nūḥ's wife is named among the treacherous). Sūrah 71 does not repeat any of these. It supplies the interior monologue missing from all of them: Nūḥ's complaint to God about the *failure* of his effort (vv. 5–9), and his prayer of final settlement (vv. 26–28). Read against the others, this sūrah is the *psychology* of the mission; the others are its *drama*.

The Qur'an's refusal to narrate the story as chronology is not an oversight to be repaired. As the source commentary observes, no continuous life of Nūḥ can be patched together from the fragments, because the fragments were never shards of a biography. Each passage is placed where its *point* is needed. Sūrah 11, which addresses the believers with the reality of divine help, tells how Nūḥ built and was mocked; Sūrah 54, whose theme is that the warning was denied, tells how *"every proud tyrant"* was swept away; Sūrah 26, which dwells on the repetition of rejection, tells how Nūḥ's people called him a madman and threatened to stone him; Sūrah 71, recited in the middle of the Makkan stalemate, tells the Prophet ﷺ and his hearers what nine centuries of patience look like from the inside — and tells the Quraysh exactly what their own ears are doing.

**The Mercy-Framed Warning: What the Sūrah Chooses to Emphasise**

The single most striking decision of this chapter is that its punishment-warning is bracketed by invitations to forgiveness. Verse 1 announces a *"painful punishment"*; verse 4 promises *forgiveness and a reprieve*; verses 10–12 promise rain, wealth, sons, gardens, and rivers on one condition — *"Seek forgiveness of your Lord! Truly He is Forgiving"*; verse 25 executes the sentence; and verse 28 closes with the prophet's own *"My Lord, forgive me and my parents…"*. The sūrah thus begins and ends with the word for forgiveness (*gh-f-r* occurs in vv. 4, 7, 10, 28), and the flood sits in the middle as the exception that had to be earned by nine centuries of refusal. The rhetorical design is a theology in miniature: punishment is never the original intention of a prophetic mission; it is what remains when every other avenue is deliberately blocked by the people themselves.

The Makkan audience heard this in the middle of a similar deadlock. The Prophet ﷺ had been calling Quraysh publicly for years; the chiefs had resolved against him; the poor believers were being tortured; and the revelation kept insisting that the gate was open. Sūrah Nūḥ told that community what a "clear warner" is contractually required to do — exhaust every method — and what God is required to do before He punishes — nothing, except to have been invited and refused. It also told the Quraysh something unflattering and precise: that the people of Nūḥ did not reject the message because it was unconvincing, but because rejecting it was the price their social order could not afford to stop paying. The names of their gods — Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, Nasr — are recited in the Qur'an as a list of loyalties, not as a list of stones (71:23). Anyone who had ever kept a false god because abandoning it would cost him standing recognised himself.

**The Resolute Messenger and the Question of the Flood's Extent**

Along with Ibrāhīm, Mūsā, ʿĪsā, and Muhammad ﷺ, Nūḥ belongs to the *ulū al-ʿazm* — the messengers of firm resolve — a rank the Qur'an gestures at when it commands the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"Therefore be patient, as were the messengers of firm resolve (ulū al-ʿazm), and do not seek to hasten them on."* (Qur'an 46:35)

The tradition also gives him a singularity of rank in the eschatological narrative: in the ḥadīth of intercession, when humanity goes from prophet to prophet on the Day of Standing, they come first to Nūḥ with the words:

> *"O Nūḥ, you are the first messenger whom God sent to the people of the earth."* (al-Bukhārī 4712; Muslim 194, from Abū Hurayrah)

And the Qur'an itself preserves for him a title no other prophet is given in the same way:

> *"…the offspring of those We carried with Nūḥ; truly he was a grateful servant."* (Qur'an 17:3)

*ʿabdan shakūrā* — grateful in the sense that he did not complain of his labour to his people, only to his Lord; and, as the commentators add, grateful on the flood-boat for every plank, which is why gratitude became his name.

Whether the flood covered the entire planet is a question the sūrah leaves open and the tradition debated. Verse 26 — *"Leave not upon the earth a single dweller of the disbelievers"* — is the only verse in the Qur'anic Nūḥ corpus that presses toward universality, and even there "the earth" may be the earth known to that community, *their* land. Elsewhere the Qur'an is careful: *"We saved him and his family"* (29:15), and in every other account the drowned are specified by deed — those who *"denied Our signs"* (7:64; 10:73), *"did wrong"* (11:37; 23:27), an *"evil people"* (21:77). That qualification, the fact that prophetic missions in the Qur'an are always to a named people, and the silence of the other retellings about a global catastrophe, led many later scholars to hold the flood to have been local to Nūḥ's region, while many earlier ones read the plain sense of Genesis and of 71:26 as universal. What matters religiously is untouched by the dispute: God drowned a civilisation that had been forewarned for a millennium, and no one can say He did so without notice.

**Cadence: A Sūrah Built on One Long Open Note**

The rhetoric of Sūrah Nūḥ is among the starkest in the Qur'an, and it is a formal instrument of the sūrah's meaning. After the first four verses, which close on the resonant *-mūn/-īn* of *ʿadhābun ʿalīm*, *nadhīrun mubīn*, and *taʿlamūn*, twenty-four consecutive verses end on a long open vowel in *-ā*, most of them on the ringing sequence *-rā*: *nahārā, firārā, istikbārā, isrārā, ghaffārā, midrārā, anḥārā, waqārā, aṭwārā, sirājā, nabātā, khurūjā, ḥiṣārā, fajjā, khusrā, kubbārā, Naṣrā, ḍalālā, anṣārā, dayyārā, kaffārā, tabārā*. The ear is beaten, verse after verse, on the same open vowel; and the very last word of the sūrah is *tabārā* — "utter ruin." A listener is thus made to experience something like the experience of Nūḥ's people: the same call, again and again, in the same key, until the last word is not an argument but a verdict. The *-rā* chain also carries an internal argument, because the rhyme-words themselves form a sequence of meanings: the *warner* (nadhīr), the *flight* (firār), the *arrogance* (istikbār), the *hidden counsel* (isrār), the *Forgiving One* (ghaffār), the *pouring rain* (midrār), the *rivers* (anhār), the *majesty* (waqār), the *stages* (aṭwār), the *lamp* (sirāj), the *growth* (nabāt), the *expanse* (ḥiṣār), the *loss* (khusr), the *vast plot* (kubbār), the *error* (ḍalāl), the *helpers* (anṣār), the *dweller* (dayyār), the *ingrate* (kaffār), and finally *ruin* (tabār). Sound and sense advance together from summons to sentence.

**The Contents in Four Movements**

The sūrah falls naturally into four movements, and the verse-by-verse commentary that follows tracks them. (i) **Commission and declaration** (vv. 1–4): the sending, the self-description "a clear warner," the threefold command — worship God, be conscious of Him, obey the messenger — and the two prizes: forgiveness and time. (ii) **The complaint of the preacher** (vv. 5–9): Nūḥ reports to his Lord that the call has been continuous, that it produced only flight, that the people physically blocked their senses, and that he had escalated his outreach through every available channel — public, private, and both. (iii) **The offer, argued three ways** (vv. 10–20): forgiveness with material abundance as its consequence; then the argument from the self (stages of creation); then from the cosmos (heavens, moon, sun); then from the earth (growth from soil, return, expanse, roads). (iv) **The verdict and the prayer** (vv. 21–28): the report of disobedience and of the leadership of wealth, the plot, the five named idols, the drowning and the Fire, and the prophet's two final supplications — for the destruction of destructive disbelief, and for the forgiveness of every believer, male and female, who enters the house of safety.

**How to Read What Follows**

Each of the twenty-eight verses is treated below as an independent discourse, exactly as the verse-by-verse method requires: nothing is compressed because the chapter is long, and nothing is merged because the source commentary grouped verses (its notes on 8–9, 10–20, 11–12, 12–13, 14–18, 19–20, and 26–27 are here separated so that each verse speaks for itself). For each verse we attend to the Arabic — roots, morphology, and the choices the translators must smooth over; to the Qur'an's own cross-references, with the relevant text quoted; to authenticated Prophetic reports, with their collections identified; to the positions of the classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Taymiyyah, and others — where they bear on the verse; and to the reasoning that makes the verse's teaching intelligible in a human life: its psychology, its social mechanics, its logic, and its application. No filler is offered in place of substance; where a verse is short, the discussion is long only insofar as the verse genuinely opens onto something.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:1

> **Truly We sent Noah to his people, "Warn your people before a painful punishment comes upon them."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Indeed We Sent" — the Grammar of an Unevasive Opening**

The sūrah begins, as many Qur'anic prophetic narratives do, with the asseverative *innā* — "truly, indeed" — followed by the first-person plural verb: *arsalnā Nūḥan ilā qawmihi*, "We sent Noah to his people." Two grammatical facts carry weight here. The first is the *emphasis*: a claim is being guaranteed. No one is doubting that God sent Nūḥ; the emphasis exists because someone doubts that *this* man — Muhammad ﷺ — has been sent. The Qur'anic prophetic formulas are almost never decorative. When the Makkan audience said of their own prophet that he was a poet, a soothsayer, or a man possessed, the revelation's stock reply is to point at a precedent and swear to it: *We sent* — the same verb, the same structure, the same kind of reception.

The second is the plural of majesty. *Arsalnā* is not "I sent," and it is not a committee. Arabic, like other Semitic languages, uses the plural first person for sovereign speech, and the Qur'an reserves it overwhelmingly for acts of originating, legislating, and judging. That register is fitting, because the sentence is introducing an *institution*: apostleship. The verb's root is *r-s-l*, whose concrete sense is a lead-rope or a thing let out gently so that it reaches its target, and from which Arabic forms *risāla*, "a message," and *rasūl*, "an envoy." A messenger in Qur'anic usage is not an independent contractor with a personal philosophy; he is a rope let down from the sender into the reach of the addressed, his standing entirely derivative. This is why the Qur'an can say of the same mission both "God sent" and "the messenger conveys" without contradiction: the rope does not manufacture the well.

**The Name Nūḥ and the Weight of a Millennium**

The sūrah's hero is named without a patronymic and without a genealogy, in contrast with the historian's habit. The Arab philologists nonetheless pondered the name: some connected *Nūḥ* with *nawḥ*, lamentation and wailing over the dead (whence *niyāḥa*, the funeral dirge), and the commentators record this connection with a certain awe, as though his very name foretold the nine centuries of grief over a people who would not listen. Whether or not the etymology is demonstrable — the name is foreign to Arabic and may simply have been transmitted in its own language — the association captures something true about the portrait the sūrah will draw.

The Qur'an supplies his tenure in one astonishing clause in another place:

> *"And We certainly sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them a thousand years minus fifty, so the flood seized them while they were wrongdoers."* (Qur'an 29:14)

Nine hundred and fifty years of preaching, and the sūrah will report its visible yield in the language of failure: *"my call has increased them in nothing but flight"* (71:6). That pairing — maximum effort, minimum apparent result — is stated deliberately, because the Qur'an refuses to measure missions by conversions. The tradition preserves the ratio: the accounts transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and the early biographers differ widely on how many believed, from a handful of households to a small number of followers, and none of them describes a mass movement. What the exegetes drew from this was a definition of success: the prophet is accountable for *conveyance*, not for *acceptance* — a distinction the Qur'an states to the Prophet ﷺ in terms he could not misread:

> *"So remind them! You are only a reminder. You are not a taskmaster over them."* (Qur'an 88:21–22)

**"Warn Your People" — the Precautionary Logic of Revelation**

The content of the commission is a single imperative: *andhir qawmaka* — "warn your people." The root *n-ḍh-r* conveys the kind of notice that puts a person on guard against something unseen but coming; a *nadhīr* is a warner in the sense that a lookout is a warner, not in the sense that an executioner is one. And the imperative is followed by the word that organises the whole sūrah: *min qabli an yaʾtiyahum* — "before there comes upon them." The preposition "before" is not filler. It asserts a moral law governing God's dealings with humanity: punishment is preceded by notice, and notice is what makes punishment just rather than merely violent. The Qur'an states the law as a divine self-restriction in verses the Makkan pagans would have recognised as a claim about the character of God:

> *"…and We never punish until We have sent a messenger."* (Qur'an 17:15)
> *"And your Lord is not one to destroy the towns until He has sent into their mother-town a messenger reciting to them Our signs."* (Qur'an 28:59)

The verse thus does the work of a legal preamble: it establishes that the defendant was served. The *ʿadhāb al-ʿalīm* — a "painful punishment" — is named so that it can be avoided, not so that it can be savoured; the sūrah's tone, from this first line to the last, is that of an alarm bell rather than of a threat.

**Why a Warning Is an Act of Love, Not of Hostility**

It is worth pausing on the human logic of *idhār*, because the modern reflex misreads it. We treat warnings as intrusive: the doctor who reports a worrying scan, the friend who says "you are heading somewhere bad," the regulator who publishes a hazard. Yet every functioning institution on earth is built on the assumption that forewarning is the highest courtesy one person can pay another. Aviation does not permit a plane to be flown into weather without a notice; medicine does not permit a surgeon to cut without consent; insurance is nothing but the monetised form of warning. The principle underneath all of it is that a being with foresight is obligated to share it with a being who lacks it, and that withholding the warning is a kind of assault.

Now place that ordinary moral intuition on the cosmic scale, and the prophetic office becomes exactly what the word implies: the extension to human beings of knowledge they did not possess about what is coming. This is why the sūrah opens not with "believe or burn" but with "warn them *before*." And it is why the Prophet ﷺ, whose entire manner of leadership is described in one transmitted line, spoke of himself in the register of the household rather than the court:

> *"I am for you in the position of a father: I teach you, and I forbid you, and I command you."* (Abū Dāwūd 14 and Aḥmad, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr — its chain is disputed, but its sense is precisely that of this verse and of 66:6)

A father warns. He does not warn because he wishes harm; he warns because he cannot bear to be blamed for silence. Nūḥ's nine centuries of *nadhāra* are the patience of that role, and the sūrah begins by insisting that the role was assigned by God, not seized by a zealot.

**The Audience of the Verse in Makkah**

Finally, note to whom the first verse really speaks. It is recited *about* Nūḥ *to* the Quraysh of the Prophet's own generation, in the mode the source commentary detects throughout: a mirror held up at an angle. Every element maps onto their situation — a man of no dynasty being sent to a people he belongs to; a warning that is unwelcome precisely because it costs nothing to believe and everything to obey; chiefs who cannot afford to follow a common believer; and a punishment whose date is withheld, so that the warning must be taken seriously today rather than negotiated for later. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both note that the Makkan sūrahs of warning were not aimed at some distant archaeological people but at the hearers present, the Qur'an saying so itself:

> *"…that I may warn you by it, and whoever it reaches."* (Qur'an 6:19)

The sūrah's very first word, then, is a guarantee, and its first command is an act of mercy: a punishment *announced in advance* is, until it arrives, a punishment that can still be cancelled.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:2

> **He said, "O my people! I am a clear warner unto you,"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"O My People" — the Possessive That Refuses to Divorce Him from Them**

Nūḥ's first recorded sentence, in a mission of nine and a half centuries, is a vocative with a possessive suffix: *yā qawmi* — "O *my* people." He does not say "O people of unbelief," nor "O generation of sinners." The same idiom recurs on the lips of the prophets throughout the Qur'an — Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb, and Ibrāhīm's son all address their tormentors as *yā qawmi* (cf. 11:50, 11:62, 11:78, 29:24) — and it is so consistently preserved in the earliest suras that the commentators treat it as an *adab*, a discipline of speech, rather than a stylistic accident.

The reason is psychologically exact. The moment a reformer labels his audience by its worst quality, the argument is over and the tribal reflex begins: he has defined himself as *outside* them, and they have defined themselves as a group under attack. The suffix "-ī" in *qawmī* keeps the relationship intact under maximum disagreement: "whatever you are doing, you are mine and I am of you; I speak from inside the household." Nūḥ will, in verse 21, report his grief to God rather than hurl it at them; the intimacy of verse 2 is what makes that later composure possible. The contrast within this sūrah is instructive: the *people* never once address him as "our Nūḥ"; they call him "a man like you" (11:27) and a "liar" (54:9, 7:64). One side speaks in kinship, the other in estrangement, and the Qur'an lets the reader weigh the difference.

**"A Clear Warner" — Why Clarity Is a Moral Requirement, Not a Rhetorical Bonus**

The self-description is *nadhīrun mubīn* — a warner who is *mubīn*. The participle *mubīn* is from *b-y-n*, "to be distinct, to separate a thing from what is around it so that its edges are visible." Arabic applies *mubīn* both to the one who makes clear and to the thing made clear (the "clear proof," *al-ḥujja al-mubayyana*; the "clear book," 43:2), and grammarians note the word covers both actively and passively: Nūḥ is clear in himself and he makes clear. There is no ambiguity about his status, his message, his terms, or his motives.

Why insist on this, since God had already commanded him to warn? Because clarity is the precondition of the justice announced in verse 1. A warning that is veiled, double-meaning, or diplomatically phrased does not discharge the obligation of *balāgh*, and therefore cannot ground accountability. The Qur'an's own theology of explanation rests on this: *mubīn* is the word attached to the language of revelation itself — *"in a clear Arabic tongue"* (26:195) — and to the Book (5:15; 12:1; 43:2). The classical exegetes draw the inference plainly: a warner is bound to make the boundary unmistakable, and the hearer is thereby stripped of the plea "I did not understand." Ibn Kathīr glosses the phrase as *mubayyan* — "making the way of salvation and the way of ruin evident" — and al-Ṭabarī notes that *mubīn* here means Nūḥ's warner-ship is *apparent to his people*, not merely true in heaven. Both readings converge: the claim is about what the audience can see.

This is a demanding standard for anyone who claims to speak for God, and the sūrah sets it as a floor, not a ceiling. It forbids three things at once. It forbids **obscurity** — prophetic speech may not be a coded message for the gifted. It forbids **ambiguity of interest** — the warner must not be secretly negotiating a benefit, which is why Nūḥ explicitly disclaims payment: *"I do not ask you for a reward; my reward is only upon God"* (11:29). And it forbids **ambiguity of authority** — he does not say "I have realised something" or "my people and I discovered a truth"; he says *I am a warner*, i.e., commissioned by One you also acknowledge. Every element of his claim can be inspected.

**The Office, Not the Man: What Nūḥ Does Not Say**

Read the sentence for its omissions. Nūḥ claims no lineage, no miracle of his own, no inherited priesthood, no coercive power, and no infallibility of his person. He claims a function. The Qur'an keeps this pattern across all its prophet-narratives: *"Say: I do not tell you that with me are the treasures of God, or that I know the Unseen, or that I am an angel; I only follow what is revealed to me"* (6:50). The reason is structural, not modesty for its own sake. If authority resided in the man, the message would be hostage to his biography; if it resides in the office, the message can be tested on its content, and the man remains a human being whose obedience is exemplary but whose person is not worshipped.

The tradition shows how seriously the Prophet ﷺ took this principle of the warner's derivative status. He said:

> *"Do not exaggerate me as the Christians exaggerated ʿĪsā ibn Maryam; say instead: God's servant and His messenger."* (al-Bukhārī 3445, from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb)

That is the boundary *nadhīrun mubīn* draws: exalt the message, do not deify the messenger. Nūḥ's people would later do the opposite with their own righteous dead — erecting statues to Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, and Nasr, men of virtue turned into objects of worship (71:23 and the ḥadīth of Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Bukhārī 4920) — and the sūrah thus places the warner's plain self-description beside the people's inflated veneration of saints, inviting the reader to notice which of the two is the honest one.

**Every Community Has Had Its Nūḥ**

The source commentary observes that this claim is not Nūḥ's private credential but the universal shape of prophethood: all messengers come as *mubashshirūn wa-mundhirūn*, bearers of glad tidings and warners (2:118, 2:213, 4:165, 6:48, 18:56), and that no community was destroyed until a warner had been sent to it (26:208). The Qur'an generalises the point into a law of history:

> *"And there was no community but that a warner has passed through it."* (Qur'an 35:24)

That verse is the reason 71:2 exists at all. The Makkan listener cannot claim that his generation is the first to be asked, nor that his people's stubbornness is unprecedented, nor that God demanded more of Muhammad ﷺ than He demanded of Nūḥ. The claim of *mubīn* also sets the measure for the *rejection*: Nūḥ's people had no excuse of ignorance, and neither did theirs — which is why in the next verses the issue will never be whether the message was clear, only what the hearers chose to do with the clarity.

**The Warner as Counsel, and How Clarifying Works in Practice**

The Arabic for the warner's function overlaps with *n-ṣ-ḥ*, sincere counsel, and the Prophet ﷺ defined the entire religion in that register:

> *"The religion is sincerity (al-dīn al-naṣīḥa). We asked: For whom? He said: For God, for His Book, for His Messenger, for the leaders of the Muslims, and for their common folk."* (Muslim 55, from Tamīm al-Dārī)

Notice what makes counsel *effective* in that ḥadīth, for it is the same set of qualities that made Nūḥ a "clear warner": it is specific, it is addressed to identifiable parties, it is not reserved for a private élite, and its motive is the good of the counselled rather than the status of the counsellor. Modern institutions rediscover this every time they debate "whistle-blowing," "informed consent," or "mandatory disclosure": an intervention works only when it is clear, early, documented, and demonstrably disinterested, and it fails when it is vague, late, secret, or self-serving. The verse thus offers a working standard for every human act of warning — the parent speaking to a teenager, the physician relaying a diagnosis, the teacher correcting a student's direction, the citizen addressing his community. Ask not only "am I right?" but "am I *mubīn*?": have I made it clear enough that no reasonable person can say they were left guessing, and have I kept my own interest out of the way so that clarity is not mistaken for a pitch?

**Clarity and the Absence of Compulsion**

One last shading. *Andhir* means warn, not compel — and the sūrah's own Nūḥ will explicitly disown the role of enforcer, saying nine times over in this chapter, in effect, "I cannot make you." The verse therefore forbids the two corruptions of preaching in every age: the corruption of *softening the warning until it says nothing* and the corruption of *hardening it until it becomes a threat*. A "clear warner" neither dilutes nor bullies. He places the danger on the table, in daylight, in language the hearer can repeat back, and then accepts — as verses 5–9 will show him doing for nine centuries — that the hearer may refuse, while his own duty remains discharged.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:3

> **that you worship God, be mindful of Him, and obey me,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Commands That Contain the Whole of Religion**

The content of the warning is now stated — and it is astonishingly compact. *An ʿbudū Allāha wa-ttaqūhu wa-aṭīʿūni*: worship God; guard against Him; obey me. Three verbs, three objects: God, God, the messenger. Al-Rāzī's observation, quoted already in the source commentary, is that these three duties can be shown to *exhaust* the domain of religion, and the claim is worth testing rather than accepting. Religion, considered as a total human orientation, must answer three questions: **toward whom** does the soul ultimately orient itself? **with what inward quality** does it act when the orientation is tested? and **by what concrete standard** is the orientation expressed in a life? The three commands answer those three questions in order. Tawḥīd names the object; taqwā names the quality; obedience to the messenger names the specification. Remove any one of the three and something predictable collapses: worship without taqwā becomes ritual that leaves the character untouched (the Prophet ﷺ's warning about the man who fasts and gains nothing from his fast but hunger); taqwā without a revealed specification becomes sentiment that each person fashions to suit himself; and obedience to a messenger without worship of God becomes the very idolatry of the righteous that this sūrah will diagnose at verse 23.

Note also the grammar: all three are governed by a single *an*, so that they form one clause with one purpose — they are not a sequence of separate demands but one integrated act. Worship is what the soul owes; mindfulness is the atmosphere in which it is performed; obedience is the shape it takes. This is why the sūrah can spend only three words on the demand and twenty-five on the response: the demand is simple; the difficulty is entirely in what people do about it.

**"Worship God": the Root of Servitude and Its Quiet Revolution**

The verb *ʿabada* comes from a root whose basic field is humility, submissiveness, and that which is trodden down; Arab lexicographers note that a smooth road much travelled is called *muʿabbad*, "made into a path by feet." The word therefore carries an image rather than an abstraction: worship is the soul's being worn smooth in the direction it goes. Whatever a person repeatedly humbles himself before, that is his god — which is why the Qur'an can describe a person's taking his own desire as a deity (25:43; 45:23) without any statue being involved. The command *ʿbudū Allāha* is thus not primarily a demand for ceremonies; it is a demand that the ultimate wear-and-tear of a human life be spent on the One who alone can bear it.

The sūrah's own account of why this comes first is supplied elsewhere: *"I did not create jinn and humans except that they should worship Me"* (51:56) — worship being, on the classical reading, not a benefit accruing to God but the *purpose* of the created thing, as a blade's purpose is cutting. And the formula of the call is repeated verbatim in the other Nūḥ passages, always with the same first item:

> *"O my people! Worship God! You have no god other than Him."* (Qur'an 23:23; cf. 7:59, 11:26, 26:106)

That the second half is a negation as well as an affirmation — "no god other than Him" — matters: *lā ilāha illā Allāh* is a demolition before it is a construction, and Nūḥ's people would demonstrate, in verse 23, exactly what has to be demolished first: not error in general but specific inherited loyalties.

**"And Be Mindful of Him": Taqwā as the Shield**

*Ittaqūhu* is from *wiqāya*, a shield — the thing placed between you and the blow. The morphology is instructive: *ittafaʿala* from *w-q-y* originally means "to take to oneself a shield," and the Qur'an elsewhere commands literally *"guard yourselves from a Fire"* (2:24; 66:6). So taqwā is not, as it is often translated, a vague "fearing God"; it is *protective behaviour arising from the conviction that consequences are real*. The classical metaphor is precisely this-worldly: ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb asked Ubayy ibn Kaʿb about taqwā and Ubayy replied with a question — did he ever walk a path thorny with undergrowth? Yes. What did you do? — "I tucked up my garment and was careful." That, said Ubayy, is taqwā. The report is preserved in the commentary literature (al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Abī al-Dunyā, among others), and its image is exact: a person walking through danger does not stop walking; he walks *differently*, gathering up whatever would drag and expose him.

Applied to the verse's order, taqwā comes second for a reason: it is the *operating system* of the worship. A worshipper without vigilance performs acts; a worshipper with vigilance performs acts while watching for the trap of showing-off, of slackness, of the small compromise that no one else sees. The Qur'an makes the shield cosmic in scope and personal in scale at once:

> *"O you who believe! Guard yourselves and your households from a Fire whose fuel is men and stones."* (Qur'an 66:6)

**"And Obey Me": the Necessity of a Particular, Followable Example**

The third command is the one that offends modern instincts, so it is worth stating the logic carefully. Nūḥ does not say "and be sincere" or "and think rightly"; he says *obey me*. Abstraction cannot be obeyed. "Worship God" leaves open: how, when, with what words, at what cost, in what priority among competing claims? A command with no specification is a command with no content, and the history of religions — as the Qur'an itself presents it (2:13–20; 4:153; 5:101–102) — is largely the history of communities inventing specifications they then called divine. The messenger's function, on the Qur'anic model, is to be the *living specification*: the point at which an unseeable God becomes an imitable conduct. Hence the tight equation the Qur'an draws:

> *"Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed God; and whoever turns away — We have not sent you as a guardian over them."* (Qur'an 4:80)

And hence the Prophet ﷺ's own restatement of the same three-term logic, in a ḥadīth whose structure mirrors this verse almost syllable by syllable:

> *"All of my community will enter Paradise except the one who refuses." They asked: O Messenger of God, who refuses? He said: "Whoever obeys me enters Paradise, and whoever disobeys me has refused."* (al-Bukhārī 7280, from Abū Hurayrah)

Two qualifications preserved by the commentators keep the command honest. First, obedience to a messenger is bounded by its own content: the Prophet ﷺ said *"there is no obedience to a created being in disobedience to the Creator"* (Aḥmad 1614; and cf. al-Bukhārī 7145: *"obedience is only in what is right"*), so the command *obey me* cannot be turned into a licence for any claimant. Second, the messenger's authority is inseparable from his transparency: Nūḥ has just called himself *mubīn*, and in 26:107–109 he adds "I ask you no reward" and "I do not withhold from you the trust of my Lord." A demand for obedience that comes bundled with financial extraction or secrecy is, by this sūrah's own standard, not a prophetic claim at all.

**Why the Order Feels Right to the Soul**

Consider the pedagogy. Ask a person first to *orient* — who is the ultimate authority of your life? Then ask him to *watch* — be careful, the road is thorny; consequences are real and near. Only then ask him to *do the specific thing*. Any other order fails. Start with the specific acts and you produce ritualism or rebellion; start with the emotion and you produce enthusiasm that evaporates; start with the demand for obedience and you produce either coercion or suspicion. The sūrah starts with the *why*, moves to the *attitude*, and ends with the *what* — and then, in verse 4, supplies the *for-what-it's-worth*: forgiveness and time. That four-part structure — purpose, vigilance, practice, incentive — is how any durable human change is actually engineered: in schools, in recovery programmes, in teams, in families.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:4

> **that He may forgive you some of your sins and grant you reprieve until a term appointed. Truly, when the term of God comes, it will not be delayed, if you but knew."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Prizes: Pardoned Debt and Bought Time**

The consequence of verse 3's three commands is stated in a jussive clause of purpose and result: *yaghfir lakum… wa-yuʾakhkhirkum* — "He will forgive you… and He will delay you." Nūḥ offers his people exactly two goods, and the choice of those two is the whole theology of the verse. Not rain, not victory, not the humiliation of their rivals — but the removal of what they owe, and the extension of what they have. In Arabic the verb of forgiveness is *ghafara*, literally "to cover," as a helmet (*mighfar*) covers the head: the sin is not denied, it is buried from sight and from consequence. The verb of delay is *aʾakhkhara*, from *akhara*, "to put back," the same root as *ākhirah*, "the after," and as *istiʾkhār*, "asking God to put a matter in the best position in time." The verse thus promises a covered record and a lengthened runway — the two things a person in trouble can actually use.

This is also a subtle argument in itself. Nūḥ's people were, in the Qur'an's telling, wealthy and fertile (71:21), and they believed their wealth and children *were* their advantage. The prophet's offer re-prices their assets: your property cannot cover a single sin, and your lineage cannot add a day to your term — but God can do both, for free, on the condition you have already refused. The most valuable goods in the world are those the world cannot manufacture or sell.

**"Some of Your Sins" — a Partitive, or a Description?**

The phrase *min dhunūbikum* — "some of your sins" — is one of the most carefully argued locutions in the whole Qur'an, because a single preposition decides a doctrine. The obvious reading is partitive: *baʿḍ*, a portion, not the whole. If so, the verse appears to say that repentance clears the minor sins while the major ones remain on the ledger. On that basis the Muʿtazila pressed the verse into service, and their insistence on a distinction between minor and major sins — with the un-repented major sin barring Paradise — stands among the sharpest chapters in the classical commentaries. Al-Zamakhsharī, himself close to that school, notes the reading and adds a second; al-Rāzī transmits both at length; Ibn Kathīr reports from Ibn ʿAbbās and others that *min* here is not partitive at all but explanatory (*li-l-bayān*), specifying the *kind* of thing removed rather than a quantity of it — the same use of *min* in *"avoid the pollutions of idolatry"* (5:90), where the idols are not a subset of filth but are themselves the filth, and in *"so shun the rijs of Satan"* (5:90). On this reading "some of your sins" means "your sins, which are indeed sins" — i.e., *all* of them.

The verse itself cannot settle the dispute, but the rest of the Qur'an can and does state the general rule: *"Truly God does not forgive that anything be associated with Him, and He forgives whatever is below that for whom He wills"* (4:48; 4:116) — forgiveness of all sins short of persistent, un-repented shirk is within divine competence and, in the sūrah's own terms, was on offer. Ibn Taymiyyah's treatment of the verse follows this line: the *min* is descriptive, the offer is total, and the real limit is not the size of the sin but the refusal to want it gone — precisely the condition verse 7 will describe in Nūḥ's people.

The prophetic tradition supplies the same principle in a formula the biographers apply to conversion itself:

> *"ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ reported that the Messenger of God ﷺ said: Do you not know that Islam demolishes what came before it, that migration demolishes what came before it, and that ḥajj demolishes what came before it?"* (Muslim 121)

The image is *yajubbu*, "cuts away, invalidates" — as a debt is cancelled rather than paid down in instalments. Whatever one decides about the grammar of *min*, the sūrah's moral is stable: nothing is too heavy to be set down, and the only unforgivable condition is the one described in the next verse — putting a finger in the ear.

**"A Term Appointed" — the Anatomy of a Deadline**

*Ajalin musamman*: an appointed, specified, numbered term. The participle *musamman* is from *samana*, "to name, to fix by name," and the phrase occurs identically at 6:2, 7:34, 10:49, 14:10, 16:61, 23:43, 35:45, 39:5 — a formula so recurrent that al-Ṭabarī treats it as a fixed term of the revelation, meaning a limit recorded with God and never negotiable. The commentators enumerate what the term may denote in this verse: the death of each individual; the moment of the punishing flood for that society; the end appointed for the world; or the Day of Judgment. The source commentary lists exactly these four, and the plurality is not indecision but design. Nūḥ is telling his people that *some* deadline is fixed, whichever of these it is, and that their present security is compatible with any of them. The genius of the warning is that it refuses to specify the date; every human being therefore has to live as though all four were possible on the same afternoon.

The closing clause makes the point unforgivingly: *"Truly, when the term of God comes, it will not be delayed, if you but knew."* The formula recurs for whole civilisations:

> *"For every community there is a term; so when their term comes, they will not delay it an hour, nor will they advance it."* (Qur'an 7:34; cf. 10:49; 16:61; 34:30; 39:42)

And the Qur'an addresses directly the psychology of a person who feels comfortably permanent:

> *"Rather, the Hour is their appointed time — and the Hour is more devastating and more bitter."* (Qur'an 54:46)

**Respite Is Not Acquittal: The Doctrine of the Open Window**

What, then, does the offer of "delay" mean, since everyone dies in any case? The commentators' answer, and the only coherent one, is that *respite is opportunity, not outcome*. Time is not being sold to them as an escape from accountability but as the space in which accountability can be pre-empted. The Qur'an insists on the converse also — that unheeded time is not neutral but *evidence against* the holder, and that a smoothly continuing life after repeated warnings is itself a form of being abandoned:

> *"So when they forgot what they were reminded of, We opened for them the gates of all things, until — in the very moment they rejoiced in what they were given — We seized them suddenly, and there they were in despair."* (Qur'an 6:44)

That verse is the classical locus for *istidrāj*, "gradual drawing-out," and the commentators read 71:4's "delay" in its light: Nūḥ promised a delay that would be salvation if they repented, and their people received a delay that became prosecution because they did not. Same word, opposite effect, and the difference was made entirely on their side of the transaction. This is why the sūrah can be preached to a complacent Makkah with one sentence: you are not being ignored; you are being given time.

**The Human Relationship with "Later"**

Nothing is more modern than this verse's diagnosis. Procrastination is not a failure of information but a failure of *imagination about time*: people discount a distant consequence to near-zero and overvalue a present comfort, which is why the smoker, the defaulter on maintenance, the estranged child who will call "next month," and the government that defers the report are all behaving with perfect rationality inside a faulty model of the future. Behavioural research calls it hyperbolic discounting; Nūḥ calls it *law kunta taʿlamūn* — "if you but knew," an expression the Qur'an uses when the failure is not that information is missing but that it is not *registered* (cf. 102:3–5; 105? no — cf. 7:179 *"they have hearts with which they do not understand"*).

Two practical disciplines follow directly from the verse, and both were made part of Islamic ethics. First, the *shortening of the horizon*: ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb's famous report of being told that a man had died in a good state and his reply that he could not certify it until he knew how his last acts went — and the Prophet's own general counsel:

> *"Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveller passing through."* (al-Bukhārī 6416, from Ibn ʿUmar, who added: "Prepare yourself as though you were dying now.")

Second, the *refusal to despair the size of one's own debt*: since the offered pardon is not measured in instalments, the correct posture of the sinner is to ask for everything, not to sort the ledger into "these I will fix now, those perhaps later." The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said:

> *"God said: O son of Adam, so long as you call upon Me and hope in Me, I shall forgive you for what you did, and I shall not mind. O son of Adam, were your sins to reach the clouds of the sky and were you then to ask forgiveness of Me, I would forgive you."* (al-Tirmidhī 3540, from Anas ibn Mālik; graded ḥasan-ṣaḥīḥ by some, its chain debated by others — the sense is corroborated by 39:53)

Sūrah Nūḥ puts that same mercy on the lips of the first of the resolute messengers, then lets the next two verses show what a people does when such an offer is made to them.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:5

> **He said, "My Lord, I have called my people day and night."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Sermon to Supplication: Where a Preacher Takes His Grief**

The sūrah pivots. Verses 2–4 were Nūḥ speaking to his people; verse 5 is Nūḥ speaking to God about them, and the shift of addressee is the hinge of the entire chapter. *Qāla rabbi* — "He said: My Lord." Three times in the last verses of the sūrah (5, 21, 26, 28) he will open with *rabbi*, and the repetition is not filler: the prophet who cannot reach the people does not stop, does not sulk, and does not retire — he *reports*. The Qur'an depicts this in the mouths of other messengers too, and in the same syntax: Yāqūb's *"I only complain of my suffering and my grief to God"* (12:86), and Shuʿayb's *"my success is not but through God"* (11:88). The reason this is a discipline rather than a vent is that a grievance poured out on people hardens them, while the same grievance taken to God purifies the caller: he must confess what he actually did, and he must ask for what only God can supply. Nūḥ's line here is the plainest ledger imaginable — "I called, and they kept fleeing" — no exaggeration, no self-pity, no accusation invented, no numbers padded.

The verb is worth attending to. *Daʿawtu* — I called, I invited — not *an dhartu* (I warned) or *balagh-tu* (I conveyed). To *daʿā ilā* is to summon someone *toward* something, and its usage in Arabic includes calling a guest to a meal and calling a horse by name. After nine centuries the most accurate description Nūḥ can give of his own work is that he kept inviting. The mission is never described in this sūrah as a campaign, a conquest, or an argument won.

**"Night and Day" — a Merism for Total Coverage**

*Bil-ʿishāʾi wa-l-nahār* — literally "in the evenings and in the days." The pairing of opposites is a merism, a figure in which two extremes assert the whole: as "the seen and the unseen," "the open and the secret," "the near and the far" mean everything in between and beside. Arabic idiom uses *al-ʿishāʾ wa-l-nahār* for "continually, without respite," and the commentators gloss it accordingly: al-Ṭabarī takes it as *dāʾiman* — without interruption; and the source commentary notes al-Qurṭubī's further shading, that "night" alludes to calling privately and "day" to calling publicly — a reading the sūrah itself will make explicit in verse 9, so the double sense is likely intended.

What that coverage bought him, in worldly terms, is next to nothing, and verse 6 will say so. The Qur'an preserves, in another sūrah, the arithmetic of the same career:

> *"And he remained among them a thousand years minus fifty."* (Qur'an 29:14)

Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and the early historians all wrestled with that figure, some treating it as the length of his mission, others as the span of his life, and the transmitted narratives about the growth of his children and grandchildren over generations multiply the difficulty. What none of them doubted is the point the number was sent to make: a duration long enough that no one could accuse Nūḥ of losing patience too soon. The commentators therefore read verse 5 as Nūḥ's *evidence* rather than his complaint — he is testifying, in court before God, that the obligation was discharged exhaustively. The same logic explains why the Qur'an repeatedly insists that messengers "made the unseen manifest" and that God does not punish *"while your Lord is not a wrongdoer"* (18:49; cf. 28:59; 35:45).

**Steadfastness as the Defining Trait**

It is transmitted among the commentators that Nūḥ was singled out for endurance among the messengers — the source commentary records the claim that he was *"the most steadfast of God's messengers,"* beginning his call at forty and continuing for nine and a half centuries with few followers. The sūrah's placement of him in the company of *ulū al-ʿazm* (46:35) is the scriptural ground for that judgement, and the Prophet's own words in the intercession-ḥadīth give him the primacy of office: *"the first messenger God sent to the people of the earth"* (al-Bukhārī 4712; Muslim 194).

The tradition supplies the cost of such constancy in a general maxim, spoken of the prophets and their successors:

> *"The people most severely tested are the prophets, then the next best and the next best. A person is tried according to the strength of his religion."* (al-Tirmidhī 2398, from Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ; graded ḥasan-ṣaḥīḥ)

And the Prophet ﷺ tied endurance-with-people, rather than withdrawal from them, to rank:

> *"The believer who mixes with the people and bears their harm is greater in reward than the believer who does not mix with them and does not bear their harm."* (al-Tirmidhī 2507, Ibn Mājah 4032; its chain is debated, its meaning is exactly that of this verse)

Nūḥ, with nine centuries of harm to bear, is the paradigm case of that ḥadīth — and his choice, when it came to exhaustion, was not to abandon the people but to take the matter upstairs.

**Reporting Rather Than Resigning: A Model for Unrewarded Labour**

Modern psychology has a name for what threatens anyone in Nūḥ's position: burnout, produced when effort is sustained while outcome is absent, and aggravated when the worker begins to read the absence of results as evidence of his own inadequacy. What the verse models is a precise antidote in three parts. First, **describe the action, not the result** — "I called," a thing entirely within his control. Second, **transfer the verdict to the only competent judge** — *rabbi*: he does not ask God to vindicate him before the people; he asks to be witnessed. Third, **continue the work in the same form** — verses 8–9 show that after this report he escalates his methods rather than abandoning his mission.

This is why the sūrah has been read for centuries as a charter for teachers, preachers, parents, and anyone whose labour matures slower than their life. The most quoted counsel the Prophet ﷺ gave on delayed harvest is this:

> *"If the Hour begins while a sapling is in the hand of any of you, and he is able to plant it before he stands, let him plant it."* (Aḥmad, from Anas ibn Mālik; graded ḥasan by some of the examiners)

Planting a sapling at the end of the world is, of course, agronomically useless and, of course, exactly the point: the act is owed whether or not the fruit is enjoyed. Nūḥ's *rabbi, innī daʿawtu qawmī* is that ḥadīth spoken a millennium before Muḥammad ﷺ — a man who kept planting on ground he knew would be flooded, because the planting was his obedience, not his argument with God.

**The Two Things the Verse Does Not Say**

Finally, mark what is absent from this line. Nūḥ does not say "and so do something about them." He does not say "I have done enough; release me from them." He states facts and leaves the decision where it belongs — a poise the Qur'an teaches explicitly to the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"So remind them, for you are only one who reminds. You are not a controller over them. But whoever turns away and disbelieves — God will punish him with the greatest punishment. Indeed, to Us is their return, then indeed upon Us is their reckoning."* (Qur'an 88:21–26)

The consistency between 71:5 and 88:21–26 is the sūrah's whole ethic of persuasion: total effort in the calling, total resignation of the outcome, total trust that nothing has been lost because nothing was recorded in the first place by the caller. The next verse supplies the answer to the question Nūḥ does not dare ask — what did my nine hundred and fifty years achieve? — and it is the saddest sentence in the chapter.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:6

> **Yet my calling has not increased them in anything except flight.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Measures a Millennium in One Negative**

Nine hundred and fifty years, compressed into a single sentence of six words: *fa-lam yazidhum duʿāʾī illā firārā*. The grammar is merciless in its precision. *Zāda* — "to increase" — takes two accusatives in Arabic: the thing increased in, and the amount of the increase. Here the people are the ones increased, and what they are increased in is *firār*, flight. The verb is negated and the sole exception is named with *illā*: there was no other growth of any kind. Not a softening, not a curiosity, not a single household of secret sympathisers reported here — the only measurable change in nine and a half centuries was an acceleration of avoidance. The Qur'an will report the same outcome from the same mouth in another sūrah, with the same verb and the same exception:

> *"…though your call only drives them further away."* (Qur'an 11:8)

The lesson the commentators draw is not that the mission was a failure but that *increase* was real — a genuine, observable, monotonically increasing effect, just not the desired one. Nūḥ does not report "nothing happened." Something did happen, and it is on the record.

**Flight Is a Response, Not Indifference**

This is the verse's most quietly logical move, and it matters for anyone who has ever been refused. One does not flee from a thing one believes to be nothing. *Firār* is an admission: the addressed recognised in the call something with gravitational pull, enough of a claim on them that distance had to be *taken*. The Qur'an states the same posture in a single simile it applies to the Makkan hearers of the Prophet ﷺ, where flight from a reminder is pictured as the panic of a hunted animal:

> *"What is the matter with those who disbelieve, how they rush toward you from the right side and from the left, in scattered groups — does any one of him of them desire that he be admitted to a Garden of bliss? Never!"* (Qur'an 70:36–38)

And of the reminder itself:

> *"What is the matter with them, that they turn away from the reminder, as though they were wild donkeys fleeing from a lion?"* (Qur'an 74:49–51)

Most piercingly, the Qur'an's own command about what one *should* be fleeing toward:

> *"…So flee to God. Truly I am to you from Him a clear warner."* (Qur'an 51:50)

That pair of verbs is the whole sūrah's theme in two words. *Firarū ilā Allāh* — flee *to* God — and Nūḥ's people chose *firār* *from* the flee-to-God. The Qur'an has placed the same root, in the same register of prophetic self-description ("a clear warner," 51:50), on either side of a single choice. Their flight was not the absence of a response but the wrong direction of a very real one; a man running away from an emergency has, by the very act of running, conceded the emergency.

**Habituation, Reactance, and the Physics of Refusal**

The psychology of this verse is precise enough to be worth naming in modern terms. Two well-documented effects are at work. The first is *habituation*: a repeated stimulus loses its power to startle, which is why the tenth alarm is ignored more calmly than the first — and this is why, in the sūrah, Nūḥ's repetition (vv. 5, 8, 9) does not intensify their response but dulls it. The second is *reactance*: when a freedom is felt to be threatened, people reassert it by choosing the opposite, sometimes regardless of the merits. A warning delivered without respecting the hearer's dignity generates resistance to the messenger first and to the message second; and the combination of the two explains the paradox Nūḥ states — more call, less listening.

The Qur'an knows both mechanisms and legislates around them. On habituation: it varies its own address, as the sūrah itself will show (open, private, argument, promise, threat), and it says plainly that its parables have opposite effects on opposite hearts — *"and God increases the wrongdoers through it only in loss"* (24:34; cf. 17:41, quoted by the source commentary: *"We have indeed varied Our signs in this Qur'an that they might reflect, though it increased them in nothing but aversion"*). On reactance, it forbids coercion in the very act of calling: *"There is no compulsion in religion"* (2:256), and it commands the gentleness that prevents the hearer from defending his pride:

> *"Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you."* (Qur'an 3:159)

The mechanism is not merely rebuked but explained: hardness of the caller produces flight from the call.

**The Parable of the Rain: Same Water, Two Outcomes**

The Prophet ﷺ supplied the classical image for a message whose reception is entirely a property of the soil, not of the rain. He said:

Al-Bukhārī and Muslim transmit from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī:

> *"The example of what God has sent me with — guidance and knowledge — is like abundant rain that strikes the earth. A tract of it was barren ground that absorbed the water, and God brought forth from it abundant pasture and green growth; another tract held the water upon it, and God benefited the people thereby, so that they drank from it, irrigated with it, and sowed; and another tract was level and hard, holding no water and growing no greenery. That first is the example of the one who understands the religion of God and benefits from what God sent me with; the second, of the one whom God has made a means of guidance for the people; and the last, of the one who turns his back on it and does not accept God's guidance."* (al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-ʿIlm; Muslim 2282)

Nūḥ's people are, in that parable, the hard level ground upon which the rain of nine and a half centuries fell and from which neither crop nor stored water was ever drawn. This is why the verse is spoken in a court and not in a diary. The record "I called, and they only fled" belongs to the section of the sūrah that Nūḥ addresses to God: it establishes *li-alla yakūna li-l-nāsi ʿalā Allāhi al-ḥujjata* — "so that mankind shall have no argument against God after the messengers" (4:165). Flight is not merely sad; it is *evidence*, and it will be cited.

**What a Caller Is and Is Not Allowed to Conclude**

Two applications of this verse deserve to be separated carefully, because both errors are common.

The first error is to conclude that a message with no results is false. The sūrah's entire structure forbids it: the most successful prophet in terms of longevity and obedience is precisely the one with no apparent results, and the Qur'an honours him with the title "grateful servant" (17:3) while it says of the Prophet ﷺ, *"perhaps you will kill yourself with grief that they do not believe"* (26:3). Success is defined in revelation as fidelity plus patience, not as attendance figures.

The second error is the opposite: to conclude that no results can only mean the audience is corrupt, and to keep repeating the identical act forever. Nūḥ does neither. He keeps the message and changes the method (vv. 8–9), which is the practical definition of wisdom in daʿwa — *"Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and beautiful admonition, and argue with them in a way that is best"* (16:125). The verse thus functions as a diagnostic: when a call produces only flight, the caller is obliged to ask two separate questions — is the content being delivered *clearly*, and is the manner *preserving the hearer's dignity*? — while never being permitted to ask the third question, whether the truth may be adjusted to be more acceptable.

**The Last Clause of Patience**

Finally, a note on tone. Nūḥ does not say, "my call made them run because they are evil." He says, "my call did not increase them except in flight" — a statement about what *his* action accomplished, leaving their motive to their own account of it. The contrast with the sūrah's later verses is instructive: only when the process reaches its end does he name the sociology of the refusal (v. 21: *"they obeyed me not, and followed him whose wealth and children increase him in loss"*). Patience, on this model, does not mean suppressing the diagnosis; it means delivering it in the right court, at the right time, and to the right Judge.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:7

> **And whenever I called them, so that You might forgive them, they put their fingers in their ears, covered themselves with their garments, persisted, and were immensely arrogant.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Verbs Describing a Single Act of Self-Defence**

The verse is built as a sequence of four past-tense verbs, and the order is not arbitrary: *jaʿalū aṣābiʿahum fī ādhānihim* (they put their fingers into their ears); *wa-staghshaw thiyābahum* (they wrapped themselves in their garments); *wa-ṣarrū* (they stuck fast); *wa-stakbarū istikbārā* (and they claimed greatness). Two outward blocks, then two inward states — ear, eye, will, ego. The movement runs from the body to the heart, exactly the reverse of how a person is reached: revelation enters through hearing, is confirmed by seeing, is consented to by the will, and is finally humbled in the self. Nūḥ's people shut the four doors in the reverse order of entry, beginning at the outside.

Linguistically, *ādhān* is plural of *udhun*, ear, and the idiom of plugging it is literal — but note what the Qur'an elsewhere records as the *words* spoken with the same gesture, in the same generation's voice:

> *"Our hearts are in coverings from what you invite us to, and in our ears is a heaviness (waqar); and between us and you is a barrier, so call or be called upon."* (Qur'an 41:5)

That *waqar* is a technical term for a plug of deafness, and it belongs to the same root *w-q-r* which yields *waqār*, "weight, dignity, majesty" — the very word used two verses later, at 71:13, to reproach them: *lā tarjūna li-llāhi waqārā*. The verbal architecture is thus exquisitely tight: a people who refused to give God His *waqār* were given *waqr* — a dead weight in the ear that no volume could penetrate.

**"Covered Themselves with Their Garments": Closing the Eye as Well as the Ear**

*Istaghshā al-thiyāb* means to draw the garment over the head and face so as not to see or be seen — the verb is the tenth form of *ghashā*, "to overcast, to envelop," used in the Qur'an for the sea covering Pharaoh's army (69:14), for sleep (25:47), and for the veils over the hearts of those who refuse (2:7, 6:25, 17:46). The image is of a man who, unable to stop being called, stops seeing the caller: he becomes his own blind. The pairing of deafness and blindness in a single gesture is a fixed Qur'anic diagnosis of refusal, most explicitly in this verse:

> *"Have they not travelled in the land so that they should have hearts by which to reason, or ears by which to hear? For truly it is not the eyes that are blinded, but the hearts within the breasts."* (Qur'an 22:46; cf. 45:23: *"…God has sealed over their hearts and their hearing, and over their sight is a covering"*)

The source commentary already notes that hearing, sight, and heart are the three faculties open or closed to revelation (see 2:7; 6:25, 46; 7:100, 179; 16:108; 17:46; 18:57; 22:46; 41:5; 46:26). What this verse adds to that schema is *agency*: the people do not report that God sealed their ears; they are reported to have plugged them with their own fingers. The sealing comes later, as a consequence, and the sūrah's ordering preserves the whole doctrine — human choice first, divine confirmation second — which is precisely the point Ibn Taymiyyah laboured in his refutation of both the fatalists (*jaabriyya*) and the self-empowered (*qadariyya*): God's "seal" is the ratification of a door the servant has already refused to open, not the theft of a door he was reaching for.

**"Persisted": The Word for a Clenched Fist**

*Ṣarrū* — from *ṣ-r-r*, to fasten, to be tight, to clamp: the same root gives *ṣirr*, "that which is hidden and binding" (an oath, a grudge, a secret), and *al-ṣirr*, "the persistent refusal." A thing that *ṣarra* does not merely stay; it *grips*. The commentators render it *aṣarrū ʿalā kufrihim* — "they clung to their disbelief," and the tenth-form verbs around it (*istaghshaw*, *istakbarū*) place all three actions in the register of deliberate effort: each of them is something they *worked at*, not something that happened to them. This is the verse's quiet accusation: indifference would have been easier, cheaper, and less telling. What Nūḥ describes is a people *labouring* not to hear — a considerable expenditure of energy for the sake of an unchanged position.

Then *wa-stakbarū istikbārā*: the *iftiʿāl* form of *k-b-r*, "to consider oneself great," with the cognate accusative thrown in for intensity. Arabic grammar calls this *mufāʿala* in a *tafaʿʿul*-like sense — the claim, not the fact: they did not *have* greatness, they *asserted* it against the one who offered them forgiveness. The Qur'an reserves the word for a single archetype:

> *"And when We said to the angels, 'Prostrate before Adam,' they prostrated, except Iblīs: he refused and was arrogant (wa-stakbara), and was among the disbelievers."* (Qur'an 2:34)

The people of Nūḥ, in one clause, are aligned with the first refusal in creation. That is why the sūrah's charge-sheet is so short: not that they lacked evidence, but that they were engaged in an act of self-promotion which evidence would have cost them.

**"So That You Might Forgive Them" — the Cruelty Measured by the Gift**

The most painful phrase in the verse is the parenthetical: *li-taghhira lahum*, "so that You would forgive them." The purpose of the call was not to defeat them, not to shame them, not to collect converts; it was a window into forgiveness, the removal of what they most needed removed. Ibn Kathīr glosses: "I did not call them to my own status or to gain from them, but so that You might forgive them" — and the line is a commentary on v. 2's *mubīn*: Nūḥ's motives were transparent, which is why the refusal is unreasoned rather than suspicious.

Read that with the whole shape of this sūrah: the offer of forgiveness is repeated in v. 4, re-offered in v. 10 as an imperative ("*Say: seek your Lord's forgiveness; He is ever Forgiving*"), and then, only after nine centuries of plugged ears, the tone changes. The sequence teaches that the *harshness* of prophetic speech is always a *response*, never an initiative — and it is worth contrasting with the Prophet ﷺ, who was shown the same scene and answered differently, at Ṭāʾif:

> *"Shall I tell you of a day more severe for the Messenger of God ﷺ than the Day of Uhud? … He said: 'O God, to You I complain of my weakness of means and my abasement before people… If You are not angry with me, I do not care — but Your relief is vaster than that. I take refuge in the light of Your Face, by which all darkness is illumined and the affair of this world and the next is set right, lest Your anger or Your displeasure descend upon me. Yours is the complaint until You are pleased.' "* (Transmitted from ʿĀʾisha by Aḥmad and others, and quoted by the biographers and the exegetes for the Day of Ṭāʾif; its chain is debated, and it is cited here for its sense rather than as an authenticated text.)

Whatever the grading of that supplication, the Qur'an states the principle in its own voice — *"Say: What would my Lord make of you were it not for your supplication to Him?"* (25:77) — and Nūḥ's complaint in verse 7 is a supplication of exactly that kind: grief turned upward, not outward.

**The Finger in the Ear, Today**

The image is not quaint; it is a description of ordinary modern behaviour, and the more honestly it is read the more of life it explains. Blocking, muting, unfollowing, leaving the room, changing the channel, refusing to read the letter, wrapping oneself in the garments of a familiar environment where the same question is never asked — these are *aṣābiʿ* in *ādhān*, and they are chosen, like everything else in the verse. Contemporary research on motivated reasoning and on "identity-protective cognition" documents a phenomenon the sūrah assumes: when a true proposition threatens a person's standing within their group, the mind does not weigh it; it *filters* it, and the more evidence arrives, the more energetic the filtering. This is why verse 6's "flight" and verse 7's "fingers" belong together, and why Nūḥ's escalation in verses 8–9 is the only rational reply: when a person will not hear, more volume does not help; a different door must be tried.

The verse also sets a standard for how the believer conducts argument. If the anatomy of refusal is ear-plugging and garment-pulling, then the correct response is not to break down the door but to remove every pretext: to keep the offer of forgiveness central, to keep the motive transparent, and to keep the methods varied and gentle. The people of Nūḥ were not defeated by a better speaker; they were defeated by their own hands, and the sūrah records which hands — theirs.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:8

> **Then I called them openly.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Thumma": The Conjunction That Tells Time**

The verse begins with *thumma*, not with *wa*: "and then." Arabic grammar distinguishes the two: *wa* joins without implying a gap, while *thumma* implies interval, and often a long one or a rank beyond what preceded. The Qur'an uses it constantly of resurrection ("*then* you will be raised"), of stages of creation ("*then* He formed him," 32:9), and here of a career. Nūḥ's *thumma innī daʿawtu lahum ʿilānā* therefore reads as a paragraph, not a sentence: after the private, persistent, unresponded-to calling of verses 5–7, an era elapsed, and then he changed the register. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both note this progression in the sense of *mubālagha* — he escalated his efforts, exhausting each avenue before opening the next — and they cite as the paradigm the very method God commands: *"Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and beautiful admonition"* (16:125), the two named modes corresponding exactly to the private and public forms this sūrah describes.

**"ʿIlānan": the Public as a Moral Category**

The noun *ʿilān* is from *ʿ-l-n*, to raise, to make conspicuous — the same root gives *iʿlām* (notification), *iʿlān* (a public announcement; in modern Arabic, an advertisement), and *al-ʿalāniya*, "openness," the Qur'an's word for the opposite of concealment (2:271, 13:10, 76:9). Revelation's default posture is openness: the Qur'an rejects the notion of an esoteric teaching reserved for initiates in the strongest possible terms, while declaring that openness changes nothing about who is addressed: *"Say: It is He who has power to send upon you the punishment from above you or from beneath your feet, or to confuse you in sects and make some of you taste the violence of others* — then He says: *the Knower of the unseen and the witnessed, the Great, the Most High — equal among you is he who conceals the saying and he who declares it openly, he who hides by night and he who strides by day."* (13:8–10, abridged). A divine message that could only be delivered in whispers would be, in the Qur'anic view, no message at all — and Nūḥ's refusal to run a secret society is a point about God, not about publicity technique.

The verse thus also functions as evidence. In a legal sense, a public call creates what modern decision-theory calls *common knowledge*: not merely "everyone knows X," but "everyone knows that everyone knows X," which changes what people can safely do and say. Nūḥ's private call reached individuals; his public call restructured the space in which the whole people had to position themselves — with the cost being, as 11:27 records, exposure to open mockery: *"The notable ones of his people said: We see you as nothing but a human being like ourselves, and we see that none follows you except those who are the meanest of us, thinking at first sight; and we see no merit in you over us — rather, we think you liars."* And in 7:66 their verdict is even blunter: *"We see you in manifest error."* He chose that openly, knowing what openness would draw. A caller who wants only the comfort of the sympathetic room never pays it.

**The Model of the Prophet ﷺ: Three Years in Secret, Then al-Ṣafā**

The source commentary notes the parallel that the earliest biographies preserve: the Prophet ﷺ preached privately for a substantial period before the public command came down, and the sequence of the sūrah's verses — private, then open, then both — is held by al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī to mirror the prophetic pattern. The moment of transition is among the best-attested scenes in the biography. When the verse *"And warn your closest kindred"* (26:214) came down, he climbed the hill of al-Ṣafā, raised the call to the clans by name, and — as al-Bukhārī records (4770, from Ibn ʿAbbās) — gathered Quraysh tribe by tribe, then concluded with his own household:

> *"O community of Quraysh! … O Fāṭimah bint Muḥammad, O Ṣafiyya aunt of the Messenger of God, O sons of ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib — I possess nothing for you from God; ask of me whatever wealth you wish."* (al-Bukhārī 4770)

Three things in that report belong to Nūḥ's verse. The *openness*: not a whispered circle but an announced assembly on a public hill. The *order of priority*: kin and household first, because the private call precedes and prepares the public one. And the *stripping of privilege*: the public declaration includes the announcement that no blood-tie buys exemption — the very point Nūḥ's sūrah will drive home in verse 28, where the only saving qualification is entry into the house *as a believer*.

**Why Public Speech Is Also a Kind of Justice**

One should not romanticise the open call. Public preaching humiliates; it forces people to take sides in front of their peers; it converts belief into social identity. But Nūḥ's *ʿilān* here is presented as an act of *fairness*, in four ways. First, it prevents a double standard: the message is not given in corners so that some are persuaded and others excluded. Second, it protects the caller: a man who preaches only in private can be accused of conspiracy, and history's heresy-hunters have always exploited that vulnerability — an open sermon leaves no such handle. Third, it removes the excuse of ignorance from those too busy or too shy to seek out the private circle. Fourth, it makes the message a *public* property, transmissible and testable by anyone, which is why the Qur'an can demand of itself: *"Say: Bring forward your proof, if you are truthful"* (2:111) and *"Say: then produce a sūrah like it and call your witnesses besides God"* (10:38).

**The Balance That Verse 9 Will Restore**

Yet a public campaign alone is a blunt instrument. Crowds hear slogans; individuals hear questions addressed to them. And the powerful — the *malaʾ* of Nūḥ's people, named in verse 21 — rarely abandon a position in public, because public reversal costs standing, and standing is what their wealth and children bought them. So the very next verse will complete the method: *"Then I proclaimed to them and I confided to them privately."* Verse 8, standing alone, is the announcement; verse 9 is the persuasion. Held together, the two verses constitute a full theory of change: establish the truth in the open square, then go and sit with each person where he actually lives, in the room where he does not have to defend his reputation. Every enduring reform movement in Muslim history has operated on that double rhythm — the sermon and the *majlis*, the *khuṭba* and the one-to-one counsel — and every one that forgot the second half produced crowds without converts.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:9

> **Then I proclaimed to them and confided to them in secret,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs of One Mission: Raising and Lowering the Voice**

The verse pairs two fourth-form verbs built on opposite roots. *Aʿlantu lahum* — "I made [the call] conspicuous for them" — from *ʿ-l-n*, to raise, hoist, make visible; *wa-asrartu lahum isrārā* — "and I secreted it to them with secreting" — from *s-r-r*, to hide, to inter, to confide. Between the pair stands the cognate accusative *isrārā*, a grammatical device (the *mafʿūl muṭlaq*) whose only function is intensification: he did not merely speak quietly to them, he *confided* with every degree of confiding. The couplet thus encloses the entire possible range of human address — from the assembled crowd to the one listener in a room — and claims that every point on that range was covered in nine centuries.

The source commentary transmits the classical ordering: al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī take the three verses as a sequence of escalation — private first, then open, then both together — the prophet "becoming ever more emphatic." Whether or not the order is strictly chronological (and *thumma* in verse 8 already told us that long intervals separate these phases, so the verses read as a summary rather than a diary), the point of the arrangement is completeness: no channel was left unused. The verse's grammar reinforces it: both verbs take the same preposition, *lahum*, "to/for them." Nothing was said *about* them behind their backs; every confidential conversation was confidential *to a person*, not *concerning* them.

**Why Secrecy Belongs to Sincere Counsel**

The modern instinct is to assume the reverse — that the open call is the honest one and private outreach is something to be inspected. The verse teaches otherwise, and the ethics behind it are precise. Public exposure of a person's error activates the very mechanism verse 7 described: pride. A chief who concedes in the assembly concedes his standing, and standing is what his wealth and children bought him; so he will dig in, and Nūḥ will have converted a question into a status contest. The private word removes the audience, and with the audience goes the necessity of performing defiance. The saying quoted widely in the Islamic literature of counsel — attributed in the adab collections to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and to earlier authorities — states the psychology in one line:

> *"Whoever admonishes his brother openly, he has disgraced him; whoever admonishes him secretly, he has adorned him."*

It is transmitted as a saying rather than as a Prophetic ḥadīth, but the Prophetic practice it generalises is documented. Ibn Masʿūd reported of the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ would vary his admonition for us across the days, for fear that we would grow bored."* (al-Bukhārī 69, Kitāb al-ʿIlm)

Note what is being corrected there: not the content, but the *dose and the delivery*, because a message that saturates becomes noise. And the same concern for gentleness as an effective means is fixed in the Prophet's ﷺ general maxim:

> *"Truly gentleness is not placed in anything except that it adorns it, and it is not withdrawn from anything except that it disgraces it."* (Muslim 2594, from ʿĀʾisha)

**The Private Call and the Architecture of a Movement**

There is also a sociological lesson in the pairing. Public proclamation produces *awareness*: everyone knows that the message exists, knows who has accepted it, and can no longer plead ignorance. Private proclamation produces *adherence*: individuals actually change. Movements that have only the first dissolve into controversy; movements that have only the second dissolve into a sect. Nūḥ's claim — "I did both, continuously, for a millennium" — is therefore a claim about the durability of his community's excuse and about the completeness of his own technique.

The early Muslim community institutionalised precisely this two-channel structure. There was the *khuṭbah*, the open sermon, which by design created common knowledge:

> *"O you who believe! when the call to prayer is made on the day of congregation, hasten to the remembrance of God and leave off trading."* (Qur'an 62:9)

and there was the *ḥalqa* and the *sirr*, the small circle and the personal follow-up — the way Abū Bakr brought men one by one, the way the Prophet ﷺ spoke to ʿUmar privately, and the way the *ṣuḥba* (companionship in a small group) was described by the early generations as the actual mechanism by which belief became character. Ibn ʿAbbās, young and eager, described how ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb would admit him to the company of the elders and explain to their surprise: "He has understanding of the Book of God" — the open assembly and the private mentoring in a single scene (al-Bukhārī, Book of Knowledge).

**What the Verse Says About the Refusers**

Notice the restraint of the report. Nūḥ does not say, "and some of them believed in secret." He says nothing at all about the effect of the private call — which, read against verse 6 ("my call increased them in nothing but flight"), is itself a statement: the concealment of the call's fruit is not the same as the concealment of the call's delivery. The people's stubbornness was not the product of insufficient intimacy or of an approach too abrupt; he exhausted both intimacy and publicity, and there was no "he only preaches in public, he never sits with us" available to them. The verse removes that excuse from the record.

**The Method, Made Universal**

The two channels are not two opinions about how to preach; they are the two ways in which any human being can be addressed at all. The open word establishes norms; the private word changes lives. Whoever wants to change a household, a workplace, a classroom, or a public opinion needs both, in this order of humility: state the truth plainly in front of whoever needs to hear it, so that no one can say it was hidden, and then sit with each person alone, so that no one has to lose face in order to gain. And whoever discovers that both have failed, as Nūḥ did, is left with the position of verses 10 onward — where the content of the message is at last named, and named in a single word: seek pardon.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:10

> **I said, "Seek your Lord's forgiveness! Truly He is ever Forgiving."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whole Message Reduced to One Imperative**

After four verses describing the *how* of nine centuries — day and night, open and secret — the sūrah finally discloses the *what*, and it is a single verb: *fa-qultu staghfirū rabbakum*. "I said: seek your Lord's forgiveness." No argument from cosmology yet, no threat, no demand for political surrender, no denunciation of the idols by name. Ibn Kathīr notes under this verse that Nūḥ began with *istighfār* because it is the entry point of everything: it simultaneously contains the admission that one is guilty, the conviction that pardon is possible, and the turning toward the One who pardons. Al-Rāzī develops the same point: the first word to a hard-hearted people cannot be "improve yourselves," because their difficulty is not a deficit of resolution but a conviction that they are not the sort of people who need pardon.

The imperative form, *istaghfirū*, is the tenth stem of *gh-f-r*, "to cover" — literally, "ask for a covering," from *ghifr*, a shield or wrapping, and *mighfār*, the helmet that covers the head in battle (the word is applied by Arab lexicographers to any covering that protects, and even to the cloud, *al-ghāfir*, which covers the sky and shelters with shade — a fitting image for this sūrah, in which forgiveness and rain are promised in two consecutive verses). And the object of the verb is not "God" but "*your* Lord" — *rabbakum* — *rabb* being the root of nurture, gradual growth, and ownership-with-care. The sentence thus says: ask the One who has been raising you, for the covering that will protect you.

**"He Is Ever Forgiving": A Name in the Intensive Pattern**

*Innahu kāna ghaffārā*. Two grammatical facts make this clause do most of the verse's work. *Ghaffār* is on the intensive pattern *faʿʿāl*, which in Arabic denotes habit, frequency, and inexhaustibility — not "one who has forgiven" but "one whose constant character is to forgive." The Qur'an's ordinary formula for the same quality, *ghafūran raḥīman*, "constantly forgiving, continually merciful," is repeated scores of times in the Book, and *ghaffār* — the stronger pattern — is used here, at 71:10, precisely where the sin is long-standing and the pardon is offered as a habit rather than a concession. Alongside that, the verb *kāna* in this construction — *inna Allāha kāna ghafūran raḥīmā*, a formula recurring dozens of times (4:29, 4:96, 33:5, 48:14) — is read by the grammarians as indicating a quality that is constant: it "was" so before it was asked, and will not change under pressure. The promise is therefore not that God becomes merciful when Nūḥ's people repent, but that He was and is and remains a Forgiving One whose forgiveness is what a repentant creature meets.

Nūḥ does not say "He is able to forgive," nor "He may forgive." The people's obstacle was not God's capacity or His discretion; it was their own refusal to request. So the verse places the certainty on the divine side and leaves only the asking on theirs.

**Repentance Is the Cure for the Sin of Self-Sufficiency**

Why is *istighfār* the first thing Nūḥ demands, rather than "abandon Wadd and Suwāʿ"? Because for a people whose problem is *istikbār* — the word used in verse 7, the same claim of greatness that marked Iblīs — the root disease is not polytheism but the inability to admit need. A polytheist who can say "I was wrong" has begun to be a monotheist already, since the sentence presupposes a Judge, a moral law, and a self that is not its own authority. Conversely, a monotheist who cannot ask pardon has taken the words of tawḥīd into a heart still committed to *istikbār*. This is why the Qur'an makes *istighfār* the companion of knowledge and the closing act of even the best lives:

> *"Know that there is no god but God, and seek forgiveness for your sin and for the believing men and the believing women."* (Qur'an 47:19)

And the Prophet ﷺ — whose record was, by revelation's own testimony, "forgiven what went before and what came after" (48:2) — embodied the principle of the *permanent* request:

> *"O people, turn penitently to your Lord and seek His forgiveness, for I turn penitently to Him one hundred times a day."* (Muslim 2702, from al-Agharr al-Muzanī)

And in the most complete form of the same request, he taught the "master of seeking forgiveness":

> *"O God, You are my Lord; there is no god but You. You created me and I am Your servant; I am upon Your covenant, as much as I am able; I seek refuge in You from the evil of what I have wrought. I acknowledge Your blessing upon me, and I acknowledge my sin — so forgive me, for none forgives sins but You."* (al-Bukhārī 6306, from Shaddād ibn Aws)

That text is a small commentary on 71:10: acknowledging *niʿma* (blessing) and *dhanb* (sin) in one breath, which is precisely the double knowledge that Nūḥ's wealthy partisans lacked.

**The Forgotten Report of al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī**

The commentators preserve, under this verse, the celebrated exchange with al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī (d. 110/728), reported in the tafsīr literature of Ibn Kathīr and others: a man complained to him of drought and he said, "Seek forgiveness"; another complained of poverty and he said, "Seek forgiveness"; another complained of childlessness and he said, "Seek forgiveness." When he was asked why he gave the same answer to three different problems, he replied: *"I did not answer them from my own mind at all — for God says: 'Seek your Lord's forgiveness; truly He is ever Forgiving; He will send the sky upon you pouring down, and increase you in wealth and children, and give you gardens and give you rivers.'"* The report is an exegetical demonstration rather than a Prophetic ḥadīth, but its logic is the sūrah's own: verse 10 exists so that verses 11 and 12 can follow.

**The Order of the Clause: Morality Before Prosperity**

Theologically, the promise is not a transaction but a *causal order*, and the commentators guard it in both directions. Ibn Taymiyyah invokes exactly this passage when he argues that the prosperity of a community is a function of its righteousness, and that a ruler's failure to secure rain, wealth, and offspring is not primarily a matter of technique but of collective conduct — an argument he makes in *al-Siyāsa al-sharʿiyya*, where the remedy for a famine-struck polity begins with the removal of manifest wrong and the command of *istighfār*, not with rhetoric. The same pairing is stated in the Qur'an as a law about the *people of towns*:

> *"Had the people of the towns believed and been mindful, We would have opened for them blessings from the heaven and the earth."* (Qur'an 7:96)

And in a second, harder law, that prosperity without righteousness is not a sign of divine approval but of being left alone:

> *"Let not the disbelievers think that Our reprieving of them is better for themselves; We only reprieve them so that their sin may increase, and for them is a humiliating punishment."* (Qur'an 3:178)

Verse 10 forbids the first error (demanding material proof as a condition of faith) and verse 3:178 forbids the second (reading unbroken prosperity as proof of God's pleasure). Held together, they leave the believer with a sober and testable promise: repentance really does tend toward blessing, and blessing does not really prove anything about the one who enjoys it.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:11

> **He will send the sky upon you in abundant pouring,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Metonym for Rain, a Promise in the Language of a Desert**

*Yursali al-samāʾa ʿalaykum midrārā*. The verb is the ordinary one for sending, releasing, letting loose; the object is "the sky," which in Arabic usage stands for what comes *from* the sky. The trope is not poetry invented for this verse: pre-Islamic Arabic already says *samāʾa ʿalaynā*, "it rained upon us," and the Qur'an uses *al-samāʾ* both for the vault and, by the standard figure of metonymy, for the rain (as in 2:22, 6:99, 16:65, 50:9). The sentence therefore says, with economical concreteness: *He will let loose the sky over you — pouring.* Al-Ṭabarī records the early glosses on *midrār* that go in exactly this direction: *kathīran dāffiqan*, "much and gushing," and "continually, one after another." The morphological pattern *miʿlāl* denotes an instrument or an intensity, and the root *d-r-r* belongs to the field of flowing — *darra* is used of milk streaming from an udder — so the word's image is the sky as a vessel opened and emptied again and again, not a single storm.

That detail is the whole promise. In an arid environment rain is not one blessing among others; it is the *precondition* of all the others that the next verse will list. No rain means no gardens, no rivers, no flocks, no surplus, no wealth, no children surviving infancy. Nūḥ names the first domino, and by naming it he tells them that the thing they most wanted — the continuation of life — is in the hand of the One they were refusing.

**Rain as the Standard Qur'anic Proof of a Moral Cosmos**

The linkage of forgiveness with rainfall is a recurring structure in the Qur'an, not a rhetorical flourish of this verse alone. Hūd says to his people in almost identical terms:

> *"And O my people! seek forgiveness of your Lord and turn penitently to Him; He will send the sky upon you in abundance (midrāran) and add strength to your strength."* (Qur'an 11:52)

And the same promise is given to another people at another moment in Nūḥ's own story: *"Seek His forgiveness, then turn to Him, and your Lord will give you provision of this world in a goodly manner, until an appointed term"* (11:3). Meanwhile, elsewhere, the mechanism is stated as a conditional law for the jinn and humans alike:

> *"And if the nations had walked upon the path, We would surely have given them to drink of water in abundance."* (Qur'an 72:16)

What the verses are teaching, then, is not a magic — "say a formula, receive weather" — but a claim that the moral order and the natural order have one author, so that a society's relation to its Creator has consequences for its relation to its environment. The commentators' own caution preserves the boundary: the promise is made to a *collective* under conditions of faith and taqwā (7:96), not to individuals as a vending machine, and the Qur'an itself notes that rain can be a trial as easily as a gift: the hypocrite's deed is parabled as a smooth rock bearing a little soil, which a cloudburst (*wābil*) strikes and leaves bare and hard (2:264).

**The Practice the Verse Generated**

Muslims read this verse as legislation for communal behaviour, and the result was the prayer of *istisqāʾ* — the public asking for rain — in which the whole community, including the weak and even the animals, exits to the prayer-ground in a state deliberately arranged to look like repentance. The authentic account of its mechanics comes from the Friday sermon, transmitted in both Ṣaḥīḥs from Anas ibn Mālik:

> *"A man entered the mosque on a Friday, while the Messenger of God ﷺ was standing and delivering the sermon, and said: O Messenger of God, wealth has perished and the roads are cut off — so supplicate to God that He relieve us. So the Messenger of God ﷺ raised his hands and said: 'O God, send us rain.' … And it was not a week before a man stood in that same station and said: O Messenger of God, buildings have collapsed and wealth has drowned — so supplicate to God to keep it from us. And the Messenger of God ﷺ raised his hands and said: 'O God, around us and not upon us.'"* (al-Bukhārī 1014; Muslim 897)

Look at what that second half does to any naive reading of 71:11. The *same water* that is the reward of the penitent is the destruction of a people; the Prophet ﷺ asks God to *redirect* the very blessing he had just asked for, when it becomes excessive. Abundance is not automatically good; it is good when it is a covering for those who asked pardon, and a flood for those who asked nothing. The same double register runs through the parallel accounts: in Hūd's narrative the water that would have been *midrār* for a repentant city became the vehicle of judgment once repentance was refused — *"until, when Our command came and the oven boiled, We said: Load into it of every species two mates, and your family"* (11:40) — and of Lot's people it is said: *"I made rain fall upon them; and evil was the rain of those who were warned"* (26:173; cf. 27:58).

**Why Blessings Are Promised to the Disobedient at All**

A fair objection arises here, and the verse answers it in its ordering. Why does Nūḥ offer material abundance to a people in the middle of rejecting monotheism? Because he is not bribing them; he is *diagnosing* them. Their disbelief was not a free-floating intellectual position — it was embedded in an economy they did not want disturbed (their notables, their idols, their flocks, their calendars). By promising them, on the sole condition of acknowledging need, the exact goods they thought their gods and their wealth secured for them, Nūḥ removes the last rational ground for refusal: their own prosperity would be *better* under obedience to God than under the idols, without any loss to them but the loss of a useless claim to divinity. Al-Qurṭubī's treatment of vv. 10–12 stresses precisely this: the offer is constructed so that nothing in it costs the hearer anything except pride. The logic is sound enough that the Quraysh later faced the identical argument when told: *"How can you disbelieve in God, when you were lifeless and He gave you life?"* (2:28).

**Modern Application: The Order of Repair**

Two applications, one modest and one large. Modest: the verse inverts the ordinary sequence of human remedies. Confronted with a drought — economic, ecological, familial, psychological — everyone's first instinct is to fix the *visible* cause: drill deeper, build more, earn more, publish more. Nūḥ starts one level below that, at the question of whether the people have any intention of ceasing what they know to be wrong. This is not obscurantism: the classical scholars read the verse as a statement about God as the actual agent behind secondary causes, which means that a society that repairs nothing but the machinery while leaving its conduct intact is like a farmer who repairs the bucket and never opens the sky. The large application is the ecological one: the sūrah supplies the grammar for a genuinely religious environmentalism. If forgiveness and mindfulness are conditions of the water, then the treatment of soil, flocks, rivers, and rainfall is not a secular technical matter but a branch of *ʿibāda* — which is exactly why the Prophet ﷺ forbade over-exploitation in his own famous line:

> *"If the Hour begins while a sapling is in the hand of one of you, let him plant it."* (Aḥmad, from Anas; its chain is debated, and it is quoted for its meaning)

Verse 11 thus does two things at once: it tells Nūḥ's people where rain comes from, and it tells every later reader that the moral register and the material register of life were never two separate accounts.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:12

> **support you with wealth and children, and assign for you gardens and rivers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"He Will Extend You": A Verb of Reinforcement**

*Wa yumdidkum bi-amwālin wa-banīn*. The verb *mada* means to stretch out, to prolong, to reinforce, and its fields in Arabic are telling: *madd al-jaysh* is sending reinforcement to an army, *madd al-baḥr* is the tide's advance, *madad* is the extension of anything in time or quantity, and *midda* is ink — the substance stretched across a page. The same verb is used of God sending five thousand angels as reinforcement to the badly outnumbered at Badr (3:125: *"your Lord will reinforce you with five thousand of the angels, marked"*). So when Nūḥ says God will *yumdidkum*, he is saying that God will supply them with what they cannot manufacture: extension — of capital, of lineage, of capacity. Wealth and children are not merely "good things" in this sentence; they are *reinforcements* in a life that is, as verse 4 has just insisted, on a clock.

**The Two Boons the People Already Worshipped**

Note the extraordinary tactic. The Qur'an reports that Nūḥ's people judged him by exactly these two assets: *"We see you as nothing but a human being like ourselves, and we see that none follows you except those who are the meanest of us"* (11:27); and *"his wealth and his children increase him nothing but in loss"* (71:21). Wealth and children were, for that society, the language of rank — the proof that a man's gods were pleasing him. Nūḥ does not deny the value of the two goods; he relocates their source and their condition. He says in effect: the very things you use as a scale of honour are *extensions from your Lord*, and He will extend you with them on one condition, which costs you nothing — asking pardon.

That relocation is the entire anti-materialism of the Qur'an in one move. It never teaches that wealth and children are evils; it teaches that they are neither proofs of merit nor objects of trust:

> *"Wealth and children are the adornment of the lower life; and the enduring good deeds are better with your Lord in reward and better in hope."* (Qur'an 18:46)

> *"O you who believe! Let not your wealths and your children divert you from the remembrance of God; and whoever does that — it is they who are the losers."* (Qur'an 63:9)

And the Qur'an preserves, for contrast, the words of the people of Saba, whose self-assessment is the mirror image of verse 12:

> *"They said: We have wealth and children, and we shall not be punished."* (Qur'an 34:35)

That last sentence is what verse 12 exists to undo. When goods are believed to *exempt* a person, they have become gods; when they are believed to be *extended* by a Lord who can also withdraw them (8:28: *"know that your wealths and your children are only a trial"*), they are simply goods.

**"Banūn": Children as a Rising Structure**

The word *banīn* (sons, children) belongs to the root *b-n-w*, whose field is elevation and construction; Arabic says of a man who is survived by grown children that he *abnā* — "he built up." Whether or not one presses the etymology, the Qur'an's pairing is exact: children are the extension of a person into time, the nearest thing to immortality a mortal can hold — and precisely for that reason the most dangerous thing to hold as a proof. The Qur'an names the illusion plainly: *"the day when wealth and children will not avail at all"* (26:88), and it puts on the lips of every people's arrogance the same claim: *"we are more in wealth and children than you, and we are not going to be punished"* (34:35; cf. 71:21). Nūḥ's offer breaks the loop by making the very thing they treasure a consequence of humility rather than of status.

**Gardens and Rivers: This World Drafted as a Sign of the Next**

*Wa yajʿal lakum jannātin wa-yajʿal lakum anhrā*. The verb *yajʿal* is repeated for rhetorical weight — "He will *appoint* for you gardens, and He will *appoint* for you rivers" — and the two nouns are the vocabulary of Paradise imported into agriculture. *Jannah* in Arabic is an enclosed garden, so called because its trees *cover* (j-n-n, to conceal) the ground: the same root gives *jinn* (hidden beings), *junūn* (the covering of the mind), *junnah* (a shield), and *janīn* (the fetus concealed in the womb). *Nahr*, a river, belongs to the field of wide openings — the same root yields *nahr*, the throat or upper chest, and the Qur'anic command *"pray to your Lord and sacrifice (wa-nḥar)"* (108:2), the throat being the open channel. So the sentence paints: coverings of green, and open channels of water.

The Qur'an never lets that image remain merely agronomic. Gardens-and-rivers is its standing name for the next life (9:72; 13:35; 47:15; 48:5), and here it is promised *in this world* to a people asked only to repent. The effect is a kind of double bookkeeping: obedience pays in the currency the hearers actually spend, and simultaneously rehearses the currency of the promise to come. It is the sharpest possible answer to the objection that religion asks people to give up the world — Nūḥ's people are told that the world's best assets run through the door they are refusing to enter.

**A Prophet's Realism About Incentives**

One should notice that this is not flattery. Prophets in the Qur'an do not buy belief: Nūḥ says explicitly *"I do not ask you for a reward for it; my reward is only upon God"* (11:29; 26:109). Yet he also tells them plainly what obedience does to a society's prospects, and in that he is following the practice of the Prophet ﷺ, who — as the collections record — gave generously to men whose hearts were still inclining, while withholding from others he loved more. Asked about it, he explained:

> *"I give to a man, and I leave another whom I love more than him, only because God will draw him down into the Fire on his face by reason of my not giving him."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Anas ibn Mālik — the report on the distribution of the spoils at Ḥunayn and the "reconciliation of hearts")

The principle is that material good may be honestly named as a *fruit* of righteousness and used to soften a hard heart, but never as the *wage* of belief. Nūḥ offers gardens; he does not sell verses. The distinction separates the sūrah's promise from every later version of the claim that faithfulness is a technique for getting rich — a claim the same sūrah demolishes at verse 21, where the wealth of the leaders turns out to have been "nothing but loss," and at verse 25, where the gardens were drowned.

**The Verse's Testable Shape**

Finally, verse 12 has an unusual property: it is stated as an empirical claim made by a man with no army, no treasury, and no followers, to people who can immediately check it. He is not asking them to accept a metaphysic on trust; he is offering a hypothesis about the world — *a community that stops exploiting, stops lying, stops worshipping its own status, and asks pardon, will find its water table rising, its flocks increasing, its children living, and its fields enclosed in green* — and inviting them to test it. The Qur'an repeats this experiment-design elsewhere, most challengingly in 2:23–24 and 52:34 (*"let them produce a discourse like it"*), and the moral force of verse 12 lies in the fact that a preaching prophet was willing to be measured on the material outcome of public righteousness. The failure of the experiment in this case — the flood that drowned their gardens — is what verse 25 records, and it confirms rather than refutes the promise, because the condition was never met.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:13

> **What is the matter with you that you do not expect from God majesty,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Diagnostic Question, Not an Accusation**

*Mā lakum lā tarjūna li-llāhi waqārā* — literally, "What is it for you, that you do not expect for God a *waqār*?" The idiom *mā lakum* is not an insult but a physician's question: it treats the state described as abnormal, requiring an explanation from the sufferer. The Qur'an uses it as a standing device to interrupt self-evidence: *"What is the matter with you that, when it is said to you, 'Go forth in the cause of God,' you cling heavily to the earth?"* (9:38); *"What is the matter with you — how do you judge?"* (68:36). Its logic is that a person who is asked "what is wrong with you?" is compelled to look for a cause outside his own position — and the cause Nūḥ is about to name, in verses 14–20, is not a missing argument but a missing capacity to weigh.

**"Waqār": Weight, Dignity, and Two Readings**

*Waqār* is from *w-q-r*, whose core sense is heaviness with stability: a *wāqir* is a stone that does not shift, and of a man of gravity Arabs say *waqura* — he has weight. The word therefore means dignity, solemnity, the quality that makes a thing taken seriously; and it is the same root that produced *waqr*, the hard plug of deafness in verse 7 (*"in our ears is a waqr"*, 41:5). The sūrah is playing on that: a people who put a heaviness in their ears will be asked why they have no heaviness — no weight — for God.

The translators and the commentators divide the clause in two directions, and both are preserved in the classical exegesis. The majority reading — carried by al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī, as the source commentary notes — takes *tarjūna* in its ordinary sense of expecting and hoping: "you do not hope, from God, honour and magnificence," i.e., you never reckon that God could raise you, dignify you, or that He is great enough to be worth obeying. A second reading, transmitted from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and preserved in al-Ṭabarī's chain, takes the clause as "you do not fear God with the majesty He is owed." The conduct that word names — weight without heaviness, dignity without display — is portrayed in the Qur'an's own picture of God's servants: *"and the servants of the All-Merciful are those who walk upon the earth in humility, and when the ignorant address them they say 'peace.'"* (Qur'an 25:63) The two are not really alternatives: to *hope for* God's magnificence and to *fear* God's majesty are the same faculty of assigning the correct weight, exercised in two directions.

**The Real Complaint: God Had Become Light to Them**

Understood this way, verse 13 is the deepest diagnosis in the sūrah. Nūḥ does not say "you do not know"; he does not say "you have not heard." He says: God has no *weight* in your calculations. And that is exactly what the rest of the sūrah demonstrates. They listened to nine centuries of speech, which is more knowledge than any later nation has had; but the man who has no weight in a person's life is not resisted intellectually, it is *displaced* socially — by the notables whose opinions carried real weight (v. 21), by the inherited names of ancestors that carried the weight of identity (v. 23), and by wealth and children, which carried the weight of survival (v. 12). The Qur'an describes this displacement in a single formula it repeats about the peoples of Mecca and of earlier prophets: *"they found their fathers upon a community, and they hurry along in their footsteps"* (43:22–23).

The two verses in which the Qur'an states the same fault as a cosmic failure are worth quoting side by side with this one:

> *"They did not measure God with His true measure, when they said: God has not sent down upon any human being anything."* (Qur'an 6:91)

> *"They did not measure God with His true measure, on the Day of Resurrection, when the whole earth will be His handful and the heavens rolled up in His right hand."* (Qur'an 39:67)

"*Did not measure Him with His true measure*" — *mā qadarūhu ḥaqqa qadrihi* — is the technical opposite of *waqār*: the failure is a failure of *qadr*, of estimation. Nūḥ's people did not deny God's existence; they denied Him *size*.

**Why the Reproach Comes Where It Does**

Structurally, verse 13 is the hinge of the whole argument section. Verses 10–12 offered pardon and prosperity. Verse 13 stops the offer and asks the *why* question — why are you refusing something that costs you nothing and pays you everything? And verses 14–20 then answer it by presenting four fields of evidence: the self (v. 14), the heavens and lights (vv. 15–16), growth and return (vv. 17–18), and the earth (vv. 19–20). That sequence is not decoration; it is a therapy for the specific complaint. If the problem is that God has become weightless, the remedy is not more threats — verse 7 has shown that threats only increase the *waqr* in the ear — but a re-measurement exercise. Four times over, Nūḥ points at something the people cannot doubt they experience, and asks them to notice who is behind it. He is trying to restore the mass of the Invisible in their estimation, because once that is restored everything else in the sūrah follows automatically.

It is worth noting, too, that the reproach is addressed to *them*, in the plural, and not to their gods or their leaders. The leaders are mentioned later (v. 21) and named only once. Nūḥ locates responsibility with the hearers: a people who assign weight wrongly are not victims of a bad system; they are the ones doing the weighing.

**The Modern Form of Weightlessness**

The verse reads with uncomfortable accuracy in a culture that has everything except gravity. Information has never been cheaper and nothing has ever been weighed less; the same feed carries a child's death, a joke, a product, a war, and a fatwa, and the medium's structure flattens them to equal lightness. The classical diagnosis of the *wāqir* — a man whose judgments are stable because he has estimated things at their true mass — describes precisely the faculty modern attention destroys first, and the loss is not confined to religion: whoever cannot hold God at His real weight will, in practice, hold whatever is nearest and loudest at a weight it does not deserve. Nūḥ's question, then, is worth asking in its own form by anyone who wonders why belief, for many contemporaries, is not contested but simply *light*: "what is the matter with you, that you do not expect from God majesty?" The answer, as verses 14–20 will show, is not a shortage of evidence. It is a habit of not looking.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:14

> **seeing that He created you in stages.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Evidence Is the Nearest One: Your Own Biography**

*Wa-qad khalaqakum aṭwārā* — "although He created you in *aṭwār*." The word is the plural of *ṭawr*, from *ṭ-w-r*, whose field is turning and circling: a *ṭawr* is one turn, one pass, one condition succeeding another, and the lexicographers gloss it as *ḥālat baʿda ḥāla*, "a state after a state." The image is of a person who has gone *round* several times and emerged each time differently shaped. What makes the choice of word wise is that a *turn* implies both sameness of axis and difference of position: the same self, in a new place. The Arabs also use *ṭawr* for a phase of an activity — "in turn (ṭawran) we do this, then that" — which is how the early commentators render it here: *anwāʿan ḥālān baʿda ḥālin*, "manifold conditions, one after another" (al-Ṭabarī; Ibn Kathīr cites Mujāhid and Qatāda to the effect: from one stage to the next, from the nutfah to the clot, to the lump, to bones, to flesh, to birth, to childhood, to strength, to old age).

Note the grammar of *wa-qad*: a circumstantial clause attached to the rebuke of verse 13. The structure means "how is it that you do not weigh God at His majesty, *when* — the fact being — He created you in stages?" The verse does not begin a new argument; it grounds the previous one. That is a recurring technique of the Qur'an, and it is pedagogically shrewd: a reproach that arrives without evidence hardens the hearer (as verses 6–7 have just illustrated), whereas a reproach answered immediately with something the hearer cannot deny turns the same hearer toward inspection. And the evidence is deliberately chosen to be the one item no human being can disown: his own past.

**The Qur'an's Own Embryology**

The same phrase *aṭwāran* occurs only here, but the list it compresses is given fully elsewhere, twice, in sequences the Makkan audience could recite:

> *"We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging clot, then from a lump of flesh, formed or unformed, so that We might make it clear to you … then We bring you forth as an infant."* (Qur'an 22:5)

> *"Then We placed him as a drop in a firm lodging; then We made the drop a clinging clot, and We made the clot a lump of flesh, and We made in the lump bones, and We clothed the bones with flesh; then We produced it as another creation. So blessed be God, the best of creators."* (Qur'an 23:13–14)

And the sūrah of prostration adds the moral of the sequence: *"Then He proportioned him and breathed into him of His spirit, and made for you hearing and sight and hearts"* (32:9) — that is, the *stages* end in the faculties which verse 7 described as plugged. The Qur'an also locates the argument inside a broader claim about the futility of arrogance, since every human being shares one biography: *"Does man not see that We created him from a drop, and then he is an open adversary?"* (36:77).

The Prophet ﷺ taught the same phases as a matter of creed, with an addition about the writing of destiny:

> *"The creation of one of you is gathered in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop (nuṭfah); then it is a clinging clot for the like of that; then it is a lump of flesh for the like of that; then God sends the angel and it is breathed into him, and he is commanded with four things: to write his provision, his term, his deeds, and whether he is wretched or blessed."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643, from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd)

Commentators from al-Qurṭubī onward note that 71:14 is one of the strongest proofs that stages of *this* life are meant here, and that verse 18 will add the stages *after* it — death and being brought forth — so that the two verses together span the whole course of a human being.

**Why "Stages" Rather Than "Creation" Would Carry the Weight**

The rhetorical choice matters. Had the verse said only "He created you," the hearers could concede a beginning and remain indifferent, because a one-off act of making can be filed away as ancient history. *Aṭwār* refuses that: a *staged* creation is one in which the creature was, at each stage, entirely unable to help itself. Nobody negotiated his way from clot to lump; nobody chose the term the angel writes; nobody decided to be born, to walk, to grow teeth, to reach the "age of forty" the Qur'an names as the point at which a person first understands his own dependence (46:15: *"until, when he reaches his full strength and reaches forty, he says: My Lord, enable me to be grateful for Your blessing … and reform for me my posterity"*). Self-authorship is the quiet fiction on which pride lives; the verse removes it by making the biography itself the exhibit.

This is why the verse answers verse 13 so exactly. If the people's disease is that God has become light, the cure is to be shown that they have never once been heavy — that at every stage they were carried.

**The Argument from Process to Power**

Second, *aṭwār* is an argument about what the same power can still do. A being who organised you through ten transformations did not need your cooperation then, and does not need it now for the next one. The Qur'an states the inference in its most compressed form:

> *"Is He who creates like one who does not create? Will you not remember? And if you count God's blessings you cannot enumerate them."* (Qur'an 16:17–18)

and in its most direct:

> *"Does man suppose that We shall not gather his bones? Yes — able are We to reshape his fingertips entire."* (Qur'an 75:3–4)

The fingertips are named because they carry the whorls that make each human hand unique; the verse claims that the power which staged a person into existence in the first place can also re-stage him at the level of that detail. This is the sūrah's implicit answer to the question of resurrection, which verse 18 will make explicit.

**Reading the Stages Inward**

The commentators did not stop at biology. Ibn al-Qayyim al-Jawziyya, and before him al-Ḥakīm al-Tirmidhī in *ʿIlm al-Awliyāʾ*, read *aṭwār* as a description of the heart's phases too — the stages of *tawba*, *zuhd*, *ṣabr*, *riḍā*, and so on — because the same word is used in Arabic for the turns of a journey, and the soul plainly passes through conditions one after another without being able to skip any. That reading is an analogical extension rather than the verse's primary sense, and the exegetes say so; but it is a legitimate one, and it preserves the sūrah's psychology: a person who cannot recall being *made* will also be unable to recall being *changed*, and will therefore try to change himself all at once, fail, and despair. The stages are the antidote to two errors at once — pride in the first case, and despondency in the second.

**A Practical Conclusion: The Memory That Makes a Person Careful**

Applied to conduct, 71:14 teaches a discipline of recollection whose effect is observable. A man who remembers that he was once a helpless thing in a dark womb does not behave as though his present abilities were his own achievement; and a man who does not behave as though they were is far easier to work with, live with, and correct. The Prophet ﷺ made the same memory the cure for the very arrogance this sūrah describes:

> *"God has revealed to me that you should be humble, so that none of you boasts over another and none of you transgresses against another."* (Muslim 2865, from ʿIyāḍ ibn Himār)

And in the verse's own terms, humility here is not self-hatred: it is an accurate estimate of who did the stages. The verse ends where verse 13 began — with the re-measurement of reality, one's own reality first.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:15

> **Have you not considered how God created the seven heavens one upon another,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Self to the Sky: The Second Exhibit**

Verse 14 turned the people's attention inward, to their own biography; verse 15 turns it outward, and does so with a verb of *seeing-that-amounts-to-thinking*: *a-lam taraw kayfa khalaqa Allāhu…* — "have you not *seen* how God created…?" In Qur'anic Arabic *raʾā* frequently governs not a visible object but a *mode*: "see how your Lord did with…" (10:24; 25:45; 105:1). The evidence offered is not that the heavens exist — no one disputed that — but the *manner* in which they exist: *sabʿa samāwāṭ ṭibāqā*. The question is about quality, not fact, and quality is what a person can inspect for himself without any authority but his own eyes.

The word *samāʾ* belongs to the root sense of height and of what lies above, and the plural *samāwāt* is the Qur'an's standing term for the celestial order. *Ṭibāq* is from *ṭ-b-q*, the field of covering one thing upon another so that they correspond — a *ṭibaq* is a layer fitted to its neighbour, and the accusative here functions as a description of state: created "in conformity, one over another." The same root yields the rhetorical term *ṭibāq* for the pairing of opposites in a sentence, because the art consists precisely in setting two matching halves against each other. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss captures it: *mutaṭābiqāt* — "conforming, one above another," with no part of one layer jarring out of true with the next.

**Seven, and Why the Number Is Not an Argument**

The seven heavens are affirmed elsewhere in the Qur'an with a frequency that makes the number itself part of the creed's furniture: *"He who created seven heavens in layers"* (67:3); *"God who created seven heavens, and of the earth the like of them"* (65:12); *"then He directed Himself to the heaven, when it was smoke, and said to it and to the earth: Come, willingly or unwillingly; they said: we come, obedient"* (41:11); *"and We made above you seven [heavens] firmly built"* (78:12). The commentators — al-Rāzī at length, Ibn Kathīr in summary — record the disagreement over what the seven are: the Ptolemaic spheres absorbed into Islamic astronomy, the seven atmospheres, the seven "created orders," or the seven heavens of the Unseen whose reality exceeds disclosure. No resolution is offered by the text, and this is the point: the sūrah's argument works on the affirmed properties (height, layering, conformity, creation), not on the anatomy. The Qur'an itself rebukes speculation that outruns what is given: *"They ask you about the Hour, when its settling will be. Say: its knowledge is only with my Lord; none will reveal it at its time. It weighs heavily in the heavens and the earth"* (7:187); and of those who dispute about it: *"Nay, but they denied that which they could not encompass in knowledge, and whose interpretation has not yet come to them"* (10:39).

Hence the ethic the classical theologians drew from verses like this one, most sharply Ibn Taymiyyah in his critique of the philosophers who held the world to be beginningless: the sūrah says *khalaqa* — created — and a created thing is by definition contingent, dependent, and not divine. The heavens are not gods, not eternal, not self-sufficient: *"Do they exist out of nothing, or are they the creators?"* (52:35). This single clause thus performs a double demolition in the Makkan context, where luminaries were reverenced and the sky was treated as a source of fate: the heavens are *made*, and what is made cannot be worshipped. Ibrāhīm's argument with his people — the star, the moon, the sun, each declining, and the verdict *"I do not love those that decline"* (6:76–79) — is the same reasoning in narrative form, and it is the background of the next verse.

**The Argument from Non-Collision**

The strongest scriptural companion to 71:15 is the challenge in al-Mulk, which turns the layering of the sky into a test any observer may run:

> *"He who created seven heavens in layers. You will not see, in the creation of the All-Merciful, any incoherence. So return your sight again and again: do you see any gap (fuṭūr)? Then return your sight twice more: your sight will return to you defeated and exhausted."* (Qur'an 67:3–4)

The root *f-ṭ-r* gives *fuṭūr*, fissures, cracks, the places where a structure fails at its joints. The verse claims that the sky is a construction without visible joints, and the rhetorical form is deliberately empirical: look again; look a third time; the looking itself is the experiment. Elsewhere the Qur'an adds that this coherence is not passive but *maintained*: *"And He has imposed the sun and the moon in continual course; and the night and the day alternate"* (35:13), *"It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor for the night to outrun the day; each swims in an orbit"* (36:40), and *"He raised the heaven, and He placed the Balance — that you should not transgress in the Balance"* (55:7–8).

That last pairing is the sūrah's whole strategy in two lines: cosmic order and moral order are named in one breath, and the reason 71:15–16 follow 71:13 is exactly that the *mīzān* in the sky is the school of *waqār* on earth. A person who has genuinely looked at a structure that cannot be rearranged without collapse, and who has not thereby acquired some sense of weight in his own judgements, is the man verse 13 is describing.

**Cosmology as Consolation, Not as Curriculum**

Finally, notice who is being addressed and why. This is not an astronomy lesson inserted into a sermon; it is a man with no followers, told by revelation to cite the sky. The force of that is pastoral: nine centuries of a private, unattended argument are set beside a public, unattended firmament, and the reader is invited to infer that the same God who keeps the layers of the sky in their places without any audience is keeping account of the preacher's effort without an audience either. The sūrah's earlier complaint — *"my call has increased them in nothing but flight"* — is answered here not with success but with a Maker who does not lose things. It is the same logic that underlies the Qur'an's promise to the Prophet ﷺ that the stories of the prophets are sent to *steady* his heart (11:120), and it explains why verses 15–18 read like a summons to look upward before verse 19 returns the listener to the ground he stands on and the roads he will take.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:16

> **and made the moon a light therein, and made the sun a lamp?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Luminaries, Two Kinds of Light**

*Wa-jaʿala al-qamāra fīhinā nūran wa-jaʿala al-shamsa sirājā*. The verb *jaʿala* — to make, to appoint, to set into a function — is repeated for each body, and the two complements differ: the moon is *nūr*, light; the sun is *sirāj*, a lamp. This is not a stylistic variation for the sake of the rhyme. The Qur'an uses the pair consistently, in the very same verse quoted by the source commentary:

> *"He it is Who made the sun a radiance (ḍiyāʾ) and the moon a light (nūr), and appointed it stations, so that you might know the number of years and the reckoning."* (Qur'an 10:5; cf. 14:33, 31:29)

and in the sūrah of the distinction's most explicit form: *"Blessed is He in whose heaven are the great signs, and He made therein a lamp and a moon giving light (qamaran munīrā)"* (25:61), and, in the sūrah of the News, the sun alone receives the adjective of burning: *"and We made a blazing (wahhāj) lamp"* (78:13). Arab philology draws the line plainly: *nūr* is what is lit, what becomes visible and makes visible; *ḍiyāʾ* and *sirāj* belong to what burns, generates, and consumes its own fuel. *Sirāj* is a lamp with a wick — the same word that gives *masrūj*, "lit," and Arabic proverbs about the *misbāḥ* which must burn itself in order to give light.

So the two bodies are given two ontologies in one clause. The sun shines from within; the moon shines from without, by receiving. The Qur'an does not here describe reflection in modern terms, and it need not: it uses the vocabulary that separates a source from a recipient, which is precisely what the physical distinction is. It is one of those places where the sūrah's phrasing is so economical that later readers found in it an accuracy its hearers would not have thought to demand.

**"Therein": Where the Pronoun Points**

The small word *fīhā* — "in it / therein" — exercised the grammarians, and the source commentary's note (that verse 16 can be read on with verses 17 and 19 as a continuation of the rhetorical question begun in verse 15) is one of the two solutions; the other is that the pronoun refers to the lowest heaven, in accordance with verses that place the lamps there:

> *"And We adorned the nearest heaven with lamps, and made them a means of protection; that is the measuring of the Mighty, the Knowing."* (Qur'an 41:12; cf. 37:6: *"We have adorned the heaven of the world with an adornment, the stars, and as a protection against every rebellious devil"*)

Both readings make the clause richer rather than vaguer. Read with verse 15, the moon and sun are inside the same ordered architecture whose layering was just affirmed — lights *within* a structure, not free-floating powers. Read with 41:12, the lights are *placed where they can be seen by the people who are addressed*, which is the point of an ornament and of a lamp alike: a lamp installed in a ceiling is a lamp installed for the sake of the floor beneath it.

**Demoting the Gods of the Sky**

The immediate polemic of verse 16 is unmistakable in a Makkan and wider Semitic setting in which the sun and moon were reverenced, addressed as givers of fate, and named in personal names and tribal cults. The sūrah does not argue with the luminaries; it *downgrades their category*: they are not deities, not agents, not signs of destiny — they are made things with functions, "a lamp" among the things made. The Qur'an states the rule for all such reverences: *"…worship not the sun and the moon, but worship God who created them, if it is He you serve"* (41:37).

The Prophet ﷺ applied precisely this principle to a superstition of his own day, when the sun eclipsed on the day of his son Ibrāhīm's death and people said the sky had darkened for the loss:

> *"The sun and the moon are two of God's signs; they do not eclipse for the death of anyone or for his life. When you see it, pray and call upon God until it is uncovered."* (al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-Kusūf; Muslim 904, from ʿĀʾisha; the wording is also transmitted from Abū Mūsā and al-Mughīra)

That report is a live commentary on 71:16: even when the heavens appear to grieve for a human event, they are signs, not participants. A *sirāj* does not care who it lights.

**The Moon as the Measure of a Common Life**

Verse 16 also quietly installs the reason the moon is called *nūr* while the sun is called *sirāj*: the moon is the body humans read. Ten verses elsewhere in the Qur'an name its function as time — *"He appointed for it stations (manāzila), so that you might know the number of years and the reckoning"* (10:5) — and the tradition built the whole of Muslim public life on that reading: Ramadan, the two ʿīds, the months of pilgrimage, the waiting-period of divorce, the cycles that determine legal status, all governed by the visibility of a crescent. The sūrah of the "great signs" pairs them with the moon in exactly this register: *"and We made the night and the day two signs, then effaced the sign of the night, and made the sign of the day illuminating, so that you might seek bounty from your Lord and know the number of years and the reckoning"* (17:12). The verse even names the sun "the clear daylight" and the moon "followed upon it" in the sūrah's most famous oath-cluster (91:1–4). A lamp gives light; the moon gives *calendar*, which is what a society actually needs in order to keep time together.

**The Analogy the Verse Invites: Reflected Light Is Still Light**

Homilists since the early centuries have read 71:16 as a parable of the relationship between revelation and its carriers: revelation is *sirāj* — a burning thing, costly to itself, generating its own light — while the believer, the scholar, the preacher, is *qamar*, illuminated by what he receives, and visible to others only to the extent that he is turned toward the source. The comparison is an inference rather than the verse's literal sense, but it is anchored in the sūrah's own prophet: Nūḥ is a moon, not a sun — nine centuries of light he did not manufacture, cast upon a people who covered their faces. And the analogy carries two corrections. A moon that pretends to shine by itself is an eclipse; and a people who attack the moon are attacking a mirror, which is why the sūrah's argument does not stop at vv. 15–16 but moves to vv. 17–18, from what they see above to what they are: the next evidence is the soil and the growth it produces, and the return that comes after the cutting.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:17

> **And God caused you to grow forth from the earth as a plant grows.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Exhibit: You Are an Agrarian Event**

*Wa-llāhu anbatakum mina al-arḍi nabātā*. The verb *anbata* is the causative of *nabata*, "to spring up, to grow by itself," and it is the verb the Qur'an uses for vegetation throughout: *"and We send down from the rain-bearing clouds pouring water, so that We may bring forth thereby grain and herbage (nabātan), and gardens packed with trees"* (78:14–16; cf. 50:9; 36:33–34). The accusative *nabātā* after the verb is the cognate accusative that intensifies the action and, per the source commentary's note, may equally be read as a state: "as growing things." In either parse the meaning is the same and it is severe: God did not *build* you, as a builder builds a house; He *grew* you, as a farmer grows a crop — and you did the growing as little as a stalk does.

That choice of verb is what ties verse 17 to verse 11, and it is the sūrah's quiet structural masterpiece. Rain was promised on the condition of asking pardon; the people are then described as *grown* from the earth; and at verse 25 the same earth's water will do the harvesting. Verse 17 belongs to an agricultural sequence: *istighfār* → *midrār* (pouring rain) → *nabāt* (growth) → *ʿawd* (return to the soil) → *khurūj* (being brought out again). The human being, in this sūrah, is a crop.

**Water, Soil, and the Meaning of "From the Earth"**

"Mina al-arḍi" — "from the earth" — carries a double reference the commentators preserve. First, the origin of the species: *"Truly the likeness of ʿĪsā with God is as the likeness of Ādam; He created him from dust"* (3:59), and *"He created you from dust, then from a drop"* (35:11), and *"…then He brought you forth as infants"* (22:5). Second, the material of every subsequent human body: whatever a person is made of now was once drawn out of the ground as food, and *"God made you grow from the earth as a plant grows"* is, in that second sense, a plain chemical statement. The Qur'an puts the two together in a single verse about provision: *"Let man look at his food: We pour the water down abundantly, then We split the earth in splits, and grow therein grain, and vine and herbage, and olive and palm, and dense gardens, and fruit and pasture — a provision for you and for your cattle"* (80:24–31).

The same logic yields a social consequence the sūrah will exploit against arrogance. Because the origin is one soil, the diversity of humans is not a hierarchy but a geology:

> *"God created Ādam from a handful which He took from the whole of the earth; so the sons of Ādam have come according to the earth: the red, the white, the black, and those between; and the soft, the hard, the evil, and the good."* (Abū Dāwūd 4693; al-Tirmidhī 2955, from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī — graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ by al-Tirmidhī)

Race, temperament, and moral tendency are here described as *varieties of the same field*, which is the exact opposite of the claim that one lineage is intrinsically above another — the very claim Nūḥ's notables were making when they said he was "a human being like yourselves" and his followers the "meanest" of them (11:27).

**The Source Commentary's Philosophical Note, Qualified**

The source observes that although "the growing things" are diverse, they remain attached to the one ontological Source, whose substance is also within them. Read carefully, the first half of that sentence is pure Qur'anic doctrine: everything grows because it is being caused to grow at every moment, and dependence is continuous, not a past event — *"Is there any creator other than God, who provides for you from the heaven and the earth?"* (35:3). The second half, however, has to be handled with the classical caution, and the tradition's own debates show why. Certain strands of Islamic thought (the later school of the *waḥdat al-wujūd*, the "oneness of being") drew from verses of this kind the conclusion that creation is a modulation of the Creator's own reality; the rebuttal of that inference is explicit in Ibn Taymiyyah and in the kalam tradition generally: the creature's being is *given*, *contingent*, and *really other* than the Giver — and the Qur'anic verb here is *anbata*, an act of an agent upon a distinct object, not an emanation of a substance into itself. The verse's theology is that the plant is *grown*; not that it *is* the Grower.

**A Botanical Psychology: What the Metaphor Does to a Person**

Notice the effect of thinking of oneself as a grown thing rather than a built one. A built thing is its builder's property and monument; a grown thing is a loan with conditions. If you are a crop, then: you required rain you did not ask for; you required soil you did not choose; you had a season and not a schedule; you can be cut without your consent; and — the most consoling part — a field that looks bare in winter is not a failure, it is *between stages*, which is what verse 14's *aṭwār* had already taught. The Prophet ﷺ described the community of believers in precisely these botanical terms, in a verse that reads like a commentary on this one:

> *"Muḥammad is the Messenger of God; and those with him are severe against the disbelievers, merciful among themselves… their likeness in the Torah, and their likeness in the Gospel: like a sown crop that puts forth its shoot, then strengthens it, then makes it thick, then it stands upright on its stalk, delighting the sowers."* (Qur'an 48:29)

The same imagery underlies the parable of a single grain producing seven ears of a hundred grains each (2:261) — a metaphor for the slow, multiplied yield of a good word and a patient expenditure — and the sūrah's own complaint in verse 6 belongs to that experience: nine and a half centuries of sowing, and the field kept refusing to be a field.

**Why the Growth Argument Persuades**

Logically, verse 17 completes what verse 15 could not. The sky can be conceded as a spectacle and admired at a distance; the crop cannot. Every hearer of this sūrah had planted, watered, waited, and harvested; every one had seen rain turn bare ground into green, and had no part in the turning. So the verse uses an experience that is *earned* rather than *observed*: growth is something you have watched happen to you. And by tying it to the promised rain (v. 11) and the promised return to the soil (v. 18), the sūrah makes the whole argument a single sentence of farming: ask pardon, and the sky will open; the sky opens and things grow; what grows goes back; and going back is not the end, because the farmer who returns seed to the ground is not burying a corpse but planting a crop.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:18

> **Then He will return you into it, and bring you forth again.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs for the End That Is Not an End**

*Thumma yuʿīdukum fīhi wa-yukhrijukum khurūjā*. The first verb is *aʿāda*, from *ʿ-w-d*, "to return, to bring back," the same root that gives *ʿīd*, "a festival" — literally, "that which comes back." The second is *akhraja*, form IV of *kh-r-j*, "to bring out," and it is reinforced by the cognate accusative *khurūjā*: He will bring you out — an actual bringing-out, not a figure of speech. Between the two stands *thumma*, the conjunction of interval that this sūrah uses for everything that takes centuries (vv. 8, 9) — so the sentence is not "you die and you rise," but "there is a stretch, and then an emerging." The whole eschatology of the sūrah is compressed into a sequence of three motions: growth (v. 17), return (v. 18a), egress (v. 18b).

The verse is, in effect, a quotation of a formula the Qur'an states elsewhere with its three stages explicit:

> *"From it We created you, and in it We return you, and from it We bring you forth another time."* (Qur'an 20:55)

and in the sūrah's own opening register, where the same three movements answer the very idea of a God who cannot remake a body:

> *"How can you disbelieve in God, when you were lifeless and He gave you life, then He causes you to die, then He gives you life, then to Him you are returned?"* (Qur'an 2:28)

**Why the Return Is Named Before the Raising**

The order of verse 18 is not decorative. *Yuʿīdukum fīhā* comes first: the body goes back into the same earth it was grown from, and only then is it brought out. The theology is that resurrection is not the *repair* of a structure but the *reconstitution* of a material — and it is a material God has already handled once. The tradition preserves a specific image of that:

> *"Everything of the son of Adam is eaten by the earth except the tailbone (ʿajb al-dhanab); in it the creation is assembled on the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-Anbiyāʾ; and Muslim, from Abū Hurayra)

Whether one reads this as a physical description or as a narrative of continuity — a "seed" of personal identity preserved through the general decomposition — the function in the verse is the same: the return to the earth is not the erasure of the person but the condition of the bringing-forth, exactly as a seed in the ground is not the end of a plant but its next stage. That is why 71:17 and 71:18 must be read as a unit, and why the sūrah's hearers, who planted every year, were given the argument in their own vocabulary: *"…so that you may know that God gives life to the earth after its death"* (57:17, abridged).

**The Argument from Agriculture to Resurrection**

This is one of the Qur'an's most persistent proofs, and it is stated as an observable inference rather than a demand for belief:

> *"And a dead earth is a sign for them: We gave it life and brought forth from it grain, and from it they eat; and We made therein gardens of palms and vines, and made springs flow forth between them, that they might eat of its fruit and of what their hands have made. Will they not then give thanks? Glory to Him who created the pairs, all of what the earth grows, and of themselves, and of what they do not know."* (Qur'an 36:33–36)

and:

> *"And among His signs: you see the earth humbled, and when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells. Truly He who gives it life is the Giver of life to the dead; He has power over all things."* (Qur'an 41:39)

And the Qur'an attaches the conclusion to the image in the two words that are 71:18's own ending: *"and We grew thereby gardens and the grain of the harvest, and the palm trees towering, with clustered spathes, as a provision for the servants, and We gave life thereby to a dead land. Thus is the coming forth (kadhālika al-khurūj)"* (50:9–11). The syllogism is exact: (i) you have seen matter that was inert become alive, repeatedly, at close range; (ii) you did nothing about it except water it; (iii) therefore the claim that the same Agent will reconstitute human bodies is not an anomaly but an *extension* of what you already accept every planting season. The sūrah's hearers — a people who had just been promised rain (v. 11) and growth (v. 17) — cannot say they have never seen dead things rise. The objection of 36:78 ("*who will give life to bones while they are decayed?*") is answered the same way: *"Say: He gives life who produced them the first time, and He is Knowing of every creation"* (36:79), and *"He created you once from dust, then from a drop, then He makes you a human being fully formed"* (35:11, abridged).

**Death in This Sūrah Is a Burial of Seed, Not a Disposal of Rubbish**

Read against verse 25, verse 18 acquires a chilling function. For the repentant, being returned to the earth is planting: the sūrah has just spent three verses (10–12) offering the *means* by which the return would be cancellation rather than sentence. For Nūḥ's people, the same return is burial without hope, because their soil received them by drowning rather than by the washing and shroud of a believer's death. The contrast between the two ways bodies reach the ground is not incidental; the entire sūrah is the difference between them.

This is also where the sūrah's argument about *waqār* (v. 13) closes. A man who expects to be brought out of the ground judges his actions differently from a man for whom the ground is the last honest statement. The Prophet ﷺ made that expectation a practical discipline rather than a fear technique — he said:

> *"I used to forbid you from visiting graves; now visit them, for they remind you of the Hereafter."* (Muslim 977, from Abū Hurayra)

and the community's whole funeral rite is a live performance of verses 17–18: the body is washed, wrapped in two unstitched cloths, carried without ceremony, laid directly against the earth in a grave whose walls are built by the living, and the mourners are told, at the moment of lowering, the words of the sūrah itself: *"from it We created you, and in it We return you, and from it We bring you forth another time."* and at the moment the body is lowered the mourners are told what to ask for:

> *"When the Prophet ﷺ had finished burying the dead, he stood at his grave and said: 'Ask forgiveness for your brother, and ask that he be made firm in his answer, for he is being questioned now.' "* (Abū Dāwūd 3221, from ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān — its chain is discussed, but the rite it prescribes passed into continuous practice)

which is why the sūrah's final verses will turn from argument to supplication: the only thing a person can carry into that questioning is what he asked pardon for.

**The Logical Place of Verse 18**

Structurally, vv. 14–18 form a single arc about the human being: created in stages (14), which implies a Maker with a method (14); the heavens and the lights, which imply a Maker with architecture (15–16); grown from the earth (17), which implies a Maker who uses matter He made; and returned and brought out (18), which implies that the Maker is not finished. The next two verses (19–20) then complete the argument by turning from what God does *to* a person to what God does *for* him: a spread earth and open roads. Nūḥ's people were thus given, in seven verses, everything required for the "weight" they lacked — and verse 21 begins the report of what they did with it.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:19

> **And God made the earth an open expanse for you,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hiṣār: The Word for Room to Breathe**

*Wa-llāhu jaʿala lakumu al-arḍa hiṣārā*. The philologists gloss *ḥiṣār* here as a wide, level expanse — ground one can walk across and see over — and the early commentators render it with *fiṭāṭan wāsiʿan*, "a broad stretch," and *bisāṭan*, "a carpet spread out" (al-Ṭabarī; al-Zamakhsharī, *wāsiʿan*; Ibn Kathīr cites Mujāhid: "*hiṣārā*: that you travel in it as you will"). That choice of word, over the Qur'an's other vocabulary for the same gift, is the first thing to notice. Elsewhere the earth is a *firāsh*, a bed (2:22); a *mahīd*, a thing spread as a cradle is spread (20:53; 43:10); a *milād*, a place of birth and spreading (78:6); a *dhulūl*, something made tractable, tamed to your feet (67:15); a *qarār*, a place of settling (27:61). Here it is *ḥiṣār*: not the softness of a bed but the *openness of a plain*. A bed is for rest; a plain is for going. Verse 20 will say so in the next breath.

The verb *jaʿala*, used now for the fourth time in five verses (16, 17, 19, and again in 19's parallel in 20), is the Qur'an's word for *appointing a thing to a function*, and it is doing theological work: the ground is not neutral terrain on which human life happens; it has been *assigned* — its flatness, its soil, its climate, its walkability.

**The Argument Arrives at the Ground Under Their Feet**

Look at the narrowing of the lens. Verse 15: the seven heavens. Verse 16: the two lights. Verse 17: the human body as a plant. Verse 18: the same body returning and being brought out. Verse 19: the floor. The sequence is an argument by decreasing distance from the hearer, and it is why vv. 13–20 constitute the sūrah's centre rather than an appendix. Whatever a person may doubt about the architecture of the sky, he cannot doubt that he is standing on ground that has been left open for him — that there was no wall, no shortage of space, no impossibility of walking elsewhere. The Makkan hearers knew this in the one way that mattered in the Arabian interior: they were a caravan people, whose survival depended on the fact that the desert, though terrible, is *passable*. The sūrah of Quraysh's own trade says it: *"for the accustomed practice of Quraysh, their accustomed practice of a caravan in winter and summer — so let them worship the Lord of this House, who has fed them from hunger and made them safe from fear"* (106:1–4). Verse 19 speaks in that idiom: your routes are not your achievement.

**"For You" — And the Obligation That Attaches to a Gift**

*Lakum*, "for you," is read by the commentators as both a concession and a summons. It is a concession, because it removes the ascetic error that the physical world is an enemy of the soul: God made the earth an expanse *for you*, and the Qur'an tells the believer not to despise the gift:

> *"But seek, in what God has given you, the abode of the Hereafter, and do not forget your share of this world."* (Qur'an 28:77)

And it is a summons, because a thing made *for* someone is a trust in his hands, not a possession against its Owner. The Qur'an names the human being *khalīfa* on this earth (2:30) and states the consequence: *"And it is He who has made you successors upon the earth, and has raised some of you above others in degrees, that He may try you in what He has given you"* (6:165). Everything a person does to the ground — how he tills it, wastes it, divides it, poisons it, or denies others its use — is therefore within the scope of this verse, since the verse describes it as an *appointment for benefit*, and a benefit converted into an instrument of harm is a breach of trust.

This is the reason the sūrah's environmental logic, begun at verse 11, is completed here: rain, growth, and a spread earth are one system given for a purpose, and verses 10–12 have already named the condition on which the system works for a society — asking pardon, meaning: ceasing to treat the world as one's own property.

**Openness as Evidence Against Excuse**

There is a juridical dimension too, which the sūrah will press in verse 20 and which the Qur'an states elsewhere as a general rule about escape from a corrupt environment:

> *"Those whose souls the angels take while they are wronging themselves — they said: in what were you? They said: we were oppressed upon the earth. They said: was not God's earth wide, so that you could have emigrated in it? It is such whose refuge is Hell, and evil is the destination."* (Qur'an 4:97)

And its positive counterpart: *"Whoever emigrates in God's cause will find on earth many a place to flee to, and abundance"* (4:100); *"Say: O My servants who believe, fear your Lord. For those who do good in this world is good, and God's earth is vast"* (39:10).

An expanse changes the moral classification of a person's situation. Nūḥ's followers could not be told "there was nothing you could have done," because the ground itself was open; and neither could his people be told that the message had nowhere to go, since the earth was *ḥiṣār* and the roads were *fajj*. Verse 19 therefore functions as the removal of an excuse: a man who claims he was trapped in an environment, in a family, or in a culture, while standing on an open expanse, has not been prevented by reality — he has declined a movement that was available.

**No Cover in the Open**

Finally, the image carries one more shading. An expanse is a place where there is nothing to hide behind. The sūrah's audience had, in verse 7, tried to hide *inside their own garments*; here God describes the ground He made as the condition of visibility, and the Qur'an couples the two ideas in a single verse, so that the openness of the ground and the totality of knowledge are stated in one breath: *"He knows what enters the earth from it and what emerges from it, and what descends from the sky and what ascends in it; and He is with you wherever you are"* (57:4); and *"nothing, small or great, escapes Him"* (34:3). A plain that exposes is matched by a knowledge that misses nothing — and this is the second half of the answer to verse 13's question about weight: a God who is *there*, on the same ground, seeing what a person does where he thinks he is unobserved. Nūḥ's people, having plugged their ears, could not hear that either — which is why the sūrah moves next from the space of the earth to the paths within it, and from there, in verse 21, to the prophet's report to God that all of it was refused.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:20

> **so that you may travel through it in wide roads."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fajj: Roads That Open**

*Litaslukū minhā subulan fajjā*. The verb *salaka* means to enter into a way and go along it — and *sulūk*, "walking a path," is the very term the later spiritual literature adopted for the interior journey of the *sālik*, the one travelling toward God. The adjective *fajj* is the decisive word: in Arabic a *fajj* is a broad opening between two mountains, a gap through which a whole tribe and its animals can pass, and from the same root comes *al-fijāj*, the plural used for open passes. A *fajj* is not merely a road; it is a road that had to be *opened*, a constriction resolved. So the verse's picture is precise: God did not only spread the plain (v. 19) but cut through the obstacles, so that travel is not a negotiation with geography but a gift with engineering in it.

The Qur'an says the same in a verse that pairs these two sūrah-words exactly as they appear here:

> *"…He who made for you the earth as a cradle, and made for you in it paths (subulā), so that you might be guided."* (Qur'an 43:10)

and the pairing of "expanse and roads" with "guidance" is not accidental: in the sūrah of al-Zukhruf, as in ours, the roads are a *sign* whose function is to be read, not only to be used.

**Why Guidance Is Argued from Transportation**

Verses 10–20 have now run their full course: pardon → rain → wealth and children → gardens and rivers → the self in its stages → the heavens and the lights → growth from soil → return and egress → the spread earth → wide roads. Every item on that list is a *means*, not an end: rain for crops, children for continuity, gardens for food, bodies for existence, the earth for standing, roads for moving. The cumulative point of the eleven verses is that a Being who supplies the entire apparatus of a person's life — down to the opening in the mountain range through which his caravan passes — is not an abstraction to be dismissed but the actual condition of everything he can do. This is why Nūḥ's argument is aimed, as verse 13 announced, at *weight*: the road you take every week without thanks is a miracle you have normalised. Normalising the miracle is what makes God light.

The verse also closes, in the source commentary's observation, a parallel with the promise to the Prophet ﷺ's own people in al-Ṣaff: *"O you who believe! Shall I guide you to a trade that will save you from a painful punishment: you believe in God and His Messenger, and you struggle in God's way with your wealth and your selves"* (61:10–11). There, as in 71:10–12, forgiveness is followed by the promise of a return; but in al-Ṣaff the "trade" (*tijāra*) replaces gardens and rivers with a different asset class — the believer's own capital and body spent in a cause. Nūḥ's audience heard the argument in agrarian terms, and the Prophet's in mercantile terms; the structure — ask pardon, and what you have will be made productive — is identical, and the Makkan trader would have detected at once that verse 20 was addressed to him in his own professional vocabulary.

**Movement as a Religious Institution**

Practically, vv. 19–20 are the Qur'anic warrant for mobility — and Islam turned travel into ritual. The *ḥajj* is a journey legislated onto the body ("*and proclaim the pilgrimage to the people; they will come to you on foot and on every lean camel, arriving from every deep pass (fajj ʿammā)*" — 22:27; the word in that verse is precisely the same *fajj*, and its plural *fijāj* is what the commentators cite here). The seeking of knowledge is a journey: the Qur'an commands travel as a method of historical learning — *"Say: travel through the earth, then observe how the end of the deniers was"* (6:34; cf. 12:111; 30:9, 42; 47:10) — and the *hijra*, emigration from an environment that crushes conscience, is a duty rather than an option (4:97, 4:100). Even the mundane act of setting out received a fixed prayer in the Sunna, which is 71:19–20 turned into supplication:

> *"When the Messenger of God ﷺ mounted his camel to travel, he said: 'God is greatest, God is greatest, God is greatest. Glory to Him who has subjected this to us, and we could never have managed it; and to our Lord we will surely return.' And he said: 'O God, we ask You in this journey of ours for righteousness and God-fearing, and for a deed that pleases You. O God, ease it for us and shorten its distance. O God, You are the Companion on the journey and the Vicegerent over the family. … O God, I take refuge in You from the hardship of the road, from a change for the worse, and from finding, on returning home, evil in my family or my wealth.'"* (Muslim 1342, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar)

Notice what that prayer assumes: the mount is "subjected to us" — the whole capacity of travel is a loan — and the traveller is a man who will return and be questioned about what he did with the roads.

**The Wide Earth and the Narrow Heart**

The last implication of the verse is the saddest in the sūrah's argument section. The people of Nūḥ were not confined. There was space, there were routes, there were other peoples, other markets, other arguments, other marriages; the wide roads were the very structure of their economy. And yet they remained locked inside five ancestral names (v. 23), defending a cult as though the alternative were not a wider field but death. The pairing of vv. 19–20 with v. 23 is the sūrah's most devastating rhetorical cut: *the earth is an expanse with wide roads — and they said, "do not abandon Wadd, do not abandon Suwāʿ."* No external wall held them. The constriction was internal, and it was chosen. The same lesson is put in the Qur'an's most general psychological law about the effect of disbelief on a person's experience of space:

> *"And whoever God wills to guide, He opens his breast to submission; and whoever He wills to leave astray, He makes his breast narrow and constricted, as though he were climbing into the sky."* (Qur'an 6:125)

A man whose chest is a *fajj* walks an open earth; a man whose chest is a shaft finds the whole earth a cell. Verse 20 is the last word of the offer; verse 21 opens the report that it was all refused.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:21

> **Noah said, "My Lord, they disobeyed me and followed those whose wealth and children increase them in nothing but loss."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Is Over: The Shift to Reporting**

The eleven verses of invitation (10–20) close, and the sūrah turns from what Nūḥ said *to* the people to what he says *about* them before God: *qāla Nūḥun rabbi innahum ʿaṣawnī*. The structure of prophetic speech in the Qur'an almost always moves on this hinge — sermon, then supplication — and the movement is itself a teaching: when the address to human beings has been exhausted, the believer does not stop speaking; he changes the audience. Compare the outcome when the same transfer was made by Ibrāhīm: *"And when he had withdrawn from them and from what they worshipped besides God, We gave him Ishāq and Yaʿqūb, and each of them We made a prophet"* (Qur'an 19:49).

Note the two verbs of the verse and what each does. *ʿAṣawnī* — "they disobeyed me" — from *ʿ-ṣ-w*, whose field is stiffness and opposition: a *ʿaṣā* is a staff because it is a rigid piece of wood, and *maʿṣiyya* names the stiffening of the soul against a command. *Wattabaʿū* — "and they followed" — from *t-b-ʿ*, the root of footprints: to *ittabaʿa* is to place one's foot where another's foot has been. The pair is a perfect description of the social mechanics of unbelief: a decision to resist (vertical, against God) executed as a decision to imitate (horizontal, after one another). The Qur'an keeps the two together for the Day when they are unwound: *"They will say: our Lord, we obeyed our masters and our great ones, and they led us from the way. Our Lord, give them double punishment and a great curse"* (33:67–68).

**"Those Whose Wealth and Children Increase Him Only in Loss"**

The object of following is described not by name but by balance sheet: *man lam yazidhu māluhu wa-waladuhu illā khusrā*. The relative pronoun is singular — "whoever," "the man such-and-such" — because the type is what matters, not the individuals; and *khusr*, loss, is the vocabulary of trade, from *kha-sara*, to have one's capital diminished. That is exactly the idiom the Qur'an uses for the whole of a person's spiritual accounting:

> *"In the transaction they made no profit, and they were not guided."* (Qur'an 2:16)

> *"Truly those who recite the Book of God, and establish the prayer, and spend out of what We have provided them, secretly and openly, hope for a trade that will never perish (tijāratan lan tabūra) — that He may pay them their rewards in full and increase them of His bounty."* (Qur'an 35:29–30)

Verse 21's verdict on the rich patrons of disbelief is not that wealth is bad and poverty good. It is that *in the case of these men* the increase was only a loss — the same property, opposite valuation, decided by what it did to the owner's soul and to the people's judgement. The sūrah will state the outcome at verse 25: their gardens drowned. The Qur'an elsewhere puts the same reversal into a narrative, the parable of the two gardens, where the rich man's sentence is the sentence of Nūḥ's patrons: *"I do not think that this will ever perish, and I do not think the Hour will come; and if I am returned to my Lord, I will find something better than this in return"* (18:35–36) — and its ending: *"And his fruit were encompassed, so he began to wring his hands over what he had spent on it, while it lay collapsed upon its trellises, and he said: would that I had not associated anyone with my Lord!"* (18:42).

**Why the Crowd Follows the Wealthy**

The verse is a piece of social analysis, and it is worth spelling out the mechanism it assumes, because the sūrah does not editorialise. Three forces are named by the surrounding verses. First, *dependency*: the followers are not primarily persuaded, they are employed — the *malaʾ* of 11:27 complain precisely that Nūḥ's followers are "the meanest of us," i.e., that the wrong class has changed allegiance, and a class that changes allegiance has to be brought back by the people who own its livelihood. Second, *the appearance of divine favour*: wealth reads as endorsement, so the wealthy man's religion looks like the safe one; the Quraysh made this inference explicitly — *"we are more in wealth and children than you"* (34:35; 43:53, where Pharaoh's court says the same: *"why were not bracelets of gold cast down upon him, or angels sent with him?"*). Third, *the immunity illusion*: money buys protection from consequences in this world, so its owner appears to be a man whom reality does not touch, and to follow him feels like borrowing that immunity. The sūrah's answer to all three is the sūrah's plot: Nūḥ, the poor, mocked, single prophet, walks out of the water; the patrons of wealth walk out of nothing at all.

The Qur'an's own commentary on this verse is the pair of verses the source commentary cites, in which the wealth and children of the hostile rich are reclassified as the *instrument* of their punishment rather than the proof of their standing:

> *"Let not their wealth or their children impress you. God only intends to punish them through them in this world, and that their souls should depart while they are disbelievers."* (Qur'an 9:55; cf. 9:85)

And the Prophet ﷺ supplied the definition that keeps either asset from becoming an idol:in the definition that keeps wealth from becoming an idol:

> *"True richness is not the abundance of goods; richness is the richness of the soul."* (al-Bukhārī 6446; Muslim 1051, from Abū Hurayrah)

And in the report of Muʿādh ibn Jabal, the Prophet ﷺ set the claim of any patron to the test of reciprocal right — the issue verse 21 raises, since the rich man believes his position obliges others to follow him:

> *"… Do you know what is God's right over His servants? … that they worship God and associate nothing with Him. … Do you know what is the servants' right over God, if they do that? … that God does not punish them."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim 30, from Muʿādh ibn Jabal)

**"Increase Them in Nothing but Loss" — The Economics of Distraction**

The verb that the Qur'an uses for what wealth and children *do* to such a man is elsewhere *alwāh*: *"Let not your wealths and your children divert you from the remembrance of God"* (63:9); and the sūrah of "Rivalry" gives the mechanism in three lines that belong beside this verse: *"Rivalry in increase diverts you, until you visit the graves. No! You will know"* (102:1–3). The increase is real — more capital, more sons, more name — and it is precisely the reality of the increase that diverts: attention is a finite endowment, and whatever absorbs it becomes, in the Qur'an's analysis, a loss-bearing investment. The Makkan listener, for whom *māl* (wealth) was literally "that to which the heart inclines" (*māla*, to lean), would have recognised the phrasing: a thing the heart leans on will, if it is not God, fall with the heart on top of it.

**What the Prophet Is Allowed to Say to God**

Finally, notice the propriety of the report. Nūḥ does not say "my Lord, they were evil and I am righteous"; he states the facts of the transaction — they disobeyed, they followed the rich, and the rich are losers — and lets the Judge draw the conclusions. The precision of the complaint matters because it will be quoted on a Day when vague accusations are inadmissible. This is the sūrah's second lesson in grief-management after verse 5: speak truly about people in front of God, not bitterly about God in front of people. And the very next line — *"and they devised a mighty plot"* — shows that Nūḥ is also naming, without embellishment, the one thing his audience had not yet done in the report: organise.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:22

> **And they devised a mighty plot.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa-Makarū Makran Kubbārā: The Grammar of a Conspiratorial Verb**

The root *m-k-r* denotes turning a matter around so that it arrives from an unexpected direction; al-Rāghib al-Asfahānī defines it exactly: *makr* is "the turning of another from his intended course by a ruse" — a plan that works by not announcing itself. The noun *makr* in the Qur'an is used on two levels: the private scheming of individuals and the public, organised design of groups against truth. Its intensity here is carried by a rare morphological form: *kubbārā*, from *k-b-r*, on the intensive pattern *fuʿʿāl* which Arabic reserves for settled, large-scale qualities (*ṭuwāl*, *ʿujāj*, *subār*). The word means "vast, enormously great," and the choice is deliberate: the sūrah does not say "they plotted greatly" but "they plotted *a plot of a magnitude*." A single indefinite noun in the accusative — *makran* — with the adjective made enormous, is the whole sentence: the crime is not a thought, it is a project.

**From Refusal to Organisation: The Moral Threshold**

Read the sūrah's sequence and the point is unavoidable. Verses 5–9 describe a people who *avoided*: they fled, plugged their ears, hid their faces, and stayed home. Verse 21 describes a people who *chose leaders* over the prophet. Verse 22 describes a people who *built something*: a plot. The escalation is not from unbelief to stronger unbelief but from a private state to a public structure — and in the Qur'an's moral physics that crossing changes everything, because a private refusal harms its owner, while a plot harms others and therefore has to be answered. The same escalation gives the Makkan suras their realism about institutions: *"And thus We made for every prophet an enemy — the devils of humankind and jinn, some of them inspiring to others adornment of speech, to delude them"* (6:112); *"and the Pharaoh said: O chiefs, I know no god for you besides me"* (28:38).

The tradition's account of what the "mighty plot" consisted of is unsentimental. The people's words to Nūḥ, in the parallel passages, are a plan of suppression: *"If you do not desist, O Nūḥ, you will certainly be among the stoned"* (26:116); and the source commentary transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās the more detailed report that when Nūḥ preached, they would strike him until he lost consciousness, wrap him in a cloth, and leave him in his house for dead — and that when he came to himself he went out to preach again. That report is preserved in the exegetical collections (Ibn Abī Ḥātim and others) rather than in the canonical ḥadīth books; it is quoted here because it makes plain what *makran kubbārā* means in practice — not debate, not ridicule, but violence organised enough to look like an accident and sustained long enough to be a policy.

**God's Counter-Plot: How "Makr" Is Predicated of God**

The verse does not describe God's response here, but the Qur'an does, repeatedly, and it uses the same word:

> *"They plotted, and God plotted, and God is the best of plotters."* (Qur'an 3:54)

> *"They plotted their plot, and with God is their plot, even if their plot were such that the mountains should be removed by it."* (Qur'an 14:46)

> *"And they devised a design, and God devised a design, while they perceived not."* (Qur'an 27:50)

> *"And they plot, and I plot (amkuru)."* (Qur'an 86:15–16)

This is one of the classical questions of divine-attribute theology, since "plotting" in creatures means deception, and deception is impossible of God. Al-Ṭabarī and the Ashʿarī and Māturīdī exegetes give the same answer: the word is used of God by *congruence of naming* (mujānasa al-ism, or what the jurists call *majāz* in the wide sense), because the *outcome* is alike — the plan of the plotter is turned back upon him from a direction he did not reckon — while the *means* differ utterly, since God is not obliged to proceed by concealment or by trickery, and knows what He discloses. Al-Rāzī formulates it: God's *makr* is "the bringing of His decree upon them from where they do not perceive it," and it is a perfection because it displays sovereignty, not because it displays cunning. The sūrah's own version of the outcome is verse 25: the plotters were drowned; the plot against one preacher ended with the whole civilisation's water.

The pastoral point, which the Makkan audience needed most, is in 14:46's half-clause "even if their plot were such that the mountains should be removed by it." A plot can be genuinely enormous and still be, in the scale on which it will actually be judged, negligible — which is why the Qur'an forbids believers to be intimidated by organised opposition: *"God is sufficient for you against those who ridicule"* (15:95, abridged), and *"…and do not fear them, but fear Me, if you are believers"* (3:175).

**The Plot's Targets, and How Plots Dress**

Verse 22's third word, "*kubbārā*," is worth holding up to modern eyes because the great plots of every century have rarely announced themselves as plots. They announce themselves as *pragmatism*, as *public order*, as *the consensus of the notables*. The Qur'an describes the equipment: *"Those who purchase with God's covenant and their oaths a small price — it is they who have no share in the Hereafter"* (3:77); *"and of the people there is he whose speech of the worldly life pleases you, and he calls God to witness what is in his heart, while he is the most fierce of opponents"* (2:204); *"when he turns away he strives upon the earth to cause corruption therein and to destroy cultivation and progeny"* (2:205). That last clause — cultivation and progeny, *ḥarth* and *nasl* — is the exact inventory of everything Nūḥ's people were protecting when they plotted, and everything verse 12 had promised to give them if they would only ask pardon. Plots against truth are, in this sūrah's ledger, attacks on farming and on children: they are, literally, the destruction of the next generation's food and the next generation's faith.

The believers' obligation, on the strength of these verses, is not to be out-plotted but to be out-*patient*. Nūḥ's reply to a *makran kubbārā* was not a counter-conspiracy; it was verse 23's exposure of the plot's real object — the five gods — followed by the plain prayer of verses 26–28. To name what an organised opposition is actually protecting is, in the Qur'an's method, the strongest available weapon against it, because a plot requires the appearance of neutrality to survive, and an exposure removes it.

**A Last Word on the Scale of "Mighty"**

The sūrah leaves one detail unexplained, and its absence is instructive: it never says the plot succeeded. It says the plot was *mighty* — and then jumps to the drowning. Mightiness in the arithmetic of this world buys time, not results. Nūḥ's people held the apparatus of coercion for nine and a half centuries and lost it in a rainstorm, and the preacher they battered unconscious was the one who walked out of the account with the title by which a whole sūrah is named.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:23

> **saying, "Do not leave your gods; do not leave Wadd, nor Suwāʿ, nor Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, nor Nasr."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Plot's Actual Text: Five Names**

Verse 22 said there was a *makran kubbārā*; verse 23 quotes it. A mighty plot, it turns out, has as its operative slogan five prohibitions and a possessive pronoun: *lā tattariḍunna ālihata-kum* — "do not, absolutely, abandon your gods." The verb *taraḍḍa* — to leave, to forsake, to cease to care for — is a rare word in the Qur'anic lexicon, and it is used here twice within a single prohibition, each time with the oath-strengthening *nūn al-tawkīd* attached: "you will by no means abandon." The doubling of verb and negation is the sound of a community holding its breath around a possession. And the gods are *their own* — *ālihata-kum* — a genitive that the sūrah keeps for the polytheists' speech (vv. 23, 28) while Nūḥ always says "God." Ownership is the tell: a god you own is an extension of yourself.

That the manifesto is a list of five proper names is the verse's most quoted characteristic, and the reason is not curiosity. Nowhere else in the Qur'an does it preserve the personal names of idols of a pre-Abrahamic people; in the Meccan material the gods are named (al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt — 53:19–23), but among Nūḥ's people the naming does something else: it makes the *history* of the gods available to inspection. Names have biographies; the verse is about to be explained by a ḥadīth that supplies them.

**The Ḥadīth That Explains the Mechanism**

Al-Bukhārī transmits in his Book of Exegesis, from Ibn ʿAbbās, on this very verse:

> *"These were the names of righteous men of the people of Nūḥ. When they died, Satan inspired their people to place, in the gatherings where they used to sit, statues of them, and to name those statues with their names. They did not worship them — until when that first generation perished and knowledge was effaced, they were worshipped."* (al-Bukhārī 4920)

The wording preserved by the exegetes, including Ibn Kathīr, adds that the five were men who had many followers, and that the shapes given them were chosen to flatter their admirers: al-Zamakhsharī records the traditions that Wadd was represented as a man, Suwāʿ as a woman, Yaghūth as a bull or a lion, Yaʿūq as a horse, and Nasr as an eagle or vulture. In the early histories of Arabian cult — the notices attached to Ibn ʿAbbās's report and Ibn al-Kalbī's *Kitāb al-Asnām* — the five had by the Qur'an's own time become the patrons of named tribes: Wadd of Kalb, Suwāʿ of Hudhayl, Yaghūth of Murād, Yaʿūq of Hamdān, and Nasr of Ḥimyar, which is why the source commentary rightly speaks of "an implicit parallel to the idols of the Makkans": the Makkan hearer was not listening about archaeology; he was hearing his own neighbourhood's cult described by its genealogy.

Read the ḥadīth as a law of history rather than an anecdote, because it is the sūrah's explanation of how verse 21's followers become verse 23's worshippers. Five steps are described. (i) There are *ṣāliḥūn*, righteous men, with real virtue and real influence. (ii) They die. (iii) The grief-stricken are moved to preserve *the places where they used to sit* — the cult begins as a memorial to a habit, which is the most human of impulses. (iv) The memorials receive the *names* of the men, so that they remain emotionally legible. (v) The generation that knew the men "perishes and knowledge is effaced" — and now the object, detached from the biography, is worshipped. Nothing was added at any stage except memory, and everything was lost by losing it.

**"Until Knowledge Was Effaced": The Epistemology of Drift**

The clause *thumma nusikha al-ʿilm* — "until knowledge was abrogated/effaced" — is the hinge of the whole account, and it is why the Prophet ﷺ legislated directly against the first three steps. The danger was not stone; it was forgetting, and forgetting is what monuments do to the third generation. Hence the prohibitions in the authentic reports, stated with a clarity that needs no interpretation:

> *"God's curse upon the Jews and the Christians: they took the graves of their prophets as places of worship."* He said this in his final illness, warning against what they had done; and in the wording transmitted from ʿĀ'isha: "do not make a grave into a place of prostration, for I forbid that." Jundub ibn ʿAbdillāh reported the same prohibition in the Prophet's ﷺ last sermon: "Whoever among you is taking an intimate friend, let him take none but God as an intimate friend — and do not take graves as places of worship." (al-Bukhārī 444; Muslim 529; Muslim 531–532)*

Beside the prohibition stands the positive form of the same discipline: *"Do not make my grave a festival (ʿīd); send blessing upon me wherever you are, for my blessing reaches me"* (Abū Dāwūd 2042, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr — its chain is debated, while the discipline it protects is precisely the one verse 23 exposes).

The mechanism the verse exposes is therefore not confined to statues. Any community that keeps honouring a founder without remembering *why* he was honoured is repeating steps (i)–(v), whether the object is a shrine, a party, a family name, an institution, or an idea that has been detached from the argument that made it. The sūrah's point is severe: the distance between venerating a good man and worshipping him is exactly one generation of not-teaching.

**Why the Idols Are Named: A Polemic in Five Words**

The five names themselves carry the argument. Whatever their philological details, they sit in the vocabulary of human want: *Wadd* from *w-d-d*, affection, the beloved; *Yaghūth* from *gh-w-th*, relief, aid, the crying-out for rescue (al-ghayth is rain itself, the relief of the drought-struck — and the sūrah has just promised *midrār* at verse 11); *Yaʿūq*, connected by the lexicographers with *ʿ-w-q*, hindrance, the warding off of an enemy; *Nasr*, victory, from *n-ṣ-r*. Even *Suwāʿ*, whose form is debated, belongs to the same register of hoped-for fortune. So the list is an inventory: love, beauty, rescue, protection, triumph. Five things every human being wants — and five things Nūḥ's people had attached to the memorials of dead men rather than to the Living One who had just, in verses 10–12, offered to give them all five on the condition of asking pardon. This is why the sūrah can move from verse 23's list straight to verse 25's drowning: the same people who would not surrender the gods of *relief* perished in water from which no Wadd came to rescue them.

That reading is confirmed by the way the Qur'an disposes of the Meccan trio in the parallel passage, since the criticism of the five idols is not about their power but about their status as names:

> *"They are nothing but names which you have named, you and your fathers — God has sent down for them no authority."* (Qur'an 53:23)

To name a thing is not to create it, and the sūrah is making an epistemological demand of its audience in the same breath that it names their gods: give the gods the *description* they actually have, and they dissolve.

**"Do Not Leave Your Gods" — The Social Function of a Cult**

Notice, lastly, what the exhortation does *not* say. It does not offer arguments for the gods' divinity; it forbids defection. *Lā tattariḍunna* is a loyalty oath in the shape of a sentence, and it comes with the reason in the preceding verse: the *malaʾ* whose wealth and children were loss. The gods, in other words, are the constitution of a class order. To "leave" them is not to change a belief but to dissolve the ritual scaffolding of a hierarchy — which is why the response to Nūḥ is not refutation but stoning (26:116) and a *mighty plot* (v. 22). The verse therefore supplies the sūrah's sociological conclusion, and it is the one the Makkan hearers were least able to deny: they too were being asked not to "leave their gods," and they too were finding that the gods were load-bearing.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:24

> **And many indeed have they led astray. And increase not the wrongdoers except in error.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict on the Gods: Not That They Are Weak, but That They Have Worked**

*Wa-qad aḍallū kathīrā*. The clause is a finding of fact, not of theology: the five idols — or rather the human institutions those names had become — "have led *many* astray." The verb is the causative of *ḍ-l-l*, *aḍalla*, "to cause to lose the way," and *kathīrā* is an object whose quantity is left open on purpose: many of their own generation, many of the descendants, many of the Arabs who inherited the cult, and many of anyone whose conscience has ever been settled by an ancestral name rather than by evidence. The source commentary notes both applications (al-Zamakhsharī takes it as "they led many astray" in the sense of misleading others; others read it as "they persisted in much error"), and the two are the same reality viewed from either side: a people who are lost become, by the fact of their numbers and their prestige, a loss for others.

Note also that the sūrah has already specified the *mechanism* at verse 21: the people did not fall into error so much as they *attached themselves* to the men whose wealth and children were loss. Verse 24 completes that diagnosis with its consequence for the wider population. Misguidance, in this sūrah, is not primarily a doctrine; it is a sociology.

**"Increase Not the Wrongdoers Except in Error" — A Supplication or a Statement?**

The second clause — *wa-lā tazid al-ẓālimīna illā ḍalālā* — is grammatically an imperative with a negative particle, and the exegetes therefore divide: is it Nūḥ's *duʿāʾ* ("my Lord, do not increase…") or a *khabar*, a statement of divine practice ("and He only increases the wrongdoers in error")? Al-Ṭabarī transmits both, and the difference in sense is small, because the sūrah has already framed the section with Nūḥ speaking to God (v. 21's *qāla Nūḥun rabbi*), and because a prayer of this kind is the verbal form of a law. What is not small, and what the verse is really saying, is that the *request is for more of what is already happening*. Nūḥ does not ask that God punish them; he asks that God not intervene to soften what their own chosen direction is producing.

That is the most difficult-sounding idea in the sūrah, and the tradition's treatment of "increase in error" is careful about it. The principle is stated in the Qur'an as a *reciprocity*, not as arbitrariness:

> *"So when they deviated, God deviated their hearts; and God does not guide a wrongdoing people."* (Qur'an 61:5)

> *"And among those of the jinn and humankind We have prepared for Hell many: they have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, ears with which they do not hear. They are like cattle — nay, they are further astray (aḍall). It is they who are the heedless."* (Qur'an 7:179)

The classical theodicists (al-Rāzī at length; Ibn Taymiyyah in his refutations of both the determinists and the libertarian *Qadariyya*; Ibn al-Qayyim in *al-Fawāʾid*, where the maxim *"the recompense is of the same kind as the deed"* — *al-jazāʾ min jins al-ʿamal* — is a recurring structural claim) all converge on the same formulation: divine "increase" is a *handing over*. God does not insert an alien error into a soul that has been working at its own; He removes the restraint the soul has been asking to be rid of. That is why the same phrase can be a supplication and a statement of law at once: the prophet asks for the cessation of a mercy the people have been spending, and the cessation is exactly what the people's conduct means.

**Why the Request Follows Verse 23**

Structurally, verse 24 is the answer to verse 23's "do not leave your gods." The chiefs' injunction has, in the sūrah's reckoning, produced the *adhlāl* of "many"; the prophet who spent nine centuries on their individuals now hands the collective result to God. And he does so in a form the Qur'an reserves for cases in which guidance has become an irritant to the hearer. Mūsā's prayer in Sūrah Yūnus is the parallel, and it is harsher in wording and identical in logic:

> *"Our Lord, wipe out their wealth and seal upon their hearts, so that they do not believe until they see the painful punishment."* (Qur'an 10:88)

Both prayers are answered by the same kind of event (water for one people, water for the other — Pharaoh's army drowned, 10:90–92), and both are preceded by explicit divine notice (for Nūḥ: 11:36; 23:27). The point the commentators draw is that these are not fits of prophetic temper but *judicial motions*: the case has been tried for centuries, the evidence of refusal is on the record, and the petitioner asks for the sentence the defendants have written for themselves.

**The Weight of Leading Many**

Verse 24's first clause — *many* — attaches a scale of responsibility that the sūrah does not let anyone dodge, and the Prophet ﷺ stated the principle in a form that turns it into a personal risk assessment:

> *"Whoever calls to guidance, he has a reward like the rewards of those who follow him, without their rewards being diminished in anything; and whoever calls to misguidance, he has sins like the sins of those who follow him, without their sins being diminished in anything."* (Muslim 2674, from Abū Hurayrah)

The logic is not a punishment invented for preachers; it is an accurate description of what influence does. Every teacher, parent, editor, platform, party chief, and pious figure in a Muslim's life is issuing causes whose effects accumulate in accounts not their own — which is why the sūrah says of the leaders of Nūḥ's people that they will carry, on top of their own, *"the burdens of those they lead to the Fire without their burdens being diminished at all"* (28:41, abridged; cf. 16:25: *"so that they may carry their burdens entire on the Day of Resurrection, and something of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge. How evil is what they load!"*).

**Reading the Verse Against "Nobody Was Harmed"**

Modern moral vocabularies often treat misguidance as a non-offence, since each person is "free to think for himself." This sūrah refuses that: the idols' harm is counted *collectively*, and the plea "they led many astray" is entered as a charge. The charge is realistic in a way that should be uncomfortable. Ideas are not received by isolated rational agents; they are received inside networks of prestige, and a person in a powerful position who adopts a falsehood converts it into an environment for hundreds of people who never chose it. The famous Makkan scene belongs here: when Abū Ṭālib died and the Prophet's ﷺ protection weakened, the pressure was not a new argument but a changed social weather — exactly the mechanism verse 21–24 describe. And the Prophet ﷺ's own warning about the smallest version of the same mechanism is the standard:

> *"Whoever establishes in Islam a good practice, he has its reward and the reward of those who act by it after him, without their rewards being diminished; and whoever establishes in Islam an evil practice, he has its sin and the sin of those who act by it after him, without their sins being diminished."* (Muslim 1017, from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh)

**The Two Halves Held Together**

Finally, notice that verse 24 does not end in despair. Its second clause hands the matter to God precisely because it cannot be settled by human pressure — you cannot un-mislead a multitude by shouting; you can only present, again and again, in the ways verses 8–9 describe. And at the same time, the verse is the sūrah's final word about the *misleaders*, before which it will speak only about the *drowned*. That transition — from the analysis of influence to the fact of the water — is verse 25, and it arrives without a single additional argument.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:25

> **Because of their sins they were drowned; then they were made to enter a Fire. And they found no helpers besides God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse With No Rhetoric Left in It**

The chapter's argument section closes, and the sentence of execution occupies one verse of eleven words. *Mimmā khaṭīʾātin ughriqū fa-udkhilū nāran fa-lam yajidū lahum min dūni Allāhi anṣārā*. Four facts, no adjectives, no appeal to the hearer, no invitation: *because of their sins — they were drowned — they were entered into a Fire — they found no helpers.* Even the sūrah's long drumming rhyme in *-rā* is now doing the work of a hammer instead of a summons, and the last word of the sentence is not "mercy" but "helpers," in the negative.

The opening preposition is one of the grammatical problems the verse is famous for. On the ordinary reading, *min* here is causal — "on account of," a use the Kufan grammarians (al-Farrāʾ among them) defend with parallel constructions in Arabic poetry, and al-Ṭabarī glosses accordingly: *"they were drowned because of their sins."* On another reading the *min* is redundant (a *zāʾida* letter used for verification), so the sense is *"for their sins they were drowned."* Both readings converge on the verse's thesis: the sentence is *retributive and specific*. The flood is not a natural catastrophe that happened to catch a people; it is an act with a named legal ground. The word for that ground is also careful: *khaṭīʾāt*, the faults and misses of *kha-ṭ-ʾ*, the same root as the *khaṭīʾa* of 20:121 and 7:23 — the sin as an *error of aim*. Not crimes of passion, but shots deliberately not taken at the target that had been held up for nine and a half centuries.

**The Divine Passive: Why No Agent Is Named**

Every verb of punishment in the verse is passive: *ughriqū*, *udkhilū*. This is the well-known *mabnī li-mā lam yusammā fāʿiluhu*, the passive of undisclosed agent, and the Qur'an uses it when it wants the mechanism removed from the discussion. The water is not the subject of the sentence; the Fire is not the subject. What is asserted is that they were *made* to be drowned and *made* to enter — and the elements themselves appear elsewhere in the Qur'an as things that receive orders rather than give them, and for Ibrāhīm, in the very idiom of elements obeying, *"We said: O fire, be coolness and peace upon Ibrāhīm"* (21:69). The same physics that would have carried Nūḥ's ark carried the drowners, because neither water nor fire has any independent power. The command that stops the water is addressed to both elements directly: *"And it was said: O earth, swallow your water, and O sky, hold back! And the matter was decided, and the vessel settled on al-Jūdī"* (11:44). And *"Do you see the fire you kindle — is it you who grow its tree, or are We the Growers?"* (56:71–72). This is why the sūrah's three exhibits (self, sky, soil) were not decoration: the people who drowned were drowned by the very rain Nūḥ had promised them as a mercy in verse 11.

**"Fa-Udkhilū Nāran": The Conjunction of Immediacy, and the Interval**

The verse's two connectives are doing theology. *Fa-udkhilū* — "and thereupon they were entered" — uses the *fāʾ* of sequence, indicating no gap between drowning and Fire: no interval of oblivion, no waiting room, no escape of sleep. The commentators read this as the fire of the *barzakh*, the intermediate existence between death and resurrection, and the verse they cite is that of Pharaoh's people, who are likewise shown their punishment before the Hour:

> *"The Fire, they are exposed to it morning and evening; and on the Day when the Hour arises: enter the people of Pharaoh into the severest punishment."* (Qur'an 40:46)

Note too that all three clauses are joined by the same *fāʾ* — drowned, *thereupon* entered into Fire, *thereupon* left without helpers — whereas the sūrah has elsewhere used *thumma*, the conjunction of long interval, for its own slow phases (vv. 8, 9, 18). The grammar is itself an argument: the punishment contains no pauses. For a people that had nine and a half centuries of *thumma*, the last three clauses of verse 25 take none.

**"No Helpers Besides God": And the Name That Mocks Them**

*Anṣār* is the plural of *naṣīr*, a helper or protector — from *n-ṣ-r*, the root that also names *Naṣr*, one of the five gods listed at verse 23. The sūrah has already arranged the irony: a people who swore not to abandon "Victory" arrive at a moment where there is no *naṣr* of any kind, from any direction, from any being. This is not wordplay for its own sake; it is the verse's argument, and the Qur'an states it as a general condition:

> *"There is no intimate friend for the wrongdoers, nor any intercessor whose word is obeyed."* (Qur'an 40:18; cf. 2:254; 3:192)

> *"And those who were followed disavowed those who followed, and they saw the punishment, and the ties between them were cut. And the followers said: would that we had a return, so that we could disavow them as they have disavowed us."* (Qur'an 2:166–167)

Two protections human beings always count on are cancelled here: the protection of *power* (the notables of verse 21, whose wealth is now underwater) and the protection of *kinship* (the family of verse 28, from which one son was severed: *"he is not of your family; it is a deed not righteous"* — 11:46). What remains, in the sūrah's arithmetic, is nothing but the relation between a soul and its Maker — and it was precisely that relation the whole sūrah had been negotiating.

**Why the Sūrah Skips the Ark**

The source commentary's note deserves emphasis: this sūrah, alone among the major Nūḥ passages, mentions neither the ark nor the rescue. Elsewhere the vessel is named (*al-falak*, 29:15; 54:13; 11:37–44; 23:26–29), described as *"a thing of planks and nails, sailing under Our eyes"* (54:13–14), and the salvation is spelled out: *"So We opened the gates of the sky with water pouring down, and We made the earth gush with springs, and the water met for an affair already decreed. And We carried him upon a thing of planks and nails"* (54:11–13). Here the believer's rescue is deliberately absent, and the omission is the last rhetorical instrument in the chapter: the Makkan hearers are being made to stand, for twenty-five verses, on the side of the sentence without the ark, so that the offer of verses 10–12 will read, on the next hearing, as the only available door. The sūrah will open that door in its final verse — and open it with the word *entry*: *"whoever enters my house as a believer"* (71:28). The drowned were *made to enter* a Fire; the living are *invited to enter* a house. The same root *d-kh-l* decides the difference between the two, and the difference is that one entry is chosen and the other is administered.

**What Remains for the Reader**

Verse 25 has no moral exhortation in it, and that is its moral. The Qur'an does not say "and so fear the water"; it reports that a nine-century opportunity ended in a single compound sentence of three clauses: cause, drowning, exposure. Everything the sūrah needs to say about how such an end is produced has already been said — the plugged ears (v. 7), the escalation refused (vv. 8–9), the pardon declined (vv. 10–12), the weightless God (v. 13), the borrowed prestige (v. 21), and the plot (v. 22). The reader is left with a list of small, ordinary, datable decisions, and the verse's implication is that no other kind of decision exists.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:26

> **Noah said, "My Lord, leave not upon the earth a single dweller of the disbelievers,"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prayer in the Form of a Prohibition**

*Qāla Nūḥun rabbi lā tadhar ʿalā al-arḍi mina al-kāfirīna dayyārā*. Grammatically it is a negative imperative addressed to God — the standard Arabic form of supplication for what the supplicant wants *prevented* — and its plain sense is total: do not leave, on the earth, *a single one who dwells* of the denying ones. The prophets' prayers of destruction are never, in the Qur'an, the first word about a people; they are the last, and the sūrah's ordering preserves the rule: nine centuries of calling (vv. 5–9), a full offer of pardon and prosperity (vv. 10–12), four arguments (vv. 13–20), a judicial report (vv. 21–24), the sentence already executed (v. 25) — and only then the prayer.

The word *dayyār* is the sūrah's most argued noun. It is on the intensive pattern *faʿʿāl* from *d-y-r*, "dwelling," and the commentators list the possible senses: any dweller, any household, any living being that moves about (*yaḍrūbi fī al-arḍ*); al-Ṭabarī transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās and others *nafsāʾ* (a soul) and *ahl dār* (the people of a dwelling); some grammarians derive it from *al-dawr*, circling, as in the traders' *ṭawwāf* — "one who goes around the earth" — hence "anyone at all who remains to move about." The intensity of the pattern is the point in every reading: Nūḥ asks that not so much as the *name* of them be left.

**Why He Prays This: The Reason Given in the Next Verse**

Nothing in the sūrah makes the prayer less severe, and nothing in the sūrah makes it vindictive either, because the reason is supplied immediately in verse 27 — "they will mislead Your servants." The petitioner does not say "they hurt me"; he says "they will go on hurting those who are Yours." A prayer for annihilation that is grounded in the protection of future worshippers is, within this sūrah's own logic, an act of *naṣīḥa* extended forward in time: he is still warning, now on behalf of the unborn.

The legitimacy of such a prayer rests, however, on something beyond motive, and the Qur'an states it in the parallel passages. Nūḥ was told before he asked:

> *"And it was revealed to Nūḥ: none of your people will believe besides the one who has already believed — so do not ask Me concerning what they do wrong. And address Me not on behalf of those who did wrong; they shall be drowned."* (Qur'an 11:36–37; 23:27)

That is the decisive background to verse 26: the prayer is not an innovation in policy but an *assent* to a sentence God had already announced, and it is even preceded by a divine rebuke about a *different* request — the one for his own son (11:45–47). Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both treat the two episodes together, and the combination is what makes the sūrah's ethics of supplication intelligible: a prophet may ask for the removal of those who destroy God's servants, and he may not ask for the rescue of a single wrongdoer once the sentence has passed; the difference is not sentiment but jurisdiction. The whole exchange is framed by the verse the Makkan audience was given as the limit on their own prophet's impatience:

> *"Not for you is the decision at all; whether He turn to them in mercy, or punish them — they are wrongdoers."* (Qur'an 3:128)

**The Prophet's Contrast at Ṭāʾif**

The contrast between Nūḥ's final prayer and the Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ conduct at the worst point of his own Makkan mission is preserved in the collections, and the sūrah is much illuminated by it. On the way back from Ṭāʾif, wounded and stoned by his own kinsmen, he was visited by the angel of the mountains, who offered: if you wish, I will close the two mountains upon them. He said:

> *"No. I hope that God will bring from their loins people who worship God alone, associating nothing with Him."* (al-Bukhārī 3371; Muslim 2333)

His general manner is fixed by reports the community cites beside such prayers: *"The believer is not one who abuses, nor one who curses, nor one obscene, nor foul-mouthed"* (al-Tirmidhī, from Abū al-Dardāʾ — graded ḥasan gharīb), and *"Make things easy and do not make them hard"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Anas). And yet the same man, when the massacre of the seventy reciters at Biʾr Maʿūna broke him, stood in prayer and pronounced a month of *qunūt* against the tribes that had betrayed the truce (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah). The two halves of that practice — patience until the door closes, then precise imprecation, then acceptance of whatever is revealed — are the sūrah's own shape. Nūḥ's prayer is therefore not a licence for rage; it is the formal, final, post-trial motion that the Qur'an permits only to those who have exhausted every other channel, and only after the sentence has been announced from above.

**The Question of the Flood's Extent**

Verse 26 is the single verse in the Qur'anic Nūḥ corpus that presses toward a *universal* flood, and the source commentary's note is exact: every other passage qualifies the drowned by their conduct (those who *"denied Our signs"* — 7:64; 10:73; those who *"did wrong"* — 11:37; 23:27; *"an evil people"* — 21:77), never by a statement that all humanity perished. The tension with Genesis 6–9, where the destruction is explicitly global, has produced three positions in the Islamic tradition. (i) The flood was universal: Nūḥ was sent to all peoples (a view the source commentary attributes to some, along with the claim that he was the first messenger sent to the whole earth), and verse 26 means what it says. (ii) The flood was local: the prayer concerned *"the earth"* known to Nūḥ — *arḍ* in Arabic being usable for "the land of," and a report to that effect being transmitted in the early exegetical corpora; on this view the other Qur'anic passages' qualification by deeds shows that only a people, not the species, was destroyed. (iii) Agnostic suspension: the text affirms the sentence on a people and says nothing that obliges a cosmographic claim.

A sound judgement has to weigh a further fact: the sūrah's own silence about the ark, and the other accounts' silence about any post-flood repopulation problem. If the flood were universal, the entire human race after Nūḥ would descend from the few households of the ark (a genealogy the Qur'an never asserts, though it does say *"We made his progeny the ones who remained"* — 37:77), while the Qur'an elsewhere addresses every people as though their descent were independent. The dispute is legitimately open, and nothing in the chapter's moral argument turns on it. The verse's function is not to teach hydrology; it is to teach that a civilisation's account can be closed, and that even a prophet does not decide when.

**What the Believer Is Meant to Feel**

Two responses to verse 26 are equally wrong: reading it as a model for cursing one's opponents, or quietly setting it aside as a primitive text. The sūrah itself supplies the correction: the prayer is uttered by a man who had asked forgiveness for the same people's sins in verse 4 and had wanted them pardoned in verse 7 (*"so that You might forgive them"*), and who will, three verses later, ask forgiveness for *all* the believers he does not know by name (v. 28). A prayer for destruction from such a mouth is not hatred; it is the last possible form of the seriousness with which he took their souls. Its religious function for the reader is the mirror: the sūrah wants me to see what nine and a half centuries of refused mercy does to a people — and to understand that if the prayer is frightening, the thing that made it necessary is more frightening still.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:27

> **Truly if You leave them, they will mislead Your servants and beget nothing but a profligate ingrate."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Reason Offered to God**

*Innaka in tadharhum yuḍillū ʿibādaka wa-lā yalidū illā fājiran kaffārā*. The verse is a conditional sentence with an emphatic *inna*, and its function is to give the ground of the preceding request. Verse 26 asked; verse 27 argues. This is a detail of enormous consequence for Islamic ethics: the prophet does not present his wish as a wish. He presents it as a forecast about harm — *they will mislead* — and about the future persons who will carry that harm forward — *they will beget*. The Qur'an presents God accepting reasons from His servants in prayer, and the etiquette of supplication that the commentators derive from this is that a request should be joined to a consideration (*manaṭ*) that makes it intelligible: "forgive me, *for* You are the Forgiving," "provide for me, *for* You are the Provider." Here it is: "do not leave them, *because* they will corrupt."

**"Your Servants" — the Victim Counted, Not the Partisan**

The first word of the harm is *ʿibādaka*, "Your servants." Nūḥ does not say "they will mislead *my* followers," which would have been true and would have made the request a quarrel between factions. He says *Your* servants, placing the endangered party under God's ownership, and thus under a jurisdiction he is asking God to exercise. Al-Rāzī draws the lesson explicitly: the leader of the believers grieves for the servants of God at large, not for the size of his own camp — a standard the Prophet ﷺ stated in the plainest possible terms when he said:

> *"The best of people are the most beneficial to people."* (transmitted by al-Ḥākim and Aḥmad, from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh; its chain is discussed, and it is quoted here for its sense, which is exactly this verse's)

and when he made the concern for others' faith the test of love of one's own: *"None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself"* (al-Bukhārī 13; Muslim 45). Nūḥ's prayer passes that test at the moment when he is entitled to be angry for his own sake: nine centuries of being called a liar, and he is thinking about *God's* servants whom these men would still corrupt.

**The Second Harm: Begetting**

*Wa-lā yalidū illā fājiran kaffārā* — "and they beget nothing but one profligate, ungrateful." The verb *walada* is used here in the active, *yalīdū*: the emphasis is not on the birth but on what is *produced* — and in Arabic usage that combination (beget + a moral description) already signals that the offspring in question are being characterised by their formation, not their biology. The verse's claim is a sociological one: a culture that has spent a millennium hardening itself against a clear warner does not produce, in its children, neutral adults who will choose freely; it produces inheritors of a defended error.

This is the reason the sūrah's whole middle section — the argument from *stages* (v. 14) — matters here. A human being is made in phases, and one of the phases is being raised. The Qur'an states the mechanism of inheritance as plainly as anywhere in the Book:

> *"Every child is born upon disposition (fiṭrah); then his parents make him a Jew, or a Christian, or a Magian."* (al-Bukhārī 1358; Muslim 2658, from Abū Hurayrah)

And it commands the opposite discipline, addressed to the believers rather than to the prophets:

> *"O you who believe! Guard yourselves and your households from a Fire…"* (Qur'an 66:6)
> *"And command your family with the prayer, and be steadfast in it."* (Qur'an 20:132)

The force of verse 27's second clause is therefore *forward-looking* — an anxiety about what the next generation will inherit — and the Prophet ﷺ made that same forward-looking responsibility the mark of every position of authority:

> *"Every one of you is a shepherd, and every one of you is responsible for his flock."* (al-Bukhārī 893; Muslim 1829, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar)

Nūḥ, speaking for a whole people's children, is behaving as a shepherd of a flock not yet born.

**"Fājir" and "Kaffār": The Two Names for One Collapse**

The pair of accusatives is not decorative. *Fājir* is from *fajr*, "to burst through a barrier" — the same root gives the dawn (*fajr*, when light splits the dark), the bursting of the springs in the flood narrative itself (*"and We made the earth gush forth with springs (fajjarnā), and the water met for a matter already measured"* — 54:13), and *fujūr*, wickedness: the man who has broken the fence of restraint. *Kaffār* is from *k-f-r*, "to cover, to bury," the intensive pattern of habitual ingratitude and denial. So the two words describe one collapse from its two ends: outwardly the *breaking through* of limits, inwardly the *burying* of what is owed. The Qur'an makes the pair, in one verse, the two classes of the Last Day:

> *"Truly the righteous are in bliss, and truly the profligate (al-fujjār) are in Hell — entering it on the Day they are promised it, and they will not escape it."* (Qur'an 82:13–16)

The theological point the verse supplies is the one the sūrah has been building since verse 4: sin left un-repentant does not stay the same size. It becomes a *character* — a *faʿūl*-type noun, an entrenched identity — and it reproduces itself socially. This is the *why* of the sūrah's earlier warning that the call only increased them in *flight* (v. 6): unaddressed refusal compounds.

**A Caution Drawn from a Hard Verse**

Muslim ethicists have taken verse 27 as warrant for the principle of *sadd al-dharāʾiʿ* — closing the avenues to harm: that it is legitimate to remove or prevent a cause of corruption even when the act in itself is neutral, and even when the cost falls on particular individuals, because the law looks to the second-order consequences that only God can fully weigh. Al-Shāṭibī's discussion of the *maqāṣid* treats this category as one of the strongest sources of juristic reasoning, and the verse's reasoning — *they will mislead, they will beget* — is a textbook case of argument from consequences.

But the verse also disciplines that principle, and any honest commentary must say so. Nūḥ's forecast was not his own conjecture: God had told him that no more of them would believe (11:36), and had forbidden him to plead for the wrongdoers (23:27). A human authority that destroys people "for the sake of their children's corruption" claims a knowledge of hearts and of futures that only the Judge possesses — which is precisely why the Shariah, in this world, proceeds on the *manifest* (open profession of faith, observable conduct, testimony, evidence) and forbids the *bāṭin* as a ground of judgment: *"do not spy"* (49:12), and *"you are not a guardian over them"* (88:22). The verse is thus a warrant for *prevention of harm* and simultaneously a boundary on *presumption about souls* — the two lessons the history of every reforming violence has needed.

**The Verse as the End of Persuasion**

Finally, note that this is the last word Nūḥ speaks in the sūrah before the final prayer of verse 28 — and its tone is not triumph. The prophet of the millennium ends his account of his people with grief for what they will never be allowed to become, and with a forecast about children he will never meet. Nine centuries of "O my people" (v. 2) end in "Your servants" (v. 27): the distance he was forced to put between himself and them, and the nearness he kept between them and God. Then he turns, in verse 28, from what they deserve to what the faithful need, and the sūrah closes.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:28

> **My Lord, forgive me and my parents, whoever enters my house as a believer, and the believing men and the believing women; and increase not the wrongdoers except in ruin."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Circles, One Verb**

*Rabbi ighfir lī wa-li-wālidayya wa-li-man dakhalā baytī muʾminan wa-li-l-muʾminīna wa-l-muʾmināti*. The verse is built as a widening: myself; my two parents; whoever enters my house in faith; the believing men and the believing women. One imperative governs all four, and the fourth category is not an individual at all but a gendered plural of the whole community of faith, known and unknown, past and future. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both remark how instructive this is as *adab al-duʿāʾ*: the supplicant begins where his obligation begins, and does not stop where his acquaintance stops. The Prophetic form of the same etiquette is exactly this sequence, self, family, then the community:

> *"…and upon Muḥammad and upon the family of Muḥammad, as You blessed Ibrāhīm and the family of Ibrāhīm — truly You are praiseworthy, glorious."* (al-Bukhārī 3370; Muslim 406, from Kaʿb ibn ʿAjra)

And the Prophet ﷺ is commanded in the Qur'an to do precisely what Nūḥ does here — seek forgiveness for his own sin *and* for the believers, in one sentence: *"Know that there is no god but God, and seek forgiveness for your sin and for the believing men and the believing women"* (47:19).

**"And My Parents": What the Petition Does and Does Not Permit**

The inclusion of *wālidayn* — father and mother, the dual of *wālid*, the begetter — has been read two ways in the exegetical tradition, and both readings are worth preserving because the second is the doctrinally careful one. The source commentary reports the tradition that Nūḥ's parents were among the believers, in which case the prayer is a son's lawful *duʿāʾ* for believing parents, an act of the filial piety the Qur'an makes the second of the two great obligations (17:23–24; 29:8; 31:14; 46:15). The alternative reads the verse in the light of the rule revealed to the Prophet ﷺ: that forgiveness may not be sought for the dead who died in association, and that even a prophet's grief must yield to that limit:

> *"It was not for the Prophet and those who believe to seek forgiveness for the polytheists, even if they are kin of one, after it has become clear to them that they are companions of the Blaze. And Ibrāhīm's seeking forgiveness for his father was only because of a promise he had made to him; but when it became clear to him that he was an enemy of God, he disavowed him."* (Qur'an 9:113–114)

If Nūḥ's parents were believers, the verse and the rule do not conflict at all; if the report is not established, then the sūrah — a Makkan text — is simply stating the natural wish of a son, and 9:113–114 later draws the line that a believer cannot cross. What cannot be doubted is the neighbouring case the Qur'an does narrate, in which Nūḥ's own petition for a person was refused: his son. *"And Nūḥ called upon his Lord and said: My Lord, my son is of my family, and Your promise is the truest, and You are the justest of judges. He said: O Nūḥ, he is not of your family; it is a deed not righteous"* (11:45–46). The sūrah thus places two facts side by side and lets them interpret each other: blood is a reason for prayer, never a ground for salvation. The Prophet ﷺ drew the conclusion once and for all:

> *"Whoever is slowed by his deeds — his lineage will not speed him forward."* (Muslim 263, from Abū Hurayrah)

**"Whoever Enters My House": The Ark, the Home, and the Sanctuary**

*Man dakhalā baytī muʾminā*. The commentators weigh three referents, and the verse survives all three. (i) The ark: Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda, and most of the exegetes take "my house" as the vessel, because the ark was the place Nūḥ's faith put those who entered it beyond the water's reach — in which case the sūrah's silence about the ark (v. 25's point) ends with a single oblique reference to it, its only appearance in the chapter. (ii) His actual house: those who took refuge with the prophet in his dwelling and believed, per reports transmitted in al-Ṭabarī. (iii) The house of worship: for "bayt" is the Qur'an's term for the sanctuary — *"that they circumambulate the Ancient House"* (22:29), *"purify My House for the circling, the standing, the bowing, the prostrating"* (2:125) — and *"whoever enters it is safe"* (3:97).

The third reading gives the verse a reach beyond the flood, and the classical *fuqahāʾ* exploited it: the sanctuary as the place where the penitent is received, and the community's house of prayer as the ark of the present age. In a Makkan setting the resonance is audible, since the Quraysh were themselves the custodians of a "house" whose protection they claimed — and the sūrah tells them that the only qualifying condition for its safety is *muʾminan*: believing.

That final accusative is the whole theology of the clause. *Muʾmin* is in the grammatical position of a *ḥāl*, a state: entry is not enough; the person must enter *in the condition of belief*. A physical boarding, a family registration, an inherited membership, a seat in a congregation — none of these is the *ḥāl* that saves. The Khārijite error of making outward affiliation decisive, and the Murjiʾī error of making a verbal profession decisive while the deeds are abandoned, are both excluded by the same small word, as they are by the sūrah's own treatment of Nūḥ's son, who was physically inside the household of a prophet.

**"And the Believing Men and the Believing Women"**

The generalisation is one of the Qur'an's fixed formulas (3:195; 33:35; 47:19; 48:5; 57:12; 66:5), and its placement here is deliberate: the prophet who could not save one son asks forgiveness for an uncounted host, and the sūrah's final affirmative sentence is thus a *universal* intercession of the kind the Prophet ﷺ was granted: *"My intercession is for the people of the major sins among my community"* (al-Tirmidhī 2436, from Anas — graded ḥasan; the theme of intercession with its limits is at 2:255 and 53:26). Belief, in this verse, is described without moral grading — no mention of the quality of prayer, no ranking of the fasting — because the petition is for *everyone* who believes, and the rest is between each soul and its Lord. ʿĪsā's words in the same register of surrender, when the case is handed over entirely, are the perfect gloss: *"If You punish them, they are Your servants; and if You forgive them, You — You alone are the Mighty, the Wise"* (5:118).

**"And Increase Not the Wrongdoers Except in Ruin"**

*Wa-lā tazid al-ẓālimīna illā tabārā*. The final word of the sūrah is *tabār*, a noun the lexicons explain as *al-ihlāk*, destruction — connected to *t-b-r*, "to break into fragments," of which *tib'r* is the dust of something crushed, as grain is crushed, or as the Qur'an describes the mountains on that Day: *"and the mountains will be like carded wool"* (101:5), and *"and they ask you about the mountains, say: my Lord will crumble them and scatter them, and leave them a smooth plain"* (20:105–106). So the sūrah's closing term is not "punishment" but *pulverisation*: not pain inflicted, but a structure reduced to powder, and the word chosen for the last syllable of the chapter is the word for what is left when nothing is left.

The clause repeats, verbatim in form, the second half of verse 24 (*"and increase not the wrongdoers except in error"*), and the repetition is the sūrah's final seal: at verse 24 the wrongdoers' *guidance* was to be withdrawn; at verse 28 their *existence* is to be finished. Nothing new is demanded. The last sentence of the chapter only ratifies the law the chapter has been describing since verse 6: a call produces flight in those who will not weigh anything, and a flight persisted in produces, at the end, dust.

The ring composition is complete and worth listing: the sūrah opens with a warning of a *painful punishment* that had not yet come (v. 1) and closes with a punishment already described as ruin (v. 28). It opens with an imperative to *warn* (v. 1) and closes with an imperative to *forgive* — addressed not to the people but to God (v. 28). The word *gh-f-r* runs through it at verses 4, 7, 10, and 28 — offer, purpose, command, and, at the end, the prophet's own need. That last placement is the sūrah's final piece of instruction: the man who spent nine centuries demanding that others ask pardon finishes by asking it for himself, first. *Rabbi ighfir lī.*

**The Sūrah's Practical Close**

Three things the reader is meant to take away, and the sūrah states all of them at once in this verse. First, that a believer's circle of concern widens as he ages toward God: Nūḥ's earliest words were about his people, and his last are about *all* the believers. Second, that the only entry which matters is entry with faith — into the ark, the house, the community, the grave, the prayer — and that no one enters it as a passenger. Third, that the story of a people who refused pardon ends not in a dramatic curse but in a report: the wrongdoers are the ones who increase, and God is asked to increase them in nothing but ruin. Between those two facts, the sūrah has spent twenty-seven verses saying that the door was open, that the offer was clear, that rain and children and gardens were promised for a single honest sentence of repentance, and that the flood is what a people makes for itself when it declines the sentence.

**Closing: The Sūrah Sealed**

**Expanded Commentary**

**What the Chapter Did With Its Twenty-Eight Verses**

Sūrah Nūḥ accomplished in twenty-eight verses what the other Nūḥ passages accomplish only with the ark, the raven, the mountain, and the rainbow: it made a millennium legible as a moral argument. The chapter's shape has been followed verse by verse above, and it may be set down here as a single line. A sending and a warning (1–2). A demand of three terms — worship, mindfulness, obedience (3). Two prizes: pardon and time (4). A report of labour without result (5–6). An anatomy of refusal (7). A record of every method tried (8–9). The whole message reduced to "seek forgiveness," with the world's goods attached as its natural consequence (10–12). Then the re-weighting of the universe in four exhibits — the staged self, the layered heavens and their two lights, the plant-like growth from soil, the return and the egress — followed by the open earth and the wide roads (13–20). Then the report to the Judge: they followed the men of capital; they built a plot; they would not surrender five names; and they led many (21–24). The sentence: drowned, entered into Fire, unhelped (25). And the two prayers: that the corruption be ended (26–27), and that the believers be forgiven (28).

**The Sūrah's Two Theologies, Held in One Hand**

Everything in it is arranged to keep two claims in balance, since the history of Islamic thought consists largely of attempts to collapse one into the other. On the human side: nothing happened to Nūḥ's people that they did not choose; the ears were plugged by their own fingers (v. 7), the garments drawn by their own hands, the flight chosen, the leaders followed, the plot devised. Responsibility is stated so relentlessly that no reader can finish the chapter feeling that the flood came too soon. On the divine side: every capacity to choose is itself a grant — the stages, the heavens, the lights, the growth, the soil, the roads (14–20) — and even the prophet's patience is a gift he reports back to its Giver (v. 5). The chapter's own reconciliation of the two is not a syllogism but a practice: the servant calls, exhausts every method, complains only to God, and leaves the verdict to the One whose term cannot be moved (v. 4). That is the *ʿaqīda* of this sūrah, and it is why it stands among the Makkan chapters that built the community's idea of prophecy: God's sovereignty does not excuse Him from telling us, and our responsibility does not excuse us from listening.

**Why It Was Recited in Makkah, and Why It Still Is**

The source commentary's judgement about the sūrah's function in the Prophet's ﷺ own mission should be the last word: it was *both* an invitation and a consolation. To Quraysh it said: your situation is not unprecedented and your excuse is not available; the people of Nūḥ had a clearer offer and a longer deadline and are drowned. To the man being called a poet and a madman in the same breath his people used, it said: you have been sent with the same message to the same kind of hearers, and the one who was honoured as *"a grateful servant"* (17:3) for a mission that produced almost nothing measurable was told, in our sūrah's own terms, that his labour had increased them in flight — and yet was called, before all creation, to the front of the queue on the Day of Standing, because he was *"the first messenger God sent to the people of the earth"* (al-Bukhārī 4712; Muslim 194). The chapter was given to a prophet in the middle of failure, to be recited to a city in the middle of hardness, and it has been recited since by every generation of preachers, teachers, and parents who have done everything and seen nothing, in the certainty that the counting is not theirs.

**How to Finish Reading a Sūrah Like This**

A reader who has followed the verse-by-verse commentary is left, as Nūḥ was left, with two sentences to say. One is about others and one is about himself, and the sūrah arranges them in that order of urgency and in that order of humility: *"increase not the wrongdoers except in error"* — meaning: do not let any of us be entrusted with guidance we will convert into hardening — and *"my Lord, forgive me."* The first verse of the chapter was a command to warn; the last was a confession that the warner needs what he was sent to offer. That is, in the end, the reason the sūrah is named after a man and not after a flood.
