# Sūrah al-Mujādilah (Chapter 58) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Woman Who Named a Chapter**

Sūrah al-Mujādilah — "She Who Disputes" — takes its name from the first word of its first scene: a woman arguing her case before the Prophet ﷺ. She was Khawlah bint Thaʿlabah, whose husband Aws ibn al-Ṣāmit had pronounced upon her the pre-Islamic formula of *ẓihār* — "You are to me as my mother's back" — leaving her neither wife nor divorcee. She pleaded, the Prophet ﷺ answered that she seemed forbidden, she pleaded again, and then she lifted her complaint to God. Before she left the room, Gabriel descended with the sūrah's opening verses. No other chapter of the Qur'an is named for a legal petitioner, and none opens so swiftly with Divine vindication: *qad samiʿa* — "God has indeed heard." Because it legislates the abolition of *ẓihār*, the sūrah is also known as Sūrah al-Ẓihār. Its twenty-two verses move from one household's tears to the eternal roster of the two parties — Satan's and God's — showing how intimately the Most High involves Himself in the affairs of the vulnerable.

**A Madinan Chapter of Law and Loyalty**

The sūrah is Madinan by the agreement of most scholars — the first in a sequence of ten Madinan sūrahs legislating for the young Muslim polity — though some hold that only its opening verses are Madinan and others assign verse 9 to Makkah (al-Ālūsī, al-Qurṭubī). Its placement after Sūrah al-Ḥadīd is itself an exegesis. Al-Ḥadīd closed with the declaration:

> *"He knows that which enters the earth and that which issues therefrom, that which descends from Heaven and that which ascends thereto. And He is with you wheresoever you are, and God sees whatsoever you do."* (Qur'an 57:4)

Al-Mujādilah opens as the living proof of that sentence: God, who is with His servants wheresoever they are, hears one woman's whisper in Madinah and answers before she rises. What al-Ḥadīd stated as doctrine, al-Mujādilah demonstrates as event.

The chapter's architecture is deliberate and symmetrical. Verses 1–4 resolve *ẓihār*: the hearing (v. 1), the abolition (v. 2), and the three-runged expiation of freeing, fasting, or feeding (vv. 3–4). Verses 5–6 pronounce the general law of opposition: those who defy God and His Messenger shall be abased, resurrected, and confronted with a record they forgot. Verses 7–10 govern secret converse: God's presence in every whisper (v. 7), the exposure of the mockers (v. 8), the purification of private speech (v. 9), and Satan's authorship of grieving rumors with trust as its antidote (v. 10). Verses 11–13 legislate the etiquette of the Prophet's ﷺ assembly: making room (v. 11), charity before private audience (v. 12), and the merciful lightening of that charity (v. 13). Verses 14–21 dissect hypocrisy: treacherous friendship (v. 14), prepared severity (v. 15), oaths as shields (v. 16), useless wealth and children (v. 17), perjury on Resurrection Day (v. 18), Satan's overwhelming (v. 19), the abasement of opposers (v. 20), and God's inscribed decree of prevailing (v. 21). Verse 22 crowns all: the incompatibility of faith with loving God's adversaries, the inscribed heart, the strengthening Spirit, the Gardens, the mutual contentment, and the party of God that prospers.

**The Sūrah's Refrains and Their Logic**

Three phrases toll through the chapter like bells. "Those who oppose God and His Messenger" sounds in verses 5, 20, and 22 — thesis, application, and final contrast. "God is Aware of whatsoever you do" seals verses 3, 11, and 13 — guarding expiation, assembly manners, and the response to relief. And the pairing of parties — Satan's in verse 19, God's in verse 22 — frames thepresent choice as eternal enrollment. Together they teach the sūrah's single logic: God hears the wronged, witnesses the secret, legislates the remedy, exposes the treacherous, decrees His own victory, and enrolls the loyal. Every verse is a facet of that one justice.

**How to Read What Follows**

What follows treats each of the twenty-two verses as an independent discourse in the established manner: the verse's translation, then expanded commentary under unique context-specific headings — Arabic roots and morphology explained simply, Qur'anic cross-references with their texts quoted, authentic ḥadīth with collections identified, the views of classical scholars (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, Ibn Taymiyyah, and others), occasions of revelation, questions of creed and Divine Attributes, psychological and social dimensions, the logic beneath each ruling, and applications to contemporary life. All direct quotations from the Qur'an, ḥadīth, and scholars appear as blockquotes. The method is unhurried: one verse at a time, each fully developed, until the sūrah stands complete — from a woman's heard complaint to the prospering party of God.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:1

> **God has indeed heard the words of she who disputes with thee concerning her husband and complains to God. And God hears your conversation. Truly God is Hearing, Seeing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Woman's Complaint That Opened a Chapter**

No chapter of the Qur'an begins more dramatically in human terms than this one. The opening word is *qad* — "indeed, certainly" — joined to the past tense *samiʿa*, "He has heard." Before the reader is told who complained, or about what, or to whom, the verdict is announced: she has been heard. The majority of commentators identify the woman as Khawlah bint Thaʿlabah, whose husband Aws ibn al-Ṣāmit, in a moment of anger, pronounced upon her the pre-Islamic formula of *ẓihār*: "You are to me as my mother's back." By the custom of the Jāhiliyyah, those few words left her suspended — neither a wife with rights nor a divorced woman free to remarry. She came to the Prophet ﷺ in Madinah, pleaded, argued, and wept, and when the Prophet ﷺ had no revelation yet to give her beyond the apparent ruling that she was forbidden to her husband, she lifted her face and complained directly to God. She had not yet left the room, the reports say, when Gabriel descended with these verses.

The poignancy of the scene is preserved by ʿĀʾishah, who was present in the adjoining room. She said:

> *"Praised be God whose hearing embraces all voices. The disputing woman came to the Prophet ﷺ and spoke to him while I was in the corner of the house, and I could not hear what she said. Then God revealed: 'God has indeed heard the words of she who disputes...'"* (Ibn Mājah 188; Aḥmad 6/46; al-Nasāʾī; graded authentic by many scholars)

What ʿĀʾishah could not hear through a curtain, God heard above the seven heavens. That contrast is the verse's first lesson.

**Three Words for Speech: Dispute, Complaint, and Conversation**

The Arabic of the verse uses three distinct speech-words, and each carries weight. *Tujādiluka* — "she disputes with thee" — comes from *j-d-l*, the root of twisting a rope. *Mujādalah* is argument in which each side pulls the cord: she presented her case, the Prophet ﷺ answered, she objected, he answered again. The sūrah takes its name from this word. Note what it dignifies: a woman's insistence before the Messenger of God is not described as impudence or rebellion. It is recorded in revelation as the sūrah's title for all time. Ibn Kathīr draws the inference plainly: God honored her persistence by making it the name of His speech.

*Tashtakī* — "she complains" — comes from *sh-k-w*, to open or expose a wound. In Arabic one "opens" one's pain to someone. She opened hers *ila Allāh*, "to God" — the preposition matters. She argued her case before the Prophet ﷺ, but her complaint, in its deepest layer, was directed to God. The Qur'an elsewhere commends exactly this direction of complaint. Jacob, bereft of Joseph, says:

> *"I complain of my anguish and my sorrow only to God."* (Qur'an 12:86)

The third word is *taḥāwurakumā* — "your conversation," literally your mutual returning of speech, from *ḥ-w-r*, to return. Al-Ṭabarī notes the tenderness of the dual pronoun: God names the exchange between the Prophet ﷺ and this distressed woman *your* conversation, placing them together in His sentence, and then adds that He Himself hears it. The scene has three participants, not two: the woman, the Messenger, and the God who listens to both.

**Hearing and Seeing: The Two Names That Answer Her**

The verse closes with two Divine Names: *Samīʿ* (Hearing) and *Baṣīr* (Seeing). Their pairing here is precise. He is Hearing, so her words reached Him; He is Seeing, so her situation — her age, her poverty, her small children, the husband who had consumed her youth — was before Him. Al-Qurṭubī observes that these two Names occur together throughout the Qur'an precisely where the oppressed need assurance that nothing about their case escapes God:

> *"Truly God is Hearing, Seeing."* (Qur'an 4:58, revealed concerning the restoration of trusts)

The theological point is firm against two errors. God hears and sees truly and perfectly — this is no metaphor for mere knowledge — yet His hearing is not like ours, needing ears, proximity, or volume. ʿĀʾishah's testimony makes the transcendence tangible: a raised voice in the next room escapes a human listener; a whispered sob escapes no Divine attention. Ibn Taymiyyah cites verses of this kind as proof that God's attributes are affirmed without likening Him to creation and without emptying the words of meaning: He hears the faintest complaint of the weakest servant, from above His Throne, without intermediary.

**Why God Began With One Woman**

There is a legislative sūrah to follow — rulings on *ẓihār*, on secret counsels, on assemblies, on charity before private audience, on hypocrisy and loyalty. Why open all of it with Khawlah? The logic is that of the Qur'an throughout: law descends through mercy, and the universal is taught through the particular. By answering one aged, powerless woman first, God establishes the principle under which every ruling in the chapter operates — that His law exists to lift injustice, not to decorate power. The same pattern opened Sūrah al-Nisāʾ and runs through the Madinan legislation: the orphan, the widow, the wronged wife are named before the powerful are addressed.

The social implication was revolutionary in its setting and remains pointed today. A pre-Islamic custom, upheld by male consensus for generations, was overturned because one woman refused to accept it and carried her case to God. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, years later as caliph, was once stopped on the road by an elderly woman who reminded him of the days when he was a boy. His companions grew impatient, but he rebuked them:

> *"This is a woman whose complaint God heard above the seven heavens. Shall ʿUmar not listen to her?"* (Reported by al-Bukhārī in headline form; narrated at length by Ibn Kathīr and others)

**Complaining to God Rather Than About God**

A final psychological distinction closes the verse. Khawlah complained *to* God, not *about* Him. The first is worship; the second is despair. To complain to God assumes He hears, He cares, and He can change the matter — it is trust wearing the clothes of grief. Modern readers who have sat in hospital corridors, in lawyers' waiting rooms, in marriages gone cold, will recognize her posture: she did what she could with the means in front of her — she went to the Prophet ﷺ, she argued, she reasoned — and when the means were exhausted, she did not collapse into cynicism. She raised the file to the Highest Court. The verse teaches that no case is too small for that Court, no plaintiff too obscure, and no custom too entrenched to be reheard. The One who is Hearing and Seeing answered her before she had even stood up to leave.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:2

> **Those among you who commit ẓihār against their wives, those are not their mothers. None are their mothers save those who gave birth to them. Truly they speak indecent words and calumny. And truly God is Pardoning, Forgiving.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Sentence That Destroys a Custom**

With a single declarative sentence, the Qur'an demolishes an institution. *Mā hunna ummahātihim* — "those women are not their mothers." The pre-Islamic Arabs had given a husband's angry metaphor the force of law: say "you are to me as my mother's back," and the wife became untouchable yet unfree. God answers not with a procedural amendment but with a statement of reality. Words do not make mothers. Biology, birth, and God's own designation make mothers. A man's utterance, however solemn his tone, cannot rewrite what God has written into nature. The Qur'an had already stated the principle in a Makkan-period verse of Sūrah al-Aḥzāb:

> *"And He has not made your wives, whom you declare to be as your mothers' backs, your mothers."* (Qur'an 33:4)

Here the principle returns as operative Madinan law, and the sūrah will now build the legal consequences upon it.

The logic extends far beyond seventh-century Arabia. Every age invents speech-acts by which the strong redefine the weak out of their rights — declarations, labels, and formulas pronounced in anger or interest and then treated as fate. The verse teaches the believer to test every such formula against reality: does this word of man correspond to anything God has made true? If it does not, then no matter how ancient the custom or how confident the speaker, it is void. Truth is measured by what is, not by who said it loudly.

**The Word Ẓihār: Why "the Back"?**

*Ẓihār* comes from *ẓahr*, "back." The classical lexicographers and commentators explain that the Arabs chose the mother's back — or in some versions, her belly or thigh — as the euphemism for what is most forbidden to approach. To say "you are to me as my mother's back" was to declare the wife's body as untouchable as the most prohibited of all bodies, while cruelly keeping her bound. Al-Ṭabarī notes the deliberateness of the image: the man reached for the most sacred bond he knew — motherhood — and weaponized it into an instrument of abandonment. That is why the verse calls the utterance by two severe names.

*Munkar* — "indecent," literally what sound nature refuses to recognize — is the Qur'anic term for what healthy conscience rejects. *Zūr* — "calumny," literally a bending away from straightness — is falsehood that perverts reality. The same pairing describes the worst sins elsewhere; bearing false witness, the Prophet ﷺ warned, stands beside associating partners with God:

> *"Shall I not inform you of the gravest of major sins? Associating partners with God, disobedience to parents..." — and he was reclining, then sat up and said — "...and indeed false speech (qawl al-zūr) and false witness." He kept repeating it until we wished he would fall silent.* (al-Bukhārī 5976; Muslim 87)

By naming *ẓihār* with these words, the Qur'an tells the husband that his sentence was not a clever legal maneuver but a sin of the tongue — a lie about God, about his wife, and about motherhood itself.

**Mothers Are Only Those Who Gave Birth**

*In ummahātuhum illā al-lāʾī waladnahum* — "None are their mothers save those who gave birth to them." The sentence is absolute and exclusive. Motherhood in the Qur'an is a rank conferred by God through the reality of birth and nurture, carrying rights no human declaration can transfer or dissolve. The Qur'an ties gratitude to God and gratitude to parents in a single command:

> *"Be grateful to Me and to thy parents; unto Me is the journey's end."* (Qur'an 31:14)

And the Prophet ﷺ, when asked who most deserves companionship, answered "your mother" three times before naming the father (al-Bukhārī 5971; Muslim 2548). To drag that rank into a marital quarrel as a rhetorical club is therefore doubly offensive: it degrades the wife and it cheapens the mother. Al-Rāzī adds a subtle observation: by defining mothers exclusively through birth, the verse also protects mothers themselves. If any angry man could manufacture "mothers" by declaration, the sanctity of real motherhood would be diluted into a figure of speech. God guards the mother's station even while defending the wife.

**Pardoning and Forgiving: The Door Left Open**

After the sternest description of the sin, the verse ends with unexpected gentleness: *wa inna Allāha la-ʿAfuwwun Ghafūr* — "And truly God is Pardoning, Forgiving." The two Names are emphatic in form. *ʿAfuww* is the One who utterly effaces the trace of sin, as though it had never been written; *Ghafūr* is the One who covers it so that it neither shames nor destroys. Their placement here is jurisprudential: the expiation detailed in the next two verses is possible only because God pardons. Had He treated *ẓihār* as an unforgivable enormity, no return would exist and Khawlah's marriage would have ended in the very court where she sought rescue. Instead, the sin is named honestly — *munkar* and *zūr* — and then a path back is opened through repentance and atonement.

Ibn Kathīr stresses the balance: the verse neither trivializes the utterance ("it was only words") nor treats words as beyond repair. Speech wounds, and the wound must be paid for — that is the subject of verses 3–4 — but the payer is welcomed, not discarded. This is the Qur'anic logic of expiation everywhere: the penalty is itself a mercy, because it converts guilt into a bridge. The husband who frees a slave, fasts two months, or feeds sixty poor people is not purchasing his wife back like property; he is demonstrating, through costly obedience, that his tongue has learned the weight of what it once threw away lightly.

**What the Tongue Owes the Home**

For the contemporary household, the verse reads as a charter of verbal responsibility. Modern marriages rarely use the formula of *ẓihār*, but they know its descendants: the threat of divorce pronounced to win an argument, the degrading label fixed upon a spouse in anger, the vow of abandonment uttered and then regretted. The verse teaches that such words are never "just words." They are *munkar* — recognized as ugly by every sound heart — and *zūr* — bent away from truth. And it teaches equally that no angry sentence, once repented and atoned, must define a family forever. Between laxity that excuses cruelty and despair that forbids return, the verse holds the middle: name the wrong, pay its due, and find God Pardoning and Forgiving on the other side.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:3

> **As for those who commit ẓihār against their wives and then go back on what they have said, let them free a slave before they touch one another; to that are you counseled. And God is Aware of whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Returning From the Word: What "Going Back" Means**

The verse opens with a precise legal phrase: *thumma yaʿūdūna li-mā qālū* — "then go back on what they have said." The classical schools differed lovingly over its exact meaning, and the difference illuminates the law's spirit. Some, following the apparent sense, understood it as retracting the declaration — the husband regrets his *ẓihār* and wishes to resume the marriage. Others, including Mālik and al-Shāfiʿī in one narration, understood "going back" as returning to what the *ẓihār* had forbidden, namely marital intimacy: the very desire to touch his wife again triggers the obligation. Al-Ṭabarī surveyed the opinions and favored the view that the "return" is the resolve to undo the separation the words imposed — in plain terms, wanting her back as a wife.

Whichever reading one follows, the humane point stands: the law does not chase the man who walks away from his sin in repentance, nor does it trap the man who walks back toward his wife in love. It places a single gate on the path of return — expiation — and opens the gate wide. The verse assumes reconciliation is the desired outcome. Khawlah's plea is being answered not with a dissolution but with a discipline that makes reunion possible.

**Freeing a Slave: The First and Highest Atonement**

*Fa-taḥrīru raqabah* — "let them free a neck." The Arabic says "neck," meaning a whole enslaved person, called by the part placed under the yoke. Before the couple touch one another — *min qabli an yatamāssā*, unanimously understood as before sexual intercourse — the husband must liberate a human being. The commentators list the conditions: the freed person must be a believing slave where possible, in good health, without defect that prevents earning, for the gift must be genuine freedom, not the discarding of a burden. If he owns no slave, he may purchase one belonging to another and free him.

The wisdom of placing emancipation first is manifold, and the scholars unfold it with admiration. First, the tongue that unjustly "bound" a free woman must "loose" a bound human being — symmetry between the injury and the repair. Second, the Qur'an consistently channels the expiation of private sins into the public good of liberation. The same atonement is prescribed for inadvertent killing:

> *"And whosoever kills a believer inadvertently must free a believing slave."* (Qur'an 4:92)

and for broken oaths:

> *"Its expiation is the feeding of ten indigent people... or freeing a slave."* (Qur'an 5:89)

Al-Qurṭubī counts these verses together and concludes that the Qur'an's long strategy was to make every human failing an occasion for human freedom — so that sin itself, repented, diminished slavery. Third, the costliness of the act teaches the tongue its lesson. A man who has paid the price of a human being's liberty to unsay one sentence will weigh his next sentence differently.

**"To That Are You Counseled": Counsel, Not Mere Command**

*Dhālikum tūʿaẓūna bih* — "to that are you counseled." The verb *waʿaẓa* means to counsel with warmth, to advise for the listener's own good. God does not say "this is imposed upon you" but "this is counseled to you" — though it is fully binding law. The tenderness of the word softens the severity of the penalty. Al-Rāzī remarks that Divine commands clothed as counsel remind the believer that every ruling, even a burdensome one, is mercy in the shape of duty. The expiation is not God's vengeance upon the husband; it is God's prescription for him — bitter medicine that heals the marriage, frees a captive, and purifies the speaker in a single act.

The closing phrase, *wa Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīr* — "And God is Aware of whatsoever you do" — seals the verse with vigilance. *Khabīr* is the One whose knowledge reaches inward realities, not only outward motions. Whether the husband frees a slave sincerely or grudgingly, whether the couple honor the pause before touching or cheat it in secret, God knows. The sentence guards the expiation against formalism: it must be done, and it must be meant.

**The Story of the Resolution**

The sequel to Khawlah's case, preserved by al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and others, shows the law descending into a real household. After these verses came down, the Prophet ﷺ told Khawlah that her husband must free a slave. "He cannot afford it," she said. "Then let him fast two consecutive months." "He is an old man; he cannot fast." "Then let him feed sixty poor people." "He has nothing to give." At that moment a large basket of dates — an *ʿarq*, holding some thirty measures — was brought to the Prophet ﷺ. Khawlah at once offered to add another basket of her own. The Prophet ﷺ approved her generosity and said:

> *"You have done well. Go and feed sixty poor people on his behalf, and return to your cousin."* (Abū Dāwūd 2214; al-Qurṭubī; Ibn Kathīr)

In other narrations the Prophet ﷺ supplied half the food and Aws the other half. Either way, the scene is luminous: revelation, poverty, prophetic charity, and a wife's devotion cooperating so that an old couple might stay together with dignity. The law proved not a hammer but a bridge.

**A Pause That Protects Intimacy**

Modern readers may ask why intimacy must wait. The answer lies in the psychology of repair. A wound inflicted by words cannot be healed by the body pretending nothing happened. The required pause — whatever the chosen expiation takes — forces the marriage to pass through acknowledgment, cost, and reflection before resuming its closest act. It tells the wife that her injury was real enough to interrupt everything, and it tells the husband that return is a privilege to be earned, not a right to be seized. Like a driver who must stop and repair the damage after striking another car before continuing his journey, the husband must mend what his tongue broke before enjoying what his marriage gives. The delay is the dignity of the process, and God — Aware of all that they do — witnesses every step of it.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:4

> **And whosoever finds not [the means], let him fast two consecutive months before they touch one another. And whosoever is unable, let him feed sixty indigent people. That is so that you may believe in God and His Messenger. These are the limits set by God, and the disbelievers shall have a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Ladder of Mercy With Three Rungs**

The verse completes the expiation ladder begun in verse 3, and its structure is itself a sermon. Rung one: free a slave. Rung two, for whoever *finds not* the means: fast two consecutive months. Rung three, for whoever *is unable* even to fast: feed sixty indigent people. Each rung descends in wealth demanded and ascends in accessibility, so that no sincere penitent stands before a locked door. The old, the poor, and the sick — Aws ibn al-Ṣāmit was all three — can still return to their wives with honor.

The jurists drew precise lines around each rung. The fast must be *mutatābiʿayn* — consecutive, two lunar months without break; a deliberate interruption restarts the count, for continuity is part of the discipline. Only a lawful excuse such as menstruation's interruption for women in analogous fasts, or genuine illness, preserves the sequence. The feeding must reach sixty distinct poor persons in the majority view, each receiving a *mudd* — roughly what feeds a person two full meals for a day — while the Ḥanafīs permit feeding one poor person over sixty days to the same total. These details, recorded by al-Qurṭubī and others, show the law's twin concerns: the atonement must be real enough to cost, and flexible enough to fit.

**Why Fasting, and Why Sixty?**

Fasting is the Qur'an's recurring school of the soul. It disciplines precisely the faculties that produced the sin: the tongue is restrained, the appetite is denied, the body learns that desire waits upon permission. The same two-month fast atones for inadvertent killing (4:92), linking the taking of life and the wounding of marriage as injuries requiring sustained self-denial to repair. Sixty, in turn, is the number that makes private repentance a public feast. One man's angry sentence, once atoned, becomes one hundred and twenty meals for the hungry. Al-Rāzī marvels at the arithmetic of mercy: the sin fed no one, but its repentance feeds sixty.

The parallel with other expiations confirms the pattern. Breaking an oath requires feeding ten (5:89); failing the fast of Ramaḍān without excuse requires, in the famous ḥadīth, the same sequence of freeing, fasting two months, or feeding sixty (al-Bukhārī 1936; Muslim 1111). The recurrence is deliberate: the Shariah answers the undisciplined appetite — whether of tongue, temper, or belly — with the disciplined appetite of fasting and generosity.

**"That You May Believe": Law as a Teacher of Faith**

*Dhālika li-tuʾminū bi-Allāhi wa rasūlih* — "That is so that you may believe in God and His Messenger." At first glance the clause surprises: is expiation not for those who already believe? The commentators explain that the verse means belief perfected, deepened, and proven. To abandon the rulings of the Jāhiliyyah — where *ẓihār* ended a marriage at a husband's whim — and to submit instead to God's costly procedure is itself an act of faith. Belief here means embodied assent: trusting that God's limits are wiser than ancestral custom, and proving that trust with money, hunger, or charity. Al-Rāzī paraphrases: God clarified His decree so that, knowing it, you might act upon it and cease to follow the Age of Ignorance — and that acting is the belief.

The sentence also answers a quiet doubt: why should intimate family matters require such heavy public consequences? Because, the verse replies, how a man treats his wife when angry is a measure of his submission to God. The home is not outside religion; it is one of religion's proving grounds.

**Limits Set by God — and the Warning Attached**

*Wa tilka ḥudūd Allāh* — "These are the limits set by God." *Ḥudūd* are boundaries no one may cross and landmarks no one may move. The same word guards the laws of divorce (2:229), inheritance (4:13–14), and fasting (2:187). Its appearance here elevates marital speech into sacred legislation: the procedure for *ẓihār* is not tribal mediation that a community may adjust at will, but a Divine boundary. The warning follows at once: *wa li-l-kāfirīna ʿadhābun alīm* — "and the disbelievers shall have a painful punishment." In context, as al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr explain, this refers primarily to those who reject these limits — who insist on judging by the customs of ignorance after God has legislated. The "painful punishment" stands in deliberate contrast to the temporary pain of the expiation: a man may find fasting two months painful, but it is a pain that heals; rejecting God's law brings a pain that does not end.

**Costly Forgiveness in an Age of Cheap Apology**

The contemporary reader lives among apologies that cost nothing — the posted statement, the deleted message, the "sorry if you were offended." This verse proposes the opposite economy. Words that wounded a person must be answered with deeds that serve persons: a freed neck, a fasted summer, sixty fed mouths. The severity is the kindness. A husband who has fasted sixty consecutive days for one sentence will never again imagine that speech is weightless, and a wife who has watched him do it will never doubt that her dignity was taken seriously. The ladder's lowest rung still demands real bread for real poor people — because in God's law, even the poorest man's repentance must enrich someone besides himself.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:5

> **Truly those who oppose God and His Messenger shall be abased, just as those before them were abased. We have indeed sent down clear signs, and the disbelievers shall have a humiliating punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn of the Sūrah: From a Household to History**

With this verse the sūrah pivots. Verses 1–4 resolved a single marriage; verses 5–6 open the horizon to all who set themselves against God and His Messenger across all ages. The pivot is not abrupt but logical. The *ẓihār* legislation was one instance of God's limits; this verse announces the fate of everyone, in any era, who tramples those limits. The particular case becomes a universal principle: God heard one woman against an unjust custom, and He will abase every power that opposes His justice. The remaining seventeen verses will unfold this theme through secret conspiracies, corrupt counsels, hypocritical alliances, and finally the contrasting destinies of Satan's party and God's party.

Al-Bayḍāwī, noted in the source commentary, reads the thrice-repeated phrase "those who oppose God and His Messenger" (vv. 5, 20, 22) as the sūrah's legislative refrain. Each occurrence marks a stage: here the general sentence, in verse 20 the sentence upon conspirators, in verse 22 the incompatibility of such opposition with true faith. The repetition is the Qur'anic method of emphasis — the same truth struck like a bell at the beginning, middle, and end of the argument.

**What "Opposing" Means: The Arabic of Yuḥāddūn**

*Inna alladhīna yuḥāddūna Allāha wa rasūlah* — "Truly those who oppose God and His Messenger." The verb *ḥādda* comes from *ḥadd*, a boundary or limit — the same root as the *ḥudūd Allāh* of verse 4. To oppose God is literally to set oneself on the other side of His boundary, to face Him across a line as an adversary faces his foe. It is stronger than mere disobedience, which may come from weakness; *muḥāddah* is taking a stand. Al-Ṭabarī glosses it as enmity expressed through defiance of God's commands and rejection of His Messenger. The Qur'an defines its gravity elsewhere:

> *"Whosoever disobeys God and His Messenger has strayed into manifest error."* (Qur'an 33:36)

and warns that persistent adversaries place themselves outside the protection their own strength pretends to offer. The verse thus draws a clean line: on one side stand those who submit to the limits, even when costly; on the other, those who legislate against them.

**Abased as Those Before Were Abased**

*Kubitū kamā kubita alladhīna min qablihim* — "they shall be abased, just as those before them were abased." *Kabata* means to overthrow face-down, to humiliate thoroughly — the abasement of an army routed and a throne overturned. The past tense of the comparison reaches back to Pharaoh drowned, to ʿĀd scattered by wind, to Thamūd seized by the cry, to the Confederates dispersed at the Trench. Each had opposed God's messenger with superior force and meticulous planning; each ended as a moral exhibit. The Qur'an elsewhere states the pattern as law:

> *"And indeed Our word has already gone forth to Our servants, the messengers, that they shall surely be helped, and that Our host shall surely be victorious."* (Qur'an 37:171–173)

Ibn Kathīr notes the consolation embedded in the threat. The small Madinan community, ringed by hostile tribes, hypocrites, and an empire's shadow, is told that history is not a gamble but a courtroom. Every previous round of the same trial ended the same way; the present round will not differ. The believers need only remain on the right side of the boundary and let time deliver the verdict.

**Clear Signs and Humiliating Punishment**

*Wa qad anzalnā āyātin bayyināt* — "We have indeed sent down clear signs." The proofs have been given: the Qur'an's inimitable clarity, the Prophet's ﷺ manifest truthfulness, the fulfilled prophecies, the answered prayers like Khawlah's. No opponent will be able to plead confusion. Clarity is what makes opposition culpable; one who defies an obscure rumor may be excused, but one who defies *bayyināt* — evidences that make truth distinct as dawn from night — has chosen his end with open eyes.

*Wa li-l-kāfirīna ʿadhābun muhīn* — "and the disbelievers shall have a humiliating punishment." Note the adjective: not merely painful (*alīm*, as in v. 4) but humiliating (*muhīn*). The punishment fits the crime with poetic justice. Those who sought eminence by opposing God — the pride of the tribal chief, the sneer of the hypocrite, the mockery of the conspirator — will receive precisely the inversion of their aim: public disgrace. The Qur'an pairs the two adjectives deliberately across its pages: pain for the body that enjoyed defiance, humiliation for the ego that demanded it.

**The Logic of Abasement**

Why must opposition to God end in abasement rather than mere defeat? The verse implies a metaphysical logic. All dignity flows from God, who is *al-ʿAzīz*, the Mighty Bestower of might:

> *"And might belongs to God and to His Messenger and to the believers."* (Qur'an 63:8)

To oppose the Source of honor while seeking honor is a contradiction, like turning one's back on the sun while demanding light. The abasement of God's adversaries is therefore not an arbitrary penalty added to their defeat; it is the natural exposure of what their stance always was — smallness posturing as greatness. Pharaoh's body preserved on the shore (10:92), Abū Jahl's corpse in the well at Badr, the hypocrites unmasked in this very sūrah: each reveals that pride against God was always hollow, and history merely removes the costume. The believer who grasps this logic stops envying the powerful defiant and starts pitying them — for their elevation is the prelude to their fall.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:6

> **On the Day when God resurrects them all together, then informs them of that which they did. God has kept account of it while they have forgotten it; and God is Witness over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day That Completes the Sentence**

Grammatically this verse completes the last: the humiliating punishment of verse 5 arrives *yawma yabʿathuhumu Allāhu jamīʿan* — "on the Day when God resurrects them all together." The commentators note two readings of the opening word, both true. Either it specifies the time of the punishment — the humiliation happens on Resurrection Day — or it carries an implied command, "Remember" or "Mention" (*udhkur*): O Prophet, warn them of that Day. Al-Ṭabarī favors the plain connection: the abasement pronounced in verse 5 will be executed when all mankind stands together on a level plain, where, as the Qur'an says elsewhere:

> *"And thou shalt see the earth a barren plain, and We shall gather them so that We leave none of them behind."* (Qur'an 18:47)

*Jamīʿan* — "all together" — is itself part of the reckoning. The conspirators who whispered in corners, the hypocrites who wore two faces for two audiences, the proud who surrounded themselves with courtiers: all will stand in one assembly with no corners, no audiences to play, and no entourage to hide behind. Resurrection is the end of privacy for those who abused it.

**Informed of What They Did: The Reading of the Record**

*Fa-yunabbiʾuhum bi-mā ʿamilū* — "then He informs them of that which they did." The verb *nabbaʾa* means to give weighty news — the same root as *nabī*, prophet. On that Day, God Himself will be the warner, reciting to each soul its own biography. The Qur'an describes the document elsewhere with terrifying precision:

> *"And the Book shall be set in place, and thou shalt see the guilty fearful of what is therein, and they shall say, 'Oh, woe unto us! What a book this is! It leaves out nothing, small or great, save that it has taken account thereof.' And they find present therein whatsoever they did."* (Qur'an 18:49)

Nothing is summarized, nothing rounded off. The secret counsel of verse 8, the mocking greeting, the oath sworn as a shield (v. 16) — each will be replayed with its context intact. Elsewhere the Qur'an adds that hands will speak and feet will testify (36:65), and that hearing, sight, and skin will bear witness against their owners (41:20–21). The criminal's own body will join the prosecution. No advocate's eloquence can answer a record kept by God and confirmed by one's own limbs.

**Kept Account While They Forgot**

*Aḥṣāhu Allāhu wa nasūh* — "God has kept account of it while they have forgotten it." Six Arabic words that contain an entire psychology of sin. *Aḥṣā* means to count exactly, to encompass in number — from *ḥaṣā*, pebbles used in counting. God counted every deed as a treasurer counts coins. *Nasūh* — "they forgot it" — describes the sinner's anesthesia. Small wrongs fade from memory; repeated wrongs stop registering; old wrongs are rewritten as youthful folly or necessity. The hypocrite who insulted the Prophet ﷺ in a whisper ten years earlier has genuinely forgotten the incident; the registry has not.

The asymmetry is the point. Human forgetting changes nothing in the Divine account, just as a debtor's lost receipt does not cancel the bank's ledger. The Qur'an states the principle universally:

> *"And thy Lord is never forgetful."* (Qur'an 19:64)

and:

> *"On a Day when God will resurrect them all... God has encompassed it, though they forgot it."* (this verse, echoed in 58:6's own logic)

Ibn Kathīr draws the pastoral conclusion: the believer should live as though his memory and God's record will one day be compared — and should hasten to erase, through repentance, what he would be ashamed to have read aloud. For repentance, sincerely offered, is the one eraser God Himself honors:

> *"Save those who repent and believe and work righteous deeds — for them God will exchange their evil deeds for good deeds."* (Qur'an 25:70)

**Witness Over All Things**

*Wa Allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd* — "and God is Witness over all things." *Shahīd* is the One present at every event, observing it directly — stronger than mere knowledge, for it implies eyewitness. The Name recurs across the Qur'an as the seal of accountability:

> *"Truly God is Witness over all things."* (Qur'an 22:17; 85:9)

Al-Qurṭubī notes that this Name answers the conspirator's central illusion — "no one saw us." Every secret counsel in this sūrah (vv. 7–10) was held in the presence of a Witness who never blinks. The hypocrites' whispers, the mockers' winks, the false oaths: all were public acts performed before God, only mistakenly thought private. To internalize *al-Shahīd* is to live one's hidden life as though the curtains were glass — which, before God, they are.

**Living Under the Witness**

The verse's practical demand is constant self-audit. Modern life offers endless forgetfulness-machines: the feed refreshes, the message deletes, the crowd moves on. The verse insists that beneath every disappearing medium stands a permanent Witness and an exact registry. The wise response is twofold and daily. First, the believer reviews his own day before God reviews it for him — the practice the Companions called *muḥāsabah*, reckoning with oneself, after the counsel attributed to ʿUmar:

> *"Reckon with yourselves before you are reckoned, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed."* (al-Tirmidhī, as a saying of ʿUmar; widely cited by the scholars)

Second, he converts every remembered fault into repentance while the registry is still open for amendment. For the Day described here is not only a threat; for the repentant it is a homecoming, when God will inform them of deeds they had despaired over — and show them pardoned.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:7

> **Hast thou not considered that God knows whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth? There is no secret converse among three, but that He is their fourth, nor among five, but that He is their sixth, nor less than that, nor more, but that He is with them wheresoever they are. Then on the Day of Resurrection He will inform them of that which they did. Truly God is Knower of all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Hast Thou Not Considered": Knowledge as Vision**

The verse opens with *a-lam tara* — "hast thou not seen?" — though no eyesight is involved. The Qur'an uses "seeing" for certain knowledge throughout, as though truth perceived by proof is as firm as objects perceived by eyes. The address is singular — to the Prophet ﷺ first, and through him to every reader. Consider, reflect, look with the mind's eye: *anna Allāha yaʿlamu mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — "that God knows whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth." Before the verse names the conspirators, it establishes the universe they conspire inside: a cosmos saturated with Divine knowledge, where heaven's vastness and earth's hidden corners are equally transparent to God. The Qur'an states the same encompassment elsewhere:

> *"And with Him are the keys of the Unseen; none knows them but He. And He knows whatsoever is on land and in the sea. Not a leaf falls but that He knows it."* (Qur'an 6:59)

A leaf's fall is registered; how then a whispered plot?

**Three, Five, Less, More: The Arithmetic of Presence**

The verse's centerpiece is justly famous: *mā yakūnu min najwā thalāthatin illā huwa rābiʿuhum* — "there is no secret converse among three, but that He is their fourth." *Najwā* is confidential talk — from *najwah*, elevated ground where two people withdraw to speak apart. The verse names three and five as examples, the typical sizes of huddled groups, then universalizes: *wa lā adnā min dhālika wa lā akthara* — "nor less than that, nor more." Two whispering in a corner, six plotting in a hall, a nation conspiring across continents: the count never excludes Him. *Illā huwa maʿahum aynamā kānū* — "but that He is with them wheresoever they are."

The classical commentators are careful about this "with-ness" (*maʿiyyah*). It is with-ness of knowledge, witnessing, and power — not of mingling or indwelling. God is above His Throne, distinct from creation, yet no place escapes His awareness. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and the mainstream tradition all gloss *maʿahum* here as "He knows them and witnesses them." The same balance governs the two great parallel verses:

> *"And He is with you wheresoever you are, and God sees whatsoever you do."* (Qur'an 57:4)

> *"And We are nearer to him than his jugular vein."* (Qur'an 50:16)

Ibn Taymiyyah devoted careful treatises to this harmony: God's transcendence (*ʿuluww*) and His encompassing nearness coexist without contradiction, for His nearness is that of the All-Knowing Witness, not of a body among bodies. The conspirator who locks the door has locked out every witness except the One who matters.

**The Occasion: Whispers Against the Prophet**

Verses 7–8 address, in their immediate occasion, the secret counsels of those in Madinah who conspired against the Prophet ﷺ — hypocrites and hostile tribesmen who withdrew into corners, exchanged glances, and spread rumors to unnerve the believers. The Qur'an describes the same behavior among the deniers generally:

> *"No new Reminder came unto them from their Lord, but that they listened to it while they played, with their hearts diverted. And those who do wrong confide in secret converse: 'Is this not but a human being like yourselves?'"* (Qur'an 21:2–3)

and warns:

> *"Or do they suppose that We hear not their secret and their secret converse? Yea indeed, and Our messengers are with them, writing."* (Qur'an 43:80)

The Madinan whispers had a cruel function detailed in the next verse's background: the conspirators would murmur and stare at passing believers, who feared the gestures meant death or defeat for their relatives on expedition. Private speech was weaponized into public torment. This verse answers the weapon with a fact: every whisper had an Auditor, and the Auditor keeps minutes for the Day of Resurrection, when *fa-yunabbiʾuhum bi-mā ʿamilū* — "He will inform them of that which they did" — the same phrase as verse 6, now applied specifically to secret talk.

**Knower of All Things: The Seal of the Argument**

*Inna Allāha bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm* — "Truly God is Knower of all things." The verse begins with His knowledge of heaven and earth and ends with His knowledge of *all things* — the circle closes. Nothing said in darkness will stay undiscussed in light. The order of the verse is itself rhetorical: universal knowledge, then the specific case of secret converse, then the Resurrection disclosure, then universal knowledge again. The whisper is trapped between two infinites.

The psychological effect on the believer is double-edged in the best way. For the wronged, the verse is pure comfort: the mockery you could not answer was heard by One who will answer it. For the tempted, it is a guardrail: the message you are about to send, the group you are about to slander in, the plot you imagine no one will trace — all of it is already witnessed. The early Muslims understood this so deeply that, as the commentators report, the verse transformed their private gatherings; men who had whispered carelessly began to weigh every aside as though spoken into a recording that would be played on the Last Day. Which, the verse says, it will be.

**No Corner Outside God**

Modern secrecy has new machinery — encrypted chats, deleted messages, anonymous accounts — but the verse's logic is untouched by technology. Every medium that hides men from men displays them still to God. The analogy of the surveillance age actually serves the verse: if human beings now accept that keystrokes may be logged and cameras may watch, how much more should the heart accept the vigilance of the One who created both the keystroke and the camera? The difference is that God's watching is not the cold stare of a state but the just witness of a Judge who also pardons — for the same verse that threatens conspirators comforts the conspired-against, and the same knowledge that records the whisper records the tearful private prayer no human ever heard.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:8

> **Hast thou not seen those who were forbidden from secretly conversing then returned to that which they had been forbidden, secretly conversing with a view to sin, enmity, and disobeying the Messenger? And when they come to thee and greet thee, they greet thee in a manner that God has not greeted thee and say within themselves, "Why does God not punish us for what we say?" Hell suffices them, burning therein. What an evil journey's end!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Forbidden Whispers, Resumed Defiance**

The verse describes a precise sequence: a group was *forbidden* from their secret conversations, then *returned* to the forbidden thing. The background, reported by al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and others, gives the scene. Certain hypocrites and hostile Jews of Madinah would gather, whisper, and glance toward the believers — who, with sons and brothers on military expeditions, feared the murmurs meant calamity had struck their loved ones. The believers complained to the Prophet ﷺ, and he forbade the offenders this cruel theatre. They obeyed briefly, then resumed. The verse now exposes both the resumption and its content: their *najwā* aimed at *ithm* (sin), *ʿudwān* (enmity), and *maʿṣiyat al-rasūl* (disobedience to the Messenger) — a threefold target covering offense against God, harm to fellow men, and rebellion against the Prophet ﷺ personally.

The structure mirrors verse 3's language of "going back" (*yaʿūdūna*) — but inverted. The husband in verse 3 goes back to his wife in repentant love; these conspirators go back to their sin in stubborn defiance. The Qur'an elsewhere names this pattern as the signature of the hardened heart: warned, they pause; unwatched, they resume. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the verse's opening *a-lam tara* ("hast thou not seen?") presents their behavior as something the whole community witnessed — the crime was open enough to be seen, though its words were whispered.

**"Death Be Upon You": The Poisoned Greeting**

The verse's second half turns to an even more brazen insult. Some visitors, instead of greeting the Prophet ﷺ with *al-salāmu ʿalaykum* ("peace be upon you"), twisted the phrase into *al-sāmu ʿalaykum* — "death be upon you." The pun differs by a single vowel, deniable if challenged, unmistakable to the ear. ʿĀʾishah, present at one such visit, flared in defense of her husband: "Death be upon you! God has cursed you and is wroth with you!" The Prophet ﷺ gently restrained her: "Go easy, O ʿĀʾishah! You must be kind." In another narration he said:

> *"God does not like indecency, nor the uttering of indecent words."* (Reported in the commentary tradition; cf. al-Bukhārī 6024, Muslim 2165 for the core incident)

When ʿĀʾishah protested that she had only answered in kind, the revelation of this verse's latter portion confirmed the matter: God had heard the greeting He never gave His Prophet, and Hell would suffice for it.

The authenticated core of the incident is preserved in the two Ṣaḥīḥs. ʿĀʾishah reported that some Jews came to the Prophet ﷺ and said, *"al-sāmu ʿalaykum"* ("death be upon you"):

> *ʿĀʾishah said: "I understood it and replied, 'And upon you be death and curse.'" But the Prophet ﷺ said: "Be gentle, O ʿĀʾishah. God loves gentleness in all things." I said: "Did you not hear what they said?" He replied: "And I answered, 'And upon you.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6024; Muslim 2165)

The Prophet's ﷺ reply — *wa ʿalaykum*, "and upon you" — returned their words to their senders without descending to their indecency. It is the model of dignity under insult: neither silent acceptance nor mirrored abuse, but a calm deflection that leaves justice to God.

**"Why Does God Not Punish Us?": The Mockers' Logic**

*Wa yaqūlūna fī anfusihim law-lā yuʿadhdhibunā Allāhu bi-mā naqūl* — "and they say within themselves: why does God not punish us for what we say?" The phrase *fī anfusihim* may mean "within themselves" (in their hearts) or "among themselves" (in their private circle); both are true. Their reasoning was a taunt disguised as theology: if Muḥammad were truly God's prophet, God would strike us down for insulting him; since no thunderbolt falls, he is no prophet. Ibn Kathīr and al-Jalālayn both gloss it thus: they made the delay of punishment into evidence against the message.

The Qur'an answers this fallacy repeatedly. Delay is not absence; respite is not acquittal. God gives the wrongdoer time to repent, and when the time expires, the account is settled in full:

> *"And were God to take men to task for what they earn, He would not leave upon the earth a single creature. But He defers them until a stated term."* (Qur'an 35:45)

The mockers mistook God's patience for God's indifference — the oldest error of the prosperous defiant, repeated by every generation that reads continued comfort as continued approval.

**Hell Suffices Them**

*Ḥasbuhum jahannam* — "Hell suffices them." Two words ofHook devastating economy. They demanded immediate punishment as proof; God answers that the punishment is certain, appointed, and more than sufficient. *Yaṣlawnahā* — "burning therein," literally roasting — and *fa-biʾsa al-maṣīr* — "what an evil journey's end!" The word *maṣīr* means destination, the place a journey finally arrives. Their whispers, winks, and twisted greetings were a road; the verse names its terminus.

The sentence also vindicates the Prophet's ﷺ gentleness. Because Hell suffices, the believer need not manufacture hell in return. ʿĀʾishah's anger was love, and the Prophet ﷺ did not condemn her love — but he corrected its expression, teaching that the dignity of the insulted lies in restraint, and the fate of the insulter lies with God. The community that internalizes *ḥasbuhum jahannam* is freed from the cycle of insult and counter-insult; it can answer mockery with composure, endure provocation without профитеering in hatred, and leave the final greeting — the one that truly matters — to the Day when God Himself will greet His Prophet with honor and his mockers with justice.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:9

> **O you who believe! If you converse in secret, then do not converse in secret with a view to sin, enmity, and disobeying the Messenger; converse in secret with a view to piety and reverence. And reverence God, unto Whom you shall be gathered.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Private Speech Is Not Evil in Itself**

After exposing the conspirators' whispers (vv. 7–8), the Qur'an turns to the believers with a crucial clarification: secrecy itself is not the sin. The verse does not forbid *najwā* — private conversation — but purifies it. *Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā tanājaytum* — "O you who believe! If you converse in secret" — assumes that believers will sometimes speak privately, and that is lawful. What matters is the *content* and *direction* of the secrecy. The same closed door may shelter a conspiracy or a charity; the same lowered voice may plot harm or plan reconciliation. The Qur'an states the positive cases elsewhere:

> *"There is no good in most of their secret converse, save for him who enjoins charity or kindness or reconciliation between men. And whosoever does that, seeking God's good pleasure, We shall grant him a great reward."* (Qur'an 4:114)

Planning alms discreetly so the recipient is not shamed, mediating between estranged spouses, counseling a sinner without exposing him — these are secret conversations that earn, as the verse says, "a great reward." The Prophet ﷺ prized such hidden goodness; among the seven shaded on the Day when there is no shade but God's is:

> *"A man who gives charity and conceals it, so that his left hand does not know what his right hand spends."* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031)

Secrecy that shields the vulnerable is virtue; secrecy that targets them is vice.

**The Forbidden Trio: Sin, Enmity, Disobedience**

*Fa-lā tatanājaw bi-l-ithmi wa-l-ʿudwāni wa maʿṣiyat al-rasūl* — "then do not converse in secret with a view to sin, enmity, and disobeying the Messenger." The three terms deliberately repeat the conspirators' program from verse 8, now as a prohibition to the believers. *Ithm* is sin against God — plotting what He forbade. *ʿUdwān* is enmity toward men — aggression, cheating, backbiting, scheming against fellow believers. *Maʿṣiyat al-rasūl* is defiance of the Prophet ﷺ — undermining his authority, mocking his commands, organizing against his community. Together they cover the three directions of wrongdoing: upward, outward, and toward the divinely appointed guide.

The order also teaches discernment. A private conversation that begins with "don't tell anyone, but..." must be weighed: does it serve God, or offend Him? Does it help people, or harm them? Would the Messenger ﷺ approve of it, or forbid it? The tradition preserves a comprehensive maxim on speech that the commentators cite beside this verse:

> *"The speech of the Children of Adam always works against them, not for them, except when it is to command the right and forbid the wrong, or to remember God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2412, from Abū Hurayrah; graded fair by al-Tirmidhī)

Private speech is not exempt from this audit; if anything, the absence of witnesses makes the audit stricter, for only conscience and God remain in the room.

**The Commanded Alternative: Piety and Reverence**

*Wa tanājaw bi-l-birri wa-l-taqwā* — "converse in secret with a view to piety and reverence." The verse does not leave a vacuum; it fills the cleaned space. *Birr* is expansive goodness — kindness, charity, reconciliation, truthfulness, every act that benefits others. *Taqwā* is reverent God-consciousness — the guarding of the heart against what displeases Him, like a traveler picking his way carefully through a thorn-covered path. The pairing is one of the Qur'an's great refrains:

> *"And cooperate in piety and reverence, and do not cooperate in sin and enmity."* (Qur'an 5:2)

Private counsel organized around these two aims becomes an act of worship: two friends planning how to relieve a debtor, colleagues arranging to cover a struggling coworker's shift, spouses quietly reconciling after a quarrel. The lowered voice, consecrated to *birr* and *taqwā*, is as much devotion as the raised voice in prayer.

**"Unto Whom You Shall Be Gathered"**

*Wa-ttaqū Allāha alladhī ilayhi tuḥsharūn* — "And reverence God, unto Whom you shall be gathered." The verse ends where verses 6–7 ended: at the Gathering. Every secret conversation has two audiences — the present few and the future All — and the verse asks the believer to speak always for the second audience. *Tuḥsharūn*, "you shall be herded/gathered," evokes the mustering of all mankind onto one plain, where private words will be replayed in public. The prospect is not meant to paralyze but to purify: whoever remembers the Gathering will either redirect his whisper toward goodness or abandon it for silence — and the Prophet ﷺ taught that this second option is itself a triumph:

> *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent."* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47)

The believer's rule for every private word is therefore simple and searching: if this sentence were broadcast at the Gathering, would I be grateful I said it? If yes — if it is *birr* and *taqwā* — whisper on. If no, the verse commands, let the whisper die unsaid.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:10

> **Secret converse is only from Satan, so that those who believe may grieve; yet he harms them not a whit, save by God's Leave. And in God let the believers trust.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Whispers Have an Author**

*Innamā al-najwā mina al-shayṭān* — "Secret converse is only from Satan." The statement is startling in its sweep, and the commentators qualify it precisely: the verse means the *evil* secret converse described in verses 8–9 — the whispering aimed at sin, enmity, and disobedience. Such scheming is Satan's craft, his signature medium. The devil cannot compel, but he can suggest; he cannot force the hand, but he can poison the ear. The huddled murmur, the exchanged glance, the rumor that sows dread — these are his instruments, and those who employ them, knowingly or not, have taken him as their prompter. The Qur'an traces the same authorship behind every whispering campaign against truth:

> *"And so We appointed for every prophet an enemy — satans from among men and jinn, inspiring one another with gilded speech, deluding."* (Qur'an 6:112)

Al-Ṭabarī notes the verse's word *innamā* — "only" — which restricts and exposes: when you see believers being tormented by mysterious murmurs, know their ultimate source. The human whisperers are Satan's loudspeakers.

**The Aim: Grief, Not Merely Harm**

*Li-yaḥzuna alladhīna āmanū* — "so that those who believe may grieve." Satan's immediate goal is not always material damage but emotional devastation. Grief (*ḥuzn*) clouds judgment, weakens resolve, sours brotherhood, and distracts from worship. The Madinan believers who watched the hypocrites whisper and feared for their relatives on expedition suffered exactly this: nights of anxiety, days of suspicion, hearts divided. Satan understands that a grieving community is a vulnerable community — sad hearts suspect one another, despair of relief, and neglect their duties. The Qur'an elsewhere shows him deploying sorrow as a tactic and shows God disarming it:

> *"Do not lose heart, nor grieve, for you shall be superior, if you are believers."* (Qur'an 3:139)

The Prophet ﷺ legislated against even the innocent appearance of such grief-causing. In an authentic ḥadīth he forbade two people from whispering together while excluding a third companion:

> *"When you are three, let not two converse privately to the exclusion of the third, for that grieves him."* (al-Bukhārī 6290; Muslim 2184)

If even blameless exclusion is forbidden because it *grieves*, how much graver is whispering designed to grieve? The etiquette of the believers must be the opposite of Satan's: include, reassure, speak openly.

**Powerless Without Permission**

*Wa laysa bi-ḍārrihim shayʾan illā bi-idhni Allāh* — "yet he harms them not a whit, save by God's Leave." The verse pivots from exposure to reassurance. Satan suggests, but only God decrees. No whisper can kill, no rumor can defeat, no conspiracy can succeed unless God permits it — and when He permits hardship, it arrives measured, purposeful, and temporary, as a trial that elevates rather than a plot that destroys. The devil himself will confess his impotence on the Last Day:

> *"And Satan will say, when the matter has been decreed: 'Verily God made you the Promise of truth; and I made you a promise, but I failed you. And I had no authority over you, save that I called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me, but blame yourselves.'"* (Qur'an 14:22)

Ibn Kathīr draws the liberating conclusion: the believer who understands this verse fears no whispering campaign. Men may murmur, headlines may darken, rumors may fly — but harm itself is in God's hand, and God's hand is with His friends. Anxiety about hidden enemies dissolves into trust in the open Protector.

**Trust as the Prescribed Antidote**

*Wa ʿalā Allāhi fa-l-yatawakkali al-muʾminūn* — "And in God let the believers trust." *Tawakkul* is not passivity but commissioned confidence: to take the lawful means — verify rumors, confront schemers, guard the community — and then to rest the heart upon God for the outcome. The grammatical form (*fa-l-yatawakkal*, "then let them trust") makes reliance a duty, not a mood. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the promise explicit:

> *"And whosoever trusts in God — He suffices him."* (Qur'an 65:3)

The practical prescription for the grief Satan intends is therefore threefold. First, refuse the whisper's premise: do not assume the worst on the basis of glances and murmurs. Second, answer secrecy with openness: the believers' affairs should be as transparent as wisdom allows, leaving Satan's insinuations no shadows to breed in. Third, anchor the heart in *tawakkul*: say as the Companions said when warned of gathering armies:

> *"God suffices us, and He is the most excellent Guardian."* (Qur'an 3:173)

— and watch grief give way to the calm that no conspiracy can penetrate.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:11

> **O you who believe! When it is said unto you, "Make room for one another in the assemblies," then make room, and God will make room for you. And when it is said, "Rise," then rise, and God will raise in degrees those among you who believe and those who have been given knowledge. God is Aware of whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Crowded Veranda and a Revealed Etiquette**

The verse descended, reports the early commentator Muqātil ibn Sulaymān, upon a scene of embarrassment at *al-ṣuffah* — the shaded veranda of the Prophet's Mosque where the poor and devoted Companions gathered. It was Friday; the space was tight. Veterans of Badr arrived late to find every place taken, and stood waiting while those seated — who had come early and loved their nearness to the Prophet ﷺ — declined to move. The Prophet ﷺ, pained at the slight to men who had risked everything at Badr, asked some who had not fought there to yield their seats. Displeasure showed on their faces, and the hypocrites seized the moment: "Did you not claim your man deals fairly? He expels earlycomers for latecomers!" God then revealed this verse — vindicating the Prophet's ﷺ action, commanding generosity with space, and exposing the grumblers.

The beauty of the revelation is that it lifts a momentary seating dispute into permanent law. What happened once at al-ṣuffah now governs every assembly of the believers until the Last Day: classrooms, mosques, councils, and gatherings. The Qur'an does not consider good manners beneath revelation; it considers them revelation's fruit.

**Make Room, and God Will Make Room for You**

*Idhā qīla lakum tafassaḥū fī al-majālis fa-fsaḥū yafsaḥi Allāhu lakum* — "When it is said unto you, 'Make room in the assemblies,' then make room, and God will make room for you." *Tafassaḥū* comes from *f-s-ḥ*, spaciousness: shift, compress, open a gap for your brother. The promise — *yafsaḥi Allāhu lakum* — mirrors the deed with Divine amplitude. Al-Rāzī gathers the classical explanations of what "room" God gives in return: expansion of provision, of the heart, of knowledge, of the grave, and of Paradise. The interchange is an instance of the Qur'an's great law of reciprocity:

> *"So remember Me, and I shall remember you."* (Qur'an 2:152)

and the ḥadīth qudsī in which God says:

> *"If he comes near to Me by a hand's length, I come near to him by a cubit's length. If he comes near to Me a cubit's length, I come near to him two outstretched arms. If he comes to Me walking, I come to him running."* (al-Bukhārī 7405; Muslim 2675)

Al-Rāzī presses the logic further: whoever opens the doors of ease for God's servants will find God opening good for him in this world and the next — so no reasonable person restricts this verse to cushions and benches. Making room includes making room in conversation for a stammering speaker, in opportunity for a junior colleague, in mercy for a repentant sinner. Every generosity with space returns as spaciousness from God. The Prophet ﷺ generalized the principle:

> *"God ceases not to aid the servant so long as the servant aids his brother."* (Muslim 2699)

**"Rise," Then Rise: The Discipline of Response**

*Wa idhā qīla unshuzū fa-nshuzū* — "And when it is said, 'Rise,' then rise." The commentators offer overlapping readings: rise from your seat for one more worthy; rise to prayer when called; rise to good deeds — to learning, to struggle, to any summons of benefit. Qatādah's gloss, preserved by al-Ṭabarī, captures them all: "Whenever you are called to a good deed, respond." The verse thus pairs two social virtues: *yield* (make room) and *rise* (answer the call). The believer is neither the miser of his seat nor the sluggard before duty. He compresses himself for others and bestirs himself for God — and both motions, small as they seem, are witnessed and weighed.

There is also a quiet rebuke to the wounded pride of those who had to stand at al-ṣuffah. To be asked to yield is not demotion; obedience in that moment is itself elevation. The community that honors its Badr veterans by seating them, and honors God's command by standing when told, distributes dignity to all: the seated are honored by the community, and the standing are honored by God.

**Raised in Degrees: Faith Crowned With Knowledge**

*Yarfaʿi Allāhu alladhīna āmanū minkum wa alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma darajāt* — "God will raise in degrees those among you who believe and those who have been given knowledge." The sentence distinguishes two elevations: the rank of faith, shared by all believers, and the further ranks of knowledge, granted to scholars in degrees (*darajāt* — plural, suggesting many ascending levels). Faith itself outranks mere information — a believing illiterate stands higher than a disbelieving savant — but among believers, knowledge elevates. The Prophet ﷺ illustrated the distance:

> *"The superiority of the scholar over the worshipper is as the superiority of the full moon over all the planets."* (Abū Dāwūd 3641; al-Tirmidhī 2682; Ibn Mājah 223)

And tradition reports that God revealed to Abraham: "I am Knowing, and I love those who know." Ibn ʿAjībah, cited in the source commentary, ranks knowledges by their objects: highest is knowledge of God's Essence through unveiling, then of His Names and Attributes, then of His decrees, then of His bounties. Every step upward in true knowledge is a step upward with God — which is why the verse makes the pursuit of learning a form of ascent, and the honoring of the learned a religious duty.

**Aware of What You Do**

*Wa Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīr* — "God is Aware of whatsoever you do." The seal returns to inwardness. Whether you yielded your seat sweetly or sullenly, whether you rose promptly or with a sigh, whether your pursuit of knowledge seeks God or status — God, the *Khabīr*, knows the interior of the act. The verse began with bodies in an assembly and ends with hearts before God. For the modern believer crowded into mosques, lecture halls, and meeting rooms, the command is unchanged and unexhausted: compress yourself with a smile, rise when called without resentment, honor the learned, seek knowledge yourself — and trust the One who sees the squeeze of your shoulder and the intention of your standing to repay both with room, and rank, beyond imagining.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:12

> **O you who believe! When you converse in secret with the Messenger, offer charity before your secret converse. That is better for you and purer. But if you do not find [the means], truly God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Tax on Monopoly: The Occasion of the Verse**

The verse addressed a very human problem. Certain wealthy Companions, delighting in private access to the Prophet ﷺ, monopolized his time with lengthy private audiences — *najwā* with the Messenger — while the poor waited at the edges, unable to approach. The Prophet ﷺ, whose courtesy prevented him from dismissing anyone, bore the burden silently. God therefore legislated a filter: whoever desired private converse with the Messenger must first offer charity (*ṣadaqah*). The requirement would deter the idle, discipline the excessive, dignify the poor (whose seating at al-ṣuffah the previous verse had just defended), and turn every private audience into a public benefit.

The command was unique in the Qur'an — no other verse imposed charity as the price of conversation — and its working was immediate. The idle stayed away, the poor were excused by the verse's own exemption, and the Prophet's ﷺ time was freed for what mattered. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib later said of this verse:

> *"There is a verse in the Book of God that no one acted upon before me, and no one will act upon after me. I had a gold piece which I exchanged for silver coins, and whenever I conferred privately with the Messenger of God ﷺ, I gave one coin in charity — until I had spent them all. Then the verse was abrogated."* (al-Tirmidhī 3300; al-Nasāʾī; Abū Dāwūd; cited by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī)

Some reports add that only an hour — or a single day — separated this verse from its abrogation in verse 13. ʿAlī's distinction thus stands eternally: he alone holds the honor of having fully practiced it.

**Better for You and Purer**

*Dhālika khayrun lakum wa aṭhar* — "That is better for you and purer." *Khayr* (better) covers benefit in this world — time ordered, poor fed, hearts disciplined; *aṭhar* (purer) covers the inward cleansing — intentions sifted, love of display washed out, sincerity refined. The pairing teaches that Divine legislation always serves both dimensions at once. The charity purified the *motive* for seeking audience (is this converse truly needed, or vanity?) and the *soul* of the seeker (giving before asking reminds the giver that he approaches a prophet, not a possession).

The logic generalizes far beyond its brief enforcement. Every claim on another's time — especially the time of teachers, scholars, and leaders — should be weighed by a question this verse plants in the conscience: have I *given* before I *take*? The believer who feeds the poor before seeking the scholar, who serves the community before petitioning its leaders, who offers before requesting, has absorbed the verse's spirit even though its letter was lifted. Access to the valuable should cost the seeker something valuable — otherwise the shameless consume what the shy never reach.

**The Exemption That Reveals the Law's Heart**

*Fa-in lam tajidū fa-inna Allāha Ghafūrun Raḥīm* — "But if you do not find the means, truly God is Forgiving, Merciful." Poverty is never a barrier to the Prophet ﷺ. Those who genuinely lacked anything to give were exempted outright — no debt, no shame, no diminished welcome. The exemption proves the law was never a tollgate for the rich but a bridle for the excessive. God names Himself here *Ghafūr* (Forgiving) and *Raḥīm* (Merciful): Forgiving of the inability, Merciful in preserving access.

Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both stress the tenderness of this clause. The poor Companion who had watched the wealthy monopolize the Prophet ﷺ might have feared the new law would price him out entirely; instead the law prices out only those who *have* and *withhold*. The empty-handed remain full members of the Prophet's ﷺ audience. The principle endures wherever religious access risks becoming a luxury good: no mosque program, no scholarly circle, no spiritual privilege may be structured so that the poor are excluded. Wherever means are lacking, God's forgiveness and mercy — extended through His community's arrangements — must supply the lack.

**Why God Legislated and Then Lifted**

The swift abrogation puzzles some readers: why command what would be withdrawn within hours? The scholars answer that the verse was a diagnostic and a discipline, not a permanent tax. As diagnosis, it revealed in a single day who truly needed private audience and who merely enjoyed it — the crowds thinned instantly, proving the point. As discipline, it trained the Companions in reverence for the Prophet's ﷺ time and in generosity toward the poor, then lifted the formal burden once the lesson was learned. Abrogation here is itself mercy: the medicine was administered, the fever broke, and the prescription was withdrawn before it could weary the patient.

The pattern has a Qur'anic parallel in the Night Vigil, first imposed upon the Prophet ﷺ and then lightened (73:2–3, 73:20), and it displays a profound legislative wisdom. God may impose a temporary rigor to break a habit, then restore ease once the habit is broken — the spiritual equivalent of a physician's short course of strong medicine. The believer's response to both the imposing and the lifting is the same: obedient gratitude. And ʿAlī's golden hour remains the verse's living monument — proof that even the briefest command of God deserves the swiftest obedience of His servants.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:13

> **Are you apprehensive about offering charity before your secret converse? If you do not do so and God relents unto you, then perform the prayer, give the alms, and obey God and His Messenger. God is Aware of whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Are You Apprehensive?": A Gentle Rebuke**

*A-ashfaqtum an tuqaddimū bayna yaday najwākum ṣadaqāt* — "Are you apprehensive about offering charity before your secret converse?" *Ishfāq* is anxious fear — the worry that giving will deplete, that generosity will impoverish, that the requirement will prove too heavy. The question is tender rather than furious: God names the Companions' hesitation aloud, acknowledges its humanity, and then lifts its cause. The wealthy had found the charity irksome; the poor had found it impossible; all had found private audience suddenly rare. Rather than condemning the apprehension, God treats it as the signal to lighten the law.

The commentators note the mercy in the interrogative form. God does not say "You have failed" but asks "Were you apprehensive?" — inviting self-recognition without humiliation. The great exegetes compare it to a teacher who, seeing his pupils strain under an exercise, asks with a smile whether the weight was heavy — and then reduces it. Divine legislation, the verse shows, is not indifferent to human capacity. It presses firmly enough to train and yields swiftly enough to sustain.

**Relenting and the Return to the Essentials**

*Fa-idh lam tafʿalū wa tāba Allāhu ʿalaykum* — "If you do not do so and God relents unto you." *Tāba ʿalaykum* — literally "He turned toward you" — is the language of repentance inverted: usually the servant turns to God (*tāba ilā Allāh*); here God "turns" to His servants with pardon and relief. The formal requirement of pre-audience charity is withdrawn; no blame attaches to those who held back during its brief life; the page is turned clean.

In its place come the standing pillars: *fa-aqīmū al-ṣalāta wa ātū al-zakāta wa aṭīʿū Allāha wa rasūlah* — "then perform the prayer, give the alms, and obey God and His Messenger." Prayer, alms, obedience: the permanent tripod on which the believer's life rests. The substitution teaches a hierarchy. Extraordinary devotions — charity before every converse, night vigils, voluntary rigors — are scaffolding; the building itself is the five prayers kept, the obligatory alms paid, and the Messenger obeyed. When God lifts a scaffolding, He points back to the building. Al-Ṭabarī notes the deliberate echo: the command to obey God and His Messenger here stands in direct contrast to the condemnation of *those who oppose God and His Messenger* in verses 5, 20, and 22. The sūrah's two parties are defined by this single fork — obedience or opposition.

**Prayer and Alms as the Substance of Obedience**

That prayer and alms are named alongside obedience is itself instructive. The Qur'an habitually pairs them as the twin proofs of submission — prayer ordering the soul toward God, alms ordering wealth toward men:

> *"And perform the prayer and give the alms."* (Qur'an 2:43, 2:110, and throughout)

Al-Qurṭubī observes that commanding them here, immediately after relenting from the special charity, reassures the believers that the *spirit* of the lifted law survives in the standing law. What the pre-converse charity aimed at — generosity purifying access to the Prophet ﷺ — the regular alms continues in broader form; what reverence for his time required, prayer and obedience preserve. Nothing essential was lost in the abrogation; only the extraordinary form was folded back into the ordinary pillars, which remain obligatory until death.

The juxtaposition also answers the wealthy monopolists permanently. They need no longer pay per audience — but they must still pay the alms, still stand in prayer beside the poor they once crowded out, and still obey a Messenger who loves the poor. The discipline changed its shape, not its direction.

**Aware of What You Do**

*Wa Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīr* — "God is Aware of whatsoever you do." The refrain that sealed verses 3 and 11 returns a third time, and its repetition across the sūrah is now clearly thematic. In verse 3 it guarded the expiation against empty formalism; in verse 11 it weighed the sincerity of yielding seats; here it witnesses the response to relief. Will the believers, freed from the special charity, grow generous in the standing alms — or will relief breed negligence? God, the *Khabīr*, observes which it is.

For the contemporary reader the verse offers a complete theology of religious burden. God may at times lay a heavy demand — a season of financial strain, an exacting teacher, a rigorous spiritual regimen — and then lift it. The lifting is not permission to collapse but redirection to the essentials: keep the prayers, pay the dues, obey the Messenger. Ease after rigor is itself a test, subtler than the rigor was. The believer passes it by letting the temporary discipline permanently sweeten his ordinary duties — praying with the reverence the special charity taught, giving with the generosity it trained, and obeying with the promptness it demanded.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:14

> **Hast thou not seen those who befriend a people with whom God is wroth? They are not of you, nor are you of them. And they knowingly swear upon falsehood.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Party Between Two Parties**

The sūrah now opens its longest section (vv. 14–21): the anatomy of hypocrisy. *A-lam tara ilā alladhīna tawallaw qawman ghaḍiba Allāhu ʿalayhim* — "Hast thou not seen those who befriend a people with whom God is wroth?" *Tawallā* means to take as protectors, allies, and intimate friends — to turn one's loyalty toward them. The immediate referents, report al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and others, were hypocrites in Madinah who prayed with the Muslims but carried their secrets to the hostile Jewish tribes. One narration names ʿAbd Allāh ibn Nabtal, who sat in the Prophet's ﷺ company and then reported all he heard; another tells of the Prophet ﷺ warning his Companions that a man would enter "looking with the eyes of Satan," then confronting him with his insults — to which the man responded with oaths and excuses. The verse generalizes from these incidents to every age's double agents: those who claim the believers' fellowship while serving the believers' enemies.

The phrase "a people with whom God is wroth" (*qawman ghaḍiba Allāhu ʿalayhim*) deliberately echoes the Fātiḥah's *al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim* — "those who incur wrath" (1:7). The hypocrites' chosen friends are those who know the truth and reject it. The Qur'an forbids such alliances of loyalty throughout the Madinan legislation:

> *"Let not the believers take the disbelievers as protectors apart from the believers. Whosoever does that has no bond with God."* (Qur'an 3:28)

Ordinary kindness to peaceful non-Muslims remains fully lawful — *"God does not forbid you, with regard to those who did not fight you on account of religion... from treating them righteously"* (60:8) — but intimate allegiance, the sharing of secrets and the entrusting of the community's safety, belongs only within the household of faith.

**Neither of You Nor of Them: The Misery of the Middle**

*Mā hum minkum wa lā minhum* — "They are not of you, nor are they of them." Eight syllables that pronounce the hypocrite's essential homelessness. He prays with the Muslims but is no Muslim; he serves their enemies but is no part of them either — for the enemies despise him as a traitor even while using him. The Qur'an describes the same suspension in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ:

> *"Wavering between this and that, belonging neither to these nor to those."* (Qur'an 4:143)

Ibn Kathīr notes the worldly truth of the sentence: the double-dealer ends trusted by no one. The believers sense his coldness; the enemies count him expendable. He imagined his two faces would double his security; they have halved it twice over. Psychologically the state is torment — constant vigilance over which face is showing, constant dread of exposure, constant performance with no audience that loves him. The verse invites pity even as it pronounces judgment: the hypocrite is the loneliest man in Madinah, surrounded by two communities and belonging to none.

**Swearing Upon Falsehood — Knowingly**

*Wa yaḥlifūna ʿalā al-kadhibi wa hum yaʿlamūn* — "And they knowingly swear upon falsehood." The oaths will be detailed in verse 16; here the emphasis falls on *wa hum yaʿlamūn* — "while they know." Their falsehood is not error but perjury: they swear they did not insult the Prophet ﷺ while knowing they did; they swear loyalty while knowing their hearts are elsewhere. Knowledge aggravates guilt. The ignorant liar may be corrected; the knowing liar has weaponized truth's own form — the oath by God — against truth itself. The Qur'an elsewhere makes knowledge the measure of culpability:

> *"And who is more unjust than one who conceals a testimony he has from God?"* (Qur'an 2:140)

Al-Qurṭubī draws the legal inference: oaths sworn to cover hostility are among the gravest abuses of God's Name, for they draft the Most Truthful as witness to a lie. The hypocrites' tongues say *wa-Allāhi* ("by God") while their hearts say otherwise — and the God invoked hears both utterances.

**Loyalty as the Test of the Heart**

The verse's enduring question faces every believer who navigates divided loyalties: where does my *tawallī* — my deep allegiance — truly lie? The test is not whom one greets politely or trades with fairly; Islam commands justice and kindness to all peaceful people. The test is whom one *serves* when interests collide: whose secrets one keeps, whose cause one advances, in whose company one's heart rests. The hypocrite failed because his body stood with the Muslims while his loyalty sat with their foes. The believer passes by unifying the two — standing, serving, and belonging in the same place. For the heart, like the body, cannot face two directions at once; and God, who sees the direction it faces, asks only that it face Him and those He loves.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:15

> **God has prepared for them a severe punishment. Evil indeed is that which they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Prepared": Punishment Already Waiting**

*Aʿadda Allāhu lahum ʿadhāban shadīdā* — "God has prepared for them a severe punishment." The past tense *aʿadda* ("He has prepared") is chilling: the punishment is not a distant possibility but a completed arrangement, readied as a host readies a chamber for an expected guest. The Qur'an uses the same verb for Paradise — *"prepared for the reverent"* (3:133) — and the parallel is deliberate. Both destinations stand prepared; each soul travels toward the one its deeds have reserved. The hypocrites, who imagined their duplicity was cleverness, have a reservation they never intended to make.

*Shadīd* — "severe" — intensifies the warning. Elsewhere the Qur'an specifies the hypocrites' station as the lowest depth:

> *"Truly the hypocrites shall be in the lowest depth of the Fire."* (Qur'an 4:145)

Why the severest punishment for those whose crimes were whispers and smiles rather than swords? Because, as al-Rāzī explains, their harm was the most insidious: the open enemy can be faced, but the hidden one corrupts from within — betraying secrets, demoralizing believers, shielding hostility behind oaths. A disease of the heart is harder to treat than a wound of the skin, and treachery wearing friendship's face does what no battlefield defeat can do: it teaches the community to distrust itself.

**"Evil Indeed Is That Which They Used to Do"**

*Innahum sāʾa mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — "Evil indeed is that which they used to do." The sentence is a moral verdict pronounced in God's own voice. *Sāʾa* — "how evil!" — is the Arabic form of exclamation, expressing revulsion. What they "used to do" (*kānū yaʿmalūn* — continuous past) includes everything the sūrah has catalogued: the resumed whispers, the poisoned greetings, the monopolizing of audiences, the carrying of tales, the sworn lies. The verse gathers the whole record into a single word of disgust.

Ibn Kathīr notes that the verdict answers the hypocrites' self-image. They considered themselves clever — the sophisticated operators who kept a foot in every camp. God calls their cleverness *sūʾ* — evil, ugliness, vileness. The redefinition is total: what Madinah's salons may have admired as political skill, heaven records as moral filth. The Qur'an applies the same reversal to every civilization's celebrated villainy, reminding the believer that history's final review is written not by contemporaries but by God.

**Severity Proportioned to Privilege**

A deeper logic links the punishment's severity to the hypocrites' privilege. They lived in Madinah — the city of the Prophet ﷺ, the radiating center of revelation. They heard the Qur'an fresh, prayed behind the Messenger, witnessed miracles of providence like Khawlah's answered plea. Light surrounded them; they closed their eyes. The Qur'an states the principle that privilege rejected deepens guilt:

> *"And whoever is blind in this world shall be blind in the Hereafter, and further astray in way."* (Qur'an 17:72)

— blindness amid light being worse than blindness in darkness. The Bedouin idolater in a distant valley never heard the Prophet's ﷺ voice; the Madinan hypocrite heard it daily and carried its echoes to the enemy's camp. The severity of *ʿadhāban shadīdan* is thus exactly proportioned: the nearer the light one refuses, the deeper the darkness one earns.

The warning extends to every believer blessed with knowledge, access, and opportunity. To sit in gatherings of remembrance yet nurse hostility toward them, to learn the Qur'an yet deploy it for status, to enjoy the community's trust yet betray it in private — these are the modern heirs of the hypocrites' commerce, and the verse prices that commerce honestly. Privilege in religion is capital; invested in sincerity it yields Paradise, and the same capital spent on duplicity purchases the prepared severity. The verse asks each reader to audit his advantages: surrounded by so much light, what have I done with it — and what, therefore, stands prepared for me?

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:16

> **They took their oaths as a shield and thus turned from the way of God; so theirs shall be a humiliating punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Oaths as Armor: The Mechanics of Deception**

*Ittakhadhū aymānahum junnah* — "They took their oaths as a shield." *Junnah* is a warrior's shield, the disc behind which a fighter hides from arrows. The hypocrites wielded *wa-Allāhi* — "by God!" — the way soldiers wield steel: every accusation met with a sworn denial, every exposure parried with a louder oath. Confronted about insulting the Prophet ﷺ, they swore they had not; caught carrying tales, they swore loyalty; questioned about their absences and alliances, they produced fresh oaths like reinforcements. The identical phrase recurs in Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn:

> *"They took their oaths as a shield, and thus turned from the way of God."* (Qur'an 63:2)

Al-Qurṭubī notes that even pre-Islamic Arabia, for all its roughness, despised oath-breaking; to swear falsely was among the deepest disgraces. The hypocrites thus sank beneath the honor-code of the Jāhiliyyah while claiming the faith of Islam. The Qur'an honors instead those who treat oaths as sacred bonds:

> *"Truly those who keep their pledge to God... for them there shall be no fear."* (cf. Qur'an 3:77)

and warns against deploying them as instruments of fraud:

> *"Take not your oaths to practice deception among yourselves, lest a foot slip after it had been firmly planted."* (Qur'an 16:94)

The oath is meant to be truth's seal; the hypocrites made it falsehood's shield — and a shield so used becomes evidence against its bearer.

**Turning and Turning Away Others**

*Fa-ṣaddū ʿan sabīli Allāh* — "and thus turned from the way of God." The verb *ṣadda* carries a double sense, and both apply. Intransitively, it means they themselves turned away: their oaths barricaded them ever further from the path, each perjury another step into the wilderness. Transitively, it means they barred *others*: their sworn lies sowed doubt among weak believers, their false reassurances covered the enemy's movements, their respectable perjury taught observers that Islam's adherents were liars. Al-Shawkānī (cited as Sh in the source) emphasizes this second sense: by disparaging the Muslims behind a screen of oaths, they obstructed others from the way. The shield, in other words, was also a roadblock.

The dynamic is timeless. Every movement of truth suffers more from false friends than from open foes, for the open foe warns while the false friend misleads. A hypocrite's sworn slander — "by God, the community is corrupt; by God, the cause is lost" — deters seekers whom no enemy's sword could have stopped. The verse teaches the believers to judge professions by patterns: oaths are cheap, but the *ṣadd* — the turning, the obstructing, the consistent drag away from God — reveals the heart.

**Humiliation for Those Who Hid Behind Honor**

*Fa-lahum ʿadhābun muhīn* — "so theirs shall be a humiliating punishment." The adjective *muhīn* returns from verse 5, and its fitness here is exact. The hypocrites used the most honorable of words — God's Name — to preserve their honor among men; their punishment will strip honor publicly. On the Day when secrets are disclosed, their shields will be displayed as exhibits: every *wa-Allāhi* replayed beside the lie it covered. The Qur'an promises this unveiling elsewhere:

> *"On the Day when secrets are examined."* (Qur'an 86:9)

Ibn Kathīr observes the poetic justice: the shield becomes the brand. What they hid behind will mark them, as a forged passport marks the smuggler at the final checkpoint. The humiliation is also communal — suffered before the very believers they deceived and the very enemies they courted, neither group offering rescue.

For the sincere believer, the verse sets two guards around the tongue. First, swear rarely and only in truth — the Prophet ﷺ warned against habitual oath-taking even when truthful, for frequency cheapens solemnity. Second, never use God's Name as a tool: neither to close a sale, nor to win an argument, nor to cover an embarrassment. The Name is for worship and for witness in grave truth — and whoever makes it a shield for falsehood will find, on the Day of *muhīn*, that shields of air stop nothing.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:17

> **Neither their property nor their children will avail them aught against God. They are the inhabitants of the Fire, abiding therein.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Shields That Fail**

*Lan tughniya ʿanhum amwāluhum wa lā awlāduhum mina Allāhi shayʾā* — "Neither their property nor their children will avail them aught against God." After the shield of oaths (v. 16), the verse names the hypocrites' remaining shields: wealth and offspring — the twin pillars of worldly security. In tribal Madinah, a rich man with many sons was untouchable: money bought allies, sons formed a militia. The hypocrites counted these assets as they counted their oaths — as protections. The verse liquidates both at a stroke: *lan* (never) *shayʾan* (in anything) — no amount of property, no number of children, will purchase the smallest exemption from God.

The sentence is nearly identical to two verses of Sūrah Āl ʿImrān:

> *"Truly those who disbelieve — neither their property nor their children will avail them aught against God."* (Qur'an 3:10, 3:116)

Its repetition across sūrahs marks it as a standing law of the Hereafter, not a remark on one occasion. Abraham stated its positive form in his prayer:

> *"The Day when neither wealth nor children avail, save for him who comes to God with a sound heart."* (Qur'an 26:88–89)

Only the *qalb salīm* — the sound, sincere, undivided heart — is legal tender on that Day. Everything else is foreign currency at a closed exchange.

**Why Wealth and Children Cannot Ransom**

The logic is both theological and practical. Theologically, wealth and children are themselves God's gifts, held in trust; offering them back as bribes is like a tenant offering the landlord his own house as payment. The Qur'an exposes the absurdity:

> *"And know that your property and your children are a trial, and that with God is a great reward."* (Qur'an 8:28)

They were the *test*, not the *answer* — instruments given to prove whether their holder would spend generously and raise righteously. To clutch them as rescuers inverts their purpose entirely.

Practically, the verse answers the hypocrites' specific calculations. Their wealth had purchased influence; their sons had secured their tribal standing; both had insulated them from consequences in Madinah. The verse warns that Madinah's economy does not extend beyond death. On the Day described in verses 6–7, when God informs each soul of its deeds, no transfer of funds and no filial pleading will alter the record. The Prophet ﷺ made the point concrete to his own beloved daughter:

> *"O Fāṭimah, daughter of Muḥammad! Ask of my wealth what you wish, but I can avail you nothing against God."* (al-Bukhārī 4771; Muslim 206, words to his household)

If the Prophet's ﷺ own kinship cannot ransom, the hypocrites' portfolios certainly cannot.

**Inhabitants of the Fire, Abiding Therein**

*Ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-nār, hum fīhā khālidūn* — "They are the inhabitants of the Fire, abiding therein." *Aṣḥāb* — companions, inhabitants — suggests belonging: as a man belongs with his household, they belong with the Fire; it is their company, their milieu, their fitting association. *Khālidūn* — abiding — seals the duration: not a visit but a residence. The sentence's bluntness matches the hypocrites' persistence: a lifetime of double-dealing, unrepented, earns an eternity of single dealing — justice without duplicity.

Ibn Kathīr notes the verse's placement as the climax of the exposure sequence: no loyalty to men will help (v. 14), no severity will be spared (v. 15), no oaths will shield (v. 16), no wealth or children will ransom (v. 17). Every refuge is systematically demolished until only two realities remain: the Fire and its inhabitants. The demolition is itself mercy to the living reader — for while breath remains, the true refuge stands open. Wealth spent in charity becomes an advocate rather than a useless hoard; children raised in faith become ongoing reward rather than helpless bystanders. The Prophet ﷺ promised:

> *"When a man dies, his deeds cease except for three: ongoing charity, knowledge that benefits, or a righteous child who prays for him."* (Muslim 1631)

Property and progeny, redirected from shields into services, can still avail — not *against* God, but *with* Him.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:18

> **On the Day when God resurrects them all, they will swear to Him just as they swear to you, supposing that they have some standing. Lo! Truly they are the liars.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Perjury in the Highest Court**

The verse stages one of the Qur'an's most astonishing scenes. *Yawma yabʿathuhumu Allāhu jamīʿan fa-yaḥlifūna lahu kamā yaḥlifūna lakum* — "On the Day when God resurrects them all, they will swear to Him just as they swear to you." The hypocrites, raised from death, standing before the All-Knowing, will do exactly what they did in Madinah: swear false oaths. The habit of a lifetime survives death itself. In the world they swore to the Prophet ﷺ and the believers — "by God, we did not insult; by God, we are with you" — and the oaths worked, buying them time and cover. Before God, they will reach for the same tool, as a forger might offer counterfeit coins at the mint itself.

The Qur'an records their oaths and their futility together elsewhere:

> *"On the Day We gather them all together, then say to those who associated partners: 'Where are your partners whom you claimed?' Then their only excuse will be to say: 'By God, our Lord, we were not associators!'"* (Qur'an 6:22–23)

— followed at once by the Divine comment: *"Behold how they lie against themselves."* Al-Zamakhsharī (Z) glosses our verse: they will swear to God that they were Muslims, that they believed, that their hearts matched their tongues. The audacity is the point: duplicity so practiced that it confronts Omniscience without flinching.

**"Supposing They Have Some Standing"**

*Yaḥsabūna annahum ʿalā shayʾ* — "supposing that they have some standing." *ʿAlā shayʾ* — "upon something" — means upon some ground, some footing, some valid position. They imagine their oaths constitute a defense, their outward professions a credit balance, their lifetime of religious performance a case. The verb *yaḥsabūna* (they suppose, they reckon) marks the entire calculation as delusion — the same verb the Qur'an uses for every self-deceived arithmetic:

> *"And they suppose that they are doing good."* (Qur'an 18:104)

Al-Zamakhsharī explains: they think their outward declarations will benefit them, as they benefited them in the world. In Madinah, saying *lā ilāha illā Allāh* with the tongue secured one's blood, property, and communal standing regardless of the heart; they assume the Day of Judgment honors the same currency. They do not yet grasp that the trial has moved from a court that judges appearances to a Judge who witnesses realities — from a jurisdiction where oaths shield to one where, as verse 6 promised, God informs each soul of what it actually did.

**"Truly They Are the Liars"**

*Alā innahum hum al-kādhibūn* — "Lo! Truly they are the liars." The sentence is absolute and exclusive: *hum* ("they themselves") with the definite article *al-kādhibūn* ("the liars") — they are liars par excellence, liars by essence, not merely tellers of occasional lies. The particle *alā* ("lo!") demands attention, as though God commands the universe to witness the verdict. Ibn Kathīr notes the completeness of the exposure: in the world their lying was suspected but deniable; on that Day it will be certified by God Himself, before all mankind, beyond appeal.

The verdict also reveals the implacability of their condition. As the source commentary observes, nothing convinces them — not prophetic warnings in the world, not resurrection itself, not the unveiled presence of God. The lie has become the man: he does not merely speak falsehood but *is* falsehood's embodiment. The Qur'an describes the same terminal state in the sealing of hearts (2:7) and the rusting of hearts (83:14): persistent insincerity eventually destroys the very faculty of sincerity, until the soul can no longer tell truth even when truth is its only hope.

**The Habit That Outlives Death**

The verse's warning to the living is severe and practical: habits of deception do not die with the body. The tongue trained in excuses, the heart trained in double bookkeeping, the conscience trained to swear first and examine later — these travel into the grave and rise with their owner. Modern life offers endless rehearsal: the embellished résumé, the edited story, the oath-like "trust me" deployed to end inquiry. Each successful deception teaches the soul that falsehood works — until the soul tries it before the One it never worked on.

The antidote is the opposite habituation: truthfulness practiced until it becomes nature. The Prophet ﷺ described the two trajectories with perfect symmetry:

> *"Truthfulness leads to righteousness, and righteousness leads to Paradise. A man keeps speaking truthfully until he is written with God as a great truth-teller (ṣiddīq). And lying leads to wickedness, and wickedness leads to the Fire. A man keeps lying until he is written with God as a great liar (kadhdhāb)."* (al-Bukhārī 6094; Muslim 2607)

Verse 18 shows the terminal station of the second trajectory: written as *al-kādhibūn* by God's own verdict, swearing before Omniscience and believed by no one. The believer who fears that station has one practice: to mean every *wa-Allāhi*, to keep every promise, and to let his tongue's outward match his heart's inward — until truthfulness, too, outlives death.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:19

> **Satan has overwhelmed them, and so made them forget the remembrance of God. They are the party of Satan. Lo! The party of Satan are losers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Overwhelmed: The Grammar of Possession**

*Istawḥadha ʿalayhimu al-shayṭān* — "Satan has overwhelmed them." The verb *istawḥadha* is rare and forceful: to gain mastery over, to encompass and subdue, as a rider masters a mount. Al-Shawkānī and al-Zamakhsharī gloss it as total domination — their obedience belongs to Satan in whatever he demands; they are his flock, driven where he wills. The preposition *ʿalā* ("over/upon") adds the image of mounting: Satan rides them. The frightening feature is voluntariness. Satan acquired no chains; they handed him the reins one whisper, one whispered gathering, one sworn lie at a time — until, as verses 14–18 traced, the habit of obeying him became their nature. The Qur'an describes the same conquest elsewhere as a siege completed from within:

> *"And Satan will say, when the matter has been decreed... 'I had no authority over you, save that I called you, and you responded to me.'"* (Qur'an 14:22)

He never broke down their door; they opened it, repeatedly, until he owned the house.

**Forgetting the Remembrance of God**

*Fa-ansāhum dhikra Allāh* — "and so made them forget the remembrance of God." Forgetting (*nisyān*) here is not innocent lapse but the withering of the heart's attachment. They forgot to remember God *with their tongues* — no prayer with presence, no Qur'an with reflection, no supplication with need — and *with their hearts* — no awe, no gratitude, no consciousness of the Witness. Al-Shawkānī notes both dimensions: tongue and heart together abandoned. The sequence is causal: Satan's mastery produces God's forgetting, for a heart cannot serve two masters, and every act of obedience to the whisperer is an act of distance from the Remembered.

The Qur'an elsewhere shows forgetting God as the threshold of self-destruction:

> *"And be not as those who forgot God, so He made them forget themselves."* (Qur'an 59:1, the sūrah immediately following this one — 59:19)

To forget God is to forget oneself: one's purpose, one's dignity, one's destination. The hypocrites forgot the One who created them for worship and remembered only the whispers that promised advantage — and in the exchange they lost the very selves they schemed to protect. The famous ḥadīth cited in the source commentary measures the resulting state:

> *"The likeness of one who remembers his Lord and one who does not remember Him is as the likeness of the living and the dead."* (al-Bukhārī 6407; Muslim 779)

By this measure the overwhelmed are the walking dead — bodies in the assembly, corpses in the spirit.

**The Party of Satan**

*Ulāʾika ḥizbu al-shayṭān* — "They are the party of Satan." *Ḥizb* is a party, faction, organized following — a term of allegiance. The verse enrolls them formally: all their scattered acts — the whispers, the poisoned greetings, the false oaths, the treacherous friendships — constitute a single membership. They imagined themselves independents, clever freelancers playing all sides; heaven files them as foot soldiers of one commander. The title also prepares the sūrah's closing contrast: verse 22 will introduce *ḥizb Allāh*, the party of God. There is no third party. Every soul's scattered choices cohere, by the end, into one of two enrollments — and the enrollment is determined not by slogans but by remembrance: those who remember God belong to His party; those whom Satan made forget belong to his.

**Losers by Definition**

*Alā inna ḥizba al-shayṭāni hum al-khāsirūn* — "Lo! The party of Satan are losers." *Khasāra* is commercial loss — to invest capital and lose it. The hypocrites invested everything — their Madinan privilege, their oaths, their wealth, their children, their very souls — in Satan's enterprise, and the verse announces the audit: total loss. The *alā* ("lo!") proclaims it universally, and the *hum* ("they themselves") restricts it exclusively: losers par excellence, whatever their worldly balance sheets showed. The Qur'an defines their loss elsewhere as the only bankruptcy that matters:

> *"Say: Truly the losers are those who lose themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection."* (Qur'an 39:15)

Worldly shrewdness that ends in this loss was never shrewdness — it was the longest con in history, with the con artist conned most of all.

The verse leaves the living reader with a diagnostic question of piercing simplicity: what do I remember through the day? The party of God remembers God — in morning and evening, in gratitude and need, in the pause before speech and the reflection after deeds. The party of Satan remembers everything else — grievances, schemes, vanities, appetites — and forgets the One. Remembrance is thus the daily referendum on membership. Every *subḥān Allāh* consciously said is a vote for the party that prospers; every day passed without Him is a drift toward the party that loses. The choice, the verse insists, is renewed with every dawn — until the dawn when enrollment closes.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:20

> **Truly those who oppose God and His Messenger, they will be among the abased.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain Returns — Now Sharpened**

*Inna alladhīna yuḥāddūna Allāha wa rasūlahu ulāʾika fī al-adhallīn* — "Truly those who oppose God and His Messenger, they will be among the abased." The refrain of verse 5 returns nearly verbatim, but its context has transformed its weight. In verse 5 the sentence was pronounced over history in general; here it falls upon specifically unmasked criminals — the whisperers, oath-breakers, and Satan's partisans of verses 7–19. The reader has now *seen* the opposition in its particulars: the resumed conspiracies, the twisted greetings, the monopolized audiences, the perjured denials. The general law now executes upon named behaviors. Al-Bayḍāwī's structural observation, noted in the source, is vindicated: the threefold repetition (vv. 5, 20, 22) frames the sūrah's argument — thesis, application, and final contrast.

*Fī al-adhallīn* — "among the abased," literally "in the most abject" — places them inside a category, enrolled as permanently as verse 19 enrolled them in Satan's party. *Adhall* is the superlative of *dhalīl*: low, humiliated, contemptible. The opposition that promised them eminence — influence with the powerful, safety in every camp, cleverness admired by all — delivers them into the ledger of the lowliest. The Qur'an elsewhere names the same destination for Pharaoh's host and every arrogant elite:

> *"Truly those who disdain My worship shall enter Hell, abased."* (Qur'an 40:60)

Abasement is not an added torture but the exposure of reality: those who opposed the Most High were always low; judgment merely publishes the fact.

**Who Counts as "Opposing"? A Necessary Precision**

The source commentary adds an important qualification: "those who oppose" means those who *outwardly oppose and conspire against the Muslim community* — active adversaries, not merely peaceful non-Muslims. The Qur'an itself draws the line with care. Toward those who do not fight the believers on account of religion nor expel them from their homes, justice and kindness remain obligatory:

> *"God does not forbid you, with regard to those who did not fight you on account of religion and did not expel you from your homes, from treating them righteously and being just toward them. Truly God loves the just."* (60:8)

And toward those who incline to peace, the believers must incline likewise:

> *"And if they incline toward peace, incline thou toward it, and trust in God."* (Qur'an 8:61)

*Muḥāddah* — adversarial opposition — is therefore a precise charge: taking a stand across God's boundary, conspiring against His Messenger and community, warring against the truth after knowing it. It does not describe the peaceful neighbor of another faith, the honest questioner, or the political neutral. The verse threatens combatants, not strangers — and it threatens them after nineteen verses of patiently documented evidence.

**Abasement as Certain Future**

*Ulāʾika fī al-adhallīn* is grammatically a present-tense enrollment with future force: they *are* (already, in God's decree) among those who *will be* abased. The certainty mirrors verse 21's decree that God and His messengers shall prevail — the two verses form a matched pair: the adversaries' abasement and God's victory are the same event viewed from opposite sides. Ibn Kathīr links them explicitly: because God has decreed His own prevalence, the prevalence of His opponents is metaphysically impossible, and their apparent ascendancy at any moment is a bubble awaiting its pin.

History supplies the commentary's illustrations. The Quraysh who boycotted, persecuted, and expelled ended begging amnesty at the Conquest; the hypocrites' confederates among the tribes were scattered; the empires that menaced Madinah — Byzantium and Persia — crumbled within a generation of this revelation. Each abasement followed the same script: pride, plot, prolongation (mistaken for approval), then sudden inversion. The verse invites the believer to read contemporary arrogance through the same lens — not with vengeful glee but with steady confidence. The outcome is enrolled; time is merely delivering it.

**The Comfort for the Opposed**

Finally the verse comforts those the opposers targeted. Khawlah was opposed by custom; the Prophet ﷺ by mockers; the poor Companions by monopolists; the believers by whisperers. To each the verse says: your opposers are opposing not only you but God and His Messenger — and that litigation has a guaranteed verdict. The believer's task is therefore not to secure the verdict (it is secured) but to deserve its shelter: to remain on God's side of the boundary through prayer, alms, truthfulness, and remembrance. Abasement awaits one party; but, as the next verse will thunder, prevailing awaits the Other — and the believer must only choose, daily, to which party his deeds enroll him.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:21

> **God has decreed, "I shall surely prevail, I and My messengers!" Truly God is Strong, Mighty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Decree Inscribed Before Time**

*Kataba Allāhu la-aghlibanna anā wa rusulī* — "God has decreed: 'I shall surely prevail, I and My messengers!'" *Kataba* means He wrote, inscribed, decreed — and the commentators (al-Jalālayn, al-Shawkānī, al-Zamakhsharī) agree the writing is upon the Preserved Tablet (*al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ*):

> *"In a Preserved Tablet."* (Qur'an 85:22)

Before Khawlah complained, before the conspirators whispered, before Madinah existed or Adam was shaped, the outcome was written: God and His messengers prevail. The oath-form *la-aghlibanna* — "I shall surely, certainly prevail" — with its emphatic *lām* and doubled *nūn*, is God's own swearing by Himself. No contingency qualifies it, no coalition can appeal it. History is not an open question anxiously awaited in heaven; it is a closed decree progressively unveiled on earth.

The verse answers every despair the sūrah's earlier passages might have stirred. The believers watched hypocrites thrive, mockers go unpunished, and conspiracies multiply — and might have wondered whether truth was losing. The decree replies: what you witness is the middle of the story, not its end. The end is written, and the writing says *aghlibanna* — "I shall prevail." The Qur'an states the same promise as a universal law:

> *"And indeed Our word has already gone forth to Our servants, the messengers, that they shall surely be helped, and that Our host shall surely be victorious."* (Qur'an 37:171–173)

**"I and My Messengers": The Twofold Victory**

The decree joins God and His messengers in a single prevailing — *anā wa rusulī* — and the scholars unfold two dimensions of this shared victory. First, prevailing by *proof* (*ḥujjah*): the messengers' arguments defeat all opposition in the court of reason and conscience. No philosopher refuted the Qur'an; no mockery answered Khawlah's answered prayer; no conspiracy produced a revelation. The evidence, once sent down as *āyāt bayyināt* (v. 5), stands undefeated forever. Second, prevailing by *sword* (*sayf*) where God wills: the historical triumph of the faithful community — Badr, the Conquest, the spread of Islam from Persia to Spain within decades of this verse. Al-Shawkānī and al-Zamakhsharī name both instruments: revelation and, when justice requires it, the sword that defends revelation's people.

A subtlety deserves notice: the decree promises prevailing to the *messengers* — yet some messengers were killed (2:61), and the Prophet ﷺ himself suffered wounds at Uḥud. How is their prevailing reckoned? The tradition answers on three levels. Their message prevailed: the truths they taught outlived their killers — where are Pharaoh's gods now, and where is Moses' testimony? Their followers prevailed: the communities they founded inherited the earth. And their persons prevail eternally: martyrdom with God is the highest victory, not defeat. The Qur'an redefines the scoreboard:

> *"And think not of those killed in God's way as dead. Rather, they are alive with their Lord, provided for."* (Qur'an 3:169)

By this accounting no messenger ever lost, and verse 21 was never falsified — not even at Uḥud, not even at Karbalāʾ, not even in any age's apparent eclipse.

**Strong and Mighty: The Names That Guarantee the Decree**

*Inna Allāha Qawiyyun ʿAzīz* — "Truly God is Strong, Mighty." *Qawī* is the One whose power never tires, never depletes, never meets resistance; *ʿAzīz* is the Mighty One who cannot be overcome and who bestows might on whom He wills. The pairing seals the decree with the natures that make it inevitable. A king's decree may fail for want of strength; God's decree *is* strength speaking. The Qur'an joins these Names wherever human power seems to threaten the promise:

> *"And God is Strong, Mighty."* (Qur'an 22:40, concerning those expelled from their homes)

Ibn Taymiyyah cites such verses against both despair and arrogance: despair doubts God's strength to fulfill His word; arrogance imagines human strength can void it. Both are theological errors before they are strategic ones. The believer who knows *al-Qawī al-ʿAzīz* neither despairs when the wicked prosper nor boasts when the righteous do — for both prosperity and adversity are parentheses inside a prevailing already decreed.

**Living Inside a Guaranteed Victory**

The practical fruit of the verse is courage without recklessness. Because the end is guaranteed, the believer can afford honesty in the middle: he need not lie for the cause (like the hypocrites' oaths), nor whisper conspiracies (like the *najwā* folk), nor despair of reform (like those who suppose falsehood has won). His duty is fidelity to the means — truthful speech, just dealing, patient worship — while the end rests with the Strong and Mighty. The analogy of the farmer serves again: he tills, sows, and waters because the harvest is promised; he does not manufacture it by anxiety. Likewise the believer serves God's cause because prevailing is written — and the writing frees him from both panic and cruelty. Victory guaranteed by God needs no lies to hasten it and fears no delays to threaten it. It asks only what verse 22 will now describe: hearts inscribed with faith, strengthened with His Spirit, content with Him — the party of God, to whom prevailing was promised before time began.

## Sūrah al-Mujādilah 58:22

> **You shall not find a people who believe in God and the Last Day loving those who oppose God and His Messenger, even if they be their fathers, their sons, their brothers, or their tribe. God has inscribed faith upon their hearts and strengthened them with a Spirit from Him. He causes them to enter Gardens with rivers running below, to abide therein, God being content with them and they being content with Him. They are the party of God. Truly the party of God — it is they who shall prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Love That Faith Forbids — and a Love It Commands**

*Lā tajidu qawman yuʾminūna bi-Allāhi wa-l-yawmi al-ākhiri yuwāddūna man ḥādda Allāha wa rasūlah* — "You shall not find a people who believe in God and the Last Day loving those who oppose God and His Messenger." The verse opens as a statement of fact with the force of law: such a combination does not exist — genuine faith and affectionate alliance with God's adversaries cannot cohabit one heart. *Muwāddah* here is not mere politeness or blood-kindness but *loving loyalty* — the heart's alliance, the siding of the soul. The believer may — and must — treat peaceful non-Muslim kin with justice and kindness:

> *"God does not forbid you, with regard to those who did not fight you on account of religion... from treating them righteously and being just toward them."* (Qur'an 60:8)

He may feed his unbelieving mother, visit his unbelieving brother, and pray for their guidance. What he cannot do is *love their opposition* — approve their war against God, take their side against the believers, or hold their hostility lightly for the sake of kinship. The Qur'an draws this line elsewhere with equal clarity:

> *"Say: If your fathers, your children, your brothers, your spouses, your tribe, the wealth you have acquired, commerce whose stagnation you fear, and dwellings you find pleasing are more beloved to you than God and His Messenger and striving in His way, then wait till God comes with His Command."* (Qur'an 9:24)

Love is ranked; and the First Beloved admits no rival.

**Fathers, Sons, Brothers, Tribe: The Hardest Test Named**

*Wa law kānū ābāʾahum aw abnāʾahum aw ikhwānahum aw ʿashīratahum* — "even if they be their fathers, their sons, their brothers, or their tribe." The verse names the four circles of natural loyalty in ascending width: the father above, the son below, the brother beside, the tribe around. No bond is exempted. The tradition preserves the occasions that embodied each circle. *Their fathers*: Abū ʿUbaydah ibn al-Jarrāḥ faced his father ʿAbd Allāh in the ranks of the enemy at Uḥud and killed him. *Their sons*: Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq challenged his own unbelieving son to single combat at Badr, pleading "O Messenger of God, let me be among the first." *Their brothers*: Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr killed his brother ʿUbayd at Badr. *Their tribe*: ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb killed his uncle al-ʿĀṣ ibn Hishām at Badr. (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Suyūṭī.)

These reports describe battlefields, not households — moments when kin had taken up arms against the Muslim community and no neutrality was possible. Beyond their occasions, the verse states a standing principle, noted by al-Shawkānī: the bond of faith overrides the bond of blood and provides the greater protection:

> *"The believers are but brothers."* (Qur'an 49:10)

Blood ties remain sacred — severing the womb is a major sin — but when blood commands what God forbids, blood must be disobeyed. The believer honors his father, cherishes his son, aids his brother, and serves his tribe in all that is good; the single exception is alliance against God. That exception, the verse insists, admits no exception.

**Inscribed Faith: Writing Upon the Heart**

*Ulāʾika kataba fī qulūbihimu al-īmān* — "God has inscribed faith upon their hearts." *Kataba* — He wrote — echoes verse 21's *kataba Allāhu* (God has decreed): the same Pen that wrote victory on the Tablet writes faith in the breast. Al-Zamakhsharī explains that God establishes faith firmly, expanding the breast for it; al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī add that He consolidates it so the believer holds the whole of it, not fragments. The image is of permanence: ink worked into the heart's tablet, not chalk on a slate. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the opposite — hearts sealed (2:7), hearts rusted (83:14), hearts locked (47:24). Against all those closures stands this opening: a heart voluntarily yielded to God, upon which God Himself does the writing.

The order of agency deserves reflection. The believers first refused the forbidden love — a human choice, costly and free — and God answered by inscribing faith — a Divine gift, stabilizing and sweet. So runs the covenant everywhere: the servant takes one step, and God takes manifold steps toward him (al-Bukhārī 7405). Loyalty offered in trial returns as faith engraved in peace.

**Strengthened With a Spirit From Him**

*Wa ayyadahum bi-rūḥin minhu* — "and strengthened them with a Spirit from Him." The commentators offer interwoven readings, each true. Some say the Spirit is Divine aid in overcoming enemies; some, the Qur'an and its proofs; some, light and faith; some, clear proof and guidance; some, God's mercy; some, the angel Gabriel who descends with support (al-Qurṭubī). Ibn ʿAjībah calls it the light of certainty. Al-Ālūsī writes, in words the source commentary preserves:

> *"What is intended here by Spirit is the light of the heart, which is the light that God casts into the hearts of whomsoever He will among His servants, and by which hearts attain peace and ascend to the heights of realization."*

The pronoun *minhu* ("from Him") may also be read "from it" — from the inscribed faith itself — since faith, once engraved, generates its own strengthening light (Ibn ʿAjībah, al-Ālūsī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī). Either reading converges: the loyal heart receives reinforcement beyond its native strength. The believer who surrendered kinship for God's sake does not face the resulting loneliness unaided; a Spirit from the Most Near accompanies him, steadying his resolve and sweetening his sacrifice. The Qur'an promises this companionship to all who remember:

> *"Verily in the remembrance of God do hearts find peace."* (Qur'an 13:28)

**Gardens — and the Contentment Beyond Gardens**

*Wa yudkhiluhum jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār, khālidīna fīhā* — "He causes them to enter Gardens with rivers running below, to abide therein." The reward begins with the familiar splendor: gardens, rivers, eternity. But the verse crowns it with something greater: *raḍiya Allāhu ʿanhum wa raḍū ʿanh* — "God being content with them and they being content with Him." Mutual contentment (*riḍwān*) — God pleased with their obedience, they pleased with His recompense and His decree. Al-Rāzī counts four blessings in the verse — inscribed faith, strengthening Spirit, admission to Gardens, mutual contentment — and declares:

> *"The blessing of contentment is the greatest of blessings and the most distinguished of spiritual ranks."*

The same mutual pleasure seals the Qur'an's noblest passages (5:119; 9:100; 98:8). Its second half — *they* content with *Him* — is the more searching: contentment with what He ordained in the world (including the painful severances this verse demanded) as well as with what He grants in the Hereafter. A ḥadīth preserved by al-Baghawī states:

> *"Whosoever has submitted is provided for sufficiently, and whomsoever God has caused to be content with that which He has given him has succeeded."*

The believers of this verse surrendered the dearest loves for God; God answers by making Himself their dearest love — and then eternity to enjoy it.

**The Party of God — and Its Prosperity**

*Ulāʾika ḥizbu Allāh. Alā inna ḥizba Allāhi hum al-mufliḥūn* — "They are the party of God. Truly the party of God — it is they who shall prosper." The sūrah's final sentence mirrors verse 19's verdict on Satan's party (*alā inna ḥizba al-shayṭāni hum al-khāsirūn* — "the party of Satan are losers") with perfect antithesis: losers there, prosperers here. The two parties, defined across nine verses by whispers versus remembrance, oaths versus truth, opposition versus loyalty, now receive their eternal titles. The party of God also appears in Sūrah al-Māʾidah, describing those who refuse protectors besides God, His Messenger, and the believers (5:55–56).

*Al-mufliḥūn* — "those who prosper" — comes from *falāḥ*, the farmer's flourishing: the thriving of well-tilled soil. The same word closed the Qur'an's opening portrait of the reverent (2:5). Between that first *mufliḥūn* and this last one stretches the whole Book — and the definition has never changed. Prosperity is not wealth hoarded (v. 17 said wealth avails nothing), nor cleverness admired (v. 15 called it evil), nor alliances hedged (v. 14 called them homelessness). It is a heart inscribed, a Spirit strengthening, Gardens abiding, and mutual contentment with God. The sūrah that began with one woman's tears ends with the roster of eternity's flourishing — and invites every reader to find his name, by loyalty, among them.
