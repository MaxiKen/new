# Sūrah al-Ḥashr (Chapter 59) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Named after a Gathering**

Sūrah al-Ḥashr takes its name from its second verse, where God describes the expulsion of the Jewish tribe of Banū al-Naḍīr from their fortresses in Madinah as "the first gathering" (*li-awwali al-ḥashr*). The root *ḥ-sh-r* means to gather, collect, and drive together — as a shepherd gathers flocks, or as leaves are raked into a single heap. From this same root comes the Qur'anic name for the Day of Resurrection, *yawm al-ḥashr*, "the Day of Gathering," of which God says:

> *"And warn them of the Day of Gathering (yawm al-ḥashr), about which there is no doubt: a party in the Garden, and a party in the Blaze."* (42:7)

The name is therefore double-edged, and deliberately so. On the surface it commemorates a historical event: a tribe uprooted and gathered out of the oasis in the year 4/625–626. Beneath the surface it gestures toward the great gathering in which every human being will be collected from every grave and assembled before God. The classical commentators were sensitive to this resonance: what happened to Banū al-Naḍīr on a small scale — security demolished, illusions stripped, hidden intentions exposed, hearts laid bare — is a rehearsal of what awaits every community and every soul on the stage of the Last Day. A sūrah that opens with the heavens and earth glorifying God and closes with God's most beautiful Names deliberately frames a local political crisis inside the widest possible horizon.

**The Second Name: Sūrat Banū al-Naḍīr**

The early scholars also knew this chapter by another name, *Sūrat al-Naḍīr* or *Sūrat Banū al-Naḍīr*, because most of its verses were revealed in direct connection with that tribe's banishment — as reported by Āl, al-Jaṣṣāṣ, and al-Ṭabarī. This is a reminder that the Qur'an is not a book of abstract principles that descended into a vacuum. It is divine speech that entered history at precise moments, answering real crises with rulings whose logic then generalizes for all time. The event becomes the lens; the verses become the prescription; and later generations inherit not only the prescription but the lens itself — the capacity to see their own crises through revealed categories.

**The Glorifiers and the Verse Worth a Thousand**

Al-Ḥashr belongs to a family of five sūrahs the scholars call *al-Musabbiḥāt*, "the Glorifiers" — chapters 57 (al-Ḥadīd), 59 (al-Ḥashr), 61 (al-Ṣaff), 62 (al-Jumuʿah), and 64 (al-Taghābun) — all of which open by declaring that everything in the heavens and the earth glorifies God. About this group the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said:

> *"Within the Glorifiers there is a verse that is better than a thousand verses."* (cited by Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Shawkānī in their commentaries)

The scholars differ over which verse is meant. Many, following early reports, apply it to the third verse of Sūrat al-Ḥadīd — *"He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward"* (57:3) — a majestic declaration of God's encompassing all of time and space. Others, as al-Shawkānī records, apply it to the final verse of this very sūrah, the inventory of the divine Names of creation with which al-Ḥashr seals its address. The disagreement itself is instructive: a single chapter of the Qur'an can contain, by common scholarly consent, a verse outweighing a thousand others — a reminder that the Book is not flat terrain but mountainous, with summits that reward those who climb deliberately.

**A Constitution Betrayed: The Road to the Naḍīr's Expulsion**

To read verses 1–10 with understanding, one must first know the history they presuppose. When the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ emigrated from Makkah to Madinah in 1/622, he found the oasis inhabited by three major Jewish tribes — Banū Qaynuqāʿ, Banū al-Naḍīr, and Banū Qurayẓah — alongside the Arab tribes of Aws and Khazraj. He bound them all, Muslim and Jew, into a single covenantal polity, the document commonly called the Constitution of Madinah. Its core clauses were reciprocal: each party would support the others against external attack, and none would make a separate alliance against the commonwealth or shelter an aggressor against it.

The covenant was tested early and often. After the Muslim victory at Badr in 2/624, Banū Qaynuqāʿ were judged to have broken its terms and were banished. After the Muslim setback at Uḥud in 3/625, the branches of treachery multiplied. Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf, a chieftain of Banū al-Naḍīr, traveled to Makkah to inflame Quraysh for revenge and composed verses inciting war against the Muslims in Madinah; he was subsequently executed with the Prophet's sanction — an episode recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. Then came the decisive breach. A Muslim envoy, ʿAmr ibn Umayyah al-Ḍamrī, killed two men of Banū ʿĀmir in circumstances that made the Muslims liable, under the Constitution, to pay blood-money jointly with their confederates. The Prophet ﷺ went in person, with a small party of Companions, to the fortresses of Banū al-Naḍīr — who were party to that liability — to request their contribution to the blood-money. They received him outwardly with courtesy and asked him to wait while food was prepared. Behind the wall, however, some of them plotted to kill him: ʿAmr ibn Jaḥsh, according to the reports preserved by Ibn Isḥāq, al-Ṭabarī, and Ibn Kathīr, was to climb onto the roof above the Prophet's seat and drop a millstone on him.

**The Deliverance from Whence They Reckoned Not**

The assassination never occurred. The Prophet ﷺ, informed — as most reports say, through the Archangel Gabriel, though others say through a woman of the Naḍīr who disclosed the plot — rose quietly, left the fortress without a word, and returned to Madinah. When his Companions realized he had departed, they followed. The tribe was then given an ultimatum: leave the oasis with their families and movable wealth, retaining the proceeds of their lands, or face the consequences of their treachery. They requested ten days' respite and consulted their allies — among them ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy, the chief of Madinah's hypocrites, who sent them the promise this sūrah quotes in verse 11: *hold fast to your fortresses; we will not abandon you; if you are fought we will fight beside you; if you are expelled we will leave with you.* Confident in walls, allies, and arms, they chose war. The Muslim forces besieged their fortresses — the sources speak of some three weeks — and the campaign remained largely a war of nerves and arrows. When the Muslims cut down and burned a stand of the Naḍīr's prized date palms visible from their ramparts, and when the promised reinforcements never materialized, the tribe's resolve collapsed. They negotiated their departure: exile with their wealth, minus their weapons, forfeiting land and crops. Most settled in Khaybar to the north; others made for Syria. Ibn Kathīr preserves the scene of their caravan — hundreds of camels, loaded with all their possessions, the women dressed in finery, singers and drums marking a proud exit — a pageant of wealth that only underlined the loss of the one thing it could not carry: the land, which God had turned over to His Messenger and, through him, to the community's poor.

**From Event to Economics: The Discovery of Fayʾ**

What made this campaign unlike any before it was that it produced wealth without battle — no cavalry charge, no storming of walls, no spoils gathered from a battlefield. That novel circumstance demanded a novel ruling, and verses 6–7 supply it: the property of the Naḍīr was not war-booty (*ghanīmah*) to be divided among the fighters, but *fayʾ* — literally "a return," from the root *f-y-ʾ* — wealth that returns to God and His Messenger because it was surrendered without the spurring of a single horse. The Prophet distributed it to the destitute Emigrants, to two impoverished Helpers, and to public needs, establishing a principle of distributive justice — that unearned communal windfalls must not become a plaything of the rich — that would later shape the fiscal policy of the entire Islamic empire. In this way a local confiscation in a small oasis became the seed of Islamic public finance.

**A Map of the Chapter**

The sūrah unfolds in five movements. Verses 1–5 narrate the Naḍīr affair and draw its lessons — God's hidden agency in history, the self-destruction of treachery, and the limits of destruction even in lawful war. Verses 6–10 legislate the *fayʾ* and, in the process, paint three luminous portraits: the Emigrants whose truth was proven by sacrifice, the Helpers whose generosity was proven by self-preference, and the successors whose prayer frees the heart from rancor. Verses 11–17 turn from believers to hypocrites, exposing the hollowness of their promises by comparing them to Satan abandoning those he seduced. Verses 18–20 summon every soul to the audit of the morrow, warn against forgetting God and thereby forgetting oneself, and divide humanity's final destinations into two unequal companies. Verses 21–24 then lift the curtain entirely: a mountain would shatter under the weight of this Qur'an, and the chapter closes with the most comprehensive inventory of divine Names in the Book — the passage the tradition calls among the "Jewels of the Qur'an," recited devotionally morning and night for fourteen centuries.

**The Ring of Praise**

Notice, finally, the architecture. Verse 1 declares: *"Whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth glorifies God; and He is the Mighty, the Wise."* Verse 24 closes: *"Whatsoever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Him; and He is the Mighty, the Wise."* The sūrah is a ring — a circle of cosmic praise inside which all of human history, with its treaties and betrayals, its sieges and redistributions, its hypocrisy and its hope, takes place. Between the two hinges nothing in the universe stops glorifying God, not even during the weeks a tribe dismantled its own homes. That is the sūrah's deepest premise: the noise of events is real, but it is not the fundamental sound. Underneath the clash of politics runs an unbroken bass-line of praise, and whoever learns to hear it — as this chapter trains its hearers to do — can face the rise and fall of nations without losing equilibrium.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary treats each of the 24 verses as an independent discourse, in strict sequence, exactly as the workflow mandates: every verse is quoted individually, then expanded through Arabic language, the classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, and others — authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the logic of each ruling, and its bearing on modern life. Where the source commentary treats verses together (vv. 3–4, 11–12, 16–17, 22–24), they are here separated and given full individual treatment. Nothing is compressed because the chapter is compact: al-Ḥashr may be short in verses, but it is long in consequence — a sūrah in which God legislates economics, dissects hypocrisy, and then, in three closing verses, teaches His servants how to name Him.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:1

> **Whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth glorifies God, and He is the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Opening Word of a Whole Family of Sūrahs**

The sūrah begins with the Arabic verb *sabbaha* — "he/it glorified" — from the root *s-b-ḥ*, whose primal meaning is to swim, to glide, to move through a medium with ease and without obstruction. From this concrete sense of smooth, effortless motion, Arabic derived *tasbīḥ*: the declaration *"subḥāna Allāh"* — "glory be to God," "God is far above every imperfection." The connection between the meanings is profound. To glorify God is not merely to utter a formula; it is to move through existence the way a fish moves through water — in perfect, unresisting conformity with one's nature and one's Lord. The fish does not argue with the sea. The stars do not negotiate their orbits. Their very being-in-motion is an act of praise. Al-Ḥashr is the second of the five *Musabbiḥāt*, the "Glorifiers" (57, 59, 61, 62, 64), and the choice of the past tense *sabbaha* — rather than a command or a future — announces a settled, accomplished fact: glorification is not something creation might do if it chooses; it is something creation is already doing. The heavens and the earth are not asked to praise. The verse simply reports that they do.

**When the Prophet's Pebbles Spoke**

The Qur'an itself insists that this glorification is real speech, not a metaphor for mechanical order:

> *"The seven heavens and the earth and whatsoever is in them glorify Him; and there is no thing, save that it hymns His praise, though you do not understand their praise. Truly He is Clement, Forgiving."* (17:44)

The last clause is tender: your failure to comprehend their praise is not evidence against it — it is only a measure of the narrowness of your ears. The Prophet ﷺ was granted windows into this hidden sound. Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh reports his words:

> *"I know a stone in Makkah that used to greet me with peace before I was sent; I know it even now."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2277)

The famous palm trunk that moaned like a child when the Prophet stepped down from it to deliver the sermon from a pulpit — an event recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī — until he gathered it into his arms and it was stilled; the report transmitted in the Musnad of Aḥmad that pebbles rested in his palm audibly glorifying God; God's subjugating the mountains to echo David's hymns, *"and We subjugated the mountains with David to glorify [Us] in the evening and at daybreak"* (38:18; cf. 21:79) — all of these confirm the same Qur'anic doctrine: inert matter is inert only to our instruments, not to its Maker. When a geologist's seismograph reads the earth's slow groan, or physics describes every particle as vibration, the believer is not reading science into scripture; he is remembering that there is no such thing in God's universe as silence.

**Every Level of Creation Is Included**

The verse is exhaustive in its grammar: *mā fī al-samāwāti* — "whatsoever is in the heavens" — and *mā fī al-ard* — "whatsoever is on the earth." Not "whosoever," as though only rational beings were meant, but *whatsoever*: the particle *mā* sweeps in angels, humans, jinn, animals, plants, minerals, winds, galaxies, and whatever exists that no taxonomy has yet named. Elsewhere the Qur'an widens the choir further:

> *"Do you not see that God is glorified by all who are in the heavens and the earth, and the birds with wings outspread? Each knows its prayer and its glorification; and God knows what they do."* (24:41)

"Each knows its prayer" — every creature has its own liturgy, fitted to its own design. The bird's liturgy is its flight and song; the bee's is its labor; the whale's is its turning through the deep. Al-Qurṭubī remarks on 17:44 that everything has a *tasbīḥ*, but God reserved the comprehension of each thing's praise for Himself — and He allowed His Prophet, as a miracle, to hear some of it. The believers' ambition is not to hear it now, but to be included in it: that their own work, study, rest, and love might become, consciously, one more voice in the universal sound.

**The Difference Between Our Praise and Theirs**

Here lies a theological distinction the scholars have long savored. The glorification of stars and stones is the worship of nature — necessary, unceasing, and unfailing, because it flows from what they are. The glorification of a human being is voluntary: we can withhold our tongues while our tongues' very cells go on praising the One who made them. This is the paradox of the human station that Sūrat al-Aḥzāb states in one breath:

> *"Truly We offered the Trust unto the heavens and the earth and the mountains, but they refused to bear it and were wary of it — yet man bore it; surely he is wrongful, given to ignorance."* (33:72)

The mountains declined the burden of free worship out of awe — an awe so heavy that, as verse 21 of this very sūrah will say, were the Qur'an sent down upon a mountain, it would be *humbled, rent asunder from the fear of God*. Man accepted the burden. That means our praise can be corrupt, hypocritical, or absent — and that also means, when it is sincere, it rises above the praise of galaxies, because it is given, not extracted. Ibn Taymiyyah captures this when he observes that all existence is already God's obedient servant in its very createdness; the human servant alone can be God's servant by choice, and this is both his danger and his dignity. The verse's universality thus becomes a gentle rebuke: the date palm that the Naḍīr watched being cut down never once broke a covenant with its Maker; the men behind the walls had.

**Why "the Mighty, the Wise"?**

The verse closes not with a generic attribute but with a precisely weighted pair. *Al-ʿAzīz*, "the Mighty," is the God whose power cannot be overpowered — nothing in creation can resist His decree; He is the Incomparable in *qudrah*. *Al-Ḥakīm*, "the Wise," is the God whose power is never arbitrary — every act of His flows from perfect knowledge and perfect purpose. Al-Rāzī reflects on why the pairing matters: power without wisdom would be terrifying randomness, and wisdom without power would be tragic futility; the universe testifies to both together, since its machinery runs — massively — with neither collapse nor waste. The order of the Names is also the logic of the sūrah that follows. Al-Ḥashr will narrate how a fortress-dwelling tribe was expelled from an impregnable oasis without a pitched battle: that is *al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty, who humbles what walls cannot protect. And it will narrate how even the cutting of palm trees was measured, permitted, and bounded by law and purpose: that is *al-Ḥakīm* — the Wise, who permits nothing without reason. A sūrah about God's intervention in history must open by declaring the two attributes His interventions most display: irresistible power, perfectly directed.

**History Inside a Symphony**

There is a final, quiet genius in beginning a chapter of political crisis with cosmic praise. The events about to be narrated — a betrayed covenant, a siege, an exile, a redistribution of wealth, the exposure of hypocrites — are the kind of events that fill newspapers and consume human attention. The verse performs a deliberate act of perspective: before you read a single line about Banū al-Naḍīr, you are reminded that while all of it happened, nothing in the heavens and the earth ever paused in its glorification. History is turbulent at its surface and serene at its depth. The believer who internalizes verse 1 acquires what psychologists would now call a stable frame of reference: engaged in the crisis of the day, but standing on the floor of an ocean whose currents never stop moving toward God. The sūrah's last verse will repeat this one almost word for word — a literary ring, its two hinges holding everything between them in the embrace of praise. Whatever else chapter 59 teaches, it teaches this first and last: *the universe is not neutral ground; it is a sanctuary, and everything in it is at prayer.*

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:2

> **He it is Who expelled those who disbelieved among the People of the Book from their homes at the first gathering. You did not think they would go forth, and they thought their fortresses would protect them from God. Then God came upon them whence they reckoned not, and cast terror into their hearts, as they razed their houses with their own hands and with the hands of the believers. So take heed, O you who are possessed of sight.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God as the Subject of the Sentence**

The verse's first words are a lesson in theology before they are a lesson in history: *Huwa alladhī akhrajā* — "He it is Who expelled." Not "the Prophet expelled," not "the Muslims expelled." The Prophet ﷺ commanded the campaign and the believers besieged the fortresses, yet the Qur'an attributes the outcome entirely to God. This is the Qur'an's consistent grammar of victory. At Badr it said:

> *"You did not slay them, but God slew them; and you did not throw when you threw, but God threw."* (8:17)

The purpose is not to erase human agency but to correct human arrogance. A community that believes its own cavalry, numbers, or generalship produced its security will be destroyed by the first battle that exposes the illusion; a community that knows victory comes *"only from God, the Mighty, the Wise"* (3:126) can neither be intoxicated by success nor shattered by setback. The same grammar disciplines the victor's conscience: what happened to the Naḍīr was not a land-grab by a strong tribe against a weak one — it was an act permitted, timed, and executed under God's governance, and therefore one for which the Muslim community would itself answer to the Expeller of tyrants.

**"Those Who Disbelieved Among the People of the Book": A Precise Indictment, Not a Blanket One**

The Qur'an is careful with its nouns. It does not say "the People of the Book were expelled"; it says *"alladhīna kafarū min ahl al-kitāb"* — those who disbelieved from among the People of the Book. The clause restricts the verdict to the covenant-breakers of Banū al-Naḍīr, not to Jews as such. The wider Qur'an guards this distinction explicitly:

> *"They are not alike. Among the People of the Book is an upright community, reciting God's signs through the night, prostrating themselves."* (3:113)

Throughout the Prophet's career, individual Jews and Jewish communities that honored their treaties remained protected under the Constitution of Madinah — as the same code that expelled the Naḍīr protected others. The lesson for every generation, including ours, is one of measured justice: guilt here is tied to a specific act of treachery by specific people, proved against them. The verse gives no warrant for punishing a religion or a lineage for the crime of a faction. Indeed, "disbelieved" in this context means, as the classical commentators note, their refusal to honor what their own scripture and their covenant obligated — the concrete disbelief of a broken oath rather than an abstract theological verdict.

**"The First Gathering": The Name of the Sūrah Explained**

The expulsion is called *li-awwali al-ḥashr*, "the first gathering," because — as Ibn Kathīr explains — it was the first time a group was driven out and gathered out of the oasis of Madinah itself; the second gathering would be Banū Qurayẓah after the Battle of the Trench, and the third the expulsion of the remaining Jews from the entire Ḥijāz in the year of Khaybar. Al-Ṭabarī records an alternative reading: "to the first gathering," meaning that the Naḍīr were initially gathered for exile to Syria, and only afterward gathered again in Khaybar. Either way, the phrase is ordinal — first — and thereby warns that history was not finished: a divine sunnah was being set in motion, of which this was only the opening instance. The sūrah's name, al-Ḥashr, forever preserves the memory that God gathers communities out of their settlements when their corruption in them becomes incurable — and the ordinal quietly hints at the final Ḥashr, the gathering of all souls on the Day of Judgment, of which every earthly expulsion is a miniature.

**The Two False Calculations**

The verse then analyzes, with devastating economy, two failures of prediction. First: *"You did not think they would go forth."* The Muslims themselves — the besiegers! — doubted that the fortress-dwellers would ever leave. The Naḍīr were rich, entrenched, armed, and allied; the rational military assessment favored their staying power. Second, and more damning: *"They thought their fortresses would protect them from God."* Note what the verse does not say — it does not say "protect them from the Muslims." The Qur'an deliberately writes God into the equation that the Naḍīr had left out of theirs. In their calculus there were walls, men, and weapons; in reality's calculus there was God, of whom walls are a single layer of dust. This is the definition of secular intelligence — brilliant modeling of visible variables, blind to the one Variable that overrides them all. The Qur'an elsewhere names this delusion as the signature error of doomed powers:

> *"They deemed their fortresses would protect them from God; but God came upon them whence they reckoned not, and cast terror into their hearts."* — the very phrase is repeated verbatim of the Qurayẓah in 33:26, marking it as a divine pattern, not a one-off event.

**Whence They Reckoned Not: The Blind Spot Where God Works**

*Min ḥaythu lam yaḥtasibū* — "from whence they did not reckon." The root *ḥ-s-b* is the language of accounting, of computing possibilities. The Naḍīr had computed every scenario: siege by force, assault on the walls, betrayal by their allies. What they had not computed was a silent walk-out by the Prophet and the sudden, total exposure of their own secret — the plot that was never executed had already defeated them. God's providence specializes in the uncomputed column of the ledger. And here the Qur'an creates a stunning mirror with a verse it revealed about the opposite case — the reverent:

> *"Whosoever reverences God, He will make a way out for him, and provide him from whence he does not reckon."* (65:2–3)

The same divine economics operate in both directions. For the person of taqwā, the unexpected is a channel of provision; for the covenant-breaker, the unexpected is a channel of undoing. Whoever leaves a blind spot in their planning — the God-dimension — should know that is precisely where their fate is being decided. Modern strategic studies call this the "unknown unknowns"; the Qur'an revealed its ultimate cause thirteen centuries earlier: it is God Who works *from whence we reckon not*, either to rescue or to requite.

**Terror in the Heart: Warfare Without Slaughter**

*"Wa-qadhafa fī qulūbihim al-ruʿb"* — "and He cast terror into their hearts." The verb *qadhafa* is violent — to hurl, to fling, as one hurls a spear. This terror was not the fruit of a massacre; the sources record that the three-week siege cost almost no blood, the exchanges being limited to arrows. The Naḍīr were defeated before the walls were breached — defeated inside their own breasts, which is the surest and least bloody field of victory. The Qur'an presents this as God's characteristic instrument:

> *"We shall cast terror into the hearts of those who disbelieve, because they ascribe partners unto God without any authority from Him."* (3:151)

>"*I shall cast terror into the hearts of those who disbelieve; so strike above the necks, and strike every fingertip of them."* (8:12)

The moral logic deserves attention rather than embarrassment. A God who can end a war by collapsing the enemy's will is choosing the option that minimizes graves. The siege of the Naḍīr is, by the standards of ancient warfare — and by the standards of much modern warfare — an astonishingly humane campaign: ultimatum before siege, siege before assault, negotiations throughout, exile rather than execution at the end. The terror the verse names was not produced by atrocity but by conscience: men who knew what they had plotted behind a wall of hospitality discovering that the wall had been transparent to Heaven all along.

**Razing Their Own Houses: The Autobiography of Treachery**

The strangest image of the verse is the most haunting: *"they razed their houses with their own hands and with the hands of the believers."* Al-Ṭabarī explains the plain sense: as the Naḍīr prepared for exile, they dismantled their own homes to carry away the doors, beams, and ceilings — the valuable fixtures — leaving shells of masonry behind. But the commentators hear the parable beneath the archaeology. A community that breaks its covenant demolishes its own house; treachery is structurally self-destructive before it is anything else. The Qur'an states this law openly elsewhere:

> *"Corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what men's hands have earned, that He may make them taste something of what they have done, that haply they may return."* (30:41)

The Naḍīr's hands, which had lifted a millstone toward a guest's skull, ended by pulling down their own rooftrees. Every betrayer is, in slow motion, a demolition crew for his own dwelling — a truth as visible in the collapse of corrupt corporations and regimes today as it was in a Madinan fortress in the year 4. And the phrase "with the hands of the believers" adds the second half of the law: God ordinarily brings the consequences of treachery *through* other human agents, so that both the sin and its reckoning remain inside human history, legible to everyone.

**"So Take Heed, O You Who Are Possessed of Sight"**

The verse ends by converting history into homework: *fa-ʿtabirū yā ulī al-abṣār*. The verb *iʿtabara* comes from the root *ʿ-b-r*, "to cross over" — as one crosses a bridge or wadi. To take heed is to cross over from the seen event to the unseen meaning it carries; it is to refuse to let experience remain mere spectacle. The Qur'an is here institutionalizing what later epistemology would call inference from evidence — and one of Islam's greatest philosophers saw the verse doing exactly that. Ibn Rushd (Averroës) cites this very passage as scriptural proof of the obligation of rational inference:

> *"The injunction to take heed is a proof of the obligatory nature of the syllogism, or of both the syllogism and legal reasoning (qiyās)."* (Ibn Rushd, Faṣl al-maqāl, 28)

In other words: God does not merely permit His servants to reason from events to principles — He commands it, and commands it under the description of *sight*. The address "O you who are possessed of sight" (*ulī al-abṣār*) is deliberately physiological: not "O you who possess eloquence" or "O scholars," but all who have eyes. The physical eye is the least unequally distributed of human endowments, and the verse implies that the lesson of the Naḍīr is available to any honest observer willing to *cross over* from the event to its Author, its pattern, and its warning. The verse is thus a standing indictment of the modern information diet: we are the most event-saturated civilization in history and perhaps the least *iʿtibār*-practicing — consuming collapse after collapse, betrayal after betrayal, without once crossing the bridge from the spectacle to the sermon that God has embedded in it.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:3

> **Had God not ordained banishment for them, He would have punished them in this world. And in the Hereafter theirs shall be the punishment of the Fire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grammar of Decree: "Had God Not Written"**

The verse turns on a single verb of astonishing weight: *kataba* — "He wrote, decreed, ordained." Writing in Arabic legal idiom is the final form of a decision: what is written is settled, signed, beyond renegotiation. The Qur'an repeatedly presents God's eternal decree under the image of writing — in a Book, on a Preserved Tablet, before the events themselves unfold. The Believer's creed includes this writtenness as an article of faith; when the Prophet ﷺ defined Islam in the famous ḥadīth of Gabriel, belief in divine decree (*al-qadar*), "its good and its evil," was among the six pillars he enumerated (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 8). Here the writing concerns a judgment already executed: banishment was not an improvisation of the battlefield; it was a sentence that had come down from the divine pen before the first arrow flew. Yet — and this the Qur'an never lets us forget — the Naḍīr chose every step that walked them into that sentence. Their plotting was free; its consequences were decreed. Islamic theology refuses to dissolve this paradox in either direction: no coercion from God that cancels human responsibility, and no freedom from man that escapes God's encompassing pen. Ibn Taymiyyah would later formulate the balance: God created the acts of His servants, and the servants truly perform them — the decree is the envelope, not the eraser, of choice.

**Exile as Mercy in Disguise**

The conditional is startling: *Had God not ordained banishment for them, He would have punished them in this world.* Al-Ṭabarī explains the implied alternative: execution and capture — the fate, in fact, that overtook Banū Qurayẓah two years later for their treachery during the Battle of the Trench. Read carefully, the verse inverts our emotional categories. What looked like catastrophe — a tribe stripped of ancestral lands and marched out of the oasis — was in the scale of divine justice the *lenient* sentence, exile instead of the sword, banishment instead of the grave. The Prophet ﷺ had offered them terms milder still — departure with wealth and continued income from their lands — and they had gambled them away on the word of Ibn Ubayy. God's decreed punishment for them was, in truth, a graduated reduction of severity at every stage of their intransigence. This teaches a principle of providence that the Qur'an states as a universal law:

> *"And We shall certainly make them taste the lesser punishment before the greater, that haply they may return."* (32:21)

Worldly pain, in God's economy, is not vengeance; it is an advance installment of warning — a smoke alarm before the fire — designed, in its severity, to be survivable enough to wake the sleeper. A punishment in this world can still be repented from; a punishment reserved entirely for the next cannot.

**The Two-Track System of Justice**

The verse lays out what the scholars call the dual reckoning: *"He would have punished them in this world"* — and even that lighter sentence was remitted into exile — *"and in the Hereafter theirs shall be the punishment of the Fire."* For those who die in their defiance, the worldly consequence does not close the account. The Qur'an's doctrine of deferred justice answers one of the deepest human complaints: the prosperous tyrant, the treacherous dealer who dies in his bed. Is treachery profitable after all? The verse's answer is architectural: this world is only the ground floor. The full settlement is stored upstairs. Two further truths hang from this. First, worldly suffering can be *expiation*: the Prophet ﷺ said that no fatigue, illness, worry, grief, or hardship afflicts a believer — down to a thorn's prick — but that God expiates some of his sins by it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5641; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2573). For the believer, pain pays debts; for the defiant, pain is only a foretaste. Second, respite is not acquittal. The Qur'an warns the prosperous wrongdoer explicitly:

> *"Let not those who disbelieve suppose that the respite We grant them is good for them; We grant them respite only that they may increase in sin; and theirs shall be a humiliating punishment."* (3:178)

The Naḍīr walked out of Madinah with their gold and their singers — Ibn Kathīr preserves the image of the caravan dressed in its finery — and an onlooker might have called that escape. The verse corrects the onlooker: what they carried away was only the perishable portion; the Fire's account remained open.

**Why Decree and Choice Meet Here Without Contradiction**

A closer look at the wording settles a classic objection before it is raised. The verse does not say "God banished them against their will"; it says God *ordained banishment* — and the preceding verses have just narrated, in detail, the free choices that led to it: the plot against the Prophet's life, the rejection of generous terms, the wager on false allies, the refusal to surrender until the palms fell. Divine decree, in Qur'anic presentation, is not a puppet-string pulled against the puppet's will; it is the perfect knowledge and encompassing will of God that includes — without coercing — the free wickedness of creatures and then writes its consequences into history. Theologians after al-Ashʿarī and al-Māturīdī labored to state this precisely; the Qur'an itself states it narratively: Pharaoh's hardness was decreed, and Pharaoh hardened himself; the Naḍīr's exile was written, and they signed it with their own plotting. For the practical life of faith this is not a puzzle to solve but a posture to adopt: *work and choose as if everything depended on you, and trust the outcome as if everything depended on the Pen* — because it does.

**The Psychology of Pain That Wakes Rather Than Destroys**

There is a finally a humane insight here that modern readers can verify in their own experience. Consequences that arrive quickly, proportionately, and survivably are corrective; consequences deferred indefinitely compound into catastrophe. A child who touches a warm stove learns at the cost of a blister; a civilization that breaks covenant after covenant without tasting consequence accumulates — in the Qur'an's own metaphor — a debt whose interest is despair. The Naḍīr received the fast, survivable version of justice: alive, wealthy, warned. That the warning did not reform them is recorded in the sequel of their story — their leadership went to Khaybar and helped organize the confederate armies that besieged Madinah in the year of the Trench. God's mercy had carried them out of Madinah intact; they used their survival to buy a second war. The verse's sobering coda — *"in the Hereafter theirs shall be the punishment of the Fire"* — belongs to those who spend mercy on munitions. For everyone else, the lesson runs in the opposite direction: when God sends you the *lesser* punishment — the exile, the loss, the discomfort that could have been annihilation — read it as an invitation to return, because the door of return, in this world, is precisely what the pain is for.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:4

> **That is because they defied God and His Messenger; and whosoever defies God and His Messenger, truly God is severe in retribution.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Splitting the Staff: The Violence Hidden in "Defied"**

The causal clause — *"dhālika bi-annahum shāqqū Allāha wa-rasūlahu"* — "that is because they split away from God and His Messenger" — carries a physical image inside its root. The verb *shāqqa* comes from *shaqq*, to split, cleave, tear in two — the source of the Arabic idiom *shaqqa ʿaṣā al-qawm*, "he split the tribe's staff," meaning he broke the community's unity and went his own way. The Naḍīr had not merely disagreed with the Prophet ﷺ; they had torn the civic and covenantal unity of Madinah in two. When they plotted to drop a stone on the head of the man who had come to them under the flag of the Constitution — the same pact that obligated them to mutual defense — they were not committing a private crime; they were splitting the staff of an entire commonwealth. This is why the Qur'an names the offense *shiqāq* against God and not merely against Muhammad: because a covenant made under God's name and sworn by His authority is, in Islamic theology, a vertical treaty. Breaking it is not only horizontal injury to one's fellow citizens; it is defiance aimed upward at the One in whose name the treaty was sealed.

**An Echo from Badr: The Qur'an's Case-Law Method**

Students of the Qur'an will hear the verse deliberately repeating a verse revealed years earlier about the prisoners of Badr:

> *"That is because they defied God and His Messenger; and whosoever defies God and His Messenger, God is severe in retribution."* (8:13)

The wording is virtually verbatim — and the repetition is jurisprudence in action. What happened at Badr (the treaty-breaking that provoked the battle) and what happened at the walls of the Naḍīr fortresses are two instances of a single revealed principle: *covenant-breaking against God and His Messenger places one under the special severity of God's retribution.* The Qur'an thereby teaches its readers its own method of generalization: from concrete event to revealed principle, from revealed principle back onto new events. Sūrah 58 states the same law in the register of history:

> *"Truly those who defy God and His Messenger will be laid low, as those before them were laid low."* (58:5)

A pattern named once is a fact; a pattern named thrice is a law of the moral universe, as stable as gravity — and just as indifferent to whether one believes in it.

**Why Treason Ranks Among the Gravest Sins**

Modern readers may wonder why the Qur'an attaches such severity — *shadīd al-ʿiqāb*, "severe in retribution" — to what might look like a political betrayal, weighing it in language usually reserved for idolaters. The answer lies in what covenants are. Human civilization is not held together by walls or weapons; it is held together by *promises kept* — treaties, contracts, testimony, marriage vows, the assumption that a signature means something. Every kept promise is a small deposit into humanity's bank of trust; every betrayal is a withdrawal made with a forged check. A community can survive poverty, defeat, even disaster, but it cannot survive the normalization of treachery, because every one of its institutions silently runs on trust. This is why God pairs the covenant with His own Name throughout the Qur'an:

> *"And fulfill the covenant of God when you have made a covenant, and do not break your oaths after they have been ratified, having made God your surety. Truly God knows what you do."* (16:91)

> *"And those who break the covenant of God after its ratification, and cut asunder what God commanded to be joined, and make corruption in the land — it is they who are the losers."* (2:27)

The believer is defined, in the very anatomy of his faith, as a person whose word can be trusted: *"those who faithfully observe their trusts and their covenants"* (23:8). Conversely, the Qur'an can say of the munāfiq, the hypocrite, that his betrayal is not incidental but constitutional — the Prophet ﷺ said: *"The signs of a hypocrite are three: when he speaks, he lies; when he promises, he betrays; and when he is entrusted, he proves treacherous"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 33; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 59). The Naḍīr affair stands as the revealed case study of this principle at the level of nations.

**The Prophet's Own Fidelity as the Standard**

The severity of this verse shines brighter against the Prophet's own scrupulousness in keeping his covenants — even with the treacherous. The Qur'an commands him:

> *"So long as they stand true to you, stand true to them; truly God loves the reverent."* (9:4)

At Ḥudaybiyyah, when terms that seemed humiliating to his Companions were signed, he honored them to the letter — and God called the treaty *"a manifest victory"* (48:1). When the Naḍīr ultimately took up arms and lost, he still allowed them to depart with their families, wealth, and even their musical procession, rather than treating them as the execution law of treason would have permitted. The point is of the highest moral importance: the severity of verse 4 belongs to God alone, and the mercy shown in the event belongs to His Prophet as a model. God declares the true gravity of the crime; the Prophet demonstrates how even the wronged state should temper justice with clemency — and the believer learns to hold both without confusing them: never excuse treachery, never delight in its punishment.

**"Severe in Retribution": The Meaning of ʿIqāb**

The closing divine attribute — *shadīd al-ʿiqāb* — repays a word study. *ʿIqāb* derives from a root meaning "to follow on the heels," and thus "consequence" — the requital that follows an act as a shadow follows the body. The Qur'an's theology of punishment is therefore never arbitrary violence; it is *sequel*, the second half of a story the sinner began writing himself. And *shadīd* — severe, intense — modifies not God's temper but the *gravity* of what defiance has unloosed. There is an old and wise observation in the tafsīr tradition that God's very Names of majesty are a mercy to the wronged: the oppressed of the earth can survive oppression because they know that somewhere above the maze of human power there is a Judge who cannot be bribed, blinded, or outrun. Erase *shadīd al-ʿiqāb* from your theology and you have not made God kinder; you have made the Naḍīr's victims — and every betrayed people in history — orphans. For daily life the verse leaves a practical discipline: keep covenant even when breaking it would be profitable, convenient, and undetectable — because it is never undetected, and because every unkept promise is, in the Qur'an's ledger, a split staff aimed at Heaven.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:5

> **Whatsoever of the palm trees you cut down or left standing upon their roots, it was by God's Leave, and that He might disgrace the iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Born from an Argument About Trees**

Few verses of the Qur'an have so precise and human a birth. As the siege of the Naḍīr's fortresses wore on, the Prophet ﷺ ordered the cutting down and burning of a stand of their prized date palms that lay within sight of their ramparts — the area the sīrah literature calls al-Buwayrah. Dates were the Naḍīr's wealth, and the palms were their pride; the sight of burning orchards was designed to bend their will, and it did. But the Naḍīr immediately weaponized the moral vocabulary of the Muslims themselves. Ibn Kathīr and al-Wāḥidī preserve their protest: *You claim, O Muhammad, that you seek goodness — is there goodness in cutting down fruitful trees? Is corruption upon the earth part of what has been revealed to you?* The objection struck home. The Prophet ﷺ was distressed; the Companions fell into open disagreement — some refusing to cut any further, since the orchards were potential spoils for the Muslims and destruction of fruit trees seemed wanton; others continuing, since pressure shortened the war and spared blood. Into this live dispute — a dispute about the ethics of means during an active campaign — the verse descended. And its ruling is a masterpiece of nuance: cutting was *lawful* under these circumstances — *"it was by God's Leave"* — yet the pause of the Companions was vindicated, since the very need for revelation proved the act was not self-evidently right; and both cutting and sparing are subsumed under one providential purpose: *"that He might disgrace the iniquitous."*

**Līnah: The Word for the Palms Worth Fighting Over**

The Qur'an's term is *līnah*, from the root *l-y-n*, "softness." Classical lexicographers differ: for some it names all date palms, for others the choicest variety — the soft, fine, superior dates as against the common kind. The choice of word is pointed either way. These were not scrub trees on a hillside; they were the soft, the excellent, the cultivated best of the oasis — the aristocrats of Madinan agriculture, and therefore the perfect index of what the Naḍīr valued most. Underneath the fiqh of the verse lies a shrewd psychology of siege: God allowed the pressure to fall exactly on the community's treasure, not on its women and children. The campaign targeted trees to avoid targeting bodies — a proportionality that makes the Naḍīr's rhetorical protest, *"is this goodness?"*, deeply ironic: the alternative to burning palms was storming walls.

**The Fiqh of War: Means Are Never Neutral**

The verse founded one of the most humane legal traditions in the history of warfare: the prohibition of gratuitous destruction. The scholars concluded — Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, and the legal commentators cited by al-Baghwī — that cutting down enemy trees and crops without operational necessity is forbidden in Islamic law; necessity, judged strictly, may permit it, and even then only to the extent the necessity requires. The Companions' practice universalized the principle. When Abū Bakr sent the army of Usāmah forth, the transmitted counsel of instruction included: do not commit treachery, do not kill women, children, or the aged, and *do not cut down fruit trees* — a text preserved in Mālik's Muwaṭṭaʾ and the standard sīrah histories. The Qur'an's own theological grounding for this ethic appears in its portrait of the corrupter:

> *"And when he turns away, he hastens about the earth to corrupt it and destroy crops and cattle; and God loves not corruption."* (2:205)

Thirteen centuries before the Hague Regulations forbade the destruction of enemy property "unless such destruction be imperatively demanded by the necessities of war," the Qur'an had already located the exact line the modern world would later rediscover: destruction must be a surgical instrument of necessity, never an expression of rage. Any reader of twentieth-century history — of scorched-earth retreats, of chemical defoliation of forests, of the deliberate starving of cities — can measure how far ahead of practice this seventh-century revelation still stands.

**"By God's Leave": Even the Axe Is Under Authorisation**

The phrase *bi-idhni Allāhi* — "by God's Leave" — does more than retroactively legalize the cutting. It subordinates every military decision, down to the treatment of individual trees, to a doctrine of *idhn*, permission. Nothing that happens in the campaign — not the siege, not the burning, not the eventual surrender — occurs on human authority alone; each act stands or falls under a warrant issued from above. The Qur'an generalizes this providential bookkeeping:

> *"No disaster strikes the earth, or yourselves, but that it is in a Book before We bring it forth. Truly that is easy for God."* (57:22)

For the believing soldier this is both humility and consolation: humility, because even his smallest decision is audited by Heaven; consolation, because the outcome of the war was never in his axe or his catapult to begin with. And there is a subtle apologetic in the phrase as well. The Naḍīr had alleged that cutting the palms contradicted revelation; the reply came that the cutting was itself *contained* in revelation — foreknown, permitted, and turned by God toward an end the protesters could not see.

**"That He Might Disgrace the Iniquitous": The Purpose of Khizy**

The verse's final clause names its goal: *fa-yukhziya al-fāsiqīn* — "that He might disgrace the iniquitous." *Khizy* is public shaming — the exposure of what was hidden. And *fāsiq*, from the root *f-s-q*, is a marvel of Arabic natural history: its primal meaning is a date bursting out of its skin — the fruit that splits its casing and escapes its proper place. A fāsiq is one who has split the casing of the covenant, burst out of the bounds God set, and thereby ruined himself as a ruined date is ruined — not by the farmer, but by its own splitting. The disgrace here is specific and surgical: the Naḍīr's pride was their wealth, and their wealth was the palms; when the palms burned, their legend of invincibility burned with them, and the watching city — including the hypocrites who had promised them rescue — saw exactly what their defiance was worth. God's punishments are often precisely aimed at the idol: the fortress-dwellers loved their walls, so they lost their homes; the palm aristocrats loved their orchards, so the orchards went first. Whatever a community places between itself and God becomes, in His economy, the first thing He addresses.

**Leadership Under Fire — and Under Criticism**

Perhaps the most quietly modern element of the verse is its portrait of leadership amid moral criticism from the adversary's own propaganda. The Prophet ﷺ did not dismiss the objection as enemy talking-points, though it was; he was *distressed* — the reports say his concern was visible — and he allowed the community to debate openly, and he waited for heaven to rule before the practice was entrenched. Here is a model for every institution: moral criticism from bad-faith sources can still contain a good-faith question, and a leader confident in God does not fear pausing to examine his own means. The verse vindicates the Prophet's tactics while honoring the scruple of the objectors — cutting was lawful, but so was the instinct that hesitated to destroy. In that double vindication, Islamic ethics received its permanent shape: a tradition in which even war has a fiqh, even an axe needs a warrant, and even a date palm has standing before the Judge of all the earth.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:6

> **As for what God has given in spoils from them to the Messenger, you spurred neither horse nor camel for it. But God grants His messengers authority over whomsoever He will, and God is Powerful over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A New Category of Wealth: The Discovery of Fayʾ**

The Arabic of the verse coins, in effect, a new fiscal category: *mā afāʾa Allāhu* — "what God made return" — from the root *f-y-ʾ*, whose core meaning is *to return*. The commentators explain: *fayʾ* is wealth that "returns" from the disbelievers to God and His Messenger — that is, back into the ownership of the community's head — because it belongs, in the deepest sense, to God, from whom every possession is on loan. Its legal definition became precise: spoils acquired from an enemy *without fighting* — by surrender, capitulation, or flight — as opposed to *ghanīmah*, booty seized in battle. The verse's phrase *"you spurred neither horse nor camel for it"* (*mā awjaftum ʿalayhi min khayl*) gives the criterion in a single vivid image: *ijāf* is the gallop of urged horses, the thunder of cavalry. No cavalry thundered at the Naḍīr's walls. There was a siege, a war of nerves, a felled orchard, and a negotiated departure. The wealth that came to the Muslims therefore did not come as the wage of combat — and God rules that it therefore may not be distributed as the wage of combat.

**Why the Distinction Is Economically Brilliant**

To feel the wisdom of the ruling, set it beside the law of war-booty that the Qur'an had already revealed:

> *"And know that whatsoever you take as spoils, a fifth of it is for God and the Messenger, and for kinsfolk, orphans, the indigent, and the traveler."* (8:41)

*Ghanīmah* — battle-booty — is four-fifths distributed among the soldiers who risked their lives, and one-fifth to public causes. The logic is compensation: those who bled carry the greater share. But apply that rule to the Naḍīr's wealth and a distortion appears: a generation of soldiers would receive, as personal windfall, the entire patrimony of an abandoned district — wealth they did not fight for, from a capitulation no assault won. The verse anticipates and forbids precisely this: *fayʾ* belongs to the Head of the community as trustee, to be allocated by revelation — which in this case meant, as Ibn Kathīr details, the destitute Emigrants, two poor Helpers (named in the reports as Sahl ibn Ḥunayf and Abū Dujānah), the needs of the Prophet's household, and the purchase of weapons and horses for the common defense. Here, revealed fourteen centuries ago, is the principle modern states keep rediscovering: *windfall revenues that cost no one's labor belong to the public treasury, not to whoever happens to be standing nearest.* Nations that have treated oil, mineral, or conquest windfalls as loot for the military or the elite have suffered the curse of unearned riches; the Qur'an's fiscal constitution for fayʾ is the standing antidote.

**The Trustee Who Died in Debt**

The integrity of any trustee-based system depends on the trustee, and God furnished the proof of His Messenger's trusteeship in the most visible way possible. The Prophet ﷺ held absolute discretion over the Naḍīr's wealth — one of the largest fortunes in the oasis — and distributed all of it to others. His personal portion of life, at its end, was famously this, as ʿĀʾishah relates:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ died while his armor was mortgaged with a Jew for thirty ṣāʿs of barley."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

The commander who controlled the fayʾ of an entire district died with his shield in a pawnshop to feed his household. In an Arabia where chieftains measured glory in herds and hoards, this was a fiscal miracle as real as any physical one — and it sealed the meaning of the verse: *God grants His messengers authority* precisely because messengers are the one category of rulers who can be trusted not to convert authority into appropriation.

**The Sunnah of God with His Messengers**

The verse's middle clause generalizes from the case to a principle: *"God grants His messengers authority over whomsoever He will."* The Naḍīr are not an anomaly; they are an instance of a recurring divine pattern — God dispossessing a defiant power and transferring its wealth to His prophet's stewardship, generation after generation. The Qur'an states the pattern explicitly in the verses revealed about the Qurayẓah and Khaybar:

> *"And He made you heirs to their land, their homes, and their property — and a land on which you had never set foot; and God is Powerful over all things."* (33:27)

Noah, whose contemporaries drowned while he inherited the earth's testimony; Moses, before whom Pharaoh's host was swallowed and whose people were given "gardens, springs, crops, and noble stations" (26:57–59); and finally Muhammad ﷺ, before whom the fortresses of the Naḍīr opened without a battle — the pattern is constant, and its lesson is aimed at every later reader: the ownership of the earth is negotiable only with its Owner. Titles, deeds, and hereditary claims hold exactly as long as God's patience holds with the conduct of their holders. *"God grants authority over whomsoever He will"* is spoken in the same grammar as the Qur'an's maxim of all history: *"Say: O God, Master of the Kingdom! Thou givest the kingdom to whomsoever Thou wilt, and seizes the kingdom from whomsoever Thou wilt"* (3:26).

**"Powerful over All Things": The Doxology of a Bloodless Victory**

The verse closes with *wa-Allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr* — "God is Powerful over all things" — and the doxology is not ornamental. The entire event it crowns was a demonstration of *qudrah* working through means no strategist would credit: a walk-out, a rumor, a felled orchard, a broken promise from Ibn Ubayy, and hearts collapsing from within. Military science would say the Muslims were too few, too poor, and too lightly armed to evict a fortress aristocracy from its own walls in three weeks without a battle. God's science says otherwise, and the verse seals the account: He is able over *all* things — including the thing most beyond armies' reach, which is the interior of an enemy's breast. For the believer, the closing Name is a budget line item in reverse: whatever resources the community lacks, the Power that evicted the Naḍīr without cavalry lacks nothing; and whatever the community holds, it holds by the same Power's leave, revocable in a campaign of three weeks. Taqwā is simply remembering which side of that equation one stands on.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:7

> **That which God has given in spoils to His Messenger from the people of the towns is for God and His Messenger, and for kinfolk, orphans, the indigent, and the traveler, that it not come to circulate among your wealthy. Whatsoever the Messenger gives you, take it; and whatsoever he forbids to you, forgo, and reverence God. Truly God is severe in retribution.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From One Oasis to All Towns: The Qur'an Legislates in Universals**

Verse 6 had ruled on the Naḍīr's wealth as a specific case. Verse 7 immediately converts the case into a permanent statute: *mā afāʾa Allāhu ʿalā rasūlihi min ahli al-qurā* — "whatsoever God grants in spoils to His Messenger **from the people of the towns**" — plural, open-ended, timeless. This is one of the clearest windows into the Qur'an's legislative method: revelation descends upon a concrete event, resolves it, and then, in the same breath, writes the general rule that will govern every analogous case until the end of history. The Naḍīr's orchards were the occasion; the fiscal constitution of the Islamic commonwealth was the result. Later generations of jurists — and caliphs like ʿUmar with the rich lands of Iraq — would return to this verse precisely because of its universal phrasing, and build upon it the entire public-finance architecture of a future empire no one in Madinah that year could have imagined.

**The Five Channels of Public Wealth**

The recipients form a revealed priority list. "For God and His Messenger" — meaning, as the commentators explain, the causes of God administered by the Messenger: worship, the sustenance of the Prophet's household (which, by his practice, meant a modest maintenance, the rest flowing onward), and the public needs of religion and defense. Then *kinfolk* — the Prophet's clan, Banū Hāshim, who were barred from receiving zakah and thus needed this channel, so that God's Book provides for the Prophet's family without ever allowing the Prophet to profit from prophethood. Then *orphans*, *the indigent*, and *the traveler* — the same constellation of the vulnerable that recurs wherever the Qur'an allocates money (2:177; 8:41; 9:60). The pattern is impossible to miss: in God's budget, the first beneficiaries of any unearned public windfall are the structurally voiceless — the child without a father, the adult without income, the stranger without a network. A treasury's true character is revealed not by whom it taxes but by whom it *first* funds.

**The Ball That Must Not Bounce Among the Rich**

Then comes one of the most economically penetrating phrases in all scripture: *kay lā yakūna dūlatan bayna al-aghniyāʾi minkum* — "so that it may not be a *dūlah* circulating among your wealthy." A *dūlah* in classical Arabic is the ball in a game, batted from player to player. The image is exact and devastating: wealth that circulates only among the rich is a plaything in a closed match, with the poor locked outside the stadium as spectators. God states the *purpose* of His fiscal law as anti-concentration — not mere redistribution, but the prevention of an oligarchic loop. Al-Qurṭubī marvels at this: the Qur'an does not condemn wealth, it condemns wealth's *recirculation* among its existing holders. The political history of the caliphate took the verse literally. When Iraq's richest lands fell, ʿUmar refused to distribute them to the conquering army as private estates, reasoning — as reported in the classical sources — that the land was fayʾ belonging to God, His Messenger, "and the generations of Muslims to come," citing this very verse: if he divided it among the soldiers of one year, nothing would remain for the Muslims of the next century. It was perhaps the first instance in history of a conquering power deliberately refusing to privatize its conquests in order to endow its future poor. The verse's phrase still reads as a one-line critique of every modern economy in which windfalls — bailouts, licenses, privatizations, war contracts — predictably ricochet among the same connected names.

**"Whatsoever the Messenger Gives You, Take It": The Charter of the Sunnah**

The verse's second half has carried a weight far beyond its context: *wa-mā ātākumu al-rasūlu fa-khudhūhu wa-mā nahākum ʿanhu fa-ntahū* — "Whatsoever the Messenger gives you, take it; and whatsoever he forbids to you, forgo." The early community understood this not as a rule about the division of spoils only, but as a general charter: the Prophet's judgments, allocations, commands, and prohibitions carry divine authority in every domain of religion. The Qur'an had already fused obedience to the Messenger with obedience to God — *"Obey God and obey the Messenger"* (4:59) — and had insisted:

> *"Nor does he speak from desire. It is naught but revelation revealed."* (53:3–4)

And it would later make his personal arbitration the very test of faith:

> *"By thy Lord, they believe not until they make thee judge in their disputes, and then find within themselves no resistance to what thou decidest, and submit with full submission."* (4:65)

Muslim jurisprudence therefore built the entire institution of the Sunnah — the Prophet's recorded practice — on this verse among others: his precedents are legislation, his approvals are permissions, and his prohibitions are law. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both link the verse to the Prophet's own careful framing of his authority:

> *"When I command you to do something, then do of it as much as you are able; and if I forbid you something, then shun it."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1337; cf. al-Bukhārī)

Notice the asymmetry the Prophet teaches: commands are bounded by capacity — Islam is a religion of ability, not of impossibility; prohibitions are absolute — because a forbidden thing is a poison, and poison is not taken "as much as one is able." The logic is medical: a doctor's prescriptions are adjusted to the patient's strength, but a warning about poison admits no dosage.

**The Vindication of a Criticized Distributor**

There was a personal dimension to the revelation. The Prophet's distributions were constantly scrutinized by those looking for favor — the Qur'an describes them: *"And of them are those who find fault with thee concerning the alms; if they are given thereof, they are content, but if they are not given thereof, behold, they are enraged"* (9:58). Against this pressure, verse 7 does something remarkable: it rules that the Messenger's allocations are not his preferences to be lobbied but God's dispositions to be accepted — *take* and *forgo*, both verbs of submission to an allocation made from above. No ruler before or since has had his budget so explicitly removed from the market of popularity. And the surrounding moral — *reverence God; truly God is severe in retribution* — closes the circle: stewardship of public wealth is not an administrative duty but a covenantal one. The treasurer of the ummah is not merely accountable to an audit committee; the final audit is conducted by the One whose retribution is severe. Every charitable board, every public treasury, every waqf administrator who has feared God at the ledger has this verse standing behind the pen.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:8

> **For the poor emigrants who were expelled from their homes and their property seeking bounty and contentment from God and who help God and His Messenger—it is, they who are the truthful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Divine Portrait Gallery**

Verses 8 through 10 are a set of three divine portraits — the Emigrants, the Helpers, and the Successors — painted not by human admiration but by God Himself, so that the community would forever know what He considers excellence in a human being. The first portrait is of *al-muhājirūn al-fuqarāʾ*, "the poor emigrants." The noun from *f-q-r* literally connotes a broken spine: a *faqīr* is one whose back is bent under need — poverty here is not romanticized but anatomically felt. These were the men and women who had left Makkah years before with nothing, because Makkah had made faith unlivable, and who had spent the interval in Madinah as guests of others' generosity, many of them the dwellers of the Veranda — the covered platform beside the Prophet's Mosque where the homeless converts of Islam slept and studied. To them, and to no one else's enrichment, the entire Naḍīr patrimony was given. God here shows His fiscal preference with precision: the first paymasters of the new commonwealth were to be those who had paid its highest price.

**"Expelled from Their Homes": The Qur'an's Refugee Charter**

The verse describes them with a legal exactness: *ukhrijū min diyārihim wa-amwālihim* — "expelled from their homes **and their property**." They were not adventurers seeking fortune; they were refugees — people to whom the word *expelled* applies, whose houses in Makkah were confiscated by their own clans the day they left. The Qur'an keeps this category of the expelled central to its moral universe, and commands special solidarity with it:

> *"And those who, after their persecution, emigrated and strove and were patient — truly thy Lord, thereafter, is Forgiving, Merciful."* (16:110)

The Emigrants of Madinah are history's paradigm case, and the verse quietly establishes what later Islamic law would make explicit: the displaced believer has a standing claim on the community's resources, honored before the community's own comfort. The modern reader should let the category broaden honestly: in a world of tens of millions of displaced people, the Qur'an's first fiscal act in its first polity was to write the refugees in as the first line of the budget. That is not a modern humanitarian innovation imported into the Qur'an; it is a Qur'anic innovation the modern world is still catching up to.

**Two Destinations: Bounty in This World, Contentment in the Next**

The verse says they sought two things: *faḍlan mina Allāhi wa-riḍwānan* — "bounty from God, and contentment." The classical commentators note the elegant division of labor: *faḍl* is the increase one seeks in this world — provision, livelihood, the means to rebuild life — while *riḍwān*, God's being pleased, is the increase one seeks in the Hereafter. The pairing refuses both errors of religious economics. It does not shame the refugee for wanting bread, land, and work — seeking God's bounty is written into the Qur'an's own description of their motive; and it does not let the seeker of bread forget that the account with a longer horizon is the pleasure of God. The believer's motive is doubled, not divided: work as though this world matters, because God's bounty is in it; intend the Hereafter in the working, because God's pleasure is beyond it. The Prophet ﷺ would define the well-rounded ambition in a single prayer he taught: *"O God, set right for me my religion, which is the safeguard of my affair; and set right for me my worldly life, wherein is my living."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2720)

**"Helping God and His Messenger": The Honor of Being God's Instrument**

How can a human being *help* God — *yunṣirūna Allāha wa-rasūlahu*? The question answers itself in the Qur'an's theology of delegation. God does not need:

> *"If you are thankless, truly God is Self-Sufficient beyond any need of you; yet He is not pleased with ingratitude from His servants. And if you are thankful, He is pleased with it for you."* (39:7)

What God "receives" from His servants is the honor of being the arena where His purposes are displayed. The Qur'an states the exchange explicitly: *"If you help God, He will help you and make firm your feet"* (47:7) — and again, *"Were it not for God's repelling some people by means of others, surely monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques wherein God's Name is mentioned much would have been destroyed"* (22:40). Helping God and His Messenger, as al-Qurṭubī explains, means striving and fighting in His cause, defending the community in which His worship is established. The Emigrants had done exactly that: expelled and impoverished, they did not retire into bitterness but became the vanguard of the commonwealth's defense. Their helping God was the proof that their migration had been for Him — and that leads to the verse's final seal.

**"It Is They Who Are the Truthful": When a Word Becomes a Verdict**

The verse ends with a verdict rather than a compliment: *ulāʾika humu al-ṣādiqūn* — "it is they who are the truthful." *Ṣidq* in Arabic is the alignment of the inner with the outer — word with deed, claim with cost. The Emigrants had said, in Makkah, *we believe* — and the Qur'an defines the believing claim as validated only by what it can bear:

> *"The believers are only those who believe in God and His Messenger, and thereafter doubt not, and strive with their wealth and their lives in the way of God. It is they who are the truthful."* (49:15)

Truth, in the Qur'an's accounting, is not sincerity of feeling but solvency of claim: the claim is "true" when the claimant pays for it. The Emigrants paid in houses, orchards, trades, and homeland. One of them, Ṣuhayb al-Rūmī, was stopped at Makkah's border by his tribesmen who said, "You came to us with nothing — you shall not leave with wealth." He surrendered his entire fortune for the right to emigrate, and the Prophet ﷺ greeted him with the words, *"Ṣuhayb has profited!"* — reported in the sīrah and Musnad literature. A man ruined in the world's ledger was declared profitable by the Prophet of God, and here in verse 8 God ratifies the entire generation with the Name of truth itself. Set against them stand the sūrah's hypocrites (vv. 11–12) — men of many words and no costs — and the Qur'an's great dividing line appears: humanity is not sorted by eloquence, lineage, or even by the vocabulary of faith, but by the price a person's words can carry. The Emigrants are *ṣādiqūn* because their claim survived its invoice.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:9

> **And those who were settled in the land and in belief before them love those who emigrated to them and find in their breasts no need for that which they were given. They prefer others over themselves, even if they be impoverished. And whosoever is shielded from the avarice of his soul, it is they who shall prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Settled in Two Homes: Land and Faith**

The second divine portrait is of the Anṣār, the Helpers of Madinah, and the verse describes them with a doubled verb: *tabawwaʾū al-dāra wa-al-īmāna* — "they settled in the Abode and in the Faith." The grammar is deliberate: Madinah was not merely their address; it was their home *and* their religion's home. Islam had reached the oasis before the Hijrah — through the two pledges of ʿAqabah, when the tribes of Aws and Khazraj, in secret meetings by night at Makkah, gave their hands to the Prophet ﷺ before they had even seen his polity — and so, by the time the Emigrants arrived, the Anṣār were not converts to a refugee movement; they were *pre-existing* believers who had rooted the faith in their own soil "before them." The honorific that the community gave them was earned, and the Prophet ﷺ sealed their rank with a creedal test that no other group of human beings received:

> *"None believes in it — love of the Anṣār — save a believer, and none hates it save a hypocrite."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 74, from Anas; cf. al-Bukhārī 17)

And on a mountain road he ﷺ said:

> *"If the people were to take one valley and the Anṣār another, I would walk in the valley of the Anṣār."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim)

**The Brotherhood That Made the Veranda Famous**

What the Anṣār did with their homes has no obvious parallel in political history. When the Emigrants arrived destitute, the Prophet ﷺ paired each refugee with a Helper in a formal pact of brotherhood (*muʾākhāh*) — and the Anṣār's response became legend. The archetype is preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī: Saʿd ibn al-Rabīʿ, one of the wealthiest Anṣār, said to his assigned brother ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, a man who had left Makkah with nothing:

> *"I am the wealthiest of the Anṣār, so I will divide my property in two and give you half; and I have two wives — look at which of them pleases you, that I may divorce her, and when her term is complete you may marry her." He said: "May God bless you in your family and your wealth. Show me the market."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

Every clause of that exchange deserves meditation. The offer: half of everything, and the readiness to release a wife for the sake of a brother's family life. The answer: blessing without appropriation — ʿAbd al-Raḥmān asks not for the wealth but for the *market*: dignity through trade rather than dependency on donation. Both men displayed a different virtue, and both virtues became sunnah: the Anṣārī's radical generosity and the Emigrant's radical self-respect.

**The Lamp Put Out: The Verse's Occasion**

The classical commentators — Ibn Kathīr and al-Wāḥidī among them — connect this verse to an incident Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim preserves. A poor man of the People of the Veranda was entrusted by the Prophet ﷺ to a household of the Anṣār. The host asked his wife what food they had. She said, "Nothing except the children's food." He said: "Put the children to sleep, bring the food, and put out the lamp when the guest sits — we will show him we are eating with him." The guest ate, and the family spent the night hungry. In the morning the host brought his guest to the Prophet ﷺ, who said:

> *"God wondered at what you two did last night"* — in the narration of the sūrah's reports: *"Truly the dwellers of the heavens are amazed at what you two have done."* Then this verse was revealed. (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2054 and the tafsīr literature)

The detail of the extinguished lamp is the theology of the whole story: the family hid even the *sight* of their hunger so that no embarrassment would touch the guest. This is *īthār* — preferring others — carried into its finest grain, where generosity extends to protecting the feelings of the one received.

**Īthār: The Summit of Generosity, and Its Limits**

The verse names the trait directly: *yuʾthirūna ʿalā anfusihim wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣah* — "they prefer others over themselves, even if they be impoverished." Islamic ethics ranks the stations of giving: to give from surplus is ordinary generosity; to give from sufficiency is nobler; to give while oneself in want — giving away the children's food — is *īthār*, the summit. The scholars of the spiritual path, such as al-Qushayrī in his Risālah, treasure it as the virtue of Abraham's guest-serving and the Anṣār's hosting. Yet the jurists add a necessary caution: īthār is a supererogatory excellence in matters of comfort and honor; when it would destroy a duty one owes — as when a father's dependents have a right to his children's food — the balance of trusts comes first. The Anṣār's act was honored precisely because it was a freely chosen extremity of hospitality, not a pattern of neglect. The verse records the miracle without legislating the impossible: the ideal is displayed so that ordinary generosity may always have somewhere higher to look.

**The Breast Trained to Want Nothing of What Others Are Given**

Alongside their giving, the verse praises something subtler: *lā yajidūna fī ṣudūrihim ḥājatan mimmā ūtū* — "they find in their breasts no need for what they were given" — namely, the Naḍīr lands that went, by God's ruling, to the Emigrants. The Anṣār had hosted the refugees, defended the city, and buried their own dead at Uḥud — and then watched the largest property settlement in the community's short history pass them by entirely. Not a farm, not an orchard, not a share. The verse praises not merely that they did not *steal* it, nor that they did not *protest*, but that their breasts — the Qur'an's organ of feeling — registered *no need at all* for it. ʿAjībah? This is the inner economy of contentment (*ghanāʾ*), of which the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Look at those who are below you, and do not look at those who are above you; it is more fitting that you not belittle God's blessing upon you."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim)

The comparison that breeds resentment is itself named as a spiritual failure; the Anṣār are praised for a heart that had stopped running that comparison altogether. Abū ʿAlī al-Jurjānī, as the commentators note, reads the phrase universally: to empty the breast of need for *whatever passes to others* — the true freedom of the heart from the world's distribution queue.

**The Last Enemy: Avarice of the Soul**

The verse then generalizes its lesson into a principle with a warning attached: *wa-man yūqa shuḥḥa nafsihi* — "whoever is shielded from the avarice of his soul — it is they who shall prosper." *Shuḥḥ* is avarice, the miserly craving that hardens into greed; the Qur'an names it the disease that destroyed nations:

> *"Be on your guard against avarice, for avarice destroyed those who were before you: it led them to shed blood and to make lawful what was unlawful for them."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2578)

Notice the hadith's escalation: avarice does not stay a private vice; it metastasizes until people kill for property and legalize the forbidden to keep it. The maxim the tradition transmits is starker still: *"Your worst enemy is the soul that lies between your two sides."* And the Qur'an recognizes the disease's divinely-authored cure as a gift — being *shielded* (a passive participle: it is done *to* the soul, by God) from one's own grasping. For the believer this is both diagnosis and prayer: left to itself, the breast manufactures need for every good that reaches another hand; the Anṣār show what a breast looks like after God has performed the surgery of gratitude upon it. The verse's final promise — *fa-ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn*, "they are the ones who shall prosper" — uses the verb of the farmer (*falāḥ*): success in the Qur'an is agricultural, a harvest long after the sowing. The Anṣār sowed homes, food, and the extinguishing of lamps; the harvest God promised them is the only one that does not spoil.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:10

> **And those who came after them say, "Our Lord! Forgive us and our brothers who preceded us in faith, and place no rancor in our hearts toward those who believe. Our Lord! Truly Thou art Kind, Merciful."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Portrait: Everyone Who Comes After**

The third divine portrait is deliberately left unfinished — because its subjects are still being born. *Wa-alladhīna jāʾū min baʿdihim* — "those who came after them" — designates first the Tābiʿūn, the generation that followed the Companions, and then, as al-Rāzī and the broader tradition insist, every generation of believers until the Day of Resurrection: the verse speaks in a grammar without an end-date. The Qur'an itself authorizes the grouping in the verse this sūrah mirrors:

> *"As for the foremost — the first among the Emigrants and the Helpers, and those who followed them with virtue — God is content with them, and they are content with Him; and He has prepared for them gardens beneath which rivers flow, therein to abide forever. That is the great triumph."* (9:100)

Three stations: Emigrants, Helpers, and the followers. Verse 10 defines what qualifies the followers for inclusion — and it is not lineage, scholarship, or conquest, but a *prayer*: a prayer for themselves, a prayer for their predecessors, and a request for a purified heart toward all believers everywhere. Membership in the blessed column of 9:100 is, astonishingly, indexed to the direction of one's hopes and the hygiene of one's rancor.

**A Curriculum in Three Petitions**

Look at what the latecomers are taught to ask. First: *"Forgive us"* — they begin with themselves, not with the past; humility before history starts with one's own account. Second: *"and our brothers who preceded us in faith"* — they pray for people they never met, whose sacrifices they enjoy, whose errors they can see but cannot repeat better. Third: *"and place no rancor in our hearts toward those who believe"* — they ask God to keep their breasts clean *forward and sideways*: toward the ancients and toward their contemporaries. The order is a complete spiritual pedagogy: self-audit, gratitude to predecessors, and love for the living community — past, present, and self held in one honest field of vision. And notice the theology inside the grammar: the believers do not claim to have cleansed their own hearts; they ask God to *not place* rancor there — the heart's purity is God's gift to be requested, not a self-evident possession.

**Ghill: The Rancor That Boils Quietly**

The word the prayer targets is precise: *ghill* — from a root meaning to boil and to be retained, water heated in a sealed vessel. *Ghill* is resentment that has not vented: the long-simmering, low-temperature bitterness of a heart that has not forgiven, has not forgotten, and does not say so. It is the specialist sin of religious communities — the scholarly rivalry, the sectarian memory, the envy of another's mosque, following, or lineage — precisely because open sins frighten the pious while the sealed boil does not. The Qur'an promises its surgical removal in Paradise:

> *"And We shall remove whatever rancor (ghill) is in their breasts; as brothers, they shall be upon couches facing one another."* (7:43; cf. 15:47)

The implication is breathtaking: *brotherhood is the post-operative state of a de-ghilled heart.* And the prayer of verse 10 asks God to perform that Paradise-surgery now, on earth, in advance. The believer essentially prays: *give me, in this community, the social quality of the Hereafter.* The Prophet ﷺ also warned how ghill blocks other graces — among the reports of the presentation of deeds is that the servant's deeds are shown while any believer holds rancor against him, and mercy is withheld until hearts reconcile (cited in the Musnad literature). A single un-vented boil, in other words, can quarantine an entire spiritual portfolio.

**"Our Brothers": The Canonization of Gratitude**

The phrase *"our brothers who preceded us in faith"* carries a legal and moral revolution inside its possessive. In the tribal world into which the Qur'an descended, identity ran by blood; your dead were your dead, and the dead of other tribes were the dead of other tribes. Verse 10 replaces blood with faith as the ligature of loyalty across time: theconverts of Yemen are brothers of the Companions of Badr; a believer born fourteen centuries later is brother to both. Al-Qurṭubī records the reading that extends the phrase even to the righteous of the People of the Book who preceded the Muslims in belief — with the implication, which he draws, that reconciliation with the Naḍīr would have been the preferred course, and banishment the sorrowful necessity after their defiance became fixed. The general Sunnah of the community is brotherhood; the specific exception of conflict was forced by covenant-breaking, not by pleasure in it. For practical religion, the verse settled the etiquette of the present toward the past — and the Prophet ﷺ enforced it with terrifying strictness toward his own generation:

> *"Do not revile my Companions; by Him in Whose Hand is my soul, were one of you to spend the like of Mount Uḥud in gold, it would not equal a handful of one of them, nor half of it."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2540)

Reviling the predecessors of faith is thus not commentary; it is the exact illness the prayer of verse 10 was revealed to prevent.

**The Mirror of 24:22: Forgiveness Flows Downstream**

The prayer's logic — that one's own pardon is somehow bound up with pardoning others — is the Qur'an's standing doctrine. When Abū Bakr swore to stop supporting a relative who had slandered his daughter ʿĀʾishah, God revealed:

> *"Let them pardon and forgive. Do you not wish that God should forgive you? And God is Forgiving, Merciful."* (24:22)

Abū Bakr — the greatest of the generation this sūrah praises — was corrected mid-oath: the traffic of forgiveness moves in a circuit, and whoever blocks the circuit blocks his own supply. Verse 10 hard-wires the same circuit into the daily prayer of ordinary believers: *forgive me as I ask forgiveness for my brothers; keep my heart clean of resentment toward every believer.* Sincerity in this duʿāʾ and sectarian contempt for other Muslims cannot coexist in one breast — the prayer is the built-in, recited solvent of the very communal poisons that later centuries would suffer.

**"Kind, Merciful": The Tenderest Pair of Names**

The prayer closes on a pairing of divine Names rare and tender: *Rabbanā innaka Raʾūfun Raḥīm* — "Our Lord, truly Thou art Kind, Merciful." *Raʾfah* is the softest of the mercy words in Arabic — the tenderness a body feels for its own wound, a mother for her child; the scholars rank it as mercy beyond mercy, pity in its most acute form. The Qur'an uses it of the Prophet ﷺ himself: *"There has come unto you a Messenger from among yourselves, for whom your welfare is grievous, who is anxious over you, *raʾūf* unto the believers, merciful"* (9:128). And the Gospel-of-God logic of the ending is this: the petitioners have just asked for the excision of bitterness from their hearts and forgiveness for a community of sinners — so they appeal, befittingly, to the two Names that promise tenderness at its maximum intensity. Whoever prays verse 10 with presence is asking the God of Everest-mercy for a house-cleaning of the heart — and the verse's placement, immediately after the portraits of the Emigrants and the Anṣār, quietly completes the gallery: the first two portraits hang framed in history; the third is a mirror, and every believer who prays this prayer steps into it.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:11

> **Hast thou not seen those who act with hypocrisy, saying to their brothers who disbelieve among the People of the Book, "If you are expelled, surely we shall go forth with you. And we shall never obey anyone against you. And if you are fought, we shall help you." God bears witness that they are surely liars.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Have You Not Seen": The Qur'an's Court of Vision**

The verse opens with the Qur'an's characteristic summons to witness: *a-lam tara* — "hast thou not seen." The Prophet ﷺ had not physically seen the exchange it is about to describe — the message ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy and his confederates sent into the Naḍīr fortresses — so the "seeing" is moral and revelatory: God shows him the hidden correspondence as though it were staged before his eyes. The formula recurs throughout the Qur'an (*a-lam tara ilā alladhīna*...) as a marker that what follows is not gossip about hidden matters but evidence entered into a divine court. And the case at bar is the anatomy of hypocrisy itself — the third and darkest portrait of the sūrah. After the truth-purchasing Emigrants (v. 8) and the self-preferring Helpers (v. 9), the Qur'an now sketches, with clinical coldness, the people of the costless promise.

**The Message Sent into the Fortress**

The historical record — preserved by Ibn Isḥāq, al-Ṭabarī, and Ibn Kathīr — states that when the Prophet ﷺ besieged the Naḍīr, their chief Ḥuyayy ibn Akhṭab sent to Ibn Ubayy and his faction of the hypocrites, reminding them of the alliance. The reply, in substance, was exactly what the verse quotes: *"Hold fast to your fortresses and prepare; we will not abandon you. If you are fought, we will fight alongside you; and if you are expelled, we will go out with you."* The verse's rendering of the promise is rhetorically perfect. It escalates in three clauses — future exit, permanent disobedience to any claimant, armed aid — each grander than the last, and all of it free: promises about tomorrow cost nothing today. Notably, the verse even legislates the future of *others*: "we shall never obey anyone against you" — the hypocrite's most fluent register is the categorical oath. And on that extravagant security the Naḍīr staked their homes.

**"Their Brothers": The Two Brotherhoods of the Sūrah**

One word in the verse does devastating comparative work: the hypocrites address the Naḍīr as *ikhwatihim* — "their brothers." A few verses earlier, the followers of the Prophet were praying for "*our brothers* who preceded us in faith" (v. 10). The Qur'an thus sets two brotherhoods side by side: a brotherhood welded by faith and expressed in prayers of forgiveness, and a brotherhood welded by shared hostility to God's Messenger and expressed in promises of war. The Qur'an names this second kinship openly elsewhere: *"O you who believe, take not My enemy and your enemy as friends... showing them love"* (60:1) — kinship of purpose is real, but it is only as durable as the purpose. The sūrah's quiet lesson: everyone belongs to some brotherhood; the question, which will decide where one spends eternity, is which.

**"God Bears Witness That They Are Liars": The Inversion of Shahādah**

The verse's verdict is forensic in the extreme: *wa-Allāhu yashhadu innahum la-kādhibūn* — "God bears witness that they are surely liars." In Islamic law, testimony establishes truth; and no testimony outranks God's. The formula deliberately mirrors the scene of hypocrisy's origin, Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn:

> *"When the hypocrites come unto thee, they say: 'We bear witness that thou art indeed the Messenger of God.' And God knows that thou art indeed His Messenger; and God bears witness that the hypocrites are surely liars."* (63:1)

The symmetry is surgical: they offered their shahādah with their tongues while withholding it in their breasts, so God offers His shahādah against them — the same verb, *shahida*, turned back on the perjurers. What makes the lying here so grave is its register: these are not fibs about trifles but sworn military guarantees made in God's sight, on which a whole tribe wagered its existence. And the verse thereby teaches the community a permanent epistemology of trust: verbal assurance is not commitment; intent is invisible to you but perfectly visible to God; and God does, in His Book, unmask specific hidden intentions by name — not so that believers may spy on hearts (which is forbidden), but so that they may never imagine that *any* heart is hidden from Heaven.

**The Three Signs of the Empty Promiser**

The Prophet ﷺ gave the community the diagnostic for the type this verse immortalizes:

> *"The signs of a hypocrite are three: when he speaks, he lies; when he promises, he betrays; and when he is entrusted, he proves treacherous."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 33; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 59)

The second sign is this verse's exact subject: the promise that was engineered, from inception, to be abandoned. Why do people make such promises? Ibn Ubayy's calculus is transparent in the sources: the Naḍīr's gold had long greased his politics; their alliance was his power base against the Prophet's growing authority; and words were his cheapest currency. He promised maximal loyalty at zero cost — and when the siege actually came and no force materialized, he sent word that, in effect, *my position is no secret to you; I see no fighting for you here; make terms.* The empty promiser is always an economist: he spends words to buy position and abandons the position when the price turns to blood. The verse's exposure of him — God's own testimony against his testimony — was the first installment of his ruin: from that day, every power in Madinah knew the market value of his guarantees.

**The Modern Brotherhood of the Costless Vow**

Nothing in this verse has aged. The pattern of the maximal promise made to be broken is the signature move of every era's fair-weather actors: the ally who pledges eternal solidarity until the war reaches its own border; the partner who vows commitment until the market turns; the recruiter — ideological, criminal, or corporate — who promises everything to those he intends to use, and is unreachable on the day of consequences; the public figure whose "I will never abandon you" is addressed, not to the promised, but to the audience. Verse 11 is God's permanent restraint on the believer's heart in a world of such promises: listen to guarantees with the ear the Qur'an trains — weigh the promiser's record of cost, remember that only God's witness runs beneath all testimonies, and reserve your own promises for what you can pay. The Prophet ﷺ said: *"Whoever guarantees for me what is between his two jaws and what is between his two legs, I guarantee for him the Garden"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6474) — the believer's tongue and desire under warranty, his word solvent, because his Witness never sleeps.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:12

> **Were they expelled, they would not go forth with them. And were they fought, they would not help them. And were they to help them, they would surely turn their backs. Then they would not be helped.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Clauses of Structured Futility**

Verse 11 reported the promise; verse 12 dismantles it — clause by clause, in a descending cascade of analysis that deserves to be read as a masterpiece of applied logic. *If* they are expelled — the hypocrites will not leave with them. *If* they are fought — the hypocrites will not fight for them. *And if* — the most improbable concession — the hypocrites *did* come to fight, they would turn their backs in flight. *And then* — final clause, sealed like a verdict — the Naḍīr would not be helped. Notice what God does with conditionals. Verse 11's promise was itself conditional (*if you are expelled... if you are fought...*); verse 12 takes the enemy's own conditional grammar and runs it forward through every branch of the tree, showing that every path terminates in the same destination: abandonment. The rhetorical structure itself teaches: a promise made without capacity or intent collapses under analysis, and God here performs the analysis publicly, in advance of the event — so that when Ibn Ubayy's forces never materialized at the fortress walls, the believers had already been told, and the Naḍīr had already been warned.

**God's Knowledge of the Unexecuted Future**

Beneath the logic lies a doctrine. These clauses describe events that *never happened* — counterfactuals, worlds that God chose not to actualize: the world where the hypocrites marched out, the world where they joined battle, the world where they stood their ground. God asserts certain knowledge of all those unwritten worlds, in perfect detail, down to the turning of specific backs. This is the Qur'an's repeated claim to exclusive omniscience over the branches of time:

> *"Say: None in the heavens and the earth knows the Unseen save God; and they know not when they shall be raised."* (27:65)

> *"And with Him are the keys of the Unseen; none knows them but He. And He knows what is in the land and the sea; and no leaf falls but He knows it, nor a seed in the darkness of the earth, nor anything moist or dry, but that it is in a Book clear."* (6:59)

For the believer, verse 12 is therefore not merely history narrated early; it is a window onto the divine mode of knowing — the reason prophecy cannot be counterfeited, and the reason trust may be placed entirely in God's descriptions of futures no market, intelligence service, or actuary can price. The precise scientific term for what God here displays is knowledge of subjunctive history — and He asserts it as easily as men report the weather.

**The Prophecy Fulfilled Within Weeks — and Ibn Ubayy's Excuse**

The event vindicated the revelation with embarrassing speed. When the siege tightened and the Naḍīr invoked the promise, Ibn Ubayy's faction — as the sīrah and tafsīr sources record — sent back words to this effect: *our position regarding Muhammad is no secret to you; but we see no fighting for you here; so submit and go.* No one marched out; no one drew a sword; the "we shall never obey anyone against you" evaporated at the first smell of real risk. Al-Ṭabarī adds the sūrah's own surgical point: even if they had come, the Muslim army would have driven them off, and the Naḍīr would have gained nothing — the promise was worthless not only in intention but in any possible execution. The verse had thereby performed, in public, the destruction of the hypocrites' political capital: every subsequent ally in Madinah would price their word at its revealed value.

**Uḥud's Ghost: The Track Record Already on File**

The believers did not need revelation to doubt the promise; they needed only memory. One year before, at Uḥud, Ibn Ubayy had done exactly what this verse predicts — he withdrew his three hundred men on the road to battle, abandoning the Prophet ﷺ with seven hundred. The Qur'an had already profiled that hour:

> *"And that He might know the hypocrites. And it was said to them: 'Come, fight in the way of God, or at least defend.' They said: 'If we knew how to fight, we would follow you.' On that day they were nearer to disbelief than to belief, saying with their mouths what was not in their hearts; and God knows best what they hide."* (3:167)

The Qur'an elsewhere describes the cowardice of the type in the language of theater: *"fighting frightens them, and you see them looking at you, turning their eyes like one swooning over death"* (33:19). The lesson for every community: past performance is not merely one data point; in questions of courage, it is nearly the whole ledger — and God, who reads hearts directly, simply publishes the ledger in advance. The believer's practical takeaway is dual: first, *audit your allies* — a promise is only as strong as the promiser's demonstrated capacity for cost; second, *audit yourself* — every vow you have made is already filed in the same heavenly ledger, and the God who predicted the hypocrites' flight sees your branches of the future just as clearly.

**"Then They Would Not Be Helped": The Theology of the Last Clause**

The verse's final clause — *thumma lā yunṣarūn*, "then they would not be helped" — is usually read of the Naḍīr, but its theology reaches further: it states the doctrine of aid itself. Help, in the Qur'an's worldview, is not a natural resource that alliances can mine; it is a gift that God extends or withholds through whatever instruments He pleases:

> *"If God helps you, none can overcome you; and if He forsakes you, who is there to help you after Him? So in God let the believers trust."* (3:160)

This is why the hypocrites' promise was structurally doomed before it was insincere: any help rendered against God's Messenger would have required God's cooperation against His own decree — an impossibility dressed up as a treaty. And it is why the Naḍīr's real error was not bad intelligence about Ibn Ubayy but a false theology about the source of victory: they sought help where help is not manufactured. The Qur'an had already given the world the correct sentence, and it remains the most consequential geopolitical fact in history — *"Victory is only from God"* (3:126) — and the believer who internalizes it holds both peace and strategy in one hand: peace, because no plot can succeed against them if God defends them; strategy, because the only alliance that finally matters is the one maintained with Heaven.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:13

> **Truly you incite more intense dread in their breasts than God. That is because they are a people who do not comprehend.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Startling Compliment with a Diagnosis Inside It**

Spoken to the Muslims, the verse is almost jarring: *la-antum ashaddu raʾbatan fī ṣudūrihim mina Allāh* — "you are more intense in dread in their breasts than God." The believer instinctively protests that nothing should be feared more than God — and the believer is right; that is precisely the verse's point. The Naḍīr's tragedy, diagnosed in one line, was an inverted scale of dread: Muhammad ﷺ and his Companions — flesh, steel, and siegeworks — occupied more space in their hearts than the Lord of the worlds. The verse is less a compliment to Muslim power than an indictment of pagan perception. God is not competing for ratings of fear; He is reporting, with sorrowful precision, a fatal miscalibration: a heart that fears armies more than it fears God has already lost, because it will make all of its decisions — treaties, plots, surrenders — by the wrong mathematics. The word for dread here, *raʾbah*, is the shudder of sudden alarm — the visceral startle of the breast — and the verse locates it anatomically: in their *ṣudūr*, the seat of feeling. Wrong fear lives in the heart; wrong fear therefore governs the whole man.

**The Qur'an's Consistent Battle-Cry: Reverse the Scale**

The diagnosis of verse 13 is the Qur'an's standing correction, repeated in both directions — rebuke to those who fear creation over the Creator, and reassurance to believers tempted the same way:

> *"That is only Satan frightening his partisans; so fear them not, but fear Me, if you are believers."* (3:175)

> *"O you who believe, take not those outside your fold as intimates... and fear Me, if you are believers."* (3:28, cf. 5:3, 5:57)

The pattern peaked in the prophets' own teaching: when Moses' people froze before Pharaoh's advancing army at the sea, he answered with the Qur'an's most famous ratio of fear: *"Fear not! Truly my Lord is with me; He will guide me"* (26:62). The math of the matter is the whole of faith: fear of God expels every other fear, but every other fear, enthroned, expels the fear of God. The early generations distilled it into the maxim often attributed to the salaf: whoever fears God, everything else fears him; and whoever fears other than God, God abandons him to fear everything. The maxim is simply verse 13 generalized — and psychological observation confirms its mechanism daily: the person governed by fear of human opinion is enslaved to an infinite number of masters with contradictory demands; the person governed by fear of God has one Master whose demands never contradict and never forget.

**Why the Naḍīr Feared the Wrong Object**

The verse names the root cause with a cognitive verb: *dhālika bi-annahum qawmun lā yaʿqilūn* — "that is because they are a people who do not comprehend." *ʿAql* is not raw intelligence — the Naḍīr were sophisticated merchants, agronomists, and fortress engineers — it is the integrative intellect that connects means to ends, visible to invisible, and computes the total reality of a situation, including its Author. The Qur'an repeatedly ties the refusal of faith to malfunctioning *ʿaql*, not to lacking information: *"And We have struck for mankind in this Qur'an every manner of similitude, that haply they may reflect"* (39:27) — yet *"their hearts are preoccupied, and they conceal their breasts"* (39:29's context of turning away). People who could calculate the yield of an orchard and the trajectory of an arrow could not perform the greater computation: that the community surviving Badr against three-to-one odds, and thriving at Madinah against every prediction, was being carried by a power that fortresses do not address. Their error was thus not emotional but rational — a category error about which variables matter. And the error had a moral root, as errors of intellect usually do: comprehending God's role in the situation would have obligated them to repent, and dread of that obligation was, at bottom, what they declined to feel.

**The Fear That Frees Versus the Fear That Enslaves**

Here the verse meets everyday human experience with surgical accuracy. There are two kinds of fear in the moral universe. Fear of God — *khashyah*, *taqwā* — is like the mountain-driver's respect for the cliff edge: it does not paralyze him; it makes him precisely careful, and that very care frees him to drive the road at all. The driver who "fears nothing" does not enjoy more freedom; he enjoys it for exactly as long as the road stays straight. Fear of men, by contrast, is a hijacking: it reprograms speech, flattery, courage, and testimony around the reactions of creatures whose approval is erratic and whose power is second-hand. The Prophet ﷺ prayed against exactly this enslavement, teaching his community to say: *"O God, I seek refuge in Thee from being overtaken by a trial, from being led astray, and from leprosy and madness"* — and, in the hadith's fuller teaching, from every bondage of dread other than God's. The Companions embodied the reversal: Bilāl on the burning sand answering his tormentors only with *"Aḥadun, aḥad* — He is One, He is One," a man at the maximum of human pain and the minimum of human fear. The fire could not intimidate him because a greater fear — love-laden awe — had already claimed the breast.

**The Verse as a Mirror for Believers**

The final caution belongs to the verse's first audience and to every later one — including the devout. It is possible to fear God's enemies more than God while wearing faith: to be paralyzed by fear of an employer's disapproval into silence about honesty; to fear the contempt of friends into abandoning prayer or principle; to fear poverty more than usury, reputation more than truth. The Prophet ﷺ named this the subtlest of snares when he warned against the "hidden shirk" of the man who beautifies his worship while an audience watches (Aḥmad; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī) — the fear of faces quietly outbidding the fear of the Face of God. Verse 13, read as a mirror, asks every believer to audit the breast the way the Naḍīr never did: *what dread, in the privacy of my chest, outweighs my dread of Him?* Whatever the honest answer names — that thing, and not the Qur'an's enemies, is the fortress that will one day fail us; and the God whom the Naḍīr declined to dread is the same God who says, in the verse that diagnoses every such fear: *"That is only Satan frightening his partisans; so fear them not — but fear Me."*

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:14

> **They will not fight you all together, save in fortified towns or from behind walls. Their might is fierce among themselves. You suppose that they are together; yet their hearts are divided. That is because they are a people who do not understand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sociology of the Fortress Fighter**

The verse delivers a military intelligence briefing — and it has proven accurate for fourteen centuries of siege-craft and faction. *Lā yuqātilūnakum jamīʿan* — "they will not fight you all together" — the Naḍīr and their like would never risk a united stand in open field, where armies are weighed honestly. *Illā fī quran munaṣṣarah aw min warāʾi judur* — "save in fortified towns or from behind walls." The Arabic for "fortified towns," *quran munaṣṣarah*, contains the root *n-ṣ-r* — the very word for "aid and victory": towns that are "aided," mutually reinforcing, each wall the back of the next. And *judur* — walls — is also the Arabic word for the low stone walls around a palm-grove: the image is of men fighting the way an orchard is enclosed, each man a tree inside his own enclosure. Behind stone, courage is cheap; in the open, it must be real. The Qur'an had already profiled this psychology in Sūrat al-Aḥzāb, revealed the year after the Naḍīr, when the confederate ten thousand melted away from Madinah's trench without a battle: *"They think the confederates have not departed; and if the confederates were to come again, they would wish they were nomads among the desert Arabs, asking for news of you; and were they among you, they would fight but little"* (33:20).

**"Their Might Is Fierce Among Themselves": The Two Readings**

The clause *baʾsunahum baynahum shadīd* is a deliberate double exposure, and Ibn Kathīr preserves both readings. Read one way: their might is fierce *within their own camp* — they strut with ferocious self-display before one another, each faction boasting to the others of valor it has never shown. Read the other way: their enmity toward *each other* is great — the camp is a theater of internal rivalry masquerading as a front. The two readings are not rivals; they are one psychology. Grandiosity within and division beneath it are the twin load-bearing walls of every brittle coalition: precisely because the alliance has no deep bond, it must be loudly maintained, and precisely because it is loudly maintained, its hollowness is invisible until the first cannon fires. At the Naḍīr's walls, the display was on schedule — the defiance, the ten-day respite, the refusal of terms — while the substance had already left the building: hearts that feared (v. 13) and allies who would not come (vv. 11–12).

**A Coalition of Appearances**

Then comes the verse's most quoted clause: *taḥsabuhum jamīʿan wa-qulūbuhum shattā* — "you suppose them to be united, yet their hearts are scattered." Two vocabularies of perception are set against each other: *ḥasiba* — what the observer estimates from the outside: uniforms, flags, joint declarations, shared hostility — and the Qur'an's X-ray of the *qulūb*, hearts *shattā*, scattered, dispersed in every direction. The classical reports differ on the referent: Ibn Kathīr records that it may describe the discord between the Jews of Madinah and their hypocrite allies, or the internal discord of the Jews themselves; the principle holds in every application. Appearance-of-unity is a manufactured product; unity-of-hearts is a created gift. The Qur'an distinguishes them sharply, because it has seen both:

> *"And hold fast, all together, to the Rope of God, and be not divided. And remember God's favor upon you: you were enemies, and He joined your hearts together, and by His blessing you became brothers."* (3:103)

The Anṣār and Muhājirūn had been tribes on the edge of civil war — the battles of Buʿāth had set Aws against Khazraj for generations — and God had fused them into a brotherhood that shared houses and dates. The Naḍīr and Ibn Ubayy's faction were allies of interest and venom, and interest plus venom cannot fuse hearts; it can only coordinate calendars.

**Discord as Divine Punishment, Unity as Divine Gift**

The Qur'an itself teaches us to read disunity theologically — not only tactically. In Sūrat al-Anʿām, God names factionalism among His forms of requital:

> *"Say: He is able to send forth upon you a punishment from above you or from beneath your feet, or to clothe you in factions and make you taste the might of one another."* (6:65)

Internal division, in other words, is not merely a political misfortune; it is a moral thermometer — a community riddled with distrust is being shown something about itself. And the inverse is equally revealed: hearts joined across tribes and classes is *not* an organizational achievement but a miracle granted to the God-fearing, as 3:103 states outright. The Prophet ﷺ anchored the sociology in a ḥadīth of staggering scope: the believers *"in their mutual kindness, compassion, and sympathy are like one body: when one organ suffers, the whole body answers it with sleeplessness and fever"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2586). One body is not a metaphor of proximity — it is a metaphor of *shared pain*. The Naḍīr coalition shared interests and felt no pain for one another; the Muslims shared pain even before they shared wealth. There, in one contrast, is the entire secret of which side wins.

**"A People Who Do Not Understand": Fiqh, the Deeper Comprehension**

The verse ends as verse 13 did, but with a different verb: *lā yafqahūn* — "they do not understand." The two diagnoses are a matched set. *ʿAql* (v. 13) is the intellect that weighs what matters; *fiqh* (v. 14) is the penetration that grasps how things really connect — the same word that would become the very name of Islamic jurisprudence, because law is precisely the discipline of seeing connections between acts and their inner reality. The Naḍīr's failure of *fiqh* was the failure to understand *where strength actually lives*. They computed walls, gold, and allies, and never computed hearts; and hearts were the battlefield on which the campaign was actually decided — theirs seized by terror (v. 2), their allies' by cowardice (v. 12), the Muslims' joined as one body by God (3:103). Every age re-runs the experiment. Coalitions of interest — cartels, empires, alliances of convenience — present the appearance of jamāʿah while their organs feel nothing for one another; and the Prophet's warning *"God's hand is with the community"* (al-Tirmidhī 2167) means that the reality, not the appearance, is what heaven aids. The believer's task, learned from verse 14, is to invest in the only unity that exists — the costly, painful, felt unity of hearts bound to God and therefore to each other — and to read the grand united fronts of the world's Ibn Ubayys with the calm of one who has read the last page of their dossier.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:15

> **Like those who shortly before them tasted the evil consequences of their affair—and theirs shall be a painful punishment—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grammar of Comparison: "Like Those Who..."**

Verse 15 is a bridge clause — in Arabic *ka-alladhīna min qablihim qarīban* — "like those who were a short time before them." The subject of the comparison is the Naḍīr and their hypocrite allies of the preceding verses; the object, as Ibn Kathīr identifies from the early reports, is Banū Qaynuqāʿ — the first of Madinah's three Jewish tribes to be banished, evicted two years earlier, after Badr, for breaking the terms of the Constitution of Madinah. The Qur'an could have simply said "they will meet the same fate." Instead it constructs a simile — *like* those — because simile is the Qur'an's instrument for teaching that history is not a sequence of singular events but a gallery of recurring patterns. What the Qaynuqāʿ tasted, the Naḍīr have now tasted; what the Naḍīr taste, whoever walks their road will taste. The comparison quietly extends the sūrah's timeline backward and forward at once: backward to the Qaynuqāʿ (2/624), forward — by the same logic — to the Qurayẓah (5/627), and beyond them to every power in history that wagers its security on covenant-breaking.

**"Tasted": The Qur'an's Sommelier of Consequences**

The verb is precise and deliberate: *dhāqū* — "they tasted." The Qur'an consistently renders the experience of consequences as *taste* — *fa-dhūqū*, "so taste!" — because taste is the most intimate and undeniable of the senses. A consequence seen at a distance can be argued with, doubted, minimized; a consequence on the tongue cannot be disputed by the one tasting it. God says to the punished in the Qur'an's own voice:

> *"So taste [the fruit of] what you earned; and for the unbelievers is the punishment of the Fire."* (3:182; cf. 8:50; 32:20)

And what did they taste? The verse says *dhāqū sayyiʾa amrihim* — they tasted "the evil of their affair." The genitive is the theology: the punishment is not an alien import bolted onto the deed; it is the deed's own *affair* — its fruit, its harvest, its natural taste once it ripens. Sin, in the Qur'an's agronomy, is a seed that carries its own flavor profile, and the flavor matures into bitterness no matter how sweet the planting seemed. The treachery of the Qaynuqāʿ tasted of exile; the treachery of the Naḍīr tasted of exile plus orchards lost plus allies exposed; the pattern compounds because compounds are how appetite works — each betrayal, unpunished, whets the next.

**The Proximity of the Example: Mercy in Recent History**

Notice the small word with large mercy: *qarīban* — "recently, a short time before." The Qaynuqāʿ had been expelled only two years earlier. God arranged the lesson of history within living, vivid, local memory — the Naḍīr did not need archaeology to learn; they needed only to look down the road their neighbors had taken. This is divine pedagogy's habit: the Qur'an constantly appeals to ruins and falls that its first hearers could verify or even remember:

> *"Many ways of life have passed away before you; so travel through the earth and see what was the end of those who denied."* (3:137)

> *"Have they not traveled through the land, that they may have hearts whereby to understand, or ears whereby to hear? Truly it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts in the breasts."* (22:46)

The appeal to evidence — travel, see, taste — is the Qur'an's invitation to reason from history rather than merely to fear its own warnings. And the shortness of the interval is itself a gift: God could have let the Naḍīr's memory of the Qaynuqāʿ fade for a generation before testing them; He let the lesson stand fresh at the gate of their fortresses. Every generation receives the same arrangement — the recent collapse of an arrogant power, the recent ruin of a treacherous scheme — usually within the span of a single news cycle. The failure to learn is never for lack of examples; it is what verse 14 diagnosed: hearts that do not *faqiha*, do not penetrate to the connection between their plot and the freshly dug trench two years old.

**The Two-Track Verdict Again — and the Doctrine of the Second Chances That Ran Out**

The verse closes the earthly ledger and opens the eternal one: *wa-lahum ʿadhābun alīm* — "and theirs shall be a painful punishment." As in verse 3, the accounting is double: the tasted consequence in this world (exile, loss, exposure) is real but partial; the un-tasted balance is stored for the Hereafter. But the pairing teaches something verse 3 did not spell out: worldly punishment did not *reform* them, and its failure to reform is what makes the second punishment just. The Qaynuqāʿ's exile was, among other things, a warning delivered to the Naḍīr; the Naḍīr absorbed the warning as intelligence to be managed rather than repentance to be made. When a community treats God's chastisements as data for better scheming, it has voted — freely, repeatedly — for the trajectory it is on. The Qur'an names the psychology with terrible economy:

> *"Let not those who disbelieve suppose that the respite We grant them is good for them; We grant them respite only that they may increase in sin; and theirs shall be a humiliating punishment."* (3:178)

**The Pattern Verified in Our Own Century**

The verse's simile-logic — *like those shortly before them* — is not a closed historical reference but a standing instrument of analysis. The twentieth century alone staged the drama repeatedly: regimes and movements that broke every covenant on their road to power, supremely confident in fortress ideologies, surrounded by allies of convenience — and then, within a span their strategists called impossible, tasting the evil of their own affair: walls that held no loyalty, allies who vanished by morning, elites discovering that the fear they had instilled in others was the only glue their coalition ever had. The believer who has absorbed verse 15 watches such events without the fatalism of the cynic or the surprise of the naive: neither "history is chaos" nor "who could have known," but the older, calmer reading — *the taste has arrived; the pattern is the same; the Book called it fourteen centuries ago.* And the reading carries an equal duty inward: the same simile addresses every private life that ignores a recently tasted consequence — the burned hand, the broken trust, the lost friendship — and reaches again for the same fire. Between the Qaynuqāʿ and the Naḍīr stood two years and a verse; between our own tasted lessons and our next choice stands only the interval we choose to make of ourselves.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:16

> **Like Satan when he says unto man, "Disbelieve!" Then when he disbelieves, he says, "Surely I am quit of you. Truly I fear God, Lord of the worlds."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Qur'an's Comparative Method: From Ibn Ubayy to Iblīs**

Verse 16 performs the deepest move in the sūrah's anatomy of betrayal. Verses 11–12 exposed the hypocrites' promise to the Naḍīr; verse 15 compared them to the Qaynuqāʿ; now verse 16 drops beneath history altogether and compares them to the original betrayer — *ka-mathali al-shayṭān*, "like Satan." Al-Bayḍāwī states the comparison plainly: as the hypocrites urged the Naḍīr to defiance, promised them victory, and then abandoned them, so Satan urges man to disbelief, promises him everything, and then abandons him at the hour of consequence. This is the Qur'an's signature method of *tamthīl* — drawing a parable that maps a visible, recent event onto the invisible, eternal pattern behind it. The Naḍīr had literally experienced the satanic structure: enticed into ruin by allies who then, quoting piety, washed their hands of them. God now tells them — and every reader after them — that their betrayal by Ibn Ubayy was not novel treachery but the oldest transaction in creation, run by its oldest dealer.

**The Dealer's Script: Call, Collapse, Disavow**

The verse compresses Satan's entire career into three beats. Beat one — the call: *"qāla li-insāni: akfur"* — he says to man, "Disbelieve!" Note the purity of the temptation: no argument is recorded, only the imperative — because the seducer's leverage is never the strength of his case but the weakness of his victim's guard, and his call always arrives dressed as liberation. Beat two — the collapse: *fa-lammā kafara* — "then, when he disbelieved" — the verb is past and accomplished; the deed is done, the door is shut, the investment is sunk. Beat three — the disavowal, and here the Qur'an's ear for hypocrisy reaches its terrifying finest: *"innī barīʾun minka; innī akhāfu Allāha, Rabba al-ʿālamīn"* — "surely I am quit of you; truly I fear God, the Lord of the worlds." The deserter quotes piety at the man he ruined. He does not say "you were stupid" or "I got what I needed"; he wraps his abandonment in the very religion he taught his victim to break. This is the signature of the satanic voice: it steals God's vocabulary to perform its exits. Anyone who has listened to a demagogue invoke God while abandoning his followers, a radicalizer quote scripture over the ruins of those he recruited, or a partner discover scruples at the exact moment loyalty became costly, has heard beat three verbatim.

**Uḥud's Scene: The Pattern Filmed Once for All**

The Qur'an had already shown the Satan-script staged at full scale on the morning of Uḥud, in a verse the commentators read as the twin of this one:

> *"And when Satan made their deeds seem fair unto them, he said: 'None among mankind shall overcome you this day, and I am indeed your defender.' But when the two hosts saw each other, he turned on his heels and said: 'I am quit of you! I see what you see not. I fear God; and God is severe in punishment.'"* (8:48)

Every clause matches: the beautified deeds, the maximal promise ("I am your defender"), the reversal "when the two hosts saw each other" — that is, the instant cost became visible — and the exit line combining disavowal with a sudden profession of fearing God. Sūrat Ibrāhīm then publishes the sequel, the speech Satan will deliver to his entire clientele on the Day of Reckoning:

> *"And Satan will say, when the matter has been decreed: 'God made you the promise of truth, and I made you a promise — but I failed you. I had no authority over you, save that I called you, and you responded to me. So blame me not, but blame yourselves. I cannot respond to your cries, nor can you respond to mine. I disbelieved in your ascribing me as partner aforetime. Truly the wrongdoers — theirs shall be a painful punishment.'"* (14:22)

Read 14:22 slowly and the entire moral architecture of temptation lies open: *God's promise was true; mine was false; I had no authority — I only called, and you answered.* The call, stripped bare, is nothing — a sound, a suggestion, a beautified impulse. The entire transaction was consummated by the answer. Satan's final speech is thus, horribly, honest: it is the confession of a con artist who never once forged a signature — his victims signed everything themselves.

**The Psychology of the Call — and the Prophet's Counter-Protocol**

The Prophet ﷺ mapped the call's entry points with clinical precision, and his Companions memorized the counter-protocol. From Abū Hurayrah, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī:

> *"Satan comes to one of you and says: 'Who created such-and-such? Who created such-and-such?' — until he says: 'Who created your Lord?' So when he reaches that, let him seek refuge in God and desist."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3276)

The instruction is a marvel of practical psychology: do not debate the spiral — exit it; the whisper feeds on engagement. And the Qur'an's wider curriculum trains the believer to recognize the call's whole repertoire — fear-mongering (3:175), the promise of poverty to the generous (2:268), the beautifying of sin "step by step" (4:27–28's idiom) — because recognition is nine-tenths of resistance. What verse 16 adds to the curriculum is the *timeline disclosure*: the seducer's promise is issued with certainty and expired with a shrug. Every satanic call is, structurally, a Naḍīr-style guarantee from an Ibn-Ubayy-style ally.

**The Longest Consistency in Moral History**

Finally, stand back and observe what the comparison asserts: that the same operating procedure has been running since the first son of Adam — the Qur'an's version of the first murder opens with precisely this offer, as the killer himself recalls: *"His soul prompted him to slay his brother, and he slew him, and became among the losers"* (5:30). Thirteen centuries of revealed warnings, and now fourteen more of human history, have not obliged Satan to update his script even slightly — no revision was ever needed, because the script's exploit has never been patched: the human tendency to answer intoxicating calls and only later, in the wreckage, ask who was calling. Verse 16 hands every reader the script in advance, with the ending printed on its face. The only remaining question — the one on which destinies divide — is whether the reader will treat the printed ending as a spoiler to be resented or as the very mercy it is: God, who owes us no preview, giving us one anyway.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:17

> **The end of both is that they will be in the Fire, abiding therein. That is the recompense of the wrongdoers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Both": The Grammar of Shared Fate**

The Arabic pronoun is dual: *fa-ʿāqibatuhumā* — "the end of the two of them." The Tempter and the tempted — in the immediate historical frame, the hypocrites who seduced the Naḍīr into ruin and the Naḍīr who were seduced; in the archetypal frame of verse 16, Satan and the man he called to disbelief — arrive at the same gate and enter by it together. This single pronoun abolishes at a stroke the two favorite defenses of every ruined follower: *"I was deceived"* and *"he made me do it."* Before the Fire, both defenses are true — and irrelevant. The deceiver bears his sin, the deceived bears his, and the shared destination does not average out; each tastes the whole of what he sent ahead. The Qur'an's courtroom of the Hereafter will be crowded with mutual recrimination precisely because everyone's case against everyone else will be valid — and useless:

> *"And whenever a nation enters [the Fire], it curses its sister-nation, until, when they have all followed one another therein, the last of them says of the first: 'Our Lord, these led us astray; so give them a double punishment of the Fire.' He says: 'For each is double, but you do not know.'"* (7:38)

For each is *double* — a terrifying arithmetic: being misled does not deduct from the misled; it adds to both accounts. Misguidance, in the Qur'an's economy, is not a resource to be mined by victims; it is a compounding liability held jointly.

**Leadership Doubles the Account — in Both Directions**

This dual accounting is why the Qur'an treats calls, examples, and precedents as the heaviest currency in the moral universe. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever institutes a good practice (sunnah) in Islam will have its reward and the reward of those who act upon it after him, without their rewards being diminished in any way; and whoever institutes an evil practice will bear its burden and the burden of those who act upon it after him, without their burdens being diminished in any way."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1017, from Jarīr ibn ʿAbd Allāh)

Read against verse 17, the ḥadīth exposes the asymmetry of influence: the caller to disbelief earns the *entire stream* of his call's consequences, while the answerer still owns his own response fully. Ibn Ubayy will meet, in his account, every Naḍīr palm felled on the strength of his promise; and the Naḍīr will meet their own signature on their own defiance. Modern readers live inside this verse more than any before them: an age of mass influence — the recruiter, the influencer, the propagandist, the trend-setter of sin — is an age in which the "both" of verse 17 will be the most populous category in the Hereafter. The verse quietly converts every platform into a ledger with unlimited co-signatories, and every follow into a potential co-authorship of consequences.

**"Abiding Therein": The Word That Broke the Revisionists**

The verse's participle is uncompromising: *khālidīna fīhā* — "abiding therein." The Qur'an's doctrine of an abiding Fire for those who chose it over every call to return — sustained by the same God who is al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm — is a pillar of its eschatology, repeated in this exact clause-pairing (Fire, abiding, recompense of the wrongdoers) throughout the Book (e.g., 18:106; 32:14; 41:28). The precise depth of that doctrine belongs to ʿaqīdah; what verse 17 contributes is its *pairing*: the eternity clause is not attached to the sin's size but to the sin's *finality* — the Tempter who never repented of tempting, and the tempted who died defending the choice they were seduced into. As verses 3–4 already established, respite was offered, terms were displayed, the lesser punishments were tasted (v. 15) as warnings; the abiding Fire is what a lifetime of refused returns finally settles into. Theologians such as Ibn Taymiyyah have noted the exacting justice of the sequence: a punishment is eternal that corresponds to an intention that was eternal — the will that says, until its last heartbeat, *I prefer this to Him.*

**Ẓulm: The Verse's Chosen Word for What Happened**

The closing name for the pair is *al-ẓālimīn* — "the wrongdoers." The choice of word is the Qur'an's final diagnosis, and it comes with the classical etymology attached: *ẓulm*, in the pure Arabic of the early authorities, is to place a thing other than in its proper place — to put the saddle on the horse's neck, the seed in the path, the crown on the wrong head. The ẓālim is therefore, before he injures anyone else, an engineer of displacement: he misplaces the Creator's rights (given to idols, allies, appetites), misplaces his own soul (in servitude to what cannot save), and misplaces the trust of others (as the Naḍīr misplaced a covenant, and their seducers misplaced a promise). And the Qur'an insists that the first casualty of this displacement is the engineer himself: *"And they wronged not Us, but themselves they wronged"* (2:57; 7:160) — you cannot misplace another's due without first misplacing your own soul's position in reality. Verse 17's verdict — *that is the recompense of the wrongdoers* — is therefore not the language of vengeance but the language of perfect bookkeeping: the Fire is simply every misplaced thing, restored at last to its proper place, with its proper owner standing in it.

**The Exit Sign Placed Before the Sentence**

Yet even this sternest of verses carries, for the reader still on the road, its embedded exit. The sentence is pronounced on "the end (*ʿāqibah*) of both" — and an end presupposes a road on which the end was chosen. Every element of the satanic transaction reviewed in verses 16–17 — the call, the response, the disavowal — has its moral mirror: God's call, running through prophets and scriptures and the quiet voice of conscience; the response, renewed each morning; and a Promise-Keeper whose disavowal of His servants is the one impossibility in the universe: *"God will not fail the believers in the promise He made them"* (14:47's principle; cf. 9:111's binding transaction of Paradise). The man in verse 16 answered the wrong voice because it arrived first, loudest, and dressed in beauty. Verse 17 has now printed, for everyone who reads it, both scripts side by side — the one that ends *"I am quit of you"* and the One that ends *"I am with you"* (2:153) — and left the timing of the answer, as it has always been, to us.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:18

> **O you who believe! Reverence God and let every soul consider what it has sent forth for the morrow; and reverence God. Truly God is Aware of whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command Framed Twice: The Architecture of the Audit**

The verse's structure is unique in the Qur'an and worth observing before its content: *taqwā* is commanded at the beginning — *"ittaqū Allāha"* — and then commanded again at the end — *"wa-ttaqū Allāha"* — with the self-audit enclosed between the two commandments like a document inside its seal. The classical commentators hear in the repetition the gravity of what is being framed: an accounting of one's own soul is not a self-help exercise to be attempted in a merely human frame of mind; it opens and closes under the fear of God, or it does not open honestly at all. Self-examination without taqwā curves into one of two deformations — either self-flagellation that despairs, or self-congratulation that lies. Taqwā is the spirit-level that keeps the audit plumb: I examine myself not to punish myself nor to admire myself, but to answer to the One who already knows.

**What Taqwā Is — and the Image the Qur'an's Teachers Used**

*Taqwā*, from the root *w-q-y*, means to shield, to guard, to take protection. The most famous image in the tradition is the Prophet's ﷺ own, given in a single clause that the Companions never forgot:

> *"Fear God wherever you are; and follow a wrong with a good deed that will erase it; and treat people with beautiful character."* (al-Tirmidhī 1987, from Abū Dharr; graded ḥasan)

The teachers of the heart expanded the image: taqwā is like a man walking a mountain path in fog, or driving a high road at night along a cliff — not paralyzed, but *attentive* — every decision made with the drop-off in mind. The driver who "respects" the mountain road slows before the bend, checks the mirror, refuses the third overtaking attempt — not because he hates driving, but because he has an accurate map of what the road can do. Taqwā is exactly that accuracy about the road of life: the bends (temptation), the cliff (sin), the other travelers (rights), and the destination (the Day this whole verse is about). Verse 18 commands it twice because the audit it demands is the most dangerous driving of all: looking at one's own record.

**"What It Has Sent Ahead for the Morrow": The Qur'an's Commercial Grammar**

The verse's central demand — *wa-tanẓur nafsun mā qaddamat li-ghad* — "let every soul look at what it has sent ahead for the morrow" — is built from the language of commerce and logistics. *Qaddama* means to send ahead, to advance supplies; a caravan traveling through the desert would dispatch provisions ahead to a way-station before the travelers arrived. *Ghad* — "the morrow" — is deliberately bare: not "the Day of Judgment" as a doctrinal term, but simply *tomorrow* — because the next world is, in the Qur'an's framing, not a distant hypothesis but the nearest of all futures: closer than the far end of your own life, which is itself not far. The Qur'an uses this provisioning grammar for the whole of moral life:

> *"And whatever good you send ahead for your souls, you will find it with God, better and greater in reward."* (73:20)

Deeds are *forwarded freight*. They travel ahead of the doer, arrive at the destination first, and are waiting — sorted, weighed, and itemized — when the traveler comes. The verse commands each soul to *look* (*tanẓur*) at its shipment: to open the manifest. What have I actually dispatched — belief, prayer, charity, truth, justice? Or betrayal, cruelty, and deferred repentance? The image is so concrete that the scholars of the Hereafter used it literally: on the Day of Resurrection, a man's deeds are what meet him, and he meets *them* before he meets anything else. The Qur'an says of the righteous that they will find *"their provision delivered to them"* (3:198's idiom of hospitality), and of the wicked that they will wish for a great separation (9:44's mood; cf. 26:102).

**The Prophet's Friday Sermon for the Poor: The Verse Lived**

Ibn Kathīr transmits the verse's most touching occasion. The Prophet ﷺ, seeing the poverty of some of the Muslims, gathered the people and recited to them the opening of Sūrat al-Nisāʾ — *"O mankind! Reverence your Lord, Who created you from a single soul..."* (4:1) — and then recited this verse. The community dispersed and returned with whatever they could carry: food, clothing, dates — until the poor of Madinah were provided for, and the Prophet ﷺ rejoiced. Two things about this event deserve meditation. First, the Prophet read the self-audit verse as a sermon on *generosity*: what you send ahead for the morrow, he taught by action, includes what you give away today — the only freight that grows lighter to carry and heavier on arrival. Second, he recited 4:1 — the verse of humanity's single origin — as the prologue: the audit of the soul and the feeding of the poor are one liturgy, because a heart truly looking at its Hereafter immediately looks at its neighbor's here-and-now.

**Muḥāsabah: The Discipline the Verse Founded**

Muslim spirituality built from this verse an entire institutional discipline: *muḥāsabah*, the daily self-reckoning. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb — the same caliph who refused to privatize an empire's fayʾ — gave the discipline its classic charter:

> *"Call yourselves to account before you are called to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed; and prepare yourselves for the greatest of presentations."* (attributed to ʿUmar; transmitted in the classical adab literature)

The reasoning is pure Qur'anic logic: the audit of the morrow is inevitable (v. 18's God *is aware of whatsoever you do*); the only variable is whether it happens today, privately, with the pen of repentance still in one's hand, or tomorrow, publicly, with the ledger already sealed. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī added time's funeral cadence: *"O son of Adam, you are but days; when a day passes, a part of you passes."* And the Prophet ﷺ supplied the classification:

> *"The firm and intelligent one (al-kayyis) is he who calls his soul to account and works for what comes after death; and the incapable one (al-ʿājiz) is he who lets his soul follow its desire while hoping in God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2459, from Shaddād ibn Aws; graded ḥasan)

Hope without shipment, in other words, is not hope; it is a cargo fraud practiced on oneself.

**The Two Blessings Humanity Squanders**

No modern application of verse 18 is more pointed than the Prophet's ﷺ audit of time itself:

> *"There are two blessings in which many people are cheated: health and free time."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6412)

Our civilization is the most future-planning in human history — pension funds, insurance actuaries, five-year strategic plans, retirement calculators to the decimal — and it applies precisely none of that sophistication to the one tomorrow that is certain. The professional who would never board a plane without an itinerary will spend forty adult years boarding the flight of life with no manifest for its arrival. Verse 18 does not condemn planning; it *out-plans* the planners: the rational actor, optimizing across all horizons, must allocate resources to the unbounded morrow first — *"and whatever you send ahead for your souls, you will find it with God, better and greater in reward"* (73:20). The nightly muḥāsabah — two honest minutes at the day's end: *what did I ship today?* — is the entire discipline the verse demands; and the God who closes the verse with *"Truly God is Aware of whatsoever you do"* reminds the auditor that the audit is never really private: the Examiner has been present at every entry, and His awareness, unlike our own, never sleeps, never flatters, and never forgets to ask.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:19

> **And be not like those who forget God, such that He makes them forget their souls. It is they who are the iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Punishment Shaped Exactly Like the Sin**

Verse 19 contains one of the Qur'an's most quietly terrifying constructions: *nasū Allāha fa-ansāhum anfusahum* — "they forgot God, so He made them forget their souls." The grammar is causal and symmetrical: the divine requital is cast in the exact mold of the human act. They forgot; therefore they are made to forget. Islamic theology names this pattern *jazāʾ min jins al-ʿamal* — recompense in the very species of the deed — and the Qur'an deploys it repeatedly to display the precision of divine justice: those who mocked were met with mockery *"God mocks them"* (2:15's idiom of requital); those who dealt in hypocrisy were punished by being left in it: *"He punished them with hypocrisy in their hearts until the day they meet Him, because they proved false to God in what they promised Him"* (9:77); and here, those who forgot God are abandoned to the amnesia they chose — until the forgetting reaches the forgetter himself. Sin, allowed to complete its logic, always returns home. The man who erases God from his horizon does not arrive at a Godless world that still contains himself; he arrives at a world in which even *himself* has begun to vanish.

**What "Forgetting Themselves" Means: The Commentators' Two Readings**

What does it mean for God to make people forget their own souls? Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ṭabarī preserve two complementary readings. First: they cease to hear what benefits the soul and to do what purifies it — forgetfulness as *neglect of the soul's maintenance*, like a man who forgets he owns a garden and lets it return to thorn. Second: they forget *the right of their souls* — the share, the due (ḥaqq al-nafs), that their own selves are owed in God's economy: the soul has claims upon its owner — feeding it truth, not falsehood; rest and worship, not exhaustion and distraction; dignity, not debasement — and the God-forgetter defaults on all of them, an embezzler of his own trust. Both readings converge on a single anthropology the verse makes non-negotiable: the self is not self-grounding. It is a trust received from God, knowable only in the light of God, and nourishable only by what God sends down. Cut the cable, and the lamp does not shine on alone; it goes dark.

**Remember Me, and I Remember You: The Reciprocity of Remembrance**

The verse's dark theorem — forget God, and He lets you forget yourself — has a luminous obverse that the Qur'an states as a covenant:

> *"So remember Me, and I shall remember you; and be thankful to Me, and be not ungrateful towards Me."* (2:152)

The symmetry is total: God's remembrance of the servant is conditional upon the servant's remembrance of God — not because God forgets (He cannot), but because remembrance is a *relationship*, and a relationship requires two present parties. The tradition also notes the exquisite inversion this verse performs on the maxim the Muslim spiritual tradition has long debated — *"man ʿarafa nafsahu fa-qad ʿarafa rabbahu"*, "whoever knows himself knows his Lord" (regarded by some as a ḥadīth, though most scholars trace it as a maxim of the early sages). Verse 19 supplies the negative proof of the maxim: whoever *forgets* his Lord ends by forgetting *himself* — because the self's deepest document, the covenant of *alast*, was signed in God's presence before birth:

> *"And when thy Lord drew forth from the Children of Adam, from their loins, their posterity, and made them testify of themselves: 'Am I not your Lord?' — they said: 'Yea, we testify!' — that you may not say on the Day of Resurrection: 'Of this we were heedless.'"* (7:172)

Self-knowledge, in the Qur'an, is therefore *anamnesis* — the un-forgetting of a testimony the soul already gave. Dhikr, the practice of remembrance, is not the acquisition of a new fact but the recovery of the oldest one: *I have a Lord, and I answered Him yes.*

**The Evidence That the Heart Rusted Over**

The Qur'an describes the mechanism of this forgetting in a medical image of rust:

> *"No indeed! Rather, there has rusted over their hearts that which they used to earn."* (83:14)

Sin accumulates as oxide on the heart's surface — each unrepented act a thin film — until the mirror that was designed to reflect God and the self cannot reflect either. And the verses surrounding verse 19's theme name the solvent the rust-proofing industry of the soul: *"and whoever believes in God, He guides his heart"* (64:11), *"truly in the remembrance of God do hearts find rest"* (13:28), *"and obey not him whose heart We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follows his desire, and whose affair is excess"* (18:28). Note 18:28's diagnostic triad, because it is the behavioral profile of verse 19's casualties: heedlessness of remembrance, desire-following, excess. The forgetting of God never stays theological; it immediately proceduralizes into a life organized around appetite — and the self, once demoted from soul to appetite-delivery system, is functionally forgotten: its origin unexamined, its destination unplanned, its dignity unclaimed.

**The Modern Epidemic the Verse Diagnosed in Advance**

It is impossible to read verse 19 honestly without recognizing our own civilization in it. The age that removed God from public memory has produced, with eerie fidelity, an epidemic of self-forgetting: identity crises that no social category can resolve; a mental-health pandemic whose deepest strands are meaninglessness and self-alienation; addiction at scale — the anesthetic economy of scrolling, streaming, substance, and purchase, each a dhikr-substitute that quiets the ache of the forgotten soul for exactly as long as the dose lasts; and a burnout culture in which human beings, traded as throughput, lose track of what they are inside their own résumés. None of this is coincidence; it is verse 19 run as a fourteen-century longitudinal study. The secular remedies — mindfulness, therapy, wellness — each borrow fragments of the cure (presence, honesty, self-examination) while amputating the cable that powers them: the remembered God. They teach a self to look at a self in a mirror nobody lit. The Qur'an's protocol remains what it always was: begin with dhikr, and let the self come back online in the light of its Lord — *"Remember God much"* (33:41), and, in the sūrah's own key, audit the morrow (v. 18) with a soul that has remembered Whose it is.

**"The Iniquitous": The Sūrah's Recurring Verdict**

The verse closes with the same Name that sealed verse 5: *al-fāsiqūn* — the iniquitous, those burst from their proper skin. The repetition traces the chapter's through-line: the Naḍīr were fāsiqūn when they split the covenant (v. 5), and fāsiqūn is now revealed to be the *end-state* of forgetting God (v. 19) — because a soul that forgets its Maker will finally violate every boundary that was meant anything only by His remembering. Between the two occurrences lies the whole sūrah's moral cartography: keep God in memory, and the soul keeps its shape, its dues, and its road; erase Him, and the shape holds only until the first pressure — then the casing splits, the contents escape, and a human being, made to be God's remembered servant, discovers that the self he forgot to feed was the only possession he could not replace.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:20

> **Not equal are the inhabitants of the Fire and the inhabitants of the Garden. The inhabitants of the Garden—they are the triumphant.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Qur'an's Axiom of Inequality**

After the self-audit of verse 18 and the warning of verse 19, verse 20 states the conclusion in the form of a theorem: *lā yastawī aṣḥāb al-nāri wa-aṣḥāb al-jannah* — "not equal are the companions of the Fire and the companions of the Garden." The verb *istawā* means to be level, equivalent, balanced — and the Qur'an uses its negation the way geometry uses an axiom: as the fixed point from which all moral reasoning proceeds. The Qur'an deploys this comparative formula across the whole of its teaching — the seeing and the blind (35:19), the living and the dead (35:22), those who know and those who know not (39:9), the believer and the corrupt ("Is one who believes like one who is corrupt? They are not equal," 32:18), and the two parties of destiny:

> *"Or shall We make those who believe and work righteousness as the corrupters in the earth? Or shall We make the reverent as the iniquitous?"* (38:28)

The point of the formula is not to grade human worth in this world — where the Qur'an's law insists on the equal dignity of every soul — but to refuse the final flattening of outcomes. A universe in which the torturer and his victim, the truthful and the perjurer, the traitor and the faithful, all end in the same silence is a universe whose moral language has no referent. Verse 20 plants the flag: endings differ, and they differ *because* the roads differed. In the Qur'an's own framing of the two destinies, God invokes precisely this sūrah's name for the Day:

> *"And warn them of the Day of Gathering (yawm al-ḥashr), about which there is no doubt: a party in the Garden, and a party in the Blaze."* (42:7)

**"Companions": You Become the Company You Keep**

The Qur'an's word for the two groups is pointed: *aṣḥāb* — companions, associates, those who keep company with a thing until its qualities transfer to them. Arabic says *ṣāḥib* of one who travels with another so long that speech, gait, and manners are shared. The Fire's people are its *aṣḥāb* — they kept company with its causes in this world: with rage, treachery, appetite, forgetfulness of God (v. 19) — until the companionship matured into an address. And the Garden's people kept company with its causes: remembrance, truth, patience, the sending-ahead of freight for the morrow (v. 18). The doctrine inside the noun is the Qur'an's recurring moral physics: you do not *arrive* at your destination; you *become* it, slowly, by daily association. The Prophet ﷺ warned and promised in exactly this grammar: a man will be raised on the Day of Resurrection *with those whom he loved* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd) — companionship is destiny, chosen one association at a time.

**"The Triumphant": The Language of the Winning Trade**

The verse's final clause names the Garden's people with the Qur'an's supreme word for success: *humu al-fāʾizūn* — "they are the triumphant." The root *f-w-z* carries the sense of winning, attaining, escaping loss — the cry of the merchant whose venture returned, the racer who touched the mark, the survivor who reached safety. It is the Qur'an's most compact verdict on what winning actually is, set against its most compact definition of losing: *"Say: shall We inform you of the greatest losers in respect of their deeds? Those whose effort goes astray in the life of the world, while they suppose they are doing good"* (18:103–104) — the terrifying ledger of effort spent, and misspent. The Garden is called the *great triumph* (al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm) precisely because every other victory is priced in perishables:

> *"God has promised the believing men and believing women gardens beneath which rivers flow, therein abiding, and pleasant dwellings in gardens of Eden. And God's pleasure is greater than that; that is the great triumph."* (9:72)

Read the clause's scale carefully: eternal gardens are listed as the * lesser* term of the promise — and God's pleasure *greater than that*. The supreme triumph is not the real estate; it is the Reconciled Face of the Lord of the worlds. Everything else in Paradise is furniture for that meeting.

**The World in Miniature: Prison and Meadow**

The Prophet ﷺ gave the believers the lens through which verse 20 becomes a daily meditation rather than an article of eschatology:

> *"The world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2956)

The saying is not a hymn to misery; it is a theory of comparative experience. The believer lives in the world under restraint the way a prisoner lives under discipline — appetites curbed, honesty enforced even when costly, every fleeting pleasure audited against an eternal morrow — and the discipline chafes precisely because his tastes have been re-formed for another country. The disbeliever, conversely, gets from the world exactly what he values: unconstrained appetite, unexamined days — a paradise, at the scale his imagination permits, which is the scale of a few decades. But prisons and meadows are stations, not verdicts. Verse 20 states the verdict: the restraints of the prisoner end at a gate into the Garden, and the meadows of the unaccountable life end at the same gate, from the other side. What each experienced in miniature during sixty years is simply continued at scale forever — *"as for him whose balance [of deeds] is heavy, he shall live a pleasant life; but as for him whose balance is light, the Pit shall be his mother"* (101:6–9).

**Deferred Gratification as the Structure of Reality**

Strip verse 20 to its logic and it teaches a single principle at cosmic magnitude: outcomes are a function of roads, and roads are chosen under the discipline of what one believes about the destination. Every system of ethics ever attempted is some version of this verse's wager — that present conduct should be governed by future reckoning. What the Qur'an adds is certainty where every human system offers conjecture: the two companies are not equal, the ledger is real, the gathering is sure (*"about which there is no doubt,"* 42:7). And because the difference is certain, the rational actor's calculation becomes simple — indeed, the Qur'an repeatedly invites the reader to do exactly this math: *"Whatsoever you have been given is the enjoyment of the life of this world and its adornment; and what is with God is better and more abiding. Will you not then understand?"* (28:60). Modern psychology has spent a century confirming the junior version of the doctrine — that the capacity to defer gratification predicts nearly everything about a life — while carefully declining to ask the senior version: what deferral is owed to an *eternal* morrow? Verse 20 answers with the sūrah's cool finality: the inhabitants of the Garden are the triumphant — because they alone, of all the traders in history, bought the only commodity that does not spoil, and held it long enough to be paid in full.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:21

> **Had We made this Quran descend upon a mountain, thou wouldst have seen it humbled, rent asunder by the fear of God. These are the parables We set forth for mankind, that haply they may reflect.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Thought Experiment That Reverses the Universe's Weight-Scale**

Verse 21 stages one of the Qur'an's most vertiginous hypotheticals: *law anzalnā hādhā al-Qurʾāna ʿalā jabal* — "Had We sent this Qur'an down upon a mountain." The verb is the verb of revelation itself — *anzala*, the same word used for the Qur'an's descent upon the Prophet's heart. Imagine, the verse proposes, that same descent landing not on a human breast but on a mountain: *la-raʾaytahu khāshiʿan* — you would have seen it *humbled*, bowed, crushed low — *mutaṣaddiʿan min khashyati Allāh* — *split asunder, rent asunder* — from the dread of God. The Arabic *taṣaddaʿa* is violent: to burst, break into fragments, as a dam bursts. The mountain — the Qur'an's own icon of permanence and unshakeable constancy (*"And you see the mountains and suppose them fixed, yet they pass as the passing of clouds — the handiwork of God, Who has perfected all things"*, 27:88) — would not merely tremble; it would disintegrate. The point of the image is comparative weight: measured against the Word of God, the heaviest thing on earth's horizon is a soap bubble. And the implied question detonates under every reader: *if the mountain would shatter, what should the human heart do — and what does its unresponsiveness say about it?*

**The Reprimand Reading: A Charge Against Hearts Harder Than Basalt**

Most of the commentators — Ibn Kathīr, al-Zamakhsharī, and ʿAjībah among them, as the source notes — read the verse as a reprimand precisely along that line: how can the son of Adam hear what a mountain could not bear, and not soften? The verse indicts a humanity that has preformed the Qur'an's reception into ritual background music — recited at speed, played at funerals, scrolled past on screens — while granite, had it been the addressee, would have split open. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, when asked why hearts do not tremble at the Qur'an, is remembered to have answered that hearts see themselves as self-sufficient — a mountain knows it is a mountain; a man forgets he is dust. The reprimand reading reaches its sharpest edge in the Qur'an's own comparison of heedless humanity to stone and worse:

> *"Then your hearts were hardened after that, till they were like stones or harder still; for among stones are those from which rivers gush, and those that split and water issues from them, and those that crash down from the fear of God. And God is not unaware of what you do."* (2:74)

Verse 21 and 2:74 form a matched pair: stones that shatter from God's fear, and human hearts that out-harden them. The scandal of the comparison is the entire sermon.

**The Capacity Reading: The Heart as the Only Vessel Built for the Weight**

Yet the verse holds a second, luminous reading that the commentators — ʿAjībah among them — unfold with wonder: why did God send the Qur'an to man and not to the mountain? Because the mountain could only be *broken* by the Word, while the heart can *bear* it — can receive it, hold it, be shaped by it without being annihilated. The Qur'an itself records the audition that preceded man's commissioning:

> *"Truly We offered the Trust unto the heavens and the earth and the mountains, but they refused to bear it and were wary of it — yet man bore it."* (33:72)

The heavens declined; the earth declined; the mountains — those very masses of v. 21 — declined out of awe. Man accepted: the only creature in the cosmos whose design specification includes carrying the Word of God without shattering. And the spiritual tradition has never stopped marveling at the ḥadīth qudsī that explains the design:

> *"My heavens and My earth embrace Me not, but the soft, humble heart of My believing servant does embrace Me."* (cited by al-Ghazzālī, Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn, III.1.5, from the devotional literature)

The entire universe is too small for God, and cannot contain His Word's full weight; a *softened human heart* is the one container He named. The two readings of verse 21 are therefore one doctrine seen from two sides: the heart's hardness is a scandal *because* the heart's capacity is a miracle. You were built to carry what mountains would die of — hardness is not your nature; it is your malfunction.

**"These Are the Parables": The Qur'an's Method Made Explicit**

The verse names its own method: *tilka al-amthāl* — "these are the parables." A *mathal* places the unseen beside the seen so that the seen teaches the unseen — the mountain is visible everywhere, and everyone has watched rock crack in fire or frost; now let that image carry the freight of what the Qur'an *is*. The Qur'an defends the method against those who sneer at simple images:

> *"God is not shy to strike a similitude — of a gnat, or of something above it."* (2:26)

And it states the method's goal in this very verse: *laʿallahum yatafakkarūn* — "that haply they may reflect." The parable is the invitation; reflection is the RSVP. Al-Rāzī notes the beautiful economy of it: God could have simply declared the Qur'an's overwhelming weight; instead He staged an image — a splitting mountain — because the mind *feels* a picture it merely *registers* a proposition. It is the difference between reading the word "avalanche" and hearing the crack on the mountain above your village.

**The Prophet's Own Reception: What Bearing the Word Looked Like**

The first bearer of the Qur'an showed what receiving it at full weight does to a human being — not annihilation, but trembling, weeping, whitening. In the Ṣaḥīḥ collections, Ibn Masʿūd relates: the Prophet ﷺ said to him, *"Recite to me."* I said: *"Shall I recite to thee, when unto thee it was sent down?"* He said: *"I love to hear it from another."* So I recited Sūrat al-Nisāʾ until I reached *"How then, when We bring from every nation a witness, and bring thee as a witness over these?"* (4:41) — and he said: *"That is enough for now"* — and Ibn Masʿūd reports: I looked, and his eyes were flowing with tears (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Book of the Merits of the Qur'an). The mountain of the verse would have shattered; the Prophet wept and stood and prayed — the human vessel bearing in softness what hardness could not survive. His Companions learned the posture, and the Qur'an prescribed it for all recitation to come: *"And when the Qur'an is recited, listen to it and be silent, that haply you may receive mercy"* (7:204).

**The Verse Better Than a Thousand**

Finally, verse 21 is one of the passages the scholars had in mind in the ḥadīth cited at this sūrah's opening: *"Within [the Glorifiers] there is a verse better than a thousand verses."* While many apply the ḥadīth to 57:3, others — as al-Shawkānī records — apply it to the closing passage of al-Ḥashr beginning here, the passage of the mountain and the Divine Names that follows. The attribution is fitting for a reason the verses themselves reveal: verse 21 is the hinge on which the whole sūrah turns from history to eternity. Everything before it — expulsions, spoils, hypocrites, audits — has been the news of one Arabian decade; verse 21 suddenly discloses the *scale of the Book that carried the news*, and verses 22–24 will disclose the God who sent it. A reader who grasps the hinge receives the sūrah's final gift: the realization that the same Word which would shatter a mountain has been placed, entire, in *your* hands — and that the only appropriate posture for holding it was modeled by the first recipient: softened eyes, attentive silence, and a heart that lets itself be split open — not shattered, but opened.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:22

> **He is God, other than Whom there is no god, Knower of the Unseen and the seen. And He is the Compassionate, the Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Entering the Jewels of the Qur'an**

With verse 22 the sūrah crosses its final threshold into the passage the tradition reveres as among the "Jewels of the Qur'an" — the most comprehensive inventory of the Divine Names in the Book: seventeen Names across three verses, or eighteen if the opening pronoun *Huwa* is itself counted, as many authorities do, as a Name of the Divine Essence. The passage is recited devotionally in obligatory and supererogatory prayers alike, and its excellence is enshrined in the report transmitted from the early ḥadīth master Fuḍayl ibn ʿIyāḍ:

> *"One who reads the end of the sūrah of 'The Gathering' [vv. 21–24] until the morning and then dies on that very day is stamped with the stamp of those who witness [God]. One who reads it in the evening and then dies that very night is stamped with the stamp of those who witness [God]."* (cited by al-Ghazzālī, Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn, "On the Recitation of the Qur'an")

The structure of the passage is itself doctrine: verse 22 opens with the Essence and omniscience clothed in mercy; verse 23 unfolds the octaves of majesty and perfection; verse 24 completes the symphony with the Names of creation and beauty — Essence, attributes of glory, attributes of act. Nothing about the sequence is accidental. God teaches His Names the way a master architect walks a visitor through a great mosque: first the foundation, then the piers, then the dome.

**"He": The Pronoun of Presence**

The passage opens with a bare pronoun: *Huwa* — "He." Arabic rhetoric regards the initial pronoun without antecedent as the language of the already-known: the speaker of these Names needs no introduction, because the entire sūrah — the entire Qur'an, the entire cosmos — has been His context from the first verse. The scholars who count *Huwa* as the eighteenth Name mean something precise by it: all the other Names describe God *in relation to something* — knowledge, power, mercy — but *Huwa* points at the Essence that precedes all relation and all description. It is the Name that is almost no name: pure Presence, prior to every category the mind can carry. The verse thus begins where theology must begin — not with an argument, but with an Assumption of presence; everything else in the three verses is commentary on the *Huwa*.

**"He Is God, Other than Whom There Is No God": Tawḥīd in Its Skeleton Form**

*Huwa Allāhu alladhī lā ilāha illā Huwa* — the phrase is the load-bearing formula of the entire religion, the skeleton of the shahādah, repeated by the Qur'an at the head of its greatest theological declarations (2:255; 3:2; 20:14's domain of it). Its grammar performs its meaning: *lā ilāha* negates every deity in the universe of possible worship — a total, unqualified denial — and *illā Huwa* then admits exactly One. The formula is a door through which everything must exit except One. On the Name *Allāh*, the commentators of the earlier sūrahs have already given the tradition's consensus: it is *al-ism al-jāmiʿ*, the all-comprehensive Name, comprising every quality the other Names convey — and it is also the most specific of Names, since unlike *al-Raḥmān* or *al-Salām* it can never be applied, in the true sense, to any being but Him. Morphologically it admits no plural, no gender-partner, no diminutive — Arabic itself seems built to shelter this one word from the grammar of multiplicity. To say *Allāh*, therefore, is to say *everything true about God at once*; the sixteen Names that follow merely unpack it.

**"Knower of the Unseen and the Seen": The Two Halves of Everything**

The first Name-pair chosen after tawḥīd is omniscience: *ʿālim al-ghaybi wa-al-shahādah* — Knower of the Unseen and the witnessed. The partition is exhaustive and elegant: *shahādah* is everything that can be witnessed — present, observable, on the record; *ghayb* is everything else — hidden in hearts, sealed in wombs, stored in tomorrow, veiled in heaven. God alone holds both halves. The Qur'an stakes this claim absolutely:

> *"Say: None in the heavens and the earth knows the Unseen save God."* (27:65)

> *"And with Him are the keys of the Unseen; none knows them but He."* (6:59)

And the jurists drew from the Name a working epistemology: since only the witnessed is humanly knowable, law and testimony can proceed only on the *ẓāhir*, the outward and provable — *"we judge by what is outward, and God takes charge of the inner"* (ʿUmar's maxim) — while God alone judges the ghayb. ʿAjībah adds the metaphysical reading: God knows each thing *before* it emerges into existence, when it is still ghayb, and *after* it appears, when it becomes shahādah — His knowledge does not succeed events; events succeed His knowledge. Now set the Name back into its context and it detonates with meaning: this sūrah has just narrated a hidden plot — the millstone planned behind the Naḍīr's wall — exposed by heaven before any witness existed. Verse 2's drama was, in retrospect, simply *ʿālim al-ghaybi wa-al-shahādah* in action. The sūrah that opens with God's expulsion of the covenant-breakers now names, at its climax, the attribute that had made their secret a public fact: there was no wall against the Knower of the unseen.

**"The Compassionate, the Merciful": Omniscience Immediately Clothed in Mercy**

Then comes the ordering that should stop every reader: after a Name of total knowledge, the passage places *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm*. Knowledge first would terrify — an omniscient God is a God before whom no thought has ever been private, no sin ever unwitnessed, no motive ever successfully disguised. The passage clothes the omniscience in mercy before the heart can flee. The two Names, from the root *r-ḥ-m* — whose primal sense is the womb (*riḥm*), the tenderness that nurtures what it carries — are the Qur'an's most repeated pairing, and the scholars have long distinguished their offices: *al-Raḥmān* is the All-Compassionate whose mercy is universal, extended in this world to every creature, believer or rebel — *"My mercy encompasses all things"* (7:156) — while *al-Raḥīm* is the Merciful whose particular, saving mercy is prepared for the faithful in the Hereafter — *"He is ever Merciful unto the believers"* (33:43). And the treasury beneath both is the Prophet's ﷺ report of God's eternal declaration:

> *"God wrote: My mercy prevails over My wrath."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3194; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2751, from Abū Hurayrah)

God *wrote* — decreed, in a Book, before creation — that the outcome of the encounter between His infinite knowledge of our secrets and His infinite mercy would be mercy's victory, for everyone who turns. That is the theology of the sequence: *Huwa* — the Presence; *Allāh* — the One; omniscience — the God from whom nothing is hidden; then mercy — the God Who, knowing everything, nonetheless reaches first for the womb-word. The reader who has walked this sūrah — treachery, exile, terror, hypocrisy — arrives at verse 22 and learns what the whole hard history was for: to clear the ground of every false god so that these Names could be the last word. On this side of the verse stands the Knower of every hidden thing; the question verse 22 leaves ringing is whether the reader will meet that omniscience beneath the wrath it precedes — or beneath the mercy it is immediately clothed in.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:23

> **He is God, other than Whom there is no god, the Sovereign, the Holy, Peace, the Faithful, the Protector, the Mighty, the Compeller, the Proud. Glory be to Him above the partners they ascribe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Octave of Majesty: The Architecture of the List**

Verse 23 unfolds eight Names in a deliberate sequence — *al-Malik, al-Quddūs, al-Salām, al-Muʾmin, al-Muhaymin, al-ʿAzīz, al-Jabbār, al-Mutakabbir* — and Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazzālī, in his Commentary on the Most Beautiful Names of God, hears the sequence as a progression from the Essence toward the acts: from what God is in Himself, utterly apart from creation, to what God does over creation. It is the theological counterpart of a musical octave: the first notes belong to Heaven's interior, the last to Heaven's grip on history. Notice, too, that the verse returns to the tawḥīd formula before the Names begin — *He is God, other than Whom there is no god* — as though to fence them off: these eight perfections are not qualities a creature may share in their fullness; they are the exclusive regalia of the One. And the fence closes at the far end with *Glory be to Him above the partners they ascribe* — the list begins and ends in tanzīh, the declaration that God is beyond every composite, every rival, every human projection.

**Al-Malik — The Sovereign**

*Malik* is the Owner-King, the One whose dominion is not delegated, defended by election, or expiring with a dynasty. The Qur'an exalts this kingship above every claimant's:

> *"Exalted be God, the True Sovereign (al-Malik al-Ḥaqq). And hasten not with the Qur'an before its revelation is completed unto thee, and say: My Lord, increase me in knowledge."* (20:114)

> *"Say: O God, Master of the Kingdom! Thou givest the kingdom to whomsoever Thou wilt, and seizes the kingdom from whomsoever Thou wilt."* (3:26)

The political theology is immediate and permanent: every earthly sovereignty is a lease, and the Leaseholder's terms include justice, trusteeship, and audit. The Naḍīr affair this sūrah narrates was, at bottom, a foreclosure notice served on tenants who imagined their fortress-deed absolute. A believer who internalizes *al-Malik* holds two attitudes in one breath: reverence toward rightful authority as delegated trust, and refusal to tremble before any throne — the confession of God's kingship is the quiet abolition of every tyranny's ultimate claim.

**Al-Quddūs — The Holy**

From the root *q-d-s*, holiness as absolute apartness from every imperfection, every composite, every limitation. The angels, who of all creatures are nearest to purity, nonetheless define themselves entirely by His holiness: *"They said: Wilt Thou place therein one who will corrupt it and shed blood, while we glorify Thee with Thy praise and hallow Thee (wa-naḥnu nusabbiḥu bi-ḥamdika wa-nuqaddisu laka)?"* (2:30). And the Prophet ﷺ, closest of human beings to the Presence, taught the words of his own nearness: in his bowing and prostration he said:

> *"Perfect and Holy (subbūḥun quddūsun), Lord of the angels and the Spirit."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 487, from ʿĀʾishah)

Al-Quddūs is the Name that disciplines all theology: it forbids every image of God as a bigger version of us — older, stronger, more vindictive or more sentimental — and declares Him *mukhalafat lil-ḥawādith*: unlike anything originated. When the Qur'an calls the earth "the holy land" (*al-arḍ al-muqaddasah*, 5:21) or Jerusalem a sanctuary, the derivative sense carries: things become holy only by contact with His appointment, never in themselves.

**Al-Salām — Peace**

*Salām* is soundness, wholeness, freedom from defect — God as the Flawless, and thus the very source and definition of peace. The Qur'an names Him the destination of all reconciliation: *"And God summons to the Abode of Peace"* (10:25). On this Name the Prophet ﷺ issued one of his gentlest corrections of his Companions' liturgy:

> *"They used to say, 'Peace be upon God,' until the Prophet ﷺ said: 'God is al-Salām — rather say: All worship is for God, and the greetings and the blessing...'"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 563, from Ibn Masʿūd)

One does not greet God with peace as though He needed it; one receives peace *from* Him — which is why the greeting of the believers, *al-salāmu ʿalaykum*, is not mere courtesy but a theological act: invoking the Name of the Flawless upon His creatures. And the Qur'an makes *peace* the very dialect of Paradise: *"Their call therein is: Glory be to Thee, O God; and their greeting therein: Peace"* (10:10). A community that trades in this greeting daily is rehearsing, sentence by sentence, the language of its eternal home.

**Al-Muʾmin — The Faithful, the Giver of Security**

From the same root that gives the religion its name (*īmān*) and its adherents theirs (*muʾminūn*) — the root of trustworthiness and safety. Said of God, the scholars give it twin offices: He is the One who *affirms* — His testimony is truth, His promise is sure, *"Who is more true than God in statement?"* (4:87); and He is the One who *grants security* — the believer's amān, the heart's unshakable safety, flows from His name. The resonance between the divine and the human use of the word is the Name's built-in sermon: God is *al-Muʾmin*, and the believer, bearing the same word as his own title, is called to be its earthly echo — the one from whose tongue and hand others are safe, as the Prophet ﷺ defined: *"The Muslim is the one from whose tongue and hand the Muslims are safe"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr). A religion whose God's Name is Security cannot be, at its root, a religion of terrorism against the innocent; every act of wanton intimidation is a forgery of this Name.

**Al-Muhaymin — The Protector, the Overseer**

The rarest Name in the list, from a root connoting watchful guardianship — to observe, to safeguard, to stand guarantee. The Qur'an uses the word of itself, in God's hand: the Qur'an is *"muhaymin"* — guardian, standard, authenticator — over the earlier scriptures (5:48). Said of God, it is the Name of total oversight: nothing escapes, nothing is unguarded, nothing is finally unaccounted. The Prophet's Companion Abū Mūsā captured its comfort when he climbed the ranks of those near God: *"He is with you wherever you are"* (57:4). For the believer, *al-Muhaymin* answers the two primal fears at once: *Is anyone watching over me?* — yes; and *Will anyone answer for what was done to me?* — yes. For the wrongdoer, the same Name is the sentence that no deletion, encryption, or denial outruns: *"And God has enumerated it, while they forgot it; and God is Witness over all things"* (58:6).

**Al-ʿAzīz — The Mighty**

Might in its unattainable superlative: power so total that access to it is impossible except by His will. God guards this attribute with a declaration unique in its sternness, transmitted in the ḥadīth qudsī of the mantle:

> *"Pride (al-kibriyāʾ) is My cloak, and Might (al-ʿaẓamah) is My mantle; whoever contends with Me in either of them, I shall cast into the Fire."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2622, from Abū Hurayrah)

Note that the Qur'an has already shown, in this sūrah's immediate neighborhood, the forged version of the Name: the hypocrites of Madinah boasted *"'izzah belongs to us and our allies"* — and the Qur'an corrected the ledger: *"'izzah belongs to God, and to His Messenger, and to the believers — but the hypocrites know not"* (63:8). All true strength is on loan from *al-ʿAzīz*; the fortunes of the Naḍīr — whose wealth could not save their homes — are the sūrah's exhibit A.

**Al-Jabbār — The Compeller**

From *jabr*: to compel, and — in the same root's other branch — to set bones, to repair the broken. The Arabic *jabbār* for a human being is a tyrant, and the Qur'an uses it so: *"And when you strike, you strike as tyrants (jabārīn)"* (26:130); *"they sought victory, and every mighty, obstinate one (kullu jabbārin ʿanīd) was disappointed"* (14:15). Said of God, the compulsion is redemptive: the One whose will prevails over every will — nothing escapes His grasp — and, in the root's tender branch, the One who *mends* what is broken. The scholars of the Names love this double exposure: the same hand that is irresistible to the tyrant is splint-setting to the crushed. The Compeller of history is the bone-setter of hearts; the refugee from oppression prays to *al-Jabbār* in both senses at once — *overrule my oppressor, and repair what he broke in me.*

**Al-Mutakabbir — The Proud**

*Kibriyāʾ* — majestic pride — is, as the mantle-ḥadīth declared, God's own garment: fitting solely for the One who actually is the Maximum. On any other shoulder the garment is stolen property, and the Qur'an traces its most famous thefts: Iblīs *"refused and was arrogant (istakbara) and was of the disbelievers"* (2:34); Pharaoh proclaimed *"I am your lord, the most high"* (79:24). The Prophet ﷺ fixed the sentence for the crime in proportion to the theft: *"No one who has an atom's weight of pride in his heart will enter the Garden."* And when asked about the man who loves that his garment be beautiful, he ﷺ answered: *"God is Beautiful and loves beauty — but pride is disdain of the truth and contempt for people"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 91, from Ibn Masʿūd). The believer's dignity is commanded; the believer's arrogance is a counterfeit of a divine exclusive — *al-Mutakabbir* is the Name that forbids its own imitation.

**"Glory Be to Him Above the Partners They Ascribe": The Fence at the End**

The octave closes with *subḥānahu ʿammā yuṣifūn* — "Glory be to Him above what they describe" — the liturgical erasure of every rival and every mis-description, just as the verse opened with the erasure of every rival deity. The pairing teaches the permanent method of Islamic theology: *affirm only what God affirmed of Himself, negate everything creaturely, and stop where revelation stops.* God gave these eight Names; He did not give license to remodel them in humanity's image — softer, pettier, more partisan. And for the worshipper, the octave offers itself as a ladder of contemplation: *He is Sovereign* — so no tyrant is final; *Holy* — so nothing defiles Him with my failures; *Peace* — so my rest is a Name; *Faithful* — so His promise outlasts my inconstancy; *Protector* — so nothing happens unwatched; *Mighty* — so my weakness has a fortress; *Compeller* — so my brokenness has a bone-setter; *Proud* — so my humility has an address. Eight Names, one verse — and every one of them, as the next and final verse will show, still held in reserve a last movement: creation itself.

## Sūrah al-Ḥashr 59:24

> **He is God, the Creator, the Maker, the Fashioner; unto Him belong the Most Beautiful Names. Whatsoever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Him, and He is the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Creative Triad: Three Names for One Act, in Three Movements**

The sūrah's final verse completes the Name-inventory with a triad of creation: *al-Khāliq, al-Bāriʾ, al-Muṣawwir* — the Creator, the Maker, the Fashioner. The classical commentators, as the source records, correlate them with the stages through which a thing passes from nothing to form: *al-Khāliq* is the Quality by which God measures out and decrees a thing *before* it exists — creation in the workshop of decree; *al-Bāriʾ* is the bringing of the thing into being — the issuance of the decree into existence; *al-Muṣawwir* is the forming of what has been brought forth with its distinctive shape, features, and proportions. The Qur'an itself provides the commentary on the triad, in a passage whose vocabulary mirrors it almost clause for clause:

> *"Exalted be the Name of thy Lord, the Most High — Who creates (khalaqa), then makes just (fa-sawwā), and who measures (fa-qaddara), then guides (fa-hadā)."* (87:1–3)

> *"O man! What has deceived thee concerning thy Generous Lord, Who created thee (khalaqaka), then fashioned thee (fa-sawwāka), then proportioned thee (fa-ʿadalaka) — in whatever form He willed, He assembled thee (rakkabaka)?"* (82:6–8)

And the same source notes the tradition's second correlation: the three Names trace the three stages of the human being in the womb — decreed, brought forth, then formed — as the Qur'an unfolds its embryology: *"Then We made the drop a clinging clot, and We made the clot a lump of tissue, and We made the lump bones, and We clothed the bones in flesh — then We produced him as another creation. So blessed be God, the Best of creators (aḥsanu al-khāliqīn)"* (23:14). The detail of 82:6–8 deserves a pause the modern reader cannot refuse: the verse is addressed to *man* — the Fashioner's most elaborate work — and asks what has *deceived* him about his Maker. It is as if God anticipates that the more intricate the artifact, the more readily its maker is forgotten; the verse answers the forgetfulness by naming the manufacturing process, link by link, on the very body of the one who forgot.

**Al-Bāriʾ: The Maker Who Works Without Flaw**

The middle Name repays a word-study. From *b-r-ʾ* — to create, and also to be free, whole, without defect — comes *barīʾ*, unblemished, and *al-bariyyah*, the creation itself. To call God *al-Bāriʾ* is to say that His making has no draft, no revision, no callback: *"Who created the seven heavens in layers? Thou seest no discrepancy in the creation of the Compassionate. Cast thy gaze again: dost thou see any flaw? Then return the gaze twice more: thy gaze will return to thee humbled, weary"* (67:3–4). The unbelieving eye itself is commissioned as quality-control inspector — and finds nothing to report. And *al-Muṣawwir* crowns the inspection: the Fashioner gives each thing not merely existence but *identity* — the leopard its rosettes, the snowflake its six-fold lattice, every human face its unrepeatable geometry. The Qur'an stakes resurrection itself on the Fashioner's precision: *"Does man reckon We shall not assemble his bones? We are able to restore even his fingertips"* (75:3–4) — the most individually formed part of the body named as proof that the Former who did it once can do it again.

**"Unto Him Belong the Most Beautiful Names": The Charter of the Whole Science**

Then comes the summarizing declaration: *lahu al-asmāʾ al-ḥusnā* — "unto Him belong the Most Beautiful Names" — the foundation of the entire Muslim science of the divine Names, and a standing command in the Qur'an:

> *"And to God belong the Most Beautiful Names, so call upon Him by them; and leave those who deviate concerning His Names. They shall be recompensed for what they used to do."* (7:180)

> *"Say: Call upon God, or call upon the Compassionate — whichever you call, to Him belong the Most Beautiful Names."* (17:110; cf. 20:8)

The Prophet ﷺ sealed the doctrine with its most famous charter:

> *"God has ninety-nine Names — one hundred minus one — whoever enumerates them (aḥṣāhā) will enter the Garden."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2736; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2677, from Abū Hurayrah)

The scholars note three careful points around the ḥadīth. First, the ṣaḥīḥ texts give the number but not the list; the familiar enumeration of ninety-nine found in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī is transmitted with a chain the scholars have discussed and not universally authenticated — the Names are gathered from the Qur'an and Sunnah, not handed down as a closed inventory, and most authorities hold God has Names beyond the ninety-nine, *"many of which are not revealed to human beings,"* as the tradition states. Second, *iḥṣāʾ* — the entry-ticket verb — has been given by the commentators three successive depths: to *memorize* the Names; to *understand* their meanings; and to *live and call upon God* by them — the last being the summit, since 7:180's command is not "memorize" but "call upon Him by them." Third, the Prophet ﷺ himself modeled the calling:

> *"O God, I implore Thee by every Name that is Thine — by which Thou hast named Thyself, or revealed in Thy Book, or taught to any of Thy creation, or assigned to Thyself in Thy knowledge of the Unseen — that Thou make the Qur'an the springtime of my heart, and the light of my breast, and the departure of my sorrow and the release of my anxiety."* (transmitted in the Musnad of Aḥmad, from ʿĀʾishah)

The duʿāʾ is the believer's use-license for the whole passage: Names revealed precisely so that servants could bank on them.

**The Ring Closes Where the Sūrah Began**

Then, with three clauses, the sūrah performs one of the great structural feats in the Qur'an: *"Whatsoever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Him, and He is the Mighty, the Wise"* — the closing sentence is the opening sentence of verse 1, returned to almost word for word. Twenty-three verses of treachery, siege, law, wealth, hypocrisy, terror, audit, and Names have passed — and the universe's fundamental sound has not changed by a semitone: everything in the heavens and the earth is still glorifying, and God is still *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm*, the Mighty, the Wise — the exact pair with which the chapter opened. The ring-composition is the sermon: history churns; the praise beneath history does not. The believer who has walked from verse 1 to verse 24 has been taught, by architecture rather than argument, the lesson the whole sūrah exists to give — that the forts of the Naḍīr, the promises of Ibn Ubayy, the terror in men's breasts, the fayʾ in the treasury, and the rancor in one's own heart are all local weather inside a climate of unbroken praise. And there is a final precision in the closure: the sūrah's *last* Names are the Names of creation — Creator, Maker, Fashioner — followed instantly by the *first* verse's universal glorification. Creation and praise are revealed as a single motion: to be made by *al-Muṣawwir* is to be tuned, from the moment of one's forming, to say *subḥāna Allāh*. The birds' flight, the mountains' silence, the believer's tongue, and even the splitting of a mountain beneath the weight of the Qur'an (v. 21) are all the same sentence in different scripts.

**A Sūrah's-Worth of Theology in Three Verses — and the Practice That Keeps It**

The tradition has always read vv. 21–24 as a unit of devotion: the Fuḍayl ibn ʿIyāḍ report cited at verse 22 promises the "stamp of those who witness" to whoever recites the passage in the morning or evening and is taken that day or night; al-Ghazzālī includes it among the regular devotions of the Iḥyāʾ; and Muslim practice to this day weaves the passage into morning and evening adhkār. The reason the passage sustains such a practice is that it is not decoration but *curriculum*: three verses that teach, in sequence, the weight of the Word (v. 21), the God who knows all and mercies all (v. 22), the eight octaves of His majesty and perfection (v. 23), and His mastery of creation with the invitation to call on Him by every beautiful Name (v. 24) — a complete theological education short enough for a child to memorize and deep enough for a lifetime to fail to exhaust. And so the sūrah that began with a political crisis in an Arabian oasis ends by handing every believer, forever, the most practical theology ever compacted: *know these Names, call by these Names, and live inside the ring of praise.* The heavens and the earth have been reciting that ring since before the Naḍīr built their first wall; the reader who reaches this final verse is simply — at last — invited to join the recitation that never stopped.
