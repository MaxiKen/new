# Sūrah al-Mulk (Chapter 67) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter That Begins with a Blessing**

Sūrah al-Mulk opens with a single word unlike any other opening in the Qur'an: *tabāraka* — "Blessed is…," "Exalted and immensely blessed is…" Only one other sūrah in the Qur'an begins with this word, Sūrah al-Furqān:

> *"Blessed is He Who sent down the Criterion upon His servant, that he may be a warner to the worlds."* (Qur'an 25:1)

and the same word closes Sūrah al-Raḥmān:

> *"Blessed is the Name of thy Lord, Possessor of Majesty and Honor."* (Qur'an 55:78)

The Arabic root of *tabāraka* is *b-r-k*, the same root that produces *barakah* (blessing, increase, and abiding good), *barakah* being related to the word *birkah*, "a pool of water" — a place where water gathers and remains rather than running off. This etymology is not incidental poetry. Blessing in the Arabic imagination is *abiding increase that comes from God*: the pool that does not dry up, the camel that kneels (*baraka*) and stays, the good that multiplies without depletion. When the Qur'an says *tabāraka Allāh*, it is not offering a courtesy title. It is declaring that God is the source from whose Essence every increase, every permanence, and every fruitfulness flows, and that He Himself is exalted above all need, all diminution, and all comparison.

The form of the verb matters too. *Tabāraka* is not the simple past *bāraka* ("He blessed"); it is the reflexive-intensive form *tafāʿala*, a pattern in Arabic that conveys fullness, reciprocity, and exaltation together. Grammarians noted that this form is used for God in a way that no creature can share: only God is described as *tabāraka*, because only He possesses blessing by essence and not by gift. To say *tabāraka Allāh* is therefore to confess that blessing belongs to Him essentially, and everything blessing-like in creation is a loan from Him that He can withdraw at will — a truth that the sūrah's final verse will demonstrate with terrifying clarity when it imagines water, the very emblem of blessing, vanishing into the earth.

**Names of the Sūrah and What Each One Promises**

This chapter carries more names than almost any sūrah of its length, and each name encodes a promise that the Muslim community has lived by for fourteen centuries.

It is called **al-Mulk** ("Sovereignty," "Dominion," "the Kingdom"), after the first verse: *Blessed is He in Whose Hand lies sovereignty*. The name anchors the sūrah's central claim — that all dominion belongs to God — and, as will be seen, that claim is the load-bearing wall upon which every argument in the sūrah rests.

It is called **Tabārak**, after its first word, and **Tabāraka alladhī bi-yadihi al-mulk**, after its opening phrase. The Prophet ﷺ himself referred to it by this opening phrase, as in the report of Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ would not sleep until he had recited 'Alif Lām Mīm — The revelation' [Sūrah 32] and 'Blessed is He in Whose Hand is sovereignty' [Sūrah 67]."* (al-Tirmidhī 2892, who graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; also Aḥmad and al-Nasāʾī)

It is called **al-Māniʿah** ("The Protector," "The Shield, or Preventer") and **al-Munjiyah** ("The Savior"), after the words of the Prophet ﷺ reported from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"Verily there is a sūrah in the Book of God — thirty verses — which will intercede for a man until he is forgiven: 'Blessed is He in Whose Hand is sovereignty.'"* (Abū Dāwūd 1400; al-Tirmidhī 2891; Ibn Mājah 3786; Aḥmad — al-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan)

and from the Companion ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd, who said of it:

> *"Whoever recites 'Blessed is He in Whose Hand is sovereignty' every night, God will protect him thereby from the punishment of the grave. In the time of the Messenger of God ﷺ we used to call it al-māniʿah (the Preventer). And in the Book of God it is a sūrah which, whoever recites it every night, has done a great deal and done well."* (al-Nasāʾī in al-Sunan al-Kubrā 10547; al-Ḥākim, who declared it ṣaḥīḥ on the conditions of the two Shaykhs)

The most dramatic of these reports is the narration that explains *why* it was called the Preventer. Al-Tirmidhī records from Abū Hurayrah that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Verily a sūrah of the Qur'an — thirty verses — argued on behalf of a man until he was forgiven: 'Blessed is He in Whose Hand is sovereignty.'"* (al-Tirmidhī 2891)

and, in the longer version from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbbās preserved by al-Tirmidhī:

> *"One of the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ pitched his tent over a grave, not knowing that it was a grave. And lo, there was a man in it reciting Sūrat 'Blessed is He in Whose Hand is sovereignty' until he had completed it. He went to the Prophet ﷺ and said: 'O Messenger of God, I pitched my tent over a grave, not knowing it was a grave, and lo, a man was in it reciting Sūrat Tabārak until he completed it.' The Messenger of God ﷺ said: 'It is the Preventer. It is the Savior, which saves him from the punishment of the grave.'"* (al-Tirmidhī 2890; al-Ḥākim; al-Bayhaqī)

It is also called **al-Wāqiyah** ("The Shielding One") and, in some reports, **al-Mujādilah** ("The One Who Argues"), after that same quality of advocacy. And the Companion ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd is reported to have said of it:

> *"I would love for it to be in the heart of every person among my community."* (al-Ṭabarānī and al-Bayhaqī; the chain has been debated, but the sentiment reflects the universal practice of the early community)

A text that earns five names across the first three generations is a text that people did not merely read — they clung to it. And the reason is not superstition about syllables. As will become clear verse by verse, Sūrah al-Mulk is, in substance, a sustained meditation on death, the grave, the Hereafter, and the absolute sovereignty of God. It is the sūrah that meets the reader exactly where the reader is most vulnerable: at the threshold of the grave. Its protective power, in the logic of the tradition, is the power of a truth deeply absorbed — a heart that has genuinely internalized *"Blessed is He in Whose Hand is sovereignty"* is a heart already oriented toward the only Hand that can help when every other hand has released its grip.

**Place, Time, and Position in the Qur'an**

Sūrah al-Mulk is Makkan by the agreement of the commentators. While no single definitive report fixes its date, the overwhelming assessment places it in the middle Makkan period, that phase in which the Qur'an's argument with the idolaters of Makkah had moved past the initial announcement and settled into sustained rational demonstration — proof from creation, proof from providence, proof from the moral structure of human experience, and proof from the fate of earlier nations. In the standard chronological listings it is counted around the seventy-seventh sūrah revealed.

Its position in the written Qur'an is equally eloquent. Al-Mulk is the first sūrah of the twenty-ninth *juzʾ* (part), and that part is named after it: *Juzʾ Tabāraka*. A reader who opens the Qur'an at Juzʾ Tabāraka has arrived at the threshold of the Qur'an's closing movement, the short, dense, hammering Makkan sūrahs that deal almost exclusively with the Last Day, the resurrection of the body, the reckoning, and the two final destinations. Al-Mulk stands at the head of that movement as its thesis statement. Everything from here to Sūrah 114 elaborates what al-Mulk announces in thirty verses.

**The Architecture of the Argument**

The commentators have long noticed that this sūrah is built with unusual discipline. Its thirty verses divide into clearly marked movements, and an attentive reader can trace the argument from one movement to the next without a single break in logic.

**Verses 1–5 — The perfection of creation.** The sūrah opens by affirming God's absolute sovereignty and power, then immediately presents the two great signs by which that power is made visible to anyone who will look: the creation of death and life as a moral test, and the creation of seven heavens layered in flawless harmony, crowned by a sky adorned with lamps that also serve as missiles against the satans.

**Verses 6–12 — The two destinations.** From creation the argument moves to consequence. The disbelievers are shown in Hell, hearing its terrible sound, being questioned by its keepers, confessing their own guilt, and acknowledging in retrospect that they had *listened* but not *understood*. Against that scene the sūrah places a single, luminous verse: *"Truly for those who fear their Lord unseen there shall be forgiveness and a great reward"* (67:12). Thirty verses of terror and tenderness are compressed into that contrast.

**Verses 13–24 — God's knowledge, God's provision, and the folly of false security.** A cluster of arguments follows, most of them cast as rhetorical questions: Does the Creator not know His creation? Who else can be your army? Who else can provide for you if He withholds? Is the one who walks face-down better guided or the one who walks upright? Who gave you hearing, sight, and hearts? Who scattered you across the earth and who will gather you back? Over and over, the sūrah corners the listener into an answer that only arrogance can refuse.

**Verses 25–30 — The challenge about the Hour, and the final question about water.** The sūrah closes where the Makkan confrontation always closed: they ask *when* the promise will come; the Prophet is told to answer that knowledge belongs to God alone and that his task is only to warn clearly. Then the sūrah turns to the mockers themselves and asks the question that strips every illusion: if God should destroy the Messenger and his followers, or show them mercy, who will shield *you*? And finally, in the last verse, the sūrah poses the question of the water — the simplest, most ordinary necessity of life — and asks who could restore it if God let it sink away.

**Three Foundations of Faith in One Short Chapter**

Theologians have observed that this sūrah establishes, in compact form, the three divisions of *tawḥīd* that the later scholarly tradition would formalize. It establishes **tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah**, the oneness of God's lordship and creative action, in the opening verses and throughout the arguments from the heavens, the birds, and provision. It establishes **tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah**, the oneness of God's right to be worshipped, by demolishing every rival object of trust — armies, idols, wealth, and intercessors. And it establishes **tawḥīd al-asmāʾ wa al-ṣifāt**, the oneness of God's names and attributes, by the precise and reverent use of the Divine Names: *al-ʿAzīz* (the Mighty), *al-Ghafūr* (the Forgiving), *al-Raḥmān* (the Compassionate), *al-Laṭīf* (the Subtle), *al-Khabīr* (the Aware), *al-Baṣīr* (the All-Seeing), and, throughout, the attribution of *al-yad* (the Hand) to God in a manner befitting His majesty without likening it to anything created.

On that last point, the early community was explicit. When God says *bi-yadihi al-mulk* — "in His Hand is sovereignty" — the Salaf, including Mālik ibn Anas, al-Awzāʿī, and Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, ruled that the phrase is to be affirmed as it came, with a real Hand suited to God's majesty, while the mind is forbidden from imagining a limb or asking *how*. As Mālik's famous answer to the questioner about God's *istiwāʾ* (rising over the Throne) put it, in a statement that became the standard formula of the school:

> *"The rising is known, the modality is unknown, belief in it is obligatory, and asking about it is an innovation."* (reported by al-Bayhaqī in al-Asmāʾ wa al-Ṣifāt, and by al-Lālakāʾī in Sharḥ Uṣūl Iʿtiqād Ahl al-Sunnah)

The same principle governs this sūrah's language. Ibn Taymiyyah summarized the rule that governs every such text: the meaning is known in Arabic, the modality belongs to God alone, and the servant's duty is affirmation without *tamthīl* (likening God to creation) and transcendence without *taʿṭīl* (stripping God of His attributes). Sūrah al-Mulk, with its cluster of Divine Names and its single bodily attribution, is a compact test-case for that rule.

**The Four Great Questions This Sūrah Answers**

Seen as a whole, the sūrah answers four questions that every human being asks, whether or not he words them as the Qur'an words them.

*Who is really in charge?* The sūrah's answer: the One in Whose Hand is the *mulk* — the ownership, the management, and the disposal of everything. Not kings, not markets, not governments, not the stars, not fortune, and not the self.

*Why are we here?* The sūrah's answer: *li-yabluwakum ayyukum aḥsanu ʿamalan* — "that He may try you, which of you is best in deed" (67:2). Existence is a test, and the currency of the test is not achievement but the quality of the deed.

*What happens when we die?* The sūrah's answer: the grave is real, the questioning is real, the Fire is real, and the Garden is real. The Fire will roar, its keepers will question, and the inmates will confess. Those who feared God unseen will have forgiveness and a great reward.

*Are we safe?* This is the question the sūrah asks most often, in the most unsettling form: *a-amintum* — "Do you feel secure?" asked twice in verses 16 and 17. It is the question the sūrah puts to every comfortable, distracted, self-sufficient person who has quietly assumed that tomorrow will resemble today. The sūrah's whole purpose is to dismantle the illusion of security without God, because that illusion — and not ignorance — is what actually damns people.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the thirty verses as a complete, independent discourse. Each verse is presented with its Arabic text and transliteration, then opened through its vocabulary and grammar, its placement in the sūrah's argument, its cross-references elsewhere in the Qur'an, the reports concerning it from the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions, the interpretations of the classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī, and others — and finally its bearing on the questions a person actually carries: fear, provision, gratitude, arrogance, death, and trust.

The method of the sūrah itself will be the method of the commentary. Al-Mulk does not argue by assertion; it argues by asking the reader to look — *fa-arjiʿi al-baṣar*, "cast thy sight again" (67:3). It asks the reader to look at the sky, at the stars, at the birds, at the water beneath his feet, at his own faculty of hearing, and at his own helplessness. Every one of those objects is ordinary, and every one of them is a doorway. The sūrah's wager is that a heart willing to look honestly at ordinary things will, sooner or later, be unable to avoid the One who made them.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:1

> **Blessed is He in Whose Hand lies sovereignty, and He is Powerful over all things,**

**Arabic**

> *تَبَارَكَ الَّذِي بِيَدِهِ الْمُلْكُ وَهُوَ عَلَى كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ*

**Transliteration:** *Tabāraka alladhī bi-yadihi al-mulku wa-huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Blessed": A Word Reserved for God Alone**

The sūrah's first word, *tabāraka*, has no exact English equivalent, which is why translations render it variously as "Blessed is," "Supremely blessed is," "Exalted is," or "Full of blessing is." Its root is *b-r-k*, and every word that grows from that root revolves around a single idea: abundance that endures. From it comes *barakah*, the increase God places in a thing so that a little of it accomplishes much; from it comes *birkah*, the pool or basin where water collects and stays instead of running off; and from it comes the *burūk* of the camel, that deliberate kneeling by which the animal settles and remains. To call God *tabāraka* is thus to say that every lasting good in existence is a stream from a single inexhaustible spring, and that God Himself is high above need, above depletion, and above anything that could add to or subtract from Him.

Morphologically, the verb is on the pattern *tafāʿala*. Arabic uses this pattern for intensity and for mutual or repeated action — *taqātala*, "they fought one another"; *tabādala*, "they exchanged" — but when applied to God, as the grammarians noted, the pattern carries a sense of majesty and self-sufficiency rather than reciprocity. Al-Zajjāj and other philologists stressed that *tabāraka* is a word of glorification (*tamjīd*) that belongs to God exclusively: no creature is ever described by this verb, because creatures receive blessing while God is the source of it. Al-Ṭabarī records that Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as "He is blessed," and reports from Mujāhid and others that it means "He is exalted" (*taʿālā*), while al-Rāzī gathered the linguistic strands into a single formula: God is the One from Whom good proceeds abundantly, continuously, and without any diminution of Himself.

The practical upshot is worth pausing on. When a Muslim says *tabāraka Allāh*, he is not simply praising; he is locating the source. Any blessing he possesses — health, children, knowledge, provision, a home, a moment of clarity — is by definition a loan, held at the pleasure of the Owner. That is precisely why the sūrah can end, thirty verses later, with the image of water vanishing into the ground: the whole chapter is bracketed by the confession that blessing has a Source, and that the Source can take it back.

**"In Whose Hand": The Disposal, Not a Limb**

The second phrase, *bi-yadihi al-mulk* — "in Whose Hand is sovereignty" — introduces a question that Muslim theology answered with care. In Arabic, *al-yad* (the hand) is used constantly for possession, control, and disposal. A merchant says "the goods are in my hand" meaning "they are at my disposal," not that he is gripping them. The Qur'an uses the same idiom for created things without any bodily implication, as when it speaks of what "your right hands possess" (*mā malakat aymānukum*).

The early Muslim community (*al-salaf*) — among them Mālik ibn Anas, al-Awzāʿī, Sufyān al-Thawrī, and Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal — ruled that texts of this kind are to be passed on as they came, *bi-lā kayf*, "without asking how." Al-Awzāʿī, when asked about the divine attributes, replied with the sentence that became a standard:

> *"We say: He is over His Throne, and we believe in what has come concerning the attributes; and we do not say 'how.'"*

And the report from Mālik concerning God's *istiwāʾ* became the classical formulation of the position:

> *"The rising is known, the modality is unknown, belief in it is obligatory, and asking about it is an innovation."* (al-Bayhaqī, al-Asmāʾ wa al-Ṣifāt; al-Lālakāʾī, Sharḥ Uṣūl Iʿtiqād Ahl al-Sunnah)

Ibn Taymiyyah later gave this position its most rigorous statement: the meaning of the word is known in the Arabic language; the *modality* (*kayfiyyah*) belongs to God alone and cannot be reached by the mind; and the servant's duty is affirmation without *tamthīl* (likening God to His creation) and transcendence without *taʿṭīl* (denying or emptying the attribute). The two errors, he argued, are mirror images: the anthropomorphist drags God down to the level of the creature, and the denier drags the creature's limitation up to God.

Later theological schools differed on the method. The Ashʿarī and Māturīdī traditions, generally speaking, permitted *taʾwīl* — figurative interpretation — in texts of this kind, and al-Rāzī in his commentary on this very verse explains *yad* as *qudrah*, "power," which is an interpretation also mentioned and considered by al-Qurṭubī. Both approaches, however, agree on the substantive point the verse is making: God's sovereignty is not delegated, contested, shared, or revocable. He does not hold the kingdom as a trustee holds an estate on behalf of an owner; He holds it as the One Whose ownership is essential.

**Mulk, Malakūt, and the Root of Kingship**

The word *mulk* comes from the root *m-l-k*, whose verbal form means "to possess," "to control," "to rule," and "to reign." From that single root Arabic builds the entire vocabulary of authority: *malik*, "king"; *mālik*, "owner" or "master"; *milk*, "property"; *malakūt*, "dominion"; and *malāʾikah*, "angels" — so called because they execute the command of the One who owns everything. The linguistic fact itself is a small theological lesson: the angels are God's because they are His agents, and the *mālik* of a thing is the one who can dispose of it as he wills.

Commentators distinguished *mulk* from *malakūt*. The former tends to be used for visible, temporal dominion — the governance of this world — while the latter, as in:

> *"So glory be to Him in Whose Hand is the dominion (malakūt) of all things, and unto Whom you shall be returned."* (Qur'an 36:83)

refers especially to the unseen and ultimate dominion, the sovereignty over the hidden structures of reality. The two are not two kingdoms but one kingdom described from two angles: the visible administration and the invisible mastery.

The Qur'an states the same truth in the prayer of 3:26, which is perhaps the closest parallel to this verse in the whole Book:

> *"Say: 'O God, Master of sovereignty (mālika al-mulk)! Thou givest sovereignty to whom Thou wilt, and Thou wrestest sovereignty from whom Thou wilt; Thou exaltest whom Thou wilt, and Thou abasest whom Thou wilt. In Thy Hand is all good. Truly Thou art Powerful over all things.'"* (Qur'an 3:26)

Note the identical closing clause. In both verses, sovereignty and power are yoked together, because sovereignty without power would be a title, and power without sovereignty would be force. God has both, and both are His by essence.

**A Kingship That Exposes Every Rival Claim**

The Qur'an's insistence on God's *mulk* is not abstract metaphysics; it is a direct challenge to the human habit of treating created things as ultimate owners. The most famous prophetic commentary on this is the report from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"God will seize the earth and roll up the heavens in His Right Hand, then He will say: 'I am the King! Where are the kings of the earth?'"* (al-Bukhārī 7382; Muslim 2787)

And in another report the Prophet ﷺ identified the name that most insults that claim:

> *"The most wretched of men in the sight of God on the Day of Resurrection — and the most hateful to Him — is a man named Malik al-Amlāk, 'King of Kings.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6205; Muslim 2143)

The point is not that political authority is illegitimate. The Qur'an acknowledges rulers, commands justice from them, and orders obedience to them in what is right. The point is that all such authority is derivative, borrowed, and revocable — a trust (*amānah*) rather than a possession. A governor who forgets this becomes a tyrant; a wealthy person who forgets it becomes anxious; and a nation that forgets it begins to treat its own power as though it were self-generated, which is the precise moral error that the Qur'an calls *istighnāʾ*, the delusion of self-sufficiency.

**"He Is Powerful over All Things"**

The verse closes with *wa-huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr*. The word *qadīr* is the intensive form of *qādir* — not merely "able" but "perfectly, exhaustively able." And the object of that ability is *kulli shayʾ*, "every thing," without exception. Al-Rāzī draws out the logical consequence: if God is able over every thing, then He is able to resurrect the dead, to re-create decomposed bodies, to restore sight to the blind, and to bring water out of barren ground — all of which this very sūrah will argue for in the coming verses. The opening verse is thus the premise; the remaining twenty-nine are the conclusions drawn from it.

There is a subtlety in the phrase *ʿalā kulli shayʾ* that the commentators exploited. God's power extends over everything that is a *thing* (*shayʾ*). It does not extend to logical absurdities — to making a thing simultaneously exist and not exist, or to creating a second god — not because His power is limited, but because such "objects" are not things at all; they are contradictions dressed in words. Classical theology was careful on this point: inability with respect to the impossible is not a defect, because the impossible is nothing. This preserves the intelligibility of the verse: *qadīr ʿalā kulli shayʾ* means every actual or possible thing, which is to say, everything.

**Why Begin with Praise Rather Than Command**

It is worth noticing what kind of sentence this is. It is not an imperative. The sūrah does not open with "Worship your Lord" or "Fear God" or "Give charity." It opens with a doxology — a statement about Who God is — and only afterward will it derive from that statement every argument, every warning, and every demand it makes. This ordering is itself a principle of the Qur'an's pedagogy: theology precedes ethics, because ethics without theology eventually collapses into preference. A person who does not believe that sovereignty belongs to God will still behave well for a while — out of habit, reputation, or temperament — but the moment obedience becomes costly, the behavior has no root. The sūrah therefore spends its first verse establishing the root.

The same structure appears in the Prophet's own teaching. When he described the greatest verse of the Qur'an, he pointed to Āyat al-Kursī, which likewise opens with who God is before it asks anything of the servant. And when he described the greatest sūrah, he pointed to al-Fātiḥah, which likewise opens with praise and lordship before it reaches the request for guidance. The pedagogical sequence is identical in every case: know who God is, and the rest of the religion becomes reasonable.

**What This Verse Does to a Human Heart**

Consider the ordinary texture of a person's life. Much of the anxiety people carry is the anxiety of an owner: the fear of losing what they believe they hold, the resentment of what they believe they are owed, the exhaustion of maintaining what they believe is theirs by right. This verse dissolves that posture at its root. If the *mulk* is in God's Hand, then a person is not an owner but a steward, and a steward sleeps better than an owner. The steward's task is finite and clear — manage what has been entrusted well, and return it — whereas the owner's task is infinite and impossible: protect everything forever.

This is not a call to passivity. The Qur'an that says "in His Hand is the kingdom" is also the Qur'an that commands work, travel, and the seeking of provision, as this very sūrah will say in verse 15: *"so travel the open roads thereof and eat of His provision."* Sovereignty belongs to God; effort belongs to the servant. The error is neither passivity nor the illusion of control, but rather activity undertaken with an open hand — working as though everything depended on you, and trusting as though everything depended on God, which is the formula the tradition inherited from the Prophet's own instruction to tie the camel and then place one's trust in God.

And for the one who recites this verse each night before sleeping, as the Companions did with the whole sūrah, it performs one more service: it hands the day back. Whatever could not be finished, protected, or secured is, at the moment of recitation, explicitly returned to the only Hand that holds it. The last conscious thought before sleep becomes not the inventory of unfinished business but a confession of ownership — *in Whose Hand is sovereignty* — and the heart, having handed the kingdom back to its King, is free to rest.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:2

> **Who created death and life that He may try you as to which of you is most virtuous in deed, and He is the Mighty, the Forgiving,**

**Arabic**

> *الَّذِي خَلَقَ الْمَوْتَ وَالْحَيَاةَ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ أَيُّكُمْ أَحْسَنُ عَمَلًا ۚ وَهُوَ الْعَزِيزُ الْغَفُورُ*

**Transliteration:** *Alladhī khalaqa al-mawta wa-al-ḥayāta li-yabluwakum ayyukum aḥsanu ʿamalan, wa-huwa al-ʿazīzu al-ghafūr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shock of a Created Death**

Few verses in the Qur'an contain a more arresting idea in fewer words than *alladhī khalaqa al-mawta wa-al-ḥayāta* — "Who created death and life." The natural assumption is that life is made and death merely happens, that life is a thing and death is the absence of a thing, like a lamp that is lit and then simply goes out. The Qur'an overturns that assumption. Death here is the direct object of *khalaqa*: it is created, measured, designed, and willed.

This is not a stylistic flourish. The classical theologians drew from it a substantive doctrine. If death were pure nonexistence, it could not be "created," since creation is the giving of measure and existence to something. Al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr therefore argued that death is a real, created state — a condition into which the living person passes and in which he genuinely *is*, experiencing either the mercy or the punishment of the intermediate realm (*barzakh*). The strongest textual support for this reading comes from the Prophet's own description of the end of death itself:

> *"Death will be brought on the Day of Resurrection in the form of a white ram, and it will be made to stand between the Garden and the Fire. Then it will be said: 'O people of the Garden, do you recognize this?' And they will crane their necks and look, and say: 'Yes, it is death.' Then it will be said: 'O people of the Fire, do you recognize this?' And they will crane their necks and look, and say: 'Yes, it is death.' And it will be commanded, and it will be slaughtered. Then it will be said: 'O people of the Garden, eternity and no death! O people of the Fire, eternity and no death!'"* (al-Bukhārī 4730; Muslim 2849)

A thing that can be brought forth, seen, recognized, and slaughtered is a thing, not a negation. The hadith confirms what the verse's grammar implies.

This matters for the ordinary Muslim in the most practical way possible: the grave is not an empty waiting room but the first stage of the Hereafter, and the Prophet ﷺ sought refuge from its punishment in every prayer. He instructed his community to do the same, as ʿĀʾishah reported:

> *"O God, I seek refuge in You from the punishment of the grave, and from the trial of the Dajjāl, and from the trial of life and death."* (al-Bukhārī 1377; Muslim 588)

And he described the condition of the deceased in terms that make the verse concrete:

> *"Verily, when one of you dies, his place is shown to him morning and evening. If he is one of the people of the Garden, then he is of the people of the Garden; and if he is one of the people of the Fire, then he is of the people of the Fire. It is said to him: 'This is your place, until God resurrects you on the Day of Resurrection.'"* (al-Bukhārī 1379; Muslim 2866)

**Why Death Is Mentioned before Life**

The sequence in the verse — death first, then life — has provoked a rich discussion among the commentators, and the range of answers is itself instructive.

Al-Qurṭubī and others observed that death is mentioned first because it belongs pre-eminently to this world while life belongs to both: every soul in this world will die, whereas life continues into the Hereafter. A report transmitted by Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Suyūṭī puts it in the most vivid terms:

> *"God has humbled the son of Adam with death, and has made the world an abode of life, then an abode of death, and made the Hereafter an abode of recompense, then an abode of eternal abiding."*

Al-Rāzī gave the psychological reason: death is more awe-inspiring and more effective at restraining the soul, and the Qur'an frequently leads with whatever most powerfully seizes the listener's attention. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Bayḍāwī pointed out that nonexistence precedes existence in the order of our own experience, as the Qur'an says elsewhere:

> *"How can you disbelieve in God, seeing that you were dead and He gave you life?"* (Qur'an 2:28)

Still others noted that *al-mawt* is lighter on the tongue than *al-ḥayāh*, and that Arabic frequently places the shorter word first, after which the verse settles on life — the abiding state — as its final note. All of these readings are compatible, and the tradition has generally held them together rather than choosing between them. The combined effect is to make death the lens through which life is evaluated: you are living a life that ends, and the ending is what gives the living its weight.

**The Purpose: A Test, Not a Cruelty**

The particle *li-* in *li-yabluwakum* states purpose: He created death and life *in order to test you*. The root *b-l-w* carries the sense of testing through experience, and it is the same root that yields *balāʾ*, the tribulation that refines a person, and *balā*, the wearing-out of a garment through use. A test in this sense is not a quiz designed to produce information for the examiner; it is a process designed to produce a condition in the one tested. Gold is put into fire not so that the refiner may learn whether it is gold — he already knows — but so that the dross may be separated from the metal and the metal may actually become pure.

This is why the verse raises the question of justice and answers it before the objector can ask. A test that is unfair would be an indictment of the Tester. The Qur'an refuses that possibility at every level:

> *"God tasks no soul beyond its capacity."* (Qur'an 2:286)

> *"Truly God wrongs not the people in anything; rather it is the people who wrong themselves."* (Qur'an 10:44)

> *"And thy Lord wrongs no one."* (Qur'an 18:49)

And the Prophet ﷺ grounded the matter in the Divine Character itself, in a ḥadīth qudsī:

> *"O My servants, I have forbidden oppression for Myself, and I have made it forbidden among you; so do not oppress one another."* (Muslim 2577)

There is a second dimension to consider: trials are not only compatible with mercy, they are instruments of it. When the Prophet ﷺ was asked which people suffer the greatest trials, he answered:

> *"The prophets, then those most like them, then those most like them. A person is tested according to his religion. If he is firm in his religion, his trials are increased; and if there is weakness in his religion, he is tested accordingly."* (al-Tirmidhī 2398; Ibn Mājah 4023 — al-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

And on the expiatory value of ordinary suffering:

> *"No fatigue, disease, sorrow, sadness, hurt, or distress befalls a Muslim — even if it be the prick of a thorn — but that God expiates some of his sins thereby."* (al-Bukhārī 5641; Muslim 2573)

From the other direction, a life of uninterrupted ease is not automatically a sign of favor. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"If God wants good for someone, He afflicts him with trials."* (al-Bukhārī 5645)

The logic here is counterintuitive but coherent. A person who is never interrupted cannot easily discover what he truly loves, what he truly trusts, or what he is capable of enduring. Tribulation is the instrument by which those things become visible — to him as much as to anyone else.

**"Best in Deed," Not "Most in Deed"**

The phrase *ayyukum aḥsanu ʿamalan* is the pivot of the verse. It does not say *aktharu ʿamalan*, "most abundant in deed," nor *aʿzamu ʿamalan*, "greatest in deed." It says *aḥsan* — best, finest, most excellent. The commentators were unanimous in noticing this, and the implication is enormous: what is weighed is quality, not volume.

Quality in an Islamic deed has two conditions, and the tradition has expressed them with memorable precision. The first is *ikhlāṣ*, sincerity — that the deed be done for God alone. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Deeds are only in accord with intentions, and every person has only what he intended. So whoever emigrated for God and His Messenger, his emigration was for God and His Messenger; and whoever emigrated for a worldly gain or a woman he would marry, his emigration was for whatever he emigrated for."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907)

The second is *ittibāʿ*, conformity to the revealed way. A deed performed sincerely but in a manner God did not legislate is not a good deed; it is a well-intentioned invention. Al-Fuḍayl ibn ʿIyāḍ captured both conditions in a single sentence when he was asked about the words of God:

> *"Whosoever hopes to meet his Lord, let him work righteousness and not associate anyone in the worship of his Lord."* (Qur'an 18:110)

He said:

> *"A deed that is sincerely for God but not correct is not accepted; and a deed that is correct but not sincere is not accepted — until it is both sincere and correct. Sincere means that it is for God, and correct means that it is in accordance with the Sunnah."*

The early community understood this so concretely that they worried about it constantly. It is related that Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq wept when he reflected on the hidden motives of the heart. And ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib is reported to have said that a person should fear for his deeds more than he fears his sins — because a deed performed for the wrong reason is far more subtle a trap than a sin committed openly, since the sin at least announces itself.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ālūsī transmit that when the Prophet ﷺ recited this verse, he paused at *ayyukum aḥsanu ʿamalan* and defined it as:

> *"The most wary of what God has forbidden, and the quickest to obey God."*

That definition collapses the whole matter into two measurable movements of the soul: restraint where restraint is required, and speed where action is required. It is a remarkably practical definition of excellence, and it rules out the two common counterfeits — the scrupulous person who never does anything, and the energetic person who never stops for a boundary.

**The Two Names That Close the Verse**

*Wa-huwa al-ʿazīzu al-ghafūr* — "and He is the Mighty, the Forgiving." The pairing is deliberate and it appears elsewhere in the Qur'an, as in:

> *"He created the heavens and the earth in truth... He is the Mighty, the Forgiving."* (Qur'an 39:5; cf. 35:28)

The logic of the pairing is worth stating plainly. Power alone would be terrifying — unlimited capacity with no restraint is the definition of a tyrant. Forgiveness alone would be meaningless — mercy without the power to punish is merely weakness with a pleasant name. The two together describe the only kind of being who can be both feared and hoped in simultaneously, which is precisely the emotional posture the Qur'an asks of the believer. The Prophet ﷺ taught a supplication that rests on exactly this balance, asking God for both *khawf* and *rajāʾ*, and the tradition summed it up in the formula transmitted from ʿAlī:

> *"The believer worships God out of love, fear, and hope: love that draws him, fear that restrains him, and hope that keeps him moving."*

There is also a direct relationship between these two Names and the test just mentioned. The *Mighty* is the One who has the power to punish anyone, at any moment, with no one to prevent Him — so the fact that a person is still breathing, still being provided for, still being given opportunities, is itself an act of deferral. And the *Forgiving* is the One whose attribute it is to cover faults, so the failures accumulated in the course of the test are not automatically fatal. The verse therefore produces neither despair nor presumption: the Mighty is to be feared, the Forgiving is to be hoped in, and the test is the arena in which the two meet.

**The Parallel Passages and Their Emphasis**

The Qur'an states this purpose of creation more than once, using almost exactly the same phrasing, and each occurrence sharpens the point:

> *"Surely We made whatsoever is on the earth an adornment for it, that We may try them as to which of them is most virtuous in deed."* (Qur'an 18:7)

> *"And He it is Who created the heavens and the earth in six days, and His Throne was upon water, that He might try you as to which of you is most virtuous in deed."* (Qur'an 11:7)

Note that in 11:7 the throne was upon water — creation begins with water, and this sūrah will end with water (verse 30). The frame within which the test takes place is, at both ends, the element without which no life exists. The test is conducted inside a world that is entirely dependent on a gift that could be withdrawn at any moment.

**What This Verse Does to the Way a Person Lives**

Taken seriously, this verse reorganizes a life around three questions, and it is worth asking all three honestly.

*What is being tested?* Not intelligence, not productivity, not social approval, and not wealth. The currency is the quality of the deed, and the quality of a deed is measured by its sincerity and its conformity to revelation. This means that the smallest act — a kind word, an honest invoice, a patient silence, a concealed charity, a prayer performed on time when no one is watching — can outweigh, in the scale named by this verse, a lifetime of visible achievement.

*Who is doing the testing?* The One who is *al-ʿAzīz* and *al-Ghafūr*. He is not a hostile examiner hoping for failure. The tradition is emphatic that the test is designed to be passable:

> *"God tasks no soul beyond its capacity."* (Qur'an 2:286)

*When does the test end?* At death — and death is created, appointed, and known. This is why the Prophet ﷺ counseled frequent remembrance of it:

> *"Remember often the destroyer of pleasures — death."* (al-Tirmidhī 2307; al-Nasāʾī; Ibn Mājah — graded ḥasan)

This is not morbidity; it is triage. A person who genuinely holds death in view stops spending his finite hours on grievances he will not remember in a year, on performances for audiences who will not attend his funeral, and on possessions he will not take with him. He spends them instead on the things that survive: relations repaired, prayers performed, knowledge taught, children raised, wrongs forgiven, and debts — moral and material — settled.

There is a final paradox in the verse worth naming. Death, which humans treat as the ultimate enemy, is here presented as part of the divine gift. Without death, there would be no urgency, no accountability, and no justice; the oppressor and the oppressed would simply continue. Death is what makes the test finite and the reckoning certain. And life, which humans treat as the ultimate good, is here presented as the arena of risk — a limited-time opportunity in which every hour spent is an hour that cannot be recovered. The verse holds both together, and then places them in the Hands of the Mighty, the Forgiving.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:3

> **Who created seven heavens one upon another; no disproportion dost thou see in the Merciful's creation. Cast thy sight again; dost thou see any flaw?**

**Arabic**

> *الَّذِي خَلَقَ سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ طِبَاقًا ۖ مَا تَرَىٰ فِي خَلْقِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ مِنْ تَفَاوُتٍ ۖ فَارْجِعِ الْبَصَرَ هَلْ تَرَىٰ مِنْ فُطُورٍ*

**Transliteration:** *Alladhī khalaqa sabʿa samāwātin ṭibāqan; mā tarā fī khalqi al-Raḥmāni min tafāwutin; fa-arjiʿi al-baṣara hal tarā min fuṭūr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Seven Heavens, Layer upon Layer"**

The word *samāwāt* is the plural of *samāʾ*, which in classical Arabic denotes everything that is above you and overarches you — the sky, the canopy, the ceiling. It comes from a root associated with height and elevation. The Qur'an uses the word both for the visible sky and for the vast created orders beyond it, which is why the commentators have long allowed a range of meaning here: some take it as the physical heavens, others as the whole of the created cosmos, and some — particularly among later metaphysical commentators such as those in the school of Ibn ʿArabī, whom al-Ālūsī discusses and often qualifies — read it as referring to grades or levels of existence, a hierarchical cosmos in which reality is arranged in ascending degrees from the material to the purely spiritual.

The qualifier *sabʿ* (seven) is stated as a matter of revealed fact, not as a scientific claim to be tested against a particular era's astronomy. The Qur'an affirms it repeatedly and without hedging:

> *"He it is Who created for you all that is on the earth; then He turned to the heaven and made them seven heavens; and He knows all things."* (Qur'an 2:29)

> *"God it is Who created seven heavens, and of the earth the like of them. The command descends between them, that you may know that God is Powerful over all things, and that God encompasses all things in knowledge."* (Qur'an 65:12)

> *"Do you not see how God created seven heavens one upon another (ṭibāqan), and made the moon a light therein and made the sun a lamp?"* (Qur'an 71:15–16)

> *"And We built above you seven strong [heavens], and made a blazing lamp."* (Qur'an 78:12–13)

> *"And We created above you seven paths (ṭarāʾiq), and We were not unmindful of creation."* (Qur'an 23:17)

The classical commentators, from al-Ṭabarī through Ibn Kathīr to al-Qurṭubī, took the seven as seven real heavens, one above another, with intervals between them that no human being has plumbed, and they explicitly rested their knowledge of the number on revelation rather than on calculation. Ibn Kathīr cites the well-known report of the Prophet's Night Journey in support of the literal reading, and al-Qurṭubī marshals the hadith of Abū Hurayrah transmitted by al-Bukhārī describing the heavens in terms of their vastness:

> *"The seven heavens compared to the Kursī (Footstool) are like seven dirhams thrown into a shield; and the Kursī compared to the Throne is like a ring thrown into a desert."* (reported by al-Bayhaqī in al-Asmāʾ wa al-Ṣifāt from Abū Dharr; the chain has been debated, though its meaning is supported by other reports)

What is worth emphasizing is the methodology the verse models. It does not ask the listener to accept a cosmology because a book said so and then stop thinking; it asks the listener to *look*. The number is revealed; the investigation is commanded. Revelation supplies the frame, and observation supplies the confirmation.

**The Word Ṭibāq: Harmony as Much as Stacking**

The adjective *ṭibāqan* is usually rendered "one upon another" or "in layers." Its root, *ṭ-b-q*, carries the idea of one thing matching, covering, or corresponding exactly to another. From it come *muṭābaqah* (correspondence, agreement), *ṭabaqah* (a level or stratum), *aṭbaqa* (to close a lid onto its container), and the idiom *ṭabaqa al-amr* (he got the matter exactly right). A *ṭibāq* is thus not merely a stack but a *fitting* — each layer corresponding to what is above and below it.

This is why several commentators, among them al-Rāzī and al-Bayḍāwī, read the word as carrying both senses at once: the heavens are layered, and they are *harmonized*. Each level fits the next with the exactness of a fitted lid. That double sense feeds directly into the next clause: a structure whose parts correspond perfectly will, by definition, show no disproportion.

**Tafāwut: The Absence of Any Incongruity**

*Mā tarā fī khalqi al-Raḥmāni min tafāwutin* — "no disproportion dost thou see in the creation of the Merciful." The word *tafāwut* comes from *f-w-t*, the root of *fawt*, "to slip past, to be missed, to fall short." The derived form *tafāwut* describes two things that fail to meet or fit: incongruity, asymmetry, disproportion, a gap where there should be a join. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī glossed it precisely in this way — a defect such that one part of a thing does not connect properly with the rest — and some commentators equated it with the *furūj* (rifts, gaps) of another verse:

> *"Have they not looked at the sky above them — how We built it and adorned it, and how it has no rifts (furūj)?"* (Qur'an 50:6)

The denial is absolute: *mā... min*, a doubled negation in Arabic that excludes the category altogether, not merely its large instances. Not "little disproportion" but "no disproportion of any kind."

Why should that be so? The Qur'an answers the question elsewhere by grounding creation in truth rather than in play:

> *"And We did not create the heaven and the earth and whatsoever is between them in play. We did not create them, save in truth. But most of them know not."* (Qur'an 44:38–39)

The same declaration recurs in 6:73, 10:5, 14:19, 15:85, 16:3, 29:44, 30:8, 39:5, 45:22, and 46:3. A thing made in truth is a coherent thing; play and caprice produce the arbitrary, the patched, and the incongruous. The absence of disproportion in the cosmos is therefore, in the Qur'an's own logic, evidence for the seriousness of its Author — and, by extension, evidence that existence has a purpose and an ending, which is precisely the conclusion the sūrah is driving toward.

**Why "the Merciful's Creation" and Not "God's Creation"**

The Qur'an is precise in its choice of Divine Names, and the use of *al-Raḥmān* here rather than *Allāh* or *al-Khāliq* is deliberate. Al-Rāzī notes that the Name of mercy is used because the whole order of the cosmos is an act of mercy before it is anything else: the arrangement, the proportion, the fine balance that makes life possible, and the intelligibility that makes knowledge possible are all gifts. Nothing in creation is a mercy to God — He is self-sufficient — so every fitted thing in it is fitted for the benefit of the creatures.

This reframes how a person looks at the world. The same sunrise that an indifferent observer sees as a mechanical event, the verse trains the believer to see as an act of the Merciful. The Name is doing theological work: it tells the reader in advance what he is looking at, so that when he looks and finds harmony, he does not conclude merely "nature is elegant" but "the Merciful has arranged this."

**Fuṭūr: Cracks, and the Echo of Fiṭrah**

*Hal tarā min fuṭūr* — "dost thou see any flaw?" The word *fuṭūr* is the plural of *faṭr*, a split, crack, or rupture, from the root *f-ṭ-r*, which means to split or cleave. The same root yields *fitrah*, the original human constitution upon which God created mankind, and *fiṭr*, the breaking of the fast, and *fāṭir*, "the Originator," one of God's Names — so called, the philologists explain, because He split open nonexistence and brought creation out of it.

The resonance is significant. A crack in the sky would mean the structure is failing; a crack in the *fitrah* would mean the human constitution is failing. The verse's question therefore applies at two levels at once: look outward and see whether there is any fissure in the fabric of the cosmos; and look inward and see whether there is any fissure in the design of your own nature. The Prophet ﷺ described the human being's original orientation in terms of that intact design:

> *"Every child is born upon the fiṭrah; it is his parents who make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Zoroastrian."* (al-Bukhārī 1358; Muslim 2658)

What cracks a person is not his created nature but what is done to it afterward.

**"Cast Thy Sight Again": The Qur'an's Empirical Imperative**

The imperative *fa-arjiʿi al-baṣara* — "so cast thy sight again" or "return the gaze" — is one of the most consequential commands in the Qur'an for how a believer is meant to think. It is addressed, grammatically, to the Prophet ﷺ, but the address extends to every reader. It commands *repetition*. One look is not enough; look, and then look again.

This is a methodological statement, and its implications are worth spelling out. First, it presumes that the truth about the world is *public* — anyone with working eyes can check. The Qur'an does not here offer a private mystical intuition available only to the initiated; it offers a claim about observable reality and invites falsification. Second, it presumes that honest investigation will vindicate the claim. The command to look again is not a dare that might backfire; it is a promise. Third, it treats observation as a religious act. Looking at the sky is not a secular pastime that interrupts devotion; in the Qur'anic economy it *is* devotion, which is why the Qur'an condemns those who pass by the signs of God *"while they are heedless of them"* (7:179) and praises *"those who remember God standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth"* (3:191).

The consequences of that command, historically, are visible. The civilization that took this verse seriously produced the observational astronomy of al-Battānī, al-Bīrūnī, Ibn al-Haytham's optics — the very science of *al-baṣar*, sight, including his demonstration that vision occurs in the brain rather than by rays emitted from the eye — and the great observatories of Marāghah and Samarqand. These men were not inventing a practice foreign to their religion; they were obeying an instruction in their scripture.

**What Looking Repeatedly Has Shown**

Every generation that has obeyed the command has found the same thing the verse predicts: the deeper the look, the more coherence appears. The unaided eye sees a sky of points of light; the telescope reveals structured galaxies, spiral arms, and clusters bound by precise gravitational relationships; instruments that detect radiation beyond the visible spectrum reveal a background uniformity across the whole observable universe; and study of the constants of physics has shown that the difference between a universe that can host life and one that cannot is measured in vanishingly small fractions. At the other end of scale, the microscope and the particle accelerator reveal a matching order: atoms, cells, molecular machinery, and laws that hold identically everywhere they have been tested.

None of this "proves" the verse in a mathematical sense, and the careful commentator will not claim that it does — science describes, and its models are revised. But the *direction* of the evidence has consistently favored the verse's claim rather than its denial: the search for disproportion has repeatedly turned up deeper proportion instead. That is precisely what verse 4 will say the searching eye discovers about itself.

**The Connection to the Qur'an's Own Consistency**

The commentators also noted an important transfer of this principle from the cosmos to the scripture. If the physical creation has no incongruity, the revelation that comes from the same Author should have none either, and the Qur'an makes that parallel claim explicitly:

> *"Do they not ponder the Qur'an? Had it been from other than God, they would have found in it much discrepancy (ikhtilāfan kathīran)."* (Qur'an 4:82)

The logic is symmetrical and it cuts both ways. A book that claims to be from the Creator of a flawless cosmos must itself be flawless; and a cosmos made by the Author of a flawless book must itself be coherent. The believer does not have to choose between trusting the Book and trusting the world, because both come from One Hand, and this sūrah's first verse established that all sovereignty is in that Hand.

**The Reader's Own Task**

The verse hands the reader a task that cannot be delegated. It does not say "ask the specialists whether there is a flaw." It says *arjiʿ al-baṣar* — return *your own* gaze. Every person can perform some version of this: watching the moon move through its phases, noticing the exactness with which a seed becomes a particular tree and not another, observing the way a wound heals, or simply considering the fact that consciousness exists at all in a universe of matter. The purpose of such looking is not to accumulate facts but to arrive at the recognition the Qur'an names elsewhere:

> *"Our Lord, Thou hast not created this in vain. Glory be to Thee! So guard us against the punishment of the Fire."* (Qur'an 3:191)

That is the intended end of the exercise. The gaze goes out into the sky and comes back carrying adoration.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:4

> **Then cast thy sight twice again; thy sight will return to thee humbled and wearied.**

**Arabic**

> *ثُمَّ ارْجِعِ الْبَصَرَ كَرَّتَيْنِ يَنْقَلِبْ إِلَيْكَ الْبَصَرُ خَاسِئًا وَهُوَ حَسِيرٌ*

**Transliteration:** *Thumma arjiʿi al-baṣara karratayni yanqalib ilayka al-baṣaru khāsiʾan wa-huwa ḥasīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Command to Look**

The previous verse commanded one return of the gaze; this verse commands another. *Thumma arjiʿi al-baṣara karratayni* — "then cast thy sight twice again." The word *karrah* means a turn, a return, a repeated onslaught; it is the same word used for a cavalry charge in which the riders wheel about and come again. Al-Ṭabarī records that some commentators understood *karratayn* literally as two further looks, while others — including a view he attributes to the early Basran tradition — read the dual form as indicating repetition generally: look again and again, as often as you like.

Either way, the rhetorical effect is the same, and it is cumulative. The first look is an invitation. The second is a challenge. By the time the verse reaches its conclusion, the reader has been sent out three times to hunt for a defect, and the Qur'an is confident enough about the result to state it in advance as a prophecy: you will fail, and your failure will be the proof.

This is an extraordinary rhetorical posture. A human author making a claim about the perfection of his work does not usually invite his readers to search for mistakes and then announce in advance that the search will humiliate them. The confidence here is the confidence of a claim that expects to survive examination. Al-Rāzī remarks on this: the Qur'an could simply have asserted that there is no defect, but instead it commands the search and predicts its exhaustion, because a proof arrived at by the reader's own effort settles in the heart in a way that an assertion never can.

**Karratayn and the Discipline of Verification**

There is a lesson about method embedded in the doubling. In the classical Islamic epistemology that grew out of such verses, a single observation is vulnerable to error — the eye can deceive, the instrument can be miscalibrated, the witness can be mistaken. What produces confidence is *takrār*, repetition, and *tatābuʿ*, converging independent confirmation. The same instinct produced the science of hadith, where a report rises to certainty only when multiple independent chains corroborate it, and it produced the experimental method in the natural sciences as practiced by Ibn al-Haytham, whose *Book of Optics* insisted that a claim about vision must be tested by repeated, controlled observation before it is accepted.

The verse thus models a virtue that modern life badly needs. The typical contemporary encounter with a claim is a single glance at a headline, followed by outrage or acceptance. The Qur'an's method is the opposite: return, look again, then look again still, and only then render a verdict. The person who is willing to look twice at the world — and twice at an argument, and twice at his own motives — is doing something the verse explicitly blesses.

**Khāsiʾan: The Return of a Repelled Envoy**

The verb *yanqalib* is striking: "thy sight will *turn back*" — the same word used for a person turning back from a journey, or for a thing being overturned. The gaze is personified as an emissary dispatched on a mission, who now returns to his sender.

And he returns *khāsiʾan*. The root *kh-s-ʾ* is used in Arabic especially of a dog driven away, repelled, and humiliated. The Qur'an uses the very same root twice more, and both uses illuminate this one:

> *"But he clung to the earth and followed his own desires; so his likeness is as the likeness of a dog: if you attack him he pants with his tongue out, and if you leave him alone he still pants with his tongue out... " (Qur'an 7:176)*

> *"He [God] will say: 'Be driven into it in disgrace (ikhsaʾū), and do not speak to Me.'"* (Qur'an 23:108)

The second of these is the more chilling: the identical word describes the final rebuke to the damned. The lexical link is deliberate and it carries a warning. A gaze that sets out to find fault with the work of God returns in the same condition as a creature repelled and put in its place. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both note that the word conveys humiliation and debasement: the eye comes back *dhālīlan*, abased, because it has attempted something beyond its station and been defeated by the thing it presumed to judge.

Notice that this is not a humiliation imposed arbitrarily. It is the natural result of the enterprise. A person who devotes himself to finding a crack in a structure that has none will exhaust himself and find nothing. The disgrace is intrinsic to the failure, not a punishment added to it.

**Ḥasīr: Exhausted, and Imprisoned**

The second adjective, *ḥasīr*, is even more layered. Its root, *ḥ-ṣ-r*, gives the sense of enclosure, confinement, and siege — *ḥiṣār* is a blockade, *maḥṣūr* is one who is hemmed in, *ḥaṣīr* is a woven mat (so called because its strands are bound tightly together). From the same semantic field, and by the natural extension of being hemmed in and unable to continue, comes the sense of exhaustion: the one who is *ḥasīr* is spent, at the end of his resources, unable to go further.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits both senses from the early authorities. Some said *ḥasīr* means *muʿyin*, "wearied"; others said it means *maḥṣūr*, "confined," as though the eye has been shut up and cannot range any further; and some combined them: the eye returns *khāsiʾan ḥasīran*, driven back and worn out, unable to penetrate and unable to proceed. The two meanings reinforce each other beautifully. The gaze that went out freely to roam the horizons comes back *confined to its socket* — it can go no farther, see no more, and accomplish nothing more. It has been reduced to what it actually is: an organ with limits.

**Why the Failure Is Good News**

It would be easy to read this verse as merely a taunt, but its function in the sūrah is constructive. The exhaustion of the eye is not the defeat of the seeker; it is the victory of the search.

Consider what actually happens to a person who takes up this investigation sincerely. He looks at the sky and finds order. He looks again, with better instruments and more knowledge, and finds more order. He looks again and finds that the order extends in directions he did not suspect. At a certain point the reasonable conclusion is not "I have not found the flaw yet" but "there is no flaw, and the coherence is a property of the thing and not of my search." The eye gives up; the intellect draws its conclusion; and the heart moves from investigation to assent.

That is the transition the sūrah is engineering. The whole purpose of verses 3 and 4 is to move the reader from "look at this" to "therefore trust the One who made it." The exhausted eye is the price of admission to certainty. Al-Rāzī makes the point directly: the failure of the eye to find defect is the strongest possible argument for the perfection of the Creator, and the humiliation of the creature before that perfection is exactly the right response — not a defect in the argument but its intended conclusion.

There is a deep psychological truth here too. Human beings are made to seek coherence, and the satisfaction of finding it is one of the deepest pleasures available to the mind. The person who hunts for incoherence instead — in the world, in a text, in another person, in himself — experiences the exhaustion this verse describes. He is always scanning, never satisfied, always finding the next small anomaly to feed his suspicion. The verse diagnoses that condition precisely: *khāsiʾan wa-huwa ḥasīr*. Driven off, and worn out.

**The Modern Face of the Wearied Eye**

The diagnosis translates into contemporary life with uncomfortable accuracy. Consider the person who spends hours each day scrolling through content designed to provoke him — the feed that rewards outrage, the comment sections, the endless supply of examples of human badness and institutional failure. That person's gaze is, in the verse's language, being sent out over and over to hunt for defect in creation. And what does it return with? Not understanding, not peace, not the ability to act — it returns *khāsiʾan wa-huwa ḥasīr*: agitated, humiliated by comparison, and exhausted.

The same is true of the professional cynic. There is a kind of intelligence that consists entirely of finding the flaw — in every argument, every institution, every act of kindness (which must be self-interested), every beauty (which must be manipulative). Such a person is often clever, and cleverness of this kind is frequently mistaken for wisdom. But the verse identifies its end point: an eye that can only see defect eventually sees nothing else, and its owner ends up confined within the narrowness of his own suspicion, unable to receive anything.

The antidote the verse prescribes is not naivety. It is *repetition with the right aim*. Look again, but look for what is there, not for what you hope to find. The Qur'an's confidence is that honest repetition will converge on proportion, not on defect.

**The Theological Edge: No Crack in the Moral Order Either**

One further implication deserves attention, because the sūrah depends on it. If the physical creation is free of disproportion, then the moral and eschatological order announced by the same Creator is also free of disproportion — a claim that carries real weight against the sūrah's original audience, who objected specifically to the resurrection.

The Makkan disbelievers did not object to God as Creator; as the Qur'an notes elsewhere, they readily conceded that God made the heavens and the earth. What they objected to was that He would reassemble decomposed bones and hold them accountable. The sūrah's argument against them is built on the premise this verse establishes: the same power that made a cosmos without a crack can certainly mend a bone; the same wisdom that fitted the layers of the heavens together did not construct a moral order that dead-ends in injustice. To accept the perfection of creation while denying the perfection of the reckoning is itself a *tafāwut* — an incongruity — and it is an incongruity in the objector's thinking, not in God's world.

**A Worshipper's Reading**

Finally, the verse can be read in the key of worship, and the tradition has often read it that way. The return of the gaze is, in one sense, the return of the servant. Sent out into the world to work, to study, to earn, to build, the human being ranges widely and imagines himself self-directing. Then something stops him — failure, illness, mortality, the sheer limit of his own capacity — and he returns. If he returns well, he returns *khāsiʾan wa-huwa ḥasīr*: stripped of presumption and spent of self-reliance. That is not the worst state in which to come home.

> *"O mankind! You are the poor, utterly in need of God; and God — He is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy."* (Qur'an 35:15)

The eye that comes back humbled has learned what the whole sūrah is trying to teach, and what its first verse announced: sovereignty is not ours, and the sooner the gaze returns to admit it, the sooner the heart can rest.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:5

> **Truly We have adorned the lowest heaven with lamps and made them missiles against the satans; and We have prepared for them the punishment of the Blaze.**

**Arabic**

> *وَلَقَدْ زَيَّنَّا السَّمَاءَ الدُّنْيَا بِمَصَابِيحَ وَجَعَلْنَاهَا رُجُومًا لِلشَّيَاطِينِ ۖ وَأَعْتَدْنَا لَهُمْ عَذَابَ السَّعِيرِ*

**Transliteration:** *Wa-la-qad zayyannā al-samāʾa al-dunyā bi-maṣābīḥa wa-jaʿalnāhā rujūman li-al-shayāṭīni wa-aʿtadnā lahum ʿadhāba al-saʿīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Structure to Ornament: Beauty as Evidence**

The first two verses of this sūrah described the architecture of the cosmos — seven heavens, layered, proportioned, flawless. This verse adds a new dimension: beauty. *Zayyannā*, "We adorned," comes from the root *z-y-n*, from which comes *zīnah*, ornament. The universe is not merely functional; it is decorated. And the decoration is placed in *al-samāʾ al-dunyā*, "the lowest heaven" — the nearest heaven, the one that human eyes can actually see.

The word *dunyā* is the feminine form of *adnā*, "nearest," and it is the very word used for this present world, *al-dunyā*, the near life. The linguistic connection is instructive: the nearest heaven is the sky of the near world, the ceiling of the only cosmic neighborhood we can observe directly. God placed the adornment where it can be seen.

The Qur'an makes the same point elsewhere with almost identical wording:

> *"Truly We adorned the lowest heaven with an adornment — the stars."* (Qur'an 37:6)

> *"And We adorned the lowest heaven with lamps (maṣābīḥ), and [made them] a guard. That is the ordaining of the Mighty, the Knowing."* (Qur'an 41:12)

The word *maṣābīḥ* is the plural of *miṣbāḥ*, a lamp — a device that holds a flame and gives light. It is worth noting the Qur'an's precision in its celestial vocabulary. The sun is called a *sirāj*, a blazing lamp:

> *"And We built above you seven strong [heavens], and made a blazing lamp (sirājan wahhājan)."* (Qur'an 78:13)

The moon is called *nūr*, a light — reflected, borrowed, and measured into phases:

> *"Blessed is He Who placed constellations in the heaven and placed therein a lamp (sirājan) and a light-giving moon (qamaran munīran)."* (Qur'an 25:61)

And the stars are *maṣābīḥ*, lamps distributed across the ceiling. Nothing in the description confuses the three categories, and nothing in it attributes divinity, influence over human destiny, or independent power to any of them — a point to which we will return.

**Qatādah's Three Purposes, and the Warning Inside Them**

The most frequently cited statement on this verse in the classical commentaries comes from Qatādah ibn Diʿāmah (d. 117/735), one of the foremost scholars of the generation after the Companions, whose report is transmitted by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr:

> *"God created these stars for three purposes: as an adornment for the heaven, as missiles against the satans, and as signs by which to travel. Whoever claims anything more than that has erred, and lost his portion, and burdened himself with that of which he has no knowledge."*

The first two purposes are named in this verse. The third is named elsewhere in the Qur'an:

> *"And He it is Who has set for you the stars, that you may guide yourselves by them through the darkness of the land and the sea. We have detailed the signs for a people who know."* (Qur'an 6:97)

> *"And landmarks — and by the stars they are guided."* (Qur'an 16:16)

The closing clause of Qatādah's report is the important part, and it deserves the modern reader's attention. In a world where astrology is a multibillion-dollar industry and where horoscope columns appear in mainstream newspapers, the Qur'an's position is unambiguous: the stars are beautiful, they are protective, and they are useful for navigation and timekeeping — and they have no power whatsoever over human character, destiny, or decision. The Prophet ﷺ stated the consequence of believing otherwise:

> *"Whoever acquires a branch of the knowledge of the stars has acquired a branch of magic; the more he increases in it, the more he increases in magic."* (Abū Dāwūd 3905; Ibn Mājah 3726 — graded ḥasan)

And in a related narration:

> *"Whoever goes to a fortune-teller (kāhin) or a diviner (ʿarrāf) and believes what he says has disbelieved in what was revealed to Muḥammad."* (Aḥmad 9532; Abū Dāwūd 3904; al-Tirmidhī 135; Ibn Mājah 639 — graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī)

The logic behind this severity is not arbitrary. Astrology transfers to created things the knowledge and the power that belong to the Creator alone. It is precisely the denial of *mulk*, of the sovereignty this sūrah began by asserting.

**Rujūm: Missiles Against the Satans**

The second purpose of the stars is given in *wa-jaʿalnāhā rujūman li-al-shayāṭīn* — "and We made them missiles against the satans." *Rujūm* is the plural of *rajm*, a stone or projectile thrown with force; the same root gives *rajama*, "to stone," and *rajīm*, "accursed, pelted," which is the epithet the Qur'an attaches to Satan himself. The word choice is therefore a small theological statement: the one who is called *al-rajīm* is the one who is pelted.

The Qur'an describes this function in several places, and the accounts are consistent:

> *"And We placed in the heaven constellations and adorned them for the beholders, and guarded them from every accursed satan — except such as steals a hearing, and there pursues him a clear, piercing flame."* (Qur'an 15:16–18)

> *"Truly We adorned the lowest heaven with an adornment — the stars — and as a guard against every rebellious satan. They cannot listen to the exalted assembly, and they are pelted from every side, driven off; and for them is a constant punishment."* (Qur'an 37:6–9)

And in Sūrah al-Jinn, the jinn themselves describe the change that occurred:

> *"And we sought to reach the heaven, but found it filled with strong guards and flames. And we used to sit therein in stations for hearing; but he who listens now finds a flame lying in wait for him."* (Qur'an 72:8–9)

The Prophet ﷺ explained the mechanism in a hadith transmitted by al-Bukhārī from Abū Hurayrah, in a report that describes the whole economy of Revelation, angels, and devils:

> *"When God decrees a matter in the heaven, the angels beat their wings in submission to His word, as though it were a chain being dragged upon a rock. And when the terror is lifted from their hearts, they say: 'What has your Lord said?' They say: 'The Truth; and He is the Most High, the Most Great.' Then those who eavesdrop hear it — those eavesdroppers being one above the other — and a flame may strike one of them before he can convey it, or he may convey it to the one beneath him before the flame strikes him, and so it is passed down until it reaches the tongue of the soothsayer or the magician. And sometimes the flame strikes him before he can convey it, and sometimes he conveys it — and with it he fabricates a hundred lies. So it is said: 'Did he not tell you such-and-such on such-and-such a day?' And they believe him because of the one word that was heard from the heaven."* (al-Bukhārī 4701; cf. 7481)

This report is one of the most illuminating texts in the entire tradition for understanding the Islamic attitude toward the unseen. Several things follow from it. First, the jinn are real, and they have agency and intention. Second, they had access to the heavenly realm that they no longer have — the Qur'an dates the change to the sending of the Qur'an, describing it as a tightening of the guard coinciding with the final revelation. Third, soothsaying has a real supernatural component, which is exactly why it is dangerous and why Islam prohibits it outright: the fragment of truth is bait, and the hundred lies are the hook.

**How to Understand the Shooting Stars**

A modern reader will reasonably ask about the relationship between the "shooting star" the tradition describes and the meteor that astronomy describes — a fragment of rock or dust entering the atmosphere at high velocity and burning up at an altitude of roughly eighty to one hundred and twenty kilometers. The question deserves a careful answer, and the classical tradition supplies the materials for one.

Ibn Taymiyyah addressed the issue directly, arguing that the physical description and the revealed purpose are not in competition. A meteor is a real physical event with real physical causes; the fact that it has a cause is precisely what makes it an instrument of the divine will rather than an exception to it. God's action in the universe is normally through means, and the meteor is the means by which the heavenly guard is enforced. What the tradition affirms is that this phenomenon has a purpose beyond its physics; what the tradition does not require is a claim that every visible meteor strikes a satan, or that no physical cause is involved.

Ibn Kathīr, discussing the reports, notes that the scholars differed on whether the pelting occurs with the very meteor that is seen, or whether the meteor is a sign accompanying an invisible striking. Both views operate within a single framework: the heavens are guarded, the devils are repelled, and the visible flame is connected to that reality by God's wisdom. What no mainstream commentator did was to treat the visible phenomenon as a metaphor with no referent, or to treat the divine purpose as a poetic gloss on an impersonal event. The guarded heaven is as real in this tradition as the orbiting rock.

**Saʿīr: The Blaze Prepared in Advance**

The verse closes with *wa-aʿtadnā lahum ʿadhāba al-saʿīr* — "and We have prepared for them the punishment of the Blaze." The verb *aʿtadnā* comes from the root *ʿ-t-d*, which yields *ʿatīd*, "ready, prepared, in readiness." The same word is used elsewhere of what is prepared for the rebellious:

> *"Say: 'It is the truth from your Lord; so whosoever will, let him believe, and whosoever will, let him disbelieve.' Truly We have prepared (aʿtadnā) for the wrongdoers a Fire whose walls will encompass them."* (Qur'an 18:29)

*Al-saʿīr* comes from *s-ʿ-r*, to kindle or set ablaze, and it is one of the names of Hell used in the Qur'an. The word carries the sense of a fire that has been lit and fanned to full intensity, not a smoldering heap.

Two grammatical details deserve attention. First, the pronoun *lahum*, "for them," most naturally refers to the satans — the rebellious jinn who attempt to breach the guarded heaven. The prepares punishment for the seducers before mentioning the punishment of the seduced. Second, the verb is in the perfect tense: the punishment *has been prepared*. Hell is not an improvisation assembled after the fact; it exists now, prepared, which is why the Qur'an elsewhere speaks of it as something that can be *shown* to the Prophet ﷺ during his lifetime and as something the deceased are already exposed to in the grave:

> *"The Fire — they are exposed to it morning and evening; and on the Day the Hour is established [it will be said]: 'Admit the people of Pharaoh to the severest punishment.'"* (Qur'an 40:46)

**The Chain the Verse Implies**

Put together, the verse contains a complete moral sequence. There is beauty (the adorned heaven). There is function (the stars serve and protect). There is intrusion (the satans attempt to steal what they have no right to). There is defense (the missiles). And there is consequence (the prepared Blaze).

The sequence maps onto human life with uncomfortable precision. A person is given a world of beauty and a realm of meaning — revelation, worship, conscience. Then an intrusion comes: the suggestion that he might take what is not his, that he might know what he has not been given, that he might reach beyond his station. The Qur'an's answer is not that curiosity is forbidden but that there is a boundary, and that the boundary is enforced. And beyond the enforcement lies a prepared consequence for those who make transgression their permanent posture rather than their occasional stumble.

**The Guard Over Revelation**

Finally, this verse is one of the Qur'an's own explanations of why revelation can be trusted. If the heavens were permeable, if devils could ascend and return with fragments of the divine decree, then revelation would be permanently contaminated: no one could distinguish a genuine message from a demonic forgery. The guarded heaven is what makes prophecy possible.

That is why this verse appears where it does. The sūrah has just established God's sovereignty and the flawless creation, and it is about to move to human accountability and the warning brought by the Messenger. Before it does, it tells the listener that the channel is clean: the heaven is guarded, the eavesdroppers are driven off with flames, and what has come down to Muḥammad ﷺ came down intact. The God who fits the layers of the sky together is the God who protects His word — a theme the Qur'an states in His own voice:

> *"Truly it is We Who sent down the Reminder, and truly We are its Guardian."* (Qur'an 15:9)

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:6

> **And for those who disbelieve in their Lord is the punishment of Hell. What an evil journey's end!**

**Arabic**

> *وَلِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِرَبِّهِمْ عَذَابُ جَهَنَّمَ ۖ وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ*

**Transliteration:** *Wa-li-alladhīna kafarū bi-rabbihim ʿadhābu jahannama wa-biʾsa al-maṣīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Hinge of the Sūrah**

This short verse performs a large structural task. Verses 1–5 described the cosmos: sovereignty, creation, the layered heavens, and the adorned sky. From here to verse 12, the sūrah describes how human beings respond to that cosmos, and where each response ends. This verse is the hinge between the two halves, and its brevity is part of its force. After the elaborate description of creation, the sūrah delivers judgment in a single line and moves on.

The movement itself is an argument. The Qur'an consistently reasons from creation to obligation: because God made and sustains you, you owe Him recognition and gratitude; and because you are a moral agent who can refuse that recognition, your refusal has consequences. A cosmos without a Creator would impose no duties; a cosmos with a Creator imposes exactly the duties this sūrah is about to describe. The signs are not decorative — they are evidence, and evidence is presented in order to be answered.

**"Those Who Disbelieve in Their Lord"**

*Kafarū bi-rabbihim* — the verb *kafara* has as its core meaning "to cover." A farmer is called a *kāfir* in the Qur'an because he covers seed with soil:

> *"Like a rain whose growth delights the tillers (kuffār)..."* (Qur'an 57:20)

Disbelief is thus, at root, an act of covering over something that is already visible. This is why the Qur'an so often describes disbelief not as ignorance but as suppression: *"And they denied them, though their souls were convinced of them, out of wrongdoing and arrogance"* (27:14). The problem is not lack of data. The data is the sky, the self, the provision, the grave. The problem is what is done with it.

The specific phrase *kafarū bi-rabbihim*, "disbelieved in their Lord," is significant. The object of their denial is God *as Lord* — *rabb*, the One who creates, sustains, nourishes, and brings to maturity. The Makkan Arabs to whom this was first addressed did not, in theory, deny that God was the Creator; the Qur'an records them saying so plainly:

> *"And if you asked them, 'Who created the heavens and the earth?' they would surely say, 'God.'"* (Qur'an 31:25; cf. 29:61, 63; 39:38; 43:87)

Their failure was practical rather than theoretical. They acknowledged the Lordship in words and then lived as though the Lordship were distributed among rivals — intercessors, idols, tribal powers, and wealth. This is the Qur'an's recurring diagnosis: the distance between what a person concedes and what he actually relies upon. Acknowledging that God created is useless if the heart turns to other things when it is frightened, or when it wants something, or when it has been wronged. That gap is *kufr*, and it is the specific thing this verse condemns.

**Jahannam: The Name and What It Means**

The word *Jahannam* has been discussed at length by the philologists and commentators. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and others report two broad views. The first is that the word is of non-Arabic origin, Arabized into the Qur'an's vocabulary from the Hebrew *Gehinnom* — the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, a place associated in the scriptural memory of the region with fire and with judgment. The second derives it from Arabic roots: from *jahm*, describing a face that is gloomy, severe, and frowning, or from the phrase *jahannama al-biʾr*, used of a well that is extraordinarily deep — an apt image for a pit with no floor.

Both etymologies converge on the same picture, and the rest of the Qur'an fills in the details. Hell is described as having gates:

> *"And surely Hell is the promised place for them all. It has seven gates, and to each gate is assigned a portion of them."* (Qur'an 15:43–44)

as having a depth, a blaze, and a sound; as being fueled by both stones and human beings:

> *"Then guard yourselves against the Fire whose fuel is men and stones, prepared for the disbelievers."* (Qur'an 2:24)

and, most importantly, as already existing and already prepared — which is the point the previous verse made with the verb *aʿtadnā*.

**Theological Caution: Justice, Not Vindictiveness**

A responsible commentary must state plainly what the tradition has always insisted: God's punishment is never cruelty, and Hell is not a place where God indulges an emotion. The Prophet ﷺ established the Divine proportion in a ḥadīth qudsī:

> *"Verily, God wrote the decrees of creation fifty thousand years before He created the heavens and the earth, and His Throne was upon the water. And among what He wrote: 'Verily My mercy precedes My wrath' — and in one narration, 'overcomes My wrath.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3194, 7422; Muslim 2751)

And in the celebrated report of the dispute between Paradise and the Fire, the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Paradise and the Fire disputed. The Fire said: 'I have been given the arrogant and the tyrants.' Paradise said: 'Why is it that none enter me but the weak and the lowly and the downtrodden?' God said to Paradise: 'You are My mercy; through you I show mercy to whom I will.' And He said to the Fire: 'You are My punishment; through you I punish whom I will. And each of you will be filled — but as for the Fire, it will not be filled until God places His Foot upon it, and it says: "Enough, enough, enough." Then it will be filled and its parts will draw together, and God will not wrong any of His creation.'"* (al-Bukhārī 4850; Muslim 2846)

The final clause is the one to hold onto: *God will not wrong any of His creation.* The Islamic position on Hell is not that God is looking for reasons to punish; it is that a person who, with full knowledge and full capacity, refuses the mercy that has been offered to him, has chosen his own destination. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"All of my community will enter Paradise except those who refuse." They said: "O Messenger of God, and who refuses?" He said: "Whoever obeys me enters Paradise, and whoever disobeys me has refused."* (al-Bukhārī 7280)

**"What an Evil Journey's End"**

*Wa-biʾsa al-maṣīr* — the verb *biʾsa* is the Arabic verb of blame, the exact counterpart of *niʿma* ("how excellent"). It is used only in formulaic expressions of condemnation, which gives the clause the character of a fixed verdict rather than a passing remark. The word *maṣīr* comes from *ṣ-y-r*, "to become, to end at, to arrive at," and it means destination, final resort, the place where a thing ends up.

The choice of *maṣīr* rather than "place" or "dwelling" carries a specific implication: life is a journey, and every journey has a terminus. A person is always moving toward something, even when he believes he is standing still. The Qur'an's people are consistently described as travelers — *"truly unto thy Lord is the return"* (96:8) — and the question is never whether one will arrive but where.

The Qur'an uses this same formula elsewhere, and the parallel is worth noting:

> *"And whoever turns his back to them on that day... has surely incurred wrath from God, and his refuge is Hell — and evil is the journey's end (wa-biʾsa al-maṣīr)."* (Qur'an 8:16)

> *"Hell, which they will [enter] to burn — and evil is the resting place."* (Qur'an 14:29)

The repetition of the formula across the Qur'an is part of how the scripture trains moral perception. Modern moral discourse largely lacks the category of a bad ending for a life. There is failure, there is unhappiness, there is misfortune — but the idea of a life that terminates in a state of loss is alien to it. The Qur'an restores that category, and it does so in the most practical way possible: by attaching it to a decision made now.

**The Special Application: Fortune-Tellers and Their Clients**

Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both note a specific reading of this verse in its immediate context. Having just mentioned the satans who eavesdrop on the heaven and the missiles that drive them off (verse 5), the sūrah now warns those who disbelieve in their Lord — and the commentators understood this as pointing specifically at those who seek knowledge of the unseen from the very sources the missiles are meant to block.

The link is logical. The devils steal fragments of truth from the heavenly realm; they deliver those fragments to soothsayers and fortune-tellers; the soothsayers wrap them in fabrications; and people then pay for the package. To trust that package is to place one's trust in a channel that God has explicitly declared to be closed and guarded.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the ruling with characteristic severity:

> *"Whoever goes to a diviner (ʿarrāf) and asks him about something, his prayer will not be accepted for forty days."* (Muslim 2230)

and, as cited earlier, the one who *believes* what he is told has gone further still. This is not because fortune-telling works — the tradition holds that it sometimes does, by the stolen fragment — but because of what it does to a person's trust. A life organized around hidden knowledge obtained through forbidden channels is a life that has quietly transferred reliance from the Lord to His enemies. That is exactly *kufr bi-al-rabb*, disbelief in one's Lord.

**The Psychology of the Alternate Route**

Why would anyone take it? The answer is worth considering, because the temptation is very modern.

People consult horoscopes, psychics, and prediction markets for the same underlying reason: uncertainty is unbearable, and the future feels like a threat. The appeal of the forbidden channel is that it promises control over what cannot be controlled. What Islam offers instead is not ignorance of the future but a different relationship to it — one built on *tawakkul*, reliance, in which the servant acts on what he knows, prays for what he hopes, and entrusts the outcome to the One who actually holds it.

The Prophet ﷺ formulated this in the instruction he gave to the young Ibn ʿAbbās:

> *"O young man, I shall teach you some words: Be mindful of God, and God will protect you. Be mindful of God, and you will find Him before you. If you ask, ask of God; and if you seek help, seek help from God. And know that if the whole nation were to gather together to benefit you with something, they could not benefit you with anything except what God has already written for you; and if they were to gather together to harm you with something, they could not harm you with anything except what God has already written against you. The pens have been lifted, and the pages have dried."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516 — graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The last sentence — *the pens have been lifted and the pages have dried* — is the direct answer to the anxiety that drives people to fortune-tellers. It is also the reason the believer can face an unknown future without needing a forbidden shortcut: the unknown is not unknown to God, and the One who holds it is the One who has already been called *al-Raḥmān*.

**The Warning and the Door**

This verse is severe, and it should be read as severe. But it should also be read in its place — before verse 12, which promises forgiveness and a great reward to those who fear their Lord unseen; and within a sūrah whose first verse named God as the source of all blessing and whose second named Him *al-Ghafūr*, the Forgiving.

The Qur'an's pattern is always this: the warning is real, and the door is open. The same God who prepared the Fire also decreed that His mercy precedes His wrath, and the same Prophet who described the punishment of the grave also said:

> *"God is more pleased with the repentance of His servant than one of you who loses his riding-beast in a barren desert and then finds it."* (al-Bukhārī 6309; Muslim 2747)

The verse's function is not to drive the reader to despair but to make the stakes unmistakable. There is a destination, and it is chosen now.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:7

> **When they are cast therein, they will hear it blaring as it boils over,**

**Arabic**

> *إِذَا أُلْقُوا فِيهَا سَمِعُوا لَهَا شَهِيقًا وَهِيَ تَفُورُ*

**Transliteration:** *Idhā ulqū fīhā samiʿū lahā shahīqan wa-hiya tafūr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Cast" — The Grammar of Helplessness**

The verse opens with a passive verb: *idhā ulqū*, "when they are cast." The subject does the going-in; someone else does the casting. This is not incidental grammar. Throughout the Qur'an, the people of the Fire are *driven*, *cast*, and *thrust*, while the people of the Garden *enter*:

> *"And those who revere their Lord are driven to the Garden in troops... and its gates are opened, and its keepers say to them: 'Peace be upon you; you have been good; so enter it to abide.'"* (Qur'an 39:73)

> *"And those who disbelieved are driven to Hell in troops..."* (Qur'an 39:71)

The two scenes are described with deliberately contrasting verbs. The people of Paradise are *sīqa* — gathered and conveyed with honor — and then they *enter*, walking. The people of the Fire are *sīqa* as well, but then they are *thrown in*, because the one thing they spent their lives refusing to do — submit — is now done to them by force, and there is no dignity left in a will that has permanently hardened against the truth.

The passive form also withholds the agent, and the tradition supplies it: the angels of punishment perform the casting. What matters for the reader is the experience being described — a human being arriving somewhere entirely against his will, with no power to slow the arrival or to leave afterward.

**Shahīq: A Sound Before a Sight**

The second clause is sensory and specific: *samiʿū lahā shahīqan*, "they will hear from it a blaring." The preposition *li-*, "for it / from it," attaches the sound to the Fire itself, not to its inhabitants. This is important. The horror that greets the disbelievers is not first and foremost the screaming of other sufferers; it is the voice of the Fire, which has a voice of its own.

The word *shahīq* denotes a drawing-in of breath so forceful that it becomes a sound — the gasp of someone struggling, or, in the classical lexicons, the braying of a donkey. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī explain it as an exceedingly ugly, repulsive sound, and they link it to a parallel description elsewhere in the Qur'an:

> *"As for those who are wretched, they will be in the Fire; for them therein is groaning and wailing (zafīrun wa-shahīqun)."* (Qur'an 11:106)

The pairing of *zafīr* (the exhaling moan) and *shahīq* (the inhaling gasp) is a complete description of a breathing that has become suffering: every breath in and every breath out is agony.

The same phenomenon is described from a distance in another passage, which shows that the Fire's voice precedes the encounter itself:

> *"When it sees them from a place far off, they will hear its raging and roaring (taghayyuzan wa-zafīran)."* (Qur'an 25:12)

Notice the verb in 25:12 — *when it sees them*. The Fire is described as having perception and intention. The tradition has generally taken such descriptions as real in their referent while belonging to a mode of existence we cannot picture: the Fire of the next world is not the fire of this one, and its "seeing" and "raging" are among the things whose modality is known only to God. What is clear is the sequence the Qur'an intends: the inhabitants hear what is coming long before they arrive, and the anticipation is itself part of the punishment.

**Tafūr: The Boiling That Never Settles**

*Wa-hiya tafūr* — "while it boils" or "as it boils over." The root *f-w-r* gives *fawr*, the act of boiling up and overflowing, and it is also the source of the adverb *fawran*, "immediately" — the idea of something surging up without delay. The verb here is in the imperfect tense, describing an ongoing state: the Fire is boiling, continuously, as they arrive and after they arrive.

The image is that of a cauldron at full boil. The Qur'an uses it elsewhere for the condition of the inhabitants themselves:

> *"Verily the tree of Zaqqūm is the food of the sinful, like molten brass, boiling in the bellies as boils scalding water."* (Qur'an 44:43–46)

The sustained physical detail in these descriptions serves a clear rhetorical purpose. The Qur'an is not interested in Hell as an abstraction. It insists on the concrete: heat, sound, sound of boiling, food that does not nourish, water that scalds, skin that is replaced so that the burning can begin again:

> *"Truly those who disbelieve in Our signs — We shall burn them in a Fire. As often as their skins are consumed, We shall replace them with other skins, that they may taste the punishment."* (Qur'an 4:56)

The purpose of such detail is not to terrify for its own sake. It is to break through the numbness that makes people treat the Hereafter as a vague possibility. A person who has a felt image of what is at stake behaves differently from one who has only a word.

**The Scale of the Thing**

The Prophet ﷺ described the Fire in terms that locate the Qur'an's vocabulary in a concrete magnitude. From ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd:

> *"The Fire will be brought on that Day with seventy thousand reins; with every rein will be seventy thousand angels, pulling it."* (Muslim 2842)

And on the intensity of its heat, he said:

> *"The fire that the son of Adam kindles is one part of seventy parts of the Fire of Hell." They said: "O Messenger of God, would it not have been enough?" He said: "It surpasses it by sixty-nine parts, all of them like its heat."* (al-Bukhārī 3265; Muslim 2843)

On the least of its punishments:

> *"The one who will be punished most lightly among the people of the Fire on the Day of Resurrection will be a man beneath the soles of whose feet two embers will be placed, from which his brain will boil as a cauldron or a cooking pot boils. He will think that no one is being punished more severely than he, though he is the most lightly punished of them."* (al-Bukhārī 6561; Muslim 213)

That last detail is worth pausing on. The lightest punishment in Hell is experienced by its subject as the worst punishment anyone could be suffering. This puts an end to any attempt to treat the descriptions as exaggeration or to console oneself with the thought that one might endure it. The Qur'an says the same thing in its own way when it describes the inhabitants' wish for annihilation:

> *"And they will cry: 'O Mālik, let thy Lord put an end to us.' He will say: 'Indeed you will remain.'"* (Qur'an 43:77)

**Why Sound Comes First**

There is a psychological precision in making the first sensation auditory. A person thrown into something is aware of the sound before he is aware of the surface, the temperature, or his own position. Sound arrives before pain does; it tells you what is happening to you before you can verify it. For the arrival in Hell, the Qur'an gives the sound first, so that the reader understands that the recognition precedes the suffering: they know where they are before they feel what it is like. That recognition is the real beginning of the punishment, and it is the theme that the next three verses develop — the questioning, the confession, and the futile wish that they had listened.

**What This Verse Is For**

A modern reader may ask why a scripture would dwell on this. The answer the tradition gives is consistent: the descriptions of Hell are a mercy, not a threat. They are the content of the *nadhīr*, the warning, without which the punishment could not justly fall at all. God does not punish a people to whom no warning came; and a warning is only a warning if it is specific enough to be frightening.

The practical effect on the believer is meant to be twofold, and both halves matter. First, it produces *khawf*, a fear that restrains the hand before a sin and hastens the feet back to obedience. Second, and more importantly, it produces *shukr*, a gratitude so sharp it borders on disbelief that one is not in that condition right now. The Prophet ﷺ taught a form of remembrance that joins the two, and his own practice was described by his wife ʿĀʾishah:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ used to say often, in his bowing and prostration: 'Glory be to You, O God, our Lord, and praise be to You. O God, forgive me' — following the Qur'an."* (al-Bukhārī 817; Muslim 484)

To hear the boiling of the Fire in this verse is, for a person who is not in it, to hear the sound of a door that is still open.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:8

> **well-nigh bursting with rage. Whenever a group is cast therein, its keepers ask them, "Did not a warner come unto you?"**

**Arabic**

> *تَكَادُ تَمَيَّزُ مِنَ الْغَيْظِ ۖ كُلَّمَا أُلْقِيَ فِيهَا فَوْجٌ سَأَلَهُمْ خَزَنَتُهَا أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ نَذِيرٌ*

**Transliteration:** *Takādu tamayyazu mina al-ghayẓi; kullamā ulqiya fīhā fawjun saʾalahum khazanatuhā a-lam yaʾtikum nadhīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Fire That Rages**

The verse continues the description of verse 7 with one of the most startling images in the Qur'an: *takādu tamayyazu mina al-ghayẓi* — "it well-nigh bursts with rage." The verb *tamayyazu* comes from the root *m-y-z*, which means to separate, distinguish, or split apart; the derived form here conveys being torn asunder, burst, or rent. The subject is the Fire, and the cause given is *ghayẓ* — seething rage, the deep and choking anger a person feels when he has been wronged and cannot respond.

Al-Ṭabarī explains that the Fire all but splits apart out of rage against the enemies of God, and he cites the parallel construction the Qur'an uses elsewhere for a person in the grip of that emotion:

> *"When they are cast into a narrow place therein, bound in chains, they will cry out there for destruction. [It will be said:] 'Cry not this day for one destruction, but cry for many destructions!'"* (Qur'an 25:13–14)

> *"Almost bursting with rage. Whenever a group is cast therein, its keepers will ask them: 'Did not a warner come unto you?'"* (Qur'an 67:8)

The commentators were careful about what this attribute implies. The Fire is a created thing; it does not possess anger in the way a moral agent does, and its "rage" is not an independent emotion directed at God's decree. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both raise the question and answer it in the same direction: the expression communicates the intensity of the punishment and the reality that the Fire is, in a sense, executing the divine displeasure against those who rejected the truth. God's *ghaḍab* (wrath) is a real attribute affirmed by Ahl al-Sunnah without likening it to human anger — a hot flush of blood and an urge to retaliate, which is what human anger is, would be a defect if predicated of God. The Fire's rage is the created instrument through which that attribute becomes visible to creatures.

The theological point can be stated simply: evil is not neutral to the universe. The cosmos is not indifferent to injustice. A person who spends his life defying his Creator arrives at a destination that is, in the most literal sense, hostile to him.

**The Arrival in Waves**

*Kullamā ulqiya fīhā fawjun* — "whenever a group is cast therein." The word *fawj* denotes a troop, a contingent, a band arriving together. The construction *kullamā* — "whenever," "each time that" — indicates repetition: group after group, arriving continuously.

This matches the broader Qur'anic picture of the Day of Resurrection as a process with stages rather than an instantaneous tableau. People are gathered, marshalled, sorted, questioned, and conducted:

> *"And those who disbelieved are driven to Hell in troops, until when they come to it, its gates are opened and its keepers say to them..."* (Qur'an 39:71)

> *"The Day We shall gather the reverent unto the Compassionate as a delegation, and drive the guilty to Hell as a thirsty herd."* (Qur'an 19:85–86)

The detail that each arriving group receives the same reception is significant. It means the questioning is not a one-time formality at the gate but a standing procedure. Every contingent hears the same question, and every contingent gives the same answer — which is what verses 9 through 11 go on to record.

**The Keepers: Khazanah**

*Saʾalahum khazanatuhā* — "its keepers ask them." The word *khazanah* is the plural of *khāzin*, a keeper, guardian, or treasurer, from the root *kh-z-n*, which gives *khizānah*, a storehouse or treasury. The choice of title is itself a statement: Hell is a place under administration, not a chaotic pit. It has custodians, records, gates, and procedures. Nothing about it is accidental.

The Qur'an describes these keepers in several places, and the descriptions consistently identify them as angels:

> *"O you who believe! Guard yourselves and your families against a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, over which are angels, stern and severe, who do not disobey God in what He commands them, and who do what they are commanded."* (Qur'an 66:6)

Their number is specified in Sūrah al-Mudaththir:

> *"Over it are nineteen. And We have not made the keepers of the Fire other than angels; and We have not made their number except as a trial for those who disbelieve."* (Qur'an 74:30–31)

And their chief is named in Sūrah al-Zukhruf, in an exchange that is one of the most haunting passages in the scripture:

> *"And they will call out: 'O Mālik, let thy Lord put an end to us!' He will say: 'Indeed you will remain. We did indeed bring you the Truth, but most of you were averse to the Truth.'"* (Qur'an 43:77–78)

Note that the chief keeper's answer contains a theological claim: *We did indeed bring you the Truth.* The keepers are not merely jailers; they are witnesses. They are the ones who delivered the message in the first place — the angels of revelation — or, on another reading transmitted by the commentators, they are the angels of punishment who are testifying that the message had in fact reached those standing before them.

**The Question: "Did Not a Warner Come unto You?"**

The question is *a-lam yaʾtikum nadhīr*, "Did not a warner come unto you?" The word *nadhīr* is from the root *n-dh-r*, and it means one who brings warning of a consequence before it arrives — as opposed to *bashīr*, the bringer of good news. The Qur'an pairs the two functions consistently, and the Prophet ﷺ is described in both terms:

> *"O Prophet, truly We have sent you as a witness, and a bringer of good tidings, and a warner."* (Qur'an 33:45)

The question asked by the keepers is not a request for information. The angels know perfectly well whether a warner came, and the answer is recorded in the next verse. The question is a *reproach* and, more precisely, the formal establishment of proof (*iqāmat al-ḥujjah*).

This distinction is central to Islamic theology. The commentators are emphatic that the interrogation is not a trial conducted to reach a verdict; the verdict is already fixed. It is a trial conducted in order that the verdict may be *known to be just* — by the one being judged, by the onlookers, and by the record. This is why the Qur'an so often shows the condemned confessing their own guilt rather than being convicted by external evidence. Their own mouths settle the case.

The principle that underlies the question is stated explicitly elsewhere:

> *"And never do We punish until We have sent a messenger."* (Qur'an 17:15)

> *"And there has been no community but that a warner has passed among them."* (Qur'an 35:24)

> *"Messengers bearing good tidings and warning, so that mankind might have no argument against God after the messengers."* (Qur'an 4:165)

The last of these gives the formal name of the doctrine: *li-allā yakūna li-al-nāsi ʿalā Allāhi ḥujjah baʿda al-rusul* — so that people have no plea (*ḥujjah*) left against God after the sending of messengers. Justice requires that the rule be communicated before the penalty is applied.

**What About Those Who Never Heard?**

The classical theologians took this principle seriously enough to ask what happens to people who lived in places or times to which no messenger came — the *ahl al-fatra*, the people of the interval between prophets. The question is not academic; it is the logical test of whether the doctrine of the *nadhīr* is real.

The dominant position among Ahl al-Sunnah, following the texts, is that such people are not punished on the basis of a message that never reached them, and that they will be tested separately on the Day of Resurrection. The Prophet ﷺ described this:

> *"Four will argue on the Day of Resurrection: a deaf man who never heard anything, a madman, a very old man, and a man who died during the interval [between messengers]. As for the deaf man, he will say: 'O Lord, Islam came and I heard nothing.' As for the madman, he will say: 'O Lord, Islam came and the children were throwing dung at me.' As for the very old man, he will say: 'O Lord, Islam came and I understood nothing.' And as for the one who died in the interval, he will say: 'O Lord, no messenger came to me.' He will take their pledges that they will obey Him, then He will send to them [a command]: 'Enter the Fire.' By Him in Whose Hand is my soul, if they enter it, it will be cool and safe for them."* (Musnad Aḥmad 16268; Abū Yaʿlā; al-Bazzār — graded sound by a number of hadith scholars, among them al-Albānī)

Other scholars held variants of this view, and some — particularly among the later Ashʿarīs — held that such people are saved outright by God's mercy without a test. What is unanimous is the premise: no one is punished for a message he could not possibly have received. The question the keepers ask in this verse is the guarantee of that principle, and it is asked of everyone who arrives.

**The Word Nadhīr and the Nature of the Message**

There is a further point worth noticing. The keepers ask about a *nadhīr* — a warner — not about a philosopher, a miracle-worker, or a sage. The question is not "did someone prove God's existence to you?" It is "did someone warn you?"

This tells the reader what the messengers were primarily sent to do. Their message was not, in the first instance, an intellectual puzzle to be solved but a warning to be heeded. The content of the warning is simple and urgent: there is a God, you belong to Him, you will die, you will be questioned, and your response to this message determines where you spend eternity. That is the *nadhārah*, and it requires a response, not an investigation.

It also explains why the Qur'an so frequently blames the rejection of the messengers on *istikbār* (arrogance), *ʿinād* (obstinacy), and *ittibāʿ al-hawā* (following desire) rather than on insufficient evidence. The evidence was adequate; the will was the problem. The next verse will make this explicit in the mouth of the condemned themselves, who will name their failure precisely: *"Had we but listened or understood..."*

**Justice Made Public**

Finally, consider what this scene says about the character of God's justice. In human courts, the convicted are often convicted on evidence they never see, by procedures they do not understand, in a language they do not speak. The Day described here is the opposite of that. Every group is addressed directly. Every group is asked a question they can answer. Every group answers with their own mouths. And the entire exchange is conducted by witnesses who were present at the delivery of the message.

Nothing is hidden, nothing is assumed, and nothing is imposed without being established first. That is the meaning of the Qur'an's declaration:

> *"And We shall set up the scales of justice for the Day of Resurrection, so no soul will be wronged in anything."* (Qur'an 21:47)

The question of the keepers is that justice audible.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:9

> **They say, "Indeed, a warner came unto us, but we denied him and said, 'God did not send anything down; you are in naught but great error.'"**

**Arabic**

> *قَالُوا بَلَىٰ قَدْ جَاءَنَا نَذِيرٌ فَكَذَّبْنَا وَقُلْنَا مَا نَزَّلَ اللَّهُ مِنْ شَيْءٍ إِنْ أَنْتُمْ إِلَّا فِي ضَلَالٍ كَبِيرٍ*

**Transliteration:** *Qālū balā qad jāʾanā nadhīrun fa-kadhdhabnā wa-qulnā mā nazzala Allāhu min shayʾin in antum illā fī ḍalālin kabīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Confession That Opens with "Yes Indeed"**

The keepers asked: "Did not a warner come unto you?" The answer begins with a single word: *balā*. In Arabic, *balā* is the particle used to answer a *negated* question in the affirmative — it means "yes indeed," "on the contrary, yes." Had the inmates wished to dispute the charge, this is precisely where they would have done so. Instead they confirm it immediately and without qualification.

That one word is doing enormous work in the sūrah's argument. The Qur'an, revealed in Makkah to a people who claimed they had never been warned, here puts a confession in the mouths of that very generation: the warner came, and they knew it. The question that the Makkan audience asked in this life — "Why has no messenger been sent to us?" — is answered by the Qur'an in advance, in the voice of the people themselves on the Day they can no longer lie.

The structure of the confession is meticulous, and it unfolds in three movements.

**First: "A warner came unto us"**

They concede the fact. Not "a warner may have come," not "we were unsure whether he was a warner," but *qad jāʾanā nadhīr* — a warner came to us, and the particle *qad* with the past verb emphasizes that the matter is settled and complete.

This concession eliminates every possible defense. A person might argue that the message never reached him, that it was garbled in transmission, that he lived too far away, or that he was too young to understand. All of that is removed by this clause. The warning arrived, it was intelligible, and it was addressed to him personally.

**Second: "But we denied him"**

*Fa-kadhdhabnā* — "so we denied him." The root *k-dh-b* means to declare something false, and the intensive form *kadhdhaba* means to treat someone as a liar, to reject his claim deliberately. It is not the language of doubt; it is the language of refusal.

This is the moment the verse identifies as the decisive one. The warner came. The evidence was adequate. And the will said no. The condemnation is not for failing to work out a difficult proof; it is for meeting a clear warning with rejection. This is why the Qur'an, throughout, attributes the disbelievers' ruin to *ẓulm* (wrongdoing), *istikbār* (arrogance), and *juhūd* (knowing denial) rather than to intellectual incapacity:

> *"And they denied them — though their souls were convinced of them — out of wrongdoing and arrogance. So see how the end of the corrupters was."* (Qur'an 27:14)

**Third: They Quote Their Own Words Against the Messenger**

The most devastating part of the confession is that the inmates do not merely summarize their rejection — they repeat the exact words they used:

> *"God did not send anything down; you are in naught but great error."*

Two claims are recorded here, and both are worth examining.

*The first claim — "God did not send anything down" — is a denial of the category of revelation.* The verb is *nazzala*, "He sent down," the standard Qur'anic term for revelation, used of the Torah, the Gospel, the Psalms, and the Qur'an itself. To deny that God has sent anything down is not to reject a particular scripture after examining it; it is to reject the very possibility that God communicates. The claim is not "this message is not from God"; it is "God does not send messages."

That is a position with a long history and a very modern face. It is the claim that the transcendent, if it exists, is silent; that the universe contains no address from its Author; that the appropriate human posture toward ultimate questions is inference without revelation. The Qur'an's response is not an argument against that position so much as a display of its consequence: the person who holds it arrives at the Day of Reckoning having been warned by the very God he said does not warn, and he is asked about it by the angels.

*The second claim — "you are in naught but great error" — is the reversal.* The disbelievers did not simply reject the warner; they diagnosed him. They declared that *he* was the one who was lost, and they used the strongest available phrase to do it: *ḍalāl kabīr*, great error, utter misguidance.

The Qur'an's irony here is precise and severe. The phrase they used against the warner is the phrase the Qur'an uses against them. The word *ḍalāl* appears throughout the scripture to describe the condition of those who reject guidance, and the same intensity — *kabīr*, *baʿīd*, *mubīn* — is applied to them. What they said about the messenger is what the Day will say about them. Their own vocabulary has been preserved to be turned around.

The same phenomenon appears elsewhere in the Qur'an in the exchanges of the Hereafter:

> *"Then some of you will turn to others, questioning one another. They will say: 'Indeed you used to come at us from the right hand.' They will reply: 'Nay, but you were not believers...'"* (Qur'an 37:27–29)

> *"And those who were arrogant will say to those who were deemed weak: 'Did we turn you away from guidance after it had come to you? Nay, but you were guilty.'"* (Qur'an 34:32)

**The Record and the Mouths That Read It**

One detail of this scene deserves emphasis: the condemned quote their own words verbatim. They are not paraphrasing; they are reproducing what they said, decades or centuries after saying it.

The Qur'an explains the mechanism:

> *"This Day We seal their mouths, and their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will bear witness to what they used to earn."* (Qur'an 36:65)

> *"This, Our record, speaks against you in truth. Indeed We were recording what you used to do."* (Qur'an 45:29)

> *"And the record will be placed, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it, and they will say: 'Woe to us! What is this record that leaves nothing small or great but has enumerated it?' And they will find what they did present [before them]. And your Lord wrongs no one."* (Qur'an 18:49)

The precision of the record is a comfort to the oppressed and a terror to the careless. A person who has been wronged by someone who has long since forgotten it, or who denied it, or who died unrepentant, is promised that the account is complete and that the transcript will be read aloud in a court from which there is no appeal. And a person who has been careless with his own tongue is warned that nothing he has said is lost. The Prophet ﷺ put it with characteristic economy:

> *"A man may utter a word that pleases God, without thinking anything of it, and God raises him thereby degrees; and a man may utter a word that displeases God, without thinking anything of it, and he is thrown thereby into the Fire — a distance greater than that between the east and the west."* (al-Bukhārī 6477; Muslim 2988)

**The Tragedy of Late Clarity**

Psychologically, this verse describes a state that human beings occasionally glimpse in miniature: the moment when a truth becomes undeniable too late to act on it. The diagnosis delivered after the window for treatment has closed. The betrayal discovered after the relationship is over. The addiction understood after the damage is irreversible. The Qur'an's point is that Hell is, among other things, the permanent experience of that moment — clarity without agency.

The Prophet ﷺ defined the boundary of the window precisely:

> *"Verily God accepts the repentance of His servant as long as the soul has not reached the throat [i.e., until the death rattle]."* (al-Tirmidhī 3537; Ibn Mājah 4253 — graded ḥasan)

And he described the openness of the door in terms that border on the unimaginable:

> *"Verily God extends His Hand by night so that the one who sinned by day may repent, and He extends His Hand by day so that the one who sinned by night may repent — until the sun rises from its place of setting."* (Muslim 2759)

The contrast with this verse could not be sharper. In this life, the words "I was wrong" open a door. In the next, the same words are spoken, and they are recorded as part of the case. The difference is not the content of the confession; it is the timing.

**What the Verse Asks of the Reader Now**

The practical implication is direct, and it is the reason this verse was revealed to people who were still alive. Every element of the confession is available to a person now, in the right order and with the opposite outcome.

First: *a warner has come.* The message has arrived, intact, in a form that can be read today. The proof has been established.

Second: *do not deny it.* Rejection is not a neutral option; it is the one response the Qur'an identifies as fatal.

Third: *do not repeat the error of diagnosing the messenger.* The claim that the warner is himself misguided is as old as the first prophet, and it has appeared in every generation since. It is worth asking, honestly, whether the objections a person raises to the message are actually objections he has examined, or whether they are the received opinions of his time and place.

And finally: *confess now.* The confession recorded in this verse is a confession that saves no one. The confession that saves is the one offered while the sun still rises in the east.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:10

> **They say, "Had we listened or had we understood, we would not be among the inhabitants of the Blaze."**

**Arabic**

> *وَقَالُوا لَوْ كُنَّا نَسْمَعُ أَوْ نَعْقِلُ مَا كُنَّا فِي أَصْحَابِ السَّعِيرِ*

**Transliteration:** *Wa-qālū law kunnā nasmaʿu aw naʿqilu mā kunnā fī aṣḥābi al-saʿīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Honest Sentence in Hell**

If verse 9 was a confession of fact, this verse is a confession of cause. The inmates do not say "we were not told." They do not say "the evidence was inadequate." They do not say "we had no choice." They identify the failure with surgical accuracy: *we did not listen, and we did not use our minds.*

The sentence is one of the most remarkable in the Qur'an because it removes, from the mouths of the damned themselves, every excuse that is commonly offered in this life. The person who says "I would believe if I saw a sign" is told by this verse that the problem was never the sign. The person who says "my upbringing made me this way" is told that the failure was an act of *his*. The person who says "God did not guide me" is told that the guidance was delivered and the reception was refused.

**Two Failures, Not One**

The verse names two distinct failures joined by *aw*, "or": *law kunnā nasmaʿu aw naʿqilu* — "had we listened or had we understood."

The first failure is *samʿ*, hearing. But the Arabic here is not the bare perception of sound. The Qur'an consistently distinguishes between hearing as a physical event and listening as a moral act. It rebukes those who "hear" without "listening":

> *"And do not be like those who say, 'We hear,' while they do not hear."* (Qur'an 8:21)

> *"And among them are those who listen to you; but have you made the deaf to hear, even though they do not understand?"* (Qur'an 10:42)

The classical commentators, among them Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, explain *nasmaʿu* in this verse as *hearing with acceptance and response* — the listening of a person who is prepared to be changed by what he hears. The disbelievers heard the recitation perfectly well. Some of them, according to the reports, listened to it secretly at night, and one of their leaders did so repeatedly before eventually accepting Islam. What they lacked was not auditory access but receptivity.

The second failure is *ʿaql*. The word *naʿqilu* comes from *ʿ-q-l*, whose root meaning is to bind or tie — the *ʿaql* being the faculty that binds ideas together, restrains impulse, and draws conclusions. To "not use one's ʿaql" is therefore not to lack intelligence; it is to fail to do the binding work: to hear the warning and not connect it to one's mortality, to see the creation and not connect it to a Creator, to observe the fate of earlier nations and not connect it to one's own.

This is why the Qur'an blames the disbelievers' condition on a failure of the *heart* rather than of the brain:

> *"And We have certainly created for Hell many of the jinn and mankind. They have hearts with which they do not understand, and they have eyes with which they do not see, and they have ears with which they do not hear. Those are like cattle — nay, they are further astray. It is they who are the heedless."* (Qur'an 7:179)

Note the instrument is present in each case: they *have* hearts, eyes, and ears. The defect is in the use.

**The Excuse This Verse Destroys**

The verse is the Qur'an's definitive answer to one of the oldest objections in theology: if God is just and powerful, why are people punished for not believing?

The answer offered here is that the failure was voluntary at the point where it mattered. Listening is something a person does. Reflecting is something a person does. Both are acts, and both were refused. The damned do not experience their fate as an imposition; they experience it as something they brought about. That is precisely why the verse is placed in their mouths rather than pronounced about them — the Qur'an wants the reader to hear the admission from the people who made the choice.

The Islamic theological tradition worked out this position carefully in the debate over *qadar* (divine decree) and human responsibility. The determinists (*Jabriyyah*) held that human acts are coerced and that the language of choice is illusory. The rationalists (*Muʿtazilah*) held that human beings create their own acts and that God cannot be said to will disobedience. Ahl al-Sunnah took the middle position, which this verse illustrates: God creates all things, including human acts, and the human being possesses a real will and a real acquisition (*kasb*) by which the act is attributed to him morally. The servant's will is created, and the servant is nonetheless the one who willed.

Ibn Taymiyyah argued that this is not a philosophical puzzle to be resolved but the plain sense of scripture, which addresses human beings as agents everywhere and never treats them as objects. He pointed out that the Qur'an's call, warning, promise, and threat all presuppose agency, and that no one who reads this verse — in which the condemned blame themselves — can consistently maintain that they were compelled.

**The Parable of the Rain**

The Prophet ﷺ described the difference between hearing and listening in a parable that is among the most quoted in the tradition, reported from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī:

> *"The example of the guidance and knowledge with which God has sent me is like abundant rain falling upon the earth. Some of the soil is good: it absorbs the water and brings forth herbage and grass in abundance. And some of it is hard: it holds the water, and God benefits people thereby — they drink from it, give it to others to drink, and irrigate with it. And some of it is a barren plain: it neither holds the water nor brings forth vegetation. That is the example of the one who gains understanding of the religion of God and benefits from what God sent me with — he learns and teaches — and the example of the one who does not lift his head to it and does not accept the guidance of God with which I have been sent."* (al-Bukhārī 79; Muslim 2282)

Three soils, one rain. The variable is not the water. This is the exact structure of the verse: the warner came to everyone; the difference was in the reception.

Note the second soil in the parable — the hard ground that holds water without producing vegetation. That is the person who *learns* without *acting*, who accumulates knowledge of the religion and transmits it without being changed by it. The Prophet ﷺ sought refuge from precisely that condition:

> *"O God, I seek refuge in You from knowledge that does not benefit, from a heart that is not humble, from a soul that is never satisfied, and from a supplication that is not answered."* (Muslim 2722)

**Listening in an Age of Noise**

The modern relevance of this verse is uncomfortable, because the contemporary problem is not a shortage of access to the message. It is the opposite. Recordings of the Qur'an are available to anyone with a phone; translations exist in every major language; the teachings of the Prophet ﷺ are a search away. By any historical measure, this generation has more access to the warning than any before it.

And yet the diagnoses the verse names are, if anything, more widespread. Consider what the attention economy does to *samʿ*. To listen is to give something sustained, undivided attention; it is to let a claim work on you before you respond to it. The architecture of modern media is designed to prevent exactly that — to interrupt, to fragment, and to keep the next stimulus arriving before the last one has landed. A person can spend years in a state of continuous partial attention and never once genuinely listen to anything.

Consider, too, what identity does to *ʿaql*. To reflect is to follow a line of thought wherever it leads. But when a conclusion is socially costly — when accepting it would require a person to break with his community, his habits, or his self-image — the intellect develops a remarkable ability to stop short of the conclusion and call the stopping rigor. The Qur'an names this dynamic elsewhere with precision:

> *"Nay, but they say: 'We found our fathers following a way, and we are guided by their footsteps.'"* (Qur'an 43:22)

**How to Listen as the Qur'an Means It**

The tradition derived from this verse — and from the parallel passage in 7:179 — a practical discipline of listening that is worth stating in steps, because it is the difference between hearing and being changed.

*First, show up with the intention of being affected.* The Companions did not approach revelation as an object of study but as an instruction addressed to them personally. Ibn Masʿūd said that when he wanted to know whether the Prophet ﷺ was addressing him, he only had to consider whether the Qur'an was commanding or forbidding something he was doing.

*Second, listen to completion.* The Qur'an rebukes those who *"make the Qur'an into divided parts"* (15:91) — taking what suits them and leaving the rest. The warner's message is a whole, and selective hearing is a form of not hearing.

*Third, act on what you know.* The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"When I forbid you from something, avoid it; and when I command you to do something, do as much of it as you can."* (al-Bukhārī 7288; Muslim 1337)

The gap between knowing and doing is not closed by more information; it is closed by obedience.

*Fourth, ask for the capacity itself.* The Fātiḥah, which every Muslim recites several times a day, contains a request for guidance precisely because guidance is not automatic. The Prophet ﷺ taught:

> *"O God, benefit me by what You have taught me, teach me what benefits me, and increase me in knowledge."* (al-Tirmidhī 3599)

**The Last Word of the Verse**

The inmates end their sentence with *mā kunnā fī aṣḥābi al-saʿīr* — "we would not be among the companions of the Blaze." The word *aṣḥāb* means companions, associates, the people who belong to a thing. It suggests a relationship rather than a location. They did not stumble into the Fire; they became its people, and it became theirs, through the companionship they chose in life — the company they kept, the priorities they adopted, and the habits they fed.

The verse's quiet implication for the reader is that the same is true in the other direction. A person becomes one of the *aṣḥāb* of whatever he consistently accompanies, and the Prophet ﷺ stated the principle in a single sentence:

> *"A person is upon the religion of his close friend; so let each of you consider whom he befriends."* (Abū Dāwūd 4833; al-Tirmidhī 2378 — graded ḥasan)

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:11

> **Thus do they admit their sin; so away with the inhabitants of the Blaze!**

**Arabic**

> *فَاعْتَرَفُوا بِذَنْبِهِمْ ۖ فَسُحْقًا لِأَصْحَابِ السَّعِيرِ*

**Transliteration:** *Fa-iʿtarafū bi-dhanbihim; fa-suḥqan li-aṣḥābi al-saʿīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word for Sin: A Tail That Follows Its Owner**

*Dhanb*, translated here as "sin," comes from a root whose original meaning is "tail." The lexicographers noted the connection, and it is a suggestive one: a *dhanb* is the deed that trails behind the one who did it and will not be shaken off. A person can walk away from the moment of the act, from the place of the act, and even from the memory of the act, but the act itself follows. In this world it follows as a consequence, and in the next it follows as a record that speaks.

The verb *iʿtarafū* is equally precise. It comes from *ʿ-r-f*, "to know, to recognize," and the derived form *iʿtarafa* means to acknowledge something one previously did not acknowledge. The choice of this form over a simpler "they said" matters: they had denied their sin in the world, and now they recognize it. The confession is a *recognition* — the removal of a veil that was self-imposed.

Notice the sequence the sūrah has constructed. Verse 8 asked whether a warner came. Verse 9 answered yes, and quoted their rejection. Verse 10 identified the failure as theirs. And now verse 11 draws the conclusion: *thus* — *fa-* — by their own words, they have convicted themselves. The particle *fa-* at the head of the verse ties the admission to everything that preceded it: their own testimony is the case against them.

**Divine Justice as a Confession, Not a Verdict Imposed**

This is one of the clearest statements in the Qur'an of what Islamic theology calls *iqāmat al-ḥujjah* — the establishment of the proof. The condemned are not convicted on evidence they never see. They are convicted by their own mouths, in a proceeding they participate in, before witnesses who delivered the message in the first place.

The Qur'an describes the same moment elsewhere, in a passage that makes the self-condemnation explicit:

> *"And those who disbelieved are driven to Hell in troops, until, when they come to it, its gates are opened and its keepers say to them: 'Did not messengers from among you come to you, reciting to you the signs of your Lord and warning you of the meeting of this Day of yours?' They will say: 'Yea, indeed!' But the Word of punishment has come due against the disbelievers."* (Qur'an 39:71)

That final clause is the key: *the Word of punishment has come due*. It has come due because the conditions were met — the warning was delivered, the rejection was real, and the time for reversal has closed. Nothing in the scene is arbitrary. The gates open to the sound of a case being closed properly.

The confessions recorded in the Qur'an from the people of the Fire follow a consistent pattern, and they are always too late:

> *"They will say, as they contend with one another therein: 'By God, we were indeed in manifest error when we equated you with the Lord of the worlds.'"* (Qur'an 26:96–98)

> *"They will say: 'Our Lord, twice You have caused us to die, and twice You have given us life; and we confess our sins. Is there then any way out?'"* (Qur'an 40:11)

> *"Could you but see when they are made to stand before the Fire and say: 'Would that we were sent back! We would not deny the signs of our Lord, and we would be among the believers.' Nay, but that has become clear to them which they used to hide before. And even if they were sent back, they would return to what they were forbidden; and they are indeed liars."* (Qur'an 6:27–28)

The last of these is the most sobering. It states that the wish to return is not genuine repentance but the wish of someone caught; were the wish granted, the behavior would repeat. This is why the Qur'an insists that the Hell described in these verses is not a punishment imposed on people who would have reformed given more time. It is the fixed state of a will that has chosen, definitively, and would choose the same way again.

**Suḥqan: "Away With Them"**

The verse closes with *fa-suḥqan li-aṣḥābi al-saʿīr*. The word *suḥqan* is a verbal noun used as a curse of removal. Its root, *s-ḥ-q*, carries the sense of being far off, of being ground into dust, of being pulverized. The standard gloss is *buʿdan* — "distance," "removal," "may they be cast far away."

The Qur'an uses the same root elsewhere to describe a place of no return:

> *"[He is] as though he had fallen from the sky, and the birds snatched him away, or the wind carried him down to a far-off place (makānin saḥīq)."* (Qur'an 22:31)

Al-Ṭabarī transmits two readings of the word from the early authorities. The first, and the majority view, is that it is a curse: "Away with them! Distance for them!" — an expression of their removal from God's mercy. The second, reported from some of the early scholars, is that *suḥq* is the proper name of a valley in Hell, a pit beneath the Fire in which the inhabitants are placed, and that the phrase therefore means "so [they are cast] into Suḥq, the companions of the Blaze." Both readings converge on the same meaning: separation, depth, and distance from mercy.

The phrase *aṣḥāb al-saʿīr* — "the inhabitants," or more literally "the companions, of the Blaze" — repeats from verse 10. The repetition is deliberate. In verse 10, they use the phrase about themselves. In verse 11, the Qur'an uses it about them. What they confessed is now declared.

**The Door That Is Open Now**

Everything in this verse is about finality, and yet the verse was revealed to people who were still breathing. That is the point. The Qur'an describes the closed door in order to make the open one visible.

The tradition is emphatic that the door is open, and open wide, for as long as a person is alive:

> *"Verily God extends His Hand by night so that the one who sinned by day may repent, and He extends His Hand by day so that the one who sinned by night may repent — until the sun rises from its place of setting."* (Muslim 2759)

> *"God accepts the repentance of His servant as long as the soul has not reached the throat."* (al-Tirmidhī 3537; Ibn Mājah 4253 — graded ḥasan)

> *"Every son of Adam sins, and the best of those who sin are those who repent."* (al-Tirmidhī 2499; Ibn Mājah 4251 — graded ḥasan)

The structure of repentance in Islam turns entirely on timing, and the timing is always now. Theologians identified three conditions for authentic repentance (*tawbah*): to abandon the sin, to regret it, and to resolve not to return to it — with the addition, where the sin involved another person's rights, of restoring what was taken or obtaining release from it. None of those conditions is difficult to state. The difficulty is entirely in the *when*, because the *when* is always later, and later is a country that may not exist.

The Prophet ﷺ gave the form of confession that opens the door, in a supplication his Companions called the *sayyid al-istighfār*, the master prayer of forgiveness:

> *"O God, You are my Lord; there is no god but You. You created me, and I am Your servant, and I am upon Your covenant and Your promise as much as I am able. I seek refuge in You from the evil of what I have done. I acknowledge Your favor upon me, and I acknowledge my sin — so forgive me, for none forgives sins but You."* (al-Bukhārī 6306)

The verb translated "I acknowledge my sin" is *abūʾu bi-dhanbī* — the same act of confession this verse describes. The difference is entirely a matter of when. Spoken in this life, that sentence opens Paradise. Spoken in the next, it is the line the inmates of the Blaze speak as their case closes.

**The Practical Discipline This Verse Teaches**

There is a habit implied by the contrast between the two confessions, and the early community practiced it deliberately.

*Confess immediately.* The practice of the Prophet ﷺ himself was to seek forgiveness constantly — he said, *"By God, I seek forgiveness from God and turn to Him more than seventy times a day"* (al-Bukhārī 6307). This despite the fact that, as the tradition holds, his past and future sins were forgiven. The habit is not a response to the quantity of one's sins but to the danger of delay.

*Do not audit only the large items.* The inmates of the Fire in this chapter are being punished for the great refusal, but the tradition consistently warns that small, unrepented acts accumulate. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Beware of minor sins, for they gather upon a person until they destroy him."* And he gave the example of people who halted in a barren valley and each brought a stick of firewood until they had gathered enough to kindle a great fire. (Musnad Aḥmad 3808; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī)

*Name the sin to God honestly.* The confession in this verse is specific: they admit *dhanbihim*, their sin, not a generic unworthiness. Half-confessions — "if I have done anything wrong" — are the habit of a person who is managing an impression rather than settling an account. God is not an impression to be managed.

And crucially: *do not wait for a better version of yourself.* The most common shape of procrastination in religious life is the intention to reform once certain conditions are met — after this project, after this phase, when life is calmer. The verse's contribution to that psychology is to point out that the confession which saves is the one made before the conditions are right. It is precisely the stammered, unfinished, imperfect turning toward God in the middle of an unreformed life that is accepted, because it is the only kind anyone has ever managed to offer.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:12

> **Truly for those who fear their Lord unseen there shall be forgiveness and a great reward.**

**Arabic**

> *إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَخْشَوْنَ رَبَّهُمْ بِالْغَيْبِ لَهُمْ مَغْفِرَةٌ وَأَجْرٌ كَبِيرٌ*

**Transliteration:** *Inna alladhīna yakhshawna rabbahum bi-al-ghaybi lahum maghfiratun wa-ajrun kabīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mercy Verse at the Center of the Fire**

For five verses this sūrah has described Hell: its blaring, its boiling, its rage, its keepers, the interrogation, and the confession of the damned. Then, without transition, comes this single verse of promise. The placement is not accidental, and it is the key to reading the whole sūrah.

The Qur'an never leaves the reader in terror without opening a door. Its pattern throughout is *tarhīb* followed by *targhīb* — warning followed by encouragement — because the purpose of the warning is not to paralyze but to move. A person who has been shown the Fire and then shown no way out will either despair or stop listening. A person who has been shown the Fire and then shown the road away from it has been given the only thing that makes the warning useful.

Thematically, verse 12 is also the sūrah's moral center. Everything before it established God's power and the reality of judgment; everything after it will argue for God's knowledge, provision, and the folly of trusting in anything else. This verse is the pivot: it states what the right response to all of that actually looks like — *khashyah*, the reverent awe of God held in the unseen — and what it earns: *maghfirah* and a *great reward.*

**Khashyah Is Not the Same as Fear**

The verb is *yakhshawna*, from *kh-sh-y*. Arabic has several words for fear — *khawf*, *rahbah*, *wajal*, *haibah* — and the Qur'an uses them with discrimination. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, whose lexicon of Qur'anic vocabulary remains standard, defined *khashyah* as *fear mingled with veneration and knowledge*. It is not the flinch of someone facing an unpredictable danger; it is the awe of someone who knows exactly whom he is facing and knows that this One is magnificent.

The Qur'an makes the connection to knowledge explicit:

> *"Only those of His servants who possess knowledge fear God (yakhshā Allāh). Truly God is Mighty, Forgiving."* (Qur'an 35:28)

The grammar of that verse is emphatic — *innamā yakhshā Allāha min ʿibādihi al-ʿulamāʾ*, "only the knowledgeable among His servants hold God in awe" — and it inverts the common assumption that fear decreases with understanding. With respect to God, the relationship runs the other way: the more a person understands, the more he is awed. A child may fear the dark because he does not know what is in it; the astronomer who has measured the distances between galaxies is not comforted by his knowledge but overwhelmed by it.

This is why the people of *khashyah* in this verse are named as a distinct and elevated class. The Prophet ﷺ himself said:

> *"By God, I am the most knowing of you concerning God, and the most awed of Him."* (al-Bukhārī 20; Muslim 1108)

Knowledge and awe, at the highest level, belonged to the same person.

**"Unseen": The Two Readings**

The phrase *bi-al-ghayb* has been understood in two ways, and the commentators — among them al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Shawkānī, and al-Ṭabarī — transmitted both.

*The first reading: they fear Him although they do not see Him.* On this reading, *bi-al-ghayb* is parallel to the description of the believers at the opening of Sūrah al-Baqarah:

> *"Who believe in the unseen (bi-al-ghayb), and establish prayer, and spend from what We have provided them."* (Qur'an 2:3)

The excellence named here is that of believing in a God one has not seen, on the strength of a report one trusts, and living by the consequences of that belief. Every human relationship of any depth contains this element: a person commits to a marriage, a friend, or a cause on the basis of a limited sample and a great deal of trust. Faith is that structure applied to God, and the verse honors it.

*The second reading: they fear Him in secret, when no one else sees them.* On this reading the excellence is sincerity — the private obedience that no one witnesses and no one rewards. This is the harder of the two, and it is the direct opposite of the hypocrisy the Qur'an condemns at length:

> *"And when they stand for prayer, they stand lazily, showing off to people, and they do not remember God except a little."* (Qur'an 4:142)

The two readings are not in competition; they are two dimensions of the same quality. The person of *khashyah* believes in what he cannot see, and obeys when no one is looking. Together they describe a conscience that runs whether or not it is monitored — an internal rather than an external regulator.

The Prophet ﷺ named such people directly. Among the seven whom God will shade on the Day when there is no shade but His shade, he listed:

> *"...and a man who remembered God in solitude, and his eyes overflowed [with tears]."* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031)

Note the detail: *in solitude*. Not in the mosque, not in a gathering of remembrance, not where the weeping might be seen and admired — alone.

**The Parallel Verse in Sūrah Yā Sīn**

This verse has a near-twin, and comparing the two is instructive:

> *"You can only warn one who follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate unseen (khashiya al-Raḥmāna bi-al-ghayb); so give him glad tidings of forgiveness and a noble reward (bi-maghfiratin wa-ajrin karīm)."* (Qur'an 36:11)

The two verses share the structure exactly: fear of God in the unseen, followed by forgiveness and a reward. The difference is in the adjectives. In Yā Sīn the reward is *karīm*, noble and generous — an adjective that in Arabic also means noble in lineage and character, suggesting a gift given with elegance and without grudging. In al-Mulk the reward is *kabīr*, great — an adjective of magnitude. The one emphasizes the *quality* of the giving, the other the *size* of what is given. Read together, they promise a reward that is both immense and bestowed with generosity.

A third passage adds a further detail about the person who fears God unseen:

> *"[For] him who fears the standing before his Lord are two Gardens."* (Qur'an 55:46)

**Maghfirah: The Covering**

*Maghfirah* comes from *gh-f-r*, whose root meaning is to cover or conceal. The same root gives *maghfar*, the helmet a warrior wears — the thing that covers and protects the head. The theological point embedded in the word is that forgiveness is not merely the cancellation of a debt recorded in a ledger; it is a *covering*. The sin is not erased from existence and then left as a naked fact; it is veiled.

This has a direct consequence for how a person understands his own past. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The one who repents from sin is like the one who has no sin."* (Ibn Mājah 4250 — graded ḥasan)

And in a ḥadīth qudsī, God's response to genuine repentance is described in terms that exceed erasure:

> *"O son of Adam, as long as you call upon Me and hope in Me, I will forgive you for whatever you have done, and I will not mind. O son of Adam, even if your sins were to reach the height of the sky, and then you sought forgiveness from Me, I would forgive you. O son of Adam, if you were to come to Me with sins nearly as great as the earth, and then you met Me not associating anything with Me, I would come to you with forgiveness nearly as great as it."* (al-Tirmidhī 3540 — graded ḥasan)

The verse's promise of *maghfirah* is therefore not a technicality. It is the announcement that the thing a person most fears about himself — his own record — is coverable.

**Ajr Kabīr: The Great Reward**

*Wa-ajrun kabīr* — "and a great reward." *Ajr* is a wage, a recompense for work done; the word is used for the payment of a laborer and for the reward of a deed. The use of wage-language is significant: it states that the reward is a matter of justice as well as grace, promised by One who does not break a promise.

The commentators, including al-Qurṭubī and al-Shawkānī, identified the great reward as Paradise. Al-Bayḍāwī added a psychological observation worth recording: the person who holds God in awe in the unseen finds that the pleasures of this world shrink in his estimation. The reward is great partly because the competition has become small. A person who is genuinely awed by God cannot simultaneously be impressed by the things that impress the people around him, and that freedom is itself part of the reward.

**The Social Logic of Fearing God Unseen**

There is a sociological principle embedded in this verse that deserves to be stated plainly, because it is one of the most practically important ideas in the Qur'an.

Every functioning society needs compliance with rules when enforcement is absent. Police cannot be everywhere; auditors cannot watch every transaction; cameras cannot see every room. A society that relies entirely on external enforcement must spend enormous resources on surveillance, and it will still fail, because surveillance changes behavior only where it is known to be present.

The person of *khashyah bi-al-ghayb* solves this problem from the inside. He is honest on the invoice no one checks. He returns the excess change. He does not take credit for work he did not do. He keeps the secret he was trusted with after the person who trusted him has died. He does not exploit the customer who cannot read the contract. And he does all of this for a reason no inspector could verify: because he believes he is seen by One he cannot see.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the social consequence of this quality in the negative form, describing its absence as the sign of a collapsing order. He said that among the signs of the hypocrite are these:

> *"The signs of the hypocrite are three: when he speaks, he lies; when he promises, he breaks it; and when he is entrusted, he betrays the trust."* (al-Bukhārī 33; Muslim 59)

And in another version:

> *"...and when he is trusted, he betrays; and when he disputes, he is abusive."* (al-Bukhārī 34; Muslim 58)

Every one of those failures is a failure of unseen conduct. The liar lies because the truth is inconvenient and the lie is undetectable; the betrayer of a trust takes what was given to him because the giver cannot compel its return. The person of *khashyah* is the structural opposite: reliable precisely where he cannot be checked.

**How Khashyah Is Acquired**

The tradition did not treat *khashyah* as a temperament one is born with. It identified specific means by which it is grown, and they are all available.

*Knowledge.* As 35:28 establishes, awe follows understanding. The more a person learns of God's names, His creation, and His law, the more rooted the awe becomes.

*Recitation.* The Qur'an describes its own effect on the person of faith:

> *"God has sent down the best of speech: a Book consistent, oft-repeated, whereat shiver the skins of those who fear their Lord; then their skins and their hearts soften to the remembrance of God."* (Qur'an 39:23)

The sequence is precise: first the shiver, then the softening. The awe comes first and the tenderness follows, which is the opposite of the sequence people often assume.

*Remembrance of death.* The Prophet ﷺ prescribed it as a means of recalibration:

> *"Remember often the destroyer of pleasures — death."* (al-Tirmidhī 2307 — graded ḥasan)

*A realistic view of one's own record.* The Prophet ﷺ drew the contrast between two ways of seeing one's sins:

> *"The believer sees his sins as though he were sitting beneath a mountain, fearing that it may fall upon him; while the profligate person sees his sins as though they were flies passing over his nose."* (al-Bukhārī 6308)

*Solitude.* Not the loneliness of isolation but the deliberate practice of being alone with God — the condition the hadith of the seven shaded identifies. A person who never spends time where no one can see him will never know whether his religion is for God or for the people around him.

**The Promise and the Warning in One Line**

The verse ends where it began, holding warning and promise in a single sentence. The people of *khashyah* are promised forgiveness — which implies that they have sins to be forgiven. They are not sinless; they are people whose sins are covered because they turned to the One who covers. And the verse follows immediately upon the description of the damned, which means the contrast is the intended reading: two groups heard the same warning, and the difference between them was not intelligence, opportunity, or circumstance. It was what they did with what they heard while no one was watching.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:13

> **Keep your speech secret or proclaim it; truly He knows what lies within breasts.**

**Arabic**

> *وَأَسِرُّوا قَوْلَكُمْ أَوِ اجْهَرُوا بِهِ ۖ إِنَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ*

**Transliteration:** *Wa-asirrū qawlakum awi-jharū bihi; innahu ʿalīmun bi-dhāti al-ṣudūr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Occasion of Revelation**

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Wāḥidī transmit from Ibn ʿAbbās the circumstances behind this verse. The idolaters of Makkah used to speak against the Prophet ﷺ in their gatherings, and then, fearing that news of it would reach him, they would say to one another:

> *"Lower your voices, lest the God of Muḥammad hear you."*

The verse came as the answer: whether you whisper or shout, it makes no difference to the One who hears. The mockery in their caution was that they wanted to hide from divine knowledge by lowering their voices, as though knowledge were a matter of volume. The verse corrects the premise, not the decibel level.

The lesson generalizes. Human beings instinctively modulate their behavior according to who they think can detect it — and the Qur'an's consistent response is to point out that the primary Observer is not affected by walls, distance, darkness, or encryption.

**Two Opposite Verbs, One Indifference**

*Asirrū* comes from *s-r-r*, the root of *sirr*, "secret," and it carries the sense of something concealed within the self — the same root produces *sarīrah*, the inner life, and *sarāʾir*, the hidden things that the Qur'an says will be tested on the Day of Resurrection. *Ijharū* comes from *j-h-r*, to make open, public, and audible; the same root gives *jahr*, the audible recitation of the Qur'an as opposed to the silent one.

The verse sets the two as alternatives and then declares the outcome identical. Grammatically the verbs are imperatives, but the sense is conditional: "whether you keep it secret or declare it openly." The point is not that one is commanded and the other forbidden; it is that the distinction that matters so much to human beings — private versus public — is meaningless to God.

The same idea is stated with even greater precision elsewhere:

> *"And if you speak aloud, then indeed He knows the secret and what is more hidden (akhfā). Truly, I know the unseen of the heavens and the earth."* (Qur'an 20:7)

Note the comparative *akhfā*, "more hidden" — there is a level of concealment deeper than a secret, namely the thought before it becomes speech, and even the stirring before it becomes a thought. Nothing is beyond the reach of the knowledge being described here.

**"What Lies Within Breasts"**

*Dhāti al-ṣudūr* is a striking phrase. Literally, "that which is in the breasts" — *ṣudūr* being the plural of *ṣadr*, the chest. Arabic places the seat of the inner life in the chest rather than the head: the *ṣadr* is where intention, resolve, love, jealousy, and doubt reside. The phrase *dhātu al-ṣudūr* means the contents of that interior space.

The expression occurs repeatedly in the Qur'an — 3:119, 3:154, 5:7, 8:43, 11:5, 29:10, 31:23, 35:38, 39:7, 42:24, 57:6, and 64:4 — and its frequency is itself part of the teaching. The Qur'an is not merely claiming that God knows human actions; it is claiming that He knows the interior of the person who performs them. The claim is stated so often because it is the one most easily forgotten in practice.

**The Crucial Limit: Knowledge Is Not the Same as Punishment**

Here a distinction of the first importance must be drawn, because it is one of the places where Islamic theology is most humane and most often misunderstood.

God's knowledge extends to every thought. But accountability does not. The passing thought, the unbidden image, the flicker of envy, the whisper of doubt that arrives unbeckoned — none of these is a sin, and none of them is recorded against a person. The Prophet ﷺ stated this explicitly, in a report the scholars describe as among the most merciful in the entire tradition:

> *"Verily, God has pardoned for my community that which arises within their souls (mā ḥaddathat bihi anfusahā) as long as they do not speak of it or act upon it."* (al-Bukhārī 5269; Muslim 127)

This is not a minor exemption. Human interior life is, by nature, chaotic. Thoughts surface unbidden from temperament, fatigue, suggestion, and spiritual attack; a person who is held morally responsible for every content of consciousness would be responsible for things he did not choose and could not prevent. The Prophet ﷺ described a group of people who came to him tormented by exactly this fear — they found the thoughts in their hearts too terrible to mention aloud — and his answer was to locate the problem outside them:

> *"That is clear faith."* (Muslim 132)

That is: the very horror they felt at such thoughts was the proof that the thoughts were not theirs in any culpable sense. What terrifies the faithful heart is, by its presence, evidence of the heart's health.

**The Asymmetry of Mercy**

The tradition goes further still and describes an asymmetry that is worth stating carefully, because it is one of the clearest expressions of the divine character in the whole religion.

The Prophet ﷺ reported from his Lord:

> *"Verily God has written down the good deeds and the bad deeds... Whoever intends to do a good deed and does not do it, God records it with Himself as a complete good deed. And if he intends it and does it, God records it with Himself as ten good deeds, up to seven hundred times, up to many times over. And whoever intends to do a bad deed and does not do it, God records it with Himself as a complete good deed. And if he intends it and does it, God records it as one bad deed."* (al-Bukhārī 6491; Muslim 131)

Read that slowly. An unperformed good intention is recorded in full. An abandoned bad intention is recorded as a good deed. And a completed bad deed is recorded as one, single bad deed — never multiplied. The ratio is not merely generous; it is structurally tilted.

This is the framework within which verse 13 must be read. Yes, God knows what is in the breasts — and the One who knows is the One who has built this asymmetry into the ledger.

**The Ethical Consequence: The Tongue**

If the interior is known, then the exterior is certainly known, and the Qur'an and Sunnah treat the tongue as the organ most in need of governance. The Prophet ﷺ gave the governing rule:

> *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good, or let him be silent."* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47)

Note the structure of the instruction. It does not say "speak good, and do not speak evil." It says: speak good, *or be silent*. Silence is presented as a live and honorable option — the default setting, in effect. And he identified the tongue as the single greatest source of ruin:

> *Muʿādh ibn Jabal asked: "O Messenger of God, will we be held accountable for what we say?" He replied: "May your mother be bereaved of you, O Muʿādh! Is there anything that throws people into the Fire on their faces — or, he said, on their noses — other than the harvest of their tongues?"* (al-Tirmidhī 2616 — graded ṣaḥīḥ)

The specific sin the Qur'an singles out is backbiting, defined by the Prophet ﷺ with a precision that forecloses every evasion:

> *"Do you know what backbiting is?" They said: "God and His Messenger know best." He said: "Mentioning your brother in a way he would dislike." It was said: "What if what I say about my brother is true?" He said: "If it is true of him, you have backbitten him; and if it is not true of him, you have slandered him."* (Muslim 2589)

The verse's relevance to this is direct. Backbiting almost always happens in the absence of the person discussed, which is to say, in the mode of *sirr*. The verse removes the premise that secrecy makes it safe.

**The Illusion of the Private Channel**

Modern life has produced an unprecedented apparatus for the *sirr* — private messages, disappearing messages, encrypted applications, anonymous accounts, and the quiet assumption that what is sent can be unsent. The technology has changed the vocabulary but not the reality.

The Qur'an's position is simple and would be unchanged if it were revealed today: there is no channel that is private from the One who created the channel. And this is meant not primarily as a threat but as a liberation, because it resolves two opposite pathologies at once.

*The first is the performance.* A great deal of modern exhaustion comes from curating a self for an audience — the public statements, the careful phrasing, the managed impression. The person who internalizes this verse is released from the need to maintain a version of himself, because the only Audience that matters already has the full picture and is not impressed by the edit.

*The second is the paranoia.* If there is no true privacy, the anxious conclusion is that one is perpetually exposed to hostile observation. But the verse assigns the observation to God, and the Name that governs the next verse is *al-Laṭīf*, the Gentle. The One who sees is not a prosecutor looking for a case; He is the One who, as the tradition puts it, *"extends His Hand by night so that the one who sinned by day may repent."*

**Integrity: The Unified Self**

The psychological fruit of this verse is integrity in the literal sense — the integration of the public and private self into a single person.

The Qur'an's word for the failure of that integration is *nifāq*, hypocrisy, and it is treated as more dangerous than open disbelief because it involves a structural split. The hypocrite's exterior and interior are wired to different masters, and the strain of maintaining the split produces the instability the Qur'an describes so precisely:

> *"And when they meet those who believe, they say: 'We believe'; but when they are alone with their devils, they say: 'We are with you; we were only mocking.'"* (Qur'an 2:14)

A person who lives by verse 13 is, by contrast, the same person in every setting. He does not need to remember which version of a story he told to whom. He does not fear the leaked message, because the message was written in a form he would defend. He is not managing an archive.

The tradition valued this quality so highly that the Prophet ﷺ was asked directly about it:

> *A man asked: "O Messenger of God, what is salvation?" He said: "That your tongue be true, your heart be sound, and your character be upright."* (Musnad Aḥmad; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī)

**The Comfortable Half of the Verse**

Finally, the verse has a consoling side that is easy to miss when it is read only as a warning.

If God knows what is in the breasts, then He knows the struggle that no one sees: the person fighting a private temptation, the one who gives in secret, the one who forgives an injury that no one witnessed, the one whose outward compliance is easy but whose inward surrender is hard-won. The tradition is emphatic that this hidden labor is seen and rewarded:

> *"Seven shall God shade on the Day when there is no shade but His shade... and a man who gave charity and concealed it, such that his left hand did not know what his right hand gave."* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031)

> *"And a man who remembered God in solitude, and his eyes overflowed."* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031)

The verse cuts both ways, and it is meant to. It is a warning to the one who plans harm in the dark, and it is a promise to the one who does good in the dark. Both are equally observed, and — as the next verse will establish — both are known by the One whose knowledge reaches even the subtlest detail.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:14

> **Does He Who created not know? He is the Subtle, the Aware.**

**Arabic**

> *أَلَا يَعْلَمُ مَنْ خَلَقَ ۖ وَهُوَ اللَّطِيفُ الْخَبِيرُ*

**Transliteration:** *A-lā yaʿlamu man khalaqa wa-huwa al-laṭīfu al-khabīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Carries Its Own Answer**

*A-lā yaʿlamu man khalaqa* — "Does He Who created not know?" The form is interrogative, but in Arabic — as in most languages — a question of this shape is an emphatic assertion. The expected answer is not "perhaps not" but "of course He knows; how could He not?"

What makes this more than rhetoric is the argument compressed inside it. The claim is not merely that God is knowing; it is that *creating something entails knowing it*. The maker of a thing knows what he made, in a way and to a degree that no observer can match. An engineer knows his machine because he chose every tolerance and every joint. A writer knows his book because he decided every word. If this is true of human makers, whose knowledge is acquired and partial, it is true a fortiori of the Maker whose knowledge precedes the thing and whose will is what brought it into existence.

The commentators framed the argument in several ways. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr record that the verse is to be read as: "Does He not know what He has created, when He is the Subtle, the Aware?" — that is, does the Creator lack knowledge of His own creation? Al-Qurṭubī and others transmitted a second reading: "Does he [the disbeliever] not know Who it is that creates, when He is the Subtle, the Aware?" — placing the ignorance on the human side. Both readings are preserved, and they point in the same direction: the knowledge belongs to God, and the failure to recognize it is a defect in man.

**The Verse as a Refutation of a Philosophical Doctrine**

There is a specific intellectual background to this verse that the later commentators addressed at length, and it is worth recovering because it shows the verse functioning as a genuine argument rather than a pious flourish.

A doctrine inherited from Aristotle and developed by the Islamic philosophers (*falāsifa*) — among them al-Fārābī and Ibn Sīnā — held that God knows things only *universally* (*kulliyyāt*), not as particulars (*juzʾiyyāt*). On this view, God knows the species "human" but not this particular human being; He knows the law of falling bodies but not this falling body. The motive behind the doctrine was philosophically serious: knowledge of particulars was thought to entail change in the knower, and change in God was taken to be impossible.

Al-Ghazzālī devoted a chapter of his *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* (*The Incoherence of the Philosophers*) to refuting exactly this, and he ruled that the doctrine constitutes disbelief because it contradicts the Qur'an's plain, repeated, and emphatic affirmation that God knows all things in their particularity. He listed it as one of three positions for which the philosophers must be declared outside Islam — the other two being the eternity of the world and the denial of bodily resurrection.

Ibn Taymiyyah took up the same argument in his *Darʾ Taʿāruḍ al-ʿAql wa al-Naql*, where he dismantled the premise rather than the conclusion: the philosophers' argument assumed that knowledge is a passive state affected by its object, whereas God's knowledge is not like created knowledge at all. He knows particulars without being changed by them, because His knowledge is eternal and His relationship to time is not the relationship of a being inside it.

This verse is one of the texts at the center of that debate, and its logic is compact: the One who *created* this particular thing — this heart, this whisper, this hidden intention — necessarily knows it. A knowledge that produced a thing in its full particularity cannot be ignorant of it afterward.

**Al-Laṭīf: Subtlety and Gentleness in One Name**

*Wa-huwa al-laṭīfu al-khabīr* — the verse closes with two Divine Names that together describe the scope and the character of God's knowledge.

*Al-Laṭīf* comes from *l-ṭ-f*, whose root meaning is fineness, subtlety, refinement — the opposite of coarseness and bulk. From it come *luṭf*, an act of subtle kindness; *alṭāf*, the imperceptible favors by which God arranges affairs; and *laṭīf* as an adjective describing both the minute and the gentle.

The commentators derived two complementary senses from the root, and the tradition has always held both together.

*The first sense is epistemic: al-Laṭīf knows the subtlest things.* Nothing is too small, too hidden, too intricate, or too fleeting to escape Him. The Qur'an states this with an image of extraordinary delicacy, in the advice of Luqmān to his son:

> *"O my son! Indeed, if it should be the weight of a mustard seed — and it were within a rock, or in the heavens, or in the earth — God will bring it forth. Truly God is Subtle, Aware."* (Qur'an 31:16)

A mustard seed inside a rock: the smallest available object inside the densest available container. The point is not merely that God can find it; it is that He was never not aware of it.

*The second sense is relational: al-Laṭīf is gentle and kind to His servants.* He provides through channels so subtle that the recipient often does not notice them until much later. The Qur'an uses the Name in exactly this sense:

> *"God is Gentle (Laṭīf) with His servants; He provides for whom He wills; and He is the Strong, the Mighty."* (Qur'an 42:19)

And the Prophet Joseph, at the moment of his reunion with his father and brothers after decades of separation, betrayal, and imprisonment, described his entire ordeal in a single phrase built on this Name:

> *"Truly my Lord is Subtle (Laṭīf) in bringing about what He wills. Truly He is the Knowing, the Wise."* (Qur'an 12:100)

That verse is the definitive commentary on the Name. Joseph's life had been, by any external measure, a catastrophe: thrown in a well, sold, enslaved, falsely accused, imprisoned, forgotten. And at the end of it, standing in power in Egypt with his family restored to him, he did not describe his rescue as a reversal of the story. He described the *whole story* as a single act of *luṭf*. The Name means, among other things, that God's management of a life is far more intricate than the life's owner can see from inside it.

**Al-Khabīr: Knowledge of the Inner Reality**

*Al-Khabīr* comes from *kh-b-r*, which means to be thoroughly informed of the inner reality of something — not its surface report but the way it actually is. From the same root come *khabar*, news or report, and *ikhtibār*, testing, which is the process by which a thing's inner reality is made manifest.

Al-Qurṭubī, following earlier authorities, distinguished the two Names in a way worth preserving: *al-Laṭīf* refers to God's knowledge of the minutest and most subtle details, while *al-Khabīr* refers to His knowledge of things before they come into existence — knowledge that is not a response to the thing's appearance but is prior to it. Others described *al-Khabīr* as the One who knows the hidden as He knows the manifest, and the unseen as He knows the witnessed.

Together, the two Names cover the full range: nothing is too small (al-Laṭīf) and nothing is too far off or too far ahead (al-Khabīr).

**The Argument Restated as a Proof of Resurrection**

It is important to see what this verse is doing within the sūrah's overall argument. The Makkan audience did not deny that God created. What they denied was that He would raise the dead — and, more specifically, that He knows and records the particular deeds of particular people in order to judge them.

The verse is a direct answer to that denial, and the logic runs in two steps. First: the One who originated a thing in all its particularity knows that thing completely (this verse). Second: the One who knows it completely can restore it completely. The Qur'an makes the second step explicit elsewhere:

> *"Does man think that We shall not assemble his bones? Indeed, We are able to restore his very fingertips."* (Qur'an 75:3–4)

> *"And he strikes for Us a comparison, and forgets his own creation. He says: 'Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?' Say: 'He will give them life Who produced them the first time; and He is, of all creation, Knowing.'"* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

The last clause of 36:79 is the same argument as this verse: the Creator knows, and therefore the Creator can re-create.

**God's Knowledge Does Not Compel**

A necessary clarification. To say that God knows all things is not to say that human beings are coerced. The Islamic position, as worked out against both the determinists and the rationalists, is that God's eternal knowledge of a person's choice is not the cause of that choice. God knows what a person will freely choose, and His knowing it does not make it necessary in the way that an external compulsion would.

This is difficult to picture, and the tradition has generally discouraged pressing into it. The analogy the theologians sometimes used is that of a person who knows, from long acquaintance, exactly how his friend will respond in a given situation. His foreknowledge is perfect and it is not the cause of the response. If that is conceivable for a creature whose knowledge is acquired, it is more so for a Creator whose knowledge is eternal and whose relationship to time is not that of an observer waiting for events.

The practical upshot is what matters, and the Qur'an states it plainly: God's knowledge does not remove responsibility, because the human being experiences his own choice as his own — and verse 10 of this sūrah showed the damned themselves accepting exactly that.

**The Modern Face of Being Known**

Contemporary life has made the experience of being observed newly vivid. Surveillance cameras, transaction records, location data, browsing histories, and the permanent archive of social media mean that a person in a modern city is recorded more thoroughly than any subject of any pre-modern state. The dominant emotional response to this is anxiety, and it is a reasonable response: surveillance is generally undertaken by parties whose interest is not the subject's welfare, and the data is used to predict, nudge, and profit.

This verse reframes the same fact in the opposite emotional key. The One who knows is *al-Laṭīf* — the Gentle. His knowledge is not extraction; it is care. The difference is not in the completeness of the knowledge but in the character of the Knower. A doctor who knows your whole history is not a threat; a stranger who knows one thing about you may be. The Qur'an's argument is that the Knower is the One who made you, and the One who made you is, by His own Name, gentle with what He has made.

For a person who believes this, the consequences are concrete. He can be honest with God, because pretending is pointless. He can be honest with himself, because the self-deception that works on others works on the Knower not at all. And he can be at peace in situations where he is genuinely unseen and unrecorded by the world, because the only Witness he needs is present.

**What to Do With the Subtle, the Aware**

The Name carries a specific instruction. If God's *luṭf* operates through channels too subtle to trace, then a person should expect help to arrive in ways he did not plan and from directions he did not anticipate — and he should not treat the unexpected turn of events as unrelated to his prayer. The tradition has a maxim for this: *man laṭafa bi-ʿibādihi fīmā lā yaʿlamūn, wa-dabbara lahum mā lā yahtadūn* — He is subtle with His servants in ways they do not know, and He manages for them what they could not have arranged.

The Prophet ﷺ taught his community to ask for this quality by name. In the supplication of distress that he taught ʿAlī:

> *"There is no god but God, the Clement, the Generous. Glory be to God and blessed is God, Lord of the Mighty Throne. Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds. O God, I ask You for that which obligates Your mercy and [the means of] Your forgiveness..."* (al-Ṭabarānī — its full text is longer and its chain is discussed)

and in the Qur'an's own words, spoken by Abraham when he was cast into the fire:

> *"God is sufficient for us, and excellent is the Disposer of affairs."* (Qur'an 3:173)

The two Names in this verse are the ground of that trust. Nothing is hidden from Him, so nothing about your situation has escaped His notice. And nothing is too subtle for Him, so nothing about the way forward is beyond His arrangement.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:15

> **He is the One Who made the earth tractable for you; so travel the open roads thereof and eat of His provision. And unto Him is the Resurrection.**

**Arabic**

> *هُوَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ الْأَرْضَ ذَلُولًا فَامْشُوا فِي مَنَاكِبِهَا وَكُلُوا مِنْ رِزْقِهِ ۖ وَإِلَيْهِ النُّشُورُ*

**Transliteration:** *Huwa alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-arḍa dhalūlan fa-imshū fī manākibihā wa-kulū min rizqihi wa-ilayhi al-nushūr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dhalūl: A Tamed Earth**

The word *dhalūl* comes from *dh-l-l*, a root that carries the sense of lowliness, yielding, and submission. In the Arabic of the desert, a *dhalūl* is specifically a camel that has been broken in and trained — an animal that kneels on command, accepts a rider, and goes where it is directed, as opposed to the refractory beast that throws its rider and bolts. To call the earth *dhalūl* is to say that the ground beneath a human being has been made manageable: it yields to the plough, holds a foundation, carries a road, holds water where it is dug, and submits to being shaped.

This is a striking claim theologically, because the earth is enormous and human beings are small. Left to itself, the ground is not obviously docile — it shakes, it floods, it hardens, it erupts, and it swallows. The verse's premise is that its docility is not a property of the earth but a property of what God has done to it, and that the docility is maintained rather than automatic. Verse 16 will make exactly that point: the same earth that is *dhalūl* can be commanded to swallow.

The Qur'an states the same gift in several other places, and each adds a dimension:

> *"And God has made the earth for you a carpet (bisāṭan), that you may travel therein upon spreading roads."* (Qur'an 71:19–20)

> *"It is He Who made the earth subservient (dhalūlan) to you, so travel in its tracts and eat of His provision; and unto Him is the Resurrection."* (Qur'an 67:15)

> *"[Pharaoh said:] 'Who is your Lord, O Moses?' He said: 'Our Lord is He Who gave everything its creation and then guided it.'"* (Qur'an 20:50)

> *"Who made the earth a cradle for you (mahdan), and inserted roads therein for you, and sent down water from the sky, and brought forth thereby pairs of various plants."* (Qur'an 20:53)

> *"Who made the earth a cradle (mahd) for you and made roads therein for you, that you may be guided."* (Qur'an 43:10)

The metaphor shifts from a tamed mount to a carpet to a cradle — an object made manageable, an object made comfortable, and an object made to hold a child. All three belong together: the world is made to carry us, to receive us, and to be rested upon.

**Manākib: The Shoulders of the Earth**

*Fa-imshū fī manākibihā* — "so walk in its manākib." The word *mankib* means, in its primary sense, the shoulder joint — the place where the upper arm meets the body, the strongest and broadest part of a person. Its plural, *manākib*, is used for the broad sides, the flanks, the highlands, and the outlying tracts of a region.

The commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī, explained it as the sides and extremities of the earth — its mountains, its elevated regions, and its far reaches — and some noted the aptness of the metaphor: just as a person carries loads upon his shoulders, so the earth bears travelers upon its broad surfaces. The instruction is therefore not merely "walk on it" but "range across it, over its shoulders and its high passes."

That breadth matters, because it rules out a narrow reading of the verse. The command is not "stay where you were born and cultivate your plot." It is: go out into the earth, widely. And the purpose named is *rizq* — provision — which implies commerce, labor, agriculture, and the whole apparatus by which human beings earn a living from the world they have been placed in.

**Eating of "His" Provision**

*Wa-kulū min rizqihi* — "and eat of His provision." The possessive is the point. The food is described as *His* before it is described as anyone's earnings. A person's labor is real and is honored, but it is not the ultimate cause of what he eats; it is the means through which the Provider delivers what He has already apportioned.

The Qur'an sets the same condition repeatedly — the provision must be sought, but it must be sought lawfully:

> *"O mankind! Eat of what is on the earth, lawful and good, and follow not the footsteps of Satan."* (Qur'an 2:168)

> *"So eat of what God has provided you, lawful and good, and be grateful for the favor of God, if it is Him that you worship."* (Qur'an 16:114)

The pairing of *ḥalālan ṭayyiban* — lawful and good — is significant. Lawful (*ḥalāl*) is a legal category; good (*ṭayyib*) is a qualitative one. The Qur'an's interest is not only in whether the transaction was legal but in whether the thing itself is decent: the food, the labor conditions, the honesty of the trade, and whether the earning harmed anyone.

The Prophet ﷺ placed lawful earning at the very top of religious practice:

> *"No one has ever eaten food better than that which he eats from the work of his own hands; and indeed the Prophet of God, David, used to eat from the work of his own hands."* (al-Bukhārī 2072)

> *"The upper hand is better than the lower hand; the upper hand is the one that gives, and the lower hand is the one that takes."* (al-Bukhārī 1429; Muslim 1033)

And he tied the seeking of one's daily bread to the fear of God in a single instruction:

> *"Seeking what is lawful is an obligation upon every Muslim."* (reported by al-Ṭabarānī from Ibn Masʿūd; a number of scholars have strengthened it through its supporting chains)

**The Two Kinds of Travel**

The Qur'an's command to travel occurs in two distinct modes, and this verse participates in both.

*The first is material travel*: to seek provision, to trade, to migrate for opportunity, to reach water and pasture. This is the sense most immediately present here, since the command is followed by "and eat of His provision." The earth was made passable so that people could move across it and live from it.

*The second is reflective travel*: to observe, to learn, to take warning. The Qur'an returns to this constantly, and the two great purposes of reflection that it names are the transience of worldly power and the power of God to originate and resurrect:

> *"Say: 'Journey through the earth and observe how He originated creation. Then God will produce the final genesis. Truly God is Powerful over all things.'"* (Qur'an 29:20)

> *"Have they not journeyed through the earth and seen what was the end of those before them? They were greater than them in power, and they tilled the earth and cultivated it more than these have cultivated it, and their messengers came to them with clear proofs..."* (Qur'an 30:9; cf. 3:137; 6:11; 12:109; 16:36; 27:69; 30:42; 35:44; 40:21, 82; 47:10)

> *"Have they not journeyed through the earth, so that they might have hearts with which to understand, or ears with which to hear? For indeed it is not the eyes that are blind, but blind are the hearts within the breasts."* (Qur'an 22:46)

The two modes are not separate. The same journey that earns a living can be a journey of reflection, and the Qur'an clearly intends the believer to be doing both at once: working with his hands and observing with his heart.

**"And unto Him Is the Resurrection"**

The final clause, *wa-ilayhi al-nushūr*, reframes everything that preceded it. *Nushūr* comes from *n-sh-r*, to spread out, to scatter, and then to bring back to life — the resurrection being the scattering of the dead from their graves and their restoration to life.

The placement of this clause at the end of a verse about travel and eating is the sūrah's characteristic move. It permits the world and then immediately places a boundary around it. Work — but you will return. Eat — but you will be asked. Travel the shoulders of the earth — but the journey ends somewhere other than where you hoped.

The Prophet ﷺ taught this orientation as a way of life, and his instruction to Ibn ʿUmar is the classic statement:

> *"Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveler passing through."* And Ibn ʿUmar used to say: *"When you reach the evening, do not expect the morning; and when you reach the morning, do not expect the evening. Take from your health for your illness, and from your life for your death."* (al-Bukhārī 6416)

Note that this is not a call to leave the world. The verse has just commanded travel and eating; the Prophet ﷺ himself traded, farmed, married, governed, and fought. The instruction concerns the *posture* of the heart toward what is being used: a traveler uses the road without building on it, and a stranger uses the city without pretending it is home.

**The Stewardship Reading**

There is a further implication of *dhalūl* that the tradition has drawn out and that deserves emphasis. The earth being made tractable is not the same as the earth being handed over without conditions. The Qur'an pairs the gift of the earth with the language of vicegerency and trust:

> *"And [remember] when your Lord said to the angels: 'Indeed I am placing upon the earth a vicegerent (khalīfah).'"* (Qur'an 2:30)

> *"And it is He Who has made you vicegerents (khalāʾif) upon the earth and raised some of you above others in degrees, that He may try you in what He has given you."* (Qur'an 6:165)

And it forbids the corruption of what has been entrusted:

> *"And do not cause corruption upon the earth after its reformation, and call upon Him in fear and hope. Truly the mercy of God is near to those who do good."* (Qur'an 7:56)

> *"Corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what the hands of people have earned, that He may let them taste some of what they have done, so that perhaps they may return."* (Qur'an 30:41)

The logic of these verses is the opposite of both extremes. The earth is not divine, so it may be used; and it is not abandoned, so it may not be abused. A *dhalūl* belongs to someone, and the one who uses it will be asked about it.

**Two Practical Errors the Verse Corrects**

The verse is positioned, as so much of the Qur'an is, against two opposite mistakes.

*The first is the denial of the world* — the notion that spiritual seriousness requires withdrawing from earning, traveling, family, and ordinary life. The verse forbids this by commanding travel and eating. The Prophet ﷺ explicitly rejected the monastic impulse when some of his Companions proposed it:

> *"As for me, I fast and I break my fast, I pray and I sleep, and I marry women. Whoever turns away from my Sunnah is not of me."* (al-Bukhārī 5063; Muslim 1401)

*The second is absorption in the world* — the condition in which earning becomes the whole of life and the Resurrection is a word one affirms but never weighs. The verse forbids this with its closing clause.

The balanced reading is the one Ibn al-Qayyim described when he wrote that the heart's relationship to the world should be like that of a traveler's body to the shade of a tree: he rests in it for a while, and then he moves on.

**The Gratitude the Verse Invites**

Finally, the verse is constructed to produce a specific emotion: gratitude for something so ubiquitous it is invisible. No one wakes in the morning and gives thanks that the ground is solid, that roads exist, that the soil will respond to seed, that the journey is possible at all. These are the background conditions of every human life, and because they are background they go unnoticed.

The Prophet ﷺ taught a definition of true wealth that cuts directly against that forgetfulness:

> *"Whoever among you wakes up secure in his dwelling, healthy in his body, and having his food for the day — it is as though the whole world has been gathered for him."* (al-Tirmidhī 2346; Ibn Mājah 4141 — graded ḥasan)

Three items: safety, health, and the day's bread. Each corresponds to an element of this verse — the tractable earth (safety), the ability to travel and work (health), and the provision (bread). The person who has all three has, by the Prophet's measure, everything; and the person who recognizes that he has them has begun to do what so few people do, which is give thanks.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:16

> **Do you feel secure that He Who is in Heaven will not cause the earth to engulf you while it churns?**

**Arabic**

> *أَأَمِنْتُمْ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاءِ أَنْ يَخْسِفَ بِكُمُ الْأَرْضَ فَإِذَا هِيَ تَمُورُ*

**Transliteration:** *A-amintum man fī al-samāʾi an yakhsifa bikumu al-arḍa fa-idhā hiya tamūr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Undoes the Previous Verse's Comfort**

Verse 15 had just described the earth as *dhalūl* — tractable, tamed, made to carry human beings. It would be possible to read that description and conclude that the ground is simply *like that*: stable, available, and reliably beneath one's feet. This verse forecloses that conclusion in a single question.

The same earth that was made tractable can be commanded to open. Its docility is not its nature; it is God's ongoing act. The moment that act is withheld, the ground beneath a person becomes the agent of his destruction. Theologically, this is the doctrine that the world is sustained moment by moment rather than self-sustaining — what the tradition calls the continuous dependence of creation upon its Sustainer.

**A-amintum: "Do You Feel Secure?"**

The opening word is the interrogative particle *a-* attached to the verb *amintum*, "you are secure." The question is a rebuke, and the thing being rebuked is a specific spiritual condition that the Qur'an names and condemns: *al-amn min makr Allāh*, feeling safe from God's plan.

The Qur'an treats this condition with unexpected severity:

> *"Do they then feel secure against the plan of God? None feels secure from the plan of God except the people who are lost."* (Qur'an 7:99)

That is a strong statement, and it is frequently misunderstood. It does not mean that a believer should live in anxiety; despair of God's mercy is condemned just as firmly. What it means is that a person should never settle into the assumption that his present safety is his by right. Security is a gift, and the gift can be withdrawn. The opposite error — *al-qunūṭ min rahmat Allāh*, despairing of God's mercy — is condemned in the same breath elsewhere:

> *"And who despairs of the mercy of his Lord except those who are astray?"* (Qur'an 15:56)

The believer's posture, as the classical scholars formulated it, is to travel between fear and hope like a bird between two wings, and to keep the two in balance: fear that prevents complacency, and hope that prevents despair. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī described it in a sentence the tradition loves:

> *"The believer combines excellence in action with fear [of its acceptance], while the hypocrite combines evil action with a sense of security."*

**"He Who Is in the Heaven": The Theological Question**

The phrase *man fī al-samāʾ* — "He Who is in the heaven" — is one of the texts around which the classical discussion of God's relationship to place (*al-ʿuluw*, loftiness) was conducted. It deserves a careful and fair presentation.

*The position of the early community.* The Salaf — among them Mālik ibn Anas, al-Awzāʿī, al-Thawrī, Ibn al-Mubārak, and Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, as reported by al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Bayhaqī — understood the phrase in its apparent sense: God is *above* the heavens, risen over His Throne, distinct from His creation and not indwelling it. In support of this reading, the commentators cited the established Arabic use of the preposition *fī* to mean *ʿalā*, "upon," as in:

> *"I will surely cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides, and I will crucify you upon (fī) the trunks of the palm trees."* (Qur'an 20:71)

The meaning there is plainly "upon," not "inside," and the commentators used the parallel to show that *fī al-samāʾ* can bear the sense of "above/over the heaven." They also adduced the many explicit statements of God's being above, such as:

> *"The Compassionate, [Who is] established upon the Throne."* (Qur'an 20:5)

> *"Do you feel secure against Him Who is in the heaven, that He will not send a sudden punishment upon you?"* (Qur'an 67:16)

> *"To Him ascends the good word, and the righteous deed raises it."* (Qur'an 35:10)

The tradition, on this reading, held two things together: God is above His creation in a manner befitting Him, and His *knowledge* encompasses everything. The distinction is important. To say God is above is not to say His knowledge is confined; the early community stated both, and the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, in a ḥadīth qudsī transmitted by Muslim:

> *"O My servants, were the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, to stand together in one place and ask of Me, and I were to give every person what he asked for, that would not decrease what I possess except as a needle decreases the sea when it is dipped into it."* (Muslim 2577)

*The position of later theological schools.* Many of the later Ashʿarī and Māturīdī theologians, concerned to avoid any implication of God's being contained by a place, interpreted the phrase as referring to God's dominion, authority, or loftiness of status rather than to spatial elevation — "the One whose command is in the heaven," or "the One who is above in majesty." Al-Qurṭubī, a Mālikī with Ashʿarī leanings, presents both readings and gives weight to the figurative one, while al-Rāzī argues for it at length.

It should be noted, in fairness, that the earlier Ashʿarī position was closer to that of the Salaf than is sometimes assumed: Abū al-Ḥasan al-Ashʿarī, in his *al-Ibānah ʿan Uṣūl al-Diyānah*, affirmed God's being above the heavens and His establishment upon the Throne without asking how, and he criticized the figurative reinterpretation of such texts. The divergence developed in the generations after him.

*The practical convergence.* For the purposes of this sūrah's argument, both readings converge on the same point, and it is the point the verse is actually making: the One who has authority over the heaven has authority over the earth beneath you. Whether the phrase is read as elevation or as dominion, the conclusion is identical — the ground you are standing on is not yours, and it is not independent.

**Yakhsif: The Earth That Swallows**

*An yakhsifa bikum al-arḍa* — "that He will not cause the earth to swallow you." The verb *khasafa* means to sink, to cave in, to be swallowed by the ground. The Qur'an uses it both as a punishment of nations and as a cosmic event at the end of time.

The parallel to this verse is exact:

> *"Do those who plot evil deeds feel secure that God will not cause the earth to swallow them, or that the punishment will not come upon them from where they are not aware?"* (Qur'an 16:45)

> *"Do you feel secure that He will not cause a tract of land to swallow you, or send against you a storm of stones? Then you would find no protector for yourselves."* (Qur'an 17:68)

The historical instance the Qur'an cites most frequently is Qārūn (Korah), the man whose wealth was so vast that the Qur'an says its keys alone were a burden for a company of strong men:

> *"So We caused the earth to swallow him and his dwelling place. And he had no host to help him apart from God; and he was not one of those who could defend himself."* (Qur'an 28:81)

And in a list of destroyed nations, the Qur'an groups this punishment with the others:

> *"So each We seized for his sin: among them were those against whom We sent a violent storm with stones, and among them were those whom the blast seized, and among them were those whom We caused the earth to swallow, and among them were those whom We drowned."* (Qur'an 29:40)

The pattern is consistent and it is part of the Qur'an's case: these are not myths. They are cited as events, in a landscape that the original audience could travel to and see.

**Tamūr: The Churning**

*Fa-idhā hiya tamūr* — "and then it is churning." The root *m-w-r* describes a violent back-and-forth motion, a swaying, an agitation. The same root describes the heaven on the Day of Resurrection:

> *"On the Day when the heaven sways with a swaying (tamūru al-samāʾu mawran), and the mountains move with a movement."* (Qur'an 52:9–10)

The image of the collapsing earth is not a silent sinkhole. It is an agitated, convulsing ground — the earth behaving like something unstable, the solid thing becoming liquid. The psychological point is precise: what is most trusted is shown to be least reliable. People do not fear the ground. They fear heights, water, fire, and other people, but the ground is the one thing taken for granted. The verse removes it.

**The Modern Illusion of Security**

The question *a-amintum* — "do you feel secure?" — lands with particular force on a modern reader, because the modern world has invested more in the feeling of security than any civilization in history.

Consider what has been built: earthquake-resistant building codes, flood defenses, insurance markets, emergency services, supply chains, pandemic preparedness, financial regulation, and medical systems that can treat conditions that were death sentences a century ago. All of this is genuine, all of it is beneficial, and none of it is the object of the verse's criticism. The Qur'an commands tying the camel. Precautions are not presumption.

What the verse targets is the *inference* drawn from the precautions — the quiet conclusion that because the ground has not opened, the ground cannot open. That inference is false, and the modern world has had occasion to relearn it. A single viral outbreak closed borders, emptied cities, and halted economies across the planet within weeks; supply chains that fed continents were interrupted by a ship stuck in a canal; financial systems that had been modeled to withstand every historical shock encountered one that had not been modeled. In each case, the assumption that had quietly become universal — that tomorrow will resemble today — was shown to be an assumption and not a law.

The Prophet ﷺ described the increase of such events as a sign of the approaching Hour:

> *"The Hour will not be established until knowledge is taken away, earthquakes increase, time passes quickly, tribulations appear, and killing increases — until wealth becomes abundant among you and overflows."* (al-Bukhārī 1036; cf. 4636)

Notice the pairing: earthquakes increase *and* wealth overflows. The condition most hostile to the memory of God is not destitution but comfortable abundance, and the verse addresses precisely that condition.

**Respite Is Not Acquittal**

There is one more dimension the commentators drew out of this and similar verses. When a person sins and nothing happens, there are two possible readings of the silence. It may be mercy — an opportunity granted for repentance. And it may be *istidrāj*, the gradual leading-on in which a person is left alone precisely because he has been left alone. The Qur'an names it:

> *"And those who deny Our signs — We will lead them on gradually from where they do not know. And I grant them respite; indeed, My plan is firm."* (Qur'an 7:182–183)

> *"And let not those who disbelieve think that Our granting them respite is better for their souls. We only grant them respite so that they may increase in sin; and for them is a humiliating punishment."* (Qur'an 3:178)

The terrifying element here is not the punishment but the delay. A person who is corrected early is, on this reading, more loved than one who is never interrupted — and the uninterrupted life, with its smooth surface and its growing sense of entitlement, is the one the verse is asking about.

The question is posed, then, not to produce terror but to interrupt an assumption. And the interruption is a mercy, because the ground is still solid now, and the moment in which that can be acknowledged is still available.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:17

> **Or do you feel secure that He Who is in Heaven will not unleash a torrent of stones upon you? Soon shall you know how My warning is.**

**Arabic**

> *أَمْ أَمِنْتُمْ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاءِ أَنْ يُرْسِلَ عَلَيْكُمْ حَاصِبًا ۖ فَسَتَعْلَمُونَ كَيْفَ نَذِيرِ*

**Transliteration:** *Am amintum man fī al-samāʾi an yursila ʿalaykum ḥāṣiban fa-sataʿlamūna kayfa nadhīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of a Paired Threat**

Verses 16 and 17 are built as a matched pair. The particle *am* that opens this verse is the Arabic connective used between two alternatives in a question — "is it this, *or* is it that?" The two threats therefore stand side by side:

- Verse 16: that He will cause the earth to swallow you from *below*.
- Verse 17: that He will send a storm of stones from *above*.

Together they enclose the human being completely. There is no direction of escape. The ground is not reliably solid, and the sky is not reliably empty. The two verses between them dismantle the two things people take most for granted, and the pairing is one of the most economical pieces of rhetoric in the Qur'an.

There is an added poignancy in the second threat, because this sūrah began by describing that sky. Verse 3 spoke of seven heavens built in flawless harmony. Verse 5 spoke of the lowest heaven *adorned with lamps*. And now, in verse 17, the same sky is described as capable of sending down a storm of stones. The heaven that was made beautiful can be made terrible, and the beauty and the terror come from the same Hand.

**Ḥāṣib: Stones Carried by Wind**

The word *ḥāṣib* comes from *ḥ-ṣ-b*, a root associated with small stones, pebbles, and gravel; *ḥaṣā* is the word for pebbles, and *ḥaṣb* is material thrown into a fire as fuel. The derived form *ḥāṣib* denotes a wind that carries and hurls stones — a stoning storm.

The Qur'an uses the same root for the fuel of Hell, which gives the word an additional resonance:

> *"Indeed you, and what you worship besides God, are the firewood (ḥaṣab) of Hell; you will come to it."* (Qur'an 21:98)

The punishing storm in this world and the fuel of the next are, in the language, related.

The verb *yursila*, "to send," is the ordinary word for dispatching something — a messenger, a wind, a rain. The storm is not an accident of meteorology; it is *sent*.

**The Historical Referents**

The commentators identified two specific events behind this threat, and both were known intimately to the original Makkan audience.

*The first is the destruction of the people of Lot.* The Qur'an describes it several times, with consistent physical detail:

> *"So when Our command came, We turned [the cities] upside down, and rained upon them stones of baked clay (ḥijāratan min sijjīl), layer upon layer, marked from your Lord. And they are not far from the wrongdoers."* (Qur'an 11:82–83)

> *"Indeed, We sent upon them a storm of stones (ḥāṣiban), except the family of Lot — We saved them at dawn."* (Qur'an 54:34)

The closing words of 11:83 — *"and they are not far from the wrongdoers"* — are worth pausing on. The ruins of that destruction lay on the trade route between Makkah and Syria, and the caravans of Quraysh passed them on their annual journeys north. The Qur'an's point is geographic as much as historical: you have seen this with your own eyes.

*The second is the destruction of the companions of the elephant.* This event was within living memory of the Makkan audience, having occurred in the very year of the Prophet's birth, roughly half a century before this sūrah was revealed:

> *"Have you not considered how your Lord dealt with the companions of the elephant? Did He not make their plan go astray? And He sent against them birds in flocks, striking them with stones of baked clay, and He made them like eaten straw."* (Qur'an 105:1–5)

The story, as preserved in the historical memory of the Arabs, concerned an army from Yemen led by Abrahah, which advanced upon Makkah with an elephant — or elephants — intending to destroy the Kaʿbah, and which was destroyed before reaching it. The significance for this verse is pointed. The Makkans were being warned by a God who had *defended their city* against a foreign army within their own lifetimes. The same power that had protected Makkah from stones could send stones upon it. Their own local history was being used as the evidence against them.

**"Soon Shall You Know How My Warning Is"**

The closing clause, *fa-sataʿlamūna kayfa nadhīr*, contains a linguistic richness worth unpacking.

*The future tense.* The particle *sa-* marks the near future — "soon you will know." The knowledge is coming, and it is coming imminently. The irony is that the knowledge arrives at the moment it has stopped being useful. The warning is vindicated precisely when it can no longer be acted upon.

*The double meaning of nadhīr.* The word *nadhīr* is of the form *faʿīl*, which in Arabic serves both as an active participle ("one who warns," a warner) and as a verbal noun ("the act of warning," the warning itself). Both senses are live here, and the commentators transmitted both readings. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr record that the phrase may mean "you will know how My warning turns out" — that is, how true it was — or "you will know how My warner is," that is, you will come to know the truthfulness of the Messenger whom you rejected, just as the people described in verses 8–11 came to know it.

The two readings are not really alternatives; they are the same truth stated twice. To know that Muhammad ﷺ was true *is* to know that the warning was true. And the Qur'an deliberately blurs the boundary between the messenger and the message, because in the Islamic understanding the messenger *is* the warning walking about in a body. The Prophet ﷺ was described as the living *nadhīr*; his very presence in Makkah was the proof that proof had been delivered.

*The rhyme.* The word *nadhīr* rhymes with the closing words of the next two verses — *nakīr* (18) and, at a distance, with *saʿīr*, *kabīr*, and the other long-ī endings that run through the sūrah. Arabic poetry and Qur'anic prose both use rhyme semantically as well as musically: words placed in the same rhyme slot are heard as belonging to the same set. Warning (*nadhīr*), reproach (*nakīr*), the Blaze (*saʿīr*), and greatness (*kabīr*) are bound together by sound as well as sense.

**The Rhetoric of the Ignored Warning**

The clause "soon you will know" is one of the most psychologically acute phrases in the Qur'an, because it names a specific and universally recognizable experience: the moment when a warning that was discounted proves true.

That moment has a structure. First, the warning is issued by someone whose credibility is not acknowledged. Then the warning is discounted, usually with reasons that sound reasonable at the time — the risk is overstated, the situation is different, the expert is alarmist. Then the event occurs. And then, with terrible clarity, the person sees that all the information he needed was available to him and that he chose not to act on it.

The bitterness of that experience is not the suffering itself; it is the knowledge that the suffering was avoidable and that the avoidance was refused. That is the emotion the Qur'an is invoking here — not as cruelty but as the accurate description of what rejecting a *nadhīr* actually leads to. The sūrah has already dramatized it in verses 9 and 10, where the inmates of the Fire supply the same structure from inside: the warner came, we denied him, had we listened we would not be here.

**The Principle Behind the Threat**

It is essential to state the theological framework that makes this verse coherent rather than arbitrary. God does not send storms of stones on populations at random. The Qur'an's doctrine, stated explicitly and repeatedly, is that punishment never precedes warning:

> *"And never do We punish until We have sent a messenger."* (Qur'an 17:15)

The sequence is invariable: messenger, message, rejection, persistence in rejection, and then consequence. The Stones that fell on the people of Lot fell after Lot had warned them for years and after they had responded by threatening him and attempting to assault his guests. The army of the elephant was destroyed while advancing to destroy the sanctuary of the God who had spared it.

And even then, the punishment is not the point. The Qur'an describes the destruction of past nations as a *sign* — an āyah — meant for those who come afterward:

> *"And We made them a precedent (salafan) and an example for those who came after."* (Qur'an 43:56)

> *"Then We destroyed the others. And indeed, you pass by their ruins in the morning and at night. Will you not then understand?"* (Qur'an 37:136–138)

**Modern Echoes**

The pattern this verse names has not changed. Warnings are issued, experts are dismissed, and the cost arrives later and lands on people who were not the ones who dismissed them. Engineering reports are filed and ignored before a bridge fails. Risk models are circulated before a financial collapse. Climate projections are published decades before the floods. Public health officials warn before the epidemic. In case after case, the phrase "soon you will know" describes exactly what happens.

What the verse adds to that observation is the religious dimension. For the believer, the ignored warning is not merely a human failure of prudence; it is a moral category, because the warnings that matter most were not issued by fallible experts but by God, through a Messenger whose truthfulness has already been established by everything described in the first fifteen verses of this sūrah.

And the corresponding comfort is real: the believer who *does* heed the warning is not spared from every hardship in this world, but he is spared from the specific bitterness of arriving somewhere he was told about and refused to prepare for. The Fātiḥah asks God to keep us on the path of *"those whom You have blessed, not of those who incur wrath, nor of those who are astray"* (1:7), and the two negative groups are precisely distinguished: those who knew and refused, and those who did not bother to know. This sūrah is addressed to both.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:18

> **And indeed those before them denied. How, then, was the change I wrought!**

**Arabic**

> *وَلَقَدْ كَذَّبَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ فَكَيْفَ كَانَ نَكِيرِ*

**Transliteration:** *Wa-la-qad kadhdhaba alladhīna min qablihim fa-kayfa kāna nakīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument from History**

The verse opens with the emphatic oath-particle *la-qad*: "and *indeed*, certainly, those before them denied." The certainty is not in doubt and needs no argument — it is a matter of record. What follows is the question: *fa-kayfa kāna nakīr*, "so how was My reproach?" or, in another rendering, "how was the change I wrought?"

The structure is a compressed syllogism, and the Qur'an leaves the conclusion for the listener to draw:

1. They denied, exactly as these people are denying.
2. God's response to that denial was catastrophic and total.
3. Therefore the same response is available now.

The rhetorical power of the verse lies in what it *doesn't* say. It does not describe the punishment. It asks a question whose answer is too well known to require stating. The original audience had the answer in their own landscape and their own oral history: the ruined dwellings of ʿĀd in the south, the rock-cut city of Thamūd on the route to Syria, the memory of the flood, the account of the overturned cities of Lot, and the recent destruction of the elephant army. To ask "how was it?" in that context was to invoke a body of knowledge too terrible to need elaboration.

**Nakīr: A Word With Two Faces**

The word *nakīr* has been discussed at length by the philologists, and the range of meaning they found is instructive.

*The primary sense is reproach and disapproval.* The root *n-k-r* gives *ankara*, "to disapprove of, to reject, to find objectionable," and *munkar*, "that which is rejected and condemned." On this reading, *kayfa kāna nakīr* means "how was My rejection of them?" — that is, how severe and how complete was My disapproval as expressed in what befell them. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī all transmit this reading, and it is supported by the identical phrase elsewhere in the Qur'an:

> *"And they denied him, so We destroyed them. Indeed in that is a sign; and most of them were not believers."* (Qur'an 26:139)

> *"Then I seized those who disbelieved — and how was My reproach (nakīr)!"* (Qur'an 35:26; cf. 22:44; 34:45)

*The second sense is alteration.* Some commentators, as the standard translations record, rendered the phrase as "how was the change I wrought" — deriving *nakīr* from the sense of *nakkara*, "to alter, to transform beyond recognition." On this reading, the verse asks: look at what I turned their condition into. They were powerful, numerous, and established; see what they became.

The two readings converge on a single picture. God's disapproval of a nation manifests as the alteration of that nation's condition: prosperity to ruin, security to terror, numbers to vanished memory. The word holds both the cause and the effect.

**The Sunnah of God With Denying Nations**

This verse invokes a doctrine the Qur'an states in formal terms — that God's manner of dealing with human societies follows a consistent, knowable pattern:

> *"The established way (sunnah) of God with those who passed away before; and you will never find any change in the way of God."* (Qur'an 33:62; cf. 33:38; 35:43; 40:85; 48:23)

> *"And that was Our way with the messengers We sent before you, and you will not find any alteration in Our way."* (Qur'an 17:77)

The word *sunnah* here means a consistent practice, an established way of acting. The claim is that God's dealings with nations are not arbitrary: rejecting a messenger after clear proof, oppressing the vulnerable, and persisting in arrogant defiance reliably precede destruction — not because God is vengeful, but because these behaviors are structurally unsustainable and because God, as the moral governor of history, intervenes.

The Qur'an is careful about the timing and the manner of that intervention. It is not immediate; nations are given respite, warned repeatedly, and left to choose. And when it comes, it is decisive:

> *"And how many a city did We destroy while it was committing wrong, so it lies fallen upon its roofs; and how many a deserted well and lofty palace!"* (Qur'an 22:45)

> *"And how many generations We destroyed before them, who were greater than them in power! So travel through the lands — is there any place of escape?"* (Qur'an 50:36)

**The Catalogue of Denial**

The Qur'an's references to destroyed nations are numerous and specific, and the variety of the punishments is itself part of the rhetoric — no nation was destroyed in a way it had prepared for.

*The people of Nūḥ (Noah):* denied for a very long period, destroyed by water.

> *"Before them the people of Noah denied; they denied Our servant and said, 'Madman!' and he was repulsed. So he called upon his Lord: 'Indeed, I am overwhelmed, so help me.'"* (Qur'an 54:9–10)

*ʿĀd:* denied Hūd, given great physical strength and building ability, destroyed by a sustained, devastating wind:

> *"And as for ʿĀd, they were destroyed by a screaming, violent wind which He imposed upon them for seven nights and eight days in succession, so you would see the people therein fallen as though they were hollow trunks of palm trees."* (Qur'an 69:6–7)

*Thamūd:* given the rock-cut city and the she-camel as a visible sign, hamstrung the camel, destroyed by a single blast:

> *"But they hamstrung her, so he said: 'Enjoy yourselves in your homes for three days. That is a promise not to be belied.' So when Our command came, We saved Ṣāliḥ and those who believed with him, by mercy from Us, from the disgrace of that Day."* (Qur'an 11:65–66)

*The people of Lūṭ:* destroyed by an overturning and a rain of stones.

*The people of Madyan and the companions of the well (aṣḥāb al-rass):* also mentioned in the catalogues.

*Pharaoh and his hosts:* destroyed by drowning, with his body preserved as a sign:

> *"So this Day We shall save you in body, that you may be a sign for those after you. And indeed, many among mankind are heedless of Our signs."* (Qur'an 10:92)

*Qārūn (Korah):* swallowed by the earth after his arrogance about wealth.

*The companions of the elephant:* destroyed by birds carrying stones of baked clay.

The list is recited in the Qur'an in summary form more than once, and each recitation functions as this verse does — as evidence, not as story:

> *"Has there not come to them the account of those before them — the people of Noah, and ʿĀd, and Thamūd, and the people of Abraham, and the inhabitants of Madyan, and the overturned cities? Their messengers came to them with clear proofs, and God would never wrong them — but they were wronging themselves."* (Qur'an 9:70)

Note the final clause of 9:70: *God would never wrong them, but they were wronging themselves.* The Qur'an is emphatic that these destructions were not acts of divine spite. They were the natural termination of a trajectory those nations had chosen.

**Why the Majority Is Not the Evidence**

There is a specific argument this verse is designed to defeat, and it is the argument the Makkan objectors actually made. Their position, as the Qur'an records it, was an argument from precedent:

> *"Nay, they say: 'We found our fathers following a religion, and we are guided by their footsteps.'"* (Qur'an 43:22)

> *"And when it is said to them: 'Follow what God has sent down,' they say: 'Nay, we shall follow that which we found our fathers upon.' Even though their fathers understood nothing and were not guided?"* (Qur'an 2:170)

The argument from precedent is powerful because it feels modest. It presents itself as humility — "who am I to differ from my ancestors and my people?" — while functioning as the most effective available defense of the status quo.

This verse's answer is devastating in its economy: *those before them denied too.* Precedent is not on your side; precedent is the prosecution's evidence. Every generation that rejected a messenger could point to the generations before it doing the same thing. The accumulation of precedent does not produce a defense. It produces a pattern of destruction.

The Qur'an makes the statistical point explicit elsewhere:

> *"And most of mankind, even if you strive for it, are not believers."* (Qur'an 12:103)

> *"And if you obey most of those on earth, they will mislead you from the way of God. They follow nothing but conjecture, and they do nothing but guess."* (Qur'an 6:116)

The logical principle here is one of the most important in the Qur'an and one of the most countercultural: numerical majority is not evidence of truth. The verse undercuts the deepest social instinct human beings have — the instinct to locate safety in numbers.

**The Consolation for the Small**

There is a second audience for this verse, and it is easy to miss. The Muslims in Makkah were few, poor, mocked, and in some cases tortured. They were being told, in effect, that they were a fringe movement in a civilization that found them ridiculous.

This verse tells them the opposite. The people who are on the wrong side of history are not the ones being mocked for believing; they are the ones doing the mocking. Every destroyed nation in the catalogue was, in its own time, the establishment. ʿĀd was stronger; Thamūd was more skilled; Pharaoh had an army and a state; Qārūn had wealth that strained the capacity of strong men to carry its keys. The believers were always the minority, and the minority was always the one that survived.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the principle in a famous exchange:

> *"Islam began as something strange, and it will return to being strange as it began; so glad tidings to the strangers."* (Muslim 145)

The historical argument in this verse is thus double-edged. It is a warning to the powerful, and it is a consolation to the small.

**What the Verse Asks of a Reader Today**

The practical discipline implied is the study of history as a moral science rather than a curiosity. The Qur'an commands it repeatedly — *"travel through the earth and see what was the end of those before you"* — and the object of the study is not dates and artifacts but a pattern.

Modern readers have access to more of this pattern than any previous generation. The archaeology, the inscriptions, the satellite imagery of abandoned cities, the histories of collapsed states — all of it is available, and all of it says the same thing that this verse says: civilizational confidence is not a defense. Rome fell; the Bronze Age collapsed across the entire eastern Mediterranean within a generation; great temples stand in jungles with no one to maintain them. The ruins are not only in the Ḥijāz.

The believer's response is not to become a pessimist about civilizations but to locate his trust correctly. Nations rise and fall; the religion survives; and the individual's task is the same in every era — to be on the right side of the pattern rather than the wrong one. As the sūrah has been insisting from its first verse, sovereignty belongs to God, and therefore the rise and fall of nations is His to arrange. *"Say: O God, Master of sovereignty, Thou givest sovereignty to whom Thou wilt, and Thou wrestest sovereignty from whom Thou wilt"* (3:26). The verse before us is that same truth written in the rubble.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:19

> **Have they not considered the birds above them, spreading and folding up [their wings]? None holds them save the Compassionate. Truly He sees all things.**

**Arabic**

> *أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَى الطَّيْرِ فَوْقَهُمْ صَافَّاتٍ وَيَقْبِضْنَ ۚ مَا يُمْسِكُهُنَّ إِلَّا الرَّحْمَٰنُ ۚ إِنَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ بَصِيرٌ*

**Transliteration:** *A-wa-lam yaraw ilā al-ṭayri fawqahum ṣāffātin wa-yaqbiḍna; mā yumsikuhunna illā al-Raḥmānu; innahu bi-kulli shayʾin baṣīr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why Birds?**

The Qur'an's choice of evidence is never accidental, and here it is especially deliberate. If the purpose were to overwhelm the listener with scale, the argument would have used stars, mountains, or oceans — and this sūrah has already used the stars. Here the argument descends to something small, ordinary, and visible several times a day.

That is the point. The Qur'an's signs are not reserved for the extraordinary; they are embedded in what people ignore. The bird is above you — *fawqahum* — every time you step outside. It is the most familiar unfamiliar thing in the world: a creature doing something physically impossible, in plain sight, so routinely that no one notices.

The psychological method is worth naming. A person can grow used to anything. Habituation is the single greatest enemy of wonder, and the Qur'an's strategy throughout is to break habituation by asking a question about the familiar: Have they not *looked*? The verb *yaraw* here can mean physical seeing or intellectual consideration, and both are intended. The command is not to acquire new data but to attend to data already in hand.

**Ṣāffāt and Yaqbiḍna: The Two Halves of a Wingbeat**

The description is precise: *ṣāffātin wa-yaqbiḍna* — "spreading [their wings] and folding them." The word *ṣāffāt* comes from *ṣ-f-f*, "to arrange in a row," and it describes the bird with wings extended in a single straight line, outspread to their full span. The word *yaqbiḍna* comes from *q-b-ḍ*, "to grasp, contract, draw in," and it describes the folding of the wings toward the body.

Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr explained the two states in terms of the mechanics of flight: the bird spreads its wings when it glides and when it drives itself downward, and it draws them in against the resistance, folding and unfolding in alternation.

What is remarkable is how well the description holds up. Modern aerodynamics describes exactly this alternation. On the downstroke, the wing is fully extended, presenting maximum surface to the air and generating both lift and forward thrust; on the upstroke, the wing is partially folded and rotated to reduce drag, so that the recovery stroke does not cancel the work of the power stroke. A bird that merely flapped flat wings up and down would waste most of its energy fighting its own recovery. The fold is what makes sustained flight possible.

The verse is not a textbook, and it would be a mistake to read it as one — its purpose is not to teach aerodynamics but to direct attention. But it is characteristic of the Qur'an that when it points at a phenomenon, it points accurately. The same word *ṣāffāt* appears in a verse that adds a further dimension:

> *"Do you not see that God is glorified by whoever is in the heavens and the earth, and the birds with wings outspread (ṣāffāt)? Each has known its prayer and its glorification; and God is Knowing of what they do."* (Qur'an 24:41)

The bird with wings outspread is not merely flying. It is praying, in a mode proper to it, with a knowledge it was given.

**"None Holds Them Save the Compassionate"**

This clause is the theological heart of the verse. *Mā yumsikuhunna illā al-Raḥmān* — "none holds them but the Compassionate."

Consider what the verse is asserting. A bird in flight has nothing beneath it. It is not resting on a branch, a ledge, or the ground. It is supported by air, and air is not a support in the ordinary sense — it is a medium that yields. The bird is held up by a set of physical relationships: the shape of its wing, the density of the air, the pressure differential, and the continuous motion that must be maintained or the whole arrangement fails. Remove any element of that arrangement for an instant, and the bird falls.

The Qur'an's claim is that the arrangement itself is sustained by God, moment by moment. This is the doctrine of continuous creation and continuous sustenance (*khalaq* and *amsāk*) that runs throughout the scripture:

> *"Indeed, God holds the heavens and the earth, lest they cease. And if they should cease, no one could hold them after Him. Indeed, He is Ever-Clement, Forgiving."* (Qur'an 35:41)

The same verb used here for the birds — *amsaka*, to hold — is used in 35:41 for the holding of the entire cosmos. The logic is identical, and the scale difference is irrelevant to the One who does the holding.

And the Name used is *al-Raḥmān*, the Compassionate. This is the third occurrence of this Name in the sūrah (verses 3, 19, and it will recur in 20 and 29), and its placement here is meaningful. The One holding the bird in the air is the One whose defining attribute is mercy. The bird is not being suspended by a neutral force; it is being carried by compassion. The verse is, in miniature, an argument that the universe is held up by love rather than by mechanism.

**The Parallel Verse in Sūrah al-Naḥl**

The near-twin of this verse sharpens the point:

> *"Have they not considered the birds, made subservient, in midair? None holds them save God. Truly in that are signs for a people who believe."* (Qur'an 16:79)

Two differences are instructive. First, al-Naḥl adds the word *musakhkharāt*, "made subservient" — the birds have been subjected, enrolled in a service they did not choose and cannot suspend. Second, it ends with *inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yuʾminūn*, "truly in that are signs for a people who believe" — which locates the value of the observation in the observer. The sign is there for everyone; it is a *sign* for those whose hearts are prepared to receive it as one.

**The Hadith of the Birds**

The most famous prophetic commentary on this theme connects the bird's condition to human trust:

> *"If you were to rely upon God with the reliance He is due, He would provide for you as He provides for the birds: they go out in the morning with empty stomachs and return in the evening with full ones."* (al-Tirmidhī 2344; Ibn Mājah 4164; Aḥmad 205 — al-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The hadith is not a recommendation of idleness. The bird, as this verse points out, is doing considerable work — spreading and folding, actively flying. The instruction is not "do not endeavor" but "endeavor without the burden of the outcome." The bird leaves, seeks, and returns; what it does not do is carry the anxiety of the seeking home with it.

And there is a second lesson in the hadith's wording. It does not say the birds find food because they are clever or persistent. It says *He provides*. The bird's effort is real, and the bird's effort is not what feeds it.

**"Truly He Sees All Things"**

The verse closes with *innahu bi-kulli shayʾin baṣīr* — "truly He sees all things." *Al-Baṣīr* is one of the Divine Names and it means the One whose seeing misses nothing: the sparrow that falls, the nest in the thorn bush, the fledgling that does not survive the night, the raptor and the prey, and every wingbeat of every bird that has ever flown.

The closing Name is not decorative. It answers an objection that the preceding clause raises. If God holds every bird in the air, and if birds fall and die by the millions, then either God is not holding them or He does not notice. The verse closes the gap: He sees. Nothing that happens to a creature happens outside His knowledge, and nothing is held without His permission to release.

This is one of the places where the Qur'an addresses the problem of natural suffering directly. It does not explain it. It asserts two facts and places them side by side: God holds, and God sees. The believer is asked to trust that the Two are compatible, and the tradition has generally held that the resolution belongs to God's wisdom rather than to human comprehension. What the verse does give is the assurance that no creature's fall is an oversight.

**Mercy to Creatures**

A further implication of the Name *al-Raḥmān* in a verse about animals has been drawn out by the commentators and the jurists. If the Compassionate holds the bird, then the bird is held by God and has standing.

The Prophet ﷺ legislated accordingly. He forbade taking animals as targets:

> *"Whoever kills a sparrow or anything larger without just cause, God will ask him about it."* It was said: "O Messenger of God, and what is its just cause?" He said: *"That he slaughters it and eats it — not that he cuts off its head and throws it away."* (al-Nasāʾī 4445; Musnad Aḥmad — graded ḥasan)

He told the story of two women whose destinations turned on their treatment of an animal:

> *"A woman was punished because of a cat which she tied up and did not feed, nor did she let it eat of the vermin of the earth, until it died."* (al-Bukhārī 3318; Muslim 2242)

> *"While a man was walking on a road, he became very thirsty. He found a well, went down into it, and drank; then he came out and saw a dog panting and eating mud out of thirst. The man said: 'This dog has reached the same state of thirst that I had reached.' So he went down into the well, filled his shoe with water, held it in his mouth, climbed out, and gave the dog to drink. God thanked him and forgave him."* They said: "O Messenger of God, is there a reward for us in [being kind to] animals?" He said: *"In every living creature there is a reward."* (al-Bukhārī 2466; Muslim 2244)

The last phrase is the important one: *fī kulli kabidin raṭbatin ajr* — in every moist liver (that is, every living thing) there is a reward. It is the practical extension of this verse. The One who holds the bird in the air notices the cup of water given to a dog.

**The Bird as an Emblem of Trust**

The verse's final application is the one the tradition has returned to most often: the bird as the emblem of *tawakkul*.

Consider the human condition by contrast. A person lies awake at night running scenarios — about money, health, reputation, children, career — and the anxiety is almost entirely about things that have not happened and, in many cases, will not. The bird does none of this, and the bird is fed. The Qur'an's question is not "why don't you stop working?" but "why do you carry what the One who holds the bird has not asked you to carry?"

That is not naivety about real dangers. The bird has predators, storms, and lean seasons, exactly as people do. The difference is in what is carried internally. The bird's stomach is empty in the morning, and the bird goes anyway.

For the modern person, whose environment is engineered to manufacture anxiety about the future — notifications, news cycles, market updates, comparison — this verse is a direct counter-liturgy. Look at the bird. It is held. And the One holding it is the Compassionate, who sees you as well.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:20

> **Who is it that will be a host for you, who may help you, apart from the Compassionate? The disbelievers are in naught but delusion.**

**Arabic**

> *أَمَّنْ هَٰذَا الَّذِي هُوَ جُنْدٌ لَكُمْ يَنْصُرُكُمْ مِنْ دُونِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ ۚ إِنِ الْكَافِرُونَ إِلَّا فِي غُرُورٍ*

**Transliteration:** *Am man hādhā alladhī huwa jundun lakum yanṣurukum min dūni al-Raḥmāni; in al-kāfirūna illā fī ghurūr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is an Answer**

The verse opens with a construction the Qur'an uses for rhetorical denial: *am man hādhā alladhī...* — literally, "or who is it that...?" The grammatical form is interrogative, but the intended answer is "no one." There is no such host; there is no such helper.

The technique is worth noticing because it recurs across the sūrah (verses 20, 21, and again in a related form at 28 and 30). Instead of asserting "your gods cannot help you," which invites argument, the Qur'an asks the listener to produce the helper. The listener is forced to search his own experience for a candidate and finds none. The conclusion is not imposed; it is arrived at.

**Jund: The Word for an Army**

*Jundun lakum* — "a host for you," "an army for you." The word *jund* denotes a military formation, a levy, an organized body of fighting men. It is not a synonym for "friend" or "patron." The Qur'an is asking a specific question: when the punishment arrives, what organized force will defend you?

The question is aimed directly at the structure of Makkan life. In seventh-century Arabia, a person's security was his tribe. There was no state, no standing police, and no standing army; what protected a man was his *ʿaṣabiyyah* — the solidarity of his kinship group, which was obligated to support him, avenge him, and go to war for him. The deeper his lineage and the larger his clan, the safer he was. The leaders of Quraysh were, in effect, the generals of such hosts.

The Qur'an's question cuts straight through that structure: who is your *jund* apart from the Compassionate? The answer, for anyone honest, is that the tribe protects against tribes, the clan against clans, and the alliance against alliances — but none of them protects against the One who sends the storm or opens the earth. The entire apparatus of tribal security has a ceiling, and the ceiling is exactly what the previous two verses described.

**The False Helpers Named Elsewhere**

The Qur'an develops this argument at length in other passages, and the parallels supply the context for this verse.

> *"But they have taken gods apart from God, hoping that they would be helped. They cannot help them, though they be a host (jund) made ready for them."* (Qur'an 36:74–75)

Note the identical word: *jundun muḥḍarūn* — a host marshalled and ready. The point is sharpened by the concession: even granting that these gods constitute an assembled army, they still cannot help. The problem is not the size of the army; it is the category error of expecting help from something that cannot act.

The most withering statement of this is the parable of the fly:

> *"O mankind! A parable is set forth, so listen to it. Indeed, those whom you invoke apart from God will never create a fly, even if they all gathered together for that purpose. And if the fly should snatch something away from them, they could not recover it from it. Feeble is the seeker, and feeble is the sought!"* (Qur'an 22:73)

The logic is airtight and brutal. The test is not whether the idol can do something difficult; it is whether it can do something as trivial as creating an insect, or recovering what an insect has taken. If it cannot do that, what is the standing to expect protection from it?

The Qur'an states the same point in the interrogative form elsewhere, forcing the same admission this verse forces:

> *"Say: 'Have you considered those you invoke apart from God? Show me what they have created of the earth. Or do they have any share in the heavens?' ... And who is more astray than one who calls upon, apart from God, those who will not respond to him until the Day of Resurrection, and who are unaware of his call?"* (Qur'an 46:4–5)

**Ghurūr: The Self-Sustaining Delusion**

*In al-kāfirūna illā fī ghurūr* — "the disbelievers are in nothing but delusion." The word *ghurūr* comes from *gh-r-r*, whose verbal form *gharra* means to deceive, to lull, to give someone a false sense of safety. The noun *ghurūr* is the state of being deceived and the deception itself.

The Qur'an uses this root in two ways that illuminate each other. It uses it for the deceiver:

> *"O mankind! Indeed the promise of God is true; so let not the life of this world delude you (taghurannakum), and let not the Deceiver (al-gharūr) deceive you concerning God."* (Qur'an 35:5; cf. 31:33)

*Al-gharūr* with the definite article is a title of Satan. And it uses the same root for the deceived, as in this verse. The connection is the point: the person in *ghurūr* has been successfully worked on by *al-gharūr*.

What makes the delusion self-sustaining is its structure. A false protector does not fail visibly in ordinary times. The idol does not collapse when invoked; the alliance does not dissolve on a calm day; the wealth does not vanish overnight. So the delusion is never corrected by experience. It is corrected only at the moment of need — and at that moment, correction is worthless.

The Qur'an dramatizes exactly that moment in Satan's own speech on the Day of Resurrection:

> *"And Satan will say, when the matter has been concluded: 'Indeed, God made you a promise of truth; and I made you a promise, and I broke it. I had no authority over you except that I called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me, but blame yourselves. I cannot come to your aid, nor can you come to my aid. Indeed, I deny your associating me with God before. Indeed, for the wrongdoers is a painful punishment.'"* (Qur'an 14:22)

The most chilling line is *I had no authority over you except that I called you, and you responded.* The delusion was not imposed; it was accepted. And the one who sold it admits, at the end, that he had nothing to back it with.

**The Only Real Helper**

The Name in the verse is *al-Raḥmān* — the Compassionate. The argument is compressed: help can come only from the One who owns everything, and the One who owns everything is the One whose mercy is the reason anything is sustained at all.

The Qur'an states the principle in its sharpest form:

> *"If God helps you, none can overcome you; but if He forsakes you, who is there who can help you after Him? And in God let the believers put their trust."* (Qur'an 3:160)

> *"And victory comes only from God. Indeed, God is Mighty, Wise."* (Qur'an 8:10)

> *"O you who believe! If you help God, He will help you and make your foothold firm."* (Qur'an 47:7)

And the Prophet ﷺ taught it as a personal rule:

> *"If you ask, ask of God; and if you seek help, seek help from God. And know that if the whole nation were to gather together to benefit you with something, they could not benefit you with anything except what God has already written for you; and if they were to gather together to harm you with something, they could not harm you with anything except what God has already written against you. The pens have been lifted, and the pages have dried."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516 — graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

**The Distinction That Makes This Workable: Means Are Not the Object of Trust**

A crucial clarification is needed here, because this verse is sometimes read as a prohibition on taking means. It is not.

Islam distinguishes between *al-asbāb* (means) and *al-tawakkul* (reliance). Taking means is commanded: the Prophet ﷺ tied his camel and said *"tie it and rely [upon God]"*; he wore armour at Uḥud; he dug a trench at the Battle of the Ditch; he appointed guides and sent scouts. The Companions who became generals and administrators did so without any sense that they were compromising their reliance upon God.

What is forbidden is *relying on the means* — locating trust in the army rather than in the One who gives victory, in the medicine rather than in the One who heals, in the savings rather than in the One who provides. The means is the mechanism; God is the agent. A person who confuses the two is in *ghurūr*, and a person who neglects the means while claiming trust is in error of a different kind. The Prophet ﷺ corrected that second error explicitly:

> *A man said: "O Messenger of God, should I tie my camel and rely [upon God], or leave it untied and rely [upon God]?" He said: "Tie it, and rely [upon God]."* (al-Tirmidhī 2517 — its chain has been discussed, but its meaning is established by the mass of evidence)

**The Amulet and the Modern Version of It**

The tradition identified a specific practice that crystallizes this verse's warning: the amulet. The Arabs of the pre-Islamic period hung *tamāʾim* — beads, shells, and inscribed objects — on their children and their animals, believing these protected them. The Prophet ﷺ ruled on it without ambiguity:

> *"Whoever hangs an amulet has committed shirk (association with God)."* (Musnad Aḥmad 17440; Abū Dāwūd — graded ṣaḥīḥ)

> *"Verily, incantations, amulets, and love-charms are shirk."* (Abū Dāwūd 3883; Ibn Mājah 3530 — graded ṣaḥīḥ)

The reasoning is the reasoning of this verse. No object protects. Protection is from God alone, and to attach protective power to a created thing is to transfer to the creature what belongs to the Creator.

The modern equivalents are not hard to identify. They are not usually objects hung around the neck; they are objects hung around the identity. Career status that will protect against irrelevance. A social network that will protect against loneliness. A portfolio that will protect against old age. A political party that will protect against chaos. A reputation that will protect against exposure. Each of these is genuinely useful as a *means*, and each becomes *ghurūr* the moment it is trusted as a protector.

The test is simple and brutal, and it is the test the verse poses: imagine the thing removed. If the removal means inconvenience, it was a means. If the removal means collapse — if a person cannot imagine how he would survive the loss of his position, his wealth, or his crowd — then it was a protector, and it was in the place that belongs to the Compassionate.

**Why the Disbelievers Are Named Rather Than Described**

One last note on the verse's closing. It does not say "they are deluded" as a description of their mental state. It says *in al-kāfirūna illā fī ghurūr* — "the disbelievers are in nothing but delusion." The structure *in... illā* is an absolute restriction: they are in *nothing* but delusion. Not partially mistaken, not mostly right with a few errors — their entire framework operates inside a deception.

That severity is softened by what follows in the sūrah and by the sūrah's opening, which named God as *al-Ghafūr*. Delusion is a state, not a permanent sentence. The person who is in *ghurūr* today can be out of it tomorrow, and the way out is the one this verse implies: stop looking for the host, and turn to the One who actually commands the armies.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:21

> **Who is it that will provide for you if He withholds His provision? Nay, but they persist in insolence and aversion.**

**Arabic**

> *أَمَّنْ هَٰذَا الَّذِي يَرْزُقُكُمْ إِنْ أَمْسَكَ رِزْقَهُ ۚ بَلْ لَجُّوا فِي عُتُوٍّ وَنُفُورٍ*

**Transliteration:** *Am man hādhā alladhī yarzuqukum in amsaka rizqahu; bal lajjū fī ʿutuwwin wa-nufūr*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Question of the Pair**

Verse 20 asked: who will be your *army* against God? Verse 21 asks: who will be your *provider*? The two questions cover the entire field of human dependence. People look to powers other than God for two things above all — protection from harm and provision of sustenance — and the sūrah demolishes both in two consecutive verses.

The construction is identical to the previous verse: *am man hādhā alladhī* — "or who is it that...?" The expected answer is no one. And the hypothetical is conditional: *in amsaka rizqahu*, "if He withholds His provision." Note that God has not withheld it. The verse asks the listener to imagine the withdrawal in order to understand the source.

This is the same pedagogical method used in the sūrah's final verse about water. The Qur'an does not normally remove the blessing in order to prove the point; it asks the listener to perform the subtraction in his imagination, because the imagination is enough if it is honest.

**Rizq: More Than Money**

The Arabic word *rizq* is broader than its English renderings of "sustenance," "provision," or "livelihood." It denotes everything that reaches a creature and sustains it — food, drink, income, health, shelter, knowledge, a child, a spouse, the capacity to work, sleep, peace of mind, and faith itself. The Qur'an uses the word for God's provision of rain from the sky and for His provision to the birds and the animals:

> *"And there is no creature upon the earth but that upon God is its provision, and He knows its place of dwelling and its place of deposit. All is in a clear record."* (Qur'an 11:6)

That verse is worth pausing on, because it answers the anxiety the present verse is designed to provoke. Provision is not a matter of chance or of market conditions alone; it is apportioned, and the apportionment is recorded. The theological claim is not that human effort is irrelevant — the Qur'an commands work and travel, as verse 15 of this sūrah did — but that effort operates within a decree that has already accounted for the outcome.

The Prophet ﷺ stated this in a report the scholars have transmitted widely:

> *"Indeed, the Holy Spirit breathed into my heart that no soul will die until it has completed its term and exhausted its provision. So fear God, and be moderate in seeking [provision]."* (reported by Abū Nuʿaym in al-Ḥilyah and al-Bayhaqī, from Abū Umāmah; graded ḥasan by al-Albānī in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Jāmiʿ)

Note the two instructions joined: *fear God, and be moderate in seeking.* Not "do not seek," but "seek moderately" — that is, without the desperation that corrupts the seeking.

**The Qur'an's Repetition of This Argument**

The argument of this verse appears over and over in the Qur'an, and the repetition is itself part of the teaching. Human beings have an extraordinary capacity to forget this specific fact and to re-attribute their provision to their own skill, their employer, their investments, or the state.

> *"O mankind! Remember the favor of God upon you. Is there any creator other than God who provides for you from the heaven and the earth? There is no god but Him; so how are you deluded?"* (Qur'an 35:3)

> *"Say: 'Who provides for you from the heaven and the earth? Or who owns hearing and sight? And who brings the living out of the dead and brings the dead out of the living? And who arranges every affair?' They will say: 'God.' Say: 'Then will you not be reverent?'"* (Qur'an 10:31)

> *"Say: 'Who provides for you from the heavens and the earth?' Say: 'God. And indeed, either we or you are upon guidance or in manifest error.'"* (Qur'an 34:24)

> *"God is He Who created you, then provided for you, then will cause you to die, then will give you life. Is there any among your 'partners' who does anything of that? Glory be to Him and exalted above what they associate."* (Qur'an 30:40)

The last of these is particularly valuable because it sets the full arc: create, provide, cause to die, cause to live. Provision is one link in a chain, and no created thing controls any link in it.

And the specific charge against the idols is made in this sūrah's own terms elsewhere:

> *"And they worship, apart from God, that which holds no power over any provision for them from the heavens or the earth, nor are they capable [of it]."* (Qur'an 16:73)

**Bal Lajjū: "Nay, But They Persist"**

The second half of the verse shifts from the argument to the diagnosis, and it does so with the particle *bal* — "nay, but," which in Arabic turns away from what preceded and replaces it with something more accurate. The expected human response to the argument would be to concede. The verse says: they did not concede; they persisted.

*Lajjū* comes from *l-j-j*, a root that describes plunging into something and refusing to turn back. The related noun *lujjah* means the deep, open sea — the place where a swimmer can no longer touch bottom. *Lajājah* is obstinacy: the condition of a person who has gone too far into a position to come out of it without losing face.

This is a precise psychological observation. By this point in the Makkan confrontation, the arguments had been made many times, and the Qur'an's diagnosis is that continued rejection was no longer an intellectual matter. The leaders of Quraysh had invested too much — too many public statements, too much ridicule, too much of their status — to reverse. Admitting the truth would have cost them more than they were willing to pay.

**ʿUtuww: Insolence as a Category**

*Fī ʿutuwwin* — "in insolence." The root *ʿ-t-w* describes the specific insolence of someone who has grown beyond accountability, the arrogance of a subordinate who has stopped acknowledging that he is subordinate. It is used in the Qur'an for Pharaoh and for the nations that were destroyed:

> *"And they were insolent (fa-ʿataw) toward the command of their Lord, so the thunderbolt seized them while they were looking on."* (Qur'an 51:44)

The word's usage is a warning in itself. The same quality that characterized the destroyed nations in verse 18 is now named in the present audience. The parallel is deliberate.

**Nufūr: Aversion as Flight**

*Wa-nufūr* — "and aversion." The root *n-f-r* means to flee, to start away, to recoil. The Qur'an uses it for the flight of animals from a predator, in one of its most vivid eschatological images:

> *"As though they were panicked asses, fleeing from a lion."* (Qur'an 74:50–51)

That image supplies the emotional texture of the word. *Nufūr* is not calm disagreement; it is a recoil — the movement of something that has been startled and wants distance. And it is used elsewhere for the specific way the disbelievers respond to the mention of God:

> *"And when God is mentioned alone, the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter shrink with aversion (tashmaʾizzu); and when those other than Him are mentioned, immediately they rejoice."* (Qur'an 39:45)

This is the key to the verse's closing diagnosis. The rejection is not "we considered your evidence and found it insufficient." It is an aversion — a flight from the implications. And what is being fled is not an argument but a consequence. To accept that God alone provides is to accept that one owes Him gratitude, obedience, and accountability. The refusal is a refusal of the obligation, and it dresses itself as a refusal of the premise.

**The Three-Stage Diagnosis**

Read together, the three words describe a single downward sequence, and the sequence is one the Qur'an traces repeatedly:

1. *Lajjū* — they persisted. The argument was presented; they declined to concede.
2. *Fī ʿutuwwin* — in insolence. The persistence hardened into arrogance, a posture of being beyond correction.
3. *Wa-nufūr* — and aversion. The arrogance became a flight from the very mention of the truth.

This progression is the Qur'an's account of how a person becomes sealed. It is not a single act of defiance but a habit of refusing that compounds, until the truth is not merely denied but found repulsive.

**The Economics of This Verse**

For the modern reader, this verse addresses the single most pervasive anxiety of contemporary life: the anxiety of provision.

The modern economy is built on that anxiety. Advertising works by creating dissatisfaction with what one has. Retirement planning, insurance, investments, and career strategy all assume — often reasonably — that provision is one's own responsibility and that a miscalculation means catastrophe. The machinery is not evil, but it is relentless, and it produces a person who is technically secure and emotionally destitute, who has enough and cannot feel that it is enough.

The Qur'an's answer is not to abandon planning. It is to relocate the trust. The Prophet ﷺ described the outcome of each orientation:

> *"Whoever makes the Hereafter his concern, God gathers his affairs for him, makes his richness in his heart, and the world comes to him despite its reluctance. And whoever makes the world his concern, God scatters his affairs, places his poverty between his eyes, and nothing of the world comes to him except what has been decreed for him."* (al-Tirmidhī 2465; Ibn Mājah 4105 — graded ḥasan)

The final clause of the second half is the theological hinge: *nothing comes to him except what has been decreed.* The anxious person does not get more by worrying; he gets exactly what was written, and he pays for it in distress along the way.

**Gratitude as the Antidote**

If the disease is the illusion of self-provision, the cure the Qur'an prescribes is gratitude — *shukr* — and it is tied to provision by an explicit promise:

> *"And [remember] when your Lord proclaimed: 'If you are grateful, I will surely increase you [in favor]; but if you deny, indeed My punishment is severe.'"* (Qur'an 14:7)

The Prophet ﷺ embodied the connection between gratitude and worship in a way that astonished his Companions. When ʿĀʾishah saw him standing in night prayer until his feet swelled, she asked him why he did this when his past and future sins had been forgiven:

> *"Should I not be a grateful servant?"* (al-Bukhārī 4836; Muslim 2819)

And he prescribed the practical discipline that counters the adaptation by which blessings become invisible:

> *"Look at those who are below you, and do not look at those who are above you — for that is more likely that you will not belittle the favor of God upon you."* (al-Bukhārī 6490; Muslim 2963)

That instruction is a direct application of this verse. If provision is from God and not from the market, then the correct comparison is not between what you have and what someone richer has, but between what you have and what you were owed — which is nothing.

**The Question the Verse Leaves Hanging**

Like the verse before it, this one ends without answering its own question. *Who will provide for you if He withholds?* The answer is silence, because there is no candidate.

And the silence is the argument. A person who has genuinely asked himself that question and waited for an answer has taken the first step out of the *ʿutuww* and *nufūr* the verse describes, because he has admitted the possibility that he is dependent. That admission, the tradition holds, is where provision actually begins — not the material kind, which comes whether or not it is acknowledged, but the kind that settles the heart.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:22

> **Is one who walks with his face stooped down more guided, or one who walks upright upon a straight path?**

**Arabic**

> *أَفَمَنْ يَمْشِي مُكِبًّا عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِ أَهْدَىٰ أَمَّنْ يَمْشِي سَوِيًّا عَلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ*

**Transliteration:** *A-fa-man yamshī mukhibban ʿalā wajhihi ahdā am-man yamshī sawiyyan ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm*

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parable That Answers Itself**

This verse is one of the Qur'an's *amthāl* — parables — and like all of them it works by asking the listener to make a judgment he cannot refuse. Two men are walking. Which one is better guided?

The question is deliberately easy. No one needs to think about it. A person who walks bent over with his face toward the ground cannot see what is ahead of him; he trips, he collides, he walks into holes and off edges, and he cannot reach a destination he has never looked at. A person who walks upright on a level, well-marked road sees where he is going, sees what is coming, and arrives. The comparison is self-evident, and the Qur'an's genius is to make the reader commit to the obvious answer before revealing that he has been describing himself.

The rhetorical form deserves a note. The verse begins with *a-fa-man*, and the *fa-* connects it to what came before: verses 20 and 21 established that no one besides the Compassionate can protect or provide. The implied continuation is: so which of the two postures makes sense — the one built on that recognition, or the one built on denying it?

**Mukhibban ʿalā Wajhihi: The Stooping Walk**

The word *mukhibb* is derived from a root meaning to walk with the head down and lowered, and the commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Zamakhsharī, and al-Bayḍāwī — explained it with consistent detail. The *mukhibb* is the one who walks with his face bent toward the ground so that he does not look ahead of him, nor to his right, nor to his left. He therefore stumbles, falls, and cannot keep a direction.

Some commentators noted a second layer: the phrase *ʿalā wajhihi*, "upon his face," can be read as describing someone falling forward onto his face — which is precisely the condition the Qur'an assigns to the disbelievers on the Day of Resurrection:

> *"Those who will be gathered on their faces to Hell — it is they who are in the worst position, and furthest astray from the way."* (Qur'an 25:34)

The verse thus works on two timescales at once. In this life, the disbeliever walks with his face down: he does not look ahead to the Hereafter, does not look to the right or left at the signs that surround him, and is absorbed entirely in the immediate patch of ground in front of his feet. In the next life, that posture becomes literal.

The Prophet ﷺ was asked about exactly this, and his answer established both the literalness of the resurrection image and the power behind it:

> *A man said: "O Messenger of God, how will the disbeliever be gathered on his face on the Day of Resurrection?" He said: "Is not the One Who made him walk upon his two feet in this world able to make him walk upon his face on the Day of Resurrection?"* Qatādah, the narrator, said: *"Certainly, by the might of our Lord."* (al-Bukhārī 4760; Muslim 2806)

**What the Downward Gaze Represents**

The parable's psychological accuracy is remarkable. A person's physical posture and his cognitive orientation are, in experience, the same thing. Consider what the stooping walker does not do:

*He does not look ahead.* To look ahead is to consider consequences — to ask where a course of action ends. The person absorbed in the immediate is not making a decision about the future; he is simply continuing. Most ruinous behavior has this structure: no one decides to end up ruined; they simply keep their eyes on the next hour.

*He does not look to the right or left.* The Qur'an places signs everywhere and repeatedly rebukes those who pass them heedlessly:

> *"And how many a sign in the heavens and the earth do they pass by, while they turn away from it!"* (Qur'an 12:105)

*He cannot see the ground he is about to walk onto.* This is the most practical of the three. A person walking face-down will step into what he could have avoided. The believer's uprightness is not merely superior aesthetics; it is safety.

**The Posture of the Age**

It is impossible to read this verse in the twenty-first century without noticing that the parable has become, quite literally, ubiquitous. The characteristic physical posture of contemporary life is the head bent forward, the eyes fixed on a small illuminated rectangle held at chest height, the shoulders rounded, and the attention entirely withdrawn from the surrounding world.

People walk into traffic, into doorframes, and into each other in that posture. They cross cities without seeing them. They sit with family and friends while looking at something else. And the content being consumed is overwhelmingly designed to keep the gaze down — the feed refreshes, the notification arrives, and the horizon never comes into view.

The Qur'an's parable is not a critique of technology; the technology is a tool, and it can be used for enormous good. The critique is of the *orientation*: a life lived with the face bent toward the immediate, in which the long view is never taken, the surroundings are never considered, and the destination is never looked at.

**Sawiyyan ʿalā Ṣirāṭin Mustaqīm: Upright on a Straight Path**

The contrast is drawn with three words: *sawiyyan*, *ṣirāṭin*, *mustaqīm*.

*Sawiyyan* comes from *s-w-y*, the root of equality, levelness, and soundness. It describes a person standing straight and balanced, whose posture is sound rather than bent. The same root gives *sawāʾ*, "alike," and *mustawin*, "level." The upright walker is level — not leaning, not listing, not off-balance.

*Ṣirāṭ* is the road, the way — and specifically, in the Qur'an, the way of God. The word appears with *mustaqīm*, "straight," in the single most-repeated prayer in Islam:

> *"Guide us upon the straight path (al-ṣirāṭa al-mustaqīm), the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favor, not of those who have incurred wrath, nor of those who are astray."* (Qur'an 1:6–7)

The appearance of the identical phrase here is not incidental. The sūrah is describing the very thing the Fātiḥah asks for. The believer's posture — upright, balanced, on a level and straight road — is the answer to the prayer, and the disbeliever's posture — bent, stumbling, directionless — is the condition the prayer is meant to rescue a person from.

Note the difference between the two halves of the parable. The stooping walker has no road mentioned: he is simply walking, somewhere, on whatever ground he happens to be on. The upright walker is on a *path* — a prepared, marked, and leveled way. Guidance in the Qur'an is not merely a direction; it is a road that has been built.

**The Criterion Is Guidance, Not Comfort**

One further detail: the question asks *ahdā*, "more *guided*" — not happier, more successful, or more comfortable.

This is important, because the parable could be misread as a claim that believers have easier lives. They do not. The straight path is not described as the easier path; it is described as the *true* one. A person walking upright on a straight road may be tired, poor, and persecuted, and a person stumbling face-down may be wealthy and comfortable. The comparison being drawn is not about the quality of the journey's comforts but about whether the journey is going anywhere.

The Qur'an makes this distinction elsewhere, in a parable that pairs a blind man with a seeing one:

> *"Is one who was dead, and We gave him life and made for him a light by which he walks among the people, like one who is in darkness from which he can never emerge?"* (Qur'an 6:122)

> *"And whoever is blind in this [world] will be blind in the Hereafter, and further astray in path."* (Qur'an 17:72)

> *"But whoever turns away from My remembrance — indeed, he will have a straitened life, and We will gather him on the Day of Resurrection blind."* (Qur'an 20:124)

The criterion is always orientation: is this person moving toward something real, with the capacity to see where he is going?

**The Medical and Psychological Resonance**

There is a genuine, well-documented relationship between posture and mood that gives the parable an additional modern resonance. A downward, collapsed posture is associated with low mood and rumination; an upright, open posture is associated with alertness and with a sense of agency. Research on posture and mood, on the effect of "awe" experiences — which characteristically involve looking up and outward — and on the mental-health effects of attention directed away from the self all point in the same direction the verse does: the bent-inward, downward-facing life is the worse life, and not only metaphorically.

The Qur'an, of course, is not making a clinical claim; it is making a spiritual one. But it is characteristic of the scripture that its spiritual claims turn out to be true at every level at which they can be tested. The person who holds his face toward the ground is, in this life, more anxious and less capable than he needs to be — and he is, in the next, in the condition the verse describes.

**How to Walk Upright**

The tradition derived from this and similar parables a set of practices that constitute, in effect, instruction in walking upright.

*Lift the gaze periodically.* The Prophet ﷺ prayed at moments when the sky was the object — the prayer for rain, the eclipse prayer — and he looked to the horizon. The Qur'an commands the same motion repeatedly: *"Have they not looked at the sky above them?"* (50:6); *"Have they not considered the birds above them?"* (67:19). The physical act of looking up is the physical counterpart of remembering God.

*Know the destination.* A person cannot walk upright toward nothing. The Qur'an's remedy for aimlessness is the memory of the return: *"And unto Him is the Resurrection"* (67:15). The Prophet ﷺ counseled the constant remembrance of death precisely because it restores the long view.

*Stay on the road that has been built.* The straight path is not invented by the traveler. It is the sum of what God has revealed: creed, worship, ethics, and law, in the form the Prophet ﷺ delivered them. A person who improvises his own route is, however upright his posture, not on a *ṣirāṭ mustaqīm* but on a path of his own making.

*Check the posture against the company.* The Qur'an's people are always described in motion and always in the company of others — "gathered in troops," "driven," "walking." The upright walker is surrounded by the upright, as the Fātiḥah's final clause implies: the straight path is the path *of those upon whom God has bestowed favor*, which is to say, a road with other people on it.

**The Question Is Put to the Reader**

The verse ends with the question unanswered, as verse 20 and 21 did. But here the silence is different, because here the answer is not "no one" but "you know very well which one."

That is the last turn of the parable's screw. The reader is not asked to learn something new; he is asked to admit something he already knows. The disbeliever knows which walker is better guided. He simply has not allowed himself to notice that the parable is about him.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:23

> **Say, "He it is Who brought you into being and endowed you with hearing, sight, and hearts. Little do you give thanks!"**

**Arabic**

> *قُلْ هُوَ الَّذِي أَنْشَأَكُمْ وَجَعَلَ لَكُمُ السَّمْعَ وَالْأَبْصَارَ وَالْأَفْئِدَةَ ۖ قَلِيلًا مَا تَشْكُرُونَ*

**Transliteration:** *Qul huwa alladhī anshaʾakum wa-jaʿala lakumu al-samʿa wa-al-abṣāra wa-al-afʾidata; qalīlan mā tashkurūn*

**Expanded Commentary**

**"He It Is" — The Emphatic Pronoun**

The verse opens with the independent pronoun *huwa*, "He." In Arabic, inserting a separate pronoun before a relative clause is a device of emphasis and exclusivity: not merely "the One who created you," but "He — and not another — is the One who created you." The construction recurs in the next verse and is one of the sūrah's closing signatures.

The emphasis answers the specific form of Makkan error. As noted earlier, the idolaters conceded that God created; what they refused to concede was that He alone deserves what follows from creating. The emphatic *huwa* closes that gap: if He alone originated you, then He alone is owed what an originator is owed.

**Anshaʾakum: Brought Forth in Stages**

*Anshaʾakum* comes from *n-sh-ʾ*, which means to originate, to cause to arise, and to bring up through stages. The same root gives *nashʾah*, a genesis or a bringing-into-being, and the Qur'an uses it specifically for the resurrection:

> *"Say: 'Travel through the earth and observe how He originated creation. Then God will bring about the final genesis (al-nashʾata al-ākhirah). Truly God is Powerful over all things.'"* (Qur'an 29:20)

The word choice is therefore loaded. By using the root that also names the resurrection, the verse quietly establishes the argument it is about to make in verse 24: the One who brought you into being once can bring you into being again.

The Qur'an describes the origination itself in detail elsewhere, and the stages it names are the ones every human being passes through:

> *"And We did certainly create man from an extract of clay. Then We placed him as a sperm-drop in a firm lodging. Then We made the sperm-drop into a clinging clot, and We made the clot into a lump [of flesh], and We made [from] the lump, bones, and We covered the bones with flesh; then We developed him into another creation. So blessed is God, the best of creators."* (Qur'an 23:12–14)

Note the closing phrase of that passage — *fa-tabāraka Allāhu aḥsanu al-khāliqīn*, "so blessed is God, the best of creators" — which returns the reader to the opening word of this sūrah. The sūrah that began with *tabāraka* is, in substance, an elaboration of what that origination implies.

**The Three Gifts, in That Order**

*Wa-jaʿala lakumu al-samʿa wa-al-abṣāra wa-al-afʾidata* — "and made for you hearing, and sights, and hearts."

The order is invariant across the Qur'an: hearing first, then sight, then hearts. The same triad appears in 16:78, 23:78, 32:9, 46:26, and here. The consistency has been remarked upon by the commentators, and several explanations have been offered.

*Hearing comes first because revelation is heard.* The primary mode in which the Qur'an reaches a human being is recitation — sound entering an ear. Sight can apprehend the created order; hearing is what receives the address.

*Hearing is also the faculty that develops first in the womb.* The Qur'an's sciences of embryonic development were not its subject matter, but the tradition noticed that the sequence in which the faculties become functional matches the sequence in which the Qur'an lists them, and it treated that correspondence as one of the signs of the Book's origin.

*The grammatical shape of the three words is itself interesting.* *Al-samʿ* is a verbal noun in the singular, used for the single faculty of hearing — a single, undifferentiated capacity. *Al-abṣār* is plural, for the two eyes and for the multiplicity of what is seen. *Al-afʾidah* is the plural of *fuʾād*, and the plural here has been explained in two ways: either as a plural of multiplicity (many people, many hearts) or as indicating the many states and modalities of the heart, which perceives, wills, loves, fears, and knows.

**Al-Afʾidah: Not Quite the Same as "Hearts"**

The word used here is *afʾidah*, the plural of *fuʾād*, rather than *qulūb*, the more common word for hearts. The distinction is worth drawing, because the Qur'an uses both, and the usage is not interchangeable.

*Qalb* (plural *qulūb*) is the heart as the center that can be turned, sealed, hardened, or sick — it is the moral and volitional core, and it is the word used in the famous statement:

> *"Indeed, it is not the eyes that go blind, but it is the hearts within the breasts that go blind."* (Qur'an 22:46)

*Fuʾād* is the heart as the seat of perception and emotion — the thing that burns, that is steadied, that returns. The root is associated with kindling, and the classical lexicons connect *fuʾād* to the burning of fire, which is why the word is used for the heart in moments of intense feeling. It is *fuʾād* that Moses describes when he asks God to untie the knot from his tongue, and it is *fuʾād* that is described in the most tender maternal image in the Qur'an:

> *"And the heart (fuʾād) of Moses' mother became empty [of all else]. She was about to disclose [his identity], had We not fortified her heart (qalb), that she would be of the believers."* (Qur'an 28:10)

The use of both words in a single verse shows the distinction at work: the *fuʾād* emptied with emotion, and the *qalb* was the thing that had to be made firm.

In this sūrah, the choice of *afʾidah* fits the context. The verse is listing the faculties by which a human being *receives* and *processes* the world: hearing receives sound, sight receives form, and the *fuʾād* is the organ that registers, feels, and understands.

**The Parallel Passages**

The triad recurs with a consistent purpose, and comparing the passages shows what the Qur'an is doing with it:

> *"And God brought you forth from the wombs of your mothers while you knew nothing, and He gave you hearing and sight and hearts (afʾidah), that you might give thanks."* (Qur'an 16:78)

> *"And it is He Who produced for you hearing and sight and hearts (afʾidah); little do you give thanks."* (Qur'an 23:78)

> *"Then He proportioned him and breathed into him of His Spirit, and made for you hearing and sight and hearts (afʾidah); little do you give thanks."* (Qur'an 32:9)

> *"And We had certainly established them in such as We have not established you, and We made for them hearing and sight and hearts. But their hearing and sight and hearts availed them nothing, since they used to reject the signs of God; and they were enveloped by what they used to mock."* (Qur'an 46:26)

Note the two different conclusions drawn from the same three gifts. In 16:78, the purpose is stated positively: *that you might give thanks.* In 23:78, 32:9, and this verse, the outcome is stated negatively: *little do you give thanks.* In 46:26, the consequence of ingratitude is stated: the faculties *availed them nothing.*

That last clause is the most sobering. The gifts are not withdrawn — the person keeps his hearing, his sight, and his heart — but they cease to function for the purpose they were given. A person can hear the recitation and not hear it, see the signs and not see them, and possess a heart that no longer registers what it was made to register.

**"Little Do You Give Thanks"**

*Qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — the construction is emphatic: "little indeed is what you give thanks for." The Qur'an repeats this verdict in 7:10, 23:78, 32:9, and here, and it is one of the scripture's most frequent judgments on the human condition.

Why is gratitude so rare? The verse itself suggests the answer by juxtaposing the gifts with the verdict. The three faculties are in continuous operation and therefore invisible. A person notices his hearing when it fails, his sight when it dims, and his heart when it breaks. In ordinary operation, they are simply the medium through which everything else is experienced, and a medium is not noticed.

The Prophet ﷺ named the two blessings most universally wasted:

> *"Two blessings that many people are cheated of: health and free time."* (al-Bukhārī 6412; al-Tirmidhī 2304; Ibn Mājah 4171)

And he prescribed the specific comparison that restores proportion:

> *"Look at those who are below you, and do not look at those who are above you — for that is more likely that you will not belittle the favor of God upon you."* (al-Bukhārī 6490; Muslim 2963)

**Shukr Is Not a Feeling**

It is important to be precise about what Islamic gratitude is, because the English word suggests a sentiment and the Arabic word does not.

The classical definition, as formulated by Ibn al-Qayyim and others from the texts, is that *shukr* has three pillars, and it is incomplete without all three: recognition of the blessing in the heart (*maʿrifah*), praise of the Bestower with the tongue (*thanāʾ*), and use of the blessing in obedience to the Bestower (*istiʿmāl fī ṭāʿatih*).

The third pillar is what makes this an ethical category rather than a mood. The *shukr* of the ear is to listen to what should be listened to. The *shukr* of the eye is to look at what should be looked at. The *shukr* of the heart is to love, intend, and fear what it should. A person who possesses all three faculties and uses them for their opposite is, by the Qur'an's measure, ungrateful — whatever he feels.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ connected gratitude to exertion. When ʿĀʾishah questioned his long night vigils:

> *"Should I not be a grateful servant?"* (al-Bukhārī 4836; Muslim 2819)

**The Faculties Will Be Questioned**

The Qur'an states that these three gifts are not only given but audited:

> *"And do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge. Indeed, the hearing, the sight, and the heart — all of those will be questioned."* (Qur'an 17:36)

The hadith literature fills in the shape of that questioning:

> *"The feet of the son of Adam will not move on the Day of Resurrection until he is asked about five: his life and how he spent it; his youth and how he used it; his wealth, from where he earned it and how he spent it; and what he did with his knowledge."* (al-Tirmidhī 2416 — graded ḥasan)

The connection to this verse is direct. The hearing that will be questioned is the hearing named here. The question will not be whether the faculty worked but what was allowed into it.

**A Modern Audit**

The verse invites a specific examination, and it is one that a contemporary reader can perform with unusual clarity because the data is now visible in a way it never was before.

*What have I listened to?* Modern devices record listening hours with precision. A person can know, to the minute, how much of his year went to recitation, to instruction, to music, to argument, to noise. The ear is the door to the heart, and what goes in stays in. The Qur'an's own principle is stated in the story of the Prophet's instruction to his community about the tongue and the ear together: guard what comes out, and guard what goes in.

*What have I looked at?* The same devices record this. The *abṣār* are, in this generation, the most heavily targeted faculty in human history — entire industries are built to capture and hold the gaze, and the content they supply is selected by criteria that have nothing to do with the viewer's good. The Qur'an's instruction about the eyes is explicit and practical:

> *"Tell the believing men to lower their gaze and guard their private parts. That is purer for them. Indeed, God is Acquainted with what they do."* (Qur'an 24:30)

*What has my heart loved?* This is the hardest of the three to measure and the most decisive. The heart — the *fuʾād* — is the organ that determines direction, and its attachments are revealed less by what a person says than by what he thinks about when he is not being observed, what he defends when challenged, and what he cannot bear to lose.

The verse's verdict — *little do you give thanks* — is not meant to produce guilt but to produce an inventory. A person cannot give thanks for what he has not noticed, and he cannot notice what he has never stopped to count.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:24

> **Say, "He it is Who multiplied you upon the earth, and unto Him shall you be gathered."**

**Arabic**

> *قُلْ هُوَ الَّذِي ذَرَأَكُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَإِلَيْهِ تُحْشَرُونَ*

**Transliteration:** *Qul huwa alladhī dharaʾakum fī al-arḍi wa-ilayhi tuḥsharūn*

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dharaʾakum: Scattered Like Seed**

The verb *dharaʾa* carries two meanings that in this verse fuse into one: to create, and to scatter or disseminate. It is the word used for sowing seed and for the propagation of living things. The related noun *dhurriyyah* — offspring, descendants — comes from the same root, as does *dhurriyyāt*, the "families" or progeny that God brings forth.

The commentators, among them Ibn Kathīr, explained that God distributed human beings across the regions of the earth and gave them differing languages, colors, forms, appearances, and statures. The scattering is not incidental to the creation of mankind; it is the manner of it.

The Qur'an uses the same root for the dissemination of other creatures, which places humanity within a single creative act:

> *"And whatsoever He multiplied (dharaʾa) for you upon the earth of varying colors — indeed in that is a sign for a people who take heed."* (Qur'an 16:13)

The parallel is deliberate: human diversity is presented in the same terms as the diversity of the natural world, and both are described as *āyāt* — signs.

**Diversity as a Sign, Not a Ladder**

This is the verse's most important social claim, and the Qur'an states it in several places with unmistakable clarity:

> *"O mankind! Indeed We have created you from a male and a female, and We made you into peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most reverent of you. Truly God is Knowing, Aware."* (Qur'an 49:13)

> *"And of His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the diversity of your languages and your colors. Indeed in that are signs for those who know."* (Qur'an 30:22)

> *"And among His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and whatever He has dispersed (baththa) throughout them of living creatures. And He is, whenever He wills, able to gather them."* (Qur'an 42:29)

Three points emerge from these passages together.

*First, diversity is intentional.* It is not a fall from an original uniformity to be overcome; it is described as a sign, which means it is meant to be contemplated and not merely tolerated.

*Second, the purpose of diversity is "that you may know one another" (li-taʿārafū).* Not that you may dominate one another, convert one another by force, or rank one another. The stated purpose is mutual knowledge — the recognition that comes from encounter.

*Third, the single criterion of rank is taqwā.* The verse in 49:13 says it explicitly: *the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most reverent of you.* Not the wealthiest, not the most numerous, not the most powerful, and not the most genealogically distinguished.

The Prophet ﷺ delivered the same message in his Farewell Sermon, in words the tradition has never stopped repeating:

> *"O people! Indeed your Lord is One, and your father is one. There is no superiority of an Arab over a non-Arab, nor of a non-Arab over an Arab; nor of a white person over a black person, nor of a black person over a white person — except by God-consciousness (taqwā). Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most reverent of you."* (Musnad Aḥmad 411; al-Bayhaqī, Shuʿab al-Īmān — from the sermon of the Farewell Pilgrimage)

And he identified the internal criterion, the one that cannot be faked:

> *"Indeed, God does not look at your bodies or your forms, nor at your wealth and possessions; but He looks at your hearts and your deeds."* (Muslim 2564)

The Prophet ﷺ also named the pre-Islamic attitude that this verse abolishes — *ʿaṣabiyyah*, tribal solidarity elevated to a principle of judgment. He said:

> *"He is not of us who calls to tribalism (ʿaṣabiyyah); he is not of us who fights for tribalism; he is not of us who dies upon tribalism."* (Abū Dāwūd 5121 — graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī)

And when asked about a man who loves his people, he distinguished between attachment and ideology:

> *"A man may love his people, but that is not ʿaṣabiyyah. ʿAṣabiyyah is when a man supports his people in wrongdoing."* (Abū Dāwūd 5121, in the same narration)

**The Logic: Scattering Implies Gathering**

The verse's two clauses are bound by a logic the Qur'an uses repeatedly. The One who scattered can gather. The One who dispersed can reassemble. The One who brought forth diversity from a single origin can return that diversity to a single assembly.

This is the resurrection argument in its most compact form, and it is the same argument the sūrah has been building since verse 2 (He created death and life), through verse 15 (unto Him is the Resurrection), and now here. The structure of the verse is: origin, dispersion, and return — the complete arc of human history in one line.

The Qur'an states the parallel explicitly with respect to animals:

> *"And there is no creature on the earth, nor a bird that flies with its wings, but they are communities like you. We have not neglected anything in the Record. Then unto their Lord they will be gathered (yuḥsharūn)."* (Qur'an 6:38)

The same verb — *hashara* — is used for the gathering of the animals and the gathering of mankind. The judgment extends, in the Qur'an's account, across the whole community of living things.

**Ḥashr: The Gathering**

The word *ḥashr* means to assemble, to muster, to bring together what has been dispersed. It is the technical term for the assembly of the Resurrection, and the Day itself is named from it: *Yawm al-Ḥashr*, the Day of Gathering.

The Qur'an's descriptions emphasize both the universality and the completeness of that assembly:

> *"The Day We shall gather the reverent unto the Compassionate as a delegation."* (Qur'an 19:85)

> *"Say: 'Indeed, the former and the latter will surely be gathered together for the appointed time of a known Day.'"* (Qur'an 56:49–50)

> *"God — there is no god but He. He will surely gather you together on the Day of Resurrection, about which there is no doubt. And who is more truthful than God in statement?"* (Qur'an 4:87)

> *"The Day He will assemble you for the Day of Assembly (yawm al-jamʿ) — that is the Day of Mutual Loss and Gain (al-taghābun)."* (Qur'an 64:9)

The last of these gives the Day a name worth reflecting on: *taghābun*, mutual loss and gain — the day on which every exchange is finally settled and every hidden deficit revealed.

The Prophet ﷺ described the gathering in terms that strip away every worldly distinction:

> *"You will be gathered on the Day of Resurrection barefoot, naked, and uncircumcised."* Then he recited: *"As We began the first creation, We shall repeat it — a promise binding upon Us. Indeed, We shall do it."* (al-Bukhārī 6527; Muslim 2859)

The recitation that accompanied the hadith is the point: the gathering is a repetition of the origination. The One who began it repeats it, and the repetition is described as a binding promise.

**The Theological Weight of "Unto Him"**

*Wa-ilayhi tuḥsharūn* — "and unto Him you shall be gathered." The preposition *ilā*, "unto," is emphatic by its front position: *unto Him*, and to no other.

That emphasis matters because the gathering has a destination, and the destination is a Person rather than a place. People are not assembled before a court, a system, or an abstract justice; they are assembled before the One who created them, scattered them, sustained them, and sent them warnings. The relationship is personal, which is what makes the reckoning meaningful.

The Qur'an states the same in the opening of the gathering scene:

> *"And the earth will shine with the light of its Lord, and the record will be placed, and the prophets and the witnesses will be brought, and judgment will be passed between them in truth, and they will not be wronged."* (Qur'an 39:69)

**The Reversal of the Scattering**

There is a final dimension worth noting, and it concerns what the gathering does to human self-understanding.

In this world, dispersion produces isolation. A person lives in one place, among one people, in one language, at one moment, and it is easy to conclude that his concerns are the world's concerns and that his generation is the one that matters. The scattering that verse 24a describes is also the scattering of perspective — each person seeing a fragment and mistaking it for the whole.

The gathering reverses this. Every generation, every people, every language, and every individual is assembled in one place at one time. The Qur'an's descriptions of that assembly consistently emphasize the shock of scale — the sense, on the part of the assembled, that they had not imagined the number.

The Prophet ﷺ was asked about the practical possibility of such an assembly and answered with an argument from the Creator's power:

> *"God will gather the people on the Day of Resurrection upon a single plain, and the Lord of the worlds will come to them..."* (al-Bukhārī 4712, in the long hadith of intercession)

And the Qur'an puts the objection and the answer together:

> *"O mankind! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then [consider] that indeed We created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop... And you see the earth barren, but when We send down upon it water, it quivers and swells and puts forth every beautiful kind. That is because God is the Truth, and because He gives life to the dead, and because He is Powerful over all things, and because the Hour is coming — there is no doubt about it — and because God will resurrect those in the graves."* (Qur'an 22:5–7)

**What the Verse Asks Now**

The practical content of this verse is a two-part instruction.

*Live as one who has been scattered by God.* That means treating the diversity of humanity as something made rather than accidental, and therefore as something to be contemplated rather than feared, ranked, or resented. Every language, every landscape, and every people one encounters is a sign.

*Live as one who will be gathered to God.* That means living in a way that will survive the assembly — because the assembly is where the scattering is undone, and everything separated in this world is brought back together in the next: the doer and his deed, the oppressor and the oppressed, the giver and what he gave, and the person and the Lord he was told about.

The sūrah has now completed its presentation of the evidence. What follows, in its final six verses, is the confrontation with those who refuse to accept it.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:25

> **And they say, "When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?"**

**Arabic**

> *وَيَقُولُونَ مَتَىٰ هَٰذَا الْوَعْدُ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ*

**Transliteration:** *Wa-yaqūlūna matā hādhā al-waʿdu in kuntum ṣādiqīn*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oldest Taunt in the Book**

This verse records a question that the Qur'an attributes to opponents in passage after passage, in almost identical wording. It appears in Sūrah Yūnus:

> *"And they say: 'When will this promise come to pass, if you should be truthful?'"* (Qur'an 10:48)

in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ:

> *"And they say: 'When will this promise come to pass, if you should be truthful?'"* (Qur'an 21:38)

in Sūrah al-Naml (27:71), in Sūrah Sabaʾ (34:29), in Sūrah Yā Sīn (36:48), and here. The near-identity of the wording across two decades of revelation and across very different audiences tells the reader something: this is not a question raised by a particular objection. It is the standing response of a person who has decided not to believe and needs a way to change the subject.

**Why the Question Is Not Really a Question**

The question has the grammatical form of an inquiry and the function of a dismissal. Three features give it away.

*First, it shifts the burden.* The actual claim being made is: there is a God, you belong to Him, you will die, and you will be held to account. The proper response to that claim is to examine it. The question "when?" changes the topic from the truth of the claim to the timing of its consequence, which is a different and much safer subject.

*Second, it is unfalsifiable in the way the asker intends.* If the Prophet ﷺ had named a date and the date had passed without the Hour, the asker would have declared the matter settled. If he had named a date and the Hour had arrived, the asker would have had no time to be right. And since he named no date, the asker could treat the absence as an evasion. Every branch of the question favors the person asking it, which is the mark of a rhetorical device rather than a genuine inquiry.

*Third, the question was asked by people who had already rejected the answer to a prior question.* The Qur'an records the same people conceding that God created the heavens and the earth (29:61; 31:25). Their objection was never to the Creator but to the Reckoner. The question "when?" is the objection to the Reckoner in its most socially acceptable form.

**The Demand to Hasten the Punishment**

The question has a more aggressive variant that the Qur'an records several times, and it is the same impulse pushed to its conclusion. Some of the Makkans did not merely ask when the punishment would come; they dared it to come:

> *"And [remember] when they said: 'O God, if this is indeed the truth from You, then rain down upon us stones from the sky, or bring us a painful punishment.'"* (Qur'an 8:32)

> *"They hasten you to bring the punishment. But God will never fail in His promise. And indeed, a day with your Lord is like a thousand years of those which you count."* (Qur'an 22:47)

> *"Those who do not believe in it seek to hasten it, while those who believe are fearful of it and know that it is the truth."* (Qur'an 42:18)

The last of these draws the essential contrast. The person who genuinely believes in the Day is *afraid* of it and does not want it hurried. The person who does not believe demands it early, precisely because he is certain it will not arrive. The demand is not courage; it is the confidence of someone who thinks he is bluffing a hand that does not exist.

**Mockery as a Psychological Strategy**

The mockery in this verse performs a specific psychological function, and it is worth naming because it is still recognizable today.

A serious claim about judgment produces discomfort. If the claim is even possibly true, then a person's life requires examination, and some of what he is doing would have to stop. That is expensive. Mockery is the cheapest available way out: it converts a claim to be evaluated into a joke to be shared, and once a thing is funny it no longer has to be answered.

The Qur'an identifies this mechanism and names it for what it is:

> *"And when those who disbelieve hear the Reminder, they almost assault you with their eyes [in rage], and they say: 'Indeed, he is mad!' But it is nothing but a Reminder to the worlds."* (Qur'an 68:51)

> *"And if you ask them, they will surely say: 'We were only conversing and playing.' Say: 'Is it God and His signs and His Messenger that you were mocking? Make no excuse — you have disbelieved after your belief.'"* (Qur'an 9:65–66)

The 9:66 verse is emphatic and, frankly, frightening: *you have disbelieved after your belief* — the mockery itself was the disbelief, and the excuse that it was only a joke is explicitly rejected.

**Theology of the Hidden Date**

Why did God not reveal the date? The commentators and theologians proposed several reasons, and they are complementary rather than competing.

*First, continuous readiness.* If the date were known and distant, repentance would be deferred until near it; if known and near, the remaining time would be panic rather than obedience. Concealment keeps every generation in the state the Prophet ﷺ described:

> *"When you reach the evening, do not expect the morning; and when you reach the morning, do not expect the evening."* (al-Bukhārī 6416, in the instruction of Ibn ʿUmar)

*Second, the test requires the unseen.* Belief in what is visible is not belief; it is perception. The whole structure of the test described in verse 2 depends on the object of faith not being coercively evident.

*Third, it is simply not man's business.* The Qur'an assigns the knowledge of the Hour to God alone, and it treats speculation about it as a category error — asking about something for which the human being has no instrument and no mandate.

**The Prophet's Own Answer**

When the Prophet ﷺ was asked directly about the Hour, he did not name a date, and his answer is the model for every Muslim who has been asked since:

> *Jibrīl asked: "Tell me about the Hour." He said: "The one questioned about it knows no more than the one asking."* (Muslim 8 — from the hadith of Jibrīl)

And he stated the limits of his own knowledge in words the Qur'an recorded:

> *"Say: 'I do not say to you that I have the treasuries of God, nor do I know the unseen, nor do I say to you that I am an angel. I follow only what is revealed to me.'"* (Qur'an 6:50)

> *"Say: 'I do not know what will be done with me or with you. I only follow what is revealed to me, and I am not but a clear warner.'"* (Qur'an 46:9)

That restraint is itself part of the evidence for his truthfulness. A person inventing a religion would be under enormous temptation to name a date — it is the single most effective way to generate urgency — and the absence of any date in the Qur'an, despite repeated demands for one, is a mark of the message's origin.

The Prophet ﷺ did indicate nearness in general terms:

> *"I and the Hour have been sent like these two"* — and he joined his index finger and his middle finger. (al-Bukhārī 4936; Muslim 2951)

Near, but not dated.

**The Suddenness**

The Qur'an pairs the hidden date with a warning about the manner of arrival:

> *"They ask you about the Hour: when is its arrival? Say: 'Its knowledge is only with my Lord. None can reveal its time except Him. It is heavy in the heavens and the earth. It will not come upon you except suddenly.'"* (Qur'an 7:187)

And the Prophet ﷺ described that suddenness in terms of ordinary unfinished business:

> *"The Hour will be established while two men have spread out a garment between them, and they will not conclude their transaction nor fold it up. The Hour will be established while a man is carrying the milk of his she-camel, and he will not get to drink it. The Hour will be established while a man is plastering his watering-trough, and he will not get to water [his animals] from it. The Hour will be established while a man has raised a morsel of food to his mouth, and he will not get to eat it."* (al-Bukhārī 6507; Muslim 2954)

The detail chosen in each case is the same: a person is in the middle of something ordinary, with something unfinished in his hands.

**The Modern Form of the Taunt**

The question has not disappeared; it has changed costume. The contemporary version is less often "when is the Hour?" and more often "where is your God?", "if religion were true, why...", or the assertion that modern knowledge has settled the matter and no further discussion is required.

The structure is identical to the one in this verse. It moves the burden from the claim to a condition the asker controls; it is constructed so that no answer satisfies; and it functions socially to permit the asker to stop thinking about the thing that unsettles him.

The Qur'an's response is also unchanged, and it is given in the very next verse: state what you know, admit what you do not know, and keep delivering the warning. The believer is not required to produce a date. He is required to be a *nadhīr mubīn* — a clear warner.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:26

> **Say, "Knowledge lies with God alone, and I am only a clear warner."**

**Arabic**

> *قُلْ إِنَّمَا الْعِلْمُ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَإِنَّمَا أَنَا نَذِيرٌ مُبِينٌ*

**Transliteration:** *Qul innamā al-ʿilmu ʿinda Allāhi wa-innamā anā nadhīrun mubīn*

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Restrictions, and Everything That Follows From Them**

This verse is built on a single rhetorical device repeated twice: *innamā*, the Arabic particle of restriction — "only," "nothing but." The double use gives the verse the structure of two boundaries drawn firmly:

1. *Innamā al-ʿilmu ʿinda Allāh* — knowledge [of the Hour] is *only* with God.
2. *Wa-innamā anā nadhīrun mubīn* — and I am *only* a clear warner.

Each restriction closes off an error, and the two together define the proper relationship between a human being and the unseen. God is not to be interrogated on matters He has not disclosed; the messenger is not to be asked for what the messenger was not given.

The instruction is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ in the imperative — *qul*, "say" — and through him to every believing community after him. The verse teaches not only *what* to say when challenged but *how much* to say: no more than what has been revealed, and no less.

**"Knowledge Is with God Alone"**

The definite article in *al-ʿilm* refers back to the question that prompted it: the knowledge of when the promise will arrive. The preposition *ʿinda*, "with" or "in the presence of," locates that knowledge as something possessed and kept by God rather than merely known by Him and available to others.

The Qur'an states this in the most comprehensive form possible:

> *"And with Him are the keys of the unseen (mafātiḥu al-ghayb); none knows them except Him. And He knows what is on the land and in the sea. Not a leaf falls but that He knows it. No grain is there within the darknesses of the earth, and no moist or dry thing, but that it is in a clear record."* (Qur'an 6:59)

And specifically about the Hour:

> *"They ask you about the Hour: when is its arrival? Say: 'Its knowledge is only with my Lord.'"* (Qur'an 7:187)

> *"Indeed, God — with Him is knowledge of the Hour, and He sends down the rain, and He knows what is in the wombs. And no soul knows what it will earn tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die. Indeed, God is Knowing, Aware."* (Qur'an 31:34)

> *"People ask you about the Hour. Say: 'Knowledge of it is only with God.' And what can make you perceive? Perhaps the Hour is near."* (Qur'an 33:63)

> *"To Him is referred knowledge of the Hour... And He knows what you do."* (Qur'an 41:47)

> *"And blessed is He to Whom belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth and whatever is between them; and with Him is knowledge of the Hour, and to Him you will be returned."* (Qur'an 43:85)

The last of these is especially relevant to this sūrah, because it joins three elements that Sūrah al-Mulk has been developing from its opening verse: dominion (*mulk*), knowledge of the Hour, and the return to God. The two passages are, in substance, saying the same thing.

**The Five Keys of the Unseen**

The Prophet ﷺ gave the doctrine a precise and memorable form, and the hadith is among the most frequently cited in the entire tradition:

> *"The keys of the unseen are five, which none knows but God: none knows what is in the womb except God; none knows what tomorrow will bring except God; none knows when the rain will come except God; no soul knows in what land it will die except God; and none knows when the Hour will be established except God."* (al-Bukhārī 1039; cf. 4778)

Note the two that bear directly on ordinary human anxiety: no one knows what tomorrow will bring, and no one knows where he will die. These are not remote theological puzzles; they are the two uncertainties that generate most of the worry in an ordinary life.

The implication for the believer is not passivity but a specific discipline: plan, then entrust. A person may save, insure, prepare, and arrange — and he does all of it knowing that the outcome is not in his hands and that the arrangement may be overturned by something he did not foresee. That posture is not pessimism; it is the accurate description of the situation, and the tradition holds that it is also the posture in which the heart is most at rest.

**But God Discloses Some of the Unseen to His Messengers**

A necessary qualification, without which the doctrine would make prophecy impossible. God's knowledge of the unseen is exclusively His, but He reveals from it what He wills to whom He wills:

> *"[He is] Knower of the unseen, and He does not disclose His unseen to anyone, except to a messenger whom He has approved; and then He dispatches before him and behind him observers, so that He may know that they have conveyed the messages of their Lord. And He encompasses whatever is with them, and He has enumerated all things in number."* (Qur'an 72:26–28)

This is the precise Islamic position, and it holds two things in balance. On one side: no creature knows the unseen independently — not a prophet, not an angel, not a saint, and not a fortune-teller. On the other: God *has* disclosed portions of the unseen to His messengers, and those disclosures are true precisely because they came from Him.

The distinction matters, and it resolves the apparent tension in the Prophet's career. He foretold the conquest of Persia and Byzantium, the fate of specific individuals, the trials that would follow his death, the rise and fall of the caliphate, and many signs of the Hour — all of which are matters of the unseen, and all of which he attributed to revelation and never to himself. He said:

> *"I do not know what will be done with me or with you. I only follow what is revealed to me."* (Qur'an 46:9)

The moment a person claims independent access to the unseen — through calculation, through astronomy, through dreams elevated to legislation, through inspiration treated as authority — he has stepped outside what this verse permits. That is why the tradition was so severe about fortune-tellers and astrologers, as noted in the commentary on verse 6: the claim to know what God has not disclosed is a claim to a share in a prerogative that this verse reserves entirely.

**"I Am Only a Clear Warner"**

The second restriction defines the Prophet's role and, with it, the limits of religious authority.

*Nadhīr* has already appeared in this sūrah at verses 8 and 9, where the keepers of Hell ask the damned whether a warner came, and where the damned reply that one did. The word is now applied to the Prophet ﷺ himself, which closes the circle: he is the warner whose coming those verses will cite on the Day of Resurrection.

*Mubīn* is an adjective that works in two directions at once, and the commentators noted both. It can mean "one who makes clear" — the warner who explains, specifies, and leaves no ambiguity. And it can mean "clearly, manifestly" — the warner whose status as a warner is obvious and undeniable. Both readings are true, and together they say: the warning has been delivered in plain language by a man whose identity as a messenger was not in doubt.

The Qur'an uses the exact phrase in several places, and the repetition fixes the role:

> *"Say: 'O mankind, I am only a clear warner to you.'"* (Qur'an 22:49)

> *"Say: 'I only warn you by revelation.'"* (Qur'an 21:45)

> *"Say: 'I am not the first of the messengers, and I do not know what will be done with me or with you. I only follow what is revealed to me, and I am not but a clear warner.'"* (Qur'an 46:9)

> *"And recite the Qur'an to whoever fears My threat."* (Qur'an 50:45)

And God addresses the Prophet in the same terms:

> *"And you are only a warner."* (Qur'an 13:7)

> *"You are only a warner."* (Qur'an 35:23)

> *"You are only a warner for those who fear it."* (Qur'an 79:45)

**The Prophet Is Not a Coercer**

One of the most important consequences of this verse is stated explicitly in the Qur'an and was lived out across the whole Makkan period. The messenger's task is delivery, not compulsion:

> *"So remind; you are only one who reminds. You are not over them a controller (musayṭir) — except him who turns away and disbelieves; then God will punish him with the greatest punishment."* (Qur'an 88:21–24)

> *"And had your Lord willed, all those on earth would have believed, all of them together. Then, [O Muhammad], would you compel the people until they become believers?"* (Qur'an 10:99)

> *"So remind, if the reminder should benefit; he who fears will be reminded."* (Qur'an 87:9–10)

The distinction is fundamental and frequently misunderstood, both by critics of Islam and, historically, by Muslims. The Prophet ﷺ was not authorized to force belief; he was authorized to deliver the message clearly, and the response belongs to the one who hears. This is why the Makkan period — thirteen years of preaching to a hostile audience, with no political authority and no coercion — is the normative model for the *daʿwah*, the invitation to God.

**What to Say When Asked**

The verse functions as a script, and it is worth appreciating how much discipline it encodes.

*It does not answer the mockery with mockery.* The askers said, "if you are truthful" — an insult disguised as a condition. The instructed response ignores the insult entirely.

*It does not speculate.* The Prophet is not told to say "soon," or "perhaps within your lifetimes," or "the signs suggest..." He is told to say: the knowledge is with God.

*It does not soften the message to reduce the pressure.* The second half of the verse reasserts the warning in full: I am a clear warner. The questioner's discomfort is not the messenger's problem to solve.

*It does not claim more than was given.* This is perhaps the hardest discipline for any religious community to maintain, and it is the one most often broken. The temptation is to add certainty where revelation left ambiguity — to name dates, to identify individuals as saved or damned, to fill in the details of the unseen with confident invention. This verse forbids it.

The Prophet ﷺ warned against the opposite error in his own community with characteristic directness:

> *"Beware of suspicion, for suspicion is the most false of speech. And do not spy, and do not pry, and do not compete with one another, and do not envy one another, and do not hate one another, and do not turn away from one another. And be, O servants of God, brothers."* (al-Bukhārī 6064; Muslim 2563)

**The Believer's Epistemology**

Taken together, the two halves of this verse describe an entire attitude toward knowledge, and it is one the modern world badly needs.

The modern environment rewards confident prediction. Pundits, forecasters, analysts, and commentators are paid for certainty, and the person who says "I do not know" is treated as weak or evasive. The result is a public discourse saturated with confident claims that are wrong with remarkable regularity, and a culture in which the honest admission of uncertainty has been trained out of public life.

The Qur'anic posture is different, and it is not timidity. It is the disciplined allocation of confidence: certainty about what has been revealed, refusal to speculate about what has not, and clarity about which is which. The Prophet ﷺ modeled it, and the verse commands it.

For an ordinary believer, this translates into a set of practical habits. Be certain that there is a God, that the message is true, that death is coming, and that the reckoning is real — these are established. Be silent on the date of the Hour, the identity of the saved, the precise wisdom behind a particular affliction, and the hidden state of another person's heart — these are not given. And be clear, in one's own mind and in one's speech, about which category a statement belongs to.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:27

> **And when they see it close at hand, the faces of those who disbelieved shall be stricken, and it shall be said, "This is that for which you called."**

**Arabic**

> *فَلَمَّا رَأَوْهُ زُلْفَةً سِيئَتْ وُجُوهُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَقِيلَ هَٰذَا الَّذِي كُنْتُمْ بِهِ تَدَّعُونَ*

**Transliteration:** *Fa-lammā ra-awhu zulfatan sīʾat wujūhu alladhīna kafarū wa-qīla hādhā alladhī kuntum bihi taddaʿūn*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Transition From Mockery to Confrontation**

Verses 25 and 26 recorded the question and the instructed answer. Verse 27 jumps forward to the moment the question is answered — not by a statement but by an arrival. *Fa-lammā ra-awhu* — "and when they see it."

The pronoun *hu*, "it," refers to the promised punishment, to the Hour, or to the Day of Resurrection. The Qur'an leaves the referent deliberately general, because at that moment the distinctions collapse: the promise, the Hour, the reckoning, and the Fire are all one thing arriving.

The word *zulfatan* means "drawn near." It comes from *z-l-f*, to bring close, to advance. The punishment is not approaching on a schedule the mockers can track; it is *suddenly near*. This is the same phenomenon the Qur'an describes elsewhere with the phrase "it will not come upon you except suddenly" (7:187).

Notice the verb: *ra-awhu*, "they see it." For the whole of their lives, the disbelievers had demanded to see. *When will this promise come? Show us. Bring it.* The verse records the answer: they see it. The request is granted, and the granting is the catastrophe.

**Sīʾat Wujūhuhum: The Face as the Ledger**

*Sīʾat wujūhu alladhīna kafarū* — "the faces of those who disbelieved shall be stricken." The verb comes from *s-w-ʾ*, "to be evil, bad, ugly," and its passive form here describes a face that has been made to bear an evil aspect — darkened, distorted, covered with distress.

The Qur'an consistently uses the face as the visible register of the soul's state on that Day. In the eschatological descriptions, faces do not merely express emotions; they carry the verdict:

> *"And those who earned evil [deeds] — the recompense of an evil deed is its like, and humiliation will cover them; they will have none to protect them from God — as though their faces were covered with pieces of darkest night. Those are the inhabitants of the Fire; they will abide therein."* (Qur'an 10:27)

> *"[Some] faces, that Day, will be downcast, laboring, weary, burning in a scorching Fire."* (Qur'an 88:2–4)

> *"And [other] faces, that Day, will have upon them dust; darkness will cover them — those are the disbelievers, the wicked ones."* (Qur'an 80:40–42)

> *"On the Day when [some] faces will turn white and [some] faces will turn black..."* (Qur'an 3:106)

And in the opposite direction:

> *"[Some] faces, that Day, will be radiant, looking toward their Lord."* (Qur'an 75:22–23)

> *"You will recognize in their faces the radiance of bliss."* (Qur'an 83:24)

The visceral accuracy of this imagery is worth noting. Human beings read faces before they read anything else; the face is the first thing a person looks for and the last thing a person can hide. The Qur'an's depiction of the Day is built on the premise that this will be supremely true then — that whatever a person has made of himself will be legible on him, instantly and to everyone.

**Why the Face Specifically Is Stricken**

The commentators offered a reading that deserves to be stated because it ties this verse to the rest of the sūrah. The stricken face is the face of a person who has just understood that the thing he mocked is true.

This is a specific and recognizable human expression, and the Qur'an names it elsewhere with precision:

> *"And there will appear to them the evil of what they earned, and they will be enveloped by what they used to mock."* (Qur'an 39:48)

The phrase *they will be enveloped by what they used to mock* is one of the most exact descriptions of ruin in any literature. The mockery does not simply turn out to be wrong; it becomes the substance of the punishment. What a person laughed at is what he is swallowed by.

**Taddaʿūn: What You Called For**

The closing clause is *hādhā alladhī kuntum bihi taddaʿūn* — "this is that for which you called." The verb comes from *d-ʿ-w* in the form *iddaʿā / taddaʿā*, and it carries two closely related meanings that the commentators both transmitted:

*Meaning one: "that which you used to demand and call for."* On this reading, the clause is a terrible grant. You asked for the punishment to be brought; here it is. You said "if this is the truth from God, then rain stones upon us" (8:32); the stones have arrived. The Qur'an records the same granting elsewhere:

> *"Those who do not believe in it seek to hasten it..."* (Qur'an 42:18)

> *"They say: 'Our Lord, hasten for us our portion [of punishment] before the Day of Account.'"* (Qur'an 38:16)

Qatādah, whose connections between passages are frequently cited by al-Ṭabarī, related this verse directly to 8:32 — the Makkans' challenge to God to rain stones upon them if the message were true. On that reading, this verse is the answer to that prayer.

*Meaning two: "that which you used to claim and allege."* On this reading, the clause states: this is the thing you asserted would never happen. You called it a fiction, a threat, a device for frightening people — and it turns out to be the only thing that is happening.

Both readings converge on the same moment: the collapse of a confident denial into an unavoidable reality.

**The Justice of Getting What You Asked For**

There is a principle here that the tradition took seriously and that deserves to be stated carefully, because it sounds harsher than it is.

A person who demands a consequence, in full knowledge of what he is demanding, and who is given exactly what he demanded, has not been wronged. The damning element in the Makkan challenge was not that they wanted evidence; it was that they wanted evidence whose arrival would destroy them, and they were willing to accept that outcome because they believed it would never come. When it comes, the transaction is complete on the terms they set.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the underlying principle of justice in general terms:

> *"Verily, God has pardoned for my community that which arises within their souls, as long as they do not speak of it or act upon it."* (al-Bukhārī 5269; Muslim 127)

and, on the gravity of what a person chooses to say:

> *"Indeed, a servant may utter a word — a single word — without thinking about it, and he falls thereby into the Fire further than the distance between the east and the west."* (al-Bukhārī 6477; Muslim 2988)

Words are not costless. The demand made in mockery is recorded as a demand, and when the demand is honored, the one who made it cannot object.

**The Suddenness Revisited**

One more feature of the verse deserves emphasis, because it is the one that makes the whole scene morally intelligible rather than merely frightening.

*Zulfatan* — drawn near. Not announced, not prepared for, not preceded by a sign the mockers would have accepted. The Qur'an's insistence on suddenness is not a literary device; it is the answer to a specific objection.

Consider what a person would do if he were told the date. If the date were far off, the information would serve no purpose except to license delay. If the date were near, the delay would be over anyway. The only state in which a person can be genuinely tested is the state of not knowing — which is why the concealment of the Hour, discussed in the previous two verses, is itself a mercy.

And the Prophet ﷺ described the arrival in the terms of ordinary, interrupted life:

> *"The Hour will be established while a man has raised a morsel of food to his mouth, and he will not get to eat it."* (al-Bukhārī 6507; Muslim 2954)

The image is not of a catastrophe approaching across a horizon. It is of a hand stopped in mid-motion.

**What This Verse Does to the Reader**

The verse's function within the sūrah is not to terrify for its own sake. It is to complete an argument that began in verse 2 with the creation of death and life as a test.

The arc runs like this: there is a test (v. 2); there are signs that establish the Tester (vv. 3–5, 15, 19, 23–24); there are warners who explained the terms (vv. 8–9, 26); there is a pattern of what happened to those who refused (v. 18); and there will be a moment at which the thing that was warned about becomes visible and the refusal becomes indefensible (v. 27).

The believer who reads this verse is meant to do one thing with it: move his own arrival forward into the present. The regret described here is not a puzzle to be solved but a fate to be avoided, and it is avoided by doing now what the people in the verse will wish they had done then. Verse 10 already gave the content of that wish in their own words — *"Had we listened or had we understood, we would not be among the inhabitants of the Blaze"* — and the listening and understanding they refer to are available, in this life, in exactly the form they were first offered.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:28

> **Say, "Have you considered whether God destroys me and those with me or has mercy upon us? Who will protect the disbelievers from a painful punishment?"**

**Arabic**

> *قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِنْ أَهْلَكَنِيَ اللَّهُ وَمَنْ مَعِيَ أَوْ رَحِمَنَا ۚ فَمَنْ يُجِيرُ الْكَافِرِينَ مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ*

**Transliteration:** *Qul a-raʾaytum in ahlakaniya Allāhu wa-man maʿiya aw raḥimanā fa-man yujīru al-kāfirīna min ʿadhābin alīm*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Opening Challenge: "Have You Considered?"**

*A-raʾaytum* — "have you seen? have you considered? tell me." The phrase is a Qur'anic formula for forcing a person to think through the implications of his own position. It is not a request for information but a demand for consistency: you have said *x*; now follow *x* to where it leads.

The construction is used repeatedly in this sūrah and its neighbors — verses 28 and 30 here, and the identical formula at 56:58 (regarding the sperm-drop), 56:63 (regarding the seed), 56:68 (regarding the water), 56:71 (regarding the fire), and 46:4 (regarding the idols). In every case, the pattern is the same: look at something you take for granted, consider what it depends on, and answer honestly about who controls it.

Here, the object of the consideration is not a created thing but a scenario: what if we perish?

**Ahlakanī: "If God Destroys Me"**

The verb *ahlaka* means to bring to ruin, to destroy, to cause to perish. The Prophet ﷺ is instructed to raise the possibility of his own death — and, notably, to raise it *first*, before the alternative of mercy.

Several considerations lie behind this.

*The Makkans were, in effect, waiting.* The reports transmitted by the commentators indicate that some of the disbelievers used to say that Muhammad ﷺ would die and his religion would die with him, and that they were content to outlast him rather than answer him. The Qur'an addresses that posture directly: suppose he does die. Suppose we perish. What then?

*It removes the person from the argument.* One of the most effective ways to avoid a message is to focus on the messenger — his lineage, his circumstances, his following, his vulnerabilities. This verse takes the messenger out of the frame entirely and asks what remains.

*It establishes the Prophet's mortality openly.* He is instructed to say it himself: God may destroy me and those with me. There is no claim of protection, no assertion of invulnerability, and no promise that this world will go well for him. That candor is part of the evidence that the message was not constructed for personal advantage.

The Qur'an states the mortality of the Prophet ﷺ without softening:

> *"Muhammad is no more than a messenger; messengers have passed away before him. So if he were to die or be killed, would you turn back on your heels?"* (Qur'an 3:144)

> *"And We did not grant to any human being before you eternity [on earth]; so if you die, would they be the ones who live forever?"* (Qur'an 21:34)

> *"Indeed, you are to die, and indeed, they are to die."* (Qur'an 39:30)

**Aw Raḥimanā: "Or Has Mercy Upon Us"**

The second branch of the alternative is *aw raḥimanā* — "or He has mercy upon us." The two branches exhaust the possibilities for this life: the believers perish, or the believers are shown mercy.

What is striking is that both branches lead to the same question. The verse is not offering a choice between a bad outcome and a good one in order to negotiate. It is saying: whichever of these happens, it has no bearing on your situation.

The underlying logic deserves to be made explicit, because it is the heart of the verse. The disbelievers' imagination was occupied entirely with the fate of the Prophet ﷺ and his small community. If they were destroyed, the objectors reasoned, the matter would be settled. The verse points out that the matter is not settled at all — because the punishment in question was never conditional on the Prophet's survival. It was conditioned on *their* response to the warning, and that response had been given.

**Fa-man Yujīru: Who Will Grant Refuge?**

The word *yujīru* is one of the most culturally loaded terms in the verse. It comes from *j-w-r*, whose primary noun is *jār*, "neighbor." In the Arab moral code, the protection of a neighbor, a guest, or a supplicant was among the most binding of obligations — a man's honor was measured by the security of those who sought his *jiwār*, his protection. To ask *man yujīru* is therefore to ask: who will take you in, who will stand between you and what is coming, who will extend to you the protection that no Bedouin would refuse even to an enemy who sought it?

The answer, of course, is no one. And the silence after the question is the verse's whole argument.

The Qur'an uses the same root for the divine attribute of giving refuge:

> *"Say: 'I seek refuge (aʿūdhu) in the Lord of daybreak...' "* (Qur'an 113:1)

> *"And He gives refuge (yujīru) and none can give refuge against Him, if you but knew."* (Qur'an 23:88)

That second verse is decisive and it is the theological key to this one: God gives protection, and *no one can protect against Him*. The entire apparatus of human protection — tribe, wealth, alliance, fortress, and army — is operable only within the sphere God permits. Outside that permission, there is no *jiwār* to be had.

And the hadith that states the personal form of this is one the Prophet ﷺ taught to a young companion:

> *"Be mindful of God, and God will protect you. Be mindful of God, and you will find Him before you. If you ask, ask of God; and if you seek help, seek help from God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516 — graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

**The Redirection of Attention**

The verse's rhetorical move can be summarized in one sentence: *stop looking at us; look at yourself.*

This is a pattern in the Qur'an's engagement with its opponents, and it is worth noting because it is so different from the ordinary dynamics of argument. The objectors attacked the Prophet ﷺ; the Qur'an does not spend verses defending him. It redirects the question to the asker's own position, over and over:

> *"And whether We show you part of what We promise them, or We take you in death, it is only upon you the clear notification, and upon Us the reckoning."* (Qur'an 13:40)

> *"Say: 'I am not the first of the messengers, and I do not know what will be done with me or with you. I only follow what is revealed to me, and I am not but a clear warner.'"* (Qur'an 46:9)

> *"Say: 'Each [of us] is waiting; so wait. You will soon know who is the companion of the level path, and who is guided.'"* (Qur'an 20:135)

The consistent message is that the fate of the messenger is not the subject under discussion. The subject is the hearer's own standing before God.

**The Psychology of Schadenfreude**

There is a psychological diagnosis embedded here that is as current as it was in seventh-century Makkah.

Human attention is drawn, with remarkable reliability, to the failures of the people we have declined to follow. The person who has rejected a path watches for evidence that those on it are hypocrites, that they have fallen, that their movement has collapsed. And when such evidence appears — and in any human community it eventually will — it produces a specific pleasure: relief that one's own refusal has been vindicated.

The verse dismantles this entirely. Suppose the worst about us, it says. Suppose we fail, we die, we are destroyed. Now answer the question that remains: who protects *you*?

The exposure is complete, because the pleasure taken in another's failure does not touch the one thing that matters. A person can be entirely right about the failings of religious people and still be entirely lost. The Qur'an's own warning on this point is severe:

> *"And do not insult those they invoke other than God, lest they insult God in enmity without knowledge."* (Qur'an 6:108)

> *"O you who believe! Avoid much suspicion; indeed, some suspicion is sin. And do not spy, and do not backbite one another..."* (Qur'an 49:12)

The community is not told that its failings will be invisible. It is told that another's failings are not a defense.

**The Two Destinies Named, and the One That Matters**

The verse names three groups in three phrases: *me and those with me* (the believing community), *us* (the same community, in the case of mercy), and *the disbelievers*. The outcome for the first two groups is left open — destruction or mercy, in this life. The outcome for the third group is not left open: *a painful punishment* from which no one can grant refuge.

The asymmetry is deliberate and it is a comfort to the believers. Whatever happens to the community of faith in this world — persecution, exile, defeat, the death of its Prophet — is one of two mercies. Either they are sustained, or they are taken to their Lord. The Qur'an says the same thing elsewhere in a single sentence that the early Muslims repeated at the death of the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"Say: 'Nothing will ever befall us except what God has decreed for us. He is our Protector.' And in God let the believers put their trust."* (Qur'an 9:51)

What is *not* open is the fate of the person who has rejected the warning. That is the only outcome in the verse that is fixed, and it is fixed by his own choice rather than by a decree he could not have avoided.

**The Pastoral Application**

Read as instruction rather than as polemic, this verse teaches a specific discipline for anyone engaged in religious conversation.

*Do not make yourself the topic.* The instinct, when mocked, is to defend oneself. The verse instructs the Prophet ﷺ to do the opposite: raise the possibility of his own destruction himself, and then turn to the question that matters.

*Do not negotiate the terms.* The verse does not offer the objectors a softer version of the message in exchange for a hearing. It restates the warning in full: a painful punishment, and no one to prevent it.

*Leave the outcome where it belongs.* The verse ends with a question whose answer is known, and it does not supply the answer. That restraint is the model. The Prophet ﷺ was told to deliver, not to compel — as the Qur'an states plainly:

> *"So remind; you are only one who reminds. You are not over them a controller."* (Qur'an 88:21–22)

There is a freedom in that instruction, and it is the freedom the verse offers to anyone who has ever been mocked for believing: the outcome does not depend on winning the argument. It depends on whether the warning was delivered clearly — which is exactly what the next verse proceeds to do.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:29

> **Say, "He is the Compassionate; we believe in Him and trust in Him, and you will soon know who is in manifest error."**

**Arabic**

> *قُلْ هُوَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ آمَنَّا بِهِ وَعَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْنَا ۖ فَسَتَعْلَمُونَ مَنْ هُوَ فِي ضَلَالٍ مُبِينٍ*

**Transliteration:** *Qul huwa al-Raḥmānu āmannā bihi wa-ʿalayhi tawakkalnā fa-sataʿlamūna man huwa fī ḍalālin mubīn*

**Expanded Commentary**

**"He Is the Compassionate"**

The verse opens with two words that carry the entire weight of the sūrah's theology: *huwa al-Raḥmān* — "He is the Compassionate." The independent pronoun *huwa* again performs its function of exclusivity: He, and not another. And the Name chosen is the one that has been threading through the sūrah from its third verse — al-Raḥmān, the Compassionate, whose Name was invoked for the flawless creation (v. 3), for the bird held in the air (v. 19), and for the only true helper (v. 20).

The choice of this Name at this point is significant in two ways.

*First, it is the answer the sūrah has been building toward.* Verses 20 and 21 asked who could protect and provide apart from the Compassionate, and the answer was silence because there is no one. This verse supplies the positive counterpart: He is the Compassionate, and therefore belief and reliance are due to Him alone.

*Second, it was a contested Name.* The Makkan Arabs were not unfamiliar with God — as noted earlier, they conceded He was the Creator. But the Name *al-Raḥmān* was, for them, either unknown or foreign, and the Qur'an records their reaction to it:

> *"And when it is said to them: 'Prostrate yourselves to the Compassionate (al-Raḥmān),' they say: 'And what is the Compassionate? Shall we prostrate to whatever you command us?' And it increases them in aversion."* (Qur'an 25:60)

> *"Say: 'Call upon God, or call upon the Compassionate. Whichever [Name] you call — to Him belong the Most Beautiful Names.'"* (Qur'an 17:110)

The historical incident that crystallizes this dispute is recorded in the account of the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah. When the Prophet ﷺ dictated the opening of the treaty to the scribe, he said:

> *"Write: 'In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful.'"* Suhayl ibn ʿAmr, the envoy of Quraysh, objected: *"As for 'the Compassionate,' by God, I do not know what it is. But write: 'In Your Name, O God,' as you used to write."* The Muslims protested, and the Messenger of God ﷺ said: *"Write: 'In Your Name, O God.'"* (al-Bukhārī 2731, 3183; Muslim 1784)

The concession concerned a written formula in a political document; it did not concern the creed. Within a few years the same Quraysh would enter Makkah with the Prophet ﷺ reciting *"In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful"* at its head. But the episode shows that the Name *al-Raḥmān* was precisely the contested ground, which is why this verse opens with it so emphatically: *He* is the Compassionate — whatever you call Him, and whether or not you recognize the Name.

**Āmannā Bihi: We Believe in Him**

*Āmannā bihi* — "we believe in Him." The verb *āmana* with the preposition *bi-* is the standard construction for faith in the Qur'an, and the choice of the preposition is meaningful: it indicates not merely assent to a proposition but a reaching out toward, an attaching oneself to, the object of faith.

Faith, in the Islamic understanding, is not a single act but a composite. The Prophet ﷺ defined it in the hadith of Jibrīl, which the scholars treated as the definitive summary of the religion:

> *"[Īmān is] that you believe in God, and His angels, and His books, and His messengers, and the Last Day, and that you believe in the divine decree, both the good of it and the evil of it."* (Muslim 8)

And he identified its practical test in a single sentence:

> *"None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself."* (al-Bukhārī 13; Muslim 45)

The verse places belief first and reliance second, and the order matters. A person cannot entrust himself to someone he does not believe in. *Tawakkul* is not a technique for managing anxiety; it is the consequence of a prior conviction about who God is. People who try to "rely upon God" without first knowing Him discover that they are relying on an idea of their own making, which collapses under pressure.

**Wa-ʿAlayhi Tawakkalnā: Upon Him We Rely**

*Tawakkul* comes from *w-k-l*, the root of *wakīl*, a trustee or agent — the one to whom an affair is entrusted. The form *tawakkala ʿalā* means to commit one's affair to someone, to lean upon him, to place the matter in his hands.

The preposition here is *ʿalā*, "upon," which conveys the image of weight being transferred: a person carrying a burden and setting it down on something capable of bearing it. The Qur'an pairs reliance with God constantly, and always in this sense:

> *"And whoever relies upon God — He is sufficient for him. Indeed, God will accomplish His purpose."* (Qur'an 65:3)

> *"And upon God let the believers rely."* (Qur'an 3:122, 3:160, 5:11, 9:51, 14:11, 58:10, 64:13)

> *"And when you have resolved, then rely upon God. Indeed, God loves those who rely [upon Him]."* (Qur'an 3:159)

The last of these is the one that resolves the apparent tension between effort and reliance. Note the sequence: *when you have resolved* — that is, after deliberation, planning, and the taking of means — *then rely upon God.* Reliance is not the abandonment of planning; it is the abandonment of anxiety about the outcome of planning.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the same balance in a single hadith that the scholars have always described as among the most comprehensive:

> *"The strong believer is better and more beloved to God than the weak believer, and in both there is good. Be eager for what benefits you, seek help from God, and do not be helpless. And if something afflicts you, do not say, 'If only I had done such-and-such, then such-and-such would have happened'; rather say, 'God has decreed, and what He wills He does' — for indeed 'if only' opens the door to Satan's work."* (Muslim 2664)

And he described the people of complete reliance in terms that define the quality precisely:

> *"Seventy thousand of my community will enter Paradise without being called to account: those who do not practice incantation, nor seek omens, nor cauterize themselves — and upon their Lord they rely."* (al-Bukhārī 5752; Muslim 220)

The three negations are the diagnostic. The person of *tawakkul* does not use incantations (because he does not seek protection from formulas), does not read omens (because he does not seek knowledge of the future through forbidden channels), and does not cauterize (which in that context meant seeking cure through means treated as though they had independent power). In each case, what is being refused is a substitute for God — which is exactly the point this sūrah has been making since verse 20.

**The Qirāʾah Variant**

The verse's closing words — *fa-sataʿlamūna man huwa fī ḍalālin mubīn*, "and you will soon know who is in manifest error" — are read in the majority of the canonical recitations as *sataʿlamūna* with the *tāʾ* of direct address: "you will know." This is the reading that makes the words part of what the Prophet ﷺ is commanded to say.

Al-Kisāʾī, one of the seven canonical readers, read it as *sayaʿlamūna* with the *yāʾ* of the third person: "soon they will know." On that reading, the command to the Prophet ﷺ ends with *wa-ʿalayhi tawakkalnā*, and the sentence "soon they will know who is in manifest error" is a statement of God — a declaration rather than an address.

The difference is subtle and the meaning is the same, but the shift in voice is worth noticing. In the majority reading, the Prophet ﷺ says it to their faces, and the warning is personal and immediate. In al-Kisāʾī's reading, the threat comes from God in the third person, and it carries the chill of a matter already settled and merely being announced.

The existence of both readings within the canonical corpus is itself a small illustration of the richness of the Qur'anic transmission: two forms, both revealed, both recited, both preserved, and neither cancelling the other.

**"Manifest Error"**

*Ḍalālun mubīn* — "clear, manifest error." The word *ḍalāl* means being lost, straying, missing the mark. *Mubīn* means clear, evident, unmistakable.

The phrase is significant because it appears in the Qur'an in two directions. It is used by the disbelievers against the messengers — *"you are in nothing but great error"* (67:9), as this sūrah recorded — and it is used by God against the disbelievers. The same phrase that the mockers used is returned to them, and the verdict is that the error is theirs.

The Qur'an records the moment when that verdict is acknowledged from the inside:

> *"They will say, as they contend with one another therein: 'By God, we were indeed in manifest error, when we equated you with the Lord of the worlds.'"* (Qur'an 26:96–98)

The acknowledgment comes from their own mouths, in the middle of the dispute, and it is too late. That is the "soon" of this verse arriving.

**The Poise of the Verse**

There is a quality in this verse that deserves to be named, because it is the emotional climax of the whole sūrah.

The Prophet ﷺ had been mocked, threatened, and told that his message was a lie and his warning an empty threat to which he could not supply a date. The instructed response is eleven words in Arabic: *He is the Compassionate; we believe in Him and upon Him we rely.*

No defense. No counterattack. No negotiation of the terms. No request for evidence to be reconsidered. Just a statement of who God is, and of what the believers therefore do.

This is the posture the Qur'an teaches throughout for the confrontation with mockery. It is stated in the most explicit terms elsewhere:

> *"And the servants of the Compassionate are those who walk upon the earth humbly, and when the ignorant address them, they say: 'Peace.'"* (Qur'an 25:63)

> *"Repel [evil] with what is better; and behold, the one between whom and you there was enmity will become as though he were a devoted friend."* (Qur'an 41:34)

> *"And when you see those who engage in [offensive] discourse concerning Our verses, then turn away from them until they enter into another conversation."* (Qur'an 6:68)

The instruction is not weakness. It is the confidence of a person who knows that the argument will be settled, and that he does not need to settle it himself. *You will soon know* is not a threat issued by someone who needs to win; it is the observation of someone who knows how the story ends.

**The Last Word Before the Last Verse**

The sūrah is drawing to a close, and this verse is its declaration of faith — its credo. It states, in a single line, the three things the entire chapter has been arguing for:

1. *He is the Compassionate* — the affirmation of God, against every rival, every idol, and every false protector named in verses 20–21.
2. *We believe in Him* — the response to the test described in verse 2, and to the warning delivered in verses 8–9.
3. *Upon Him we rely* — the answer to the fear that verses 16–17 were designed to provoke, and to the anxiety about provision addressed in verse 21.

And then the outcome: *you will soon know who is in manifest error.* The sūrah has made its case, delivered its warning, and stated its position. What remains is the final question — the one about the water, which is the simplest, most ordinary, and most unanswerable question of all.

## Sūrah al-Mulk 67:30

> **Say, "Have you considered? Were your water to vanish into the ground, then who would bring you flowing water?"**

**Arabic**

> *قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِنْ أَصْبَحَ مَاؤُكُمْ غَوْرًا فَمَنْ يَأْتِيكُمْ بِمَاءٍ مَعِينٍ*

**Transliteration:** *Qul a-raʾaytum in aṣbaḥa māʾukum ghawran fa-man yaʾtīkum bi-māʾin maʿīn*

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's Final Word Is a Question**

Sūrah al-Mulk opens with a declaration — *Blessed is He in Whose Hand lies sovereignty* — and closes with a question. That is not an accident of composition. The declaration is what God has told us about Himself; the question is what we are left to answer.

And the question is deliberately left hanging. The verse does not say "then God would bring it." It asks: *who* would bring it? The reader is expected to supply the answer, and there is only one available. To answer it aloud is to make the testimony of faith in the most concrete possible circumstance: not "God is great" in the abstract, but "God — there is no one else — could restore the water."

The pedagogical wisdom of ending on a question is considerable. A statement can be agreed to and forgotten; a question demands an answer, and the person who answers it has made the argument his own. This is why the sūrah has used the interrogative form so heavily in its second half — verses 20, 21, 22, 25, 28, and 30. The whole chapter is, in its final movement, a sustained interrogation that leaves the reader holding the conclusion.

**Ghawr: Water That Has Sunk Away**

The word *ghawr* comes from *gh-w-r*, whose verbal form *ghāra* means to sink, to descend, to disappear below the surface. The related noun *ghawr* denotes a lowland or a depression — the place where water runs to and vanishes. A *ghāʾir* is a thing that has receded.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the phrase as: if your water were to sink away into the earth so that it becomes unreachable, out of the grasp of buckets and beyond the reach of any means of drawing it. The image is not of water that has become dirty or scarce in the ordinary sense; it is of water that has gone — withdrawn into the ground, leaving the wells dry.

This is precisely the scenario the Qur'an describes elsewhere as a standing possibility rather than a hypothetical:

> *"And We sent down water from the sky in measure, and settled it in the earth; and indeed, We are able to take it away."* (Qur'an 23:18)

The parallel verse in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah uses the identical interrogative structure and supplies the divine answer explicitly:

> *"Have you considered the water that you drink? Is it you who brought it down from the rain-cloud, or are We the One Who sends it down? If We willed, We could make it bitter — so why do you not give thanks?"* (Qur'an 56:68–70)

Taken together, the two passages establish the framework: the water is sent down by God, it is settled in the earth by God, and both its quality and its availability are contingent on His will.

**Maʿīn: Flowing, Clear, and Available**

The final word of the sūrah is *maʿīn*. The commentators explained it in two complementary ways.

*The primary sense is "flowing"* — water that runs on the surface, visible and accessible, as opposed to water that has sunk below and must be sought. Al-Ṭabarī transmits this from the early authorities.

*The secondary sense is "clear, sweet, and pure"* — water that is pleasant to drink rather than brackish. Al-Ṭabarī also records this reading, and the two are not in conflict: what is being described is water that is both available and wholesome.

The same word appears once elsewhere in the Qur'an, in the description of what the people of Paradise are given to drink:

> *"There will be circulated among them a cup from a flowing spring (min maʿīn), white and delicious to those who drink."* (Qur'an 37:45–46)

The echo is deliberate and it is beautiful. The sūrah's very last word is the word the Qur'an uses for the drink of Paradise. The question — *who will bring you water, maʿīn?* — contains, in its closing syllable, the promise of what that water will be for those who answer correctly.

**The Argument From Contingency**

This verse is the sūrah's final instance of a form of reasoning that runs through the whole chapter and that the theologians later formalized. It can be stated simply.

Everything a person depends on is itself dependent. The water depends on the rain; the rain depends on the cloud; the cloud depends on the wind; the wind depends on the atmosphere; and so on, through a chain in which every link is contingent. A chain of contingent things cannot explain itself; it requires a terminus that is not contingent. The question *who would bring you flowing water?* is that argument compressed into a single, concrete, thirsty sentence.

The sūrah has used this form repeatedly, each time with a different object:

- Verse 19: who holds the bird in the air? No one but the Compassionate.
- Verse 20: who will be your army? No one.
- Verse 21: who will provide if He withholds? No one.
- Verse 28: who will grant you refuge? No one.
- Verse 30: who will bring you water? No one.

And in the last case, the object is the most ordinary and most indispensable thing in human life. The argument does not end on the stars, or on the mountains, or on the seas. It ends on a drink of water — because if the argument holds for water, it holds for everything.

**Why Water Has This Place in the Qur'an**

Water occupies a unique position in the Qur'an's vocabulary of signs, and it is worth surveying, because the final verse of this sūrah is drawing on the whole body of that imagery.

*Water is the origin of life:*

> *"And We made from water every living thing. Will they not then believe?"* (Qur'an 21:30)

*Water is the means by which dead land is revived — which is also the Qur'an's most frequently used analogy for the resurrection:*

> *"And it is He Who sends the winds as glad tidings before His mercy; and We send down pure water from the sky, that We may give life thereby to a dead land, and give it as drink to those We created — cattle and men in multitudes."* (Qur'an 25:48–49)

> *"And you see the earth barren; but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and puts forth every beautiful kind of growth. That is because God is the Truth, and because He gives life to the dead, and because He is Powerful over all things."* (Qur'an 22:5–6)

*Water is the emblem of every blessing:*

> *"And We send down from the sky blessed water, and cause to grow thereby gardens and the grain of the harvest."* (Qur'an 50:9)

> *"Have you not seen that God sends down water from the sky and makes it flow as springs in the earth; then He brings forth thereby crops of various colors?"* (Qur'an 39:21)

*Water is the means by which God tests and humbles:*

> *"And He it is Who sends down rain after they have despaired, and spreads abroad His mercy."* (Qur'an 42:28)

And withdrawal of water is one of the forms His punishment takes, as this verse implies.

**The Modern Edge of the Question**

It is impossible to read this verse in the twenty-first century without noticing that the hypothetical has, in many places, become actual.

Aquifers that took millennia to fill are being drawn down in decades. Major rivers now fail to reach the sea in dry years. Cities that were built on the assumption of permanent water have begun to ration. Deserts are advancing in some regions while others flood with increasing violence. And in the places where the water has gone, no one has brought it back — not engineering, not desalination at scale, not policy, not money. The question *fa-man yaʾtīkum bi-māʾin maʿīn* is, at this moment, being asked literally in a dozen languages by people standing at dry wells.

The Qur'an is not making a hydrological claim, and it would be wrong to read it as one. But the verse does establish a framework within which such events are to be understood: water is a trust, it is given in measure, it can be withdrawn, and its restoration belongs to the One who sent it. That framework produces an ethic — moderation in use, prohibition of waste, and gratitude — which is precisely what the tradition derived from it.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that ethic in concrete terms, even at the moment of ablution:

> *He passed by Saʿd while he was performing ablution and said: "What is this excess, O Saʿd?" He said: "Is there excess in ablution?" He said: "Yes — even if you were at a flowing river."* (Ibn Mājah 425; graded ḥasan)

Even at a river. The prohibition on waste is not a function of scarcity; it is a function of the giver.

And he identified the charity that matters most:

> *Saʿd ibn ʿUbādah said: "O Messenger of God, my mother has died. Shall I give charity on her behalf?" He said: "Yes." He said: "Then which charity is best?" He said: "Giving water to drink."* (Abū Dāwūd 1681; al-Nasāʾī 3664 — graded ḥasan)

The best charity, in the Prophet's judgment, is the thing this verse names as most precious and most precarious.

**The Closing Frame of the Sūrah**

Consider the arc from beginning to end.

The sūrah opened with *tabāraka* — blessing, abounding and abiding. The word's own root, as noted at the outset, is connected to *birkah*, the pool where water gathers and stays.

It closes by imagining water receding into the ground.

Between those two poles, the sūrah has traversed sovereignty, creation, death and life as a test, the flawless heavens, the guarded sky, the Fire and its confession, the mercy held for those who fear God unseen, the knowledge that reaches into the breasts, the tractable earth, the sudden punishments, the birds, the false protectors, the faculties of hearing and sight and heart, the gathering, and the Hour.

And it ends where it began, on the same note it struck in the first verse: everything you have is held by a Hand, and the Hand can open or close. The One in Whose Hand is sovereignty (v. 1) is the One in Whose Hand is the water (v. 30). To confess the first is to have no difficulty with the second.

**The Reciter's Last Thought**

There is a final application specific to this sūrah, and it is the reason the verse deserves to be read slowly by anyone who recites al-Mulk at night as the Prophet ﷺ did.

The Sunnah, as recorded from Jābir, was that the Prophet ﷺ would not sleep until he had recited Sūrah al-Sajdah and Sūrah al-Mulk:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ would not sleep until he had recited 'Alif Lām Mīm — The revelation' and 'Blessed is He in Whose Hand is sovereignty.'"* (al-Tirmidhī 2892 — graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

Consider what a person who follows that practice is given as his final conscious thought before sleep. Not a summary of the day's failures. Not a plan for tomorrow. Not a list of unfinished business. But a question, asked by God, about the one thing without which he would not live through the night:

*Were your water to vanish into the ground, who would bring you flowing water?*

And the answer, if it is honest, is the answer of the whole religion. It is the answer Abraham gave when he was thrown into the fire and left without any means; the answer Moses gave at the sea with the army behind him; the answer the Prophet ﷺ gave in the cave when the searchers were at the entrance:

> *"Do not grieve; indeed God is with us."* (Qur'an 9:40)

It is the answer of a person who has read thirty verses about sovereignty, mercy, death, and return, and who now closes the Book in the dark, entrusts the water and the breathing and the morning to the One who owns them, and sleeps.

*Fa-man yaʾtīkum bi-māʾin maʿīn?*

> *"Say: 'He will give life to them Who produced them the first time; and He is, of all creation, Knowing.'"* (Qur'an 36:79)
