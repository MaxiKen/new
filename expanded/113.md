# Sūrah al-Falaq (Chapter 113) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Chapter of the Dawn That Dispels the Dark**

Sūrah al-Falaq, "The Daybreak," is the 113th chapter of the Qur'an and the first of the two short chapters that close the Book. It takes its name from the word *al-falaq* in its very first verse, where the Prophet ﷺ is commanded to say, *"I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak."* The name is not incidental: it captures the central movement of the entire sūrah, which is the believer's flight from every form of darkness — physical, moral, occult, and spiritual — into the light of the One who splits the dawn and thereby separates night from day, confusion from clarity, and harm from safety. The sūrah is paired with the one that follows it, Sūrah al-Nās (Chapter 114), and together the two are called **al-Muʿawwidhatān**, "The Two Seekers of Refuge" or "The Two Protectors." They are unique in the Qur'an as chapters given wholly to the grammar of *seeking refuge* (*taʿawwuḍh*), and Muslims have recited them as a daily shield against evil from the time of the Prophet ﷺ until now.

**When and Where It Was Revealed**

The overwhelming majority of the classical commentators place Sūrahs 113 and 114 in the **Makkan period**. This is the view of al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and the great majority of the exegetical tradition. However, the question is not closed by consensus. Ibn Kathīr (IK) and al-Rāzī (R) are among those who, on the strength of the well-known *asbāb al-nuzūl* account — the account of the spell cast upon the Prophet ﷺ by a Jew of Yathrib, which fits the chronology of the Madinan years — inclined toward a Madinan dating. Others, such as al-Bayḍāwī (Bḍ), al-Zamakhsharī (Bg), and al-Zajjāj (Z), left the matter open, acknowledging that the evidence, while pointing strongly toward Makkah, does not amount to certainty. The practical consequence of this scholarly disagreement is nil for the believer: regardless of the season of its descent, the sūrah is undeniably Qur'anic, and its protective function is unaffected by the dating debate. What matters far more than the date is the occasion that the tradition has preserved, to which we now turn.

**The Occasion of Revelation: The Spell and the Cure**

The most widely transmitted *asbāb al-nuzūl* for these two sūrahs concerns an event in which the Prophet ﷺ fell gravely ill as the result of *sihr* — black magic — worked against him. The narrator is ʿĀʾishah, the Mother of the Believers (may God be pleased with her), and the account is recorded in the two most authentic collections. In al-Bukhārī's wording:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ was bewitched until he imagined that he had done something which he had not done. Then one day — or one night — while he was with me, he prayed and then came to me and lay down. Then he suddenly got up and said: 'O ʿĀʾishah, do you know that God has shown me the truth regarding that about which I asked Him? Two men came to me, one sat at my head and the other at my feet. One of them said to the other: "What is the illness of this man?" The other said: "He has been bewitched." He said: "And who bewitched him?" He said: "Labīd ibn al-Aʿṣam, a man of the Banū Zuraysh (a branch of the Jews of the Banū Qaynuqāʿ)." He said: "With what?" He said: "With a comb, and the hair stuck to it, and a spathe of a male date-palm, under a stone in a well called Dharwān."' Then the Messenger of God ﷺ went to the well, and he said: 'This is the well which was shown to me. Its water is like the dye of henna, and its date-palm trees are like the heads of devils.' Then he ordered that the (knots) be brought out and untied, and God healed him. Then the Messenger of God ﷺ said: 'Indeed God healed me, and I concealed it from the people, and I did not want the Banū Zuraysh to be praised for it (i.e., to have an evil reputation spread) nor to be known for it.'"* (al-Bukhārī, *Kitāb al-Ṭibb*, no. 5765; cf. Muslim, *al-Salām*, no. 2189)

According to the early commentators, the Archangel Gabriel came to the Prophet ﷺ and disclosed both the location of the knotted string upon which the sorcerer had whispered his incantation and the two sūrahs by which the spell was to be undone. The Prophet ﷺ sent ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib to recover the string, and as the fourteen verses of the two sūrahs were recited over it, the fourteen knots came loose one by one. This detail — fourteen verses undoing fourteen knots — is preserved by al-Qurṭubī and stands as a quiet testimony that the remedy God provides is precise, proportionate, and complete: every knot of harm has its counterpart in a word of healing.

**The Muʿawwidhatān in the Life of the Prophet ﷺ**

The protective role of these two chapters is fixed by multiple authentic reports. ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir relates that the Prophet ﷺ said to him:

> *"Do you know what has been revealed to me tonight? Two sūrahs the like of which has not been revealed in the Torah, nor in the Gospel, nor in the Psalms, nor the Qur'an: 'Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak' (113), and 'Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of mankind' (114)."* (Muslim, *al-Muʿawwidhatān*, no. 814; al-Tirmidhī, graded *ṣaḥīḥ*)

This report is remarkable: the Prophet ﷺ is told that no scripture prior to the Qur'an was given a parallel to these two chapters. Their uniqueness lies precisely in their function — they are a direct, divine, repeatable weapon against the hidden harms that assail a human being.

The Prophet ﷺ also established the daily practice of reciting them. In the wording of al-Tirmidhī (graded *ḥasan*), Abū Dāwūd, and al-Nasāʾī:

> *"Say: 'He is God, One' (112), and the two seekers of refuge (113 and 114) when you enter the evening and when you enter the morning, for they suffice you against everything."* (al-Tirmidhī, no. 3575; Abū Dāwūd, no. 5082; al-Nasāʾī)

And the Prophet ﷺ himself used them as a *ruqyah* (a healing recitation) for his two grandsons, al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī reports the wording:

> *"I seek refuge for you both in the perfect words of God, from every devil and poisonous creature, and from every evil eye."* Then he said: *"Thus Ibrāhīm used to seek refuge for Isḥāq and Ismāʿīl."* (al-Bukhārī, no. 3371; Muslim, no. 2186)

In his final illness, the Prophet ﷺ would blow over himself and wipe himself with his hands as he recited the Muʿawwidhatān — and when he could no longer do so, ʿĀʾishah would recite them over him and wipe his body with his own hand, as she recounts in al-Bukhārī (no. 5731). This is the earthly bookend to the account of his own bewitchment: the man once healed by these words spent his last days healing himself with them.

**The Canonical Debate: Were They "Revealed" or Merely a Supplication?**

The early community did not treat these chapters carelessly, and a striking report shows the seriousness with which the Companions guarded the integrity of the Qur'an. There was a difference of recension among the Companions: ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, in his personal codex and in his teaching, reportedly did not include the Muʿawwidhatān, treating them as a special supplication rather than as part of the Qur'anic text. This was not a denial of their sanctity but a difference in codicological practice. Zirr ibn Hubaysh narrates how the matter was resolved:

> *"I asked Ubayy ibn Kaʿb: 'O Abā al-Mundhir! Your brother Ibn Masʿūd said so-and-so — that "The Two Protectors" do not belong to the Qur'an.' Ubayy said: 'I asked God's Messenger ﷺ about them, and he said: "They have been revealed to me, and I have recited them (as part of the Qur'an)."' Then Ubayy added: 'So we say regarding these verses what God's Messenger ﷺ has said.'"* (recorded by Ibn Ḥajar in *Fatḥ al-Bārī* and traced to the early sources)

The resolution is decisive for the *ʿaqīdah* of the community: the decisive criterion is not a Companion's private codex but the explicit statement of the Prophet ﷺ that these verses were *revealed* to him and that he recited them *as Qur'an*. The eventual consensus (*ijmāʿ*) of the *ummah* placed them firmly within the Book. The episode is itself a lesson in how the Muslim community safeguards revelation: differences in personal practice never override the explicit word of the Messenger ﷺ, and the preservation of the text rested ultimately on his ﷺ testimony, not on the notebook of any individual Companion.

**Why the Chapter Matters for the Modern Believer**

Sūrah al-Falaq speaks to a permanent human condition. Every age has its darknesses — the unseen harms that strike from the cover of night, the secret malice of those who scheme behind closed doors, the quiet corrosion of envy that eats away at the bonds between people. The sūrah does not tell the believer to pretend these dangers do not exist, nor does it license fear or superstition. Instead it performs a single, repeated, disciplined act: it locates *every* danger — general and specific, cosmic and intimate — and then anchors the believer's protection in *one* place: the Lord of the daybreak. The structure of the sūrah is a funnel. It begins with the widest possible sweep ("from the evil of what He has created"), then narrows through the darkness of night, to the blowers upon knots (occult practitioners), and finally to the most personal and pervasive evil of all — the envier in our midst. The movement teaches that nothing is too large for God's protection and nothing too small to be named before Him.

What follows is the verse-by-verse expanded commentary. Each verse is treated as an independent, fully developed discourse, drawing upon the Arabic language, the classical exegetes (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿAbbās, and others), the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, and the living reasoning that connects this fourteen-hundred-year-old text to the conditions of the modern human being.

## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:1

> **Say, "I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Proclaim, Not Merely to Pray**

The sūrah opens with the single word *qul* — "Say." This is one of the most frequent divine imperatives in the Qur'an, appearing in well over three hundred places, and its function here is the same as everywhere else: it is God instructing His Messenger ﷺ to *utter* a message aloud, to declare it, to make it a public and repeatable formula. *Qul* signals that what follows is not a private whisper of the heart alone but a spoken weapon, a liturgical sentence to be placed on the tongue and carried into the world. For the believer who recites this sūrah behind the Prophet's ﷺ instruction, the word *qul* collapses the distance between then and now: when we say "Say: I seek refuge…," we are obeying the same command, taking the Prophet's ﷺ words into our own mouths. The sūrah is therefore inherently communal and trans-historical — it was revealed to be said, and said again, by every generation.

**The Grammar of Protection: What "I Seek Refuge" Really Means**

The next word is *aʿūdh* — "I seek refuge," from the root *ʿ-w-dh* (ع-و-ذ), which carries the sense of fleeing to a secure place, of resorting to a shelter, of taking something strong as one's guard. In classical Arabic, *iʿādhah* is what one does when one places a weak person under the protection of a strong one, or when a traveler seeks the shelter of a wall against the wind. The verb is in the first-person singular: the act of seeking refuge is intensely personal. No one can seek refuge *for* you in the deepest sense; the heart that wants protection must itself turn. And yet the object of the refuge is *bi* — "in" — the Lord of the daybreak. The preposition *bi* denotes clinging and attaching oneself to a thing for security, the way one clings to a fortress. The believer does not seek refuge *from* God (as though He were the threat); he seeks refuge *in* God (because He is the only secure ground). This single preposition quietly answers a thousand superstitions: the refuge is not in a charm, a star, a saint, or a spell — it is in the Lord Himself.

**The Word Rabb: The One Who Nourishes, Owns, and Brings to Perfection**

The object of refuge is *Rabb* — "the Lord" — of the daybreak. *Rabb* is one of the most loaded words in the Arabic Qur'an. From the root *r-b-b*, it means the owner, the master, the sustainer, the one who brings something up from nothing to its full maturity. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī and the lexicographers note that *tarbiyyah* is the gradual nurture of a thing toward its perfection, the way a shepherd tends a flock or a gardener tends a plant. To say "Lord of the daybreak" is thus to say: the One to whom I flee is the Possessor and Sustainer of the dawn itself — the dawn did not make itself, and it cannot protect itself; it is owned and sustained by the same God who now receives my plea. This is the first logical move of the sūrah: before naming any danger, it fixes the identity of the Protector. A person who cannot name what protects him will clutch at anything; the sūrah begins by naming Him precisely.

**Al-Falaq: The Cleaving of the Dawn**

Now the pivotal word: *al-falaq*. The commentators explain that *falaq* here means "the light of dawn" or "the rising dawn" (Sh). The root verb *falaqa* (ف-ل-ق) means "to cleave" or "to split" — to rend something into two. In the physical world, the dawn is that moment when the light appears to *cleave* the dark belly of the night, splitting it open so that day can pour through. This is why God describes Himself elsewhere, in a verse the commentators cite as the lexical key to this very word:

> *"He is the Cleaver of the daybreak (fāliq al-iṣbaḥ), and He has made the night for rest, and the sun and the moon for reckoning."* (Qur'an 6:96)

The same root appears in the description of the splitting of the date-stone (*falaq al-nawā*), the splitting open of the earth for the sprout, and — most theologically — in the splitting of the Red Sea for Moses. *Falaq* is therefore the principle of emergence: the moment when what was hidden, compressed, and dark breaks open into light and relief. The Lord of the daybreak is the Lord of *all* such breakings-open: the breaking of a long trial, the breaking of despair by hope, the breaking of ignorance by knowledge, the breaking of falsehood by truth.

**Al-Falaq as a Name of Hell: The Reverse Image**

The richness of the word does not stop at the dawn. Several of the great exegetes — al-Qurṭubī, al-Shawkānī, and al-Ṭabarī among them — report that *al-falaq* is also taken by some authorities as a *name of Hell*, or as a particular prison within Hell, or even as one of the trees or mountains of the Fire. Ibn ʿAbbās is cited in this connection. Why would the same word mean both the dawn and a region of Hell? Because *falaq* in its root sense is "that which is cleft/split open." Just as the dawn is the cleft of light, Hell is — in one image — the cleft that splits open to receive the doomed, the place where the earth itself is rent. The juxtaposition is not a contradiction but a theological depth-charge: the very word that names the believer's refuge (the Lord of the dawn) also names, in its other sense, the destination from which he seeks to be saved. The One who owns the dawn also owns the Fire; seeking refuge in Him is seeking refuge from both the darkness of this world and the Fire of the next. The word holds both mercies and warnings in a single syllable.

**The Daybreak as the Triumph of Truth Over Falsehood**

The metaphor deepens when *falaq* is read, as the lexicographers of *Tāj al-ʿArūs* note, as "the emergence of truth after uncertainty." Under this reading, "the Lord of the daybreak" is "the Lord of the emergence of truth from the fog of doubt." This connects directly to one of the Qur'an's central declarations about its own mission:

> *"Truth has come, and falsehood has vanished. Indeed, falsehood is ever vanishing."* (Qur'an 17:81)

To seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak is, then, to seek refuge in *truth* against *falsehood* — to place oneself under the light that dispels confusion. The darkness of night in this sūrah is not merely the absence of sunlight; it is the absence of clarity, the cover under which error, whispering, and envy breed. The dawn is the victory of discernment. The believer who opens this sūrah is, in effect, asking to be kept on the side of what is real and illuminated, and to be rescued from what thrives only in obscurity.

**Why the Lord of the Daybreak, and Not Simply "the Lord"?**

One may ask: if God is the Lord of all things, why specify "the Lord of the daybreak" rather than simply "the Lord"? The specificity is itself the lesson. The spell that afflicted the Prophet ﷺ operated at night, in a well, under a stone, in darkness — the kingdom of *ghāsiq* (the dark), which the sūrah will name explicitly in verse 3. The cure that God sends names the *exact opposite* of that darkness: the daybreak. It is as if God is saying: the harm came under cover of night; I answer it with the power that splits the night. The choice of "Lord of the daybreak" tells the believer that his protection is not generic but *targeted* — God meets the specific shape of the danger with the specific shape of His light. For the modern reader this is a quiet reassurance: the God who answers prayer is not distant and indifferent; He names the precise darkness you face and brings against it the precise dawn you need.

**The Psychological Logic of Beginning With Light**

There is a deep psychological wisdom in making the dawn the first image of protection. Human beings are most vulnerable in the dark — not only physically but emotionally. Fear, uncertainty, and the sense of threat swell at night, when the senses are narrowed and the imagination runs unguarded. The dawn is the universally shared symbol of relief: the long night is over, the predators retire, the path becomes visible, and the traveler can move without stumbling. By fixing the mind's eye on the Lord *of the daybreak*, the sūrah trains the believer to meet fear with the image of coming light. It is the spiritual equivalent of a person driving through a dangerous mountain pass at night who keeps his confidence by remembering that the road is real, the destination exists, and daylight — with its clarity and safety — is certain to arrive. *Taqwā*, mindfulness of God, is exactly this: driving through the dangerous pass of life with the headlights of His promise on, knowing the dawn belongs to Him.

**The Theological Anchor: Refuge Is in God Alone**

Verse 1 also quietly settles a question of *ʿaqīdah* (creed). The sūrah is revealed as a protection against magic, the evil eye, and envy — all of which the pre-Islamic Arabs (and many cultures since) addressed through counter-spells, amulets, and the invocation of lesser powers. The opening verse insists that the refuge is *bi-Rabb al-falaq* — in God alone. The words of the sūrah are not themselves a magic talisman that works by some impersonal force; they work because they attach the speaker to the Living God who authored them. Ibn Taymiyyah and the scholars of the *salaf* were emphatic on this point: a *ruqyah* is only valid when it consists of the words of God, His Names and Attributes, or understandable, permitted speech, and when the believer's heart trusts in God through those words — not in the words as independent objects of power. Verse 1 establishes this orientation from the first breath: the daybreak belongs to its Lord, and so does the help that comes with it.

**The Verse in the Daily Life of the Believer**

Practically, this verse invites a habit: to begin the engagement with any danger — a frightening diagnosis, a hostile environment, a season of confusion — by naming the Lord of the dawn as one's refuge. The Prophet ﷺ taught, as noted in the introduction, that the Muʿawwidhatān "suffice you against everything" when recited morning and evening. The sufficiency is not magical immunity from all hardship; it is the sufficiency of a settled heart that knows where its protection lives. A believer who has said *aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi al-falaq* has, in that moment, refused to hand his fear over to panic, to superstition, or to despair. He has placed it, deliberately, in the hands of the One who splits the dawn. That placement is itself the first half of the cure.

**A Closing Reflection on the Funnel of the Sūrah**

Finally, notice the architecture. This first verse names the Protector and the act of refuge. The four verses that follow will name four categories of danger, each introduced by *min sharri* — "from the evil of." The sūrah is therefore a crescendo of *naming*: name the Refuge, then name the dangers one by one, so that none of them remains a shapeless terror in the shadows. By the time the sūrah ends, the believer has brought every category of harm — cosmic, nocturnal, occult, and interpersonal — into the light of speech and surrendered it to God. Verse 1 is the doorway; what follows is the careful sweeping of the house.
## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:2

> **from the evil of what He has created,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Category of Danger: Creation Itself**

Having named the Refuge in verse 1, the sūrah now names the first and widest circle of danger: *min sharri mā khalaqa* — "from the evil of what He has created." The preposition *min* ("from") and the noun *sharr* ("evil") will recur in every remaining verse, binding the whole chapter into a single chain of protection. Here, at the head of that chain, the scope is maximal: *mā khalaqa* — "what He created" — encompasses everything that exists besides God, because everything besides God is His creation. The verse is a deliberate sweep of the widest net. Before descending to specific threats (night, sorcerers, enviers), the believer asks protection from the harmful dimension of *all* created things. It is the spiritual equivalent of a traveler, before detailing each hazard of the road, first praying for safety from "whatever the journey may bring."

**The Word Khalaqa and the Totality of the Created Order**

*Khalaqa* (خ-ل-ق) means to create, to bring into being from nothing, to fashion with wise measure. The Qur'an repeatedly emphasizes that God's creation is total and purposeful:

> *"And He created everything, and determined it with [precise] determination."* (Qur'an 25:2)

> *"Were they created by nothing, or were they the creators [of themselves]? Or did they create the heavens and the earth? Rather, they are not certain [in faith]."* (Qur'an 52:35–36)

Because "what He has created" includes the angels and the heavens, the oceans and the mountains, the human body and the human mind — the believer is here seeking refuge from the *evil* that can attach to any of these. The evil is not in the essence of creation (for all that God made is, in its origin, good and purposeful) but in the *harmful potential* that resides in certain created things when they are turned loose upon the unprotected servant: the claw of the predator, the venom of the creature, the storm, the disease, the whispering of the devil, and the sinful inclination of the human heart. The verse does not say "from what He created" as though creation were evil; it says "from the *evil* of what He created" — a precise distinction that the next sections will unpack.

**The Exegetes' Range of Interpretations**

The classical commentators did not reduce this verse to a single meaning; they enumerated several layers, each valid, each deepening the others. Al-Ṭabarī gathers the principal views:

- **A reference to Satan and his progeny** — the chief created source of evil in the moral order. Iblīs is a created being (a jinn), and his mission is to lead mankind astray; seeking refuge "from the evil of what He has created" is, on this reading, a plea against the deceiver and his host.
- **A reference to the Hellfire** — the ultimate created locus of punishment, from whose evil every believer hopes to be shielded.
- **A general reference to all that is evil** — every harmful thing in existence, without specification.
- **A reference to all the evil committed or manifested by those whom God has created** — that is, not the creatures themselves but the evil *acts* that issue from created wills.

Al-Zajjāj and others add a further, subtle layer: creation itself acts as a *veil* — it both hides the Creator (by occupying the eye with the visible) and reveals His Qualities and Attributes (by displaying His wisdom, power, and mercy). The "evil" one seeks refuge from, on this reading, includes the danger of being so absorbed in the creature that one forgets the Creator, or of mistaking the gift for the Giver. The verse thus operates on every level at once: physical harm, moral harm, and the harm of heedlessness.

**The Theological Problem: If God Created Evil, How Do We Seek Refuge From It in Him?**

This verse raises the oldest question in theology: if God created everything, and evil exists, does God "create" evil, and if so, how can we seek refuge from His own creation *in* Him? The Qur'an and thescholars answer with a careful distinction that protects both God's absolute power and His absolute goodness. God is the Creator of all *things* — including the vehicles and capacities through which harm occurs (fire, the serpent's fang, the human capacity to choose wrongly, the existence of Iblīs). But *sharr* (evil) as a moral or harmful quality is not an independent partner to God; it is a permitted dimension within a wise, purposeful order. The Qur'an declares:

> *"Whatever good befalls you is from God, and whatever evil befalls you is from yourself."* (Qur'an 4:79)

This does not contradict "the evil of what He has created," because the verse speaks of the *created thing's* harmful potential, while 4:79 speaks of the *moral attribution* of evil deeds to the human will. God creates the furnace; the sinner walks into it by his own choice. God creates the capacity for envy; the envier activates it. The believer's refuge, then, is not from a God who is the author of injustice, but from the harmful consequences of a created world whose every thread is in God's hand — and precisely *because* every thread is in His hand, He alone can shield the servant from being burned by it. The refuge is rational: only the Owner of the furnace can keep you from the fire.

**The Wisdom of a General Clause Before the Specifics**

Structurally, verse 2 is the broad canopy under which verses 3, 4, and 5 are the specific beams. The sūrah moves from the universal to the particular: first "all created evil," then "the darkness of night," then "those who blow on knots," then "the envier." Why begin with the widest claim? Because the specific dangers that follow are all *instances* of the general one. Night is part of "what He created"; the sorcerer is part of "what He created"; the envier is part of "what He created." By stating the general first, the sūrah assures the believer that *no* evil — however strange, however personal, however hidden — falls outside the protection being sought. There is no loophole through which a danger could say, "that verse did not name me." The general clause closes every loophole; the specific verses then show the believer the *shapes* the general danger typically takes, so he can recognize them when they appear.

**Cross-References: The Created Order and Its Perils**

The Qur'an elsewhere confirms that created things can indeed be sources of harm from which the servant needs protection. Consider:

> *"And from the evil of the retreating whisperer — who whispers in the breasts of mankind — from among the jinn and mankind."* (Qur'an 114:4–5)

This pairing (in the following sūrah) makes explicit what 113:2 implies: among "what He has created" are the whispering devils, both jinn and human. And the very purpose of creation is described without apology for its hardships:

> *"He who created death and life to test you [as to] which of you is best in deed."* (Qur'an 67:2)

The test requires the *possibility* of harm; a world without any capacity for evil would be a world without moral trial, and therefore without moral growth. Seeking refuge "from the evil of what He has created" is thus not a rejection of the created order but a petition to pass through it safely — to be tested without being destroyed, harmed without being overcome.

**Ibn Kathīr and the Comprehensive Reading**

Ibn Kathīr, synthesizing the earlier reports, settles on the comprehensive reading: the verse covers all evil that God created in the world — the devils, the harmful beasts, the calamities, and the evil inclinations of men — and it is a master-key that the four following specifics merely particularize. Al-Qurṭubī adds that the phrase teaches *tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah* in action: by seeking refuge from created evil *in the Creator*, the believer affirms that nothing in creation has autonomous power to harm apart from God's permission (*idhn*). A scorpion's sting, a tyrant's rage, a sorcerer's spell — none of these can touch the servant except by the leave of the Lord of the daybreak. The refuge is therefore also a confession: *You alone govern even the harm, and to You alone I turn against it.*

**The Psychological and Practical Payoff**

For the modern believer, verse 2 performs an act of *naming and surrendering*. Anxiety often grows in proportion to vagueness: the undefined "something bad" looms larger than any defined threat. By praying "from the evil of what He has created," the believer drags the formless dread into the light of speech and hands it to God. He is not denying that the world contains real dangers — fire burns, poisons kill, malice wounds — but he is refusing to let those dangers dictate his interior state. He acknowledges the full catalogue of possible harm and then places the whole catalogue under divine protection. This is the difference between a person who is *afraid of everything* and a person who is *aware of everything but anchored in God*. The first is paralyzed; the second is free to act, because his security does not depend on the absence of danger but on the presence of the Refuge.

**A Modern Analogy: The Laboratory and the Shield**

Think of the created world as a vast laboratory containing both medicines and toxins. God created both the penicillin and the pathogen; both are "what He has created." The believer does not pray to abolish the laboratory — that would abolish the medicines too — but to be protected from the toxins while benefiting from the cures. Verse 2 is the request for the shield that lets you walk the laboratory without being poisoned. It is a profoundly balanced posture: realistic about danger, confident in the Protector, grateful for the good, guarded against the harmful. It refuses both the naive optimism that denies evil and the paranoid fear that magnifies it.

**Transition to the Specifics**

Having cast the widest net, the sūrah now tightens it. The next three verses will name the concrete forms in which "the evil of what He has created" most often strikes the human being: the cover of night, the whispered spell, and the envious heart. Each is a face of the same general danger; each receives its own targeted plea. Verse 2 is the umbrella; what follows is the rain it was made to withstand.
## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:3

> **from the evil of darkness when it enshrouds,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Descent Into the Night**

The sūrah now narrows from the universal ("what He has created") to a concrete and ancient human fear: the dark. *Wa-min sharri ghāsiqin idhā waqaba* — "and from the evil of the dark (or the moon) when it sets." The verse connects directly back to verse 1: the refuge is in the *Lord of the daybreak*, and the first specific danger named is the *darkness* that the daybreak dispels. The structure could not be tighter. The Protector is the One who brings the light; the peril is the thing the light destroys. To seek refuge in the Lord of the dawn is, by immediate implication, to seek refuge from the reign of night.

**The Word Ghāsiq: Darkness, the Moon, and What Sets**

*Ghāsiq* (غاسق) is explained by the majority of commentators as "the darkness of night" — the blackness that spreads when the sun is gone. The root *gh-s-q* conveys intensity and sinking, the way darkness "sinks" over the land. However, the exegetes preserve a celebrated alternative rooted in a report from the Prophet ﷺ himself. ʿĀʾishah (may God be pleased with her) narrates that the Messenger of God ﷺ looked toward the moon and said to her:

> *"O ʿĀʾishah, seek refuge in God from this, for this is al-ghāsiq when it sets (waqaba)."* (recorded by al-Qurṭubī and traced through the chains; al-Ṭabarī mentions the report)

On this reading, *al-ghāsiq* is the **moon**, specifically at the moment it sets — *idhā waqaba*. The verb *waqaba* (و-ق-ب) means to enter, to sink, to set — as the sun or moon "enters" below the horizon. A minority of commentators took the verse even further, reading *ghāsiq* as **the Pleiades (al-Thurayyā)** when it sets, because of a folk belief that disease and pestilence became prevalent during the period when that luminous star-cluster declined in the sky. Al-Ṭabarī records these variant interpretations without forcing a single choice, and the classical method tolerates them precisely because the protective intent of the verse covers all of them: whether the "darkness" is the night itself, the setting moon, or a waning constellation, the believer seeks refuge from the harm associated with the hour when light withdraws.

**Why the Night Is the Hour of Evil**

The sūrah singles out darkness not because night is evil in itself — God made the night for rest, as 6:96 declares — but because darkness is the *condition* under which evil-doers operate. Cover enables wrongdoing. A thief prefers the unlit street; a conspirator prefers the closed room; the sorcerer works his knots by the well in the dark. The Qur'an itself draws this connection in passages the commentators cite alongside this verse. Speaking of those who plot against the Messenger ﷺ:

> *"They conspire by night (yakhāṣimūna fīhi wa-yamkuruuna bihi bi-al-layli) with a plotting of which He [God] is not pleased."* (Qur'an 4:81, as glossed by the commentators in the context of nightly conspiracy)

And again, describing the hypocrites and wrongdoers who shrink from the light:

> *"They conceal their conversation [by night] from Him…"* (Qur'an 4:108)

The pairing of "night" with "conspiracy" and "concealment" is no accident. Darkness is the natural ally of everything that fears exposure. By seeking refuge "from the evil of the dark when it enshrouds," the believer is asking God to protect him from all that thrives in concealment — the plotted harm, the unseen ambush, the secret sin that spreads when no one is watching.

**The Setting of Light as a Symbol of Vulnerability**

There is a deeper, almost existential reading. *Idhā waqaba* — "when it sets" — marks the moment of transition, the vulnerable cusp when one source of safety (light) withdraws and another danger (darkness) arrives. Human life is full of such cusps: the loss of a job, the death of a protector, the fading of youth, the onset of illness, the loneliness of a strange place. These are the "settings" of our personal light, and they are precisely when harm finds its opening. The verse trains the believer to recognize the pattern — *when the light withdraws, seek the Lord of the dawn* — rather than to succumb to fear at the very moment protection is most needed. The dark is not the end of the story; it is the signal to call upon the One who ends all darkness.

**The Moon, the Eclipse, and the Prophet's Practice**

The report that the Prophet ﷺ pointed to the moon and taught *al-ghāsiq when it sets* connects this verse to a living practice. The Prophet ﷺ instructed his community, when the sun or moon is eclipsed, not to treat the eclipse as an omen of someone's death (a pre-Islamic superstition) but to turn to prayer and remembrance of God. In the ḥadīth of Abū Masʿūd al-Anṣārī:

> *"The sun and the moon are two signs of God; they are not eclipsed for the death or life of anyone. So when you see them, call upon God, magnify Him, pray, and give charity."* (al-Bukhārī, no. 1044; Muslim, no. 912)

Thus the "setting moon" of this verse becomes, in the Sunnah, an occasion not of fear but of *taʿawwuḍh* and *ṣalāt al-kusūf* — a retreat to God exactly at the moment light withdraws. The verse and the Sunnah together teach a single discipline: when the heavenly light dims, do not worship the dark; seek refuge in the Lord of the dawn.

**Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and the Layered Meanings**

Al-Ṭabarī presents the interpretations in sequence — darkness of night; the moon at setting; the Pleiades — and notes that the grammatical form (*ghāsiq*, a descriptor of what "sets") accommodates all of them. Ibn Kathīr favors the primary meaning of "the darkness of night" while acknowledging the report about the moon, and he ties the verse firmly to the theme of protection from the hidden harms that move under cover of dark. Al-Qurṭubī weaves in the *ḥadīth* of ʿĀʾishah and the eclipse practice, showing that the verse is not a theoretical abstraction but a directive with a corresponding act of worship. The convergence of the commentators is that the protective petition covers *every* diminution of light — literal and moral — and that the proper response to any such diminution is to turn to God.

**The Moral and Spiritual Darkness**

Beyond the physical night, the verse speaks to *moral* darkness — ignorance, heedlessness, and the "night" of the soul that settles when a person turns away from guidance. Just as physical darkness hides the path and invites stumbles, spiritual darkness hides the truth and invites sin. The Prophet ﷺ described the condition of the one far from remembrance in terms of inward gloom. Seeking refuge "from the evil of darkness" therefore includes a prayer against the inner night: the confusion that makes wrong seem right, the despair that makes sin seem inevitable, the isolation that makes a person forget he is watched by God. The Lord of the daybreak is also the Lord of *al-ḥudā* — guidance — and the believer asks Him to keep the inward dawn from being swallowed.

**Psychological Insight: Fear of the Dark and the Need for Light**

Modern psychology confirms what the ancients knew: humans are biologically and emotionally more anxious in darkness. The narrowing of vision, the amplification of sound, the activation of the threat-response system — all make the night a crucible of fear. The sūrah does not condemn this fear or tell the believer to "man up" and ignore it. Instead it *sanctifies* the fear by giving it a proper object: do not fear the dark as though it had power of its own; fear its Author, and seek refuge in Him. This is the healthy route out of anxiety. A person who is taught to dread the dark for its own sake becomes superstitious, seeing demons in every shadow. A person taught by this verse to say *aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi al-falaq min sharri ghāsiqin idhā waqaba* transforms dread into devotion. The fear is acknowledged, named, and surrendered — which is the only way fear is ever dissolved.

**Modern Applications: The Darkness of Secrecy and Isolation**

In the contemporary world, the "darkness when it enshrouds" wears new clothes. It is the closed-door meeting where a decision is made to cheat you. It is the anonymous comment section where strangers conspire to destroy a reputation. It is the hidden surveillance, the undisclosed contract, the unlit stairwell, the untreated depression that "sets" like night over a person's mind. The verse's wisdom is to refuse to live at the mercy of what is concealed. The believer prays for protection from whatever is being decided, whispered, or plotted in the dark — and then, practically, he walks toward the light: he seeks transparency, he builds accountable relationships, he brings his own secrets into the open before they fester. The spiritual refuge and the practical habit reinforce each other. A community that prays *min sharri ghāsiqin* will also, naturally, build well-lit streets and open institutions, because both instincts come from the same source: darkness is where harm hides, and light is where safety lives.

**The Logical Principle: Exposure Dispels Hidden Harm**

The logical thread running through this verse is simple and sound: *most harm depends on concealment; therefore the remedy is exposure to the Light who owns the dawn.* You cannot be ambushed by what you can see. You cannot be poisoned by a plot you have brought into the open. You cannot be enslaved by a secret sin you have confessed and repented. The verse points the believer to the ultimate Source of exposure — God, before whom nothing is hidden:

> *"And there is not concealed from your Lord an [atom's] weight within the heavens or within the earth…"* (Qur'an 10:61)

Seeking refuge in the Lord of the daybreak is, then, seeking refuge in the One before whom all darkness is already light. The night that terrifies the servant is, to Him, as bright as noon. To attach oneself to Him is to step, in a real sense, out of the dark.

**Transition to the Occult**

Having named the darkness of night — the broad arena in which hidden harm operates — the sūrah now points to a specific inhabitant of that darkness: the one who works evil by whispering and blowing over knots. Verse 4 moves from the *environment* of evil (the night) to a *practitioner* of evil (the sorcerer). The descent continues, from the general to the particular, from the atmosphere to the agent.
## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:4

> **from the evil of those who blow upon knots,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Practitioner of Hidden Harm**

The sūrah now names a specific agent of the darkness it described in verse 3: *wa-min sharri an-naffāthāti fī al-ʿuqad* — "and from the evil of those who blow upon knots." This is the verse most directly tied to the *asbāb al-nuzūl* of the spell cast upon the Prophet ﷺ. The "blowers upon knots" are the sorcerers who tie a string into knots and whisper incantations — often blowing or spitting lightly over each knot as they recite — thereby binding a curse or a spell into the physical object. The Prophet ﷺ was afflicted by exactly this: the comb, the hair, and the knotted string placed under the stone in the well of Dharwān. This verse is, in the plainest sense, the divine answer to that exact act of malice.

**The Word Naffāthāt: Blowing, Whispering, and the Feminine Form**

*Naffāthāt* (نفاثات) is the feminine plural of *naffāthah*, from the root *n-f-th* (ن-ف-ث), which means to blow or to spit lightly — the soft puff of breath one directs onto something while whispering. In the context of sorcery, it is the act of breathing the incantation into the knots. The noun is grammatically feminine plural, which has led some to suppose the verse refers only to women. The commentators are clear that this is not the case. Al-Rāzī and al-Zajjāj (Z) explain that the feminine plural form is used here in a general sense that includes human beings of both sexes — just as Arabic often uses the feminine plural for a mixed or generic group, or uses it for emphasis on the despicable nature of the act. Ibn Kathīr likewise notes that the reference is to *practitioners of magic* generally, male and female, even though the historical incident involved a woman (the sorceress allied with Labīd ibn al-Aʿsam, or the report's "women who blow on knots"). The point is not the gender of the wrongdoer but the *method*: harm delivered through whispered, hidden, manipulative means.

**The Knots: Binding Harm Into Matter**

*Al-ʿuqad* (العقد) is the plural of *ʿuqdah* — a knot, a bond, a tie. Knotting is the ancient, near-universal technology of sympathetic magic: the sorcerer ties a knot for each intended effect, speaks the spell into it, and believes the victim is thus bound. The well-documented account of the Prophet's ﷺ bewitchment specifies that the string held **eleven knots**, and that as the fourteen verses of the two Muʿawwidhatān were recited over them, the knots came loose — the relief corresponding, in the traditional telling, to the loosening of each bound affliction. The image is potent: evil is "tied up" in secret; deliverance is the untying that only God's words can accomplish. The knot is the perfect symbol of *hidden constraint* — the victim is bound without knowing by whom, where, or how, because the binding happened in darkness, under a stone, at the bottom of a well.

**Islam's Stance on Magic: Real, Forbidden, and Not a Path to Power**

This verse sits within a broader Qur'anic teaching about sorcery that the commentators always cite alongside it. Magic (*siḥr*) is real in the Islamic worldview — it can cause real harm — but it is strictly forbidden and is treated among the gravest sins. The Qur'an narrates that magic was taught to mankind by the devils, not by God, and that those who pursue it cut themselves off from the good of the Hereafter:

> *"But they [the people] followed what the devils recited concerning the kingdom of Solomon. And Solomon did not disbelieve, but the devils disbelieved, teaching people magic… And they learned from them that by which they cause separation between a man and his wife. And they do not harm anyone except by the permission of God. And they learned that which harms them and does not benefit them. And already they knew that whoever purchased it [magic] would have no share in the Hereafter. And wretched is that for which they sold themselves, if they only knew."* (Qur'an 2:102)

Two points from this crucial cross-reference illuminate verse 4. First, even the harm of magic occurs *only by the permission of God* (*illā bi-idhni Allāh*) — which is exactly why the refuge in verse 1 (the Lord of the daybreak) is effective: the same God who permits the test also authorizes the cure. Second, the magic is described as something that "separates between a man and his wife" — a direct echo of the Prophet's ﷺ own affliction, in which (as some reports add) the spell disturbed his relations with his wives. The Qur'an thus names the social devastation magic wreaks, and the sūrah equips the believer to seek protection from it.

**Not Counter-Magic, but the Words of God**

A subtle but vital lesson of this verse is *how* the believer is to be protected. The remedy God revealed was not a stronger spell, not a rival knot, not a counter-charm — it was *His own words*, the Muʿawwidhatān. The Prophet ﷺ undid the sorcerer's knots not by magic but by reciting revelation over them. This establishes the Islamic principle of *ruqyah* (legitimate spiritual healing): protection and cure come through the speech of God and the remembrance of Him, never through imitating the sorcerer. The one who fears the "blowers upon knots" must not become a blower upon knots himself. The boundary is absolute. Seeking refuge in the Lord of the daybreak means trusting His words to undo what the devil's words have bound.

**The Metaphorical Reading: Every Hidden Schemer Is a "Blower on Knots"**

While the primary reference is to literal sorcery, the commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and others) affirm a broader, metaphorical application that makes the verse timeless. "Those who blow upon knots" becomes a figure for *anyone who works harm in secret through deception, whispering, and manipulation*. The "knots" are the conspiracies, the smear campaigns, the hidden clauses in a contract, the rumors sown in the dark. The "blowing" is the soft, plausible, poisonous speech that binds others to a falsehood. Read this way, the verse protects the believer from the entire class of covert malice — the office intrigue, the political plot, the social media whispering campaign, the emotional manipulation disguised as care. The enemy need not believe in magic for the verse to apply; he need only operate by hidden, binding, harmful speech.

**Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr on the Incident and Its Lesson**

Al-Ṭabarī, in his *Tafsīr*, connects this verse explicitly to the account of Labīd ibn al-Aʿsam and the well of Dharwān, and he cites the variant readings and reports that clarify the feminine form as general. Ibn Kathīr, in his commentary, reproduces the full ḥadīth of ʿĀʾishah about the bewitchment and then draws the practical conclusion: the believer should recite the Muʿawwidhatān for protection, exactly as the Prophet ﷺ did, because these are the verses God sent down *specifically* as a shield against this kind of harm. Ibn Kathīr also emphasizes that the verse proves the reality of magic (contra those who would dismiss it) while simultaneously forbidding the Muslim from ever practicing it. The two truths stand together: magic is real enough to need protection from, and forbidden enough to never be practiced.

**Theological Safeguard: The Devil Has No Independent Power**

It is worth stating plainly what this verse presupposes about power. The "blowers upon knots" can do nothing except by God's leave. The sorcerer's knot is, in the final analysis, a string — and a string has no power over a human life unless the Lord of the daybreak permits the trial. This is why the sūrah never tells the believer to fear the sorcerer; it tells him to seek refuge in God *from* the sorcerer's evil. The fear is redirected upward, to its only legitimate object. A believer who understands this cannot be terrorized by occult threats, because he knows the threat is bounded by the permission of the very God he is addressing. The knot may be real, but the Knower of the knot is greater, and the Looser of the knot has already supplied the words to untie it.

**Psychological and Social Implications**

The verse speaks to a real and recurring human vulnerability: the fear of being harmed by someone we cannot see, in a way we cannot prove. Victims of covert manipulation — emotional abuse, gaslighting, character assassination — often describe a sense of being "bound" by forces they cannot name. This verse gives that experience a name and a response. It says: name the hidden harm before God, seek refuge in Him, and do not descend into the manipulator's methods to fight back. The social implication is a cleaner one still: a community that regularly recites *min sharri an-naffāthāti fī al-ʿuqad* is a community that refuses to conduct its conflicts in the dark. It brings accusations into the open, it demands evidence, it refuses whisper-campaigns. The verse, recited sincerely, is a quiet force against the whole culture of hidden malice.

**A Modern Analogy: The Whisper Network and the Open Forum**

Think of a workplace where decisions are made in closed rooms and communicated through rumors. The "knots" are the unspoken agreements; the "blowing" is the gossip that seals them. An employee targeted by this system feels exactly the powerlessness the verse addresses. The healthy response — both spiritual and practical — is twofold: first, the inward *taʿawwuḍh*, entrusting the matter to God; second, the outward insistence on transparency, on bringing the hidden decision into a forum where it can be seen and answered. The verse does not authorize paranoia; it authorizes *light*. The believer assumes that hidden harm is real, seeks God's protection from it, and then walks toward the open where knots cannot hold.

**Transition to the Most Intimate Danger**

The sūrah has moved from the widest circle (all creation's evil), to the environment (the dark), to a practitioner (the sorcerer). It now makes its final and perhaps most piercing move: from the *stranger* who works harm in the dark, to the *neighbor, friend, or kinsman* whose heart harbors envy. Verse 5 names the evil that is closest to home — the envy of one who stands near you, whose goodwill you might have assumed, and whose secret ill-will can wound more deeply than any spell.
## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:5

> **and from the evil of the envier when he envies."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final and Most Intimate Danger**

The sūrah closes with its most personal petition: *wa-min sharri ḥāsidin idhā ḥasad* — "and from the evil of the envier when he envies." Having swept from the cosmic ("what He has created") to the environmental (the dark) to the occult practitioner (the blower on knots), the sūrah ends at the human heart — specifically, the heart corroded by envy. This is the danger nearest to home, because the envier is often not a stranger in the night but a person in your circle: a relative, a colleague, a friend, even a coreligionist. The harm of envy is unique in that it requires no knot, no spell, no darkness — only a heart that resents another's good. The sūrah thus arrives, by a deliberate descent, at the evil that lives inside the community itself.

**The Word Ḥāsid and the Root of "Cutting"**

*Ḥāsid* (حاسد) is the active participle from the root *ḥ-s-d* (ح-س-د), which carries the core meaning of "to cut" or "to reap" (the same root yields *ḥaṣad*, "harvest"). The linguistic image is striking: envy is conceived as a *cutting*. The envier, in resenting another's blessing, "cuts" at it — and in the process cuts himself. The classical lexicographers note that *ḥasad* is to wish the removal of a blessing from another, or to wish its transfer to oneself at the other's expense, accompanied by a dislike of the other's having it. It is not merely admiring what someone has; it is *resenting* that they have it. The "cutting" metaphor captures the self-destructive character of the vice: the envier attacks a good that God placed with another, and in doing so attacks the wisdom of God's distribution — which is a knife turned ultimately on the envier's own soul.

**Idhā Ḥasad: The Specified Moment of Emission**

The verse does not say merely "from the evil of the envier"; it specifies *idhā ḥasad* — "when he envies." This temporal precision is theologically and psychologically loaded. The danger is not the envier's static disposition alone but the *active moment* in which envy is released — when the feeling translates into the evil eye, the spoken malice, the undermining act. The Prophet ﷺ taught that envy can "shoot" from the envier toward the envied in the form of the evil eye (*al-ʿayn*), and that this is a real and harmful transmission. By specifying "when he envies," the verse seeks protection precisely at the point of emission — the instant the poisoned glance or the resentful intention leaves the heart and reaches toward its target. It is a plea for a shield that intercepts the arrow at the bowstring.

**Envy as the Root of All Evil: The Two First Sins**

The commentators place this verse at the headwaters of human wrongdoing. The first sin committed in the heavenly realm was Satan's envy of Adam — when God commanded the angels to prostrate before Adam and Iblīs refused out of resentful pride, refusing to accept that God had honored a creature above him:

> *"And [mention] when We said to the angels, 'Prostrate before Adam,' so they prostrated, except Iblīs. He refused and was arrogant and became among the disbelievers."* (Qur'an 2:34; cf. 7:11–13)

The first sin committed upon the earth was the envy of Cain (Qābīl) toward his brother Abel (Hābīl), which drove him to the first murder:

> *"And recite to them the news of the two sons of Adam in truth, when they both offered a sacrifice… But it was accepted from one of them and not from the other. [The one] said, 'I will surely kill you.' [The other] said, 'Indeed, God only accepts from the righteous.'"* (Qur'an 5:27; cf. 5:30–31)

Al-Ṭabarī and others, drawing on these narratives, state that envy (*ḥasad*) is the root from which countless evils grow — schism, murder, backbiting, the violation of bonds. It is therefore said that the one who envies the favor bestowed upon another is, in reality, opposing the blessings of God — contesting His wisdom in giving, and His justice in distributing. To seek refuge from the envier "when he envies" is thus to seek refuge from the oldest and most prolific source of human evil.

**The Caliph's Wisdom: The Envied Is the Wronged**

The source text preserves a remarkable observation from the Umayyad Caliph ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz (may God have mercy on him, r. 99–101 AH), one of the most revered rulers in Islamic history for his piety and justice:

> *"I cannot think of any wrongdoer more likely to be wronged than one who envies another."* (cited by al-Zajjāj and in the commentaries)

This is a profound piece of moral psychology. The envier imagines he is "getting even" with the person who has the blessing, but in truth he wrongs himself first: he torments his own heart, he squanders his own peace, and he earns the displeasure of God — all while the envied person often remains untouched. The envier is, in the caliph's phrase, the wrongdoer who is most certainly wronged, because his own hand deals the first blow to his own soul. The verse's petition therefore carries a double mercy: it shields the *target* of envy from harm, and it implicitly warns the *envier* that his vice is self-destructive. The believer who recites it learns to pity the envier even while seeking protection from him.

**The Prophet's Warnings Against Envy**

The Sunnah is emphatic that envy is a disease to be cured, not a feeling to be indulged. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Do not envy one another, do not inflate prices against one another, do not hate one another, do not turn away from one another, and do not undercut one another in trade; but be servants of God as brothers."* (al-Bukhārī, no. 6064; Muslim, no. 2559)

And he warned that envy consumes the very deeds a person accumulates, the way fire consumes wood:

> *"The disease of the nations before you has crept into you: envy (*al-ḥasad*) and intense hatred (*al-baghḍāʾ*). Envy consumes good deeds the way fire consumes wood — or he said, grass."* (al-Tirmidhī, no. 2510; Aḥmad, with a sound chain)

The imagery of consumption is exact: just as fire reduces fuel to ash, envy reduces a person's record of good deeds to nothing, because the envious heart is fundamentally at war with God's wisdom. The believer who recites *min sharri ḥāsidin idhā ḥasad* is, in effect, inoculating himself against this consumption — praying both that he be spared the envier's arrow and that he himself be kept free from the envy that would burn his own deeds.

**The Evil Eye: When Envy Becomes Physical Harm**

The close connection between envy and the evil eye (*al-ʿayn*) is central to this verse's protective intent. The Prophet ﷺ affirmed the reality of the evil eye:

> *"The evil eye is real (al-ʿayn ḥaqq). And if anything were to overtake the Divine Decree, it would be the evil eye."* (Muslim, no. 2188; al-Bukhārī, no. 5740)

The *ruqyah* the Prophet ﷺ taught for al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn — *"I seek refuge for you both in the perfect words of God, from every devil and poisonous creature, and from every evil eye"* (al-Bukhārī, no. 3371; Muslim, no. 2186) — is the practical outworking of this very verse: the envier's eye is the "poisonous creature" from which the perfect words of God are the shield. The Muʿawwidhatān are therefore, in the living practice of the *ummah*, the prescribed defense against the evil eye, and this final verse is its scriptural anchor.

**Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and the Completeness of the Sūrah's Arc**

The classical exegetes note that this fifth verse completes a deliberate progression. Al-Ṭabarī lays out the four refuges as concentric circles closing in on the human condition: (1) all creation's evil — the outermost ring; (2) the darkness of night — the environment of hidden harm; (3) the blowers on knots — the deliberate occult agent; (4) the envier — the intimate moral evil. Ibn Kathīr underscores that the sūrah thus protects the believer from every direction: from above (cosmic), from around (environmental), from the occult (sorcerous), and from within his own community (envy). No angle of attack is left unguarded. The final verse is placed last because it is the most *subtle* and the most *constant*: sorcery is rare, but envy is everywhere, and it wears the face of a friend.

**The Logical Principle: Envy Presupposes a False Zero-Sum World**

Underneath the religious teaching is a sound logical principle. Envy rests on the implicit assumption that the world's goods are a fixed, zero-sum pool — that your gain is necessarily my loss. Islam rejects this premise at its root by teaching the infinite, unrestricted generosity of God. The blessings He gives one servant do not diminish what He can give another; His *faḍl* (bounty) is not a pie to be divided but a fountain with countless spouts. The envier, therefore, is not only sinning; he is *reasoning badly* — fighting a reality (God's wise distribution) that cannot be undone by resentment. The cure for envy is precisely the conviction that the verse cultivates: the Lord of the daybreak distributes light freely, and the dawn that rises on your neighbor's window rises also, undiminished, on yours. Gratitude (*shukr*) for one's own portion and *tabarruk* (blessing) for another's — "may God increase him in good" — is the rational antidote to the zero-sum delusion of envy.

**Psychological and Modern Implications**

In the modern age, envy has been industrialized. Social media constructs a perpetual highlight reel of other people's lives, and the human tendency to compare is fed a firehose of comparison-material. The result is a documented rise in anxiety, depression, and resentment tied directly to "social comparison." The verse speaks directly to this condition. The believer is taught to name the envy he encounters (and the envy he might feel) and to seek refuge from its evil — both the evil it does to others and the evil it does within his own heart. Practically, this means: when you feel the sting of comparison, do not nurse it; say *aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi al-falaq min sharri ḥāsidin idhā ḥasad*, then actively bless the person you envied. The outward blessing ruptures the inward resentment; the tongue retrains the heart. This is not pious cosmetics — it is cognitive behavioral therapy fourteen centuries old, sanctioned by revelation.

**The Social Bond: Why Envy Is the Community's Greatest Threat**

Envy is the acid that dissolves *ummah*. Where envy reigns, people rejoice at others' losses, sabotage others' gains, and refuse to cooperate — because another's success feels like a personal defeat. A society of enviers is a society of isolated, anxious, mutually distrustful individuals. The sūrah's final petition is therefore a prayer for the health of the community itself: protect us from the envy that would turn brother against brother. The Prophet's ﷺ command to "be servants of God as brothers" is the positive counterpart to this negative petition. The verse protects the *bonds*; the Sunnah builds the *bonds*. Together they form the immune system of a healthy community.

**The Closing of the Funnel: From the Cosmic to the Intimate**

Step back and see the whole architecture. Verse 1 fixed the Refuge — the Lord of the daybreak. Verse 2 cast the widest net — all creation's evil. Verse 3 named the environment — the dark. Verse 4 named the agent — the sorcerer. Verse 5 names the heart — the envier. The funnel has closed completely: there is no danger outside it. The believer who has said these five verses has, in five breaths, surrendered to God every form of harm he can imagine — from the impersonal forces of nature, to the cover of night, to occult malice, to the resentment of a friend. And he has surrendered them all to the *same* Protector. The sūrah ends not with a threat but with a containment: all evil, named and bounded, placed under the care of the One who splits the dawn. The light has, in speech, already broken the dark.

**A Final Reflection: The Sūrah as a Daily Shield**

The Prophet ﷺ taught that the Muʿawwidhatān "suffice you against everything" when recited morning and evening (al-Tirmidhī, no. 3575; Abū Dāwūd, no. 5082). "Everything" is exactly the scope of this sūrah's five petitions. To make these five verses a daily habit is to clothe oneself, invisibly, in a protection that reaches from the edge of creation to the center of the human heart. The modern believer, besieged by visible and invisible pressures, could do no better than to let *Sūrah al-Falaq* be the last thing on his tongue at night and the first shield over his day — a small chapter, five verses, an infinite refuge.
