# Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt (Chapter 100) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Short Chapter That Gallops Like Its Opening Words**

Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt — "The Chargers" — is the hundredth chapter of the Qur'an, the eighth of the ten sūrahs that begin with the disconnected letters Ḥā-Mīm clustered at the end of the written text, and one of the most compressed, most kinetic, and most complete rhetorical units in the entire Book. In eleven verses occupying barely a third of a muṣḥaf page, the sūrah accomplishes a full argument: it opens with the sound and image of war-horses thundering across ground — panting, striking sparks, raiding at first light, trailing dust, plunging into the very center of an enemy mass — and then pivots, in a single clause, to the least horse-like of God's creatures: man, who is stubbornly ungrateful to his Lord. The horses, instinct-bound animals lacking free choice, obey their riders with total commitment; the free human being, for whom obedience would be a free act of love, drags his feet before his Master. The chapter thus reads in the tradition as one long, implicit rebuke delivered through a mirror: watch the chargers, then look at yourself.

The sūrah takes its name, as the naming convention of the Qur'an goes, from the mention of the chargers (*al-ʿādiyāt*) in its first verse. In the oldest counting it contains eleven verses; it belongs to the thirtieth and final part (juzʾ) of the Qur'an, where it sits immediately after Sūrah al-Zalzalah, "The Earthquake" (99), and immediately before Sūrah al-Qāriʿah, "The Striking Calamity" (101). That neighborhood is not accidental in the tradition's reading: three consecutive short sūrahs each culminate in the resurrection and the laying bare of what human beings did and were. Al-Zalzalah declares that the earth will cast forth its burdens and report its news because *her Lord has inspired her*; al-ʿĀdiyāt declares that graves will be emptied and chests turned inside out; al-Qāriʿah declares that deeds will be weighed like wool and dust in a balance. A reader moving through the end of the muṣḥaf is being drilled, sūrah after sūrah, in the certainties of the Last Day — drilled, because the material is short enough to memorize and sharp enough to remember.

**When Was It Revealed? The Case for Makkah and the Case for Madinah**

Most commentators maintain that al-ʿĀdiyāt was revealed in the early Makkan period, most likely soon after Sūrah al-Qāriʿah (101); a minority, citing Āl al-Shaykh and al-Ṭabarī's record of other opinions, place it at the beginning of the Madinan period. The case for Makkan attribution rests on the sūrah's style, which exhibits the classic fingerprints of the earliest revelation: a series of oaths by dramatic created things, a compressed eschatological warning, a rhyme-driven rhythm, and a polemical address to an audience still steeped in unbelief. On the other side, the case for Madinan attribution rests on readings of the subject matter: the raiding imagery of verses 1–5, and the reference in verse 8 to man's fierce love of "good things," were understood by some exegetes to presuppose the community of believers already engaged in campaigns. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah and others noted that where content could be read either way, chronology should be decided by style — and style speaks Makkan here.

The majority position also has a spiritual logic the commentators cherished: a Makkan sūrah about raiding chargers shows that the Qur'an's first audience, still a persecuted handful with no army at all, was being trained from the beginning to think in terms of mobilization. The oaths taught them that the life of faith is not a lounge but a campaign — that when the command finally comes, the believer must be able to move with the readiness of a cavalry unit at first light. When the actual campaigns came in Madinah, the vocabulary was already in their hearts. Conversely, if the sūrah is Madinan, then it stands as retrospective instruction to a community that had already learned to ride: a reminder that all this muscle and motion is worthless if the rider's own heart is *kanūd* — ungrateful to the Lord who gave him the horse, the arm, the morning, and the victory.

**The Reports About Its Occasion: Three Windows on Five Oaths**

Several distinct accounts are transmitted about the circumstances in which these verses descended, and each illumines a different face of the text. In the first, reported by al-Thaʿlabī and recorded in al-Ṭabarī's chain of transmission, the Prophet ﷺ dispatched a military expedition under the command of al-Mundhir ibn ʿAmr al-Anṣārī toward a clan of Banū Kinānah. When news of the expedition was delayed, the hypocrites of Madinah said, "They have all been killed." So God gave news about this expedition and revealed, *By the panting chargers* — meaning, according to this report, the horses of that very expedition. The revelation thus functioned as real-time intelligence and consolation at once: the believers learned that their absent brothers were alive, galloping, and in motion, while the mockers learned that God's knowledge outruns every rumor. Under this account, the oaths of verses 1–5 are a divine attestation of the expedition's riders — an official report, issued from heaven, on men still unaccounted for on earth.

The second account holds that the verses refer to the Battle of Badr, the first great confrontation of the new community. But the tradition itself flagged an obstacle: an interpretation attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib counts only two horses at Badr, the rest of the mounts being camels — a detail of logistical poverty that historians of the campaign confirm, and which makes the Five-Oath cavalry imagery of the sūrah hard to confine to that battle. The third account, likewise attributed to ʿAlī, lifts the image out of warfare altogether: the oaths depict that portion of the ḥajj pilgrimage in which the pilgrims rush on their mounts from Mount ʿArafāt to Muzdalifah and on to Minā — the moving wall of humanity that even today flows through the night and at dawn of the Day of Sacrifice. Al-Bayḍāwī adopted this breadth of reading, holding that the verses can be applied to both horses in battle and camels on pilgrimage, since each fulfills a function considered worthy of being sworn upon by God: the pilgrim surge is a raid directed at nothing but forgiveness, a campaign whose enemy is one's own sins, a charge whose prize is the pleasure of the Lord of the House.

The very existence of multiple reported occasions, none displacing the others, teaches a principle the classical scholars never tired of applying: an oath in the Qur'an is frequently *general in scope* even when a specific event suggested its wording. Asbāb al-nuzūl — the occasions of revelation — illuminate the text; they do not imprison it. The exegete al-Wāḥidī, who systematized the study of occasions of revelation, insisted that a verse revealed about one situation remains true in every situation it describes. The panting chargers can be al-Mundhir's riders, Badr's vanguard, the pilgrim surge at Minā, and every believer who has ever thrown himself out of bed before dawn — because the verse honors the *category* of energetic obedience, not merely its instances.

**The Structure: Five Oaths, Three Verses of Diagnosis, Three Verses of Warning**

The architecture of the sūrah is as clean as an arrow's shaft. Verses 1–5 form a single, rolling oath: five participial phrases, each one step deeper into the action of the chargers — *panting, striking sparks, raiding at dawn, kicking up dust, penetrating to the center of the enemy altogether*. Verses 6–8 deliver the sworn assertion — the *jawāb al-qasam*, the "response to the oath" — that man, for all his natural gifts, is ungrateful to his Lord, a witness against himself, and fiercely in love with wealth. Verses 9–11 complete the argument with a question and a certainty: *Does he not know that when what lies within graves is turned inside out and what lies within breasts is made known, truly on that Day their Lord shall be Aware of them?* Diagnosis in the middle, verdict at the end, oath as the seal on the diagnosis, resurrection as the courtroom in which the verdict is executed.

Classical rhetoricians counted the lām of emphasis (*li-kanūd, la-shahīd, la-shadīd, la-khabīr*) in this sūrah as among the densest in the Qur'an: four heavy emphasis particles in six clauses of assertion. The style itself argues. God does not merely state that man is ungrateful; He *swears* it five times over and stamps each statement with a particle that forecloses all doubt. A reader who feels the weight of the oath is meant to ask why so much emphasis was spent on so short a claim — and the answer is the whole history of the human race, which has never once stopped supplying fresh evidence for it.

**Why Horses? Why Oaths? Why Sparks at Dawn?**

The commentators devoted attention to the question of why God swears by His creatures at all, when creatures are His works. Al-Ṭabarī transmitted the settled principle that God swears by what He honors among His creation, magnifying the thing sworn about by means of the thing sworn by; Ibn Taymiyyah added that such oaths are both praise of the creature and warning to mankind — a double effect: the sworn object is ennobled, and the hearer is shaken awake. Five oaths in five verses is among the longest oath sequences in the Qur'an, matched in intensity by sequences such as *By those sent forth in succession* (77:1–6) and *By those who drag forth* (79:1–5). The expositors agreed that a multiplicity of oaths signals both the gravity of what is to be said and the hardness of the hearts that must be convinced: God is patient with human skepticism to the point of repeating His adjuration five times before He dares to say what we are.

Horses, meanwhile, occupied a unique place in the Arab world and in the Prophet's ﷺ own teaching. The Qur'an names them among the pleasures for which hearts compete —

> *"Made to seem fair unto mankind is the love of passions, among them women, children, hoarded heaps of gold and silver, horses of mark, cattle, and tillage."* (Qur'an 3:14)

— and then commands their military use:

> *"Prepare against them what you can of power, including steeds of war, to strike terror into the enemy of God and your enemy."* (Qur'an 8:60)

The Prophet ﷺ said, in a ḥadīth agreed upon by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"There is goodness tied to the forelocks of horses until the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī 2842; Muslim 1873)

And in the fuller narration from Abū Hurayrah, also agreed upon:

> *"Whoever ties a horse in the way of God, believing in God and confirming His promise, then the horse's food and drink and dung, all of it, will be in his scale on the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī 2853; Muslim 1874)

So the charger in this sūrah is not a war-poem's ornament. It is a creature already sanctified by revelation — and precisely for that reason it becomes the perfect mirror for man. The horse spends everything, breathes hard, bleeds its hooves into sparks, and holds nothing back; the man who rides it cannot manage even a fraction of that devotion for the Lord who made both horse and rider. The classical tradition pressed the same contrast into the soul: al-Bayḍāwī, following the path of the spiritual masters, read the oaths as an allusion to "charging souls" — souls that, when something of the light of holiness appears to them, convey it to others, strike the sparks of spiritual knowledge, raid their own caprice and bad habits, raise in themselves the desire for God, and finally penetrate altogether into the midst of those gathered in ʿIlliyyūn, described in the Qur'an as the register of the righteous on high:

> *"No indeed! Truly the register of the righteous is in ʿIlliyyūn. And what will convey unto you what ʿIlliyyūn is? A register inscribed, witnessed by those brought near."* (Qur'an 83:18–21)

Under this reading the sūrah is not only a rebuke; it is a training manual. The same ferocity of motion the horse gives to its rider, the believer is invited to give to his own purification — a famous report, whose chain the critics have debated but whose meaning the tradition has universally received, even says:

> *"The striver is the one who strives against his own soul."* (al-Tirmidhī 1621, with a chain graded weak by many critics but the sense affirmed across the tradition)

**A Chapter of Sound: Hoofbeats Written as Rhyme**

Finally, no honest reading of al-ʿĀdiyāt can ignore its sound design. The five oath verses end in sharp, monosyllabic rhyme-words — *ḍabḥan, qadḥan, ṣubḥan, naqʿan, jamʿan* — drummed out in a rhythm that imitates what it describes: short stressed beats followed by a rolling ending, like hooves striking earth. The early listeners did not need a lecture on onomatopoeia; their ears, trained on the desert, heard cavalry in the consonants. This is part of the Qur'an's own claim to inimitability — that its form is fitted to its content at the level of pure sound. The sūrah that will condemn man's flightiness arrives sounding like the most disciplined motion on earth. Even the grammar cooperates: verse 1 uses the emphatic particle and the oath; verses 6–8 stack lāms of emphasis; verse 9 opens with an interrogative that corners the reader (*a-fa-lā yaʿlamu* — "Is he then not aware?"); verse 11 closes on a superlative of divine knowledge, *la-khabīr*, God "All-Aware," with another emphasis-lām driving it home. The chapter begins in the noise of hooves and ends in the silence of a Heart-reader who misses nothing. Between those two points, eleven verses hold the whole moral history of mankind.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1

> **1 By the panting chargers,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath Multiplied Five Times: Why God Swears at All**

The sūrah opens not with a command or a story but with an oath — and not with one oath, but with the first link of a five-part chain of oaths that will run to the end of verse 5. This immediately places al-ʿĀdiyāt inside a venerable Qur'anic pattern. The Book of God swears by the dawn and the ten nights (89:1–2), by the stars and the streaming sky (84:16–18), by those dragged forth in ranks (79:1–5), by the heaven and the night-comer (86:1), and here by charging horses. The scholars of tafsīr settled early on the principle that God swears by His creation because creation magnifies what is sworn about: al-Ṭabarī transmitted from the early generations that God swears by things among His creatures "in which He has placed great good," thereby honoring both the sworn object and the truth being asserted. Ibn Taymiyyah drew out the double effect — the oath ennobles the creature and shakes the hearer. A man hearing five successive oaths in five successive verses is being told, in effect: *what follows is not opinion; heaven itself has put its credibility behind it.*

Why is such intensity needed? Because the claim to which these oaths will give rise in verse 6 — *truly man is ungrateful to his Lord* — is the single claim human beings resist most stubbornly. No one argues seriously that the sky is not blue; everyone argues, silently or aloud, that his own ingratitude is excusable. The multiplicity of the oath is a response to the multiplicity of our excuses. Rhetoricians among the Arabs held that a speaker multiplies oaths when he fears his audience will not believe him, or when what he says is momentous. Both motives converge here, and the Qur'an had already used the same device in the same cause: *No indeed! Man is rebellious* (96:6) comes after oaths as well. The oath is heaven's patience with human skepticism, taken to the limit of five repetitions.

**The Vocabulary of the Verse: ʿĀdiyāt and the Strange Word Ḍabḥ**

Two words carry this verse, and both reward slow attention. *Al-ʿādiyāt* is the plural participle from the root ʿayn-dāl-wāw, whose basic sense is to run, to course, to charge forward — the root also yields *maʿdin* of water reaching the surface and the *ʿudw* of a limb, because a limb "reaches" where the body intends. The word pictures an animal in full, directed motion: not grazing, not standing, but advancing with the whole of its strength toward a goal. The definite article (*al-*) particularizes them: these are *the* chargers, a body of known mounts — known, on one reading, to the audience by the report of a recent expedition; known, on the broader reading, as a category the whole Arab world could see in its mind's eye at once.

*Ḍabḥan* is rarer and stranger. The root ḍāl-bā-ḥā is famous from *wa-naḥar* — *So pray to your Lord and sacrifice* (108:2) — where it means to slaughter. But the Arab lexicographers, including Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī in his *Mufradāt*, record a second, specialized sense: *ḍabḥa* is the labored, panting breath of an animal pressed to the very limit of its speed — the short, hard gasp that rises from a runner's chest. The grammatical form is a cognate accusative (*mafʿūl muṭlaq*), a device the Qur'an uses to render the meaning of the preceding word absolute and vivid: the chargers are panting-panters, gallopers in the very act of exhausting their breath. Some exegetes noted with wonder that the same root can point both to death (slaughter) and to the breath of life (panting) — as though the horse in its charge spends its very life-breath, holding nothing in reserve. The Arabic language, in a single adverb, gives us the horse's whole condition: maximum effort, audible effort, effort that can be *heard* from a distance. That is what God chooses as the opening note of His oath — not the horse at rest in its stable, admired for its beauty, but the horse spent for its master.

**The Horses of Revelation: How Scripture Itself Honors the Charger**

The Qur'an returns to horses again and again, and always with the same layered attitude: they are beautiful, they are beloved, they are useful, and they are dangerous to the heart that loves them more than God. They appear in the inventory of worldly delights —

> *"Made to seem fair unto mankind is the love of passions, among them women, children, hoarded heaps of gold and silver, horses of mark, cattle, and tillage. Those are the enjoyment of the life of this world. And God, with Him is the beautiful return."* (Qur'an 3:14)

— and in the command to readiness —

> *"And horses, mules, and asses, that you may ride them and as adornment; and He creates what you do not know."* (Qur'an 16:8)

Notice the closing clause of 16:8: God says of transport animals *He creates what you do not know* — a sentence the early community could not have exhausted, and one that modern readers, who have seen engines and aircraft succeed the horse, can feel resonating beyond its first horizon.

The most instructive horse passage in the Qur'an concerns Solomon, and it anticipates, word for word, the disease this sūrah will diagnose. The Book of God narrates:

> *"When at evening the outstanding steeds were presented to him, he said: 'Indeed, I have loved the love of good things in preference to the remembrance of my Lord,' until the sun vanished behind the veil. 'Bring them back to me,' and he began to stroke their shanks and necks."* (Qur'an 38:31–33)

Solomon — a prophet-king with the mightiest stables of his age — caught himself lingering over magnificent horses until prayer-time slipped away, and immediately named his condition: love of *good things* over remembrance of God. In Sūrah Ṣād the story ends in self-correction; in Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt the same infection is diagnosed in the rest of mankind and traced to its terminal stage: *he is fierce in his love for good things* (v. 8). A reader who wonders why this little sūrah begins with horses has only to read Ṣād 31 side by side with it: the horse is the testing point where a heart is measured — whether its Lord outranks its stables.

The Prophet's ﷺ own words fixed the horse's double character forever. He said:

> *"Horses are of three kinds: they are a reward for one man, a shelter for another, and a burden upon a third. As for the one for whom they are a reward: he is a man who ties them for the sake of God and prolongs their tether in a field or garden; whatever they graze in that field or garden will be good deeds recorded for him as much as their grazing... As for the one for whom they are a shelter: he is a man who keeps them to spare himself from begging... And as for the one for whom they are a burden: he is a man who keeps them out of pride and showing-off before people."* (al-Bukhārī 2851; Muslim 1875)

The same animal, the Prophet ﷺ taught, can be worship, livelihood, or vanity — the horse does not decide; the heart of the rider does. That is the exact physics of this sūrah: five verses of glorious chargers, followed by a verse about the human heart they expose.

**The Occasion of the Verse: A Heavenly Intelligence Report on Missing Riders**

The reported occasion of revelation makes the oath concrete and personal. The Prophet ﷺ sent a military expedition under al-Mundhir ibn ʿAmr al-Anṣārī toward a clan of Banū Kinānah. The riders were gone long enough that news stopped flowing back; Madinah's hypocrites, who relished every believer's misfortune, announced, "They have all been killed." In that moment of suspended grief and poisoned rumor, these verses descended, and according to the report transmitted by al-Thaʿlabī and preserved in al-Ṭabarī's commentary, God thereby gave news of the expedition — *By the panting chargers*, meaning the horses of al-Mundhir's riders. The revelation worked as comfort and correction in one breath: the believers learned their brothers lived and moved, and the mockers learned that God does not depend on couriers for His information. He swears by the very horses of the missing men, because He watches them at this instant, panting across the desert.

The attributed commentary of Ibn ʿAbbās, preserved in the great tafsīrs, compresses the general lesson:

> *"It concerns the horses of the emigrants and the helpers — they are those by which God swore."* (attributed in the commentaries of al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī)

Under this account the sūrah is a divine press-release issued for a ragged unit of thirty riders, and the theology is astonishing: God of the seven heavens, swearing by the sweaty mounts of unspectacular men, to vindicate them against a whispering campaign. Any believer today whose honest work is slandered while it is still in progress can take the point: the One who watches need not wait for witnesses.

**The Pilgrim Surge at Dawn: ʿAlī's Wider Reading**

The interpretation attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib lifts the imagery from war to worship: the oaths depict the pilgrims' rush on their mounts from Mount ʿArafāt to Muzdalifah and on to Minā — the mighty, moving river of humanity that flows through the night of the tenth of Dhū al-Ḥijjah and crests at dawn, the Day of Sacrifice. Al-Bayḍāwī accepted the breadth deliberately: the verses can be applied to horses in battle *and* camels on pilgrimage, because both fulfill a function worthy of being sworn upon by God. The logic is exact. A ghāzī rides at dawn toward an enemy with weapons; a ḥājjī rides at dawn toward his Lord with Talbiyah. Both are raids — the second merely has a better prize. The Qur'an itself describes the pilgrim surge in motion:

> *"Then let them complete the rites prescribed for them, perform their vows, and circle around the Ancient House."* (Qur'an 22:29)

— a passage whose immediate context, the scattering from ʿArafāt (*thumma afīḍū min ḥaythu afāḍa al-nāsu*, 2:199), uses the verb *afāḍa* — to pour forth in flood — the same hydrodynamic energy the lexicographers hear in *ʿādiyāt*. Pilgrims who have stood in the dawn at Muzdalifah with hundreds of thousands of moving believers, dust in the air and voices in the dark, have heard these five verses with their bodies before their ears.

**From Hoofbeats to Hearts: What a Panting Animal Says to a Casual Believer**

The verse, read with the tradition, delivers a moral jolt that no paraphrase softens: the dumb animal, which possesses neither free will, nor revelation, nor hope of Paradise, holds back nothing for its master. Man, who possesses all three, holds back nearly everything for his. The Qur'an had already told us that all creation performs its praise whether we perceive it or not —

> *"The seven heavens and the earth and whatever is in them exalt Him; and there is nothing that does not exalt His praise, but you do not understand their exaltation."* (Qur'an 17:44)

— so the panting charger is simply the *audible* case of a universal fact: the whole created order runs hard after its Lord's command, and the only runner who dawdles is the one who knows Whom he runs to. Hence the tradition's application of this verse to the believer's own dawn. The Companions' fajr was their ghārah: *Their sides forsake their beds, calling upon their Lord in fear and hope* (32:16) is the cavalry-charge of the soul, and the Prophet ﷺ defined the summit of this energy in the ḥadīth of Jibrīl: *To worship God as though you see Him, for if you do not see Him, He surely sees you* (Muslim 8). In modern terms: the verse interrogates our effort budget. Most of us have, somewhere, a pursuit we ride the way the charger rides — a career, a business, a training plan, a cause — into which we throw total breath and total will. The verse does not condemn that energy; it re-targets it. God swore by maximum effort in motion because maximum effort is His due, and the scandal of the age is not that human beings cannot run but that they run hardest for what pays least. A believer who cannot find in his whole week one act pursued with the horse's totality — one fajr caught at the edge of night, one hour of learning, one secret charity given in full expenditure — has not yet heard this oath as an oath, but only as a picture.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:2

> **2 by the strikers of sparks,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Picture: Fire Struck Out of Hard Ground**

The second oath zooms from the whole animal to its hooves. *Al-mūriyāt* is the plural participle from the root wāw-rā-yāʾ, whose verb *awrā* means, in the lexicographers' usage, to kindle a fire — the same verbal field that gives the *mīrāt* of a newly lit flame. *Qadḥan* is a cognate adverb specifying the act: a sharp strike that knocks something off a hard surface. Together the two words describe a phenomenon every Arab rider knew intimately: at night, or in the grey before dawn, a herd at full gallop over stony ground throws showers of sparks, because iron-shod or hard hooves strike rock with such speed and weight that friction ignites a flash. The charger does not merely move through the darkness; it *lights* its own way by the violence of its effort. Ibn Kathīr's comment is characteristically economical: the striking of hooves on rocks causes sparks to fly. The oath is thereby narrowed and intensified: God does not swear merely by horses running, but by horses running so hard they produce fire.

Two features of the image deserve philosophical attention. First, sparks are not the horse's intention; they are the by-product of total commitment. The horse thinks about the raid, not about light — yet light follows its effort. Second, no single strike produces the effect. One hoof-fall on one stone yields one dying spark; it is the accumulated percussion of a thousand strikes per minute, sustained for miles, that turns ground into a lamp. The Qur'an invites us elsewhere to see even the familiar flame of the hearth as a divine mystery —

> *"Have you seen the fire which you kindle? Is it you who produced the tree thereof, or are We the Producer?"* (Qur'an 56:71–72)

— and this verse extends the same wonder to the accidental, self-made fire of a charging cavalry: even the light a creature manufactures by its own motion exists only because God wired stone, iron, air, and muscle to answer one another in that exact way.

**The Tactics Behind the Oath: Dawn Raids and the Ethics of the Knock at Dawn**

Why are the chargers striking sparks at all? Because, as the next verse will say, they are *raiders at dawn* — and dawn raids were the decisive tactic of the desert. An army moves by night, covers ground unseen, and strikes at first light, when the enemy is still unprepared. The sīrah literature records that the Prophet ﷺ institutionalized exactly this discipline, and Anas ibn Mālik transmitted the rule together with its astonishing ethical brake:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ used to attack the enemy at dawn (kāna yaghzū subātan). If he heard the call to prayer in a locality, he would refrain from attacking its people; otherwise he would treat them as combatants."* (al-Bukhārī 946)

The detail belongs to the moral history of warfare: even the shock tactic of the dawn raid was conditioned by the sound of the adhān — by the presence of worshippers, by the possibility that a settlement was already within the fold of faith. The sparks of verse 2, then, are not romantic torches; they are the operational signature of a community trained to act while the world sleeps, and to refrain the moment its reason for action evaporates. Energy, in this sūrah, is never unaimed.

**Sparks of Knowledge: The Spiritual Reading of the Masters**

Al-Bayḍāwī, reading the oaths as an allusion to "charging souls," assigns to this verse the most beautiful transfer of meaning in the sūrah: the charging souls, when something of the light of holiness appears to them, *strike the sparks of spiritual knowledge* (al-maʿārif) and convey them to others. The image is exact in every particular. The dark ground over which the soul runs is the unlit mass of habit and heedlessness; the flint is the hard discipline of practice — fasting, night prayer, memorization, service; the spark is an insight, a sudden understanding that flares where no light seemed possible; and the shower of sparks, sustained over years, is what the tradition calls a lamp for others. The Masters of the path drew the operational lesson that sparks must be struck at speed: insights, like sparks, die in midair unless they land on fuel. A thought of God that arrives in the heart and is not followed at once by an act — a prayer said, a wrong righted, a verse recited — goes out exactly as a spark goes out. Hence the classical counsel to "seize the moment of resolve," and hence the Prophet's ﷺ insistence that deeds be done with excellence and without delay: a report transmitted by al-Bayhaqī, whose chain critics have examined and graded sound in sense and strong in practice, records him as saying:

> *"God loves that when one of you does a work, he does it with excellence."*

**The Fire Belongs to God: Doctrine Beneath the Imagery**

There is ʿaqīdah inside the sparks. A modern chemist can describe the strike as friction, heat transfer, and combustion, and be right at every step; the Qur'an's question in 56:71 — *Is it you who produced the tree thereof, or are We the Producer?* — stands underneath every step and asks a prior question: who authored the coupling of stone and fire, and who is making it work this very instant? The sparks at the hooves, like the fire in the hearth, are a *creation in continuous dependence* — what the theologians called the ordinary custom of God (ʿādah Allāh). The verse therefore quietly trains the reader in the doctrine that all secondary causes are God's customary instruments: the rider plans the raid, the horse makes the speed, the hoof makes the spark — and every layer of the chain is God acting through the natures He gave. Ibn Taymiyyah's repeated principle applies: the agent's act is truly the agent's, and God's creation is truly God's, without confusion and without duplication.

**Repeated Small Strikes: A Law of Light That Anyone Can Test**

The most practically usable content of this verse is its account of how light is actually produced: not by one heroic gesture, but by cumulative, rhythmic, unglamorous strikes. This is a law the believer verifies at every scale. In worship, the five prayers — small strikes, repeated for decades — produce a life that glows, while the single spectacular retreat-weekend devotion that is never repeated produces a spark that dies in the air. In learning, fifteen minutes of Qur'an a day, year upon year, outproduces a burst of months followed by abandonment; the Prophet ﷺ stated the principle in exactly these terms: *The deeds most beloved to God are those done consistently, even if they are small* (al-Bukhārī 6464; Muslim 783). In character, each small refusal of anger, each small check of the tongue, is a hoof-fall; character is the glow that gathers. And in society, every small act of justice is a spark: a society is not reformed by one lightning-strike revolution but by millions of tiny percussions against its hard stones — honest invoices, kept promises, refused bribes — until, at some unmarked point, the darkness is actually lit. The verse dignifies this whole economy of small repeated effort by making it the material of God's own oath: heaven swears by accumulated percussion. Conversely, the verse carries a warning that the raider context makes explicit — the power to strike light is also the power to burn, and sparks thrown by men are no more aimless than the men who throw them. Every capacity in this sūrah will be audited: the same hoof that lights the night is the hoof that will answer for where it carried its rider.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3

> **3 by the raiders at dawn,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Ghārah: The Tactic That Made Cowards of Armies**

*Al-mughīrāt* comes from the root ghayn-rā-wāw, the root of *ghazw* (a campaign) and, more precisely, of *ghārah* — the sudden raid, and by established Arabic usage, the raid *launched at first light*. The adverb *ṣubḥan* states the timing openly: in the early morning, as the night's darkness loosens but before the sun gives the enemy time to see, think, and form ranks. Among the Arabs the dawn raid was the weapon of the disciplined and the daring: it demanded a night march by unfamiliar ground, silence under stars, and then a charge in the hour when sentries doze and camps are scattered. A commander who could deliver a ghārah owned the morning; an army that lost its mornings lost its wars. The word God chooses is thus a word already saturated with discipline, courage, initiative, and cost — and He swears by the raiders themselves, the ones who paid the cost of the tactic every single time: the mounts and their riders, moving while everyone else was warm under wool.

The historical reports about this sūrah line up with the word choice. The expedition of al-Mundhir ibn ʿAmr al-Anṣārī toward Banū Kinānah, whose delay and rumored death occasioned the revelation on one account, was precisely such a unit — riders whose horses would have been doing what verse 3 says, riding into first light. And the pilgrimage reading attributed to ʿAlī supplies a ghārah of a different kind: the camels that rush from Mashʿar (al-Muzdalifah) to Minā do so at dawn on the Day of Sacrifice, the last day of the ḥajj — the Day of ʿEid, when the whole valley pours forward in a single motion toward the throwing of the pebbles and the sacrifice. War's dawn raid and pilgrimage's dawn surge are placed in one grammatical series, as if to say that the same human capacity — to be awake and in motion at the edge of night — can be spent on taking or on consecration.

**Why Dawn? The Hour the Qur'an Keeps Returning To**

The dawn occupies a privileged place in revelation, and this verse joins a long series of texts that treat the first light as charged with witness. God swears by it directly:

> *"By the dawn and the ten nights."* (Qur'an 89:1–2)

> *"By the morning brightness and the night when it is still."* (Qur'an 93:1–2)

And in the verse every Muslim recites daily, the dawn recitation is singled out for a distinction no other prayer receives:

> *"Establish prayer from the declining of the sun to the darkening of the night, and the recitation of dawn; indeed, the recitation of dawn is (ever) witnessed."* (Qur'an 17:78)

The commentators explain *mashhūd* — "witnessed" — as witnessed by the angels of the night and the angels of the day at their changing of shifts: fajr is the moment when heaven's two watches overlap, and the standing believer is seen by both. The Prophet ﷺ weighed the same hour on the scales of value:

> *"The two units of the dawn prayer are better than the world and all that it contains."* (Muslim 725)

> *"Whoever prays the dawn prayer (al-ghadāh) is under the protection of God, so let not God call you to account for neglecting His covenant."* (Muslim 657)

Put these beside verse 3 and the design of the sūrah tightens further: God swears by raiders at dawn, and then, in the life of the believer, appoints *the same hour* as the meeting point of His protection, angelic witness, and worldly bounty. The believer's fajr is his daily ghārah — a raid on his own sleep, his own comfort, and the enemy of his soul, launched at the very hour this sūrah's oaths describe.

**The Enemy's Own Dawn Tactics: Satan's Nightly Knots**

The sīrah and the ḥadīth literature make explicit what the sūrah's imagery implies: the believer is not the only one who operates by night. The Prophet ﷺ described the devil's counter-raid on human resolve in the hours that precede the morning:

> *"When one of you goes to sleep, Satan ties three knots at the back of his head, and over each knot he says: 'A long night is before you, so sleep on.' If he wakes and remembers God, one knot is untied; if he performs ablution, another knot is untied; and if he prays, a third knot is untied, and he rises in the morning active and in good spirits; otherwise he rises lazy, sluggish, and with a defiled soul."* (al-Bukhārī 1142; Muslim 776)

Here is the war of the dawns in a single narration: one combatant rides into the morning with momentum; the other is defeated before the sun rises by three knots he never noticed. The spiritual masters read Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt against exactly this battlefield. Al-Bayḍāwī's allegorical reading assigns the raiding at dawn to the soul's assault on its own *caprice and bad habits* — the decision to strike first, before appetite wakes fully, before the day's excuses have assembled. Ibn al-Qayyim's observation in *Madārij al-Sālikīn* that the heart's key is the fajr assembly captures the same doctrine: whoever wins the first hour wins the day; whoever cedes it spends the day retaking lost ground.

**Blessing Placed in the Early Hours: A Law of the Ummah**

The Prophet ﷺ turned the dawn's advantage into a standing supplication for his nation:

> *"O God, bless my nation in its early mornings."* (al-Tirmidhī 1215; Ibn Mājah 2236 — graded ḥasan by the critics)

And his Companions read the results operationally: it is related of Ibn Masʿūd and others of the early Muslims that they dispatched their trade caravans and serious work at the break of day, saying that the Prophet's ﷺ prayer had made the early morning the hour of provision and success. The principle has outlived the horse. Modern organizations and athletes rediscovered, with studies and graphs, what this verse and this supplication already declared: momentum, judgment, and discipline are bought in the hours when willpower is cheapest and distraction is weakest. The believer possesses, in the fajr congregation, a technology that manufactures this advantage daily and attaches it to remembrance rather than to mere productivity — a ghārah launched on the soul's behalf every single morning.

**Initiative as Worship: What the Raider Teaches the One Who Waits**

Beneath the tactics lies a moral category the Arabic language isolates in this verse: *initiative*. The raider does not wait to be attacked; he moves first, at cost, in the dark, toward the objective. The Qur'an commands the same initiative in the direction of forgiveness:

> *"And hasten (sāriʿū) to forgiveness from your Lord and a Garden as wide as the heavens and the earth, prepared for the God-fearing."* (Qur'an 3:133)

The verb *sāriʿū* — race, hasten — makes the believer a raider after mercy rather than a resident waiting for it. In application: the tawbah made tonight rather than "eventually"; the apology delivered before pride sets in plaster; the charity given when the need is still urgent; the class attended while enthusiasm is unspent; the reconciliation attempted while the other heart is still reachable. Every one of these is a dawn raid on a situation that hardens by noon. The verse's warning side is equally practical: whatever you keep postponing, the raiders' oath is a reminder that the world does not wait — opportunities have their ṣubḥ, their single first-light, after which the camp has already formed and the gate has closed. God swore five times by motion so that no believer could ever again mistake delay for caution.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4

> **4 raising thereby a trail of dust,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Naqʿ: A Cloud That Testifies**

The fourth oath follows the raiders into their own wake. *Fa-atharna* is from the root alif-thā-rā — *athāra*, to stir up, to rouse, to raise what was lying still (the same root will return at the end of the sūrah, transmuted from dust to graves, when God asks whether man knows that what lies within graves will be *būthira*, stirred and raised). *Naqʿ* is the lexicographers' word for the fine, scattered dust kicked up by the pounding of hooves — not the standing haze of a calm road but the rising, rolling cloud that a body of riders drags behind it. The pronoun *bihi*, "thereby," locates the source: the dust is raised by the very charge just sworn upon — the panting, the sparking, the dawn assault produce, as their inevitable shadow, a visible trail. Ibn Kathīr and the early exegetes take the image without embellishment: a cavalry unit in motion announces itself by a dust-cloud that anyone within sight of the horizon can read. Before the riders arrive, their trail has already spoken.

**The Root That Returns: Traces Written Down**

The choice of root here is one of the sūrah's quiet masterstrokes. When the Qur'an speaks of the record of deeds, it uses this same root, thāyāʾ-rā, for the *traces* a human being leaves behind:

> *"Indeed, it is We who revive the dead and write what they have sent forward and their traces (āthārahum); and everything have We enumerated in a clear register."* (Qur'an 36:12)

The early commentators explained *āthār* in this verse as two things: the deeds sent ahead of a person, and the traces those deeds leave in the world — the good a man did that continues moving after his death, the guidance others found through him, the charity that kept walking. Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt, by making dust-trails the material of a divine oath, invites precisely this identification: nothing that moves in obedience leaves no record. The raider believes the desert will cover his passage by morning; the Qur'an answers that the naqʿ itself is testimony. And the tradition states the same law for the believer's hidden movement: the Prophet ﷺ said that the dust raised in the path of God has its own exclusivity —

> *"The dust in the way of God will never be combined with the smoke of Hellfire."* (Abū Dāwūd 1732; al-Nasāʾī 3114 — graded ṣaḥīḥ)

A believer's honest exertion, even the physical grime of it, is a credential no fire will touch.

**Dust as Honor: The Lowliness Inside the Glory**

There is also a corrective in the image that the spiritual commentators cherished. The charger's most glorious moment — full speed, mane flying, sparks flying, plunging into the enemy's mass — is wrapped, head to tail, in the humblest substance on earth: dust. The element of the low, the overlooked, the trodden, becomes the banner of the mighty. Sūrah al-Ḥajj had already instructed the believer to see elevation and abasement joined in the rites of pilgrimage —

> *"Then let them complete the rites prescribed for them, perform their vows, and circle around the Ancient House... Then let them tend to their grooming and fulfill their vows."* (Qur'an 22:29)

— the pilgrim finishes the mightiest act of his life by tending his personal grooming, dust and all. The lesson the masters drew: true striving never floats free of humility. A service of God that cannot bear to be dusty — that demands a polished image, an audience, a clean narrative — is already drifting from the raid toward the pageant. The horse does not stop to check whether its dust-cloud looks impressive; it runs, and the cloud is what running leaves behind. Every worker in God's cause has to learn the difference between dust as by-product (sincerity) and dust as advertisement (display). The Qur'an condemned display in the plainest terms: *Those who spend their wealth to be seen by people* (4:38), *who make a show* (107:6). The raider's oath honors the dust of motion, not the posing in it.

**The Trail That Follows Every Life: Modern Traces of an Ancient Law**

The naqʿ extends naturally to a modern condition the first audiences could not have imagined and the verse nonetheless describes perfectly: every human being now raises a data-cloud. Every search, message, purchase, and route is a trail of dust no desert wind can erase, archived in machines and readable years later. The ancients' dust vanished by noon; ours compounds. The verse thereby supplies an ethic for a digital age in one image: you are always raising a trail; the only question is what the trail will say. It also dignifies an older, slower truth — that a life's real effect is mostly its wake, not its headlines. Parents raise a naqʿ in their children's habits; a teacher's dust settles in decades of pupils; a single honest shopkeeper leaves a trace in a neighborhood's trust that outlasts his shop. The Qur'an commands the believer to think in traces, not moments: *Say: Each works according to his disposition, and your Lord knows best who is best guided on the path* (17:84) — and its hereafter vocabulary is built on trails brought forward: *mā qaddamū wa-āthārahum*, what they sent ahead and what they left moving.

**Between Display and Denial: The Two Corruptions of the Trail**

Classical readers of this verse noted that the trail admits two corruptions, and the sūrah's context polices both. The first is display: raising dust for the sake of being seen, the social-media piety whose real audience is not God — against which the Prophet ﷺ named the chief of all fears: *What I fear most for you is minor shirk — showing-off* (Aḥmad, in the Muṣnad, in the famous report of Maḥmūd ibn Labīd). The second is denial: the fantasy that motion leaves no trace — that deeds done in darkness are lost, that the archive of heaven has gaps. The oaths close that fantasy down. The God who swears by dust-clouds is the God who reads them: *That Day you will be exposed — no secret of yours will remain hidden* (69:18). The practical consequence is a life lived in calm transparency before God: do not spend your strength striking poses in the dust, and never believe the dust hides you. Ride straight, ride hard, raise what you must raise — and remember that every grain of it is already written, *in a clear register* (36:12).

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:5

> **5 penetrating to the center altogether,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Wasaṭa: Into the Middle, Through the Middle, All the Way**

The fifth and final oath is the climax of the sequence, and its verbs are the most violent of the five. *Fa-wasaṭna* — from *wasaṭa*, to enter the middle, to arrive at the very center — is completed by *bihi jamʿan*: thereby, into *jamʿ*, the gathered mass, the packed body of enemy troops. The commentators, following al-Bayḍāwī, take the image at face value: this alludes to the manner in which the chargers penetrated to the center of the enemy's formation. It is the most costly act on the battlefield. A raider can strike the perimeter and withdraw; to drive *into the center* means passing the front line, the second line, and everything between — exposure to attack from every side at once, with no retreat worth the name. Cavalry doctrine across every age converges on the same truth: a charge is decisive only if it holds together and goes through. Halted at the edge, it is a nuisance; shattered in the middle, it is a massacre. Only the charge that penetrates *to the center altogether* changes the battle.

The word *jamʿan* carries a second possible force that the grammarians noted: "together," "as one body." Read this way, the verse honors not only depth of penetration but *unity* of penetration — a wedge, not a scatter. The riders arrive as a single mass because they hold formation through the very zone where formations die. The Qur'an legislates the believer's version of that cohesion explicitly:

> *"And hold fast, all of you together, to the rope of God, and do not separate; and remember God's favor upon you when you were enemies and He joined your hearts, and you became brothers by His favor."* (Qur'an 3:103)

> *"The believers in their mutual love, mercy, and compassion are like one body: if one limb complains, the whole body responds with sleeplessness and fever."* (Muslim 2586)

A divided community charges like dust; a united one penetrates like this verse describes. The five oaths thus end by teaching what armies learn by blood: motion, speed, discipline, and daring are all incomplete without *cohesion at the moment of maximum danger*.

**A Complete Battle in Five Verses: The Architecture of the Oath**

Step back from the five oath-verses and their compressed narrative becomes visible. Verse 1: the chargers pant — maximum physical exertion. Verse 2: they strike sparks — friction of sustained speed against hard ground. Verse 3: they raid at dawn — tactical initiative under cover of night's end. Verse 4: they raise a dust-trail — the visible testimony of their passage. Verse 5: they plunge into the enemy's center — the objective attained at the highest cost. It is a complete operational sequence — preparation, propulsion, timing, evidence, penetration — delivered in five flashes of rhyme, each verse shorter than a heartbeat of the action it describes. The commentators observed that the Qur'an's oath sequences work this way by design: *By those sent forth in succession... by those who publish the reminder* (77:1–7) marches through the stages of revelation in seven snaps; here, five snaps reconstruct a whole campaign. The style itself models the discipline it swears by: no verse wastes a word, just as no rider in the wedge wastes a stride.

**The Word Wasaṭ Turned Back on the Believers: A Nation of the Center**

An instructive wordplay runs beneath the surface. The Qur'an elsewhere names the Muslim community itself with this same root: *Thus We made you an ummah of the middle way (ummatan wasaṭan) that you may be witnesses over mankind* (2:143). The nation that follows the raiders' example into the *wasaṭ* of the enemy is itself appointed a *wasaṭ* nation — balanced, central, holding the middle between extremes, neither monastic flight from the world nor absorption into it. The tradition's readers paused over this convergence: the quality that makes cavalry decisive — full-hearted centrality, no loitering at the edges — is the quality the ummah is commanded to embody as a way of life. Extremes are easy; the centered path is the discipline. A believer who lives at the edges of his own religion — present at the perimeter of prayer, committed to the outskirts of the community, engaged with faith only when convenient — has not yet learned the verse's geometry: God's oaths climax at the center, not at the fringe.

**The Soul's Own Charge: Bayḍāwī's ʿIlliyyūn Reading**

Al-Bayḍāwī supplies the interior reading that completes the sequence's spiritual translation: the charging souls, after conveying light, striking sparks of knowledge, and raiding their own caprice, finally *penetrate altogether into the midst of those who are in ʿIlliyyūn* — the highest register of the righteous described in Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn:

> *"No indeed! Truly the register of the righteous is in ʿIlliyyūn. And what will convey unto you what ʿIlliyyūn is? A register inscribed, witnessed by those brought near."* (Qur'an 83:18–21)

On this reading the five verses map the whole mystic ascent: breath spent, sparks struck, bad habits raided, desire raised, and at the end the soul plunges into the company of the brought-near — arriving, like the charge, at the *center*, where the near ones stand, with the same totality the horse gives its rider and the rider gives his Lord. Ibn Taymiyyah's recurrent doctrine stands behind the image: the heart's motion toward God admits of degrees, and the difference between the righteous and the brought-near is measured precisely in the completeness of the heart's advance — how far past the perimeter of obligation it has ridden into the territory of love.

**All-In Is the Only Standard: The Modern Half-Commitment Problem**

The verse's last word for contemporary life is the simplest and the hardest: *altogether*. You cannot half-enter the center of anything. A surgeon cannot half-commit to the incision; a pilot cannot half-commit to the landing; a spouse cannot half-commit to a marriage and keep the marriage; a student cannot half-learn and keep the knowledge. Our own generation is uniquely trained in the opposite skill — keeping every option open, committing to nothing fully, sampling life at its edges — and it reaps the corresponding harvest: shallow skills, shallow loyalties, shallow worship. Against all of it stands the fifth oath: heaven's own standard of action is penetration to the center. The Qur'an defines the believers with exactly this totality:

> *"The believers are only those who believed in God and His Messenger and then did not doubt, and strove with their possessions and their lives in the way of God. Those — they are the truthful ones."* (Qur'an 49:15)

And the Prophet ﷺ placed the same all-in standard on the heart's supreme allegiance:

> *"None of you truly believes until I am more beloved to him than his father, his children, and all of mankind."* (al-Bukhārī 15; Muslim 44)

Notice what these five verses never contain: hesitation, hedging, reserve. The horse is not praised for its restraint or its balanced portfolio of loyalties; it is praised for spending everything, at the point of greatest risk, in one direction. When the fifth oath closes, the audience has been shown five verses of perfect commitment — and is now, by every rule of rhetoric, braced for a divine tribute to some human body of riders. What comes instead is the pivot on which the whole sūrah turns, and it comes against *us*: *truly man is ungrateful to his Lord*.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:6

> **6 truly man is ungrateful to his Lord,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pivotal Verse: The Sworn Response No One Expected**

After five verses of thunder, the reader waits for the oath's response — the *jawāb al-qasam* that an Arabic oath always promises. By every rule of expectation built up by verses 1–5, the statement to come should honor somebody: the riders, the commanders, the men of the dawn charge. Instead, the Qur'an turns the searchlight around and illuminates the audience itself: *truly man is ungrateful to his Lord*. The chargers were the mirror; this is the face in it. Commentators across the tradition — al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr — read verses 6–8 together as the sworn assertion, with the lām of emphasis in *la-kanūd* doing the formal work of answering the oath. The rhetorical reversal is deliberate and devastating: the audience, warmed up by cavalry heroics, is suddenly told that the species it belongs to is heaven's most unreliable creature. The sūrah thus completes what its oaths began: five verses of God's creatures performing flawlessly, followed by the sworn verdict that the one creature with a *choice* performs worst.

**The Word Kanūd: A Whole Psychology in Four Letters**

*Kanūd* is a word so dense the lexicographers needed several images to unpack it. From the root kāf-nūn-dāl, it denotes one who is ungrateful and disloyal — and the lexicographical tradition adds two pictures that the Qur'an deploys with precision. First, *kanūd* describes land that yields nothing: a soil that receives the rain and returns no crop, a field that takes blessing and gives back barrenness. Second, it describes a person who is petty, churlish, and miserly — one whose eye never admits that he has been given anything worth acknowledging. Ibn ʿAbbās glossed it bluntly as *kaffār* — a denying ingrate; the attribution preserved in the commentaries continues:

> *"Kanūd means ungrateful, denying the blessing."* (attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās in the commentaries of al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī)

And the lexicon *Lisān al-ʿArab* records the summary definition: the *kanūd* is "the one who remembers evil and forgets good." Note the asymmetry built into the word: the kanūd does not forget everything; he selectively forgets. Favor evaporates from his memory; injury calcifies. A man can live forty years on unearned gifts and one month on an unpaid slight — that man is kanūd, and this verse is his diagnosis. The Qur'an uses the exact word only twice more, and both uses confirm the reading. At the sea, in the storm, the drowning sailor prays to God with an exclusivity he never grants Him on land:

> *"And when harm touches you upon the sea, those you call upon vanish except Him; but when He delivers you to land, you turn away; and man was ever ungrateful (kanūd)."* (Qur'an 17:67)

And the verse of life, death, and resurrection closes on the same word: *It is He who gave you life, then will cause you to die, then will give you life again; indeed man is ungrateful (kanūd)* (22:66) — ungrateful, that is, for existence itself, for the loan of consciousness that he repays with denial of the Lender.

**To His Lord: Why the Possessive Aggravates the Crime**

The verse does not say man is ungrateful simply; it says he is ungrateful *to his Lord* — *li-rabbihi* — and the possessive pronoun is doing heavy work. *Rabb* is the Lord who creates, owns, nurtures, guides, and brings to completion; the one "his" attaches to is the very being whose rain grew his food, whose air fills his lungs, whose design wrote his genome. The kanūd's ingratitude is thus not a neutral offense against an abstract deity; it is *treachery inside a relationship of total dependence* — closer to a guest who strips the house that feeds him, or a patient who curses the surgeon mid-operation, than to a stranger's rudeness. The Qur'an has a technical term for this betrayal: *kufr al-niʿmah*, denial of blessing — and it pairs thanklessness and denial as two faces of one act:

> *"And when your Lord proclaimed: If you are grateful, I will surely increase you; but if you deny, indeed My punishment is severe."* (Qur'an 14:7)

In the Qur'an's logic, *shukr* and *kufr* are not distant opposites but adjacent ones: kufr is ingratitude grown rigid, gratitude withheld until it hardens into denial. This is why the command to gratitude sits at the center of the covenant:

> *"So remember Me; I will remember you. And be grateful to Me and do not deny Me."* (Qur'an 2:152)

The two commands of 2:152 are the positive and negative of a single law: to be grateful to Me and not to deny Me are the same act of the heart, performed or withheld.

**The Pattern God Swears About: The Sea and the Shore**

Why did the truth of this verse require five oaths? Because it is the truth man argues against hardest, and the Qur'an documented the argument as it happened. When affliction strikes, the same man who denies God calls Him with a singular, undivided heart; when relief comes, the call evaporates:

> *"And when affliction touches man, he calls upon Us — lying on his side, or sitting, or standing. But when We remove his affliction from him, he passes on as though he had never called upon Us for an affliction that touched him."* (Qur'an 10:12; cf. 39:8, 39:49)

> *"When We bless man, he turns away and withdraws aside; and when evil touches him, he is in despair."* (Qur'an 17:83)

> *"So when they ride in the ship, they call upon God, making their faith purely His; but when He brings them safe to land, they associate others with Him."* (Qur'an 29:65)

The pattern is so constant that the Qur'an treats it as anthropological law, not complaint. The Prophet ﷺ drew the same portrait from life in a narration transmitted in the collections:

> *"Wondrous is the affair of the believer: all of it is good for him, and that belongs to none but the believer. If prosperity touches him, he is grateful, and that is good for him; and if adversity touches him, he is patient, and that is good for him."* (Muslim 2999)

The believer is defined here as the standing refutation of kanūd — the one human whose gratitude is not weather-dependent. Everyone else matches the storm pattern the verses above describe: atheist in ease, desperate monotheist in the sinking ship, forgetful again on dry land.

**The Anatomy of Ingratitude: Entitlement, Comparison, and Forgetting**

The psychological mechanics of kanūd deserve honest examination, because the condition has a structure, and structure means it can be treated. Three mechanisms power it. First, *entitlement*: the moment a blessing is mentally reclassified as a right, gratitude becomes impossible — a salary becomes "what I'm owed," health becomes "normal," a mother becomes "background." The Qur'an punctures this by naming every good as *biḥisāb* — a gift: *And He gave you of all that you asked of Him; and if you counted the favor of God, you could not enumerate it* (14:34; cf. 16:18: *if you tried to count God's blessings you would not encompass them*). Second, *comparison*: the eye trained upward sees only what it lacks; the kanūd lives with his eye on his neighbor's plate, so the feast before him registers as deficit. The Prophet ﷺ legislated the cure as a standing discipline:

> *"Look at those below you and do not look at those above you, for it is more likely to keep you from disparaging God's blessing upon you."* (Muslim 2963, 2970)

Third, *forgetting*: gratitude decays by simple memory erosion — the wonder of yesterday becomes today's furniture. Hence the Prophet's ﷺ teaching that ingratitude has a social face as well:

> *"He who does not thank people has not thanked God."* (Abū Dāwūd 4811; al-Tirmidhī 1949 — graded ṣaḥīḥ)

— because unmannered gratitude toward men is proof that one's gratitude toward God is abstraction, not practice.

**The Daily Audit: How the Tradition Turned the Diagnosis into a Cure**

The tradition responded to this verse with an entire economy of thankfulness, small enough to practice hourly. The Prophet ﷺ weighed the ordinary graces of a single morning:

> *"Whoever among you wakes up secure in his person, healthy in his body, and possessing his day's provision — it is as if the whole world has been gathered for him."* (al-Tirmidhī 2305; Ibn Mājah 4146)

— a formula that revalues a routine morning as equivalent to world-empire, precisely because kanūd misvalues routine as nothing. He taught the table's liturgy —

> *"God is pleased with a servant who eats a morsel and praises Him for it, and drinks a draught and praises Him for it."* (Muslim 2734)

— and his own night prayer, recorded by ʿĀʾishah, concentrated the whole matter into one standing question:

> *"O God, I ask You for pardon and well-being in this world and the Hereafter... O God, I ask You for pardon and well-being in my religion, my world, my family, and my wealth... and do not make this world our greatest concern."* (Muslim 2720)

The practical program is therefore not complicated, only constant: begin the morning by counting three specific blessings before counting a single problem; reclassify three "rights" back into "gifts" (your heartbeat, your mother, your sight); thank one human being by name today, since thanks to people is the field test of thanks to God; and when the sea calms — when the crisis passes, the pain eases, the job returns — hold the shore-prayer you swore on the ship. The verse swears that man defaults to kanūd; the tradition's liturgies exist to default-proof the soul against its own diagnosis. A believer who has never felt the verse apply to him has read it as history; the one who has felt it — who remembers forgetting — has read it as revelation, which is what it is.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:7

> **7 and truly he is a witness to that,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Lām of Emphasis: A Defendant Who Signs His Own Confession**

The sūrah's second emphasis-lām falls on a strange and terrible statement: not only is man ungrateful to his Lord — *he is, over that, a witness* (*ʿalā dhālika la-shahīd*). The grammatical structure is that of a second sworn assertion stacked on the first: the oath in verses 1–5 responds twice, and the second response doubles the weight. In a courtroom, a confession carries its greatest authority when the accused signs it himself. This verse reports that in the case of man's ingratitude, the accused has already signed: the denial is not hidden in an unlit corner where only God sees it; it is a fact *witnessed* — and, as the commentators discovered, the witness named by the pronoun may be either God or man, or, in the reading of the tradition's depth, both at once.

**Witness One: God, the Ever-Present Shāhīd**

One line of exegesis — recorded in al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn Kathīr, and the materials of al-Ṭabarī and al-Thaʿlabī — reads *huwa* ("he") as referring to God: God is witness to man's ingratitude. The Qur'an names God al-Shahīd and declares Him witness over everything with a regularity that makes the reading natural:

> *"Say: God is witness between me and you, and this Qur'an has been revealed to me that I may warn you thereby and whomever it reaches."* (Qur'an 6:19)

> *"God is a Witness over all things."* (Qur'an 58:6; cf. 3:98; 4:33; 22:17; 33:55; 85:9)

On this reading the verse removes every hiding place at a stroke. The ingratitude of verse 6 happens in secret — the forgotten prayer, the withheld thanks, the blessing consumed without a glance upward — and a man might conclude that what no one saw did not, in any consequential sense, happen. The verse replies that the doing and the witnessing are simultaneous: God is *shahīd* — present, seeing, recording — at the exact moment of the act. Sūrah Qāf states the machinery plainly:

> *"And We have already created man and know what his soul whispers to him, and We are nearer to him than the jugular vein — as the two receivers, seated on the right and the left, record. Not a word does he utter but there is a watcher near him, ready."* (Qur'an 50:16–18)

The doctrine of the recording angels is thus the operational form of God's witness: the divine knowledge that needs no angels nevertheless appoints them, so that on the Day of Judgment man will face his own transcript, kept by witnesses he never dislodged from his shoulders.

**Witness Two: Man Against Himself — Conscience, the Internal Court**

The other reading — and the one many exegetes preferred — takes *huwa* as man himself: man is a witness against his own ingratitude, because deep in himself he *knows*. The Qur'an maintains this claim about the human interior with striking confidence. The fiṭrah — the original nature on which every child is formed — carries an in-built moral perception, and the Prophet ﷺ taught that it operates even when the world's opinions get loud:

> *"Righteousness is that with which the soul is tranquil and the heart is tranquil; and sin is that which wavers in the soul and shifts in the breast, even if people give you opinion after opinion (fatwā after fatwā)."* (recorded in the forties of al-Nawawī's collection; al-Bukhārī 52; Muslim 2553, from Wabṣah ibn Maʿbad and al-Nuwwās ibn Simʿān)

On the Day of Judgment the Qur'an announces that this internal witness will be formally admitted to testify:

> *"Rather, man against himself will be a witness, even as he presents his excuses."* (Qur'an 75:14–15)

That couplet is one of the most psychologically exact in scripture: the same self that will testify also *excuses* — man argues both sides of his own case, and the excuses themselves become evidence of the knowledge they try to bury. The commentators explained verse 7 through this lens in two registers. In this life, man is witness to his ingratitude because his conscience flags every act of it at the moment of commission — the twinge, the dropped eyes, the argument with himself that only makes sense if a standard exists. In the next life, he becomes witness in the fullest juridical sense, when God strips away the possibility of pretense and makes the man and his own body testify together:

> *"On the Day when their tongues, their hands, and their feet will bear witness against them as to what they used to do."* (Qur'an 24:24)

> *"Today We seal their mouths, and their hands speak to Us, and their feet testify to what they used to earn."* (Qur'an 36:65)

> *"And they will say to their skins: 'Why did you testify against us?' They will say: 'We were made to speak by God, who made all things speak... and you did not used to conceal yourselves lest your hearing, your sight, and your skins testify against you — but you thought God did not know much of what you did.'"* (Qur'an 41:21–22)

The last clause diagnoses verse 7's disease exactly: *you thought God did not know much of what you did*. Ingratitude and sin persist on a wager — the wager that witnessing is absent. The verse settles the wager.

**Why Both Readings Are One Reading**

The classical scholars, in allowing *huwa* to range over God and man, were not recording confusion but a truth about the structure of accountability: the crime of ingratitude is witnessed from *above* and admitted from *within*, and judgment becomes inescapable precisely because the two witnesses never contradict each other. Al-Ṭabarī's method in such verses — to carry every linguistically sound interpretation — serves theology here: God the Witness guarantees the record's accuracy; man the witness guarantees the record's fairness, since in the end no one will be condemned for what he genuinely did not know of himself. The disbelievers of the Qur'an are described as conceding their own guilt at the last — *They will say: "Yes, our Lord"* in answer to God's questions (6:23), and *Thus do they admit their sin* (67:11) — because the witness within was never deceived, only outvoted. Sūrah al-Zalzalah, the sūrah immediately preceding this one, gave the same courtroom its verdict procedure: *that Day mankind will come forth in dispersed companies to be shown their deeds; so whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it* (99:6–8). The verb is *see* — the evidence is self-evidencing, because the witness is internal.

**Muḥāsabah: The Discipline of Taking the Witness Stand Early**

What does a believer do with a verse that makes him the witness against himself? The early Muslims answered with an institution the tradition called *muḥāsabah* — the daily audit of the soul, taking the witness stand voluntarily before the trial compels attendance. It is attributed to ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, and repeated across the literature of the ṣaḥābah and their successors:

> *"Reckon yourselves before you are reckoned, and weigh your souls before they are weighed."* (attributed to ʿUmar; recorded by al-Dhahabī and the hagiographers of the Companions)

The Prophet ﷺ framed the same audit as the definition of intelligence:

> *"The intelligent one is he who subdues his soul and works for what comes after death; and the incapable is he who follows his soul and merely hopes upon God."* (al-Tirmidhī 3713 — graded ḥasan-ṣaḥīḥ)

The practice the scholars prescribed is concrete: at the close of day, review the day as a witness would — where did I withhold thanks? what did I consume without acknowledging its Giver? where did I excuse in myself what I condemn in others? — then close the ledger with istighfār for the uncovered items and gratitude for the covered ones. In modern vocabulary, the verse mandates an uncompromised internal audit against the human talent it most exposes: *rationalization*, the machinery by which a man knows a thing and simultaneously constructs a reason to keep doing it. Verse 7 addresses that machinery by name. You may out-argue your friends, your judge, even your therapist's patience; you will not out-argue the two witnesses this verse swears by — the Lord above who saw, and the self within that knew. Better to hire the second witness as counsel now, while testimony can still be changed into repentance, than to meet him on the Day when *every soul will come forth with a driver and a witness* (50:21), and the excuse department, as the Qur'an says of that Day, *will be closed*: *And they will present their excuses on that Day* — but the presentation will convict rather than clear (6:24? — cf. 16:47; 77:36: *no permission will be given them that they should excuse themselves*).

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:8

> **8 and truly he is fierce in his love for good things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Emphasis and the Engine of the Disease**

The sūrah's third emphasis-lām completes the diagnosis: *wa-innahu li-ḥubbi l-khayri la-shadīd* — with two particles of emphasis loaded into one clause, as though heaven brackets the statement on both sides lest any reader slip past it. Verse 6 stated the symptom (ingratitude); verse 7 secured the evidence (man's own testimony); verse 8 names the engine: an intensity of love — *shadīd* is a word of severity, force, and extremity — directed at *al-khayr*, "the good things." Most commentators, from the earliest generations onward, explained *khayr* here as wealth and possessions; and the usage is authentic Arabic: the Arabs called wealth *al-khayr* — "the good" — so thoroughly that to say of a man "there is no good in him" could mean nothing more than that he was poor. Ibn ʿAbbās and the early exegetes took the word in that commercial sense; al-Rāzī and others widened it to everything the self desires of this world. Both readings converge in practice, because wealth is simply the general power over worldly goods — the "good things" in their most convertible form. And a lexicographical irony deserves to be spelled out: the Qur'an lets man's own vocabulary stand in the indictment. He has named his possessions *khayr* — "the good" — and so, when the verse declares that he loves "the good" fiercely, it is reporting his own bookkeeping: the category he reserved for the true good has been mortgaged to the market.

**The Qur'anic Catalogue: What Intense Love Does to a Life**

This verse sits in a dense field of Qur'anic teaching about the human attachment to things. The Book of God had already inventoried the objects of that attachment, horses included:

> *"Made to seem fair unto mankind is the love of passions, among them women, children, hoarded heaps of gold and silver, horses of mark, cattle, and tillage. Those are the enjoyment of the life of this world; and God — with Him is the beautiful return."* (Qur'an 3:14)

It had profiled the psychology with clinical precision in the anxiety-triad of Sūrah al-Maʿārij:

> *"Indeed, man was created anxious (halūʿ): when evil touches him, despairing; and when good touches him, withholding (manūʿ) — except the praying ones."* (Qur'an 70:19–22)

Halūʿ when poor, manūʿ when rich: the verse measures the disease by its two symptoms — panic in poverty, grip in plenty — and immediately narrows the exception to those who keep prayer. Sūrah al-Takāthur compressed the whole career of this love into a biography: *Competition in increase (al-takāthur) diverts you, until you visit the graves* (102:1–2) — the love keeps its appointments until the one appointment it cannot keep. And Sūrah al-ʿAsr's neighbor, Sūrah al-Humazah, gave the love its end: *Woe to every backbiter, slanderer, who gathers wealth and counts it, thinking his wealth will make him immortal* (104:1–3). The Prophet ﷺ supplied the experimental proof of the law that the love has no ceiling:

> *"If the son of Adam possessed two valleys full of gold, he would wish for a third; nothing fills the belly of the son of Adam but dust; and God turns to the one who turns to Him."* (al-Bukhārī 6439; Muslim 1050)

And he added the anatomy of old age — that even the decay of the body leaves this love standing:

> *"The heart of an old man remains young in two things: love of life (al-dunyā) and length of ambition (ṭūl al-amal)."* (al-Bukhārī 6439)

A faculty that outlives sight, hearing, and strength is not a passing appetite; it is a rival center of gravity. The sūrah's word *shadīd* — fierce, intense — is exact.

**Why the Love Is Fierce: The Mechanics of Attachment**

Why *fierce*? The commentators and the psychologists answer in parallel. Love of wealth is fierce because it is not really about objects; it is about survival, status, power, and fear — the whole self-image rides on the balance. Hence the escalation described in the hadith of the two valleys: each acquisition is reabsorbed into "the self," so that losing a tenth feels like dying a tenth, and the appetite simply recalibrates and reaches again. Modern life has industrialized the mechanism: an economy of advertising exists to manufacture this exact intensity — to convert products into identity, identity into craving, craving into purchase — while the mathematics of social display (visibly better cars, visibly better holidays) converts neighbors into permanent reference points. The Qur'an named the culture of comparison and the fear that drives it: *Do not strain your eyes toward what We have given some pairs of them to enjoy — the splendor of the present life — that We may test them therein; and the provision of your Lord is better and more enduring* (20:131), and it diagnosed the philosophy beneath the hoarding: *thinking his wealth would make him immortal* (104:3). Wealth-love is fierce because it is funded by mortality-fear; the love is a counterfeit immortality project. Its cure cannot therefore be mere restriction — it must be a replacement object for the heart's throne.

**The Prophet's Own Door: Wealth Loved Differently**

Islam does not abolish the love of good things; it disciplines the love by moving wealth out of the heart's throne and back into the hand's toolkit. The Prophet ﷺ affirmed earning:

> *"The upper hand is better than the lower hand."* (al-Bukhārī 1429; Muslim 1033)

He affirmed provision for one's family as charity — *A dinar you spend in the way of God, a dinar you spend to free a slave, a dinar you give in charity, and a dinar you spend on your family: the greatest in reward is the one you spend on your family* (Muslim 995) — and he redefined wealth itself as a state of the soul: *Richness is not the abundance of possessions; true richness is the richness of the soul* (al-Bukhārī 6446; Muslim 1058). Then the community demonstrated the alternative in persons. At the mustering of Tabūk, when the call went out for equipment, ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān outfitted the army so completely that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Nothing that ʿUthmān does after this day can harm him."* (al-Bukhārī 3699)

And in the famous report of ʿUmar's competition with Abū Bakr, ʿUmar arrived with half his wealth and asked what Abū Bakr had brought; told the amount, he asked, "Did you leave anything for your family?" and received the answer that has summarized the verse's cure for fourteen centuries:

> *"I left for them God and His Messenger."* (recorded by al-Tirmidhī 3675; the report of the Tabūk fundraising is also preserved in the Ṣaḥīḥ of al-Bukhārī)

Abū Bakr did not cease to love his family; he relocated the family's security from his safe to God's promise — wealth loved as a trust, spent with an open hand, and held with an open fist. The Qur'an set the exchange rate for that relocation: *You will never attain righteousness (al-birr) until you spend from that which you love* (3:92) — the only known therapy for fierce love: spend the beloved thing, and the throne empties.

**The Audit of Love: A Practical Test from the Verse**

The verse gives every reader a diagnostic he can run tonight. Love is measured by attention, time, anxiety, and sacrifice — so ask where those flow. Where does your attention go when nothing is demanded of it? What does your anxiety orbit — the Giver or the gifts? What have you actually sacrificed for the Hereafter lately, and what for the portfolio? If the answers cluster on *al-khayr* in its commercial sense, then the sūrah's three-fold oath has found its modern instance: a kanūd heart, self-witnessed, fiercely in love with "the good things" — and the sūrah's remaining verses are about to explain what happens to such a heart when the graves give up their contents. The believer's target is not poverty of possessions but *poverty of attachment*: the state the Prophet ﷺ described in his own prayer — *O God, make the provision of this world sufficient for me, and make my provision in the Hereafter the best of my provision* — and achieved in his own life, dying, as the reports relate, with his armor pledged to a Jew for barley, the owner of nothing fierce at all.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9

> **9 Does he not know that when what lies within graves is turned inside out,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Corners: A-fa-lā Yaʿlamu**

With verse 9 the sūrah changes key. The rolling oaths are done; the three-part diagnosis (ingratitude, self-witness, fierce love of "good things") is complete; and now comes not a statement but a question — *a-fa-lā yaʿlamu*, "Is he then not aware? Does he not know?" — a form the Qur'an uses to pin a hearer between two facts he cannot deny. The rhetorical structure matters. The question does not ask whether man knows in the sense of possessing information; everyone possesses it. It asks why his knowledge is not operative — why a creature who *knows* that graves empty out still lives as though his own will not. The question therefore sits in perfect continuity with verse 7: man is a witness to his own ingratitude, yet a witness who keeps behaving as if the courtroom were never scheduled. The Qur'an had already identified this split — knowing-yet-living-as-if-unknowing — as the signature disease of the heedless: *Do you think that most of them hear or understand? They are only like cattle — no, more astray from the way* (25:44). Knowledge that does not travel to behavior is, in the Qur'an's anthropology, a kind of livestock condition; the question of verse 9 is designed to be undodgeable precisely because every reader must answer for the gap between what he knows and what he is doing at the moment of reading.

**The Word Būthira: The Stirring of What Slept**

*Buʿthira* is the passive of the root bāʾ-thāʾ, whose verb *baʿatha* means to raise, rouse, stir up — and in the Qur'an's technical vocabulary, to resurrect. The same root names the sending of messengers (*baʿathnā fī kulli ummatin rasūlan*, 16:36): God's pattern of raising is one — He raises warners in history and He raises the dead at history's end, and disbelief in the second is, for the Qur'an, refusal of the first's plain logic. The object raised here is *mā fī al-qubūr* — "what lies within the graves": not the graves as architecture but their contents — bodies, and with them every occupant. The image of graves turned inside out (*buʿthira* used of overturning contents) belongs to the Qur'an's great apocalyptic inversions, the family of passages in which the created order's standing arrangements are reversed:

> *"When the earth is shaken with its shaking, and the earth brings forth its burdens."* (Qur'an 99:1–2)

> *"And when the earth is stretched out, and casts forth what is within it and becomes empty, and hearkens to its Lord, and it must."* (Qur'an 84:3–5)

> *"When the sky is stripped away, and when Hell is set ablaze, and when the Garden is brought near — then every soul will know what it has brought."* (Qur'an 81:11–14)

The commentators, noting the family resemblance, call these inversions a deliberate teaching device: the end is described as the world's *contents laid open* — earth emptied, sky rolled, hearts exposed — so that the reader's own interiority feels the mechanism in advance. The immediacy is sustained by the grammar: *idhā buʿthira... wa-ḥuṣṣila* — "when... and when..." — two conditions of one apodosis whose main clause lands in verse 11. The structure makes the Last Day feel not like a doctrine but like a schedule already loading.

**Resurrection as a Race: The Chargers Ride Again**

Here the sūrah's opening imagery returns transfigured, and the cross-references make the return explicit. The same creatures who raided at dawn in verses 1–5 will find the whole of mankind moving with their energy on the Day of the great uprising:

> *"The Day they come forth from the graves swiftly, as if they were racing to a goal."* (Qur'an 70:43)

> *"And when the trumpet is blown, behold, from the graves they rush forth to their Lord."* (Qur'an 36:51)

> *"Their eyes downcast, coming forth from the graves as if they were scattered locusts, hastening toward the caller."* (Qur'an 54:7–8)

The lexicon completes the design: *buʿthira* — the very verb of verse 9 — is the verb of *stirring to motion*, and the raider's world supplies its vocabulary: the raised dust of verse 4 (*atharna*) and the stirred graves of verse 9 (*buʿthira*) are the same gesture, at two scales. The literary point is profound: what man spends his life refusing to do voluntarily — rise and run toward his Lord — he will be made to do under compulsion, with every step recorded. The chargers obeyed their rider and were honored with a divine oath; man refuses his Rider and will ride anyway, in a stampede he did not choose, toward a courtroom he cannot skip. The commentators also recorded that the earth itself will be a party to the proceedings: *That Day she will report her news, because your Lord has inspired her* (99:4–5) — the ground that took the raider's dust will give its testimony.

**The Certainty of Resurrection: Doctrine Against the Objection**

Since the Makkan audience's chief objection was the possibility of reassembling the decayed body, the Qur'an confronted that objection at its strongest point — and chose the strangest possible evidence, the fingertips:

> *"Does man think that We shall not gather his bones? Yes indeed, We are able to restore his very fingertips."* (Qur'an 75:3–4)

> *"And he has struck for Us a parable, forgetting his own creation, saying: 'Who will revive the bones when they are rotted away?' Say: He will revive them who produced them the first time — and He is the Knower of every creation."* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

The logic is the Qur'an's standard argument from the greater to the lesser: the One who produced from nothing can re-produce from something; origin guarantees re-origination. Ibn Kathīr and the school of reasoned theology (Ibn Taymiyyah included) pressed the same syllogism: every age's scoffers assumed their science had found the floor of the possible — the corruption of the body — and every age's science then moved the floor. Modern biology, which can clone from a single cell's nucleus, has made the Qur'an's "fingertips" — the very emblem in the Prophet's ﷺ sermons of what can be distinguished anew — feel less like hyperbole than like underbid: *The Hour and I have been sent like these two,* and he joined his two fingers, forefinger and middle finger (al-Bukhārī 4828; Muslim 2951) — the end so close to the beginning that between them there is only the width of a finger.

**Visit the Graves: The Therapy of the Opened Eyelid**

The tradition did not leave verse 9 as spectacle; it prescribed the site. The Prophet ﷺ, who initially forbade grave-visitation, then reversed the ruling with its reason attached:

> *"I had forbidden you to visit graves. Visit them now, for they remind you of the Hereafter."* (Muslim 976–977; Abū Dāwūd 3235)

A visit to the graveyard is, in this teaching, a session of applied exegesis of verse 9: stand among the burūr (the graves) that have not yet been *buʿthira* and let the sight do the work the question does — collapse the distance between knowing and feeling. The practice was given liturgical form: when placing the dead in the grave, the Prophet ﷺ said, *and he made those present say: "In the name of God and upon the religion of the Messenger of God"* (Abū Dāwūd 3213 — graded ṣaḥīḥ), and after burial he would stand and say, *Ask forgiveness for your brother and pray for his steadfastness, for he is now being questioned* (Abū Dāwūd 3221). The grave, in the Qur'an and the Sunnah alike, is not a terminus but a waiting room with a clock. And the verse's question puts the clock in every reader's hand: *Does he not know...?* — know it, then, before the day the knowing becomes seeing; for on that day, *whoever did an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever did an atom's weight of evil will see it* (99:7–8), and the knowing will have stopped buying anything at all.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:10

> **10 and what lies within breasts is made known,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Ḥuṣṣila: Marrow Drawn from Bone**

The second clause of the resurrection question deepens the exposure from bodies to interiors. *Ḥuṣṣila* is the passive of the root ḥā-ṣād-lām, whose sense in the lexicographers is extraction — drawing out what is lodged within, as marrow is drawn from bone, or what is mixed is separated until the pure element stands alone. What is drawn out is *mā fī al-ṣudūr* — "what lies within the breasts": the chests' hidden cargo of intentions, loves, hatreds, envies, secrets, resolves, doubts, and the thousand silent motives that never reached the tongue. The grammatical form is a emphatic passive with no named agent: the contents *will be* extracted — by no one in particular, as if extraction were simply what the Day *does*, a property of that morning the way fire's property is heat. Verse 9 promised the graves emptied; verse 10 promises the chests emptied too. Between them, the two great hiding places of the human being — the earth, where bodies are concealed, and the breast, where souls are concealed — are scheduled for the same procedure: contents out, lids open, everything visible.

**The Qur'an's Constant: He Knows What Is Within the Breasts**

No theme recurs in the Qur'an with more insistent regularity than God's knowledge of the breasts. The Book of God says it in every register — reassurance, warning, doctrine, prayer:

> *"And whether you conceal what is within your breasts or reveal it, God knows it; and He knows what is in the heavens and what is in the earth."* (Qur'an 3:29)

> *"Indeed, they fold up their breasts to hide from Him. Surely, even when they cover themselves with their garments, He knows what they conceal and what they reveal. Indeed, He knows what lies within the breasts."* (Qur'an 11:5)

> *"And conceal your speech or reveal it; indeed, He is Knowing of what lies within the breasts. Does He who created not know, while He is the Subtle, the Aware?"* (Qur'an 67:13–14)

The rhetorical question of 67:14 — *Does He who created not know?* — is the whole doctrine in one line: the Maker's knowledge of the made is a property of making, like a composer's knowledge of his own score; it is not surveillance added to the world but the world's own inner constitution. Al-Rāzī and the school of reasoned theology drew the corollary that steadies every reader: God's knowledge is eternal and non-acquiring — He does not learn your secret when you learn it, nor was He surprised by any intention you ever formed. What changes on the Day of Judgment is therefore not God's knowledge but *access*: His knowledge, always complete, becomes *manifest*, demonstrated before all creation, so that even the knowers of the secret can no longer pretend otherwise. Sūrah al-Burūj named that day for exactly this operation — *the Day when secrets are tested* (86:9) — and Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah described the moment as exposure without cover: *That Day you will be brought forth, and no secret of yours will remain hidden* (69:18).

**The Doctrine of Intention: Why Hearts, Not Deeds, Are the Final Currency**

Why would a court of deeds subpoena hearts? Because in Islam the deed is only the shell of the act, and the intention is the act itself. The entire moral architecture of the religion stands on the first words of the first ḥadīth of al-Bukhārī's Ṣaḥīḥ:

> *"Actions are but by intentions, and every person shall have only that which he intended."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907)

The Prophet ﷺ explained there that migration to God and His Messenger and migration to a wife or a business produce identical physical motions with opposite moral identities — the body performs; the breast defines. The same doctrine generates the famous accounting of intentions:

> *"Whoever intends a good deed and does not do it, God writes it down with Himself as a full good deed; and if he intends it and does it, God writes it as ten good deeds to seven hundred times, to many times over. And if he intends a bad deed and does not do it, God writes it down with Himself as a full good deed; and if he intends it and does it, God writes it as one bad deed."* (al-Bukhārī 6491; Muslim 131)

Notice where the ledger's marvels live: an unrealized good intention is banked *in full*; an abandoned evil intention is converted into good. The currency of heaven is the breast's content, not the public record — and this is precisely why the contents must be *ḥuṣṣila*, drawn out and weighed. Two people may perform the identical external act — a charity, a prayer, a marriage, a raid — and belong to opposite eternities, distinguished only by what lay within the breasts. The verse of the Prophet's companions-in-law, the hypocrites, makes the stakes explicit: *They say with their tongues what is not in their hearts* (48:11) — a species of fraud the Day of Extraction is built to end. The Prophet ﷺ stated the audit's instrument with complete clarity:

> *"God does not look at your bodies or your forms, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds."* (Muslim 2564)

**Hidden Good, Hidden Malice: Two Futures Inside the Same Verse**

The extraction cuts both ways, and the tradition dramatized both edges. On the side of hidden good, the Prophet ﷺ ranked secrecy among the highest protections of sincerity, naming among the seven whom God will shade on the Day when there is no shade but His: *a man who gives charity so secretly that his left hand does not know what his right hand has spent* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031). What was hidden for God's sake is publicly disclosed for God's glory: the Day of ḥuṣūl is also the day of honors finally awarded. On the side of hidden malice, the exposure is not decorative. The betrayer is marshaled under a banner with his crime's name on it — *On the Day of Resurrection a banner will be raised for the treacherous one, and it will be said: "This is the betrayal of so-and-so, son of so-and-so"* (al-Bukhārī 6177; Muslim 1735) — and the man whose public persona outran his private heart is handed his true biography in front of the assembled creation. The scholars summed up the asymmetry in a formula every student of the tradition memorizes: whatever you concealed *for God*, God discloses for you; whatever you concealed *from God*, God discloses against you. The extraction itself is neutral; the valence is set by the intention the extraction finds.

**Living Before the Open Chest: Applications in the Age of Performance**

Every age has hypocrites; this age has an industry of them, because this age built infrastructure for performance. The believer lives under a permanent, ambient demand to appear — curated feeds, polished causes, charity as content, piety as brand — and the pressure subtly relocates the audience of every good act from God to the gallery. Verse 10 answers that condition with a single scheduled event: a day when the gallery's applause is worth nothing and the breast's contents are the only exhibit. Practically, the tradition teaches the believer to pre-live the extraction in small, testable habits. Give one act of goodness a week that *no living soul will ever learn of* — the sealed charity, the anonymous repair, the secret kindness to someone who cannot benefit you — deliberately training the heart to enjoy an audience of One. Purify the visible acts by asking the audit question the scholars taught: *would I still do this if no one could ever know?* — and where the honest answer is no, either change the deed's audience or change the deed. And take the comfort the verse equally supplies: the One who will lay breasts open has already seen everything in yours — nothing about you needs explaining to Him, no slander can stick in a court that reads intentions, and no secret suffering is unnoticed by the One who said, in the ḥadīth qudsī of Sūrah Qāf's neighborhood, *I am with him when he remembers Me* (recorded by al-Bukhārī 7405 and Muslim 2675 in the narration of the drawing near). The chest that will one day be opened in public can live every day open before God — and that, the sūrah's last verse will say, is not a threat at all, but the safest place a heart can stand.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:11

> **11 truly on that Day their Lord shall be aware of them!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Lām: A Sentence That Names No Punishment and Is Heavier Than Any**

The sūrah's last verse lands on its fourth and heaviest particle of emphasis — *la-khabīr* — and the choice of what to emphasize is the most studied feature of the whole chapter. After graves overturned (v. 9) and breasts laid open (v. 10), the listener braces for a sentence of doom: a mention of the Fire, the reckoning, the words *so woe that Day to the deniers*. Instead, the Qur'an says only: *truly on that Day their Lord shall be Aware of them* — *inna rabbahum bihim yawmaʾidhin la-khabīr*. No punishment is named. None is needed. The exegetes, from the early generations through al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī, read the restraint as the verse's eloquence: to say of a defendant, on judgment morning, that the Judge is fully acquainted with him — with nothing remaining to be seen, argued, or excused — is already the beginning of the verdict. The sentence's terror lies in its quietness. A man can survive an angry judge who misses evidence; no one survives a calm omniscience that misses nothing. And the source text of the tradition drew out the psychological reading exactly: God's awareness is a standing fact of every moment, but *on that Day* human beings will themselves become cognizant of it — the pretending will be structurally impossible, self-deception will have no surface left to stand on, and the deniers of intention will be confronted with the intentions they denied. As the Qur'an records of that morning's admissions: *And they will say to their skins: "Why did you testify against us?"... and thus do they admit their sin* (41:21–22; 67:11); and of the companies driven to the Fire: *...and it will be said: "Did no warner come to you?" They will say: "Yes, a warner came to us, but we denied..."* (39:71) — the confession embedded in the word *yes* is what la-khabīr forecloses.

**The Word Khabīr: Knowledge from the Inside**

*Khabīr* is one of God's most distinctive Names, and the lexicographers distinguished it carefully from its neighbor *ʿalīm*. *ʿAlīm* is the Knower — the One whose knowledge encompasses all things in their facts; *khabīr* — from the root khā-bā-rā, which in Arabic carries the sense of expert, tested familiarity — is the All-Acquainted: the One whose knowledge is *informed* from within, intimate, experiential in the manner of the one who has traversed what he knows. Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī's gloss runs along these lines: *khabīr* is he from whom nothing is hidden and by whom everything is known precisely, in its inward reality and not merely its outward form. The verse thereby tells man how he is known: not as a file, not as a number, but as a surgeon knows what he holds — totally, interiorly, without distance. And the Name comes with the possessive that has governed the sūrah from verse 6: *rabbahum* — *their Lord*. The One who will be Khabīr over them is the very One they were kanūd toward; the relationship of nurture is not suspended by judgment but carried into it. No stranger presides over this court. The Lord who gave the lungs their breath, the horse its speed, the morning its light, and the heart its chances — He is the One whose awareness finally arrives in public. Sūrah al-Mulk had already asked the question that verse 11 answers: *Does He who created not know, while He is the Subtle, the Aware (al-Laṭīf al-Khabīr)?* (67:14). And a nearby verse had already told the believers what to do with the pairing: *And know that God knows what is within yourselves, so be mindful of Him* (2:235).

**Exactness That Reaches the Animals: The Width of the Acquaintance**

How exact is the Khabīr's knowledge? The Prophet ﷺ answered with two narratives he set side by side, one of mercy, one of ruin — and both, astonishingly, about animals. He told of a woman of the Children of Israel who found a dog dying of thirst at a well, drew water in her shoe, and gave it to the animal to drink:

> *"So God thanked her and forgave her."* (al-Bukhārī 2465; Muslim 2245)

And he told of a woman who imprisoned a cat, neither feeding it nor letting it hunt, until it died:

> *"She entered the Fire because of it."* (al-Bukhārī 2363; Muslim 2242)

A drink given in secret to a dog, centuries before the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime, was seen, *thanked*, and repaid with forgiveness; a cat's starvation in a locked room, witnessed by no human record, was seen and requited. If the Khabīr's audit reaches the water in a shoe and the hunger in a cage, what reach must it have for the intentions inside a human breast — the charity never thanked, the betrayal never noticed, the kindness given to someone too distracted to see? The doctrine of divine awareness is thus simultaneously the most frightening fact available to an unjust heart and the most consoling fact available to a sincere one. The slandered worker, the unrecognized mother, the giver whose gift was stolen by ingratitude — all of them are standing in the most fully witnessed courtroom in existence. Nothing given to God is ever lost in transit: *Indeed, those who believed and did righteous deeds — their Lord will guide them by their faith; rivers will flow beneath them in gardens of bliss* (10:9).

**Why the Name of Knowledge Ends the Chapter: Mercy inside the Warning**

The scholars observed that the Qur'an closes many of its warnings on doors left open, and this verse is a doorway of exactly that kind. God's being *khabīr* on the Last Day presupposes His being *khabīr* every day before it — which means every day before it is a day of amnesty. The awareness that will convict has, until that Day, been inviting, recording repentance, and crediting the least sincerity. The Qur'an posed the theology as a divine question, and its answer is the whole logic of the sūrah's ending:

> *"Why would God punish you if you are grateful and believe? God is Appreciative, Knowing."* (Qur'an 4:147)

A Lord who asks why He should punish the grateful has no appetite for punishing; the Fire is for those who run out of gratitude and refuse every recall. Hence the Prophet ﷺ anchored the whole span of life to this ending — *Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your sickness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your work, and your life before your death* (recorded by al-Ḥākim and al-Bayhaqī, graded ṣaḥīḥ by the critics) — and he taught the standing prayer of the one who has understood verse 11: *O God, Maker of the heavens and the earth, Knower of the seen and the unseen... I seek refuge in You from the evil of my self* (Muslim 2716). Even the plea for refuge is addressed, fittingly, to the Knower of the unseen.

**The Sūrah Whole: From Hoofbeats to the Final Khabīr**

Read now as a single, closed argument, Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt moves like its own chargers. Five oaths build the image of a creature that spends everything, in disciplined motion, at dawn, leaving a visible trail, driving to the center (vv. 1–5). The sworn verdict reverses the spotlight onto the only creature in the picture with free will: man is *kanūd* — ungrateful in his selective memory; *shahīd* — witnessed against himself from above and within; *shadīd* — fiercely in love with what he calls "the good" (vv. 6–8). Then the question *Does he not know?* opens the last act: graves stirred, breasts extracted, and the closing emphasis — on that Day, their Lord is Aware of them (vv. 9–11). Every technical term of the chapter returns in the end: the *stirring* of dust (v. 4) becomes the *stirring* of graves (v. 9); the *trail* raised in motion (v. 4) becomes the record of deeds (36:12); the charge to the *center* (v. 5) becomes the rush from the graves to the Caller (70:43); the *Lord* of verse 6's ingratitude is the *Lord* of verse 11's awareness. And the choice left standing between the bookends is the one the first community read out of it: the horse runs for its master and is honored with a divine oath; the soul that runs for its Lord — conveying light, striking sparks of knowledge, raiding its own caprice at dawn, raising the dust of service, plunging into the company of those brought near — becomes itself worthy of the oath it recites. The verse man must answer is short enough to carry through a lifetime, and the answer begins the moment the recitation ends: *So hasten to forgiveness from your Lord* (3:133) — at dawn, at speed, altogether — *before a Day comes from God that cannot be turned back* (42:47), on which the Lord of the chargers is, over every rider, *la-khabīr*.
