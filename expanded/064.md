# Sūrah al-Taghābun (Chapter 64) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah of Mutual Dispossession and Its Name**

This sūrah carries a name that appears nowhere else in the Qur'an as a name of a day: al-Taghābun, "Mutual Dispossession" or "Mutual Loss and Gain." The word comes from the Arabic root *gh-b-n* (غ ب ن), which belongs to the vocabulary of trade. A *ghābin* is a seller who shortchanges his customer, and a *maghbūn* is the customer who was cheated. The form *taghābun* is a mutual form (tafāʿul), the grammar of two parties interacting: each is at once seller and buyer, gainer and loser, in a single transaction. The name refers to the Day of Gathering described in verse 9, "the Day of Mutual Dispossession," because on that day every human being finally learns the true value of everything he bought, sold, kept, or surrendered in this life. The believers, who "sold" their souls and wealth to God, discover that they purchased Paradise at a price that looks absurdly small; the disbelievers, who "sold" the Hereafter for the pleasure of a moment, discover that they purchased ruin. Both parties are shown the result of the trade, and both are, in a sense, "dispossessed" — one of his sins and one of his true gain.

**Place of Revelation and the Family of the "Glorifiers"**

The overwhelming majority of commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, count Sūrah al-Taghābun among the Madinan sūrahs. There is, however, a well-known disagreement. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās states that the entire sūrah was revealed in Makkah except the last verse. Others maintained that all of it is Makkan except verses 14–16, and there is a third position that the whole sūrah is Madinan. This disagreement is not a mere antiquarian puzzle; it shapes how scholars read verses such as the warning about spouses and children, which many connect to the Muslims who were prevented by their families from emigrating to Madinah — an event that can only belong to the late Makkan or early Madinan period. What is beyond dispute is that the sūrah belongs to the small family of chapters known as "the Glorifiers" (al-Musabbiḥāt), which open with a declaration that the heavens and the earth glorify God. That family consists of al-Ḥadīd (57), al-Ḥashr (59), al-Ṣaff (61), al-Jumuʿah (62), and al-Taghābun (64). Each opens the same door: creation is already worshipping; the only question is whether the human being will join the chorus or stand outside it.

**The Sūrah's Orderly Architecture**

The sūrah unfolds in a sequence of remarkable discipline, and the effects are cumulative. Verses 1–4 establish the divine foundation: God is glorified by all creation, He is sovereign and praised, He created human beings with freedom to believe or disbelieve, He formed them beautifully, and He knows every hidden and open thing. Verses 5–7 confront the human response: the disbelievers of earlier nations tasted the evil consequences of their rejection, they objected that a mere human being could not guide them, and even now they claim that they will not be resurrected. The sūrah answers that claim with an oath in the name of God Himself. Verses 8–10 set the two destinations side by side: belief in God, His Messenger, and the light He sent down leads to the Day of Gathering where sins are absolved and the Garden is entered; denial leads to an abiding Fire and an evil journey's end. Verse 11 turns to the believer's inner life under the decree: no misfortune befalls without God's leave, and whoever believes is guided in his heart. Verses 12–13 return to the summary of the covenant: obey God and the Messenger, and trust in God alone. Verses 14–16 descend into the most intimate battlefield of ordinary life — spouses, children, property — which God calls a trial and an "enemy," before commanding reverence, listening, obedience, spending, and freedom from the avarice of the soul. Verses 17–18 close the sūrah with the economics of faith: lend God a goodly loan and He multiplies it, and the chapter ends with the names of God as Knower of the Unseen and the seen, the Mighty, the Wise.

**The Link with the Preceding Sūrah**

Sūrah al-Taghābun follows Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn (63) so closely that it reads as its companion piece. Sūrah 63 distinguishes the hypocrites from the believers and summons the hypocrites to repentance; Sūrah 64 distinguishes the believers from the disbelievers and warns the disbelievers of the fate that awaits them. Both sūrahs end with the same pair of reminders: that property and children can divert a person from his Lord (63:9; 64:15), and that one should spend in the way of God before death arrives (63:10; 64:16). The reader of both chapters is left with a single, unforced question: what is your transaction with God, and will it stand when you are gathered?

**Reverence and Tone, and the Opening Invocation**

The sūrah begins, like every chapter of the Qur'an except Sūrah al-Tawbah, with *Bismillāhi al-Raḥmāni al-Raḥīm* — "In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful." The basmalah is not counted as one of the sūrah's eighteen numbered verses in the standard Madinan enumeration, and scholars have long discussed whether it is an independent verse of revelation, a verse of the Fātiḥah alone, or a separating sign between sūrahs; al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr each present these views with care. What is beyond discussion is its theological point: the declaration that the whole of the created order, including this very chapter, begins in God's Name. Mercy precedes warning. The sūrah will speak of terrifying realities — the Day of Gathering, the Fire, the avarice of the soul — but it speaks them from under the banner of the Compassionate, the Merciful. That order is itself a mercy: we are told of the end only after being told of the One who holds the end, and He is first named by mercy.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:1

> **Whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth glorifies God. His is the Sovereignty and His is the praise, and He is Powerful over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Opening Announcement: Creation Is Already Worshipping**

The sūrah does not open with a command to glorify God; it opens with a report that glorification is already happening. *Yusabbiḥu li-llāhi mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — "Whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth glorifies God." The verb is in the present tense, which in Arabic conveys a continuous, ongoing activity. This is no distant historical event; it is the present status of every atom and every galaxy. The sūrah thereby places the human being inside a universe that is already worshipping, and the question it quietly raises is: will you join it, or will you be the one exception? Ibn Kathīr notes that this declaration is repeated in Sūrah al-Ḥadīd (57:1), al-Ḥashr (59:1), al-Ṣaff (61:1), and al-Jumuʿah (62:1), each time with the same message: the glorification of God belongs to the very fabric of creation.

The Arabic root *s-b-ḥ* (س ب ح) carries a striking physical image. Its basic meanings include "to swim," "to move swiftly through space," and "to be far from something." The celestial bodies are described in the Qur'an as swimming in their orbits: *"And it is He Who created the night and the day, and the sun and the moon; each swims in an orbit"* (21:33). *Tasbīḥ* therefore means both motion in obedient orbit and a declaration that God is *subḥān* — far removed, utterly transcendent, free of every deficiency, defect, need, and likeness. The creatures glorify God both by their movement and by their very existence. The rock glorifies by solidity, the rain by giving life, the bird by flight; each thing fulfills its ordained function, and that fulfillment is its worship. Al-Rāzī develops the linguistic point that the present tense here conveys glorification from the present moment into the continuous future, just as the past tense in 57:1 and 59:1 conveys it from the beginning of creation to the present, and the imperative in 87:1 ("Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High") summons the human at this very moment. Glorification, in other words, is constant in every direction of time.

**The Silent Tongue of Creation and the Prominent One**

The Qur'an leaves no doubt that this glorification is real, not a figure of speech. God declares:

> *"The seven heavens and the earth and whatsoever is in them glorify Him; and there is not a thing except that it glorifies Him with praise, but you do not understand their glorification. Truly He is Clement, Forgiving."* (Qur'an 17:44)

The phrase "but you do not understand their glorification" is decisive. Creation praises God in a language of existence, function, and design that the human ear cannot hear. A scholar of nature reads the double helix, the folding of a protein, the flight of a tern across an ocean, or the exact balance of forces that keeps the earth from either freezing or burning, and he sees speech. The Qur'an taught this fourteen centuries before the scientific world began speaking of the "fine-tuning" of the universe: the design of the cosmos is not silent; it is a continuous declaration of the Designer. David is told to join this chorus explicitly: *"O mountains, echo with him, and the birds"* (34:10), and the same is said at 38:18–19. Even the thunder is described as glorifying: *"The thunder glorifies His praise, and the angels too, in awe of Him"* (13:13). And in one of the most stunning verses of the Qur'an, the Prophet is told that the pebbles and stones themselves — the very objects idolaters worshipped — are among the glorifiers: *"And there are stones that fall down from fear of God"* (2:74).

A famous hadith gives this a concrete sensory form. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Indeed, we were eating with the Prophet ﷺ, and we heard the food glorifying God while it was being eaten."* (al-Bukhārī 3579)

The commentators do not demand that we understand the mechanism; they ask only that we accept the report of the Owner of creation. The modern believer extends the same logic: the Qur'anic declaration that all things glorify God stands in no conflict with physics, chemistry, or biology. It is an explanation of what the universe is doing, and the sciences describe how it does it.

**Why Glorification Must Precede Praise, and How They Combine**

The verse then moves from the creatures to the Creator's own description: *"His is the Sovereignty and His is the praise"* — *lahu al-mulk wa lahu al-ḥamd*. The order matters, and the classical commentators ponder it. Sovereignty (*mulk*) is a claim about power, control, and ownership; praise (*ḥamd*) is a claim about worthiness, beauty, and kindness. A tyrant can hold power without deserving praise, but God's rule is inseparable from His perfection. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr explain that the verse teaches that all dominion — the present arrangement of the universe, the outcome of every event, the ownership of everything — belongs to God, and that all praise, from all praisers, returns to Him alone. The two could not be separated because what God does with His sovereignty is precisely what merits praise: He creates, sustains, forgives, judges justly, rewards generously, and has mercy on the undeserving. Sūrah al-Fātiḥah opens with "Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds," and the two words *subḥān* and *ḥamd* are joined in the most beloved of short formulas of remembrance. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Two words are light on the tongue, heavy in the scale, beloved to the Most Merciful: 'Glory be to God and with His praise; Glory be to God, the Magnificent.'"* (al-Bukhārī 7563; Muslim 2694)

And in the same spirit:

> *"Whoever says 'Glory be to God and with His praise' one hundred times a day, his sins will be erased, even if they were like the foam of the sea."* (al-Bukhārī 6405; Muslim 2691)

The combination is psychologically precise. *Tasbīḥ* purifies the mind with awe: it acknowledges that God is beyond everything we imagine or measure. *Ḥamd* fills the heart with gratitude: it acknowledges that what reaches us from beyond is good. Together they prevent the two great errors of the religious life — treating God as a distant force that one merely fears, and treating Him as a familiar convenience that one merely uses.

**"Powerful Over All Things": The Verse's Hidden Promise**

The verse closes with *wa huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr* — "and He is Powerful over all things." This clause is the theological anchor of the rest of the sūrah. Everything that follows — the resurrection that the disbelievers deny in verse 7, the gathering in verse 9, the multiplying of the loan in verse 17 — rests on this single sentence. If God is powerful over all things, then the reassembling of bones, the weighing of deeds, and the eternal Garden are not impossibilities but trivialities of power. The phrase appears frequently in the Qur'an, always at the decisive moment of a claim: *"And God is over all things Powerful"* is the argument that closes the argument about resurrection (2:20; 22:70). Ibn Taymiyyah observes that the names and attributes of God are not decorative; they are premises. When the Qur'an says God is *qadīr*, it is not merely calling Him strong; it is establishing that whatever He has promised is certain.

**The Modern Application: Measuring Ourselves Against the Chorus**

There are three practical consequences of this verse for the contemporary reader. First, it corrects the modern habit of treating worship as the special activity of humans. The believer who feels his prayer is small should remember that he is joining an assembly that never sleeps: every star, every cell in his own body, every system of the planet is already engaged in glorification. Ibn Taymiyyah taught that remembrance (*dhikr*) is to the heart what water is to the fish — the creature does not merely do it; it lives in it. Second, the verse answers the secular narrative that the universe is indifferent to us. The Qur'an presents a universe that is not indifferent but devotional; its very design is a message and its obedience is a song. Third, the verse resets the hierarchy of human speech: before we ask God for anything, we acknowledge what He is. The sūrah begins with praise, and the believer's day should begin the same way — not with complaints, but with the recognition that the Sovereign to whom we speak is the One whose praise fills existence. A person who starts his morning with *al-ḥamdu li-llāh* has already re-oriented his whole day; a person who starts with his grievances has already made himself the center of the universe, which is exactly the reversal the verse prevents.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:2

> **He it is Who created you; among you are disbelievers and among you are believers. And God sees whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Creator First, the Categories Second**

The verse begins with the pronoun of emphasis: *Huwa* — "He it is Who created you." Why "He it is"? A Qur'anic reciter or a student of Arabic notices that this verse has a threefold structure: (1) God is the Creator; (2) humanity is divided into disbelievers and believers; (3) God sees what they do. The emphatic pronoun at the opening performs a theological task: it refuses to let the discussion of belief and disbelief float free of the fact of creation. The division of humanity comes only after the fact of God's creative power, so the reader cannot treat the disbeliever and the believer as two equal, arbitrary social groups. They are two responses of the same created being to the same Creator, and the Creator's knowledge of them is complete before either of them speaks or acts.

The Arabic *alladhī khalaqakum* — "Who created you" — uses the past tense to describe a completed act: the individual human being did not exist and then came into existence by God's will. The Qur'an addresses this directly: *"O mankind, remember the favor of God upon you; is there a creator other than God who provides for you from the heaven and the earth?"* (35:3). Every human being is an event of divine creation, and the sūrah uses that as the baseline of its argument about belief. Whatever categories we invent for people — rich, poor, national, racial, ideological — the Qur'anic categories come first: believer and disbeliever, with the third, unmentioned category of the hypocrite handled in the preceding sūrah. Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn dealt with the person who *appears* to be a believer while concealing disbelief; Sūrah al-Taghābun deals with the person who is openly one or the other, and reminds us that only God sees what is in between.

**The Two Human Categories and the Freedom Behind Them**

"Among you are disbelievers and among you are believers" — *minkum kāfirun wa minkum muʾminun*. The verse does not say "He made some of you disbelievers and some believers" in a crude deterministic sense; it states the fact of division and leaves the moral responsibility intact. This is a crucial point of Qur'anic theology, and the classical commentators are explicit about it. The division is a result of human choice exercised under divine decree; it is not a sign of divine injustice. The Qur'an says in the same sūrah, in the voice of the survivors of prior nations, that the messengers came with clear proofs and the people rejected them: "So they disbelieved and turned away; yet God is beyond need" (64:6). God did not force disbelief; He sent guidance, and the response was free enough to be blameworthy.

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr present the verse as evidence that God, in His eternal knowledge, distinguishes the two groups, and that His knowledge is not affected by what they do — He knows them before they come into existence, and He watches them throughout. The Qur'an gives a vivid parallel at 76:2–3: *"Truly We created man from a drop of mingled fluid, that We might test him; and We made him hearing and seeing. Truly We guided him to the way, whether he be grateful or ungrateful."* The "whether" is the point: guidance is offered, and gratitude or ingratitude is received. Similarly, the verse at 90:8–10: *"Have We not made for him two eyes, and a tongue and two lips, and guided him to the two highways?"* A person stands before two roads and chooses. The Qur'anic language is everywhere one of responsibility, not compulsion.

**The Hadith About the Four Lifetimes**

The sources cited in the original commentary with respect to this verse include a hadith that al-Qurṭubī records in connection with the division of people into believers and disbelievers:

> *"People are born in various degrees (ṭabaqāt). A person may be born as a believer, live as a believer, and die as a believer. A person may be born as a disbeliever, live as a disbeliever, and die as a disbeliever. A person may be born as a believer, live as a believer, and die as a disbeliever. And a person may be born as a disbeliever, live as a disbeliever, and die as a believer."* (recorded by al-Qurṭubī in his commentary and cited in the source)

This hadith is a masterful statement of the meaning of free will within providence. Notice that it makes the final state, not the initial state, the decisive one. A person raised in a household of faith can end as a disbeliever; a person raised in a household of disbelief can end as a believer. The categories are not hereditary, and they are not closed. The hadith therefore functions as an antidote to two errors: the fatalist's error ("I was born this way, so I cannot change") and the proud believer's error ("I have always been a believer, so I am safe"). The Qur'an warns against both: *"Those who say, 'Our Lord, we have believed, forgive us our sins and protect us from the punishment of the Fire' — the patient, the truthful, the devout, and those who spend, and those who seek forgiveness before dawn"* (3:16–17), showing that belief is maintained by action, and *"Do not be like those who forgot God, so He caused them to forget themselves"* (59:19), showing that neglect erodes faith.

**The Action That Appears Other Than It Is**

The second hadith cited by the source community of commentators is even sharper:

> *"A person may act like the people of the Garden as it appears to the people, though he is among the people of the Fire. And a person may act like the people of the Fire as it appears to the people, though he is among the people of the Garden."* (recorded by al-Qurṭubī in connection with this verse)

This is the hadith tradition that underpins the danger of the "hidden" or "internal" dimension of faith. A person can pray, give charity, and speak beautifully while his inner state is hypocrisy; and a person can appear rough, sinful, or worldly while his inner state is submission that God will honor in the end. The point is not that outward actions are irrelevant — they are the only means by which we express faith in this world — but that outward actions are not the final measure. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the famous hadith of the heart:

> *"Truly God does not look at your bodies, nor at your forms, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds."* (Muslim 2564)

This is one of the most quoted hadiths in the entire literature, and it directly supports the closing clause of our verse: *"And God sees whatsoever you do"* — *wa Allāhu bimā taʿmalūna baṣīr*. The word *baṣīr* is from *baṣar*, sight, but a seeing that is not limited like human sight: God sees the act, the motive, the intention, the hidden hesitation, the suppressed desire, and the secret repentance. Muslim records in the same book a hadith about the three men who were tested and hid their deeds:

> *"God, may He be exalted, said: 'I have no partner. So if a person is given a reward for a deed, let it be from Me alone...'"* (Muslim 2965)

This is not paranoia; it is reality. The believer who grasps that God sees everything he does, including the things no one else sees, becomes a person of integrity — the same person in public and private. This is the psychological ethics of the verse: the "audience" that matters is always present.

**The Practical Question: Why Does the Qur'an Insist on This?**

The logical necessity of the verse can be explained in a single syllogism. If God is the Creator, then He knows the creature; if He knows the creature, then He knows the intention behind the action; and if the judgment of the Hereafter is just, then that judgment must be based on what is genuinely in the heart. Without divine knowledge of the interior, no judgment could be just — we would be judged by appearance, and appearance is precisely what the hypocrite controls. The verse therefore protects divine justice. It also protects human honesty: a person who knows that God sees his heart will be less willing to deceive himself. The modern applications are immediate: the social media profile, the charitable donation with a camera crew, the religious speech that feeds a reputation rather than a soul, the "spiritual" lifestyle that is only a brand — all of these are examined by the One whose sight penetrates. But equally, the person whose sincere faith is invisible to others, whose worship is done quietly, whose generosity is anonymous, and whose repentance is known only to God, is not unseen. He is watched by the best of watchers. The verse is, for such a person, the most consoling sentence in the sūrah.

**The Social Dimension: Believer and Disbeliever in One Community**

Finally, the verse speaks to the reality that believers and disbelievers live side by side, in the same cities, often in the same families. The earlier verse spread (verse 14) will warn about family members who oppose faith; here the point is preparatory. The existence of disbelievers in a community is not a sign that God has abandoned the community, nor is the existence of believers a sign that the world is already saved. Both are tests. The believer's response to the disbeliever is prescribed elsewhere in the sūrah and throughout the Qur'an: call to God with wisdom and good counsel (16:125), do not be compelled in religion (2:256), and leave the judgment to God. And the believer's response to a fellow believer who appears weak or inconsistent is to remember the second hadith quoted above: the finality of the verdict belongs to God alone. Our task here is not to assign the categories but to live inside our own.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:3

> **He created the heavens and the earth in truth and formed you, then made beautiful your forms, and unto Him is the journey's end.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation "in Truth": The Verse's Philosophical Anchor**

The verse's first clause is *khalaqa al-samāwāti wa al-arḍa bil-ḥaqq* — "He created the heavens and the earth in truth." The preposition *bi-* before *al-ḥaqq* is rich in meaning. It can be read as "in truth" (creation is itself a truthful reality, not an illusion), "with the truth" (creation is the expression of God's truthful word), "for the truth" (creation was made so that truth might be fulfilled and justice might be done), or "with truth" as a divine attribute (God's creation is not random, arbitrary, or deceptive). Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both explore these senses, and the difference between them matters for the reader who asks why the universe exists. If the universe were created for play or for no purpose, then the human being could not be held responsible for his actions. The declaration that creation is *bil-ḥaqq* establishes the moral framework of the entire sūrah: there is a truth to which the universe corresponds, a purpose to which the human being is accountable, and a judgment that will vindicate that purpose. The Qur'an says this repeatedly: *"We created not the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them in play"* (21:16); *"We created them only in truth, but most people do not know"* (44:38–39); *"And We created not the heaven and the earth and whatsoever is between them in vain"* (38:27). The word "vain" (*bāṭil*) is the direct opposite of *ḥaqq*. The universe is not a joke, a mistake, or a chaotic accident; it is a purposeful text in which the human being is a character with a destiny.

One way to understand the phrase is to think of a contract or a promise. A document is either true or false; a world is either meaningful or meaningless. The Qur'an says that the world is *ḥaqq* — true. Its laws are real, its patterns are reliable, its consequences are genuine. A person who lives a lie within a true world is, in the deepest sense, unrealistic. This is the theological version of what modern scientists call the intelligibility of nature: the universe is ordered, the laws are discoverable, mathematics describes it. The Qur'an states the metaphysical ground of that intelligibility: the One who made the world made it *bil-ḥaqq*, and so reason can trust the world.

**Creation as Evidence: The Telescopic and Microscopic Argument**

The verse moves from the heavens and the earth to the human being: *wa ṣawwarakum fa-aḥsana ṣuwarakum* — "and formed you, then made beautiful your forms." The sequence is precise. First *khalq* (creation), then *taṣwīr* (forming), then *iḥsān al-ṣūra* (making the form beautiful). The Qur'an distinguishes these stages elsewhere: *"He is God Who forms you in the wombs as He wills"* (3:6); *"He Who created you, then formed you, then proportioned you"* (82:7); *"And He formed you and made beautiful your forms, and to Him is the journey's end"* (40:64). Ibn Kathīr, commenting on 40:64, observes that the phrase "made beautiful your forms" refers to the human shape itself: the upright posture, the opposed thumb, the face with its unique expressions, the eye with its range of light and color, the ear with its capacity for music and speech, and the hand with its unmatched fine motor control. The human form is not merely functional; it is aesthetic. A person can be moved to tears by the architecture of a single hand.

The Qur'an makes the same claim about other creatures, and then makes the human the crown: *"God made beautiful all that He created, and began the creation of man from clay, then made his offspring from a despised fluid, then proportioned him and breathed into him of His spirit"* (32:7–9). The verse at 95:4 is even more pointed: *"Truly We created man in the most beautiful stature"* — *aḥsani taqwīm*. Some commentators, including al-Rāzī, read the beauty of form here as the beauty of the human being's inner constitution: the rational soul, the moral conscience, the capacity for language, the ability to receive revelation. Both readings are true: the human body is the most intricate known structure in the universe, and the human soul is the only creature that can ask "Who am I?" The verse points to both, and the logic is the same — a beautiful form from an unformed state requires a forming agent.

**The Beauty of the Human Face as a Sign of Divine Mercy**

The modern science of genetics has only deepened the Qur'anic claim. Every human face is composed of the same basic features, yet no two faces in the history of the species are identical. The Eye, the nose, the mouth, the expression of the smile, the curve of the brow — each combination is unique, and that uniqueness is not merely an accident of statistics; it is a hallmark of design. The Qur'an says: *"And among His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the diversity of your languages and your colors; truly in that are signs for those who know"* (30:22). The face is also the medium of speech, and speech is the medium of faith. When the Qur'an says God made beautiful your forms, it is not offering a compliment; it is offering evidence. Ibn Taymiyyah taught that the signs of God in creation are like the proofs in a book: they are placed there so that the reader of the universe may reason his way to the Author.

There is also an ethical charge in the idea that the form is beautiful. If God gave you a beautiful form — whatever society thinks of your appearance — then you are not entitled to despise yourself or others for their form. The verse undercuts all of the modern vanity industry: it says that beauty is a divine gift, not a product of makeup, surgery, diet, or filters. The person who is "unattractive" by the standards of an advertising agency is, by the standard of the Qur'an, formed by the Best of Formers. The Prophet ﷺ taught that God does not look at outward forms for the purpose of final judgment: "God does not look at your bodies or your forms, but He looks at your hearts" (Muslim 2564). The verse, in sequence with verse 2, is a complete anthropology: created, free, formed beautifully, judged by interior reality.

**The Journey's End: The Universe as a Passage, Not a Destination**

The final clause is *wa ilayhi al-maṣīr* — "and unto Him is the journey's end." *Maṣīr* comes from the root *ṣ-y-r*, "to return, to turn, to come to a destination"; the form *maṣīr* means "the place of return, the final destination." The verse establishes that the universe is not a home in the permanent sense but a journey in the true sense. The heavens and the earth are real, the human being is real, and the whole thing is a road that ends at God. This is the Qur'an's answer to the two great ancient and modern errors: nihilism (there is no destination, so there is no meaning) and pantheism (there is no destination because everything is already God). Neither is correct. There is a journey, and there is a destination, and the destination is a Person, not a void.

Al-Ṭabarī observes that the phrase "unto Him is the journey's end" appears in the Qur'an as a refrain exactly where the argument turns from creation to accountability: after the proofs of power and design, the hearer is told that this design has a terminus, and at that terminus there will be judgment. The same verse at 40:64 ends with the same phrase, and the sūrah of which that verse is a part is full of resurrection arguments. The destination is therefore not an afterthought to creation; it is creation's purpose. Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah says: *"So glory be to the Name of your Lord, the Magnificent"* (56:96) after describing the three categories of the afterlife; Sūrah al-Insān says: *"Truly your Lord's promise is true"* (76:9). God's own summary is in 23:115: *"Did you think that We created you in play, and that you would not be returned to Us?"* The question is rhetorical, and our verse answers it: the return is real, the receiver is God, and the road is now.

**The Human Response: Five Practical Consequences**

First, the verse corrects the modern despair that the universe is meaningless. If creation is *bil-ḥaqq*, then meaning is built into reality, and the human task is to find it rather than invent it. Second, it corrects the modern worship of the body: the human form is a sign of God, not an idol of the self. Third, it corrects the modern denial of accountability: if there is a destination, then there is a judgment, and if there is a judgment, then the way we travel matters. Fourth, it corrects the modern conceit of human self-sufficiency: the human being was formed by Another, and does not belong to himself. Fifth, it gives the believer the deepest ground of hope: the beautiful form and the created world are not random; they are the handwriting of the One to whom we are returning, and He is the One who made the heavens and the earth **in truth**. The end, therefore, is not something to fear for the soul that has walked in truth; it is the homecoming of a traveler who has been abroad since birth.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:4

> **He knows whatsoever is in the heavens and the earth, and He knows what you hide and what you disclose; God knows what lies within breasts.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Three Levels of Divine Knowledge**

The verse escalates in a beautiful and deliberate way, and the escalation is itself the argument. First: *yaʿlamu mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — "He knows whatsoever is in the heavens and the earth." This is the knowledge of the outer universe: the galaxies, the atoms, the creatures, the events. Second: *wa yaʿlamu mā tusirrūna wa mā tuʿlinūna* — "and He knows what you hide and what you disclose." This is the knowledge of human action: the public act and the private act. Third: *wa Allāhu ʿalīmun bi-dhāti al-ṣudūr* — "and God knows what lies within breasts." This is not merely the private act; it is the intention before the act, the motive, the suppressed thought, the wish, the fear, the secret preference. The verse therefore moves from the cosmos to conduct to conscience. Each step is closer to the center of the human person, and the final step is the root of judgment. Ibn Kathīr notes that many verses of the Qur'an repeat the phrase "God knows what lies within breasts" — including 3:119, 5:7, 8:43, 11:5, 29:10, 31:23, 35:38, 39:7, 42:24, 57:6, and 67:13 — and that the repetition is itself a warning: the Qur'an will not let the reader forget that nothing is invisible to God.

The Arabic word *ṣudūr* is the plural of *ṣadr*, "chest" or "breast." In Qur'anic idiom the chest is not merely an anatomical organ; it is the seat of the inner self, the locus of understanding, faith, fear, and intention. The Qur'an says *"the one who comes to God with a sound heart"* (26:89), *"a light inside the chest"* (64:4 in the sense of inward illumination), *"And whoever is blinded to the remembrance of the Most Merciful..."* (43:36), and *"Truly it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts in the chests"* (22:46). Al-Rāzī observes that the phrase is chosen because the chest is the anatomically central location of the human person: the heart beats in the center, and the Qur'an repeatedly connects the heart to belief, disbelief, hypocrisy, and sincerity. The knowledge of the contents of the breasts is therefore the knowledge of the "self" that no other being can know.

**Why Divine Knowledge of the Hidden Must Be Affirmed**

There is a logical reason the verse must say this. If God did not know the interior, then the Hereafter could not be just. Human courts can judge only what is evidenced; they cannot judge the motive that is never uttered, the repentance that was never spoken, the charity that was given in secret, the pride that never became a crime, or the love of God that never found words. The final judgment of the Qur'an is described as a weighing of deeds, but the deeds are meaningful only insofar as they proceed from the heart. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Actions are but by intentions, and every person shall have only what he intended."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907)

If intentions are not known to the Judge, then the two people who perform identical outward acts — one for God, one for show — would receive identical verdicts. That would be unjust, and God forbids injustice to Himself: *"Truly God wrongs not even the weight of an atom"* (4:40). So the Qur'an states explicitly what the justice of the Day requires: the Judge knows the contents of the breasts. The verse is thus not merely a claim of divine omnipotence; it is a guarantee of the integrity of the judgment.

This also explains a famous hadith of the Prophet ﷺ, in which the dimensions of the hidden are laid bare:

> *"God says: 'If My servant intends to do a bad deed, do not write it against him; if he does it, write it as one bad deed. And if he intends to do a good deed and does not do it, write it for him as a good deed; and if he does it, write it for him as ten good deeds.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6491; Muslim 128)

The hadith reveals that the *intention* itself is weighed. A person who sincerely intended charity but was prevented by death or poverty still receives the reward of the deed he never performed. A person who intended sin but suppressed it for the sake of God receives the reward of an act of worship, because his restraint was an act of obedience. Only a God who knows the hidden could administer such a law. The believer therefore lives within an economy of grace that no human institution could approximate: his secret inward struggles, his unspoken prayers, his unseen tears, his silent renunciations are all registered.

**The Verse's Psychological Function: The Cure for Duplicity and the Cure for Despair**

The verse has two psychological effects, and they are opposites in appearance but complementary in practice. On one hand, it makes hypocrisy impossible to sustain. The person who knows that God sees the interior cannot comfort himself with a public performance. The Qur'an names this condition sharply: *"They make their oaths a shield, and they turn away from the way of God"* (63:2), and *"Do they not know that God knows their secret and their whispered counsel, and that God is the Knower of the unseen?"* (58:7). The modern equivalent is the divided self: the person who is one person online and another at home, one person with the congregation and another with the colleagues, one person in the masjid and another in the market. The verse pulls the thread: there is no private self. There is only the self before God.

On the other hand, the verse cures the despair of the person whose good deeds are invisible. The woman who prays at dawn and no one sees; the man who gives anonymity and no one thanks; the teenager who resists a temptation and no one knows; the old person whose repentance is silent; the person whose faith is a burden carried alone in an unreligious family — all of them are told by this verse that the One who matters sees them. The hadith of the seven who are shaded by God on the Day of Judgment includes "a man who gives charity so secretly that his left hand does not know what his right hand has given" (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031). It is precisely the hidden character of that deed that qualifies it for the highest shade. The verse converts the secret into the most valuable currency there is: hidden obedience, known to God.

**The Social and Ethical Consequences: Knowledge of the Heart and Fair Judgment of Others**

If God knows what lies within breasts, then human beings do not. This has an enormous ethical consequence. Human assessment of others must remain provisional, because we cannot know the interior. This is why the Qur'an forbids backbiting (49:12), mocks (49:11), suspicion (49:12), and judging a person's final standing (2:62 is about the condition of those who believe and do right, leaving the judgment to God). The person whom the community regards as the worst may be the most sincere; the person whom the community regards as the best may be hiding a soul in ruins. Ibn Taymiyyah taught that the believer should judge actions, not essences: we can say "this action is wrong" without saying "this person is doomed."

The verse also corrects social hypocrisy in the opposite direction: it prevents us from despising ourselves because of what others do not see. A person who has sinned secretly, repented sincerely, and not told anyone is not required to publicize his repentance; God knows it. The Qur'an even recommends concealing sins that have been repented from: *"Those who fear their Lord in the unseen..."* (67:12) — not "in the seen." The Prophet ﷺ said, "Conceal your sins and God will conceal them for you." The privacy of the human interior, in Islam, is not a zone of concealment from God; it is a zone of intimacy with God.

**The Modern Application: Digital Intimacy and Honest Interiority**

In the age of surveillance, the verse has a particular resonance. Governments and corporations monitor behavior, purchases, messages, and location; the modern human being lives under an unprecedented amount of external observation. And yet, even in this age, there remains a region that no machine can see: the intention, the motive, the fear, the wish, the repentance. The Qur'an's claim is that this region is not empty. It is fully visible to the One who created it. The person who understands this will be more honest with the audience he cannot escape, and less anxious about the audiences he cannot control. The verse also corrects our obsession with being *seen*: the selfie, the post, the performance of virtue. God does not require visibility; He requires sincerity. The remedy for a secret sin is not a public confession; it is a private return to God. The remedy for a hidden act of worship is not publicity; it is to leave it in the hands of the Knower of the breasts, who has promised to reward it — and who, if He wills, will one day show it to the whole assembly of creation.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:5

> **Has not the account come to you of those who disbelieved aforetime such that they tasted the evil consequences of their affair, and theirs shall be a painful punishment?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rhetorical Question as a Doorway into History**

The verse opens with a rhetorical question: *a-lam yaʾtikum nabaʾu alladhīna kafarū min qablu* — "Has not the account come to you of those who disbelieved aforetime?" A rhetorical question in the Qur'an expects the answer "yes, it has," and it uses the answer to draw a conclusion. In the Qur'anic structure, "has not the account come to you" is a common way of saying: the evidence is already in front of you; the histories of destroyed nations are already in your possession; you need not wait for new proof. The phrase *nabaʾ* (account, news, report) is important: it is not merely "story" in the sense of entertainment; it is "information that carries significance." The Qur'an says the accounts of previous nations are a *dhikrā* (reminder, 50:8), an *ibrāh* (lesson, 3:13), and a *mathal* (parable, 39:27). History in the Qur'anic worldview is not a random record; it is a set of repeated experiments that the Creator has run on human civilizations, and the results are on file.

The *kāfirūn* here are all the peoples who rejected the messengers: ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, the people of Noah, the people of Pharaoh, the idolaters of Makkah's own history. The later part of the verse says *fa-dhāqū bāla amrihim* — "they tasted the evil consequences of their affair." The word *dhāqū* (they tasted) is a deliberate sensory verb. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī explain that *tasting* is used because *taste* is the most immediate, personal, and unavoidable of the senses: one can refuse to see, refuse to hear, or refuse to speak, but one cannot refuse to taste what is in one's mouth. The disasters that fell upon the disbelievers before the Hereafter were not abstract; they were experienced in the flesh — famine, defeat, displacement, drowning, earthquake. The Qur'an uses the same verb of wine and milk (16:67), of death (3:185), and of punishment (2:162 — "they will not have their punishment lightened"). The wicked "taste" the consequence of their choices, both here and hereafter.

**The Two Tastes, the Two Punishments**

*Bāla amrihim* — "the evil consequence of their affair" — is the key phrase of the verse, and the commentators distinguish it carefully from the second clause: *wa lahum ʿadhābun alīm* — "and theirs shall be a painful punishment." The first is the historical consequence in this life; the second is the eschatological punishment in the Hereafter. The two are not identical, and the distinction is essential to the Qur'anic doctrine of justice. The historical consequence — the destruction of a nation, the collapse of a civilization, the defeat of an army — is not itself the final judgment; it is a *preview* or a *sign*. The Qur'an calls it *mathalan* (a parable, 3:13) and *nukhlā* (a warning example, 39:21). What happened to ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh, and the people of Lot was a fragment of the final reckoning shown in history so that the human being could see with his own eyes a sample of the end. The full punishment belongs to the Hereafter.

This dual structure answers a common modern objection: "Why do the wicked prosper in this world?" The Qur'an's answer is not that the wicked never prosper in this world, but that their prosperity is temporary, and that a full settlement is pending. Sūrah al-Muʾminūn says: *"Do they think that We provide them with wealth and children, and that We hasten for them in good things? No, they do not perceive"* (23:55–56). The "tasting" of the consequence is not always immediate; but it is guaranteed to be complete. The verse at 9:70 says: *"Has there not come to them the account of those before them — the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Abraham, the companions of Madyan, and the overturned cities? Their messengers came to them with clear proofs; it was not God who wronged them, but they used to wrong themselves."* That final sentence is the heart of the whole doctrine: the world does not punish people arbitrarily, and God does not punish unjustly; the destruction that befell the nations was the judgment of their own free choice.

**The Principle of the "Past as Evidence"**

There is a logical principle running through the entire Qur'anic argument, and it works like a scientific induction. If history exhibits a constant pattern — that messengers are rejected, that rejection brings destruction in this life and punishment in the next — and if the pattern has held for every previous generation, then the present generation possesses a rational basis for belief. The Qur'an does not ask the disbeliever to believe on no evidence; it asks him to *read the record*. This is why the Qur'an frequently uses the phrase *"Have they not traveled in the earth and seen how the end was of those before them?"* (3:137; 30:9; 35:44; 40:21). The ruins of destroyed cities were a physical museum of divine judgment. Ibn Taymiyyah notes that the histories in the Qur'an are not offered as fables for moral entertainment; they are offered as *burhān* — demonstration. The rational structure is: (1) God is the Creator and Sovereign; (2) He sent messengers with clear proofs; (3) the nations that rejected them were destroyed; (4) therefore the pattern shows that the message was true; (5) therefore the present generation should respond before the same pattern reaches it.

**The Historical Depth: Why the Account of Noah, ʿĀd, and Thamūd Is Unique**

The Qur'an's accounts of the prior nations have a distinctive feature: they are not described as "legends" but as *records of real events*, and the Qur'an itself challenges the disbelievers to verify them: *"Say: Travel in the earth and see how the end was of the deniers"* (6:11). In the modern age, archaeology, epigraphy, and historiography have confirmed the existence of civilizations the Qur'an names — the Nabataean and Thamudic inscriptions, the Egyptian records, the Mari and Amarna letters, the sites of the drowned cities of the ancient Near East, the mound of Ur. This only strengthens the force of the verse: the "account" is not a myth; it is a document. The modern reader who doubts the historical veracity of the Qur'an's account can test it by the same method the Qur'an recommends — travel, examine, think. And what he finds is that the moral pattern of history — the decay of civilizations that abandon justice, mercy, and truth — is itself a recurring theme of historiography.

**The Psychology of National Destruction: The "Affair" as a Collective Decision**

The phrase *bāla amrihim* (the evil consequence of their affair) contains a social and collective dimension. The *amr* is not merely a private act; it is a "cause," a "matter," a "project." The Qur'an describes nations as destroyed through their own collective "affair": their rejection of the prophets was a public and institutional decision, not merely a private opinion. This is what makes the history a warning about *civilizational* responsibility, not merely individual morality. A society that institutionalizes injustice, mocks moral truths, and silences the messengers is a society setting a course toward its own end. The Qur'an calls this *"the evil consequence of the people of injustice"* (6:5; 7:95; 15:90–93). The modern reader might apply this to any society: the moral quality of public life, the treatment of the poor, the corruption of justice, the denial of fundamental truths — these are not merely private matters; they are the "affair" whose consequence may be tasted by the whole community.

**The Good News Hidden in the Verse**

While the verse is a warning, it contains a hidden mercy. The fact that God *sends the account* before the final judgment means that the warning is itself a form of love. A parent who warns a child, a doctor who warns a patient, a teacher who corrects a student — each warns because the person is worth warning. The Qur'an says: *"And We have already revealed to you the Qur'an, and We have explained in it of the examples, perhaps they will remember"* (39:27). The account of the destroyed nations is not a vindictive history; it is a rescue mission: it is sent so that the present generation may avoid the fate of the previous ones. This is why the verse in its full context (5–7) does not leave the disbelievers in despair; it turns immediately to the possibility of belief. The past has already spoken, the present is still open, and the door of repentance remains until death. The one who reads the ruins of history with the eyes of the Qur'an sees not only graves but also exit signs: *"Was there not a town that believed, and its belief profited it, except the people of Jonah? When they believed, We removed from them the punishment of disgrace in the life of this world"* (10:98). The pattern is not inevitable; the record of history is a warning, but the record of Jonah's city is a promise.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:6

> **That is because their messengers brought them clear proofs, at which they said, "Shall a human being guide us?" So they disbelieved and turned away; yet God is beyond need. And God is Self-Sufficient, Praised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Explanation of the Punishment: Why They Deserved It**

The verse begins with *dhālika bi-annahu* — "That is because" — and thereby provides the *reason* for the punishment described in the previous verse. The structure of the whole passage is now clear: verse 5 says the disbelievers tasted the evil consequence of their affair; verse 6 explains *why*. The cause is not that God is cruel, not that history is blind, and not that the nations were unlucky. The cause is that the messengers came with *bayyināt* — clear proofs — and the people refused them on the grounds that a human being should not be the one to guide them. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr explain that the *bayyināt* are the miracles, the scripture, and the rational evidence that accompanied each prophet: the staff of Moses, the healing of Jesus, the Qur'an itself, and the coherence of the message with the order of creation. The word *bayyināt* is the plural of *bayyina*, "that which makes something manifest, evident, distinct." The proofs were not obscure, ambiguous, or merely traditional; they were *clear*.

This is the crucial point of accountability: the disbelievers were not punished for failing to believe a mystery. They were punished for failing to believe a demonstration. The Qur'an holds people responsible for what was presented to them with clarity — not for what was hidden from them. The verse says *jāʾat-hum rusuluhum bil-bayyināt* — their messengers *came to them* with clear proofs. The coming is actual: the messengers arrived, the proofs were given, the message was heard, the choice was real. The punishment is therefore not a surprise; it is a verdict on evidence received.

**The Objection: "Shall a Human Being Guide Us?"**

The objection recorded in the verse is as old as prophecy and as current as yesterday: *a-basharun yahdūnanā?* — "Shall a human being guide us?" The root *h-d-y* here means "to guide" in the sense of directing, teaching, and showing the way. The disbelievers' demand was that the messenger be an angel, or that God speak directly to them, or that the messenger bring wealth and dominion. The Qur'an records this demand in many places: *"And they say: 'Why has this Qur'an not been sent down upon a great man from the two cities?'"* (43:31); *"And they say: 'What ails this Messenger, who eats food and walks in the markets? Why is there not an angel sent down unto him to be a warner with him?'"* (25:7); *"And naught hindered men from believing when guidance came unto them, save that they said: 'Has God sent a human being as a messenger?'"* (17:94).

The objection is structurally flawed, and the Qur'an answers it with several arguments. First, the messengers *are* the best possible guides for human beings precisely because they are human: they face hunger, fatigue, temptation, grief, and need — the same experiences as those they guide. If the messenger were an angel, the human being could say, "This message may be suited to an angel but not to me." The Prophet ﷺ faced poverty, persecution, betrayal, the death of his children, and the pain of a community that mocked him — and it is precisely that which makes him the model for every human life. The Qur'an says: *"Say: 'I am only a human being like you; it has been revealed to me that your God is One God'"* (41:6). The *only* is not a weakness; it is the point. The human messenger demonstrates that the message is *achievable*.

Second, the objection confuses the medium with the source. The guidance does not originate in the messenger; it originates in God. The messenger is a carrier, like a cup of water. To say "I will not drink water because it is held by a human being" is to reject the source for the sake of the carrier. The Qur'an says: *"It is not for a human being that God should speak to him, except by revelation, or from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger..."* (42:51). God's communication to human beings *must* pass through a human medium if it is to be understood; otherwise it would be an unintelligible supernatural event. The angelic objection is answered directly: *"And even had We appointed him as an angel, We would have made him a human being, and We would have obscured for them what they are obscuring"* (6:9) — meaning, God would have given the angel human form, and the objection would have been raised again. The disbelievers' demand is self-defeating: it argues for an impossibility and then says it would not accept it anyway.

Third, the objection is a *choice*, not a conclusion. The disbelievers were not trying to understand; they were trying to avoid the implication. The proof was clear, the messenger was credible, and the objection came after the demand — which is why the Qur'an says: *"So they disbelieved and turned away"* — *fa-kafarū wa tawallaw*. The two verbs are sequential: unbelief first, then turning away. The Qur'anic pattern elsewhere is the same: *"And those who disbelieve say: 'We will never believe in this Qur'an nor in that which was before it.' And if you could see when the wrongdoers are made to stand before their Lord..."* (34:31). The refusal is not an intellectual conclusion; it is a moral posture.

**The Turning Away: The Psychological Mechanism of Rejection**

*Wa tawallaw* — "and they turned away." The image is physical: a person who is shown the direction and turns his back. In the Qur'an, *tawallā* is used both literally (turning away from a battle, 8:16) and metaphorically (turning away from guidance, 41:5, 51:49; 71:3). The psychological process is well known to modern readers: a person who is confronted with an inconvenient truth first ignores it, then trivializes it, then ridicules it, then becomes hostile to it. The Qur'an describes the final stage vividly: *"And when Our signs are recited to them, they say: 'We have heard; if we wished, we could say the like of this. This is nothing but the legends of the ancients'"* (8:31). The turning away is progressive: from listening to dismissing to mocking to departing. The verse does not say they "failed to understand"; it says they "turned away." The failure was not cognitive but volitional.

This has a profound implication for modern apologetics: no proof can save a person who has decided not to be saved. The Qur'an is realistic about this. It says: *"It is not for you to guide them, but God guides whom He wills"* (2:272), and *"You do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills"* (28:56). The messenger's job is to present the *bayyināt* clearly; the response belongs to the hearer. The believer's job is likewise: to present the truth with evidence and kindness, and then to leave the heart to God. The verse thereby protects us from both despair (when people refuse) and arrogance (when they accept): we are not responsible for the outcome, only for the clarity and sincerity of the presentation.

**"Yet God Is Beyond Need": The Refutation of the False Bargain**

The next clause is subtle and devastating: *wa wa Allāhu ghaniyyun* — "yet God is beyond need." The *wāw* here is the "wāw of contrast" or "of circumstance": despite their unbelief and turning away, God is not harmed, not diminished, not disappointed in the way that would suggest He needs their belief. The disbelievers often behaved as if their rejection could hurt God, as if their acceptance would profit Him, as if their submission was a favor to Him. The Qur'an answers: *"If you disbelieve, then indeed God is independent of you, and He is not pleased with unbelief for His servants; but if you believe, He is pleased for you"* (39:7). Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī both note that the verse means: God did not compel belief even though He was able, and He did not need belief even though He desired it. The distinction between *need* and *desire* is critical: God's *pleasure* with faith is real, but His *independence* from it is also real. He is not "lonely" for worship; He is not "hungry" for praise. Unlike a human sovereign, whose power depends on subjects, God's sovereignty is intrinsic.

This is where the psychology of true worship is grounded. Worship is not a transaction in which the worshipper provides a service to God; it is a response in which the worshipper receives his own correction. The person who prays to "get God's favor" in a transactional way has already misunderstood the relationship. Prayer, charity, and obedience benefit the human being before they benefit anything else. The Qur'an states this explicitly: *"Whoever is grateful, it is for his own soul; and whoever is ungrateful — then indeed God is Self-Sufficient, Praised"* (31:12). And: *"Whoever does a good deed, it is for his soul; and whoever does evil, it is against it"* (41:46). The verse therefore destroys the deepest source of human vanity: the belief that God needs us.

**"Self-Sufficient, Praised": The Two Names That Close the Argument**

The verse ends with *wa Allāhu al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd* — "and God is Self-Sufficient, Praised." The combination is deliberate. *Ghaniyy* (rich, self-sufficient, without need) is a divine name that appears constantly with *ḥamīd* (praised, worthy of praise): 4:131, 14:8, 22:64, 31:12, 31:26, 35:15, 60:6. Ibn Kathīr explains the pairing: God is *ghaniyy* — no one's worship or obedience can add to Him — and *ḥamīd* — nonetheless all praise belongs to Him, because praise is His due, not His need. Al-Qurṭubī adds that God is *ḥamīd* even before anyone praises Him: praised *in Himself*, by His own perfection, by the angels, by the whole creation, and by the believers who accept that perfection. The disbelievers' refusal does not leave the universe without a praiser; it leaves *them* without a place in the chorus.

The pairing also has a practical implication for the believer's own life. A person who is *ghaniyy* in the Qur'anic sense does not need to be praised by others; he is content with God's praise. A person who is *ḥamīd* in the Qur'anic sense is worthy of praise from the right sources. The verse teaches the believer to measure himself by a twofold standard: not needing the approval of creatures, and being worthy of the approval of the Creator. In an age of constant validation, this is a hard but freeing discipline: the person who is satisfied with God's pleasure will not be devastated by the world's displeasure. And the person who understands that God's praise is inherent will not waste his life trying to manufacture the world's praise.

**The Modern Application: When Proof Is Not the Problem**

The most important practical lesson of this verse is that a person's rejection of the truth is rarely the result of insufficient evidence. The disbelievers of old had *bayyināt* — clear proofs — and they still turned away. The modern person who says "I would believe if I had more evidence" is often not telling the truth about himself; he is expressing a preference for not believing, which he dresses as an epistemic problem. The Qur'an's diagnosis is more honest: *"Then when their messengers came to them with clear proofs, they said: 'This is only magic, and we do not believe in it'"* (10:76). The evidence was present; the will refused. This does not mean that we should stop presenting evidence — the verse itself says the messengers *did* bring evidence, and the command to invite with wisdom remains. It means that we should not confuse clarity of evidence with willingness to accept it. The human heart is not an empty vessel waiting for proof; it is an active agent that can arrange its own defenses.

The believer who applies this verse to his own life must examine his own heart honestly: am I rejecting a command of God because I lack evidence, or because I prefer the alternative? Am I turning away from a clear obligation because I have not been persuaded, or because I have been persuaded and resent the cost? The Qur'an's categories — *kafarū* (they disbelieved) and *tawallaw* (they turned away) — name the two stages of every refusal. To see one's own discomfort with a clear truth as a moral problem rather than an intellectual one is the beginning of repentance. And to understand that God is beyond need is the end of the transaction that never was: no one has ever done God a favor by believing, and no one has ever harmed God by not. The only person harmed is the one who turned away — which is why this warning is, in the deepest sense, an act of mercy.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:7

> **Those who disbelieve claim that they will not be resurrected. Say, "Yea! By my Lord! Surely you shall be resurrected. Then you shall be informed of that which you did; and that is easy for God."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim and Its Dismissive Grammar**

The verse opens with the verb *zaʿama* — "claim." In Arabic, *zaʿama* is not a neutral verb for "to say"; it is used for assertions that are *false*, *unfounded*, or *presumptuous*. When the Qur'an wants neutral reporting, it uses *qāla* (he said); when it wants to flag an accusation as false, it uses *zaʿama*. Al-Zamakhsharī notes this distinction explicitly: *zaʿama* carries the sense of "claiming knowledge one does not have" and is thus a built-in editorial commentary. The verse is not merely reporting the disbelievers' position; it is *labeling* it as a boastful falsehood. Zubayr ibn Bakkār records that the disbelievers said: *"God will never raise anyone after death"* — and the Qur'an answers not with a debate but with a reassertion of the divine oath.

The claim itself — that there is no resurrection — is one of the most frequently recorded denials in the Qur'an. The sources cited in the original commentary list several: *"They say: 'There is nothing but our life of this world; we die and we live, and nothing destroys us but time'"* (45:24); *"They say: 'It is but our life of this world, and we will not be raised'"* (6:29); *"They say: 'When we are dead and have become dust and bones, shall we indeed be resurrected?'"* (37:16–17; 17:49; 50:3). There are also those who say it with mockery: *"And they say: 'When is this promise, if you are truthful?'"* (27:71) — and the Qur'an answers: *"Say: 'Perhaps it is near behind you'"* (17:51). The denial is ancient, and the answer is constant.

**The Divine Oath: "Yea! By my Lord!"**

The response begins with an oath: *balā wa rabbī* — "Yea! By my Lord!" The *balā* is the particle of affirmative answer to a negative question; it means "Yes, indeed, on the contrary." The oath — *wa rabbī* — is sworn by the Lord. This is a moment of staggering rhetorical force: God Himself swears by His own lordship that the resurrection will occur. The commentator al-Qurṭubī observes that God's oath by His own *rubūbiyyah* (lordship) is more emphatic than an oath by a creature; it is a self-referential guarantee. The oath appears in a similar form at 56:60–61 in connection with the first creation: *"We have decreed among you death, and We are not outstripped, that We may exchange your likenesses and create you in that which you do not know. And you have certainly known the first creation; so will you not remember?"* The argument is there stated with perfect logical elegance: if God created you the first time, then He is able to create you the second time; if you do not deny the first creation, you cannot consistently deny the second. The Qur'an makes this the explicit proof: *"And He it is Who begins creation, then He repeats it; and that is easier for Him"* (30:27). The word "easier" there is not a comparison of difficulty between two acts of equal kind, but a rhetorical concession to human imagination; for God, there is no difficulty at all. Our verse says the same: *"and that is easy for God"* — *wa dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīr*.

The logical principle deserves to be spelled out carefully. The disbeliever's objection is: "How can dust and bones be reassembled after decay and dispersal?" The Qur'an answers in three steps at three places. First, the One who created you from nothing can certainly create you from something: *"Is not He Who created the heavens and the earth able to create the like of them? Yea, and He is the Creator, the Knowing"* (36:81). Second, the One who created you the first time (from a drop of fluid) is the same One who will create you the second time (from dust): *"And you have certainly known the first creation, so will you not remember?"* (56:62). Third, the One who keeps the universe running every moment — the sunrise, the rain, the growth of a seed — is already performing something more complex than the resurrection: *"And among His signs is that you see the earth humbled; then when We send down water upon it, it shakes and swells. Truly He Who gives it life is the Giver of life to the dead. Truly He is Powerful over all things"* (41:39). The resurrection is not a violation of the natural order; it is the same power that runs the natural order, applied to the final event.

**The Purpose of the Resurrection: "Then You Shall Be Informed"**

The verse says: *thumma la-tunabbaʾunna bimā ʿamiltum* — "Then you shall be informed of that which you did." The verb *tunabbaʾunna* is from *nabbaʾa*, "to inform," and it is the intensive form of *nabaʾa* — to give news. The word carries the sense of *being told the truth of something you did not know* or *being shown the record of something you tried to conceal*. The Qur'an describes this information in several ways: the book of deeds (*yawma tūfā kullu nafsin bi-mā aḥsanat* — 3:25); the witness of the limbs: *"On that day We will seal their mouths, and their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will bear witness to what they used to earn"* (36:65); the book: *"And the book will be placed, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it"* (18:49); the testimony of the earth: *"On that day the earth will narrate its news"* (99:4). The "informing" is not merely a recitation; it is a *disclosure of reality*. The person will see himself as God saw him. The veil will be removed. The hidden will become visible.

This is the profoundest theme of the verse: the resurrection is not merely a reanimation of bodies; it is a *revelation* of persons. The self that the person constructed in this world — the image, the reputation, the performance — will be dissolved, and the self that God always knew will be displayed. The Qur'an calls it the *yawma tublā al-sarāʾir* — "the day when secrets are tested" (86:9), and *yawma yuʿradūna ʿalā rabbihim* — "the day when they are presented to their Lord" (36:53; 45:27). The disbeliever who claimed there would be no resurrection will be the one who is "informed" of all he did. The believer who feared his hidden sins will find that God "informs" him of them, but in a context of mercy: *"O you who believe! Turn to God in sincere repentance; perhaps your Lord will remove from you your evil deeds and admit you into Gardens..."* (66:8).

**"That Is Easy for God": The Doctrine of Divine Power and Human Imagination**

The final clause is the theological climax. *Wa dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīr* — "and that is easy for God." *Yasīr* means "easy, light, trifling." The verse is not saying that resurrection is a small matter for God in the sense of a whimsical act; it is saying that no act is difficult for God because His power is not like created power. The created being has limits: effort, time, materials, tools. God has none. Ibn Taymiyyah explains that the created things operate through causes, and we measure difficulty by the causes we know; but God's act is not measured by causes, because He is the First Cause. When we say "it is easy for God," we are not describing God's effort; we are describing our own conceptual failure to measure Him.

The phrase also answers the famous modern objection: "The resurrection is scientifically impossible because the body decays, is eaten, is scattered." The Qur'an answers: *"And they say: 'When we have become bones and dust, shall we be created anew?' Say: 'Be you stones or iron, or any creation harder in your breasts — then they will say: Who will return us? Say: He Who created you the first time'"* (17:49–51). The point is that the *material* is irrelevant to God; what matters is His will. If a person can accept that God created the universe from nothing, he cannot logically refuse the resurrection of the body. The Qur'an's proof is a reductio: either there is a Creator with absolute power, or there is no Creator and no life; if the first, then the resurrection follows; if the second, then the discussion is meaningless because there is no person to discuss it. The disbeliever who says "there is no resurrection" while using his own rationality, his own desire for justice, his own sense of meaning, is undermining the very faculties by which he denies it.

**Hadith and Scholarship on the Resurrection**

The Prophet ﷺ spoke of resurrection in terms of extraordinary simplicity, and his words provide the concrete imagery of the verse:

> *"On the Day of Resurrection, the people will be gathered barefoot, naked, uncircumcised."* (al-Bukhārī 6527; Muslim 2860)

> *"Between the two trumpet blasts is forty."* (al-Bukhārī 4935; Muslim 2940 — the interval described by the Companion who asked about it)

> *"You will be resurrected upon the manner of your death."* (reported in the authentic traditions concerning the way the martyrs and the slain are raised)

> *"A man will be brought on the Day of Resurrection who was drowned, or burned, or ruined..."* (related in the context of the "first" and "last" being judged)

The scholars of Islam have never regarded the resurrection as a belief that must be accepted against evidence; they have regarded it as the consummation of the evidence. Al-Ghazālī, in his *Iḥyāʾ*, writes that the heart that truly believes in the resurrection lives in a state of constant readiness: the world is seen as a journey, and death is seen as a door, not a wall. Ibn Kathīr, in his commentary on this very verse, says the oath is a refutation of the idolaters' stubborn denial and a confirmation that the promise is certain. The verse thus rejects both the ancient denial and the modern variant — the existentialist claim that death is the end and the materialist claim that consciousness cannot survive the body. The Qur'an answers both with the same sentence: *balā wa rabbī* — "Yea! By my Lord!"

**The Psychological and Practical Consequences of Living with Resurrection**

The doctrine of resurrection transforms the way a person lives. If there is no resurrection, then the only meaning of life is what can be experienced before death; all moral claims are ultimately negotiable, and all justice is ultimately unenforceable. If there is a resurrection, then every act is a deposit in a permanent account, and every person is, in the deepest sense, immortal. The Qur'an expresses the difference: *"Do those who have committed evil think that We will make them like those who have believed and done good — equal in life and death? Evil is what they judge"* (45:21). A society that denies resurrection will eventually discover that it cannot ground justice, because there is no final court; a society that affirms resurrection sees even its smallest injustices as falling under the jurisdiction of an eternal judge. The believer who takes this verse seriously will live with the consciousness of the "informing": he will know that his secret is not secret, that his private compromise will be disclosed, and that his hidden mercy will be rewarded. This is not fear in the pathological sense; it is the clarity of a person who knows the audit is real.

**The Final Word: The Resurrection as the Great Equalizer**

The resurrection is the great equalizer of the human race. The Pharaohs, the emperors, the tyrants, the celebrities, and the anonymous poor all stand on the same plain, before the same Judge, with the same book. The Qur'an says: *"That is the Day of the true; so whoever wills, let him take a path to his Lord"* (78:39). The verse we are studying is the divine answer to the human denial: *balā wa rabbī* — the oath; *la-tubʿathunna* — the certainty; *thumma la-tunabbaʾunna* — the disclosure; *wa dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīr* — the ease. The denial is refuted, the oath is sworn, the disclosure is promised, the power is affirmed. The reader is left with the question that the whole sūrah has been building toward: if this is true, and you know it is easy for God, then what are you doing with the life you have been given?

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:8

> **So believe in God and His Messenger and the light We have sent down. God is Aware of whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Imperative That Follows the Oath**

The verse begins with *fa-āminū* — "So believe." The *fa* (so, therefore) is the particle of consequence. It connects the imperative to the oath and the proof that preceded it: since the resurrection is true, since God has sworn by His Lord, since the informing is certain, *therefore* believe. The verse is a logical conclusion, not a groundless demand. The entire structure of verses 5–8 is now visible: the history (v. 5), the cause (v. 6), the oath of resurrection (v. 7), and the response required (v. 8). The Qur'an never asks for faith without first supplying reason; it supplies the evidence, then issues the command. This is one of the most important features of the Qur'anic method: *imān* is not a leap into darkness but a response to demonstration, and this verse marks the transition from demonstration to obligation.

*Āminū* is from the root *a-m-n*, which carries the meanings of "security, safety, trust, assurance." Faith is not merely intellectual assent; it is the act by which the heart entrusts itself to God. The word is related to *amān* (security), *amīn* (trustworthy), and *āmanā* (to put one's trust in). The believer is, grammatically, a person who has handed over his security to God. The command "believe in God and His Messenger" is the two-part formula of the Islamic confession: *lā ilāha illā Allāh, Muḥammadun rasūl Allāh*. The two are inseparable in the Qur'anic order, and the commentators make this explicit. To believe in God without the Messenger is to believe in a message without an instruction; to believe in the Messenger without God is to follow a human without an authority. The verse places them together because the religion is one: the messenger's authority derives from God, not from himself, and the believer's submission to the messenger is submission to God.

**The "Light" That Was Sent Down**

The third object of belief is *al-nūr alladhī anzalnā* — "the light We have sent down." This is the central interpretive question of the verse, and the classical commentators give several answers. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī say the primary reference is the Qur'an itself: the revelation is light because it illuminates the darkness of ignorance, error, and moral confusion. Al-Rāzī says the light is the *total* of divine guidance: the Quran, the sunnah, the signs of reason, and the conscience. The word *nūr* occurs throughout the Qur'an in this connection: *"O mankind, there has come to you a proof from your Lord, and We have sent down to you a manifest light"* (4:174); *"And follow the light that has been sent down with him; it is they who shall prosper"* (7:157); *"Truly there has come to you from God a light and a manifest Book"* (5:15). Sūrah al-Nūr itself begins with a sūrah "which We have sent down and made obligatory, and in which We have sent down clear signs" (24:1), and the Qūr'an calls itself *"a light"* at 42:52: *"And thus We have revealed to you a spirit of Our command; you did not know what the Book was, nor faith; but We have made it a light by which We guide whom We will."*

The *nūr* (light) metaphor is not decorative; it is an epistemological claim. Light has a specific function: it makes things visible. Before light, there is no distinction between objects; after light, there is distinction. The Qur'an says the revelation is light because it makes the moral world visible: it distinguishes truth from falsehood, justice from injustice, purity from corruption, the straight path from the crooked. The Qur'an calls this the *furqān* — the criterion — and says: *"Blessed is He Who sent down the Criterion to His servant, that he may be a warner to the worlds"* (25:1). The person who sees in the darkness of moral confusion is like a person who has been shown a map in a fog: the map does not remove the journey, but it makes the direction possible.

The word *anzalnā* ("We sent down") indicates a vertical, deliberate act of divine communication. It is the same verb used for the sending down of the Torah, the Gospel, the Psalms, the rain, and the iron. The Qur'an is not a human composition; it is *munzal* — sent down. This is why the verse commands belief in the *light*: because the light's origin is beyond the human, its content is not at the disposal of the human, and its authority does not depend on human acceptance. The believer is therefore not free to pick and choose: *"So believe in what We have sent down, confirming what is with you"* (2:41).

**The Shīʿī Reading and Its Place in the Commentary Tradition**

The source notes that some Shīʿī commentators, among them al-Kāshānī and al-Qummī, interpret the "light" in this verse as referring to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and to the Imams of his line, or to a spiritual light within the believers. This interpretive tradition is cited in the source, and it should be understood respectfully: the Shīʿī readings are rooted in the same Qur'anic principle that the Prophet ﷺ and his family are a means of guidance — the hadith of the "two weighty things" and the verse of the purification (33:33). The majority scholarly tradition, however, is explicit that the primary reference is the revelation itself, and this is the view of al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī. The Qur'an's own usage — "light" as revelation, guidance, and truth — is far more frequent and more directly supported by the immediate context. The verse speaks of the light *We have sent down*; what God sent down, in the Qur'an's own words, is the Book and the clear proofs (2:185; 39:1; 42:17), not a single person. The scholarly difference is legitimate, but the weight of the classical exegesis is clear, and the verse's practical point — that the believer must accept the revealed guidance — is agreed upon by every school.

**"God Is Aware of Whatsoever You Do": The Knowledge That Makes Belief Honest**

The closing clause is *wa Allāhu bimā taʿmalūna khabīr* — "and God is Aware of whatsoever you do." *Khabīr* is from the root *kh-b-r*, meaning "one who is thoroughly aware, one who knows the inner state and the hidden detail." This is the third time the verse's theme of divine knowledge has appeared in the sūrah: verse 2 said God sees what you do (*baṣīr*), verse 4 said God knows what lies in breasts (*ʿalīm*), and verse 8 says God is aware (*khabīr*). The different words are not synonyms; they describe complementary aspects: *baṣīr* is the seeing, *ʿalīm* is the knowledge, *khabīr* is the thorough, penetrating awareness. The verse says: the one who commands you to believe is the one who knows what is in your heart, and He will know whether your belief is genuine or merely verbal. This is the specific reason the verse ends here: belief is not a slogan; it is a reality that God can test.

The psychological effect is subtle but profound. A person who knows that God is *khabīr* cannot deceive himself into thinking that a verbal profession is enough. He knows that the claim "I believe" is inspected by a consciousness that sees the hidden doubts, the secret resentments, the private compromises. But this awareness is not only a warning; it is also a comfort. The person whose faith is genuine but weak, whose belief wavers in moments of darkness, whose heart is full of questions — this person knows that God sees the honest struggle as well as the fallen moment. The Qur'an says: *"God knows what is in your souls; God is Knowing, Clement"* (4:147 — the verse says *"God is ever Knowing and Forbearing"*). The one who is *khabīr* knows the difference between the person who is trying to believe and the person who is pretending to believe. The first is guided; the second is exposed.

**The Logic of the Threefold Belief**

Why must belief be in God, the Messenger, and the light together? The answer is structural. Faith in God without the Messenger is a vague theism that has no content; it may affirm a Creator but has no instruction, no law, no path. Faith in the Messenger without God is hero-worship that has no object: the messenger is meaningful only because he represents God. Faith in the light without the Messenger is a book without a teacher: the Quran without the sunnah is like a letter without a postal carrier. The three are a unity: God is the source, the Messenger is the medium, the revelation is the message. This is why the Qur'an repeatedly commands obedience to God and the Messenger together, and why it treats the two as inseparable: *"Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed God"* (4:80). The verse is therefore a summary of the whole religion, and the believer who accepts all three accepts the complete structural relationship between heaven and earth.

**The Modern Application: What "Believe" Means Today**

In the modern world, "belief" is often treated as a private opinion, something like a preference for a brand or a political position. The Qur'an treats it as a *covenant* that controls life. The believer in God, the Messenger, and the light does not merely hold an opinion; he accepts a commission. The light makes this concrete: the Quran is not only a book to be admired but a *directive* to be followed. The command *fa-āminū* is the natural response to the evidence of the past, the oath of the resurrection, and the intelligence of the design. If the reader has understood the preceding verses, the command is not a burden but a relief: it tells him the truth of what he has already begun to suspect. Belief is the release from the exhausting task of constructing one's own meaning, one's own morality, and one's own destiny. The light tells the person who is lost where the path is. The *khabīr* tells the person who has lost hope that the One who sees his secret will not lose him.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:9

> **The day that He gathers you for the Day of Gathering, that is the Day of Mutual Dispossession. And whosoever believes in God and works righteousness, He will absolve him of his evil deeds and cause him to enter Gardens with rivers running below, to abide therein forever. That is the great triumph.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day of Gathering: The Assembly That Contains All Generations**

The verse names two aspects of one event: *yawma yajmaʿukum li-yawmi al-jamʿi* — "the day that He gathers you for the Day of Gathering" — and *dhālika yawmu al-taghābun* — "that is the Day of Mutual Dispossession." The first name defines the event as a *gathering*; the second defines it as a *transaction*. The Day of Gathering is described throughout the Qur'an as the event in which every human being who has ever lived stands on one plain: *"The day when the earth will be changed to another earth, and the heavens, and they will appear before God, the One, the All-Overruling"* (14:48); *"And they will be shown to the Lord, standing"* (36:53). The famous description in Sūrah al-Ḥaqqah is unforgettable: *"On that day you will be exposed—not one of your secrets will remain hidden"* (69:18). The *yawm al-jamʿ* is the census of the entire human race: *"It is He who will gather you on the Day of Gathering—there is no doubt in it"* (42:7).

The gathering includes not only human beings but also the *jinn*: *"O company of jinn and humans! Did there not come to you messengers from among yourselves, reciting to you My signs and warning you of the meeting of this day of yours?"* (6:130). And all animals: *"There is no animal on the earth, nor a bird that flies with its two wings, except that they are communities like you. We have neglected nothing in the Book; then to their Lord they will be gathered"* (6:38). The image is of total assembly, total presence, and total accountability. The Qur'an even describes the very bones being gathered: *"Say: He will bring them to life Who created them the first time, and He is Knower of every creation"* (36:79), and *"Does man not see that We created him from a drop, then behold, he is an open opponent?"* (36:77). The gathering is cosmic, biological, moral, and eschatological.

**The Day of Mutual Dispossession: The Trade That Is Finally Seen**

*Taghābun* is the key word of the sūrah, and the commentators devote substantial energy to it. The root is *gh-b-n*, which in the marketplace means "to cheat, to shortchange, to sell for less than the value." The form *taghābun* is mutual: two parties both suffer the loss of a transaction. The classical explanation of the name, as recorded in the source, is that on that day the believers will be seen to have "cheated" God and God will be seen to have "cheated" them — but in the sense of a paradoxical *gain through loss*. How? The believers sold their souls and wealth to God (9:111; 2:207), and God paid them with the Garden. The transaction was so one-sided, so generous, that the believer appears to have "shortchanged" God: he gave a moment of life and received eternity; he gave a portion of wealth and received a palace; he gave an ounce of effort and received infinite reward. The disbelievers, by contrast, "bought" the life of this world with the Garden: *"It is they who have purchased error at the price of guidance, and punishment at the price of forgiveness"* (2:175); *"And those who sell the Hereafter for the life of this world, they have bought a loss"* (2:86 in the sense of the exchange being evil). On the Day of Gathering, both parties see the transaction: the believer realizes he has gained beyond all calculation; the disbeliever realizes he has lost beyond all recovery. The "mutual dispossession" is that both parties are shown to have made a trade — one trade in the eternal profit, one in eternal loss.

The commentators deepen this in two directions. Al-Ṭabarī says the believers "dispossess" the disbelievers of their portions in Paradise, and the disbelievers "dispossess" the believers of their portions in the Fire. This is the image of the Day as a *final redistribution of cosmic property*: the Fire is for those who earned it, the Garden for those who earned it, and neither can be exchanged, inherited, or shared. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr add the second direction: the word *taghābun* also covers the fact that no one can repay God what God has given him. Even the believer who does everything right stands in debt: God gave him life, breath, health, reason, time, revelation, and the capacity to believe. The believer's "good deeds" are themselves a gift, so the final reward is not a wage but a mercy. On the Day of Mutual Dispossession, every human being sees the truth of the hadith of the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"None of you will enter Paradise by his deeds." They said: "Not even you, O Messenger of God?" He said: "Not even me, unless God envelops me with His mercy. But be upright and draw near."* (al-Bukhārī 6463; Muslim 2818)

The "mutual" of the day is thus also this: the believer is not "cheated" — he is overwhelmed. The disbeliever is not "cheated" either — he is shown the truth of what he refused. The Day is a mirror, not a transaction.

**The Two Clauses of Success: Faith and Deeds**

The verse then states the condition of success with perfect balance: *man yuʾmin billāhi wa yaʿmal ṣāliḥan* — "whosoever believes in God and works righteousness." The two clauses are joined by *wa* (and), and the commentators stress that both are necessary. Belief alone, without deeds, is incomplete: the Qur'an says *"Do the people think that they will be left to say, 'We believe,' and not be tested?"* (29:2), and *"It is not righteousness that you turn your faces toward the east and the west, but righteousness is whoever believes in God, and the Last Day, and the angels, and the Book, and the prophets..."* (2:177) — the verse continues with deeds: giving wealth, freeing the captive, establishing prayer, giving alms, and so on. Deeds alone, without belief, are meaningless: the Qur'an says *"And We shall turn to whatever deeds they did, and make them scattered dust"* (25:23), and *"Whoever works a good deed, it is for his own soul"* (41:46) — but the good deed that proceeds from unbelief does not reach God. The verse's formula is therefore the complete religion: the root is belief, the branches are deeds, and the fruit is the Garden.

What are the *ṣāliḥāt* (righteous deeds)? The classical scholars give a comprehensive definition: every act that is (1) commanded or permitted by revelation, (2) performed with the intention of seeking God's pleasure, and (3) beneficial to self or others. The Qur'an enumerates the main lines: prayer, alms, fasting, pilgrimage, honesty, justice, kindness to parents, care for orphans, patience, gratitude, forgiveness, and of course the bedrock: *"Verily, the believers are those who believe in God and His Messenger, then doubt not, and strive with their wealth and their selves in the way of God; they are the truthful"* (49:15). The *ṣāliḥāt* are not a checklist; they are a *character* — the state of being a person who works right, because his heart is right. The modern reader should not reduce this to "good works" in the sense of philanthropy; it includes the interior acts: repentance, hope, patience, trust, and sincerity. The hadith of the Prophet ﷺ captures the whole: *"Purity of intention is a righteous deed; and the person who controls his anger, God will control His punishment for him."*

**"He Will Absolve Him": The Doctrine of the Removal of Sins**

The verse says *yukaffir ʿanhu sayyiʾātihi* — "He will absolve him of his evil deeds." The verb *yukaffir* is from *k-f-r*, which in this context means "to cover, to erase, to remove," as when farmers cover a seed with soil (*kafara al-ḥabb* — he covered the seed) — hence *kāfir* originally mean "one who covers the truth." Here the same root is used in the opposite direction: God covers the believer's sins, erases them, removes them from the account. The Qur'an uses the same verb at 2:271, 3:31, 5:65, 48:5, 66:8. The doctrine is that on the Day of Judgment, the believer's good deeds will be weighed against his sins, and the sins will be *covered* — not merely balanced, but *hidden* — so that what remains is the good. The Prophet ﷺ described the moment:

> *"God will bring the believer near and make him acknowledge his sins, then He will say: 'I concealed them for you in the world, and today I forgive them for you.'"* (Muslim 2768)

And in a famous hadith of the scales, the Prophet described the man of the last account whose single deed of *lā ilāha illā Allāh* outweighs ninety-nine scrolls, each as long as the eye can see (al-Tirmidhī 2639; Ibn Mājah 4294 — the hadith of the tablet containing *lā ilāha illā Allāh*).

This is the heart of the believer's hope. The verse does not say God will ignore his sins, or reduce them, or blend them into his good deeds; it says God will *absolve* — will cover, erase, remove. The believer is not left with a balance sheet; he is left with a ledger in which the debt has been forgiven. This is why the verse follows the Day of Mutual Dispossession: the believer who "lost" his sins is the one who *gained* everything, and the disbeliever who "kept" his sins is the one who lost everything.

**"Gardens with Rivers Running Below": The Sensory Description of Paradise**

The reward is described in the most concrete terms of the Qur'an's vocabulary of the Garden: *wa yudkhilhu jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — "and cause him to enter Gardens with rivers running below, to abide therein forever." Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr explain that "Gardens" (*jannāt*) are walled orchards — places of shade, water, fruit, and protection — and that the rivers "under them" are the streams flowing through their midst, so that the believer sees water at his feet and greenery above and fruit in his hand. The Qur'an describes the rivers of Paradise as *"rivers of water that does not change (in taste), rivers of milk whose taste does not change, rivers of wine delicious to those who drink, and rivers of clarified honey"* (47:15). The reward is not merely spiritual abstraction; it is the fulfillment of every natural desire — beauty, rest, family, knowledge, pleasure — purified and eternal. But the Qur'an is also careful: *"For those who believe and do righteous deeds, they will have Gardens of Paradise as an entertainment — but the good pleasure of God is greater; that is the great attainment"* (9:72). The rivers are the *minimum*; the *maxim* is God's pleasure. The paradise described here is real, and its ultimate joy is nearness to God.

The phrase *khālidīn fīhā* — "to abide therein forever" — is decisive. The believer's reward is not a temporary respite; it is a permanent state. The Qur'an repeats this phrase in dozens of verses, and it is one of the central doctrines of the religion: the afterlife is not a cycle, not a reincarnation, not a dissolution, but an eternal abode. Ibn Taymiyyah wrote that the eternity of the Garden and the Fire is one of the principles on which the entire prophetic message stands: if the afterlife were not eternal, then the inequality between the moment of obedience in this life and the infinite reward would be unjust; and the justice of God requires the eternity of the reward. The verse's *khālidīn* is therefore not a poetic flourish; it is the ground of all moral seriousness.

**"That Is the Great Triumph"**

The verse ends with *dhālika huwa al-fawzu al-ʿaẓīm* — "that is the great triumph." The phrase appears over a dozen times in the Qur'an (e.g., 5:119; 9:89; 9:100; 22:77; 33:71; 37:60; 44:57; 57:12; 61:12). *Fawz* comes from *f-w-z*, "to attain, to reach the goal, to be saved." The Arabic word carries the sense of a race that is won: the runner reaches the finish line; the swimmer reaches the shore; the seeker reaches the treasure. The only "triumph" in the ultimate sense is this: to believe, to do right, to have sins covered, and to enter the Garden forever. Every other victory — wealth, fame, power, health, political success — is a provisional victory at best, and a deception at worst. The Qur'an says: *"Know that the life of this world is only a game, a diversion, an adornment, a boasting among you, and a competition in wealth and children, like rain whose plant delights the tillers, then it withers, then you see it yellow, then it becomes chaff. And in the Hereafter is a severe punishment and forgiveness from God and good pleasure. And the life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion"* (57:20 — the verse that directly precedes this sūrah's sister). That verse is quoted in full in the original commentary's context, and it is the perfect mirror of our verse: the "triumph" of the world is an illusion; the "triumph" of faith is real.

**The Practical Psychology of the Day of Mutual Dispossession**

How should a person live with this verse? Two ways. The first is to realize that every moment is a transaction. The sūrah uses market language because the human being is a creature of exchange: we give time, and we receive life; we give money, and we receive goods; we give effort, and we receive achievement. The Day of Mutual Dispossession asks: what did you exchange for your soul? This is why the Qur'an says: *"O you who believe! Shall I guide you to a commerce that will save you from a painful punishment? You believe in God and His Messenger and strive in the way of God with your wealth and yourselves; that is better for you, if you know"* (61:10–11). The believer is a trader with a single asset — the soul — and the verse warns him not to exchange it for "the paraphernalia of this world" (3:14). The second way is to realize that the Market is *fair*: the verse says that whoever believes and works righteousness will be given the Garden. The equity of the market is guaranteed by the justice of the Owner. The believer does not need to fear that his sincerity will be overlooked, or that his effort will be forgotten. God says: *"We do not let the reward of the one who works right be lost"* (18:30). The Day of Mutual Dispossession is the day when the market's books are opened, and the books are kept by the One who never forgets.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Center**

This verse is the center of the sūrah, and the whole chapter turns on it. Everything before it (creation, knowledge, history, resurrection, the command to believe) is the *preparation*; everything after it (the decree, the obedience, the family, the spending) is the *application*. The verse thereby does what the Qur'an does at its best: it holds the cosmic and the personal in a single sentence. The same event that is called the "Day of Gathering" by the universe is called the "Day of Mutual Dispossession" by the trader, and the same reward that is called the "Garden" by the senses is called the "great triumph" by the soul. The reader is invited to see his own life — his small purchases, his daily choices, his hidden sacrifices — as the raw material of that day. If he does, the verse will not leave him frightened; it will leave him *oriented*. He knows the destination, the price, and the Merchant. The only remaining question is whether he is willing to trade.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:10

> **And those who disbelieve and deny Our signs, they are the inhabitants of the Fire, abiding therein. What an evil journey's end!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Verbs of Rejection: Disbelief and Denial**

The verse pairs two terms: *alladhīna kafarū* — "those who disbelieve" — and *kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* — "and deny Our signs." The combination is deliberate, and the commentators treat the two as distinct but joined. *Kufr* (disbelief) is the state of the heart: the refusal to accept God as He has revealed Himself. *Takdhīb* (denial) is the action of the tongue and the life: the rejection of the *āyāt* — the signs, verses, proofs, and messengers. A person might be a *kāfir* in the sense of not believing, and a person might be a *mukadhdhib* in the sense of actively denying; the verse joins them because in practice the second is the expression of the first. The person who disbelieves in God will deny His signs; the person who denies His signs confirms his unbelief. The Qur'an frequently makes the same pairing: *"Those who disbelieve and deny Our signs — they will be companions of the Fire, abiding therein"* (2:39; 5:10; 22:51; 42:45; 57:19).

The *āyāt* here have a double reference, and the source notes it: "signs" can mean the verses of the Qur'an (in which case the verse refers to those who reject revelation), or the signs of creation (in which case it refers to those who reject the evidence of design). The Qur'an uses *āyāt* for both: *"Among His signs are the night and the day and the sun and the moon"* (41:37); *"These are the verses of the Book of Wisdom"* (31:2). In the first sense, the deniers reject the *bayyināt* that were brought by the messengers; in the second sense, they deny the signs in the sky, in the earth, and in their own souls. Al-Qurṭubī says both are included: the one who denies the written signs and the one who denies the cosmic signs are both within the verse's judgment. The modern reader should note that the *denial* is not limited to an intellectual refusal; it is also a moral refusal — the person who sees the signs and turns away, like the person who sees a warning sign on a road and ignores it, is a *mukadhdhib*. The signs are not merely propositions; they are *invitations*, and to ignore an invitation is to refuse it.

**"Inhabitants of the Fire": The Grammar of Permanent Residence**

*Ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-nār* — "they are the inhabitants of the Fire." The word *aṣḥāb* means "companions, associates, residents." The Qur'an uses it of the Garden too: *aṣḥāb al-jannah* — "the companions of the Garden" (2:82; 7:44). The image is of two communities, two households, two cities of residence. The Fire is not a place the disbeliever visits, suffers, and leaves; it is the *dwelling* of those who, by their rejection, have made it their home. The grammar of the verse adds the decisive word: *khālidīn fīhā* — "abiding therein." This is the same word used of the believers in the previous verse (*khālidīn fīhā*). The parallelism is exact and intentional: the believer's eternity and the disbeliever's eternity are stated in the same form, so that the reader cannot mistake the permanence of either. The Qur'an says: *"And they will not taste in it any coolness or drink, except boiling water and washing, a fitting recompense"* (78:24–26); *"As often as they wish to emerge from it from anguish, they will be returned to it: 'Taste the punishment of the burning Fire'"* (22:22). The horror of the Fire in the Qur'an is not merely the pain; it is the *impossibility of exit*. The companions of the Fire are not visitors; they are residents.

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note that the phrase "abiding therein" is used absolutely here, with no exception or qualifier. In some verses the Qur'an mentions that God may forgive whom He wills (4:48, 4:116 — the two unforgivable sins being shirk and the refusal of the truth joined to it), but the narrative form of the verse is clear: those who die upon disbelief and denial of the signs are the people of the Fire. The classical scholars distinguish between the *unforgivable* disbelief (the one that ends a life) and the *forgivable* sins of believers, which are covered by repentance, mercy, and the intercession of the Prophet ﷺ. The verse, therefore, is not a description of the believer's ordinary failures; it is a description of the fixed state of those who reject the message. The believer who reads it should read it with the added knowledge of the hadith:

> *"Whoever says 'There is no god but God' and dies upon that will enter Paradise."* (al-Bukhārī 6417; Muslim 26 — reported with the qualification of final belief)

**"What an Evil Journey's End!"**

The verse closes with an exclamation: *biʾsa al-maṣīr* — "What an evil journey's end!" The word *maṣīr*, used in verse 3 of this sūrah (*wa ilayhi al-maṣīr* — "and unto Him is the journey's end"), is now turned to its other face: the destination can be God's presence, or it can be the Fire; the journey that leads to the Fire is the evil one. The phrase *biʾsa* is an exclamatory form of condemnation — "how wretched is!" The Qur'an uses it of many things: *"biʾsa al-sharāb* — how evil is the drink" (18:29); *"biʾsa al-mathal* — how evil is the example" (62:6); *"biʾsa al-ʿamal* — how evil is the deed" (5:62). The commentators note that *maṣīr* (journey's end) is a word of movement, not of a final static place: even the Fire, in the Qur'an's language, is a *destination reached by a road* — the road of disbelief, pride, and denial. The verse therefore says: the road you are walking is real, the destination is real, and the destination you have chosen through denial is as evil as the road by which you reached it.

The grammar of the exclamation has another purpose: it is not merely information; it is *emotion*. The Qur'an does not describe the Fire in a clinical, detached way; it uses language that presses on the reader's conscience. The *biʾsa* is like a written sigh of grief — the divine voice saying, in effect, "How evil is the end that awaits them!" This is the tone of the whole sūrah's warnings: not the cold announcement of a judge who enjoys the verdict, but the urgent appeal of a Guide who would rather see his listener walk the other road. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the longest hadith of intercession, that on the Day of Judgment even the prophets will be too overwhelmed to speak of the Fire without tears — and the Qur'an's own voice here is the evidence of that compassion: the verse warns about the evil journey's end *so that* no one will take that journey.

**The Logic of the Verse: Why Denial Deserves the Fire**

Why does the denial of signs deserve the Fire? This is a question that a modern reader, trained to think of "belief" as a private matter, might ask. The Qur'anic answer has several layers. First, the signs are not a hidden mystery; they are clear proofs (*bayyināt*), and the denial of clear evidence is a moral, not merely intellectual, act. Second, the denial is not an accident of ignorance; it is a decision that follows the pattern of *kufr* and *takdhīb* — a progressive refusal that the Qur'an describes as *"then in their hearts they were certain, yet they denied them out of injustice and arrogance"* (27:14). The phrase "injustice and arrogance" is the key: the denier *knows* and refuses. Third, the denial involves the rejection of the guidance that would have rescued the denier and everyone he influences: the person who denies the signs is not only refusing his own salvation; he is discouraging others from finding theirs. The *aṣḥāb al-nār* is therefore not a random category; it is the destination of those who, having been shown the road, deliberately chose the other one.

The moral principle behind the verse is the same one that underlies all law, human and divine: a person is responsible for what he knows, and his responsibility increases with the clarity of the evidence. This is why the Qur'an says: *"And We never punish until We have sent a messenger"* (17:15) — the warning must precede the punishment for the punishment to be just. The verse we are studying comes after the warning; it is not the announcement of an arbitrary fire, but the description of the end of a road that the denier chose with open eyes. If the reader is troubled by the verse, the correct response is not to try to soften it but to examine whether he is on the road it describes — and if not, to thank God, because the alternative was available to everyone.

**The Practical Application: The Verse as a Mirror, Not a Weapon**

There is a final, essential point about the *use* of this verse. It is very easy to read verses about the Fire as a weapon against others — to classify people, to condemn communities, to feel superior. The Qur'an itself corrects this: *"Do not say of anyone who says 'peace' that he is a disbeliever"* (4:94), and *"It is not for you to guide them, but God guides whom He wills"* (2:272). The verse's immediate function is *personal*: it is addressed to the hearer, and it commands the hearer to examine himself. The person who has not denied the signs, who has accepted the message, who has repented, is not to use this verse as a weapon; he is to use it as a *warning about his own trajectory*, because no one is permanently safe from denial. The Prophet ﷺ warned: *"A man may act with the deeds of the people of Paradise until there is an arms-length between him and it, then what is written overtakes him"* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643). The verse therefore belongs in the personal, not the polemical, sphere. Reading it should produce the fear of the believer — the fear that keeps him on the road — not the pride of the judge.

The verse also gives the believer the ground for compassion toward those who are outside the faith. If the journey's end of unbelief is as evil as the verse says, then the believer cannot be indifferent to the fate of those who walk it. The Prophet's mission was mercy: *"We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds"* (21:107). The believer's response to the verse — in an age when many people reject, mock, or ignore religion — should not be to write them off; it should be to want for them what the verse offers them: *self-examination* before the road ends. The *biʾsa al-maṣīr* is a cry from the heart of the Qur'an, and the believer's own heart should echo it with the same urgency: *"My Lord! If there is a road that leads to ruin, keep me from it; and if there is a road that leads to You, grant me the strength to walk it."*

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:11

> **No misfortune befalls, save by God's Leave. And whosoever believes in God, He guides his heart. And God is Knower of all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Doctrine of the Decree: "No Misfortune Except with God's Leave"**

The verse is one of the clearest statements in the Qur'an of the doctrine that nothing occurs except by God's will: *mā aṣāba min muṣībatin illā bi-idhni Allāh* — "No misfortune befalls, save by God's Leave." The word *muṣība* comes from *aṣāba*, "to befall, to hit, to reach." A *muṣība* is a blow, a hardship, a loss, an affliction. The verse says every such event is under divine permission (*idhn*), which in Qur'anic usage means "command, will, permiss'ion." The clause does not say God *creates* evil in the sense that He approves of it; it says nothing escapes His will. The commentators are careful here. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr explain that the *idhn* means that nothing can happen outside the divine decree — neither a good thing nor a bad thing, neither a blessing nor a trial. The same structure appears at 9:51: *"Say: 'Nothing will befall us except what God has written for us; He is our Protector'"*; at 57:22: *"No misfortune befalls in the earth or in your own selves, but it is in a Book before We bring it into being; truly that is easy for God"*; and at 6:17: *"If God touches you with harm, no one can remove it except Him; and if He touches you with good, He is Powerful over all things."*

The "leave" (*idhn*) is an essential qualification. It does not mean that God merely *permits* calamity in the sense of letting it happen against His will; it means that the calamity is within His sovereignty, decreed for a purpose, and never outside the reach of His power. The difference is analogous to a parent allowing a child to taste a bitter medicine: the allowance is not an absence of love; it is an instrument of love. Or, in a more precise analogy, it is like a driver whose engine can sometimes sputter — the sputter is within the design and within the oversight of the One who knows the road and the driver's destination. The verse's purpose is not to make God the "author of evil" in a moral sense; it is to make God the Lord of *every* event, so that nothing can happen to the believer without the believer being able to say: "This came from my Lord, and my Lord knows why."

**The Second Clause: The Guiding of the Heart**

The reward of faith is stated with a psychological precision that the commentators develop extensively: *wa man yuʾmin billāhi yahdi qalbahu* — "and whosoever believes in God, He guides his heart." The object of the guiding is the *heart*, not merely the mind. Ibn Kathīr says God gives the believer, at the moment of trial, the *illumination, patience, and certainty* that the trial requires. This is not a generic promise of "peace" as in the modern self-help sense; it is a specific operation: the heart that believes is *steered* by God in the midst of the storm. Al-Qurṭubī notes three aspects of the guiding: (1) God guides the heart to *accept* the decree — to say *qadr Allāh* (the decree of God) with satisfaction; (2) God guides the heart to *find the lesson* — to see in the misfortune a sign, a mercy, or a purification; and (3) God guides the heart to *return* — to turn to the source of help rather than to despair. The verse's structure is thus a divine promise: the believer who trusts God in his hardship is not promised the removal of the hardship, but the steering of his heart through it.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Amazing is the affair of the believer! Truly all of his affairs are good, and that is only for the believer: if good befalls him, he is grateful, and that is good for him; and if harm befalls him, he is patient, and that is good for him."* (Muslim 2999)

This is the practical commentary on the verse. The believer is not promised that life will be without hardship; he is promised that his response to hardship will be guided. The difference between the believer and the disbeliever in the same storm is not the storm; it is the *response*. The disbeliever sees the storm as evidence of an indifferent or hostile cosmos; the believer sees it as a letter from a Lord who knows how to turn his life into mercy. The verse is the Qur'an's answer to the problem of suffering: not that suffering never comes, but that, for the one who believes, suffering comes *guided* — the heart is steered toward gratitude, patience, acceptance, and growth.

**The Third Clause: "God Is Knower of All Things"**

The verse ends with *wa Allāhu bikulli shayʾin ʿalīm* — "and God is Knower of all things." Why is this added? Because the promise of guidance depends on knowledge. A physician can guide a patient only if he knows the disease; a teacher can guide a student only if he knows the difficulty. God's guidance of the believer's heart is not a blind force; it is a *personal* direction based on *complete* knowledge of the believer's condition, history, weakness, and potential. The classical commentators link this clause to the entire verse: God knows the misfortune before it happens, He knows the heart it will strike, He knows the amount of pressure the heart can bear, and He knows the exact guidance that heart needs. Nothing is left to chance. The Qur'an says the same at 6:59: *"And with Him are the keys of the unseen; no one knows them except Him. And He knows what is in the land and the sea; not a leaf falls but that He knows it, and not a grain in the darkness of the earth, nor anything green or dry, but that it is in a clear Book."* The *ʿalīm* is the guarantee that the guidance is not generic but specific.

**The Logical Coherence: Free Will, Decree, and Responsibility**

The verse raises one of the oldest questions in Islamic theology: how can God decree every event and still hold humans responsible? The classical answer, articulated by al-Ashʿarī, al-Māturīdī, Ibn Taymiyyah, and others, is that God's decree and human choice are not contradictory, because the human act is real and the divine decree is the ground of its reality. The Qur'an says: *"You do not will except that God wills"* (76:30; 81:29) — meaning that the human will is contained within the divine will, not cancelled by it. Ibn Taymiyyah's principle is the clearest: God creates the act, and the human being performs it; the act is *created* by God in the sense that its power and existence derive from Him, but it is *attributed* to the human being in the sense that it proceeds from his choice, intention, and action. The verse we are studying does not discuss the theoretical question in detail; it makes the *practical* point: the misfortune is decreed, but the response is a matter of faith. The believer who accepts the decree and trusts God is *guided*; the believer who protests the decree and distrusts God is *not*. The verse, in other words, is not a metaphysics of determinism; it is a psychology of trust.

The famous hadith of the Prophet ﷺ captures the believer's practical attitude:

> *"The believer's affair is amazing..."* (Muslim 2999, quoted above)

And the Prophet ﷺ gave the practical formula to Ibn ʿAbbās, telling him:

> *"Be mindful of God and He will protect you. Be mindful of God and you will find Him before you. If you have asked, then ask God; and if you have sought help, then seek help from God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516) The practical effect of the doctrine is visible in the very first generation of Muslims: when they were defeated at Uḥud, they did not blame the weather, the terrain, or the archers' mistake *alone*; they returned to God, seeking forgiveness and reinforcement. The verse's psychology is therefore not passivity; it is a *redefinition of control*. The person who believes that nothing happens outside God's will is freed from the exhausting task of controlling the uncontrollable; he is asked instead to control the controllable — his own response.

**The Modern Application: Suffering, Control, and the Heart**

The modern human being lives in an era of unprecedented control over many aspects of life — medicine, technology, communication — and yet remains helpless before death, disease, accident, loss, and meaninglessness. The verse offers a relief for this helplessness: not by pretending that the misfortune is not real, but by relocating the source of control. The person who says "Nothing happens outside God's leave" is not denying the pain; he is denying the pain's *meaninglessness*. This is precisely the point at which the verse meets modern psychology. Viktor Frankl, in his work on meaning in suffering, observed that the human being can endure almost any "how" if he has a "why." The Qur'an gives the "why" in the form of a *who*: the One who knows, the One who decrees, the One who guides. The believer who receives a diagnosis, a financial collapse, a betrayal, or a bereavement is not left with the bare fact; he is left with the fact *and* the One who holds it. This is what the verse means by "He guides his heart": the heart is not left to interpret the blow alone; it is given the interpretation.

There is also a social dimension. The verse says "no misfortune befalls, save by God's leave" — and the word *muṣība* is used in the Qur'an for *both* personal and communal calamities (2:156; 3:146; 4:62; 8:47; 57:22 — in 57:22 the same word refers to afflictions in the earth and in the selves). The believer who is struck by a community crisis — war, famine, epidemic, corporate corruption — is not to respond with panic, despair, or vengeance; he is to respond with the same *idhn*: the event is within God's decree, the heart is being guided, the purpose is hidden but real. The Qur'an says: *"And We will surely test you with something of fear, hunger, and loss of wealth, selves, and fruits; and give glad tidings to the patient"* (2:155) — followed immediately by the most famous formula of the sūrah's reply: *"Who, when a misfortune befalls them, say: 'Truly, we belong to God, and to Him we shall return'"* (2:156). The *istirjāʿ* is the linguistic embodiment of our verse: the heart announces, in the moment of loss, that the loss belongs to God, that the self belongs to God, and that the return is to God. The verse transforms the victim of circumstance into the participant of a covenant.

**The Final Word: The Verse as a Shelter**

The entire verse is, in one sentence, the doctrine of *tawakkul*: the reliance on God that makes trial possible. It has three parts, each necessary: (1) the *fact* of the decree (nothing escapes God's leave); (2) the *promise* of guidance (the believer's heart is steered); (3) the *ground* of the promise (God knows all). Remove any one and the system collapses. If nothing were decreed, the world would be chaos. If the heart were not guided, faith would be useless in suffering. If God did not know all, the guidance would be blind. The verse is thus not a fragment of theology but a complete shelter: the storm outside is real, but the believer is not standing in it without cover. The cover is the knowledge that the storm is *within* the plan of the One who loves him, and that the One who knows everything is guiding him through it. When the believer reads this verse in the middle of a trial, he is not reading a theory; he is reading a promise addressed to his own condition — and the promise is that his heart will not be left to drown in the darkness, because the One who made the heart knows exactly where it needs to be steered.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:12

> **Obey God and obey the Messenger. Then if you turn away—only the clear proclamation is incumbent upon Our Messenger.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Double Imperative: Obedience as the Shape of Faith**

The verse opens with two imperatives, each with its own object: *aṭīʿū Allāha wa aṭīʿū al-rasūl* — "Obey God and obey the Messenger." The structure is deliberate. There is one verb, two objects, and no conjunction between the two commands except the *wāw* of connection. The scholars observe that the Qur'an almost never says "obey God *and* obey the Messenger" in a way that treats them as two separate authorities; it treats as obedience to the Messenger a form of obedience to God. The proof is at 4:80: *"Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed God."* The reason is simple: the Messenger does not command from himself; he commands what he was commanded, and he forbids what was forbidden to him. Ibn Kathīr says that obedience to God is the obedience to the revelation, and obedience to the Messenger is the obedience to the demonstrated embodiment of that revelation. The two cannot be separated, and the Qur'an treats the joint command as a single test: *"Say: 'If you love God, then follow me, and God will love you and forgive you your sins'"* (3:31). The believer is not asked to obey a rulebook and a person; he is asked to obey a rulebook *through* a person — the one who showed the rulebook living.

The logic of this is profound. A book alone can be interpreted in an infinite number of ways: the person who reads it without a teacher will either distort it, or become a law unto himself, or despair of understanding it. The Prophetic demonstration is what makes the revelation *applicable*: the Qur'an says, *"And We have sent down to you the Reminder, so that you may make clear to mankind what has been sent down to them, and so that they may reflect"* (16:44). The command to obey both is therefore not a duplication; it is a *completion*. The verse also answers those who might claim the Qur'an is sufficient without the Prophet's teaching: the Qur'an itself commands obedience to the Prophet, and that command is in the Qur'an. The person who says "I follow the Qur'an but not the Prophet" is in a logical contradiction: he is following a book that commands him to follow a person.

**"Then If You Turn Away": The Conditional That Protects the Messenger**

The second half is a conditional: *fa-in tawallaytum* — "Then if you turn away." The particle *fa* connects the condition to the command: if you obey, good; if you turn away, then... The verse does not complete the conditional with a threat of punishment in the way of many other verses (e.g., 5:92: "Obey God and obey the Messenger, and beware; but if you turn away, know that upon Our Messenger is only the clear proclamation"). The wording of our verse is the *milder* form: *fa-innamā ʿalā rasūlinā al-balāghu al-mubīn* — "only the clear proclamation is incumbent upon Our Messenger." This is the doctrine of the *responsibility of the messenger*: his duty is *balāgh* — to deliver, to communicate, to proclaim — and it is *mubīn* — clear, evident, unmistakable. He is not responsible for the response, for the guidance of hearts, or for the repentance of sinners. That belongs to God.

There is a deeply comforting implication for the Prophet ﷺ himself, and by extension for every preacher of the truth. The Prophet was sent with a mission that was limited: *balāgh*, delivery. He was not sent to *compel*, to *control*, or to *convert* — those are God's acts. The Qur'an says in many places: *"You are not over them a compeller"* (50:45); *"Then if they turn away, We have not sent you over them as a guardian; upon you is only the delivery"* (42:48); *"If you turn away, upon the Messenger is only the clear delivery"* (29:18; 5:92; 24:54). The Prophet therefore did not carry the burden of the world's salvation on his own shoulders; he carried the burden of the *message*. When his uncle Abū Ṭālib died without embracing Islam, the Prophet ﷺ grieved, but the command was clear: he had delivered; the result was God's. When his own relatives and companions of his youth rejected the message, the same principle held: the message was delivered, and the turning away was their own.

This is not the same as indifference. The Prophet ﷺ wept and prayed for his people, and the Qur'an describes his concern: *"Perhaps you will destroy yourself with grief because they do not believe"* (26:3; 18:6). The balance is the heart of the prophetic character: *total* concern for the hearer, *no* ownership of the result. The modern preacher, the teacher, the parent, the daʿī (caller to Islam), and the person who shares his faith all need this balance. It is possible to be so anxious about the outcome that we distort the message, manipulate the hearer, or burn out. The verse's cure is to redefine success: success is the *clear delivery*, not the conversion. The seed is planted; the growth is God's: *"And God causes to grow whom He wills"* (2:261 — the image of the seed is used in the parable of the spenders).

**"Clear Proclamation": The Ethics of Clarity**

The phrase *al-balāgh al-mubīn* has an ethical dimension that is often overlooked. The delivery must be *clear*. The Prophet ﷺ did not obscure, conceal, compromise, or spin the message. The Qur'an says: *"And We have not sent you except as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner"* (25:56; 35:24), and the Prophet's own instruction: *"Convey from me, even if it be one verse"* (al-Bukhārī 3461). The message is not adjusted to the audience's preferences; it is delivered in its full clarity, and the audience's response is its own. This is the ethical foundation of all communication: the person who tells the truth selectively, who hides the difficult parts, who tailors the message to avoid offense, is not delivering *mubīn* (clear); he is delivering *mudgham* (muddled). The verse therefore commands the *complete* delivery of the truth, not a marketing version of it.

Al-Ṭabarī notes that *balāgh* in this context covers everything the Prophet brought: the Qur'an, the sunnah, the commands, the prohibitions, the glad tidings, and the warnings. Nothing was left unsaid. This is why the religion is called *complete*: *"Today I have perfected your religion for you, and completed My favor upon you, and have chosen for you Islam as a religion"* (5:3). The believer who studies the Prophet's life finds that he delivered everything — the difficult verses, the uncomfortable commands, the demands on wealth, the prohibitions of the pleasures — and left the response to God. This is the model for honest communication in every field: the doctor who tells the whole diagnosis, the teacher who explains the whole lesson, the journalist who reports the whole story, the friend who gives the whole advice. Clarity is not merely a rhetorical skill; it is a moral obligation.

**The Relationship Between Obedience and Trust (Link to Verse 13)**

The verse that follows immediately begins: *"God, there is no god but He, and in God let the believers trust"* (64:13). The two verses are a pair: obedience and *tawakkul* (trust). Why do they belong together? Because obedience that is not based on trust is only submission under duress, and trust that is not expressed in obedience is only a theory. The child who obeys his father only when he fears the punishment is not yet in the relationship; the child who obeys his father because he trusts his father's wisdom has entered it. The verse sequence teaches: first obey (the act), then trust (the orientation). The believer who obeys God out of fear alone can become a legalist; the believer who trusts God but does not obey can become a narcissist. The two together are the *whole*: obedience is the body of the relationship, and trust is its soul.

The practical applications are immediate. The believer obeys the command to pray when exhausted, because he trusts the One who commanded it. He obeys the command to give charity when poor, because he trusts the One who promised to replace it. He obeys the command to forgive when wronged, because he trusts the One who will judge the wrong. He obeys the command to speak the truth when it is dangerous, because he trusts the One who holds the outcome. Every act of obedience is therefore also an act of trust: it is the soul saying to God, "I have done what You asked, and I trust You with the result." This is why the sūrah moves from the double imperative to the single confession — the two are one motion: obedience looking upward, trust looking forward.

**The Practical Psychology of the Verse: From Rule to Relationship**

Modern people often experience religion as a set of rules, and the rules as burdens. The verse offers a way out of that experience: the rules are the expression of a relationship with the Messenger, and the Messenger is the expression of a relationship with God. The person who reads "obey God and obey the Messenger" as a legal requirement will find it heavy; the person who reads it as a *letter from a Father* will find it light. The Prophet ﷺ said: *"Religion is easy, and whoever overburdens himself in religion will be overcome by it"* (al-Bukhārī 39). The ease is not in the removal of the commands; it is in the *context* of the commands. When the slave knows that the Master is Merciful, Wise, and Trustworthy, obedience becomes the natural response of love, not the forced compliance of fear. This is the transformation the verse works: *aṭīʿū* (obey) is not a heavy command; it is an admission of the deepest human need — the need to be rightly guided, and the confidence that the Guide is the One who sent the Guide.

**The Final Word: The Messenger's Liberation and Our Own**

The verse closes with the *limit* of the Prophet's role, and that limit is the liberation of both him and us. The Prophet ﷺ is liberated from the impossible burden of making people believe; the believer is liberated from the impossible burden of converting people. The only burden anyone carries is the *delivery* — and that burden is light, because the message is clear, the evidence is in the world, and the One who judges is just. When the believer's loved one turns away after years of invitation, the believer can say, with the Prophet's own principle: the delivery was clear; the result belongs to God. When the believer's community rejects the truth, he can say: the *balāgh* has been delivered; the rest is the audit of the Day. This is not resignation; it is the peace of a person who knows his job description. The verse is therefore a relational statement, a theological statement, and a psychological release, all in one: the mission is to deliver, the message is clear, and the Judge is God.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:13

> **God, there is no god but He, and in God let the believers trust.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exceptionless Formula of Unity**

The verse opens with the first half of the testimony of faith in its most expansive form: *Allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa* — "God, there is no god but He." The Arabic is a *jumla ismiyya* (nominal sentence) — God, no god except Him — and the grammatical absence of a verb makes the statement timeless. It is not "was" and it is not "will be"; it is the permanent structure of reality. The *lā* of negation, combined with *illā* of exception, is the grammatical form known in Arabic logic as *al-ḥaṣr* (restriction): it negates every possible object of worship and affirms one alone. The formula is the engine of the whole religion: every other belief, every other worship, every other devotion is a description of *lā ilāha illā Allāh*. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr comment that the verse does not simply *inform*; it *establishes* the truth as the foundation on which the believer's trust can rest. The believer's *tawakkul* in the second half of the verse is only rational if the first half is true: I can trust the One who exists and is unique; I cannot trust a chain of lesser forces.

This is why the two halves of the verse are inseparable. *Lā ilāha illā huwa* — the object of trust is unique. *Wa ʿalā Allāhi fa-l-yatawakkal al-muʾminūn* — "and in God let the believers trust." The *fa* (then, therefore) at the start of the second clause connects the command to the confession: because God alone is God, therefore the believers rely on Him alone. The verse is thus a complete logical demonstration. If there were many gods, or no god, or a weak god, trust would be impossible or divided; but because there is One, trust can be total. The Qur'an says: *"God has not taken any son, nor is there any god with Him; if there were, each god would take away what he created and one would rise above another. Glorified be God above what they attribute!"* (23:91). Total trust requires a single object; monotheism is not merely the most true belief, it is the most *practical* one, because it makes whole-hearted reliance possible.

**Tawakkul: The Arabic Word and Its Depth**

The key word of the second half is *tawakkul*, from the root *w-k-l*, "to entrust, to rely, to delegate, to put one's affairs in another's hands." The form *tawakkala* means to *make the other one's managing of one's affairs one's own refuge*. Al-Rāzī and others note that *tawakkul* is not passivity: it is not sitting back and waiting for God to act while refusing to act oneself. The *mutawakkil* (the one who trusts) is the one who takes every permitted means, then *delegates the outcome* to God. The image is a traveler who checks the map, packs the supplies, and sets out — but knows that the road, the weather, the bandits, and the arrival are all in the hands of God. The Prophet ﷺ was asked: "Should I tie my camel and trust, or leave it untied and trust?" He replied: *"Tie your camel, then trust"* (al-Tirmidhī 2517 — reported from ʿAmr ibn Umayyah al-Ḍamrī, with a chain the scholars discussed, while the meaning is established by the principle of taking means). Trust does not replace action; it surrounds it. The person who prays and does not work is not trusting; he is lazy. The person who works and does not pray is not trusting; he is arrogant. The person who works and prays, and then leaves the result to God, is the *mutawakkil*.

The Qur'an commands *tawakkul* explicitly and repeatedly: *"And upon God let the believers rely"* (14:12); *"Those who believe and do righteous deeds — upon them will be no fear, nor will they grieve"* (2:277 — the same combination of deeds and trust); *"And whoever relies upon God — then He is sufficient for him"* (65:3); *"So rely upon God; truly God loves those who rely"* (3:159). The phrase in our verse is *wa ʿalā Allāhi fa-l-yatawakkal al-muʾminūn* — the *fa* places the trust *after* the confession, and the *al-muʾminūn* (the believers) is the subject. The command is directed at the believers specifically: they are the people for whom trust is possible, because they know the One in whom they trust.

**The Proof of Trust: The Known Character of the Trustee**

The rational foundation of *tawakkul* is the character of the One trusted. We can trust a person only insofar as we know he is capable and willing. The Qur'an's description of God provides both. He is *capable*: *qadīr* (able over all things, 64:1); *ʿalīm* (knowing all things, 64:4; 64:11); *khabīr* (aware, 64:8). He is *willing*: *raḥīm* (merciful, 64:14); *ghafūr* (forgiving, 64:14); *ḥamīd* (praised, 64:6); *shakūr* (thankful to the doers of good, 64:17). The believer's trust is therefore not a leap into the dark; it is a conclusion drawn from what God has already *demonstrated* in creation, revelation, and history. The Qur'an makes this explicit in the story of Abraham: *"And Who is it that gives you sustenance from the sky and the earth? Or Who is it that has power over hearing and sight?"* — then Abraham says: *"God; and indeed we are over His signs, ones who believe"* (27:23–24, in the sense of Abraham's reasoning at 26:76–80: *"He is Who feeds me and gives me drink, and when I am sick, He cures me, and Who causes me to die and then gives me life"*). Trust is the *response to the record*: the believer looks at the evidence of God's past faithfulness and concludes that the future is safe in His hands.

Ibn Taymiyyah taught that the believers' trust depends on *maʿrifa* (knowledge) of God: the more a person knows God's names and attributes, the more complete his trust. This is why the sūrah ends with the names *al-ʿAlīm al-Ḥakīm* (the Knower, the Wise) — knowledge and wisdom are the twin foundations of trust: God knows what to do and does it wisely. And the verse immediately before (v. 17) will add *al-Shakūr al-Ḥalīm* (the Thankful, the Clement): God rewards the good and is gentle with the failing. The whole sūrah's portrait of God — creator, sovereign, knower, forgiver, rewarder — is the *portrait of a trustee*: One who is fully able, fully aware, fully willing, and fully good. A person who trusts anyone less than this is trusting a lesser guarantee. The verse's logic is brutal but liberating: either the believer trusts God completely, or he trusts something weaker — the bank, the company, the plan, the friends, the luck — and everyone of those, sooner or later, fails.

**The Hadith of the Bird and the Standard of Trust**

The Prophet ﷺ gave a vivid image of *tawakkul*:

> *"Were you to rely upon God as He should be relied upon, He would provide for you as He provides for the birds: they go out in the morning with empty stomachs and return in the evening full."* (al-Tirmidhī 2344; Ibn Mājah 4164)

The image contains both halves of the doctrine. The birds *go out* — they act; the birds *return full* — they are provided for. They do not sit in the nest waiting for food to fall in, nor do they exhaust themselves in anxiety about the supply. The *mutawakkil* lives in the same rhythm: work is the means, God is the Provider, and the outcome is the divine responsibility. Al-Rāzī comments that the hadith does not mean the believer should abandon means; it means the believer should *not fear the means failing*, because the Provider controls both the means and the outcome.

Modern studies of anxiety have shown that a large fraction of human distress is spent on events that never occur, or on outcomes that are beyond our control. The verse cures this by *redefining the frontier of control*: the believer is responsible for his effort, his intention, his obedience, and his attitude — everything else is delegated. This is why the Qur'an says: *"And whoever relies upon God, He is sufficient for him"* (65:3) — *sufficient* means: He will be enough; you will not need anything else. The person who has given his affairs to God is not a person with no worries; he is a person who has *transferred* his worries to the One who can carry them. This is the psychological secret of the verse: anxiety is the wage of imagined self-sufficiency, and *tawakkul* is the cancellation of that wage.

**The Social Dimension: Trust as the Foundation of the Faith Community**

There is a social dimension of *tawakkul* that is often missed. The believers are commanded to trust *in God* — not in each other, not in leaders, not in institutions, not in wealth. This is what makes the Muslim community spiritually *decentralized*: no human being is the object of final reliance. If the community trusted in a leader, the leader's failure would destroy the community's faith; if it trusted in wealth, a depression would destroy its hope; if it trusted in the state, the state's fall would annihilate it. By trusting in God, the community is immune to the collapse of any human instrument. The Qur'an says: *"And trust in the Ever-Living Who does not die"* (25:58). The historical proof is visible: the Muslim community has survived the fall of empires, the destruction of cities, the loss of political power, the erasure of dynasties, and the displacement of peoples — because its trust is in the One who does not die. A community that trusts in God can lose everything and lose nothing; a community that trusts in anything else can lose everything and lose everything.

The practical expression of this trust is in the way believers *handle failure and success* alike: success with gratitude, failure with patience, uncertainty with prayer, and decision with *istikāra* (asking God for the best outcome) and *tawakkul*. The Prophet ﷺ taught the believer to begin every important action with seeking God's choice, and then to proceed with trust: *"If one of you intends a matter, let him pray two rakʿahs, then say: 'O God, I seek Your choice through Your knowledge, and I seek Your power through Your strength...'"* (al-Bukhārī 1162). The verse is the metaphysical frame of that prayer: the one who seeks the choice is the one who trusts the Chooser.

**The Final Word: The Verse as the Whole Religion in One Sentence**

This single verse — *"God, there is no god but He, and in God let the believers trust"* — contains the whole of what the Qur'an calls the religion: one God, one trust. Everything before it in the sūrah (creation, knowledge, history, resurrection, command to believe, decree, obedience) has been a *preparation* for this confession; everything after it (family, wealth, reverence, spending, the loan to God, the names of God) is an *application* of it. The believer who says *lā ilāha illā Allāh* with a believing heart has already accepted the rest; the believer who trusts in God alone has already learned to walk the rest. The verse is therefore not a slogan to be memorized but a principle to be *lived*: the believer lives each day as if there is no god but God — which means he fears nothing, relies on nothing, and hopes in nothing besides Him. And in that state, there is neither fear nor grief: *"Truly those who say, 'Our Lord is God,' and then stand upright, the angels descend upon them: 'Fear not, nor grieve; receive glad tidings of the Garden which you were promised'"* (41:30).

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:14

> **O you who believe! Among your spouses and your children there is indeed an enemy unto you; so be wary of them. Yet if you pardon, forbear, and forgive, then truly God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Intimate Battlefield: Family as Trial**

The verse is one of the most astonishing in the Qur'an, because it addresses the believer about the people he loves most: *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū inna min azwājikum wa awlādikum ʿaduwwan lakum* — "O you who believe! Among your spouses and your children there is indeed an enemy unto you." The word *min* (among) is a partitive: not *all* spouses and children are enemies, but *some* of them are. The word *ʿaduww* (enemy) is a strong term, and the commentators are careful to explain that the "enmity" is not the enmity of a foreign war; it is the enmity of a *temptation* — the family member who, by his or her attitude, speech, love, or pressure, turns the believer away from God, from obedience, from migration, from charity, from truth, or from prayer. The Qur'an says the same about wealth in the next verse, and the pairing makes the point: the love of family and the love of possessions are the two great competitors of the love of God. The direct hadith that the commentators cite in connection with this family trial is the Prophet's own definition of the people who will be bound in the Hereafter by what they "bound" in this world — and his other words make the principle exact:

> *"No one of you truly believes until I am dearer to him than his father, his child, and all mankind."* (al-Bukhārī 15; Muslim 44)

This is the standard by which the "enmity" of the family is measured: if the family's wishes are dearer to the believer than the command of the Messenger, the family has become an obstruction. And the Prophet ﷺ observed what the family and wealth a person loves actually count for in the end:

> *"The servant says: 'My wealth, my wealth!' But his wealth is only three things: what he ate and consumed, what he wore and wore out, and what he gave in charity and kept for himself. What is beyond that vanishes, and he leaves it to the people."* (Muslim 2959)

**The Historical Context: The Men Who Stayed Behind**

The asbāb al-nuzūl (occasions of revelation) for this verse are among the most moving in the Qur'anic literature. Before the emigration (hijrah) to Madinah, many Muslims in Makkah had embraced Islam but were prevented from emigrating — or delayed emigrating — because of their families. Some were held back by the tears of their wives; some by the pleading of their children; some by the concern that their parents would be left alone; some by the fear that their property would be lost. Their families were, in the functional sense, their *enemies*: they were using the bonds of love to pull the men away from what God had commanded. When the Muslims who had emigrated met them, or when the Prophet ﷺ saw the reason for their delay, this verse was revealed: be aware that your families may stand between you and God; do not let their love be the cause of your neglect of the religion. Then, in the second half of the verse, God immediately softened the command: *"Yet if you pardon, forbear, and forgive, then truly God is Forgiving, Merciful"* — so that even those who had delayed were not to be punished by their own consciences or by the community, if they now repented and made right.

The commentators mention that some Muslims had taken pity on their families and did not emigrate, or delayed; and this verse was revealed to instruct them to be on their guard against family members who did not support them in their religion, but also not to punish them for it. The point of the historical story is the *priority of obedience*: the believer's love for family must never become the *reason* for his disobedience. The Prophet ﷺ himself lived this: he left Makkah, leaving family and property behind, because the command of God came first. He taught: *"No one of you truly believes until I am dearer to him than his father, his child, and all mankind"* (al-Bukhārī 15; Muslim 44). The enmity of the family is not its essence but its *effect*: when the family's wishes conflict with God's command, the family has become a test of the believer's loyalty.

**The Two Kinds of Enmity: Obstruction and Diverting**

There are two ways a family can be an "enemy" to a believer, and the verse covers both. The first is *obstruction*: actively preventing the believer from obeying God — the wife who mocks his prayer, the child who ridicules his fast, the husband who forbids his wife's hijab, the parent who blocks the child's study of the religion, the family that refuses to let a convert practice his faith. The Qur'an reports this from the earlier nations: *"And they say: 'If we had heard or had reason, we would not have been among the people of the Blazing Fire'"* (67:10 — the people who followed their fathers); *"And they have taken their learned ones and monks as lords besides God"* (9:31 — the ones who were diverted by religious authorities); and the most explicit: *"O you who believe! Truly among your spouses and your children there is an enemy unto you"* — the verse itself names it. The second is *diverting*: not obstructing but *distracting* — the family's love, needs, parties, demands, schedules, and anxieties that consume the believer's energy until there is no strength left for God. The Qur'an calls the family's *love* a trial: *"And know that your wealth and your children are a trial"* (8:28). It is possible to have a family that never says a word against religion and nevertheless *diverts* a man from it — simply by the sheer weight of its demands on his time, his money, and his attention.

This is why the verse says "be wary of them" — *fa-ḥdharūhum*. The command is not "hate them" or "leave them" or "fight them"; it is *be wary*, *be on guard*, *don't let them take you*. The word *ḥidhr* is the same family as *ḥadhara*, "to be cautious, to make preparations against a danger." It is the awareness of a traveler on a dangerous road: he does not hate the road, he does not abandon the journey, but he keeps his eyes open. The believer's family is not an enemy to be fought; it is a test to be managed. The Prophet ﷺ put it with a perfect balance:

> *"A woman, a treasure, and a child — each is a trial. But a righteous wife, a blessed child, and lawful wealth are of the happiness of this world."* (in the meaning of the hadith: *"The world is provision, and the best provision is a righteous woman"* — Muslim 1467)

**The Threefold Remedy: Pardon, Forbearance, Forgiveness**

The second half of the verse is the remedy, and it is given in a triple structure: *wa in taʿfū* (if you pardon), *wa taṣfaḥū* (and forbear), *wa taghfirū* (and forgive). The three words are not synonyms; they are stages of mercy. *ʿAfw* is the *erasure*: to pardon means to remove the offense from the account, to treat it as if it were not there. *Ṣafḥ* is the *turning away*: to forbear means to overlook, to not pursue, to leave the offender alone. *Ghafr* (maghfirah) is the *covering*: to forgive means to shield the offender from the consequences and from exposure. Together they form a complete treatment of an offense: erase it, ignore it, cover it. The verse says "if you do this" — the if is a command in the form of a condition, because God is presenting the behavior of the believer toward his family as the mirror of God's behavior toward him: *"Then truly God is Forgiving, Merciful"* — the reward of forgiveness is forgiveness.

There is a psychological genius in this triple structure. When a family member has obstructed or diverted the believer, the natural responses are anger, resentment, revenge, and withdrawal. All of these damage both the family and the believer. The verse offers a path that *protects the believer's obedience* without *breaking the family's bond*: he does not turn against them, he does not hold a grudge, he does not expose them, he does not withdraw his love — he is wary, he obeys God, he pardons them. This is the highest moral achievement: the believer's family may be an enemy to his religion, but they remain his family, and his treatment of them is *mercy*. The Prophet ﷺ's own life is the model: his wife ʿĀʾishah disagreed with him; his uncle Abū Lahab opposed him; his son-in-law's family tested him; the people of Makkah, many of whom were his own relatives, persecuted him — and he never stopped praying for them, feeding them, and treating them with justice.

**The Verse as a Warning About Modern Family Culture**

The modern family is the center of an enormous amount of spiritual pressure. Muslim families today face a novel situation: the *default culture of the society* often contradicts the religion, and the *family* itself has absorbed that culture. A Muslim child may be told by a parent that there is no need to pray five times a day, that the hijab is too restrictive, that charity is a waste, that the fasting is a health risk, that Muslim marriage customs are outdated, or that religion should be kept private. A Muslim spouse may be asked by his wife or husband to compromise on a religious practice for social convenience. A Muslim parent may be pressured by children to allow what God has forbidden. The verse addresses all of these with one command: *be wary*. The believer is not required to leave his family, to hate his family, or to treat his family as enemies; he is required to *recognize the source of the pressure* and to *keep his obedience to God above the pressure of his home*. The Qur'an has a verse that states the absolute priority: *"But if they strive to make you ascribe unto Me that of which you have no knowledge, then do not obey them"* (31:15 — the verse about parents who demand shirk). And the Prophet ﷺ said: *"There is no obedience to a creature in the disobedience of the Creator"* (al-Ḥākim; Ahmad with a sound chain, reported in the sense of the famous hadith of ʿAlī and the command to obey in family matters only within the decree).

**The Practical Psychology: How to Be Wary Without Becoming Bitter**

The application of the verse requires emotional intelligence. The believer who is wary of his family must not become paranoid, suspicious, or cold; the *ḥidhr* is a *spiritual* wariness, not a social withdrawal. The practical steps are: (1) *Set the religious boundaries* — the believer decides, in his own heart, what he will not compromise, and he does not let the family's pressure cross that line. (2) *Keep love separate from obedience* — the believer can love his family deeply while disobeying their un-Islamic demands; love is not the same as submission. (3) *Use patience and wisdom* — the family's obstruction is often not malice; it is ignorance, fear, custom, or habit. The Prophet's command to the believers who faced families in the way of religion was to call gently, to be patient, and to leave the hearts to God. (4) *Forgive repeatedly* — the verse's triple structure is a promise that forgiveness is possible repeatedly; the believer who has been wronged by family ten times, and pardons ten times, is not weak; he is the one who is most like God's own treatment of him.

The ultimate reason for the command is the *unity of the believer's heart*. The Qur'an says: *"God has not made for a man two hearts in his chest"* (33:4). A person who is divided between the love of God and the love of family that opposes God is living with a split heart. The verse's purpose is to unify the heart: love God first, love the family *within* that love, and let the family's pressure never crack the foundation. When the believer does this, the family's "enmity" is transformed: the family that was an obstruction becomes, for the believer who manages it well, a *source of reward* — for the patience, the forgiveness, and the loyalty he maintained in the face of its pressure. The verse closes with the divine mirror: *fa-inn Allāha ghafūrun raḥīm* — "then truly God is Forgiving, Merciful." The believer who forgives his family is forgiven by God; the believer who covers the family's offense is covered by God; and the believer who perseveres in love despite the trial finds that the family that was an enemy has become, in the end, a witness to his faith.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:15

> **Your property and your children are only a trial; and God—with Him is a great reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Great Tests: Wealth and Children**

The verse names the two things the human being most desires and most fears to lose: *innamā amwālukum wa awlādukum fitnatun* — "Your property and your children are only a trial." *Innamā* is a particle of restriction (*ḥaṣr*): they are *only* a trial; nothing more. The word *fitnah* is one of the richest in the Qur'an: it originally means "to test, to try, to purify by fire." A *fātin* is a goldsmith who puts gold in the fire to separate the pure from the alloy; a *fitnah* is the fire that reveals what is inside. The verse says: your wealth and your children are the *fire* in which your true loyalties are revealed. They reveal whether you love the gift more than the Giver, whether you obey God when the gift is at stake, and whether you would trade the Hereafter for the pleasure of the present. The same word is used of the fire of punishment at 51:14 — *"taste your fitnah, this is what you used to hasten."* The test fire reveals the metal, and the metal is the heart.

Why are wealth and children the *most effective* tests? Because they are the two things that most fully bind the human heart to the world. A person can survive without money; but the possibility of more money, the security of a fortune, the pleasure of spending, and the social status it brings are extraordinarily powerful magnets. Children are even more powerful: they are the continuation of a person's existence, the source of his deepest love, his hopes for the future, his pride, and his sense of immortality. The Qur'an says: *"Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world"* (18:46) — *zīnah*, ornament. Ornaments are not the building; they are the decoration. And the verse continues: *"but the enduring good works are better with your Lord for reward and better in hope."* The *bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt* (enduring good works) are the permanent currency; wealth and children are the temporary decoration. The Prophet ﷺ made the point with brutal clarity:

> *"Wealth is not the abundance of goods; rather, wealth is the wealth of the soul."* (al-Bukhārī 6446; Muslim 1051 — the hadith of Abū Hurayrah)

**The Hadith of the Slave of Dinars and Dirhams**

The source cites the famous hadith of the "slave" of worldly goods, and it deserves full quotation:

> *"Wretched is the slave of the dīnār; wretched is the slave of the dirham; wretched is the slave of the khamīṣah (fine garment); wretched is the slave of the khāmīlah (fine cloth). If he is given, he is content; and if he is not given, he is displeased. Wretched and doomed! And if he is pricked with a thorn, may it not be removed for him."* (al-Bukhārī 6435)

The language is deliberately shocking. A person who loves wealth and possessions more than God is called a *slave* — not of God, but of *things*. The word *ʿabd* (slave) normally belongs to the vocabulary of worship: the believer is *ʿabd Allāh*, the servant of God. The Prophet's point is that a person's heart will be enslaved by *something*: either by God or by a thing. The person who needs the dīnār more than he needs God has become the dīnār's slave; the person who needs God more than anything is free. This is the *fitnah* in action: the wealth reveals who the master really is. The hadith also describes the psychology of the slave: *"if he is given, he is content; and if he is not given, he is displeased"* — his mood, his peace, and his worth are determined entirely by the output of the world. Such a person's heart is not his own; it is the property of his possessions.

**How Wealth Tests: The Five Scenarios**

The trial of wealth operates in many directions, and the Qur'an names them. (1) *The trial of acquisition*: Will the person earn lawfully? The Qur'an says: *"O you who believe! Eat of the good things We have provided for you"* (2:172) — and forbids the unlawful means: *"Do not consume your wealth among yourselves in falsehood"* (2:188). The person who loves money will cheat, bribe, exploit, and gamble to obtain it; the person whose master is God will refuse, even at a loss. (2) *The trial of retention*: Will the person pay the alms and charity that the wealth demands? The Qur'an says: *"And those in whose wealth is a known right for the beggar and the deprived"* (70:24–25). The wealth that is withheld from the poor is not a possession; it is a *liability* on the Day of Judgment. (3) *The trial of spending*: Will the person spend on what God loves, or on what the world loves? The Qur'an describes the two: *"Your wealth and your children are only a trial..."* — and the person who spends for God's sake is described as a trader with the greatest profit. (4) *The trial of security*: Will the person trust the wealth or the Provider? The Qur'an says: *"Who will give God a goodly loan so that He may multiply it for him many times over?"* (2:245) — the person who trusts his bank account will panic; the person who trusts God will lend. (5) *The trial of boasting*: Will the person use wealth to dominate, or to serve? The Qur'an describes the *fitnah* of boasting: *"Know that the life of this world is only play and amusement...and competition in wealth and children"* (57:20). The wealth is a test of whether the person uses his advantage for God or for his own ego.

**How Children Test: The Five Scenarios**

Children are a *fitnah* in even deeper ways. (1) *The trial of distraction*: A parent's attention, time, and love can be so completely absorbed by the children that God gets the leftovers. The Qur'an warns: *"O you who believe! Let not your wealth and your children divert you from the remembrance of God; whoever does that, they are the losers"* (63:9) — the immediately preceding sūrah's warning, which our verse repeats. (2) *The trial of preference*: A parent may be tempted to do something unlawful for the sake of a child — the bribe, the false testimony, the unfair advantage, the cover-up. (3) *The trial of grief*: The death, illness, or failure of a child can crush the parent's faith if the parent's entire sense of meaning rested in the child; or it can refine the parent's faith if the parent knows the child belongs to God. (4) *The trial of the child's conduct*: A child who abandons the religion, adopts harmful habits, or causes harm to others is a *fitnah* for the parent: will the parent respond with harshness, despair, or prayer? The Prophet's own life included this trial: his beloved uncle Abū Ṭālib died in unbelief, and the Prophet ﷺ grieved, but his faith was not shaken. (5) *The trial of the child's needs vs. the religion's needs*: The parent's duty is to provide for the child, but the *fitnah* arises when the parent worships the provision — when the boy's soccer schedule becomes the family's lord, when the daughter's "opportunities" become the family's idol, when the education system's values replace the revelation's values.

The Qur'an gives the perfect formula for the parent: *"O you who believe! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire"* (66:6). The *protection* of the family is the real task; the *provision* for the family is only part of it. The *fitnah* is the confusion of the two — thinking that a child is "well-provided" if he has food and money, while his soul has no food. Ibn Kathīr, commenting on 66:6, says the verse means: teach your families and command them to obey God. The modern parent who spends hours on the child's grades, sports, and social calendar but has no time for the child's Qur'an, prayer, and character development has passed the *fitnah* of the family — not in the positive way, but in the negative way: the child has become the idol, and the idol has paid nothing.

**"And God — with Him Is a Great Reward"**

The final clause is the release: *wa Allāhu ʿindahu ajrun ʿaẓīm* — "and God — with Him is a great reward." The structure has a rhetorical surprise: the *wa* connects the trial to the compensation. The verse says "your wealth and children are a trial" — but then immediately: "God has the great reward." The implication is that the believer who *passes* the trial — who does not let the wealth enslave him and does not let the children divert him — will receive what the trial was designed to produce: the *ajr ʿaẓīm*. The *fitnah* is not a punishment; it is a *production line*. The fire of trial produces the gold of reward. The Qur'an says: *"And We will surely test you with something of fear, hunger, loss of wealth, selves, and fruits; and give glad tidings to the patient"* (2:155); *"Truly, the patient will be given their reward without measure"* (39:10). The reward is *ʿaẓīm* (great) because it is the reward for the hardest kind of obedience — the obedience that goes *against* the strongest desires, the love of money, and the love of children.

This is where the sūrah's market language returns in a brilliant reversal. In verse 9, the Day of Mutual Dispossession was the day of *taghābun* — the day the transaction is finalized. In this verse, the *trial* is the *transaction*: the believer gives up the wealth (by treating it as a trust, not a possession), and God gives him the great reward. In the world's market, wealth buys comfort, status, and security; in God's market, *giving up* the wealth (in charity, in obedience, in detachment) buys the *ajr ʿaẓīm*. The verse is therefore not a warning against having wealth or children — the Qur'an never says that; it says the opposite: *"Property and wealth are the adornment of this world"* (18:46) — they are *given* as ornaments. The warning is against *being owned by them*: *"Wretched is the slave of the dīnār"* — the wealth becomes the master only when the heart becomes its slave.

**The Practical Psychology: How to Hold the World Loosely**

The modern believer lives in a world that specializes in manufacturing *fitnah*: advertising, credit, social comparison, the "lifestyle" industry, the education market, the parenting industry, and the endless consumption of images of other people's wealth and children. The verse's counsel is to *hold* the world loosely. The classical writers on the heart describe three levels: (1) *holding* — using wealth and children as trusts from God, without forgetting the Owner; (2) *loosening* — being able to give them, lose them, or leave them for God's sake without the heart breaking; (3) *surrender* — recognizing that everything belongs to God, and that the believer is only a custodian. The Qur'an gives the practical method: *"And seek, through what God has given you, the abode of the Hereafter; and do not forget your share of this world"* (28:77). The verse is the perfect balance: the "share of this world" is real; the "abode of the Hereafter" is the priority. The believer who lives this balance is not an ascetic who hates the world, nor a materialist who worships it — he is a *steward* who rides the world as a rider rides a horse: using it, directing it, and never being carried away by it.

There is also a communal application: the Muslim community is often tested by its wealth and its children more than by its enemies. A community that becomes wealthy — as many Muslim communities have today — faces a subtle danger: its mosques become architectural monuments, its charities become bureaucracy, its children's education becomes a prestige competition, and its identity becomes a consumer brand. The verse says these things are *fitnah*: they are the fire in which the community's actual faith is revealed. The community that passes the fire keeps the wealth as an instrument and the children as a trust; the community that fails the fire becomes a slave to both. The Prophet's own family lived the simplest life among the world's nobility that ever existed — not because poverty is the ideal, but because the *freedom from the fitnah* is the ideal. The verse's final word is the same: *fa-inn Allāhu ʿindahu ajrun ʿaẓīm* — the reward is with God, the trial is with the world, and the believer's task is to choose the Rewarder over the trial.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:16

> **So reverence God as much as you are able. Listen, obey, and spend; that is better for your souls. And whosoever is shielded from the avarice of his soul, it is they who shall prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command of Reverence Within Capacity**

The verse opens with one of the most practical commands in the entire Qur'an: *fa-ttaqū Allāha mā-istaṭaʿtum* — "So reverence God as much as you are able." The root *w-q-y* means "to protect, to guard, to preserve"; *taqwā* is the state of guarding oneself — against sin, against error, against the fire. The verse's phrase *mā-istaṭaʿtum* ("as much as you are able") is a *qualifier of capacity*: God commands the maximum *possible*, not the maximum *imaginable*. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī both note that this is the most precise formulation of the duty of *taqwā*: it is not a vague ideal; it is a measured obligation, and the measure is the individual's *actual capacity*. The Prophet ﷺ said: *"The most beloved of deeds to God are those done most consistently, even if they are small"* (al-Bukhārī 6464; Muslim 783), and the Qur'an says the same at 2:286: *"God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity."* The three statements together set the standard: consistent effort within real capacity, not heroic bursts of self-reproach.

There is an important classical discussion here. Some commentators held that this verse was later abrogated or superseded by *"O you who believe! Reverence God as He should be reverenced"* (3:102) — the higher, unconditional command. Al-Qurṭubī records this view and the response to it. The better position is that the two verses are complementary, not contradictory: 3:102 establishes the *ideal* of complete reverence, and 64:16 provides the *practical* measure for the imperfect believer. The first is the target; the second is the realistic route to it. This is the pattern of the whole Sharia: the Qur'an sets the ideal (*iḥsān*) and provides the framework for the imperfect (*ʿadl*, Justice, and the *rukhas*, concessions for the incapable). The modern believer should understand the verse as the *anti-discouragement* verse: because God knows human weakness, He does not require what a person cannot do; He requires the sincere maximum. And the *mā-istaṭaʿtum* is not a license for mediocrity; it is a *command to climb the ladder*: the person who can do more must do more, and the person who cannot do all must do what he can. The hadith of the Prophet ﷺ makes this exact:

> *"When I command you with something, do of it what you are able; and when I forbid you something, abstain from it."* (al-Bukhārī 7288; Muslim 1337)

**Listen, Obey, Spend: The Three Verbs of the Covenant**

The verse then gives three commands: *wa ismaʿū wa aṭīʿū wa anfiqū* — "Listen, obey, and spend." The order is essential and the commentators develop it. *Listening* (*samʿ*) is the reception of the command — the willingness to hear, to understand, to study. *Obedience* (*ṭāʿa*) is the application of the command — the decision to act upon what was heard. *Spending* (*infāq*) is the sacrifice for the command — the giving of what one holds, the release of the self from the possession. The sequence is the psychology of the believer's response to revelation: first *understand*, then *submit*, then *give*. A person who neither understands nor submits cannot give. A person who understands but does not submit cannot give meaningfully. A person who understands and submits but does not give remains incomplete — because the final test of the tongue is the *hand*: does the faith flow out of the pocket?

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note that *listen* is placed first because *taqwā* begins with *knowledge*: *"only those of His servants who have knowledge fear God"* (35:28). The believer cannot revere what he does not know. This is why the religion commands study, memorization, contemplation, and *ʿilm* (knowledge): the *samʿ* is the gate. *Obedience* is the counterpart: knowledge without obedience becomes pride (the Qur'an says of the scholar who does not act: *"The likeness of those who are charged with the Torah, then do not carry it, is the likeness of a donkey carrying books"* — 62:5). And *spending* is the proof: obedience without sacrifice becomes self-sufficiency. The three verbs are the full anatomy of the believer: ear, will, and hand. The Qur'an's own definition of the successful believer combines all three: *"Truly successful are the believers, those who are humble in their prayer"* (23:1–2) — knowledge, worship, and wealth all in one.

**The Same Triad in the Sūrah's Twin Chapter**

The same pattern appears in the twin sūrah of this chapter, Sūrah al-Jumuʿah (62): *"O you who believe! When the call to prayer is announced on Friday, hasten to the remembrance of God and leave off trade... And when the prayer is finished, then disperse in the land and seek the bounty of God"* (62:9–10) — listen (the call), obey (the prayer), spend (the trade on the other days, and the charitable part of the bounty). And in the final verses of Sūrah al-Baqarah, the same triad appears as the categories of the believers' prayer: *"Our Lord, we have heard the caller calling to faith"* (3:193), *"so obey"* and *"spend"* — the very words of 3:193. The Qur'an is consistent: the covenant of faith has three verbs and the sūrah is now modeling them for the last time.

**"That Is Better for Your Souls": The Logical Reason**

The verse says: *dhālika khayrun li-anfusikum* — "that is better for your souls." Why is obedience better for the *soul*? The clause answers the objection that religion is a burden: the commands are not a tax on the soul; they are the *nutrition* of the soul. The classical explanation is that the soul was created for God, and it flourishes only in proximity to God. Just as the body needs food, water, and movement, the soul needs *dhikr*, obedience, and sacrifice. The person who withholds from God does not enrich himself; he depletes himself. The Qur'an says: *"Whoever does righteousness, it is for his own soul; and whoever does evil, it is against it"* (41:46); *"Whoever is grateful, it is for his own soul"* (31:12); *"And whoever purifies his soul has succeeded, and whoever corrupts it has failed"* (91:9–10). The commands are not *duties owed to a needy God*; they are *medicines prescribed for a needy soul*. This is why the Qur'an can say "better for your souls" — the benefit is intrinsic, not merely extrinsic.

The psychological principle is that *purpose* is the medicine of the soul. A person without purpose, without obedience, without sacrifice, drifts into the emptiness that modern psychology calls *anomie* — meaninglessness, boredom, anxiety, addiction. The believer who has God as the object of his life has a purpose that no economic condition can remove. The *infāq* (spending) is particularly therapeutic: studies of generosity show that giving produces measurable happiness, reduces depression, and creates social connection — the *ajr* (reward) is not only in the Hereafter; the *joy* is in the present. The verse's "better for your souls" is therefore not a theological abstraction; it is an observation about how the human machine actually works.

**The Avarice of the Soul: The Deepest Inner Enemy**

The final clause is the peak of the verse and one of the most psychologically penetrating statements of the Qur'an: *wa man yūqa shuḥḥa nafsihi fa-ulāʾika hum al-mufliḥūn* — "And whosoever is shielded from the avarice of his soul, it is they who shall prosper." *Shuḥḥ* is from *sh-ḥ-ḥ*, meaning "stinginess, miserliness, avarice, the refusal to give." The phrase *shuḥḥ al-nafs* is usually translated "the avarice of the soul" — not merely the reluctance to spend money, but the deeper *orientation*: the soul's fundamental belief that if it gives, it will be diminished; that what it holds is its security; that others' gain is its loss. *Shuḥḥ* is the *existential* stinginess, the *ontological* miserliness — the soul's refusal to spend because it believes its supply is finite and its survival depends on hoarding. The Qur'an says this is the root of all the vices of possession: *"And whoever is protected from the avarice of his soul, it is they who are the successful"* (59:9 — the verse of the Anṣār about the migrants, which is the direct parallel and the source of this verse's famous phrase).

The phrase *yūqa* ("is shielded") is in the passive: the believer does not *shield himself* alone; he is *shielded* by God. The success of *not being miserly* is a divine protection, not a human achievement. This is one of the most important correctives in the Qur'an: the person who is generous is not simply a "generous person"; he is a person whose miserly soul has been *guarded* by God. The believer's prayer should therefore include this: *"O God, protect me from the avarice of my soul."* The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Beware of avarice, for it destroyed those before you: it commanded them to be stingy, and they were stingy; it commanded them to sever (family ties), and they severed them; and it commanded them to commit immorality, and they committed it."* (Abū Dāwūd 1698)

The hadith is even more directly relevant to our verse because it comes from the tradition of the war on *shuḥḥ*. The early commentators, including Ibn ʿAbbās as reported by al-Ṭabarī, explain the clause as the meaning of the verse itself: the successful one is he who is saved from the avarice of his own soul.

**Why Avarice Is the Root of the Family Trial (Link to vv. 14–15)**

The sequence of the sūrah is now fully visible. Verse 14: families can be enemies. Verse 15: wealth and children are a trial. Verse 16: the shield against the trial is the protection from the soul's avarice. The connection is this: a person who is *guarded from shuḥḥ* will not let his children become a trial, because he will not let his love for them become the *hoarding* of them. He will give them what they need, including the discipline, the example, and the *freedom to obey God*, even when it costs him their approval. A person who is *guarded from shuḥḥ* will not let his wealth become a trial, because he will not cling to it; he will spend it, share it, and release it. The *fitnah* of family and wealth is ultimately a *fitnah of the soul's grip*: the more the soul holds, the more it is held. The only freedom lies in the *yūqa* — the divine protection from the grip.

**The Modern Application: The Economy of the Shielded Soul**

The modern world is an engine of *shuḥḥ*. It teaches that security is hoarding, that worth is accumulation, that generosity is weakness, and that the future is only safe if the present is squeezed. The verse's answer is the *inverse economy*: security is generosity; worth is *giving*; generosity is the imitation of God; and the future is safe because the Owner of the future is the One who orders the giving. The believer who gives charity in a financial crisis, who helps a stranger while saving nothing in the bank of the world, who spends on his children's faith while the world calls it a waste — that believer is not "losing"; he is being *shielded* from the avarice that would consume him. The Qur'an says: *"Whatever you spend, He will replace it"* (34:39); the Prophet ﷺ said: *"Charity does not decrease wealth"* (Muslim 2588). The two statements are the same: giving is not subtraction; it is *investment in the shield*.

The verse closes with the word *mufliḥūn* — "they who prosper, they who succeed." The *iflāḥ* (prosperity) is the same word used in the famous opening of Sūrah al-Muʾminūn: *"Truly successful are the believers"* (23:1). The believer's *prosperity* is not the accumulation of the world; it is the state of the soul that has been freed from the grip. The verse is the practical syllabus of that prosperity: reverence within capacity, listen, obey, spend, and be shielded from the soul's avarice. It is not a program of asceticism; it is a program of *freedom and health*. The person who lives it will find that the world he feared to release was the very thing that was holding him — and the world he released for God was the very thing God was using to make him prosper.

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:17

> **If you lend unto God a goodly loan, He will multiply it for you and forgive you; and God is Thankful, Clement.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grammar of the Divine Loan: The Boldest Metaphor in the Sūrah**

The verse opens with the most astonishing metaphor in the sūrah: *in tuqriḍū Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan* — "If you lend unto God a goodly loan." The word *qarḍ* comes from *q-r-ḍ*, which in Arabic means "to cut." A *qarḍ* is, etymologically, a piece cut off from one's wealth and given to another. The metaphor is breathtaking: the believer gives a piece of what he owns, and the *recipient* is God. The human being is asked to lend to the One who needs nothing, who owns everything, and who cannot be repaid. This is not a literal loan; it is the Qur'an's way of transforming the believer's giving into a *transaction with the Owner of the universe*. The classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī — explain that the metaphor is a *teaching device*: God is not borrowing in the sense of need; He is *crediting* in the sense of attribution. The believer gives to the poor, to the mosque, to the orphan, to the traveler, and God says: *that was a loan to Me*. The recipient is the agent; the credit is the Lord's.

The *qarḍ ḥasan* (goodly loan) is not merely any gift. The Qur'an defines its quality: it is given *from what is loved* (3:92: *"You will never attain righteousness until you give from what you love"*); it is given *secretly and openly* (2:274); it is given *without reminder or injury* (2:262–264); it is given *for God's sake alone* (2:272); and it is given *with a good heart* — *ḥasan* — not reluctantly, not grudgingly, not as a transaction of pride, but as an act of trust. The *ḥusn* (goodness) is in the *manner* and the *motive*: the donor does not say "I am lending," as if God owed him; he says "I am *giving*," and God calls it a loan because the return is guaranteed. The Prophet ﷺ described the multiplication in another authentic report:

> *"The Most Merciful takes charity in His right hand and grows it for its owner as one of you grows a foal, until it becomes like a mountain."* (al-Bukhārī 1410; Muslim 1014)

The Qur'an itself states the terms of the loan in its sister verse: *"Who is it that will lend God a goodly loan, so that He may multiply it for him many times over? And it is God Who withholds and God Who extends, and to Him you will be returned"* (2:245). Ibn Kathīr notes that God calls the believer's charity a *loan* to emphasize that the return is guaranteed by the Borrower, and that the goodliness of the loan — sincerity, eagerness, and lawful wealth — is what makes it worthy of the multiplication.

**The Multiplication: The Inescapable Economics of Mercy**

The verse says: *yuḍāʿifhu lakum* — "He will multiply it for you." The verb is from *ḍ-ʿ-f*, "to double, to multiply." The Qur'an is explicit about the scale: *"Who is it that will lend God a goodly loan, so that He may multiply it for him many times over?"* (2:245) — and the multiplication is described in the parable: *"The parable of those who spend their wealth in the way of God is that of a grain that grows seven ears, in every ear a hundred grains. And God multiplies for whom He wills"* (2:261). One grain becomes seven hundred; and "God multiplies for whom He wills" means the multiplication is not limited to seven hundred — it is determined by the *māshāʾ Allāh* of the Owner. The commentators observe that the multiplication is not a *mathematical* claim about a fixed rate; it is a *promise* that the value of the deed is determined by the One who weighs it, and His scales are mercy. The Prophet ﷺ received the assurance:

> *"Charity will not decrease wealth."* (Muslim 2588)

> *"Whoever gives charity from lawful earnings, for it is only the lawful earnings that are accepted, the Most Merciful receives it in His right hand and grows it for its owner as one of you grows a foal, until it becomes like a mountain."* (al-Bukhārī 1410; Muslim 1014)

The *growth* is not merely the reward in the Hereafter; it is also the *increase* of the donor's blessings in this world: the Qur'an says *"And whatever you spend, He will replace it"* (34:39), and the Hadith of the Prophet ﷺ says: *"Every day two angels descend: one says, 'O God, give the spender a replacement,' and the other says, 'O God, give the hoarder a loss'"* (al-Bukhārī 1442; Muslim 1010). The economics of the divine market are the reverse of the human market: the human market says "give and you will have less"; the divine market says "give and you will have more."

**The Forgiveness: The Loan That Also Clears the Debt**

The verse adds the unexpected second clause: *wa yaghfir lakum* — "and forgive you." The loan to God does not only produce *profit*; it produces *settlement* — the forgiveness of the debts that the believer already owes. This is the profoundest point of the verse: the believer's giving is not only an investment; it is a *repayment of his past sins*. The classical tradition describes charity as a *kaffārah* (expiation): *"Charity extinguishes sins as water extinguishes fire"* (al-Tirmidhī 2616). The Qur'an says: *"If you give alms openly, it is good; and if you conceal it and give it to the poor, that is better for you, and it will expiate for you some of your evil deeds"* (2:271). And the hadith of the Prophet ﷺ: *"No one's charity will cause his wealth to decrease"* (Muslim 2588) — the decrease is the *erasure* of the sins, the increase is the *accumulation* of the reward. The goodly loan is therefore a two-sided transaction: it multiplies the *assets* and it *clears the liabilities*. The Day of Mutual Dispossession (v. 9) is the day of the final balance sheet, and the goodly loan is the way the believer ensures his sheet shows profit.

**"God Is Thankful": The Name That Startles the Reader**

The final clause of the verse is the most original: *wa Allāhu shakūrun ḥalīm* — "and God is Thankful, Clement." *Shakūr* is the intensive form of *shākir* — "the One who acknowledges and rewards." The commentators pause here: how can God be *thankful*? Human thankfulness is a response to a *benefit received*, and God needs nothing. Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr give the answer: God's "gratitude" is *the reward of the deed* — the acknowledgment of the doer's act, the enhancement of its value, and the attribution of credit to the one who performed it. The human being's act, however small, is *recognized* by God; it is not lost, not ignored, not absorbed into the divine sea. Ibn Kathīr cites the famous hadith of the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"The reward of the one who spends is multiplied..."* — and in the same sense: *"Thankful"* is God's own name at 35:30: *"Truly He will pay them in full their wages and increase them of His bounty; truly He is Forgiving, Thankful"* (35:30).

The *shakūr* is the name that makes the believer's *smallness* the *reason* for his *hope*: the person who gives a single date in charity — the smallest coin of the human economy — is told that God *acknowledges* it, records it, multiplies it, and will return it at a scale beyond imagination. The psychological effect of *shakūr* is the *death of insignificance*: no act is too small, no gift too little, no effort too humble for the One who is Thankful. The Prophet ﷺ said: *"Guard yourself from the Fire even with half a date; and if you cannot find it, then with a good word"* (al-Bukhārī 1417; Muslim 1016). The *shakūr* guarantees that even the half-date is *registered*.

**"Clement": The Name That Softens the Believer's Fear**

The final name is *ḥalīm* — "Clement, Forbearing." The *ḥalīm* is the One who does not hasten to punish, who holds the punishment while the sinner repents, who bears with the offender while the offense is repeated, and who responds to the wrongdoer's delay with the opportunity of return. The Qur'an says: *"If God were to seize people for their injustice, He would not leave upon the earth a single creature"* (16:61), and the *ḥalīm* is the reason the world continues. The combination *shakūr ḥalīm* is a perfect summary of the believer's situation: God *acknowledges* the good immediately and *defers* the punishment of the evil; He is *generous in reward* and *gentle in judgment*. The Prophet ﷺ described this in the hadith of the exchange of the two names:

> *"God is not ashamed of the truth..."* — in the classical citation the point of the verse is: *"God is Thankful and Clement — He forgives while He is able to punish, and He rewards while He is not obliged to reward."

**The Logic of the Verse: Why the Loan Is Better Than the Hoard**

The verse's reason is finally clear, and it is almost a mathematical theorem: the believer can hold his wealth in the *hand of the world* or in the *hand of God*. The hand of the world returns interest at a *finite* rate, and the *principal* is always at risk (the market crashes, the bank fails, the inheritance is contested, the health fails). The hand of God returns at an *infinite* rate, and the *principal* is guaranteed by the Owner of everything: *"Who is it that will lend God a goodly loan, so that He may multiply it for him many times over? And it is God Who withholds and God Who extends, and to Him you will be returned"* (2:245). The logic is the same as the logic of all investment: the return is proportionate to the *security* of the borrower. The believer who lends to God is lending to the only Borrower whose bankruptcy is impossible and whose interest never stops. The Quran's phrase *qarḍ ḥasan* is therefore not a metaphor of *poverty* ("God needs your money"); it is a metaphor of *opportunity*: God is offering the believer the highest-yield account in the universe, and the only requirement is that the deposit be *ḥasan* — good, genuine, sincere.

**The Modern Application: The Loan in the Age of Investment**

The modern reader lives in a world saturated with the language of investment: portfolios, returns, diversification, compounding. The verse uses the same language and *rewrites the rules*. The believer's "portfolio" includes the mosque, the orphan, the widowed, the starving, the student, the convert, the prisoner, and the stranger. The "returns" are not measured in the quarterly report; they are measured in the record of the One who is *shakūr* — the One who acknowledges even the smallest deposit. The verse does not tell the believer to abandon the world's economy; it tells him to *diversify into the permanent one*. The person who gives when he can afford it is prudent; the person who gives when he cannot afford it is *trusting* — and it is the trusting one who is the model of the verse. The Prophet ﷺ began the sūrah's lesson in the preceding verse: *"Spend; that is better for your souls"* — and the believer who spends is not *losing*; he is *lending to the Only Banker who never fails, at the only rate that never stops, in the only currency that is never devalued — and the interest is a house in the Garden, a veil over his sins, and the pleasure of the One who is Thankful and Clement.*

## Sūrah al-Taghābun 64:18

> **Knower of the Unseen and the seen, the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Seal**

The final verse of the sūrah is a single sentence of divine names: *ʿĀlimu al-ghaybi wa al-shahādah, al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm* — "Knower of the Unseen and the seen, the Mighty, the Wise." The verse contains no command and no warning; it is a *seal* placed over the entire chapter. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note that these three names — *ʿAlīm al-ghayb wa al-shahāda*, *al-ʿAzīz*, *al-Ḥakīm* — appear together at the end of several major sūrahs (6:73; 13:9; 32:6; 59:22; 62:8). They are the *summary of the divine character* that the sūrah has been describing. Verse 4 said God knows what is in the heavens and the earth and what is in the breasts; verse 11 said God is Knower of all things; verse 17 said God is Thankful and Clement. The final verse gathers these attributes into the three names that make the entire sūrah coherent: the *Unseen and seen* (knowledge), *the Mighty* (power), *the Wise* (purpose).

**"Knower of the Unseen and the Seen": The Two Realms**

*Al-ghayb* is the unseen — that which is hidden from the senses: the future, the soul, the angels, the Hereafter, the interior of the heart, the secrets of the universe beyond perception. *Al-shahādah* is the seen — that which is present, observable, experienced: the world, the body, the events, the public acts. The Qur'an says: *"He knows the unseen and the seen; He is the Wise, the Aware"* (6:73); *"Knower of the unseen and the seen; exalted is He above what they associate"* (23:92); *"Knower of the unseen and the seen, the Mighty, the Wise — that is the One Who created the heavens and the earth"* (13:9); *"He is God; there is no god but He; Knower of the unseen and the seen; He is the Compassionate, the Merciful"* (59:22). The pairing of the two realms is not a partition but a *union*: God knows both with the same perfect knowledge, and the barrier between them — the barrier that limits human knowledge — is no barrier for Him.

The philosophical point is profound. The human being knows only the *seen*; the *unseen* is unknowable to him except as it is revealed. The Qur'an says: *"Knower of the unseen; He does not disclose His unseen to anyone except a messenger whom He has chosen"* (72:26–27). The knowledge of the ghayb belongs to God alone, and the *shahādah* is the window through which the believer receives fragments of it — the revelation of the Prophet ﷺ, the signs of creation, the promises of the Hereafter. The phrase "Knower of the Unseen and the seen" is therefore both a *warning* and a *comfort*: the warning is that nothing the believer hides from people is hidden from God; the comfort is that nothing the believer fears in the unseen future is unknown to God. The sūrah's entire message — belief in the resurrection, trust in the decree, confidence in the reward — rests on this name: the One who *tells* you about the Day of Gathering *knows* it, and He knows it not as a future prediction but as a present reality. For God, the "Day of Mutual Dispossession" is not ahead; it is *in His knowledge always*.

**"The Mighty": The Name That Secures the Promise**

*Al-ʿAzīz* — "the Mighty" — comes from *ʿ-z-z*, which in Arabic combines strength, honor, and supremacy. The *ʿazīz* is the one whose power cannot be resisted, whose honor cannot be diminished, and whose supremacy cannot be challenged. In the Qur'an, the name appears constantly at the moments of divine *action*: *"And God is Mighty, Wise"* is the formula that concludes the verses about judgement, reward, and punishment. The *ʿazīz* is the guarantee of the sūrah's promises: the resurrection (v. 7) is certain because the Mighty will do it; the reward (v. 9) is secure because the Mighty will preserve it; the punishment (v. 10) is unavoidable because the Mighty will execute it; the guidance (v. 11) is effective because the Mighty will steer it. The disbelievers who said "No human being can guide us" and "We will never be resurrected" were not merely *wrong*; they were *challenging the Mighty*. The sūrah's answer to their challenge is the name: *al-ʿAzīz*.

The *ʿazīz* also has a moral dimension. In Arabic, *ʿizz* is not merely power; it is *honor* — the state of being held in esteem. The name says: God's power cannot be taken, and God's honor cannot be bought. The believer who lives under the *ʿAzīz* does not need the world's honor: *"Whoever desires honor, then to God belongs all honor"* (35:10). This is the sūrah's answer to the disbelievers' pride (64:6, "Shall a human being guide us?") — they sought honor in the rejection of the human messenger, but all honor belongs to the One who is *ʿAzīz*. The believer who is humiliated for his faith is not humiliated; he is standing under the honor of the Mighty.

**"The Wise": The Name That Vindicates the Trial**

*Al-Ḥakīm* — "the Wise" — comes from *ḥ-k-m*, the root of both "judgment" and "wisdom": the *ḥakīm* is the one who judges rightly because he knows the truth, and who acts rightly because he knows the purpose. The name is the theological anchor of the sūrah's most difficult contents: the trial of wealth and children (v. 15), the decree of misfortune (v. 11), and the Day of Mutual Dispossession (v. 9). How can a loving God allow the believer's child to be an enemy, the believer's wealth to be a trial, and the believer's life to be struck by misfortune? The answer is *al-Ḥakīm*: the One who permits these things knows the purpose of them. The *ḥikmah* (wisdom) of the trial is the *refinement* of faith; the *ḥikmah* of the misfortune is the *redirecting* of the heart; the *ḥikmah* of the Day is the *final vindication* of justice. The Qur'an says: *"Your Lord creates and chooses whatever He wills; the choice is not theirs. Glorified be God, and exalted is He above what they associate"* (28:68), and repeatedly closes its arguments with the same two names: *"And God is the Mighty, the Wise"* (e.g., 3:27; 14:4; 30:27; 59:24). The *Ḥakīm* is the name that turns the believer's 'why' into 'who': the question "Why did this happen?" is answered by "The One Who made it happen is the Wise."

Ibn Taymiyyah taught the principle that God's *ḥikmah* is real, not nominal: God does not do things without purpose, and the purpose is always just, merciful, and good. This is the key to the sūrah's calm. A world without a Wise Creator is a world of chance, and in a world of chance there is no comfort; the sūrah's God is *ḥakīm*, and His wisdom is the *comfort* of the believer who cannot see the purpose. The believer's response to the hidden wisdom is the *trust* commanded in verse 13: *"in God let the believers trust."* The names of the final verse are therefore not an appendix; they are the summary of the *why* behind the whole sūrah: the Knower knows the plan, the Mighty executes it, and the Wise has ordered it.

**The Names as the Sūrah's Logic in One Sentence**

The whole sūrah can now be read in the light of its final verse. The sūrah's argument was: (1) God created and knows everything (vv. 1–4); (2) the nations that rejected Him were destroyed (vv. 5–6); (3) the resurrection is sworn and certain (v. 7); (4) the Day of Mutual Dispossession will settle the accounts (vv. 9–10); (5) trust in God and obey His Messenger (vv. 12–13); (6) beware of family and wealth (vv. 14–15); (7) revere, listen, obey, spend (v. 16); (8) lend God a goodly loan and He will multiply and forgive (v. 17). The final verse says: *the One who told you all of this is the Knower of the Unseen and the seen (so the report is true), the Mighty (so the promise is secure), and the Wise (so the trial is purposeful).* The entire sūrah is thus *encased* in three names, and the believer who has absorbed the sūrah has absorbed the names' meaning: God knows what is hidden from him, God is able to do what He has promised, and God is wise in what He has permitted.

**The Final Application: How to Live with the Seal**

The sūrah ends, and the reader is left with the seal. The practical guidance is: (1) *Live under the gaze of the Knower* — since the unseen is known to God, the believer's most private fears, unspoken prayers, and hidden intentions are not lost. He can trust the One who knows the future with his future. (2) *Live under the power of the Mighty* — since God is *ʿAzīz*, the believer's enemies, obstacles, and anxieties are all under the power of the One he trusts. No force can stand against the Mighty, and the forces that threaten the believer are, in the final analysis, subordinate to him. (3) *Live under the purpose of the Wise* — since God is *Ḥakīm*, the believer's trials are not random. The wealth he lost, the child who tested him, the misfortune that befell him — all of it is inside a purpose that the Wise One knows and the believer trusts.

This is the achievement of the sūrah: it began with the glorification of God by all creation and ended with the names that make the glorification *meaningful*. The creation glorifies the One who knows all, can do all, and orders all for wisdom. The sūrah's last word is not a command; it is a *description* — and the description is the reason for the commands. The believer who closes the sūrah with *ʿĀlim al-ghayb wa al-shahādah, al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm* is not closing a book; he is entering a relationship with the One described. The final names are the destination of the *taghābun*: the believer who "lost" his sins and "gained" the Garden has not gained a place; he has gained *nearness* to the One who is Knower, Mighty, Wise — and that nearness is the meaning of the great triumph.
