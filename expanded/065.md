# Sūrah al-Ṭalāq (Chapter 65) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter That Turns a Painful Permission into a Path of Taqwā**

Sūrah al-Ṭalāq, "Divorce," is a Madinan chapter of twelve verses that takes its name from the legal and moral architecture it lays down for the dissolution of marriage. It is also known, in the reports gathered by al-Qurṭubī and others, as *Sūrat al-Nisāʾ al-Qusrā*, "the Shorter Sūrah Regarding Women," set in deliberate juxtaposition to Sūrah 4, *al-Nisāʾ*, "Women." The pairing is not ornamental. Al-Nisāʾ legislates the rights of women across inheritance, marriage, guardianship, and communal protection; al-Ṭalāq legislates the rights of women at the precise moment those rights are most easily forgotten — the moment a household is coming apart. A community that can keep justice when affection has died has learned something that no wedding sermon can teach.

The sūrah belongs to the Madinan period, when the first Muslim polity was no longer a persecuted handful but a functioning city with households, property, children, and courts. Makkan revelation had concentrated on God, the soul, and the Hereafter. Madinan revelation had to teach a people how to live together when living together failed. Divorce had existed in pre-Islamic Arabia, often as an instrument of male caprice: a man could dismiss a wife, reclaim her, dismiss her again, and so trap her in a cycle of harm without release. The Qur'an had already begun to dismantle that abuse in Sūrah al-Baqarah (2:226–237). This sūrah returns to the same subject not because the earlier legislation was incomplete in principle, but because principle without procedure is easily betrayed. Al-Ṭalāq supplies the missing precision: when the waiting period begins, how it is counted, where the woman lives during it, who pays, what pregnancy and nursing require, and — most searching of all — what God does for the man or woman who observes these limits when observing them is costly.

**Why a Whole Chapter After al-Baqarah Already Legislated Divorce**

A reader who has studied 2:226–237 may ask why the Book returns to divorce at all. The answer is both legal and spiritual. Al-Baqarah established the skeleton of the law: the waiting period of three courses (2:228); the husband's right of honorable retention or honorable release (2:229, 231); the prohibition of taking women back in order to harm them (2:231); the finality of a third divorce (2:230); the nursing of children (2:233); and the waiting period of the widow (2:234). What al-Baqarah did not fully specify is the waiting period of women who no longer menstruate, of those who have not yet menstruated, and of those who are pregnant. Those three cases are the explicit occasion of 65:4, as the report of Ubayy ibn Kaʿb preserved by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī attests. Procedure is not a lesser form of revelation. A right that cannot be counted cannot be enforced; a waiting period that cannot be identified cannot protect anyone.

There is a second reason, and it is the sūrah's true burden. Al-Baqarah legislated divorce inside a long chapter of many subjects. Al-Ṭalāq isolates the subject and then, from verse 8 onward, surrounds it with the fate of nations that "insolently defied the Command of their Lord and His messengers." The juxtaposition is the argument. Household law is not a private courtesy. It is among the *ḥudūd Allāh*, the limits set by God, and a people that treats those limits as optional is walking the same road as the ruined towns. Inheritance law in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ received the same surrounding (4:13–14). Whenever the Qur'an grants women rights that pre-Islamic custom found unprecedented, it immediately reminds the community that these rights are not social fashion but divine boundary.

**The Most Hated of What Is Permitted**

Islam does not forbid divorce. It places it, uniquely, among the permitted things that God most dislikes. A well-known ḥadīth, transmitted from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar and recorded by Abū Dāwūd (2178) and Ibn Mājah (2018), states:

> *"The most hated of permissible things to God is divorce."*

The hadith critics have discussed the strength of its chain — some grading it weak, others accepting it as ḥasan in light of supporting evidence — but the meaning is independently confirmed by the Qur'an's own tone. Divorce is permitted because some marriages become a harm that piety itself cannot repair. It is disliked because the household is the first school of mercy, and its rupture, even when lawful, is a grief. The Prophet ﷺ himself was given permission, in 33:28–29, to offer his wives a choice between the world and God — a permission that included the possibility of releasing them — and the reports about Ḥafṣah, discussed at verse 1, show that the law of this sūrah touched even his own household. Permission without dislike becomes casual. Dislike without permission becomes cruelty. The sūrah holds both.

**Two Movements in Twelve Verses**

The chapter divides with unusual clarity. Verses 1–7 are legislation: the waiting period, the house, the witnesses, the provision, the special cases of age and pregnancy, the nursing wage, and the rule that spending follows means. Verses 8–12 are admonition: ruined towns, a severe reckoning, a reminder sent down, a Messenger who brings people out of darkness into light, and a closing cosmology in which the Command that governs a divorce also governs the seven heavens and the seven earths. The movement from the bedroom to the cosmos is not a change of subject. It is the sūrah's central claim: the God who measures a waiting period in days is the same God who measures galaxies, and the man who thinks a household injustice is too small for heaven to notice has not understood either.

What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary. Each verse is treated as a complete, independent discourse — as the verse-by-verse method demands — drawing upon the Arabic language, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and others), the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, and the living reasoning that connects this Madinan legislation to the conditions of the modern human being.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:1

> **O Prophet! When you divorce your wives, divorce them for the waiting period and count well the waiting period, and reverence your Lord. Expel them not from their houses; nor shall they depart, unless they commit a flagrant indecency. These are the limits set by God, and whosoever transgresses the limits set by God has surely wronged himself. Thou knowest not: perhaps God will bring something new to pass thereafter.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command Spoken to One Man and Meant for Every Household**

The sūrah opens with *Yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu* — "O Prophet" — the second-person singular of direct address. Al-Jalālayn, following the mass of commentators, notes that the vocative names the Prophet ﷺ while the ruling is for the entire community. The same double signification appears elsewhere:

> *"Whatever good befalls you is from God, and whatever evil befalls you is from yourself. And We have sent you to mankind as a messenger."* (Qur'an 4:79)

A law that begins in the Prophet's own house cannot later be dismissed as a private courtesy for lesser men. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī preserve the report that the Prophet ﷺ divorced Ḥafṣah bint ʿUmar, and that this verse — or the instruction surrounding it — was connected to that event. It was said to him:

> *"Take her back, for she is one who fasts often and stands often in night vigil, and she is one of your wives in the Garden."* (transmitted by Abū Dāwūd 2283, al-Nasāʾī, and Ibn Mājah 2016; cited by al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Wāḥidī)

The report is a quiet revolution in the ethics of divorce. The reason given for taking her back is not convenience, not politics, not the alliance with ʿUmar. It is her worship. Character, not heat of the moment, is to govern the most irreversible of household decisions. Even the Messenger of God ﷺ is shown receiving the law rather than standing above it.

**What "Divorce Them for the Waiting Period" Actually Requires**

*Fa-ṭalliqūhunna li-ʿiddatihinna* — "divorce them for their waiting period" — is the sūrah's first legal hinge. The particle *li-* can mean "for" or "in accordance with" the waiting period, and it can also be read, as some of the grammarians and jurists noted, as "during" the waiting period. Both readings feed one ruling. A man may not fling the word of divorce into a moment of menstruation, nor into a period of purity in which he has had intercourse, and then call the utterance a completed legal act. The waiting period — *ʿiddah*, from *ʿadda*, to count — is not a formality attached after the fact. It is the frame inside which a valid divorce is pronounced.

The *ʿiddah* of the menstruating woman is the three courses already legislated in al-Baqarah:

> *"Divorced women shall wait by themselves for three courses."* (Qur'an 2:228)

If the marriage was never consummated, no waiting period is imposed at all:

> *"O you who believe! If you marry believing women and then divorce them before you have touched them, there shall be no waiting period for you to reckon against them. But provide for them and release them in a fair manner."* (Qur'an 33:49)

The contrast is precise. Consummation creates a bond that time itself must unwind. Non-consummation creates a contract that can be released at once, with provision and fairness, but without the medical and social counting that pregnancy and lineage require. The waiting period exists, among other reasons, to establish whether a child is on the way, to give anger a chance to cool, and to keep lineage unconfused. A law that ignored those three facts would be a law that preferred male speech to biological and social reality.

**The Incident of ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar and the Prophetic Correction**

The most famous lived commentary on this verse is the case of ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar. He divorced his wife while she was menstruating. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb reported the matter to the Prophet ﷺ, who was displeased, and who gave an instruction that the collections of al-Bukhārī and Muslim have preserved with care. In the wording cited by al-Qurṭubī in connection with this verse, the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Tell him to take her back and keep her until she is clean, then to wait until she gets her next period and becomes clean again, whereupon, if he wishes to keep her, he can do so. And if he wishes to divorce her, he can divorce her before having sexual intercourse with her; that is the prescribed waiting period that God has fixed for the women one intends to divorce."* (al-Bukhārī 5251; Muslim 1471; cited by al-Qurṭubī)

Several teachings sit inside a single correction. First, a divorce pronounced in menstruation is not allowed to stand as the intended legal act; the woman is taken back. Second, the next valid window is a period of purity in which no intercourse has occurred — so that the divorce, if it comes, is not entangled with desire, conception, or confusion about the count. Third, the Prophet ﷺ explicitly identifies this procedure as *the* waiting period God has fixed. The sunnah here is not a pious extra. It is the verse read aloud in a living house.

The psychological wisdom is as sharp as the legal one. Anger spikes during tension; menstruation is already a time of physical and emotional strain; a man who is permitted to divorce in that hour is a man invited to confuse irritability with decision. The law removes the match from the dry grass. Taqwā, in the analogy the instructions themselves recommend, is like driving a dangerous road in a storm: awareness does not make the road shorter, but it prevents the crash that haste makes inevitable.

**"Count Well the Waiting Period" — Why Precision Is a Form of Reverence**

The verse does not stop at commanding the divorce. It adds *wa-aḥṣū al-ʿiddata* — "and count well the waiting period." *Aḥṣū* is from *ḥaṣā*, pebble-counting, the old Semitic image of tallying one stone at a time so that nothing is lost. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī note that the command to count is addressed, in the first instance, to the husband, because the legal consequences — resumption, finality, housing, and maintenance — run on his calendar. But the woman is not a passive object of someone else's arithmetic. She knows her own body, and the later jurists made her report of her courses a primary evidence. Counting well is a shared honesty.

Why does revelation care about a calendar? Because an uncounted waiting period becomes a weapon. A man who "forgets" the days can reclaim a woman at the last hour in order to start the harm over again — the very abuse 2:231 forbids. A man who pretends the period is over can expel her before her right to the house has expired. A woman who conceals a course can confuse lineage. The command to count is therefore a command to refuse self-serving fog. In a modern register: keep a record, tell the truth about dates, do not let emotion rewrite the clock. Reverence for God and accurate bookkeeping are here the same act.

**Houses That Remain Hers During the Count**

*Lā tukhrijūhunna min buyūtihinna wa-lā yakhrujna* — "Expel them not from their houses, nor shall they depart." Two prohibitions, two directions of the same justice. The husband may not drive her out. She may not leave. Al-Qurṭubī reads the first as the wife's remaining right over the husband, and the second as the husband's remaining right over the wife. They are no longer to engage in conjugal relations, but they are not yet strangers. The house — *buyūtihinna*, "their houses," with the feminine possessive — is named as hers for the duration, even if the title deed is his. Language itself assigns her a dwelling-right the man's anger is not allowed to revoke.

The parallel in the Prophet's own household is 33:33, where the wives of the Prophet ﷺ are told to remain in their houses. The principle generalizes. A woman in her waiting period is not a guest to be dismissed and not a prisoner to be humiliated. She is a person whose legal status is in suspension, and suspension requires a stable place. Modern analogues are grimly easy to find: the partner locked out with a changed key the night of the argument; the woman sent back to her family before any count has begun, so that reconciliation becomes geographically impossible. The verse treats that pattern as a transgression of God's limits, not as a private housing choice.

**Flagrant Indecency Is Not a Suspicion**

The exception is tightly drawn: *illā an yaʾtīna bi-fāḥishatin mubayyinah* — "unless they commit a flagrant indecency." *Fāḥishah* is a grave indecency, typically adultery or a public lewdness that cannot be doubted. *Mubayyinah* means clear, evident, made plain. Al-Rāzī insists that a woman cannot be expelled on the basis of a suspicion or an unsubstantiated accusation. Charges of infidelity in the Qur'an require four trustworthy witnesses:

> *"As for those of your women who commit an indecency, call four among you to bear witness against them."* (Qur'an 4:15)

The same evidentiary wall stands here. A husband in the bitterness of divorce is the last person whose unaided accusation should empty a house. The law refuses to let jealousy wear the mask of piety. If the indecency is proven in the way the Book itself requires, the housing protection lapses because the woman has herself broken the terms of the remaining bond. If it is not proven, the house remains hers. The difference between those two sentences is the difference between a legal order and a vendetta.

**The Limits Set by God, and Who Is Harmed by Crossing Them**

*Tilka ḥudūdu Allāh* — "These are the limits set by God." *Ḥudūd* are boundaries, the edges of a territory one does not cross. The Qur'an uses the same phrase after the inheritance verses (4:13) and after the fasting and divorce verses of al-Baqarah (2:187, 2:229–230). Household law is thereby placed in the same category as the great public statutes. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both emphasize that the one who transgresses these limits *ẓalama nafsahu* — "has surely wronged himself." The victim of an unjust divorce-procedure is obvious: the woman expelled, the child confused, the lineage clouded. The verse names a less obvious victim: the transgressor. He has not merely harmed another. He has placed himself outside the boundary inside which God protects him.

The logic is the logic of every wise limit. A speed limit does not exist to impoverish the driver; it exists because the road will punish the man who treats it as optional, and the punishment will not pause to ask whether he meant well. Taqwā is careful driving. The man who expels his wife to "teach her a lesson," or who divorces her in her menses because he cannot bear another night, has not displayed strength. He has displayed the inability to stay inside a clearly marked lane. The harm returns upon him as legal consequence in this world and as account in the next.

**"Perhaps God Will Bring Something New to Pass"**

The verse ends with a sentence that is not law but hope: *lā tadrī laʿalla Allāha yuḥdithu baʿda dhālika amran* — "Thou knowest not: perhaps God will bring something new to pass thereafter." Al-Jalālayn and al-Qurṭubī explain the "something new" as the possible reconciliation of hearts during the waiting period after a first or second divorce, before the third makes the separation final:

> *"If he divorces her, she is not lawful to him thereafter until she marries a husband other than him."* (Qur'an 2:230)

The waiting period is therefore not only a medical and legal device. It is a divinely mandated pause in which God Himself may act upon two hearts that, an hour earlier, could not stand each other. Human beings are bad prophets of their own future feelings. The man who is sure tonight that he is done may not be sure in three weeks. The woman who is sure she will never forgive may find, in the quiet of remaining under the same roof without the pressure of intimacy, that the grievance was smaller than the life they built. The verse does not command reconciliation. It forbids the haste that makes reconciliation impossible.

This is why the house must not be emptied and the count must not be faked. Empty the house, and you empty the very space in which "something new" could happen. Falsify the count, and you steal from God the time He asked for. The modern pattern of instant rupture — pack a bag, change the locks, announce the end on the same night as the quarrel — is efficient. It is also, by this verse's measure, a refusal to let God work. The believer who keeps the waiting period is not being naive. He is admitting that he does not know his own tomorrow, and that the Knower of the unseen may yet write a different sentence than the one anger drafted tonight.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:2

> **So when they have fulfilled their term, take them back in an honorable way or separate from them in an honorable way. And call two just persons among yourselves to witness and uphold the testimony for God. By this is exhorted whosoever believes in God and the Last Day. And whosoever reverences God, He will appoint a way out for him,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Honorable Exits, and No Third**

The waiting period of verse 1 now reaches its end, and the husband is given exactly two doors, both of them marked *bi-maʿrūf* — "in an honorable way," according to what is known to be right. *Fa-amsikūhunna bi-maʿrūfin aw fāriqūhunna bi-maʿrūfin*: take them back honorably, or separate from them honorably. The sentence is the twin of 2:231:

> *"And when you divorce women and they have fulfilled their term, either retain them honorably or release them honorably. Retain them not to cause harm so as to transgress. Whosoever does that has surely wronged himself."* (Qur'an 2:231)

The repetition across two sūrahs is the Qur'an's way of underlining a temptation it has already seen. The third option men invent for themselves is the dishonorable retention: taking the wife back at the last hour of the waiting period, not from desire to rebuild the marriage, but in order to start the divorce clock again and so imprison her in a cycle of waiting. Al-Baqarah names that motive outright — "retain them not to cause harm." Al-Ṭalāq does not need to repeat the motive because it has already called the whole procedure a *ḥadd* of God. What it adds is the pairing of the two honorable acts as equal in dignity. Reconciliation is not a defeat. Final separation, done with fairness, is not a crime. The crime is using either door as a weapon.

*Maʿrūf* is that which is known to be decent by revelation and by the sound custom of the community. In this context it means: restore the marriage because you want the marriage, with its rights and its kindness, or complete the divorce while paying what is owed, speaking without slander, and refusing to punish the woman for a bond that has ended. The opposite of *maʿrūf* is *munkar*, the rejected. A man who resumes a wife in order to hurt her, or who dismisses her with humiliation, unpaid rights, and a campaign against her name, has not used either of the two doors the verse opened. He has climbed through a window of his own making.

**Why the Heart Must Be Examined Before the Word of Return**

Al-Qurṭubī, reading this verse with 2:231, insists that the intention of resumption is itself a legal and moral condition. The waiting period is a mercy. To turn that mercy into a snare is to take a divine concession and invert it. The psychology is familiar in every age. A person who cannot bear to be the one who was left will, at the last moment, snatch the other back — not to love them, but to avoid the public fact of loss. Another will resume in order to negotiate a better exit, or to punish a family that took the wife's side. The verse does not deputize a judge to open the chest and inspect the heart. It places the inspection on the believer himself, and then reminds him that the Last Day will complete any inspection he evades.

This is why the next clause of the verse invokes belief in God and the Last Day as the audience of the ruling. Laws that depend on inner motive cannot be fully policed by courts. They are policed by eschatology. A man who believes that every intention will be weighed is a man who cannot afford a theatrical reconciliation. A man who does not believe that will always find a lawyer's reading that lets him do as he likes. The verse is written for the first man, and it warns the second.

**Witnesses Called for God, Not for Pride**

*Wa-ashhidū dhaway ʿadlin minkum* — "And call two just persons among yourselves to witness." *ʿAdl* here is the quality of the witness: upright, fair, not a partisan of either spouse. The dual *dhaway* specifies two. The command is masculine plural, addressed to the community of the spouses, not to a professional class of notaries, though later legal systems rightly developed documentation around it. Al-Qurṭubī records a genuine juristic disagreement: some of the scholars held the witnessing of resumption (and, on some views, of the divorce itself) to be obligatory (*wājib*); others held it to be recommended (*mustaḥabb*). The disagreement is about legal force, not about wisdom. Whether a judge will void an unwitnessed return is a school question. Whether a household should let so grave an act pass as a private mutter is not.

Why witnesses at all? Because divorce and resumption change the status of two people, the lineage of possible children, the rights of housing and maintenance, and the freedom of the woman to marry another man. An unwitnessed status is a status that can be denied the next morning. The husband who resumed in secret can later claim he did not; the husband who divorced in secret can later claim he did. Two just witnesses convert a domestic event into a public fact, which is the only kind of fact a community can protect. *Wa-aqīmū al-shahādata lillāh* — "and uphold the testimony for God." The testimony is not a favor to the husband, not a humiliation of the wife, not a performance for the neighborhood. It is for God. The same ethic governs testimony in the great verse of the debt (2:282) and in the warning:

> *"And do not conceal testimony. Whoever conceals it, his heart is indeed sinful."* (Qur'an 2:283)

A witness who shades the truth to protect a friend, or who refuses to come because "it is not our business," has not understood the preposition *lillāh*. The business is God's.

**Belief in God and the Last Day as a Divorce Ethic**

*Dhālikum yūʿaẓu bihi man kāna yuʾminu bi-Allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhir* — "By this is exhorted whosoever believes in God and the Last Day." The verse does not say that only believers may divorce. It says that only believers will take the exhortation to heart. Unbelief can imitate the forms — hire the witnesses, keep the calendar — and still empty them of their purpose. Faith supplies the missing audience. God sees the intention of resumption. The Last Day will display the unpaid mahr, the slander whispered to relatives, the child used as a messenger of spite. The pairing of the two beliefs is the pairing already made in 2:177 and throughout the Book: God as origin of the law, the Last Day as its enforcement when earthly courts fail.

The social effect is large. A community that treats divorce as a private collapse will produce private injustices that no one is entitled to name. A community that treats divorce as an act performed before God, with witnesses, honorable retention or honorable release, and a counted waiting period, has converted a wound into a procedure. Procedure does not remove grief. It prevents grief from becoming predation. That is the whole political philosophy of this sūrah in one clause.

**The Promise of a Way Out**

The verse then opens a door that seems, at first, to belong to another subject: *wa-man yattaqi Allāha yajʿal lahu makhrajan* — "And whosoever reverences God, He will appoint a way out for him." The sentence continues into verse 3, but it begins here, and it must be read here first, in the context of divorce. Al-Qurṭubī and the jurists of the occasion of revelation understand the "way out" in this setting as the reward of those who observe the requirements of divorce despite the difficulty they entail. The man who houses his wife during the ʿiddah when he would rather be rid of the sight of her; the woman who remains when she would rather flee; the husband who pays when money is short; the pair who call witnesses when pride wants secrecy — these are people for whom God promises an exit that they cannot yet see.

Al-Rāzī widens the lens, and the language of the verse permits the widening. Anyone who endures an affliction with taqwā will be granted a way from the Fire to the Garden in the Hereafter. The particular and the general are not rivals. The particular is the sūrah's immediate mercy: keep the limits in this painful household matter, and God will open a door you cannot engineer — reconciliation, or an honorable new life, or a provision you did not budget. The general is the sūrah's theology: taqwā is never a dead end. It is, by God's promise, a corridor.

The word *makhraj* is an exit, a place of going out. It implies that the reverent person is, for a time, in a tight space. Divorce is that tight space: shame, money, children, families taking sides, the nights of second-guessing. The verse does not deny the tightness. It forbids the belief that the only way out is to break God's limits. The false exit is always available: expel her, stop paying, lie about the dates, refuse the witnesses, slander her to justify yourself. That door opens onto a worse room. The promised *makhraj* is God's door, and it opens only for the one who would not take the false one.

In modern life the false doors have multiplied: dissipating assets before a settlement, coaching children to despise the other parent, using immigration status or family honor as leverage, dragging a process so that the weaker party concedes. Each of these can look like strategy. This verse names them as the refusal of taqwā, and it places opposite them a promise that no lawyer can match: God Himself will appoint the way out. The believer's work is to stay inside the limits. The exit is not his to cut through the wall.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:3

> **and will provide for him whence he reckons not. And whosoever trusts in God, He suffices him. Truly God fulfills His Command. God has indeed set a measure for all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Provision from a Direction the Calculator Did Not Face**

Verse 3 completes the sentence begun at the end of verse 2. The one who has taqwā is promised not only a *makhraj*, a way out, but *wa-yarzuqhu min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasib* — "and will provide for him from where he does not reckon." *Yaḥtasib* is from *ḥisāb*, reckoning, accounting. It is the verb of the person who has already added up his sources and found them short. God promises a provision that does not appear on that ledger. The Arabic does not say that God will provide from where the servant *cannot* reckon, as though the servant were intellectually deficient. It says from where he *does not* reckon — from a direction he was not facing, because his fear had narrowed his field of vision to the obvious doors.

The commentators, including al-Qurṭubī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Suyūṭī, report that this verse and the last sentence of the preceding one were connected with a poor man who had numerous children. When he asked the Prophet ﷺ for help, the Prophet ﷺ told him:

> *"Fear God, and be patient."*

Not long after, a son of this man, who had been captured by the enemy, escaped and led away many of the enemy's sheep — four thousand according to some reports (al-Qurṭubī, al-Wāḥidī), one hundred according to others (al-Rāzī). The man came to the Prophet ﷺ to ask what he should do with the animals. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Keep them. They are yours."* (reported by al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Suyūṭī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Wāḥidī)

They would technically have been spoils of war; the Prophet ﷺ assigned them to the father. The story is not a promise that every fearful household will receive a flock of sheep. It is a lived instance of the verse's grammar: the man had counted his options and found only the Prophet's door; the Prophet sent him back to taqwā and patience; the provision arrived from a direction — a captured son's escape — that no budget could have included. Al-Rāzī and others also preserve the wider reading: the verse is a general law of the moral universe, not a one-time anecdote. Taqwā rearranges the causes.

**Why Rizq Is Mentioned in a Chapter About Divorce**

A reader may still ask why provision is invoked here, in a sūrah about waiting periods and houses. The answer is that divorce is, among other things, an economic event. Two households must now be funded from the means that formerly funded one. Children must be fed. A pregnant woman must be maintained until delivery (verse 6). A nursing mother may be owed a wage. The man who fears that keeping the limits will impoverish him is being told, in the middle of the law, that the Author of the law is also the Author of provision. The fear that makes men cheat the ʿiddah, expel the wife to save rent, or withhold the nursing wage is a theological error before it is a legal one. It assumes that rizq is produced by one's own cleverness at the edge of the rules. The verse says the opposite: rizq is produced by God, and it is increased, not decreased, along the path of taqwā.

The Qur'an had already stated the law of provision in other keys:

> *"And there is no creature on earth but that upon God is its provision."* (Qur'an 11:6)

> *"And whoever fears God — He will appoint for him a way out, and will provide for him from where he does not reckon."* (Qur'an 65:2–3)

The second is this sūrah's own wording, which later generations of Muslims have recited in every kind of tightness, not only in divorce. The particular setting remains the best commentary. When the right thing looks unaffordable, the verse forbids the conclusion that the right thing is therefore suspended. God has not issued a law and then forgotten to fund those who keep it.

**Tawakkul: Trust That Is a Tenet, Not a Mood**

*Wa-man yatawakkal ʿalā Allāhi fa-huwa ḥasbuhu* — "And whosoever trusts in God, He suffices him." *Tawakkul* is from *wakala*, to entrust a matter to a representative. The *mutawakkil* is the one who appoints God as the trustee of his affair. *Ḥasbuhu* means "his sufficiency," his enough. The sentence is among the most frequently quoted in the Qur'an, and its relatives are many:

> *"If you believe in God, then trust in Him, if you are believers."* (Qur'an 5:23)

> *"And upon God let the believers trust."* (Qur'an 14:11)

> *"And whoever trusts in God, He suffices him."* (Qur'an 65:3)

The virtue is extolled throughout the Book, as the commentary on 14:11–12 observes. Here it is placed next to God's *qadar*, His measuring-out, and next to the waiting period. Al-Rāzī's insight is exact: observing the ʿiddah is itself a form of tawakkul. The man who waits, when waiting costs him, is a man who has handed the outcome to God. The man who will not wait is a man who trusts his own haste more than he trusts the Measurer.

Tawakkul is not a mood and not an excuse. The Prophet ﷺ, when a man asked whether he should let his camel go and trust in God, answered:

> *"Tie her, and trust in God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2517, from Anas)

The rope is the waiting period, the housing, the witnesses, the provision according to means. The trust is that these ropes, tied as God commanded, will not strangle the one who ties them. Passivity that refuses to tie the rope is not tawakkul; it is negligence wearing a pious name. Anxiety that ties extra ropes of injustice — expulsion, withheld money, false dates — is not prudence; it is the claim that God will not suffice. The verse answers that claim with a pronoun of astonishing intimacy: *fa-huwa ḥasbuhu*, He, God Himself, is his enough.

**Faith Includes the Measuring-Out**

The verse then says *inna Allāha bālighu amrihi* — "Truly God fulfills His Command," or "God attains His purpose." No human resistance, including the resistance of a husband who hates the waiting period, can prevent what God has decided to accomplish. And then: *qad jaʿala Allāhu li-kulli shayʾin qadrā* — "God has indeed set a measure for all things." *Qadr* is measure, amount, appointed extent. It is the same root as *al-qadar*, the Divine measuring-out which the famous ḥadīth of Gabriel lists among the pillars of faith. In the report of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, recorded by Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ defined faith as:

> *"Faith is to believe in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and al-qadar, the good of it and the evil of it."* (Muslim 8)

The sūrah thus inserts a tenet of ʿaqīdah into a paragraph of family law. The waiting period has a measure. The provision has a measure. The hardship has a measure. Pregnancy has a measure that verse 4 will name: until delivery. Nursing has a measure that 2:233 already named: two full years for whoever wishes to complete the suckling. Nothing in this painful process is formless. The God who "has set a measure for all things" is the God of 54:49:

> *"Indeed, We have created all things according to a measure."* (Qur'an 54:49)

To keep the ʿiddah is to consent to live inside that measure. To break it is to pretend that one's own preferred timeline is more real than God's.

**The Psychology of "He Suffices Him"**

Sufficiency is the answer to a precise fear. The divorcing man fears that housing his wife will ruin him. The divorced woman fears that the waiting period will leave her suspended without a future. Both fear the neighbors' talk, the family's pressure, the nights of not knowing. *Ḥasbuhu* does not promise that the fear will feel untrue. It promises that the fear is not the last word. The last word is God's sufficiency, which may arrive as money, or as an opened heart, or as a patience that makes the time bearable, or as a new life after an honorable separation. The servant's part is to trust; God's part is to be enough. The verse assigns the parts and forbids the servant from trying to play both.

In contemporary terms, the verse is a direct critique of the scarcity-panic that produces unjust divorces. People harm each other most when they believe there is not enough — not enough money, not enough time, not enough respect left — and that therefore the rules of decency are a luxury. The Qur'an replies that sufficiency is not a function of the visible pile. It is a function of the relationship with the One who feeds the creature whose provision cannot be seen (11:6). A man who believes that can afford to be fair. A man who does not believe that will always experience fairness as a loss he cannot bear. The difference between those two men is not income. It is tawakkul.

**Measure as Mercy, Not as Trap**

Finally, the closing clause — a measure for all things — must be heard as mercy. Human beings in pain want either infinite delay or instant ending. God gives neither. He gives a counted period, a house for its duration, a provision scaled to means (verse 7), a pregnancy that ends at delivery (verse 4), and a nursing arrangement that can be transferred if the parents cannot agree (verse 6). These are measures. They protect the woman from being discarded without time, the man from being bound without limit, the child from being forgotten in the war of the adults, and the community from the chaos of uncounted statuses. A measure is a kindness to finite creatures. The One who set it "fulfills His Command": the period will end, the provision will come, the purpose will be attained. The believer's work is not to improve the measure. It is to inhabit it until God opens the *makhraj* He has already promised.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:4

> **As for those of your women who no longer await menstruation, if you are unsure, then their waiting period is three months, as it is for those who are yet to menstruate. But as for those who are pregnant, their term is until they deliver. And whosoever reverences God, He will appoint ease for his affair.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Closed a Gap the Community Had Already Noticed**

Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Wāḥidī preserve the report that Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, the Companion famed for his recitation, said to the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"People say that there remain women regarding whom nothing was mentioned in the Qur'an, the young and those who are pregnant."*

Then this verse was revealed. The report is a model of how Qur'anic legislation actually grew. Al-Baqarah had given the waiting period of the menstruating woman in three courses (2:228) and the waiting period of the widow in four months and ten days (2:234). The community, living the law, noticed the uncovered cases: the woman whose courses had ceased, the girl who had not yet begun them, and the woman who was carrying a child. Ubayy did not treat the silence as a license to improvise. He brought the gap to the Prophet ﷺ, and God answered with a verse. Revelation here is not an abstract code dropped once and for all in a vacuum. It is a living address to a community that is trying to be just and is honest enough to admit where it does not yet know how.

The phrase *in irtabtum* — "if you are unsure" — has been read in more than one direction. The dominant reading, which al-Qurṭubī records, takes it as uncertainty about the legal ruling itself: if you are in doubt about how to count the ʿiddah of these women, then here is the count. Another reading, which al-Qurṭubī also notes, connects the phrase back to verse 1: do not expel them from their houses if you are unsure that the waiting period has concluded. Both readings serve the same ethic. Where the body does not give a clear sign, the law supplies a clear number, so that neither haste nor delay can hide behind ambiguity.

**Three Months When the Body No Longer Keeps the Old Calendar**

*Wa-allāʾī yaʾisna min al-maḥīḍi min nisāʾikum* — "As for those of your women who have despaired of menstruation." *Yaʾisna* is from *yaʾs*, despair, the cessation of hope that a thing will occur. The women in view are those whose courses have stopped, typically through age. Their waiting period cannot be three menstrual courses, because the sign that 2:228 uses is no longer given. God does not leave them in a legal fog. He substitutes a solar measure for a biological one: *fa-ʿiddatuhunna thalāthatu ashhur* — three months.

The substitution is itself a teaching. Law in this sūrah is not enslaved to a single marker. When the marker of menstruation is present, it is used, because it is the most precise available evidence of the womb's state. When it is absent, time itself becomes the evidence. Three months is long enough to make a hidden pregnancy unlikely to remain hidden, and short enough that a woman is not imprisoned in an endless ʿiddah. The God who "set a measure for all things" (65:3) here names the measure.

The widow's ʿiddah remains distinct, and the commentators are careful not to confuse the two:

> *"And those of you who die and leave wives — they shall wait by themselves four months and ten days."* (Qur'an 2:234)

Death and divorce are different ruptures. The longer widow's period honors a bond that ended by a decree no spouse chose, and it gives grief a legally protected season. The three months of this verse are the divorce-measure for a body that no longer menstruates. Mixing the two would be a failure of the very precision the sūrah demands.

**Those Who Have Not Yet Menstruated**

*Wa-allāʾī lam yaḥiḍna* — "and those who have not yet menstruated." The same three months apply. The clause has been the subject of juristic and ethical attention across the centuries, because it presupposes the possibility of a marriage contracted before menstruation has begun. The classical schools treated such contracts inside the marital norms of their time, with consummation delayed until physical readiness as they understood it. What the verse itself is doing is narrower and must not be lost in later controversy: it refuses to leave any divorced woman without a countable ʿiddah. If the biological sign has not yet appeared, the temporal sign is imposed. No woman is left in the category Ubayy named — "women regarding whom nothing was mentioned."

The ethical center of the clause, for a modern reader, is the same center as the rest of the sūrah: the law's first loyalty is to clarity of status, protection of lineage, and the prevention of harm. A waiting period exists to answer questions that haste cannot answer. Where the body is silent, the calendar speaks. The community is not permitted to invent a fourth, uncounted status — "we do not know what to do with her" — and then to treat that ignorance as a permission to discard or to confine.

**Pregnancy Ends the Count at Birth, Not at a Number of Months**

*Wa-ulātu al-aḥmāli ajaluhunna an yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunna* — "But as for those who are pregnant, their term is until they deliver." This is among the most precise sentences in Qur'anic family law. The ʿiddah of the pregnant woman is not three courses, not three months, not four months and ten days. It is the pregnancy itself, and it ends when the child is born. *Ajal* is an appointed term; *yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunna* is the laying-down of the burden they carry.

The ruling solves several problems at once. It establishes lineage: the child born in this period is the child of the marriage that is ending, with all the rights of naming, maintenance, and inheritance that follow. It prevents a woman from marrying another man while carrying the first man's child. It also, in a case the jurists discussed at length, can be shorter than any other ʿiddah: if a woman delivers a day after the divorce, her waiting period is done. The reverse is also true: if the pregnancy lasts its full term, her ʿiddah is longer than three months. The measure follows the child, not the convenience of either parent.

A famous test of this verse was the case of Subayʿah al-Aslamiyyah, whose husband died while she was pregnant and who delivered shortly after. The Prophet ﷺ told her that she was free to marry, confirming that the ʿiddah of pregnancy ends at delivery even in widowhood. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record the report. The ḥadīth does not abolish 2:234; it shows how this verse specifies it for the pregnant widow. The two verses together yield a coherent rule: the waiting period is the longer of the two concerns — grief's season, or the child's arrival — and in pregnancy the child's arrival is the decisive term.

**"If You Are Unsure" as a Moral Posture, Not Only a Legal Clause**

Al-Qurṭubī's second reading of *in irtabtum* — do not expel them if you are unsure the period has ended — deserves to be lived even by those who prefer the first reading. Uncertainty is a recurring condition in family breakdown. Dates are disputed. Memories are partisan. A missed course is read as pregnancy by one spouse and as stress by the other. The verse's posture in the face of that fog is conservative of the woman's protection: when unsure, do not expel; when unsure how to count, use the numbered measures God just supplied. The opposite posture — when unsure, decide in my favor — is the posture of *ẓulm*, the self-wronging of verse 1.

Modern analogues are everywhere: contested paternity, disputed dates of separation, arguments over whether a menopause has truly begun. The Qur'an's method is not to pretend these uncertainties will vanish. It is to give default measures that err on the side of time, housing, and the child's rights. A community that treats uncertainty as a weapon against the weaker party has not read *in irtabtum*.

**Ease Appointed for the Reverent**

The verse ends as verses 2 and 5 also end, with taqwā attached to a divine response: *wa-man yattaqi Allāha yajʿal lahu min amrihi yusrā* — "And whosoever reverences God, He will appoint ease for his affair." *Yusr* is ease, facilitation, the untying of a knot. Al-Qurṭubī understands it here, in one reading, as the easing of reconciliation before the divorce is complete, if the couple keep these conditions. He also allows the more general sense: divorce is hard, the waiting period with its residual contact is hard, and God lightens the affair of those who do not add injustice to the hardship.

The pairing of *yusr* here with *makhraj* in verse 2 and with the wiping of sins in verse 5 is a deliberate pedagogy. Each legal block of the sūrah is sealed with a spiritual promise, so that no one can say the law is only burden. Taqwā in the ʿiddah of the older woman, the young woman, and the pregnant woman will be rewarded with ease. The ease may be inner — a heart that can bear the days — or outer — a provision, a reconciliation, a clean ending that does not poison the years after. The verse does not specify which, because the Appointed of ease is also the Knower of what this particular affair requires.

The logical principle is the same one that verse 7 will state openly: God does not task a soul beyond what He has given it, and He brings ease after hardship. A pregnancy that becomes the entire ʿiddah is a hardship of one kind; three months without the familiar sign of menstruation is a hardship of another. Both are measured. Both, if inhabited with taqwā, are promised ease. The believer's part is not to choose a more convenient measure. It is to keep the one God named, and to wait for the ease that God, not the self, will appoint.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:5

> **That is the Command of God that He has sent down unto you. Whosoever reverences God, He will absolve him of his evil deeds and honor him with reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Law Named as Revelation, Not as Custom**

*Dhālika amru Allāhi anzalahu ilaykum* — "That is the Command of God that He has sent down unto you." The demonstrative *dhālika* points back to everything that verses 1–4 have legislated: the timing of divorce, the counted ʿiddah, the house, the limits, the two honorable exits, the witnesses, the three months, the term of pregnancy. All of it is now gathered under one name: *amr Allāh*, God's Command, and under one origin: *anzalahu*, He sent it down. The verb is the verb of revelation. Household procedure is not being offered as tribal wisdom, prophetic advice, or a useful social policy. It is being ranked with the rest of what came down from above.

The ranking matters because divorce law is the kind of law people are most tempted to treat as negotiable. Inheritance, prayer, and fasting have public visibility. The treatment of a wife in her waiting period happens behind a door. By calling these rulings a sent-down Command, the verse closes the excuse that private life is a different jurisdiction. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr read the sentence as a reaffirmation that the restrictions on the waiting period are part of the revealed Law — *sharīʿah* in the strict sense, not ʿurf that may be displaced when inconvenient.

The addressee is *ilaykum*, unto you, plural. After verse 1's "O Prophet," the community as a whole is now named as the recipient. No household may say that the Command was sent to the Messenger ﷺ alone. The descent is upon them.

**Why the Sūrah Keeps Sealing Law with Taqwā**

Verses 2, 4, and 5 all end with a taqwā-clause, and a careless reader may hear repetition. The clauses are not copies of one another. Verse 2 promised a *makhraj*, a way out. Verse 4 promised *yusr*, ease in the affair. Verse 5 promises two further gifts: the covering of evil deeds and an honorable reward. The sūrah is building a complete economy of obedience. Keep the limits, and God will open an exit you cannot see. Keep them, and He will ease the knot of the affair itself. Keep them, and He will deal with the sins that accumulated on the way — including, one may fairly hear, the sins that helped bring the marriage to this point — and will honor the servant with reward.

The structure is pastoral as well as legal. A person in the wreckage of a marriage often cannot imagine any future except more wreckage. The Qur'an refuses that imagination three times in five verses. The future of the reverent is not wreckage. It is exit, ease, forgiveness, and honor. These are not decorations added to sweeten a bitter statute. They are the stated consequences of treating a bitter statute as God's Command rather than as an obstacle to one's will.

**Evil Deeds Absolved: How Good Erases What It Finds**

*Man yattaqi Allāha yukaffir ʿanhu sayyiʾātihi* — "Whosoever reverences God, He will absolve him of his evil deeds." *Yukaffir* is from *kufr* in its root sense of covering: God covers the sins, wipes them, does not hold them in the open. Aḥmad ibn ʿAjībah, in the commentary tradition, notes that God covers evil deeds through good deeds, which wipe them away. The principle is Qur'anic:

> *"Indeed, good deeds wipe away evil deeds."* (Qur'an 11:114)

And it is Prophetic. The Messenger ﷺ said, in the counsel recorded by al-Tirmidhī from Abū Dharr and Muʿādh:

> *"Fear God wherever you are, follow an evil deed with a good deed and it will wipe it out, and treat people with good character."* (al-Tirmidhī 1987; he graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

In the setting of this sūrah the application is almost unbearably practical. Marriages that end have usually accumulated a sediment of small and large wrongs: words said to wound, rights delayed, tenderness withheld, suspicions nourished. The waiting period, kept with taqwā — housing maintained, dates counted honestly, witnesses called, provision paid — is itself a sequence of good deeds laid over that sediment. The verse promises that God will treat those deeds as a covering. A man cannot undo every harsh sentence he spoke in the marriage. He can, in the ʿiddah, refuse to add a new injustice, and God may cover what he cannot himself repair.

The related verse in the next sūrah deepens the same hope:

> *"O you who believe, turn to God in sincere repentance. It may be that your Lord will absolve you of your evil deeds and admit you to Gardens beneath which rivers flow."* (Qur'an 66:8)

Forgiveness in 65:5 is not a vague spiritual mood. It is attached to taqwā in a specified legal field. The person who wants his sins covered has been told where, in this chapter, to stand.

**Honored with Reward, Not Merely Pardoned**

The verse does not stop at covering. *Wa-yuʿẓim lahu ajrā* — "and honor him with reward," or "make great for him a reward." *Yuʿẓim* is to magnify, to make immense. Pardon restores a person to zero. Reward takes him past zero into honor. The sūrah refuses to treat the keeper of divorce-law as someone who has merely avoided punishment. He is someone God will honor.

The psychology of that promise is exact. Divorce, even when done rightly, often feels like failure. Families whisper. The person who kept every limit may still walk away with a sense of having lost. God does not share that account. In His account, the one who reverenced Him in the hardest domestic hour is a candidate for magnified reward. The honor may be unseen in this world — a secret dignity in the heart, a barakah on the next chapter of life — and it will not be unseen in the next. Either way, the verse relocates the tribunal. The neighbors are not the judges of whether this divorce was a disgrace. God is, and He has already announced how He judges the reverent party to it.

**Command, Covering, Honor: A Complete Transaction**

Read as a unit, verse 5 is a complete transaction between heaven and the household. Heaven sends down a Command. The servant meets it with taqwā. Heaven responds with two gifts that the servant cannot give himself: a covered past and an honored future. No human court can offer that package. A court can enforce housing and maintenance. It cannot wipe the heart's ledger or magnify a reward in the unseen. The verse therefore explains why this legislation is revealed rather than merely enacted. It is not only a social technology. It is a corridor of tawbah and ajr running through the middle of family breakdown.

For the modern reader drowning in the language of "irreconcilable differences" and "moving on," the verse offers a different grammar. Moving on, in the Qur'an's sense, is not the suppression of the past. It is the covering of the past by God, which is a different and better thing, and the receiving of an honor that does not depend on the marriage having succeeded. A failed marriage kept within God's limits is, in this verse's accounting, a site of possible greatness. That sentence, if believed, would change the way a community treats its divorced members, and the way they treat themselves.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:6

> **Let them dwell where you dwell according to your means, and do not harm them so as to put them in straits. And if they are pregnant, then spend on them until they deliver. Then if they are suckling for you, give them their compensation, and consult together in an honorable way. And if you have difficulty with one another, then another will suckle for him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Dwelling Scaled to His Life, Not to His Anger**

*Askinūhunna min ḥaythu sakantum min wujdikum* — "Let them dwell where you dwell, according to your means." The verb *askinū* is a command to house them, to make them dwell. The measure is *min ḥaythu sakantum*: from where you yourselves dwell — not a lesser shed invented for the occasion, not a different neighborhood chosen to isolate her, but the standard of the house the man actually inhabits. Then the qualification: *min wujdikum*, from your means, your capacity, your finding. A poor man is not commanded to rent a palace for the ʿiddah. A rich man is not permitted to house her in a way that insults the standard of his own life. Justice here is analogical. Her roof during the waiting period should be a version of his roof.

Al-Qurṭubī records the juristic split that grew from this sentence. Some scholars argued that the verse obliges shelter only, and that food and other provision are a separate matter, owed in some cases and not in others depending on whether the divorce is revocable. Others maintained that the man must continue to spend on the woman's provision as he did in the marriage, at least for the duration of the ʿiddah of a revocable divorce. The disagreement is real and was argued with evidence. What no school worth the name disputed is the core: she is not to be made homeless by his decision to divorce, and the housing that is given must not be an instrument of humiliation.

Verse 1 had already said "expel them not from their houses." Verse 6 now states the positive form of the same duty. Not-expelling is a prohibition. Housing is a command. A man might keep her legally "not expelled" while making the house unlivable — locking rooms, cutting food, filling the air with insult. The next clause is written for that man.

**Harm That Tightens the Chest Until She Leaves**

*Wa-lā tuḍārrūhunna li-tuḍayyiqū ʿalayhinna* — "and do not harm them so as to put them in straits." Two verbs of injury sit together. *Tuḍārrūhunna* is from *ḍarar*, harm, the same root that 2:231 and 2:233 use for the harm forbidden around divorce and nursing. *Tuḍayyiqū* is to narrow, to straiten, to make a space too tight to inhabit. The verse names a strategy as old as domestic cruelty: do not strike her or throw her into the street; simply make the house so bitter that she "chooses" to leave, and then claim that you did not expel her. God closes that strategy by naming its purpose. Harm whose aim is constriction is itself the expulsion verse 1 forbade, wearing a thinner disguise.

The commentators understood constriction in material and in psychological forms: reducing food, withholding comforts, crowding her into a worse room, filling the house with abuse, or using relatives to make her days unendurable. Modern forms write themselves: turning off heat, confiscating documents, controlling movement, filling a shared space with silence that is itself a weapon, or performing generosity in public and tightness in private. The verse's test is simple. If the conduct is designed to make her go, it is forbidden, whatever name it is given.

The analogy of taqwā as careful driving applies with special force here. The road of the ʿiddah is already narrow. A man who adds harm is a driver who, finding the lane tight, swerves into the pedestrian. The law asked him only to hold the wheel until the measured miles were done.

**Pregnancy Converts the ʿIddah into a Full Maintenance**

*Wa-in kunna ulāti ḥamlin fa-anfiqū ʿalayhinna ḥattā yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunna* — "And if they are pregnant, then spend on them until they deliver." *Anfiqū* is the verb of nafaqah, spending, maintenance. For the pregnant woman the debate about shelter-only versus full provision is, in this clause, settled by revelation itself. He must spend on her until she lays down her burden. The child she carries is his child. Feeding her is feeding the child. The divorce, even if irrevocable, does not sever that fact.

The term matches verse 4: her ʿiddah is until delivery, and her maintenance is until delivery. Status and support run on the same clock. A man who stops spending because "we are divorced" while his child is still unborn has not understood which relationship the spending serves. It serves the child, through the mother's body. The Qur'an had already bound the two lives together in 2:233; this verse makes the binding explicit for the case of divorce.

**Suckling Is a Paid Labor, Not a Sentiment to Be Extorted**

*Fa-in arḍaʿna lakum fa-ātūhunna ujūrahunna* — "Then if they are suckling for you, give them their compensation." *Ujūr* is wages, hire, the payment due for work. The mother who nurses after the divorce is not being asked for a gift. She is performing a service *lakum*, for you — for the father and for the child who is his responsibility — and the service has a wage. The verse dignifies nursing by placing it in the language of contract rather than in the language of unpaid natural duty. A divorced woman may nurse, and if she does, she is owed money. She may also, as the end of the verse will say, not nurse.

The conditions of suckling were already laid down in al-Baqarah:

> *"And mothers shall suckle their children two full years, for those who wish to complete the suckling. The father of the child is responsible for their provision and clothing according to what is honorable. No soul is tasked beyond its capacity. No mother shall be harmed on account of her child, nor father on account of his child."* (Qur'an 2:233)

Al-Ṭalāq now specifies the post-divorce form of the same ethic. Two years remains the completed term for whoever wishes it. The wage is the form that "provision" takes when the parents' household is no longer one. Some scholars, as al-Qurṭubī notes, held that the man must pay for the provisions of both mother and child; others held that he pays only for the child, the mother's wage being a separate hire. In either reading the child is not to go hungry because the marriage ended, and the mother is not to be conscripted into unpaid labor because she is the mother.

**Consultation Honorable Enough to Survive a Broken House**

*Wa-taʾmirū baynakum bi-maʿrūf* — "and consult together in an honorable way." *Taʾmirū* is from *amr*, to take counsel, to arrange a matter jointly. The divorced parents are commanded to confer. Divorce ended the marriage. It did not end the joint responsibility for the child, and it did not license a war in which the child is the field. *Bi-maʿrūf* again: the consultation must be conducted according to what is known to be decent — without humiliation, without using access to the child as revenge, without making the wage a humiliation, without making the refusal to nurse a slander.

The sentence is the social heart of the verse. Law can order housing and payment. It cannot, by itself, make two angry people plan a feeding schedule. The Qur'an therefore commands the character of their talk. A child whose parents can still confer honorably has not lost everything. A child whose parents can confer only through insults has been made to inhabit their quarrel. The verse forbids that habitation.

The parallel in 2:233 is the clause already quoted: no mother harmed on account of her child, nor father on account of his. Harm-through-the-child is a distinct species of injustice, and both sūrahs name it because both know how common it is. Modern forms include withheld visitation to extract money, withheld money to extract visitation, and the coaching of a child to despise the other parent. None of these is consultation. All of them are *ḍarar*.

**When They Cannot Agree, Another Woman Will Nurse**

*Wa-in taʿāsartum fa-sa-turḍiʿu lahu ukhrā* — "And if you have difficulty with one another, then another will suckle for him." *Taʿāsar* is mutual hardship, a knot that the two of them cannot untie together. Al-Jalālayn explains: if they cannot reach agreement because the father withholds payment or the mother refrains from suckling, the mother is not to be compelled, and another woman may be chosen to suckle the child. The pronoun *lahu* — for him — refers to the child, or to the father on the child's behalf. Providing for the suckling child remains the father's legal duty, because he is obligated to provide for all of his children's needs.

The clause is a mercy to both sides and a protection of the infant. The mother is not a prisoner of her milk. If the wage is unjust, or if her body or her heart cannot bear the nursing in this broken state, she may stop, and the child will still be fed. The father is not a prisoner of one woman's terms if those terms become extortion; he may hire another nurse. The child is not a prisoner of their deadlock. Someone will suckle for him. The law's loyalty, when the adults fail to agree, is to the small body that cannot negotiate.

Islamic law later developed from this verse and from 4:23 the doctrine of milk-relations: the woman who nurses a child becomes, in specified conditions, a foster-mother, and marriage-barriers follow. The present verse is the practical door to that doctrine. A nurse hired in a divorce is not a mere employee in the child's story. She may enter the child's kinship map. That possibility, held in view, should make the choice of "another" a careful one — which is itself a reason for the parents to prefer honorable agreement if they can still reach it.

**The Verse as a Map of Post-Marriage Justice**

Taken as a whole, verse 6 is a map of what justice looks like after love has failed. Housing at the standard of the man's own life. No constriction designed to drive her out. Full spending if she is pregnant, until birth. A wage if she nurses. Consultation that remains decent. A third person if the two cannot agree. Every clause answers a known abuse. Read the verse backwards, and you can reconstruct the pre-Islamic and post-Islamic temptations it was written to kill: the dumped wife, the starved pregnant woman, the unpaid mother, the child used as a weapon, the deadlock in which an infant goes hungry.

The modern family court, at its best, is an attempt to do by procedure what this verse does by revelation. At its worst, it becomes the very field of *taʿāsur*, mutual hardship, that the last clause names. The Qur'an's contribution is not only the rulings. It is the insistence that these rulings are *amr Allāh* (verse 5), that taqwā will be rewarded with ease and covering, and that the child — *lahu*, for him — is the one for whom the whole apparatus exists when the adults have run out of agreement. A community that recites this verse and then lets divorced women become homeless, or lets infants become bargaining chips, has recited it as sound without letting it become law.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:7

> **Let one who has abundant wealth spend from his abundance. And let one whose provision is measured spend from that which God has given him. God tasks no soul beyond that which He has given it. God will bring ease after hardship.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Spending That Follows the Actual Hand, Not the Neighbor's Hand**

The last legal verse of the sūrah is an economic constitution for the duties just imposed. *Li-yunfiq dhū saʿatin min saʿatih* — "Let one who has abundant wealth spend from his abundance." *Saʿah* is spaciousness, plenty, the opposite of the constriction (*ḍīq*) forbidden in verse 6. The man whose hand is wide is commanded to spend widely. Generosity here is not optional virtue. It is the form that justice takes when the means are large. A wealthy man who houses his pregnant former wife in scarcity, or who pays a nursing wage calculated to the cheapest possible figure, has not fulfilled *min saʿatih*. He has fulfilled a lesser law of his own invention.

Then the counterpart: *wa-man qudira ʿalayhi rizquhu fa-l-yunfiq mimmā ātāhu Allāh* — "And let one whose provision is measured spend from that which God has given him." *Qudira ʿalayhi* is the passive of measure: his rizq has been measured out tightly. He is not excused from spending. He is excused from spending what he does not have. He spends *mimmā ātāhu Allāh*, from what God has given him — the same God who, in verse 3, promised to provide from where the servant does not reckon. The poor man's duty is real and proportionate. The rich man's duty is real and proportionate. Equality in this verse is not equality of sums. It is equality of sincerity relative to means.

Al-Qurṭubī records two juristic uses of the verse. Some took it to mean that the amount paid to the former wife depends upon both her needs and his circumstances — a two-sided assessment. Others held that what is due is limited to her immediate needs, with his wealth affecting the standard of housing and maintenance but not creating an open-ended claim upon his surplus. He also notes that the verse has been treated as evidence that the husband (or former husband, in the relevant cases) is solely responsible for providing for the child, whether the couple is married or divorced. The child did not divorce anyone. The child's hunger is not a joint optional charity. It is the father's burden, scaled to what God has placed in his hand.

**The Theology Inside the Accounting**

*Lā yukallifu Allāhu nafsan illā mā ātāhā* — "God tasks no soul beyond that which He has given it." The sentence is a sister of the great closing of al-Baqarah:

> *"God tasks no soul except with that which it can bear."* (Qur'an 2:286)

The difference in wording is itself a commentary. 2:286 speaks of capacity (*wusʿah*). 65:7 speaks of what God has actually given (*mā ātāhā*). In a financial verse, the second wording is the more surgical. The question is not "what could this man theoretically bear if he rearranged his whole life?" The question is "what has God placed in his hand?" Duty is indexed to gift. A law that demanded from the poor man the spending of the rich would be a law that denied this sentence. A law that allowed the rich man to spend like the poor would be a law that denied the first clause of the verse.

Ibn Taymiyyah and the jurists who worked in his wake often treated this principle as a master-key of taklīf, legal responsibility: God does not impose a duty whose material condition He has not provided. The principle protects two people at once. It protects the poor from crushing demands dressed up as piety. It protects the rights-holder from the rich man's plea of poverty while his abundance is visible. Both abuses are forms of lying about what God has given.

**Why Ease Is Promised After the Spending, Not Instead of It**

*Sa-yajʿalu Allāhu baʿda ʿusrin yusrā* — "God will bring ease after hardship." *ʿUsr* is hardship, tightness, the measured provision of the second clause. *Yusr* is ease, the spaciousness of the first clause, now promised as a future God will make. The future tense *sa-yajʿalu* is a divine commitment, not a wish. Al-Qurṭubī reads it in two registers. In this life, God may ease the circumstances of the one who spent from a tight hand — additional means, an opened door, the *makhraj* of verse 2 arriving as money. In the Hereafter, God may ease the servant's way into the Garden because he strove to observe the Command when it was difficult. Both readings are true to the Book. Neither reading permits the servant to wait for ease before he spends. The order is: spend from what you have been given, then ease comes. The inverted order — ease first, then I will keep the law — is the order of those who do not trust verse 3.

The sūrah of expansion had already written the same law in a more universal key:

> *"For indeed, with hardship comes ease. Indeed, with hardship comes ease."* (Qur'an 94:5–6)

The repetition in 94:5–6, as the commentators noted, is a confirmation: the ease is as certain as the hardship, and it is attached to it, not merely promised after an indefinite delay. 65:7 takes that universal law and seats it at the end of a paragraph about child-support and nursing wages. The man counting coins after a divorce is standing inside Sūrah al-Sharḥ whether he knows it or not. The tightness he feels is not evidence that God has abandoned the Command. It is the *ʿusr* with which *yusr* has been paired.

**A Verse That Judges Two Kinds of Unfairness**

Every community that has administered this law has met two recurring unfairnesses, and the verse is written against both. The first is the wealthy man who performs poverty. He has *saʿah* and spends as though he had *qadr*. He hides assets, delays, litigates the minimum. The verse's first sentence is his indictment. The second is the community, or the former spouse, that treats the poor man's small payment as an insult because it is small. The verse's second sentence is his defense. God has named both the abundance and the measure, and has forbidden any human being to confuse them.

The same two unfairnesses appear in modern child-support battles with almost no translation required. Forensic accounting exists because some hands that are wide pretend to be closed. Humiliation of the working poor exists because some hearts cannot forgive a small payment for being small. 65:7 is equally uninterested in both pretenses. Show what God has given. Spend from that. Do not spend from what He has not given. Do not withhold from what He has.

**Hardship Is Not a Permanent Address**

The last four words of the legal section — ease after hardship — are also the sūrah's pastoral close before the cosmic warning begins. Verses 1–7 have asked a great deal: counted time, a shared house without intimacy, witnesses, provision, pregnancy-maintenance, a nursing wage, honorable consultation. A listener might conclude that God has only tightened. Verse 7 refuses that conclusion. The tightness is acknowledged (*ʿusr*), assigned a duty scaled to gift, and then promised an ending (*yusr*). The God of this sūrah is not a legislator who enjoys constriction. He is a legislator who will not let constriction become an excuse for injustice, and who will not let justice become a constriction without relief.

For the woman waiting, the sentence means that this ʿiddah is not the rest of her life. For the man spending from a measured provision, it means that his present hand is not the measure of his forever. For the child being nursed under a contract his parents did not want to sign, it means that the adults' hardship is not his destiny. God will bring ease after hardship. The legal section ends on a future tense because the people inside it need a future. The next section will show what happens to towns that refused the Command that this future was attached to.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:8

> **And how many a town insolently defied the Command of its Lord and His messengers, such that We called it to a severe reckoning and punished it with a terrible punishment!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why Ruined Cities Appear in a Chapter About Divorce**

The sūrah turns, without a connective of apology, from nursing wages to ruined towns. *Wa-ka-ayyin min qaryatin ʿatat ʿan amri rabbihā wa-rusulihi* — "And how many a town insolently defied the Command of its Lord and His messengers." *Ka-ayyin* is a word of multitude: how many, how often. The listener is not being told of one famous city. He is being told that the pattern is crowded. *ʿAtat* is insolence, arrogant refusal, the stiff-necked turning away of one who has heard and will not bow. The object of the insolence is *amr rabbihā wa-rusulihi* — the Command of its Lord and of His messengers. The same word *amr* that verse 5 used for the divorce rulings is now used for the Command that destroyed cities. The repetition is the argument.

Al-Qurṭubī and the commentators who follow the legal occasion of the sūrah are explicit about why this turn happens here. The rulings of verses 1–7, and of 2:222–237 before them, differed sharply from pre-Islamic custom. They gave women housing, counted time, maintenance, a nursing wage, and protection from constriction. Some found those rights unprecedented and therefore optional. The Qur'an answers not with a softer restatement of the rights but with a history lesson. Nations that treated God's Command as optional were not debated into ruin. They were ruined. Similar warnings surround the inheritance verses, which were also controversial because of the unprecedented shares they gave women:

> *"These are the limits of God, and whoever obeys God and His Messenger, He will admit him to Gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein. And that is the great triumph. And whoever disobeys God and His Messenger and transgresses His limits, He will admit him to a Fire, abiding therein, and he shall have a humiliating punishment."* (Qur'an 4:13–14)

Household law and inheritance law are sealed with eschatology for the same reason: they are the laws most likely to be quietly dropped when they cost a man money, pride, or control. Verse 8 is the sūrah's way of saying that quiet dropping is not quiet in heaven.

**Insolence Is a Town's Act, Not Only a Man's**

The subject is *qaryah*, a town, a settled community. The Qur'an often names cities as moral agents because cities have customs, and customs are how private refusals become public law. A single man who expels his wife in her ʿiddah is one transgressor. A town that considers such expulsion normal is a town that has *ʿatat ʿan amri rabbihā*. The verse is therefore not only a threat to the individual cheat. It is a threat to a culture. When a community's common sense diverges from the sent-down Command, the community has begun the insolence this verse names, even if the courts still recites the verses on Friday.

The pairing "its Lord and His messengers" closes a further excuse. Some will claim to honor God while ignoring the Messenger who delivered the details. The waiting period's timing, the witnesses, the nursing wage — these are Messenger-taught specifications of the Lord's Command. To accept God as Legislator in the abstract and then to dismiss the Prophet's ﷺ procedure is the very split the verse refuses. The town is destroyed for defying both.

**Two Punishments, or One Punishment with Two Names**

*Fa-ḥāsabnāhā ḥisāban shadīdan wa-ʿadhdhabnāhā ʿadhāban nukrā* — "such that We called it to a severe reckoning and punished it with a terrible punishment." *Ḥisāb shadīd* is a reckoning that is hard, tight, without the leniency God often shows in this world. *ʿAdhāb nukr* is a punishment that is terrible, unrecognized, monstrous — *nukr* meaning that which the soul refuses as too hideous to be familiar. Al-Qurṭubī reads the pair as this world and the next: the severe reckoning as the punishment they receive in this life (siege, famine, overthrow), and the terrible punishment as that of the Hereafter. Other commentators allow both to fall in this world, or both to describe the single storm of divine justice from different angles. The grammar supports the sequential reading: We reckoned, and We punished. Audit, then sentence.

The first-person plural — *ḥāsabnāhā*, *ʿadhdhabnāhā* — is the "We" of Divine majesty. The town is not a victim of chance or of geopolitics alone. The same God who, in verse 3, fulfills His Command and sets a measure for all things, here fulfills it against a community that would not be measured. The continuity of the Divine "We" across provision (verse 3) and destruction (verse 8) is a warning against the sentimental reading of God's mercy. Mercy is the offer of verses 2–7. Verse 8 is what remains when the offer is refused with insolence.

**Reckoning as the Opposite of the Uncounted ʿIddah**

The word *ḥisāb* in this verse should be heard against *wa-aḥṣū al-ʿiddata* in verse 1. God asked the husband to count the waiting period. The town that would not count what God asked it to count is itself counted, severely. The one who refuses the small audit is subjected to the large one. The logic is almost pedagogical. A people trained to keep precise faith with a woman's three months is a people that will not be shocked by a precise faith with their own deeds. A people that treats the small count as beneath them will meet a count that is not small.

The Prophet ﷺ taught the personal form of the same truth in the ḥadīth of ʿĀʾishah, recorded by al-Tirmidhī and others:

> *"Whoever is reckoned with a full reckoning is destroyed."* She said: "Did God not say, 'Then he will be reckoned with an easy reckoning'?" He said: "That is the presentation. But whoever is interrogated in the reckoning is destroyed." (al-Bukhārī 103; Muslim 2876; the wording of destruction in interrogation is in the two Ṣaḥīḥs)

An easy reckoning is a mercy given to those who lived inside the limits. A severe reckoning is the *ḥisāb shadīd* of this verse, reserved for the insolent town. The sūrah has been training its hearers, for seven verses, to live in such a way that their reckoning might be easy. Verse 8 shows the other possibility.

**The Social Logic of Making Private Law a Public Fate**

Why would God destroy a town over family law? The question assumes that family law is private. The Qur'an does not. A town that permits the expulsion of women, the starvation of the pregnant, the unpaid nursing of infants, and the uncounted confusion of lineage is a town that has already begun to eat its own future. Children of uncertain parentage, women without roofs, fathers who treat maintenance as optional — these are not bedroom issues. They are the decomposition of the only institution that turns biological reproduction into a moral community. The "terrible punishment" of such a town may take the historical forms the Qur'an elsewhere names — the cry, the sinkhole, the flood, the unseen host — and it may also take the slower form of a society that can no longer raise the next generation in safety. Either form is *nukr*: a punishment the soul should have found unimaginable until it arrived.

The verse therefore belongs in a modern commentary not as an antique war-oracle but as a claim about the scale of household justice. A legislature that treats family obligations as soft law, and contract and finance as hard law, has inverted this sūrah. Here, the hard law is the ʿiddah, the house, the wage. The ruined town is the commentary on what happens when that ranking is refused. Insolence is not only the shout of a pagan city at a prophet. It is the shrug of a believing city at a verse.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:9

> **Then did it taste the evil consequences of its affair, and the end of its affair was loss.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Taste as the Pedagogy of Consequence**

*Fa-dhāqat wabāla amrihā* — "Then did it taste the evil consequences of its affair." The connecting *fa-* is sequential: after the severe reckoning and the terrible punishment of verse 8, the town is now described from the inside of the experience. *Dhāqat*, it tasted. The Qur'an uses taste for punishments and for mercies because taste is knowledge that has entered the body. A warning heard is still outside. A consequence tasted is no longer deniable. The same verb appears across the Book for this interior education:

> *"So they tasted the evil consequence of their affair, and the end of their affair was loss."* (Qur'an 64:5 — a near-twin of this verse)

> *"And they will taste a painful punishment."* (Qur'an 6:30 and elsewhere)

Al-Qurṭubī says they tasted the consequences of disbelief. Ibn Kathīr says they regretted their actions when regret was no longer of any benefit to them. Both comments are right, and the verb holds them together. Taste is too late to be useful as prevention. It is useful as verdict. The town now knows, in the only way it would accept knowing, that the Command it mocked was not a sermon. It was a description of reality.

*Wabāl* is the evil outcome, the ruinous harvest, the unwholesome result of a deed. *Amrihā* is "its affair" — the same word *amr* that has run through the sūrah as God's Command (65:5, 65:8) now appearing as the town's own affair. The linguistic turn is severe. They would not submit to God's *amr*; they are made to swallow the *amr* they chose instead. A community's affair, when it is built on insolence, has a flavor, and the flavor is *wabāl*.

**The End That Was Already Present in the Beginning**

*Wa-kāna ʿāqibatu amrihā khusrā* — "and the end of its affair was loss." *ʿĀqibah* is the end, the sequel, what comes after. *Khusr* is loss, the word of the commercially ruined, the same root as Sūrah al-ʿAṣr's "indeed mankind is in loss" (103:2) and as the "losers" who lose themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection (39:15). The tense is *kāna*, "was" — not only "became." The commentators have long heard in that tense a claim about moral reality: the end was loss because the affair was loss, even when it still looked like power. Insolence does not become ruin at the last minute. Insolence is ruin that has not yet been tasted.

This is why regret, as Ibn Kathīr notes, arrives too late. The town's leaders, if they could speak from the punishment, would speak the speech the Qur'an gives other ruined peoples:

> *"Our Lord, we have seen and we have heard, so send us back; we will work righteousness. Indeed, we are now certain."* (Qur'an 32:12)

Certainty after taste is not the certainty faith asked for. Faith asked for certainty from the warning. The sūrah has been warning for eight verses. Verse 9 is the description of those who postponed certainty until there was nothing left to do with it.

**Loss as the Exact Opposite of the Promises Just Made**

The placement of *khusr* is the sūrah's own commentary by contrast. Verses 2–7 promised the reverent a way out, provision from the unreckoned direction, sufficiency, ease in the affair, covering of sins, magnified reward, and ease after hardship. Verse 9 names the opposite account in a single word: loss. The two futures are not random. They are the two ʿāqibahs of two relationships to the same Command. Keep it, and the affair's end is *yusr* and *ajr*. Defy it, and the affair's end is *khusr*. The sūrah does not admit a third, mixed end in which a town keeps its insolence and also keeps its prosperity forever. It may keep the prosperity for a time — the Qur'an elsewhere calls that a respite (3:178; 14:42) — but the ʿāqibah, the sequel that counts, is loss.

For a man in the middle of a divorce, the verse is not a distant civic myth. His "affair" is the ʿiddah he is now conducting. If he conducts it with taqwā, verses 2–7 have named his sequel. If he conducts it with the town's insolence — expulsion, constriction, unpaid wage, false count — verse 9 has named his sequel, even if the sequel takes years to become visible. Some losses are immediate: a child alienated, a reputation justly damaged, a later marriage poisoned by the habits of the first ending. Some losses are eschatological and will not be visible until the severe reckoning. The verse is not required to specify which. *Khusr* covers both.

**The Psychology of Tasting What One Would Not Hear**

There is a human pattern the verse captures with unpleasant accuracy. People often do not believe a limit is real until they have paid for crossing it. The drunk driver who would not believe the storm was dangerous believes it when the car has already left the road. Taqwā, to return to the given analogy, is the decision to believe the road is dangerous before the crash. The insolent town is the driver who treats every sign as an insult to his skill. Verse 8 is the crash. Verse 9 is the body's knowledge of the crash.

This is why the Qur'an so often pairs hearing and tasting as two grades of knowledge. Those who hear the messengers and act have the higher grade: they are saved the taste of *wabāl*. Those who will not hear are not left in ignorance. They are taught by taste, which is a brutal teacher and a thorough one. A community that wants to be spared verse 9 has only one method: to treat verses 1–7 as what verse 5 called them, a Command sent down, before the affair develops a flavor that cannot be spit out.

**Loss That Cannot Be Relabeled as a Lesson We Chose**

Modern speech is adept at renaming ruin. A collapsed family culture becomes "liberation." A generation of unfathered children becomes "new family forms." The impoverishment of divorced women becomes "the price of autonomy." The Qur'an is willing to discuss real goods that can accompany the end of a harmful marriage — that is what honorable separation in verse 2 is for. It is not willing to call *khusr* by a flattering name. When a town defies the Command of its Lord and His messengers, the end of its affair is loss, and the loss will be tasted, and the taste will not be improved by the slogans that preceded it.

The verse's usefulness to a believer is therefore diagnostic. After a divorce, after a policy, after a generation, one may ask: does this affair taste of *wabāl*, or of the *yusr* God promised the reverent? The mouth knows. The sūrah is only asking the community to believe its own tongue before the ʿāqibah is sealed.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:10

> **God prepared a severe punishment for them. So reverence God, O possessors of intellect, who have believed. God has certainly sent down unto you a reminder:**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Punishment Already Prepared, Not Invented in Anger**

*Aʿadda Allāhu lahum ʿadhāban shadīdā* — "God prepared a severe punishment for them." *Aʿadda* is to prepare, to make ready in advance, to set in waiting. The verb matters. The punishment of the insolent town is not a Divine improvisation, as though God were provoked into a rage He had not anticipated. It is a prepared consequence, built into the moral structure of the world the way the crash is built into the physics of the icy road. The people of verse 8 and 9 did not surprise their Lord. They walked into a punishment that had been made ready for whoever would choose their choice.

The adjective *shadīd* repeats the *ḥisāb shadīd* of verse 8. Severity is not cruelty. It is the matching of consequence to insolence. A light punishment for the arrogant refusal of messengers would be a false signal; it would teach the next town that messengers may be refused at an acceptable cost. The prepared severity is, in that sense, a mercy to those who have not yet refused. It keeps the warning honest.

**The Turn from "Them" to "You"**

*Fa-ittaqū Allāha yā ulī al-albāb alladhīna āmanū* — "So reverence God, O possessors of intellect, who have believed." The *fa-* is the *fa-* of result: therefore. Because a punishment has been prepared for insolent towns, therefore you — not they — are addressed. The sūrah has finished pointing at the ruined. It now puts its hand on the living community's shoulder. *Ittaqū Allāh*: have taqwā of God. The same taqwā that verses 2, 4, and 5 attached to way-out, ease, covering, and reward is now attached to the avoidance of prepared punishment. Taqwā is one posture with two faces. Toward promise, it is the key that opens. Toward warning, it is the care that does not step over the edge.

The addressees are given two titles, and the order of the titles is itself a teaching. First: *ulī al-albāb*, possessors of intellect. Then: *alladhīna āmanū*, who have believed. Intellect is named before faith, not because intellect is higher, but because the warning has just been historical, and history is offered to the mind. A person who can look at ruined towns and not draw the inference this verse draws has not used the *lubb* he was given. Faith then specifies which possessors of intellect are meant: those who have already believed. The sūrah is not, at this moment, preaching to the unconverted. It is asking believers to think.

**What "Possessors of Intellect" Means in the Qur'an's Own Usage**

*Ulū al-albāb* (and its sister *ulī al-albāb*) is a Qur'anic title of honor, appearing in a constellation of verses that the commentators treat as a single portrait. The phrase occurs in 2:179, 2:197, 2:269; 3:7, 3:190; 5:100; 12:111; 13:19; 14:52; 38:29, 38:43; 39:9, 39:18, 39:21; 40:54, among others. It does not mean "those who scored well in worldly cleverness." *Lubb* is the kernel, the inner essence of a thing as opposed to its husk. The possessors of intellect are those who possess true knowledge of the inner essence of things — so 5:100 and 39:9 are read in the commentary tradition. They are people who can tell the husk of a custom from the kernel of a Command.

Al-Rāzī and those after him note that the Qur'an attaches this title to people who remember, who reflect upon the heavens and the earth, who hear a reminder and follow the best of it, who understand that the inner of a legal limit is protection rather than deprivation. In this verse the title is applied to believers standing after a family-law revelation. The implication is almost startling: keeping the ʿiddah, housing the divorced woman, paying the nursing wage — these are acts of *lubb*. The man who thinks such acts are for the simple, while the sophisticated evade them, has inverted the Qur'an's ranking of minds. The true intellect is the one that can see a ruined town in a cheated waiting period, before the town is ruined.

**A Reminder Sent Down, Whose Identity Verse 11 Will Unfold**

The verse ends on a colon, in meaning if not in Arabic punctuation: *qad anzala Allāhu ilaykum dhikrā* — "God has certainly sent down unto you a reminder." *Qad* affirms. *Anzala* is again the verb of revelation, the same verb as verse 5's "He has sent down unto you." What was there called *amr*, Command, is here called *dhikr*, reminder. The two names are two functions of one descent. As Command, it binds. As reminder, it awakens. A community that has already heard the rulings of verses 1–7 does not need new information so much as it needs to be reminded that the information was from God.

Most commentators read *dhikr* here as the Qur'an itself, the Reminder sent down. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī note that, in relation to verse 11, it could also be read as a reference to the Prophet ﷺ, who is himself called a reminder in other passages, and whom verse 11 will name as "a Messenger reciting unto you the clear signs of God." The two readings are not enemies. The Qur'an is the reminder in speech. The Messenger is the reminder in person. God has not left the possessors of intellect with a bare warning about ancient towns. He has sent down, into their own midst, a *dhikr* that makes the warning present.

The ethics of being a possessor of intellect follow at once. An intellect that will not be reminded is only cleverness. The *lubb* that the Qur'an praises is an intellect that can be reached — that hears "therefore reverence God" after a history lesson and does not reply that history is for other people. Believers are capable of the town's insolence. That is why they, not the ruined, are being addressed. The severe punishment was prepared for "them." The reminder has been sent down to "you." The distance between those two pronouns is the distance taqwā is asked to keep.

**Intellect as the Faculty That Connects a House to a Cosmos**

There is a further reason this title appears here, at the hinge between family law and the cosmology of verse 12. A small mind treats a waiting period as a small rule. A *lubb* treats a waiting period as one instance of the Divine *amr* that also descends among the seven heavens (65:12). The possessors of intellect are those who can hold a household procedure and a universe in the same thought without finding the combination strange. That combination is this sūrah's distinctive gift. Verse 10 names the people capable of receiving it. Verse 11 will name the Messenger who recites it. Verse 12 will name the cosmos in which it is true.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:11

> **a Messenger reciting unto you the clear signs of God to bring those who believe and perform righteous deeds out of darkness into light. And whosoever believes in God and works righteousness, He causes him to enter Gardens with rivers running below, to abide therein forever. God has indeed prepared for him a beautiful provision.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reminder Takes a Human Voice**

Verse 10 ended with "a reminder." Verse 11 opens by naming what, or whom, that reminder is: *rasūlan yatlū ʿalaykum āyāti Allāhi mubayyināt* — "a Messenger reciting unto you the clear signs of God." The grammar in the dominant reading makes the Messenger himself the reminder sent down — not that he is uncreated speech, but that God sent him as the living *dhikr* who recites the sent-down speech. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both allow this identification. The other reading, that the reminder is the Qur'an and the Messenger is in apposition as its reciter, yields the same practical sense. What the possessors of intellect have been given is not a rumor about ancient towns. It is a Messenger, present, reciting *āyāt mubayyināt* — signs that make things clear.

*Yatlū* is recitation, the following of words in order, the same verb used for the Prophet's ﷺ public reading of the Book. *Mubayyināt* is the plural of the same word verse 1 used for "flagrant" indecency — *mubayyinah*, that which is made plain. The Book that requires four witnesses for a charge of indecency is itself a set of signs that do not love fog. Family law, eschatology, and cosmology are all recited as *mubayyināt*. A community that claims the rulings of verses 1–7 are unclear has not been listening to the reciter this verse names.

**Out of Darkness into Light: The Function Named Across the Book**

*Li-yukhrija alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti min al-ẓulumāti ilā al-nūr* — "to bring those who believe and perform righteous deeds out of darkness into light." The purpose of the recitation is not information for its own sake. It is extraction: *yukhrija*, to bring out, to lead forth, as from a well or a prison. The from-to is *min al-ẓulumāti ilā al-nūr* — from darknesses, plural, into the light, singular. Ibn Kathīr glosses the movement as from disbelief and ignorance to belief and knowledge. The plural darknesses is a Qur'anic habit: error is many, scattered, mutually contradictory; light is one. The same phrase is a refrain of the Book:

> *"God is the Protector of those who believe; He brings them out of darkness into light."* (Qur'an 2:257)

> *"There has come to you from God a light and a clear Book, by which God guides those who seek His pleasure to the ways of peace, and brings them out of darkness into light, by His leave."* (Qur'an 5:15–16)

> *"[This is] a Book that We have sent down unto thee, that thou mightest bring mankind out of darkness into light, by the leave of their Lord."* (Qur'an 14:1)

> *"He it is Who sends down upon His servant clear signs, that He may bring you out of darkness into light."* (Qur'an 57:9)

Sūrah 42 adds that scripture itself is made a light:

> *"Thou knewest not what scripture was, nor faith. But We made it a light whereby We guide whomsoever We will among Our servants."* (Qur'an 42:52)

In 65:11 the function is ascribed to the Messenger's recitation, which is exactly 14:1's ascription: the Prophet ﷺ brings people out by the Book, by his Lord's leave. Darkness, in the present sūrah's setting, includes the darkness of pre-Islamic divorce custom — women expelled, waiting periods uncounted, pregnant wives unsupported, infants used as leverage. Light includes the twelve verses of this chapter. A man who has heard this sūrah recited and then returns to those customs has not been failed by the Messenger. He has refused the extraction.

**Belief and Righteous Deeds as the Two Wings of the Exit**

The people brought out are not "those who believe" alone. They are *alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* — those who believe and perform righteous deeds. The pairing is one of the Qur'an's most frequent, and it is never accidental. Belief that does not become deed is a claim. Deed that is not rooted in belief is a husk. The extraction from darkness requires both wings. In this sūrah the righteous deeds have been specified with unusual concreteness: count the ʿiddah, do not expel, house according to means, spend upon the pregnant, pay the nursing wage, consult honorably, spend from what God has given. A believer who recites 65:11 with emotion and cheats 65:6 with his accounts has attempted to fly on one wing.

The verse then repeats the pairing in the singular, as a standing offer: *wa-man yuʾmin bi-Allāhi wa-yaʿmal ṣāliḥan yudkhilhu jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — "And whosoever believes in God and works righteousness, He causes him to enter Gardens with rivers running below." *Man* is whoever — man or woman, as the grammar of the promise throughout the Qur'an confirms (33:35; 16:97). The God who legislated the waiting period is the God who admits to Gardens. The continuity of the Subject is the sūrah's theology in miniature. Family law and Paradise are not two departments.

**Gardens, Rivers, Abiding, and the Echo of Earlier Promises**

The image — Gardens with rivers running below, abiding therein forever — is among the Qur'an's most repeated descriptions of the reward, appearing in 2:25, 3:195, 5:12, 48:5, 64:9, and many others. Its appearance here, after seven verses of divorce procedure and two of ruined towns, is a deliberate relocation of the stakes. The man who wondered whether housing his former wife was worth the cost is being told what God has decided the cost is worth. Not a social compliment. Gardens, rivers, *khālidīna fīhā abadan*, abiding therein forever.

The rivers running below are, in the commentary tradition, actual rivers of the Garden — water, milk, wine, honey, as 47:15 details — and they are also the image of a life that is no longer dry. The sūrah that spoke of measured provision and hardship (*ʿusr*) ends the believer's trajectory in a landscape of unmeasured water. The contrast with verse 9's *khusr*, loss, is complete. Two affairs, two sequels: loss that is tasted, and Gardens that are entered.

**A Beautiful Provision, Prepared as the Punishment Was Prepared**

*Qad aḥsana Allāhu lahu rizqā* — "God has indeed prepared for him a beautiful provision." *Aḥsana* is to make beautiful, to do excellently. *Rizq* is the same word as verse 3's provision from where he does not reckon, and verse 7's measured provision. The sūrah has now used rizq three times, in three registers. In verse 3, rizq is the unreckoned worldly gift that follows taqwā. In verse 7, rizq is the measured worldly gift from which the poor man spends. In verse 11, rizq is the beautiful provision of the Garden, prepared (*qad aḥsana*, already made excellent) for the one who believes and works righteousness. The word that named the nursing wage's economic anxiety is the word that names eternity's feast. God is one; provision is His; the scales differ.

Notice the parallel with verse 10: "God prepared (*aʿadda*) a severe punishment for them." Here: "God has made beautiful for him a provision." Both futures are prepared. The possessors of intellect are being asked to choose which preparation they will walk toward. The Messenger recites so that the choice is made in light, not in the darknesses from which he was sent to extract them.

**What "Clear Signs" Do to a Divorce**

It is worth asking, finally, what it means for the signs recited in a divorce-sūrah to be *mubayyināt*, making-clear. They make clear that the end of a marriage is not the end of moral life. They make clear that the child is not a weapon. They make clear that means, not spite, set the level of spending. They make clear that taqwā in this field is rewarded in this world and the next. And they make clear, by the very fact of a Messenger reciting them, that no one in the community can claim isolation from the knowledge. The signs have been recited *ʿalaykum*, upon you, over you. Darkness after that recitation is a chosen darkness.

The practical fruit is a kind of inner relocation. The believer in the middle of an ʿiddah is not merely enduring a legal inconvenience. He is, if he believes and works the righteousness these verses named, being brought out of darkness into light, and is walking toward a Garden whose provision has already been made beautiful for him. That is a different description of the same difficult weeks. The Messenger's recitation exists to make that description believable enough to act on.

## Sūrah al-Ṭalāq 65:12

> **God it is Who created the seven heavens, and from the earth the like thereof. The Command descends among them that you may know that God is Powerful over all things and that God has encompassed all things in knowledge.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Legislator of the Waiting Period Is the Creator of the Heavens**

The sūrah that began in a house ending a marriage ends in the creation of the seven heavens. *Allāhu alladhī khalaqa sabʿa samāwātin wa-min al-arḍi mithlahunna* — "God it is Who created the seven heavens, and from the earth the like thereof." The predicate opens with the Name, *Allāh*, as if to prevent any remaining doubt about who has been speaking for twelve verses. The One who said "count well the waiting period" is the One who created seven heavens. The juxtaposition is the sūrah's last and largest argument. A man who thinks a household ruling is too small for God to police has not considered the size of the God who is policing it.

The seven heavens are a Qur'anic given, named in a series of verses that this one joins:

> *"He it is Who created for you all that is on the earth. Then He turned to the heaven and fashioned them seven heavens, and He is Knowing of all things."* (Qur'an 2:29)

> *"The seven heavens and the earth and whosoever is in them glorify Him."* (Qur'an 17:44)

> *"Say: Who is Lord of the seven heavens and Lord of the Great Throne?"* (Qur'an 23:86)

> *"So He ordained them seven heavens in two days, and revealed in each heaven its command."* (Qur'an 41:12)

> *"Who created seven heavens in layers. You see no discrepancy in the creation of the Compassionate."* (Qur'an 67:3)

> *"Do you not see how God created seven heavens in layers?"* (Qur'an 71:15)

> *"And We built above you seven strong ones."* (Qur'an 78:12)

What is unique to 65:12 is the next clause: *wa-min al-arḍi mithlahunna* — "and from the earth the like thereof." This is the only verse of the Qur'an that alludes to seven earths, or seven lands, matching the seven heavens in number. The uniqueness is not a problem for the commentators. It is a door.

**Seven Earths: Layers, Climes, and a Hadith About Stolen Land**

The phrase "the like thereof" has been read in more than one responsible way, and the tradition did not feel compelled to collapse them into one. Al-Qurṭubī records the reading of seven lands, one upon another, corresponding to the seven heavens stacked in 67:3 and 71:15. That reading is supported by a ḥadīth whose authenticity is not in doubt. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever usurps a span of land unjustly, it will be made into a collar around his neck on the Day of Resurrection from the seven earths."* (al-Bukhārī 2452; Muslim 1610; cited by al-Qurṭubī on this verse)

The ḥadīth does two things at once. It confirms that "seven earths" is Prophetic language, not a commentator's speculation. And it attaches the cosmology to justice in land — which, in a sūrah about housing a divorced woman and not expelling her, is not a distant attachment. The man who seizes a span of earth unjustly meets, on the Day, a collar made of the seven earths he thought were too large to notice his span. The man who expels a woman from "her houses" (65:1) is in the same moral neighborhood: he has treated a dwelling-right as if no heaven were watching. The ḥadīth is the eschatological form of the sūrah's first prohibition.

A second reading, also recorded by al-Qurṭubī, takes the seven earths not as stacked layers but as seven lands spread across the earth — the seven climes or, in a later geographical vocabulary, the great continental divisions of the inhabited world. "The like thereof" then means a corresponding multiplicity of earthly realms, not a vertical geology. The Command that verse 12 says "descends among them" would, on this reading, descend across the breadth of the world, not only through its depths.

**Ibn ʿAbbās and the Verse He Would Not Fully Open**

Al-Ṭabarī preserves from Ibn ʿAbbās a sentence of extraordinary reserve:

> *"Were I to tell you of the interpretation of it, you would disbelieve, and your disbelief would be your denial of it."* (al-Ṭabarī)

The Companion most famous for Qur'anic interpretation refused, in this instance, to say all that he thought the verse contained, because he judged the listeners unable to bear it without denial. The refusal is itself a commentary. Not every truth about the cosmos is owed to every audience at every time. There is a taqwā of knowledge as well as a taqwā of action: to speak what will profit, and to withhold what will only provoke a rejection that then becomes a sin. Ibn ʿAbbās, who opened so many verses, closed this one partway, and the tradition honored the closing by recording it.

He is also reported, in a transmission cited by Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī, to have said:

> *"Seven lands: in each land there is a prophet like your prophet, and an Adam like Adam, and a Noah like Noah, and an Abraham like Abraham, and a Jesus like Jesus."* (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī)

The report has been received with the caution it requires. Its chain and its meaning have been discussed, and many scholars treated it as among the *isrāʾīliyyāt* or as a saying whose apparent sense is not the sense that binds belief. What can be said without straining is that Ibn ʿAbbās heard in "the like thereof" a multiplication of worlds vast enough that the prophetic pattern itself might be reflected across it — and that he also judged much of this territory unsafe for casual talk. The two reports together teach a posture: the verse is larger than a simple geography, and the believer is not required to map it beyond what the Prophet ﷺ mapped. The ḥadīth of the seven earths and the usurped span is such a mapping. Cosmological fiction is not.

**The Command Descends Among Them**

*Yatanazzalu al-amru baynahunna* — "The Command descends among them." *Yatanazzalu* is the form of repeated, ongoing descent — not a single drop but a continuing coming-down. *Al-amr* is again the sūrah's keyword: the Command of verse 5, the Command of the Lord and His messengers in verse 8, the Command God fulfills in verse 3. Here it descends among the seven heavens and the seven earths. 41:12 had already said that God "revealed in each heaven its command." 65:12 generalizes: the Command is the traffic between the levels of creation. Nothing in the stack is outside it.

Al-Rāzī and others heard in this descent the decrees of provision, death, and life, the angels moving with what they have been commanded, the revelation coming down to messengers. In the reading that takes the seven earths as a sign of revelation's universality, the descent is the sending of messengers to every community, as the Qur'an states elsewhere:

> *"We indeed sent a messenger unto every community: 'Worship God and shun false deities.'"* (Qur'an 16:36)

The two hearings — cosmological administration, and the universality of revelation — are two sides of one *amr*. The God whose Command runs the heavens is the God whose Command reaches every land, and is the God whose Command reached a divorcing household in Madinah and, by this sūrah, every divorcing household after it. There is no tier of creation, and no street of a city, in which the Command does not descend.

**"That You May Know": Knowledge as the Purpose of the Cosmos-Verse**

The verse then names its own purpose with a *li-taʿlamū* — "that you may know." Two knowledges are intended. First: *anna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr* — "that God is Powerful over all things." Second: *wa-anna Allāha qad aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilmā* — "and that God has encompassed all things in knowledge." Power and knowledge, *qudrah* and *ʿilm*, the two attributes that together make legislation meaningful. A legislator who knew but could not enforce would be a commentator. A legislator who could enforce but did not know would be a tyrant. God encompasses every thing in knowledge — including the uncounted day, the constricted house, the unpaid wage, the intention of the resumption — and is Powerful over every thing — including the opening of a *makhraj*, the sending of unreckoned rizq, the ruin of an insolent town, and the admission to Gardens.

*Aḥāṭa* is to encircle, to surround so that nothing is outside the ring. God's knowledge is not a searchlight that might miss a side-street. It is an encompassment. The waiting period that verse 1 asked human beings to count well is already counted in that encompassment. The "something new" that verse 1 said God might bring to pass is already known in it. The measured provision of verse 7 is known and the unreckoned provision of verse 3 is known. There is no private injustice. There is no unnoticed obedience. Encompassment is the theological ground of both fear and hope in this sūrah.

**Why a Divorce Chapter Ends with Metaphysics**

A reader trained to separate "practical" sūrahs from "theological" ones will find 65:12 a surprise. It is not a surprise inside the Qur'an's own mind. The Book does not believe that a rule can survive without a metaphysics. If God did not create the seven heavens and their like from the earth, if the Command did not descend among them, if He were not Powerful over all things and encompassing of all things in knowledge, then verses 1–7 would be advice. They are not advice. They are the local form of the same Command that runs the stacked heavens. The woman in her ʿiddah is living inside a cosmos, not inside a custom. The man who houses her according to his means is aligning a household with the administration of the worlds.

Ibn Taymiyyah's school would later insist, in the language of ʿaqīdah, that God's attributes of power and knowledge are real, unlimited, and not to be emptied by metaphorical evasion. This verse is one of their Qur'anic homes. *ʿAlā kulli shayʾin qadīr* is a total claim. *Aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilmā* is a total claim. Between them they close every gap in which a servant might hide a cheated count. They also open every gap in which a servant might hope: the God who encompasses also suffices (65:3), appoints a way out (65:2), appoints ease (65:4), covers sins (65:5), and brings ease after hardship (65:7). Unlimited power and unlimited knowledge are terror to the insolent town and relief to the reverent household.

**The Span of Land and the Span of a Life**

Return, at the end, to the ḥadīth that al-Qurṭubī placed beside this verse. A span of land, unjustly taken, becomes a collar from the seven earths. The image is almost unbearable in its proportion: the smallest theft measured against the largest earth. That proportion is the sūrah's proportion. A waiting period is a span of days. A room in a house is a span of walls. A nursing wage is a span of coins. Against the seven heavens and the seven earths these look like nothing. Against the Command that descends among the seven heavens and the seven earths they look like everything they actually are: instances of the *amr* that runs creation. To usurp them is to put on, in the unseen, a collar one cannot see yet.

The believer who finishes this sūrah is meant to walk back into the household — the one ending, or the one continuing, or the one he will someday have to end — with a different inner weather. He knows that the days can be counted because the Measurer has set a measure for all things. He knows that the house can be kept without constriction because the Creator of seven heavens is not too great to care who sleeps under which roof. He knows that the wage can be paid from a measured hand because provision is God's, and a beautiful provision has already been prepared. He knows that insolence has a taste and an end, and that intellect is the faculty that believes this before the taste arrives. And he knows, last, that he does not know — *lā tadrī*, verse 1 — what new thing God may bring to pass, because the Knower who has encompassed all things has not yet shown him tomorrow.

That is a complete education. It began with a divorce and ended with the seven heavens, and the Qur'an does not consider that a change of subject.
