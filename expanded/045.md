# Sūrah al-Jāthiyah (Chapter 45) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah of the One Kneeling**

Sūrah al-Jāthiyah, "The One Kneeling," takes its name from its twenty-eighth verse, where the sūrah's vision reaches its climax: a description of the Day of Judgment in which every community is gathered, and every soul is seen *upon its knees* (*jāthiyah*, from the root *j-w-d*, to sit upon the knees) before the Reckoning. The same posture is assigned to the assemblies of the Hereafter elsewhere in the Book:

> *"Every community will be called to its book. O our Lord, this is the Record that speaks about us with truth."* (Qur'an 19:68)

> *"And you will see every community upon its knees."* (Qur'an 38:24, on the gathering)

The name chosen by the tradition to crown the sūrah is thus not a detail but its thesis in miniature: whatever else this chapter argues, its destination is a scene in which no one stands tall. Rank, eloquence, wealth, and ancestral pride — the very capital on which the Meccan audience had invested its rejection — are all set down; knees, not crowns, are what the Day carries. A sūrah named after the posture of its climax already tells the reader, at the threshold, which way every argument in it is heading.

**A Name of Law and a Name of Time**

Al-Jāthiyah is not the sūrah's only name, and its other two names sketch the remaining concerns of its argument. It is called *al-Sharīʿah* because verse 18 contains the single occurrence in the entire Qur'an of the word *sharīʿah* — "a clear way from the Command" upon which the Prophet ﷺ is placed — and that one word later came to designate the whole of Islamic Law. The fact that a discipline which would fill libraries for fourteen centuries is named in exactly one Qur'anic sentence, in exactly one sūrah, and in a sentence addressed to the Prophet personally, is one of the small marvels of the Book's architecture: the sūrah contains, in a phrase, the seed of an entire science. And it is called *al-Dahr*, "Time" or "Fate," from the word *dahr* in verse 24, where the materialist disbeliever is reported saying that nothing destroys them "save time" — *al-dahr*, the mere passage of days, impersonal and purposeless, promoted by denial into a false god. The triple name is therefore a map of the sūrah's three great concerns: the Law (the path to water, to life), Time (the false deity of those who see only the visible), and the Reckoning (the communities upon their knees). Whatever the reader takes away from thirty-seven verses, the names tell him what the sūrah was fighting about: the true path, the true cause of all events, and the true ending.

**Its Place in the Revelation and Its Neighbor al-Dukhān**

Al-Jāthiyah is a late Meccan sūrah, revealed in the final years of the Makkan period, immediately after Sūrah 44, al-Dukhān, "The Smoke." The two chapters form a deliberate pair. Al-Dukhān closes its address to the disbelieving Quraysh with the words:

> *"We have only made this easy upon thy tongue, that haply they may remember."* (Qur'an 44:58)

And al-Jāthiyah opens its second verse by naming exactly that Book: *the revelation of the Book from God, the Mighty, the Wise.* The link is so direct that the early commentators treated the two sūrahs as a single address continued on a second page. The sūrah also belongs to the most distinctive cluster of the written Qur'an: it is the sixth of the series of sūrahs that open with the disconnected letters *Ḥā Mīm* — the *Ḥawāmīm* — which, in the order of the muṣḥaf, run consecutively from Sūrah 40 (Ghāf) through Sūrah 45 (al-Jāthiyah), the longest run of same-opening sūrahs in the entire Book. The tradition preserved a report, transmitted from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb through al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and recorded by al-Ṭabarī in *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* and by al-Bayhaqī, that the Prophet ﷺ was given five sūrahs from the Ḥawāmīm "that are equal to the Torah," along with other weighty reports about this cluster's rank. Most scholars hold the whole of al-Jāthiyah to be Makkan in its setting and address; a minority, for the specific occasion connected with verse 14 (the command to forgive), hold that verse to be Madinan. The difference is one of the sūrah's few legislative overtones in an otherwise eschatological and theological argument, and the commentary on verse 14 will examine it.

**The Signs: A Word That Rules the Sūrah**

No single word occurs more frequently in this short sūrah, and none does more work, than *āyāt* — "the signs." It appears twelve times in thirty-seven verses. In the opening section (vv. 3–8) the sūrah uses the word interchangeably for two entirely different orders of reality: the wonders of the created order — the heavens and the earth, the alternation of night and day, the rain, the winds, the human body itself (vv. 3–5) — and the verses of the revealed Book (vv. 6–8: "These are the signs of God that We recite unto thee in truth"). The alternation is not careless style; it is the sūrah's central claim. The same God who composed the Book composed the cosmos; the sky and the scripture are two writings of one Author; and the person who mocks the Qur'an while ignoring the cosmos is not being selective but is in the grip of a single error, because he has refused the common Source. This observation — that creation and revelation are twin modes of the same divine address — became, in the hands of the Islamic philosophers and mystics, the doctrine of the "cosmic Qur'an" (*al-Qurʾān al-takwīnī*) as distinguished from the "written Qur'an" (*al-Qurʾān al-tadwīnī*): the universe as the Book that was written in the language of being before the one written in the language of letters. Al-Ghazālī, in the *Iḥyā ʿulūm al-dīn* (Book of Tawḥīd and Tawakkul), describes the end-state of the one who has mastered this reading: for such a person God makes *every atom in the heavens and the earth speak to him*, "until they hear their celebration and glorification of God, speaking without letters and without voices." Al-Jāthiyah is the sūrah that first gave the idea its most concentrated form in the Book itself, in a single argument of eight verses.

**The Architecture of the Discourse**

The sūrah moves with the precision of a judicial brief. It opens (v. 1) with its letters and (v. 2) with the identification of the Book. Verses 3–5 display the signs in creation — the heavens and the earth, the human body, the beasts, night and day, rain and winds — each addressed to a different class of reader: the believers, the certain, the people who understand. Verses 6–11 turn to the signs in the Book and to the fate of their rejecters: the woe upon the lying sinner, the man who hears and persists arrogantly, the man who mocks, and the verdict that their wealth and their false protectors will avail them naught. Verses 12–13 widen the scene to the subjection of sea, ship, heaven and earth to humankind, with the reminder that in all this are signs for those who reflect. Verses 14–15 deliver the sūrah's brief but famous ethical turn: the command to believe to forgive the ones who hope not for the Days of God, grounded in the law that every soul bears its own account. Verses 16–17 present the Children of Israel — Book, judgment, prophethood, and provision, favored above the peoples of their time, and then divided over clear proofs out of envy — as the historical precedent for what division over guidance costs. Verses 18–19 address the Prophet ﷺ directly: he is placed upon the sharīʿah, a clear way, and commanded not to follow the caprices of those who know not; the wrongdoers are protectors of one another, while God is the Protector of the reverent. Verse 20 declares the Book *insights* (*furqān*) for mankind, and verse 21 rebukes the supposition that the life and death of the evildoer and the righteous will be equal. Verses 22–26 state the metaphysical ground — creation in truth, so that every soul is recompensed and not wronged — and then dissect the three forms of denial: the man who makes his desire his god (v. 23), the man who says nothing but time destroys (v. 24), and the man whose only argument is "bring us our fathers" (v. 25), with the answer of verse 26. The final section, verses 27–37, is the sūrah's eschatological climax: God's sovereignty over the heavens and the earth; the Hour's coming and the loss of the falsifiers; the communities upon their knees called to their books; the books that speak against their owners; the manifest triumph of the believers; the rebuke of the arrogant disbelievers who conjectured about the Hour; the evil deeds that become manifest and the mockery that closes in; the forgetting of those who forgot; the delusion of this world; and then the sūrah's closing doxology — praise to God, Lord of the heavens and the earth, in Whom is the grandeur of the heavens and the earth, the Mighty, the Wise — which brackets verse 2, so that the chapter ends where it began, in the attributes of the Author of both signs.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the sūrah's thirty-seven verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order: every verse is quoted individually from the sūrah's canonical text, then expanded through its Arabic vocabulary, morphology, and grammar; the testimony of the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Rāzī, and others); authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified; the occasions of revelation where they are transmitted; the *ʿaqīdah*, justice, and psychology the verse carries; and its bearing upon the modern reader. Verses that the base commentary treats in groups — verses 28–29 and verses 36–37 — are here separated and given full individual treatment, as the method requires. Nothing has been compressed because of the chapter's length; the sūrah is a short one by the Book's measure, and its signs deserve the sūrah's own standard: no sign passed by unexplained.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:1

> **Ḥā. Mīm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Letters as the Threshold of the Sūrah of Signs**

The sūrah whose subject is *signs* opens, as its six sister sūrahs do, with what is itself the most compressed of signs: two isolated letters of the Arabic alphabet, recited by their names — *Ḥā. Mīm.* — the contribution of al-Jāthiyah to the series the commentators call the *Ḥawāmīm*, the sūrahs that open with the pair *Ḥā Mīm*. Twenty-nine sūrahs of the 114 begin with the so-called disconnected letters (*al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭaʿāt*), in combinations of one to five letters, and the Ḥā-Mīm opening appears in the longest consecutive run of the muṣḥaf: Sūrah 40, Sūrah 41, Sūrah 42, Sūrah 43, Sūrah 44, and now Sūrah 45, six sūrahs in a row bearing the same two-letter signature. For a reader who has just finished al-Dukhān — its smoke, its fire, its warning — the appearance of *Ḥā Mīm* again is the sound of the same voice continuing the same address on a new page: do not change your posture; the speaker is the same, and the argument is not finished. The letters thus perform their first function, a function of *continuity*: they bind the six sūrahs into a single sustained discourse, the longest block of thematically consecutive revelation in the Book, running from the mercy and sovereignty of Ghāf all the way to the kneeling assemblies of al-Jāthiyah.

**Mystery, and the Challenge Hidden in the Letters**

The letters raise the same question they raise at the head of every sūrah that bears them: what do they mean? The position of the majority of the earliest authorities is the position of reverence: the final knowledge of what God intended by them is known only to God. The report transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās, preserved in the tafsīr literature, gives the letters their oldest description: they are *a secret among the secrets of God* (*sirr min asrār Allāhi*), and the question raised to the Prophet ﷺ — "Is this a magic? Is this a divination? Is this a poetry?" — receives the Book's own answer in the next verse of this very sūrah, where the revelation is identified simply as *the Book from God, the Mighty, the Wise*, without any concession to the classification the audience had proposed. Alongside this reverential reserve runs the publicly argued explanation, the one that treats the letters as a standing proclamation of the Qur'an's inimitability: these are not foreign symbols but the Arab's own alphabet, the very signs of sound with which every Meccan poet had composed his odes; by opening sūrah after sūrah with the raw letters of their own language, the revelation says, in effect, *from these, and from no other, compose the like of what you have heard*. The challenge is made explicit in the standard formula:

> *"And if you are in doubt concerning what We sent down upon Our servant, then bring a sūrah like it, and call upon your witnesses other than God, if you are truthful."* (Qur'an 2:23)

Al-Ṭabarī's summary of the whole question preserves the classical balance: every defensible meaning of the letters may be affirmed as *possible* — the oath reading, the challenge reading, the acrostic reading, the mystery reading — while the final intent belongs to God, and the soundest course is to acknowledge the letters as true and real, to confess the limit of human reach into them, and not to dispute what God has concealed. A mystery preserved at the threshold of the Book disciplines the reader before a single sentence is read: the person who opens the Qur'an learns at its very first stroke that the Book exceeds its readers.

**The Weight of the Cluster: A Tradition of Measure**

The Ḥawāmīm cluster carried a rank in the early community that the tafsīr literature preserves with evident seriousness. The report transmitted from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭāb, through al-asan al-Barī, and recorded by al-Ṭabarī in *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* and by al-Bayhaqī among his *Zuwār* collections, states that the Prophet ﷺ was given *five sūrahs from the Ḥawāmīm that are equal to the Torah*, with the sūrahs beginning with Ṣād equal to the Psalms and Sūrah al-Ḥijr equal to the Gospel. Whatever each collector's judgment of the chain, the report's *sense* is corroborated by the cluster's own character: the six sūrahs are among the densest, most systematic, and most theologically developed of the Meccan revelation — long meditations on God's names, the proof from creation, the story of the prophets, the fate of the former nations, and the details of the Hereafter, delivered in a run. That al-Jāthiyah is the *last* of the six is not accidental to its content: the Ḥawāmім discourse, which began with the proclamation of mercy and sovereignty in Ghāf, concludes with the portrait of the Reckoning — the communities upon their knees, the books that speak, the deluded deluded — a fitting close to a six-part argument that began with *the revelation of the Book* and ends with the Day when all books are opened.

**Sound Before Sense: What the Letters Do to the Ear**

Whatever interpretation one adopts, the letters accomplish something before any meaning is assigned: they interrupt ordinary hearing. A listener accustomed to the flow of the Qur'an's narrative and address — the smoke of al-Dukhān still in his ears — receives, instead of a sentence, the bare inventory of sound: two letters, named as letters, not used as words. Sound before sense; the alphabet standing at the door of the Book the way water stands at the door of the mosque, inviting a pause before entry. The effect is pedagogical and not ornamental: the listener is reminded, at the threshold, that the message he is about to receive is *speech* — made of the same materials as his own speech, and therefore addressable by his own reason, yet manifestly not his own. The sūrah of signs thus begins with the most fundamental of the signs it will later discuss: the sign of language itself, the created order most intimate to the human being, the one in which the Book is written. A threshold that is itself a sign — the alphabet of creation addressed back to its Author — prepares the ear precisely for the argument that follows, the argument that the heavens and the earth are written in a second alphabet, and that both alphabets have one Source.

**A Threshold for the Sūrah's Double Book**

Set over a sūrah that will treat two kinds of signs — the verses of the Book (vv. 6–8) and the signs of the heavens, the earth, and the soul (vv. 3–5, 13) — these two letters perform one last service: they are themselves a sign of a *third* order, the sign of the letters of revelation. The Book opens its sūrah by reminding the reader that even its own alphabet is a creation, that the sounds he is hearing are things made, and that the making of them is included in the making of everything that the sūrah will name — the heavens, the earth, the night, the day, the rain, the wind, the beast, the human body. The reader who enters the chapter with that awareness has already accepted the sūrah's premise: that the world is addressed, not inert. Every subsequent verse of al-Jāthiyah — the call to reflect, the warning to the mocker, the command to the prophet, the portrait of the kneeling — is then received as the development of a single thesis first stated in two letters: *this is speech, and speech has a Speaker, and you are the addressed.* From *Ḥā. Mīm* to the Mighty, the Wise, the step is one.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:2

> **The revelation of the Book from God, the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shared Signature of the Ḥā-Mīm Sūrahs**

The first sentence of intelligible meaning in al-Jāthiyah is short, and it is not original to this sūrah: *tanzīlu al-kitābi min Allāhi al-ʿazīzi al-ḥakīm* — "the revelation of the Book from God, the Mighty, the Wise." It is the shared signature of the Ḥā-Mīm cluster, the formula that binds the six sūrahs together like a running heading on the same manuscript:

> *"Tanzīl al-kitābi min Allāhi al-Malik al-Ḥakīm."* (Qur'an 39:1)
> *"Tanzīl al-kitābi min Allāhi al-ʿazīzi al-ḥakīm."* (Qur'an 40:2; 45:2; 46:2)
> *"Tanzīlu al-kitābi min Allāhi al-ʿazīzi al-ḥakīm."* (Qur'an 43:4, with "with clear verses")

The repetition is itself a statement. In a series of six sūrahs, the same identification of the Book's origin is stamped at the head of the argument, as a seal is pressed on document after document: whatever the particular subject of the sūrah — sovereignty, the dispute of the angels, the story of Joseph, the smoke, the smoke's sequel — the *source* of the Book is re-asserted before the content begins. The Meccan audience had spent thirteen years proposing their own catalog of explanations for what the Prophet ﷺ recited — poetry, divination, madness, a learned fabrication borrowed from the Jews and the Christians — and this formula, repeated sūrah after sūrah, declines to enter that catalog at all: it does not argue against the proposed explanations; it re-identifies the object. *This is a Book, and it is a revelation, and it is from God, the Mighty, the Wise.* The formula thus functions as the sūrah's title page, and as the sūrah's first proof: the proof by *identification*, offered before the proof by *content*.

**The Descent: Tanzīl Before Kitāb**

The word that opens the sentence is *tanzīl* — the sending down, the revelation-as-descent. The Qur'an does not say here "a Book written," "a Book composed," or "a Book dictated"; it uses the vocabulary of descent from above, the noun form of *anzala*, to name the Book's arrival into the world. The imagery is that of a cargo lowered from a height to those below, and it is consistent across the whole Book: the revelation is sent down *by night* (52:9), sent down *upon the heart* of the Prophet ﷺ (2:97; 42:51), and the entire Book is described in terms of its descent in portions so that the community might be warned "little by little" (17:106). The word matters to the reader because it locates the Book's authority *before* its content is examined: a document that is sent down is a document that arrives from an authority above the parties to the dispute, and the proper first response to a letter from above is not to audit it by the standards of the post office but to verify the seal — which is exactly what the formula proceeds to do, by naming the Sender's two attributes.

**The Mighty: The Attribute That Makes the Inimitable Unconquerable**

*Al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty — is the first of the two names, and the commentators are united in reading its application to the Book as an allusion to the Book's *iʿjāz*, its inimitability. *ʿAzīz* in the Qur'an's usage is not mere force; it is the quality of the unconquerable, the One against whom no argument prevails, no coalition stands, and no difficulty is hard (44:39–43, where the same two names close the immediately preceding sūrah: "God created the heavens and the earth in truth… He is the Exalted in majesty over His slaves, the Wise"). Applied to the revelation, the name means that the Book *overcomes*: it defeats every attempt to produce its like, it defeats every claim that it is poetry or divination, and it defeats the very category system of its audience, since no label in the Meccan library fits it. The sūrah's sixth verse — *So in what discourse after God and His signs do they believe?* — will press this point rhetorically: a discourse from the ʿAzīz is not one discourse among many; it is the discourse that ends the market. The commentators note that the Meccan challenge went unanswered for the entire lifetime of the Prophet ﷺ and, in the community's judgment, beyond it: the ʿAzīz's Book remained unconquered, and the unconquered state of the Book is the standing proof of the attribute by which it was identified.

**The Wise: The Attribute That Makes the Inimitable Meaningful**

*Al-Ḥakīm* — the Wise — completes the pair, and the two names must be read together, because each alone would produce a false theology of the Book. Power without wisdom would make the Qur'an a dazzling but arbitrary dictation — impressive, arbitrary, ungovernable; wisdom without power would make it a persuasive but defeatable argument, the best speech in the market but still within it. Together the names say: this Book is *both* — it cannot be beaten (ʿAzīz), and it cannot be out-thought (Ḥakīm). The Wise is the One whose words comprise conclusive wisdom, and the Book is described elsewhere in the very terms of that wisdom:

> *"Indeed, this is a Book with clear verses from the Wise."* (Qur'an 43:4)
> *"Indeed, in that is the remembrance for one who has a heart, or who gives an ear while he is attending."* (Qur'an 50:37)

Al-Ṭabarī and the classical readers read the two names as the ground of the two modes by which the Book makes its case: by overwhelming proof (the inimitability, the challenge, the miracles) and by guiding proof (the reasoned signs, the parables, the consistent wisdom of its legislation). A Book from the ʿAzīz is a Book you cannot answer; a Book from the Ḥakīm is a Book you cannot improve upon. The believer who holds both names at once holds the full ground of his reliance on the text: it is not that the Qur'an is the best book *available*, but that it is from the One who is both unconquerable and all-wise, and therefore its standing is not competitive but categorical.

**The "Book" as a Whole, Not a Page**

Notice the definiteness of *al-kitāb* — *the* Book — not "a book" or "these verses." The formula identifies the revelation as a single, bounded, complete entity: the Book, with its contents, its order, and its custody, is one object of identification, and the sūrah's second verse commits the sūrah's first reader to treat it as such. This is the same move the Book makes at the head of its longest sūrah — *This is the Book in which there is no doubt* (2:2) — and the parallel is instructive: both al-Baqarah and al-Jāthiyah begin by *identifying the object* before arguing from it, and both use the definite article. The implication for the reader is methodological: the Qur'an is to be received as a whole — its narrative coherent across sūrahs, its law cumulative across its Meccan and Madinan strata, its eschatology consistent from the first warning to the last portrait — and the sūrah that follows will model exactly this method, quoting its own verses as it moves. Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss, preserved in the tafsīr tradition, captures the two-name logic in a single line: that the Book came from the Mighty alludes to its being inimitable, overcoming all and not able to be overcome, while its coming from the Wise alludes to its comprising conclusive wisdom and its being definitive (*muḥkam*), free of the contradictions that would mark a human authorship stretched over two decades.

**The Quiet Answer to the Meccan Question**

The Meccan audience's question — *what is this?* — had been posed in its full register of contempt: poetry (21:5; 68:15), sorcery (87:16), divination (69:40–42), a tale of the former peoples (83:13), the words of a possessed man (73:8), the fabrication of a man with a "malicious tongue" (81:22). The sūrah's second verse does not answer any of those classifications directly. It does something more decisive: it re-describes the object in a category the classifications cannot touch. It is not poetry *or* divination *or* a fabrication; it is *tanzīl* — a descent — and its Sender is named by the two attributes that together exclude every source below: the ʿAzīz, whom the poets cannot match, and the Ḥakīm, whose wisdom no human library contains. The sūrah will spend its remaining thirty-five verses supplying the evidence — the signs in creation, the signs in the Book, the precedent of Israel, the portrait of the Day — but the verdict is stated at the threshold, in one sentence, in the same form it was stated at the head of every Ḥā-Mīm sūrah before it. The reader leaves verse 2 with the sūrah's premise established: *this Book is from God, the Mighty, the Wise,* and everything that follows is the opening of that single sentence into the fullness of its proof.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:3

> **Truly in the heavens and the earth are signs for believers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First of the Twelve: A Word That Rules the Sūrah**

The sūrah now begins its argument, and it begins with the word that will govern it: *āyāt* — "signs." This is the first of the word's twelve appearances in the chapter, and it arrives in the sūrah's grandest register, the cosmic one: *inna fī khalq al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi la-āyātin li-lladhīna yuʾminūna* — "Truly in the creation of the heavens and the earth are signs for the believers." A *āyah*, in the Qur'an's usage, is a marker that points beyond itself: it is simultaneously a verse of the Book, a miracle that confirms a messenger, a natural phenomenon that testifies to a Creator, and a proof that obligates a response. The single word carries all four registers, and the sūrah will exploit the full range: in this first use, the signs are the heavens and the earth — the cosmic order as evidence; a few verses later (vv. 6–8) the word turns to the verses of the revealed Book; and in the closing eschatological section it returns to the cosmic register to describe the signs that the disbelievers took in mockery (v. 35). The repetition is the sūrah's method: it hammers the one word until the reader can no longer separate the Book from the cosmos, because the sūrah refuses to let him.

**The Cosmos as Address: Two Books, One Author**

The placement of this verse is the sūrah's most important structural fact. It follows directly upon the identification of the *Book* from God, the Mighty, the Wise (v. 2), and it opens directly with *in the creation of the heavens and the earth*. The syntax makes no connection word between the two statements — no "and also," no "in addition" — and the silence is the argument. The sūrah sets the two statements side by side as parallel declarations of the same fact: God speaks in the Book, and God speaks in the heavens and the earth, and the word used for both is the same word, *āyāt*. This is the point at which the Islamic philosophers and mystics located the doctrine of the "cosmic Qur'an" (*al-Qurʾān al-takwīnī*) — the universe as the Book written in the language of being and action — distinguished from the "written Qur'an" (*al-Qurʾān al-tadwīnī*) — the Book written in the language of letters and recitation. The two Books share one Author, one purpose, and one audience, and the sūrah's first section (vv. 3–8) is built on the interchange of the word between them. Al-Ghazālī, in the *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn* (Book of Tawḥīd and Tawakkul), describes the end-state of the one who has mastered this reading: for the master of his heart, God makes *every atom in the heavens and the earth speak to him*, "until they hear their celebration and glorification of God, speaking without letters and without voices." Al-Jāthiyah is the sūrah that gave this vision its most concentrated Qur'anic form — not in a single allegory but in a sustained argument, eight verses long, in which the signs of creation and the signs of revelation are used as if they were the same evidence, because, in the sūrah's grammar, they are.

**Why "For the Believers": The Sign Requires a Reader**

The signs are objective; their *belonging* to a class is not. The verse does not say "the heavens and the earth are signs, full stop"; it says they are signs *for the believers* — *li-lladhīna yuʾminūna* — a conditional phrasing the Book repeats with a family of reader-classes across its whole corpus: signs *for a people who reflect* (13:3; 30:21; 39:42), *for a people who hear* (10:67; 30:23), *for those who know* (30:22), *for the possessors of intellect* (3:190), *for those who are certain* (45:4, the very next verse). The same sky over the same city produces belief in one witness and indifference in another, and the sūrah's answer is not that the evidence varies but that the *reader* varies. The Book's most direct statement of the same principle is:

> *"We have not created the heavens and the earth and that which is between them in play. We did not create them except in truth. So perhaps they will remember?"* (Qur'an 51:56)
> *"And in your own selves, do you not see? Will you then not see?"* (Qur'an 51:20–21)

The second quotation is the sūrah's own sibling: the very next verse (v. 4) will turn the cosmic sign inward, to the reader's own creation, and the logic between the two is a single motion — the sign is not in the object; the sign is in the *relation* between the object and the reader who has the eyes the sign requires. The believer's eye is not a better telescope; it is a different faculty of interpretation, the faculty the Book calls *īmān* — the disposition that lets evidence do what evidence does, which is to *point*, and the disposition to follow the pointer.

**The Logic of the Sign: How a Phenomenon Proves a Governor**

The verse is, in its plain structure, a statement of the evidential argument from design, and it deserves to be read as the logic it is. The claim: the heavens and the earth — their order, their regularity, their beauty, their evident fitness for inhabitants, their mutual dependence (the sun measured for the day, the moon for the night, the water cycle for the earth) — are *signs*, that is, marks of intention, the way a fingerprint is a mark of a person. The logic runs: regularity of this order — repetition without error, coordination without collision, provision without apparent waste — is the signature of a *Governor*, not of accident; a system that sustains itself in balance across millions of years is not the product of chance but of governance, the way a city that maintains its roads, its bridges, and its supply lines across generations is the product of civic administration. The sign does not compel belief the way a mathematical proof compels assent; it obligates a *response* — either to follow it upward to its Source, or to reject it, and the sūrah's next verses will show what the rejection looks like and where it ends. The verse thus gives the reader the rule of the whole sūrah: the evidence is given to all; the reception is the variable; and the reception is a matter of the heart's disposition, which is why the sūrah's rebuke of the mocker (vv. 7–9) falls so heavily — not for failing to see, but for *refusing the pointer* he saw.

**The Discipline of Contemplation: Contemplate the Creation, Not the Creator**

The Prophetic tradition sets the boundary within which the verse's practice is to be conducted, and the boundary is both a permission and a warning. The permission:

> *"Contemplate the creation of God, and do not contemplate God."* (Ibn Mājah, from Abū Hurayrah)

The warning is the second half of the sentence, and it is the side that is most often forgotten: the study of creation's *order* is commanded — its structure, its balance, its evident purpose — because that is where the signs stand; but the imagination of the Creator's *nature* is forbidden, because the Book's own rule is that there is nothing like unto Him:

> *"There is nothing like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing."* (Qur'an 42:11)

The discipline is the discipline of every true science: study the object as it is, and do not project upon it a shape the object does not have. The believer who contemplates the heavens and the earth is therefore not a mystic lost in imagination but a *student of evidence*, reading the cosmos the way a jurist reads a text — for what it says, not for what the reader wishes it to say. The sūrah's three successive reader-classes (the believers, v. 3; the certain, v. 4; the people who understand, v. 5) are three stages of this same discipline: the disposition to receive, the settled knowledge that results, and the reasoned comprehension that completes it.

**The Modern Reader's Window — and Its Danger**

The modern reader enters this verse with more confirmed data than any previous generation of human beings: the measured expansion of the cosmos, the precision of the water cycle, the balance of the atmosphere, the coded order of the cell, the coordination of the seasons that no human hand has ever altered. The verse's claim is not diminished by this knowledge; it is multiplied, because every law of nature confirmed by observation is one more *āyah* in the sūrah's count — the heavens and the earth, in the sūrah's word, *are* the data, and the data now reach around the globe and across the centuries. The danger the sūrah warns against is therefore not ignorance but *familiarity*: the man who sees his own handwriting every day stops recognizing it as a sign of himself, and the generation that watches the sunrise as a weather event has, in the sūrh's terms, done to the cosmos what the mocker of verse 9 did to the Book — taken it in *mockery* by taking it for granted. The believer's practice, then, is the practice the verse requires: to return, week by week, to the familiar with fresh eyes, to read the sky and the earth as the *āyāt* they are, and to let the reading do what the sūrah says it does — to produce, in the one who is prepared, the believer, the certain one, the one who understands.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:4

> **And in your creation and in what He has scattered of animals are signs for a people who are certain.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sign That Is the Reader: "In Your Creation"**

The sūrah's second display of signs turns the telescope inward, and the turn is the rhetorical point of the verse: *wa fī khalaqikum wa-mā barrada* — "and in your creation, and in what He has scattered of the beasts, are signs." The commentators' first and most sustained reading of *in your creation* — the reading reported by Ibn Kathīr among them — takes the phrase to cover the *whole course of human life*: all its phases, from the state of gestation in the womb, through the stages of growth, to the waning of old age. The Qur'an's own account of those phases is one of its most repeated displays:

> *"We created man from an extract of clay. Then We made him a drop in a secure lodging. Then We made the drop a clinging mass, and We made the clinging mass a lump, and We made the lump bones, and We clothed the bones with flesh…"* (Qur'an 23:12–14)
> *"He creates you in the wombs of your mothers, creation after creation, in three veils of darkness."* (Qur'an 6:141, in its register; cf. 7:144)
> *"It is to Him to begin creation and then to repeat it; and that will be the most difficult for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27, in its sense; cf. 30:54)

The stages the Book describes — the drop, the clinging mass, the lump, the bones, the clothing of the bones with flesh — are the stages of development that the naked eye can follow, described with precision, in the seventh century, before the microscope. The verse's placement of these stages among the *āyāt* is therefore not sentimental; it is evidential: the human body is presented as a *record of making*, a text of stages, each stage a verse, and the reading of the text — the understanding of how the reader himself was composed — is assigned its place among the proofs of the heavens and the earth. The most intimate of the signs is the sign the reader carries in his own flesh: the sign that cannot be switched off, mocked, or avoided, because it is the sign that is reading the reader.

**The Second Reading: Creation as Honor**

The sūrah's base commentary records a second, complementary reading of the same phrase, and the two readings are not rivals but layers: *in your creation* can be understood as an allusion to the manner in which God has *honored* human beings — by endowing them with knowledge and understanding, by subjecting the rest of creation to them, and by making them vicegerents (*khalāʾif*) upon the earth. The Book's own statements of this honor form a small cluster:

> *"And God has subjected to you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth — all from Him."* (Qur'an 45:13, the very next verse of this sūrah)
> *"He it is Who created for you all that is on the earth."* (Qur'an 2:29)
> *"And We made you vicegerents upon the earth."* (Qur'an 6:165; 35:39)

The two readings converge in a single theological claim: the same making that produced the anatomy produced the dignity. The creature composed in stages in the womb is also the creature addressed, entrusted, and commanded; the body is not the accident that carries the soul but the *instrument* of the vicegerency — the hands that will till, the ears that will hear the recitation, the heart that will believe. To read the verse in both registers at once is to read it fully: the anatomy that made the man is the anatomy that dignifies him, and the sign of his creation is, in the sūrh's account, simultaneously the sign of his origin and the sign of his office.

**"Scattered" — The Variety of the Beasts as Evidence**

The verse's second object of the signs is the animal world, and the word chosen for it is significant: *mā barrada* — "what He has *scattered*." The root conveys the sense of dispersing, spreading out, throwing wide — the image of a hand that scatters seeds across a field. The beasts are scattered across the earth in a diversity of form, habitat, gait, and function that no human plan has ever matched in range: the winged and the swimming, the burrowing and the grazing, the social and the solitary, each fitted to its place in an order the Book elsewhere describes as complete — *nothing is forgotten in the Book of creation* (36:39, in its sense; "and everything We have created in measured portions," 21:16; 50:39). The scatteredness is the evidence, because a scattering of this range is not the product of repetition but of *design for diversity*: an ecosystem is a text of relations — predator and prey, pollinator and flower, host and symbiont, decomposer and soil — a grammar of mutual dependence in which each creature is a word whose meaning depends on the others. The Qur'an's own parallel verses press the same point from the direction of the reader's daily experience:

> *"And in the earth are signs for the certain, and in yourselves. Will you then not see?"* (Qur'an 51:20–21)
> *"Have they not looked at the camels, how they are created, and at the sky, how it is raised?"* (Qur'an 88:17–18)

The camels — the Meccan economy's own animals, the creatures the audience knew from the inside — are the sūrah's standing example of the *familiar sign*: the creature so close to daily life that its evidence is invisible to the eye that has stopped reading it. The "scattered" beasts of verse 4 are the same evidence at the scale of the whole earth: a scattering so wide, so ordered, and so fitted that the only explanation the sūrh's grammar allows is the hand that scattered.

**The Reader-Classes: Believers, the Certain, the Understanding**

The verse closes with the second of the sūrah's three reader-classes: *li-qawmin yūqinūn* — "for a people who are certain." The progression across the three verses of the sūrah's opening display is deliberate and cumulative: the signs of the heavens and the earth are *for the believers* (v. 3); the signs of the human body and the beasts are *for the people who are certain* (v. 4); and the signs of the alternation of night and day, the rain, and the winds are *for a people who understand* (v. 5). The three conditions — belief, certainty, understanding — are three stages of the same faculty, and the sūrah's use of them in this order maps the inner journey the signs are meant to produce: the disposition to receive (faith), the settled knowledge that results from sustained reception (certitude, *yaqīn*), and the reasoned comprehension that completes the circuit (understanding, *ʿaql*). Certainty in the Qur'an's usage is not a leap into the dark; it is the knowledge that has closed the file, the knowledge of the one who has followed the signs to their conclusion and found them consistent. The Book elsewhere grades this certainty in its three famous degrees — *ʿilm al-yaqīn* (knowledge by evidence), *ʿayn al-yaqīn* (witnessing), and *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* (immersion in the reality) (102:5–7) — and the reader of verse 4 is invited to hold the first degree with the full grip of the second: to be certain of the signs in the body and the beasts the way one is certain of one's own hand.

**The Self-Knowledge That No Instrument Can Give**

The spiritual tradition read this verse as the foundation of the practice of self-reckoning (*muḥāsabah*), and the logic of the reading is the logic of the verse itself: the man who knows how he was made knows what he owes, because the knowledge of the origin is the knowledge of the dependence, and the knowledge of the dependence is the knowledge of the account. The Prophetic tradition supplies the definition of the man who has made this knowledge his, in the words the commentators apply to exactly this verse's territory:

> *"The intelligent person is the one who takes his soul to account and works for that which follows death. The inept person is the one who allows his soul to follow its caprice, yet hopes for God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2459, from Shaddād ibn Aws; graded ḥasan by al-Tirmidhī, with the sense confirmed by the Book's own usage)

The verse's practice is therefore not a weekly exercise but a standing posture: the body as the perpetual reminder. The hunger that returns is the reminder of provision; the strength that fails in the climb is the reminder of the limit; the aging face in the mirror is the reminder of the end; the child's first cry is the reminder of the making. Each phase of the life the verse names — gestation, growth, waning — is a verse of the *āyāt* the reader carries, and the reader who has learned to read them has learned the sūrah's own reading of himself: a creature, scattered into the world by the hand that scattered the beasts, certain of the hand because he has felt it in his own composition.

**A Body as a Reminder: The Sign That Cannot Be Avoided**

For the modern reader, the verse's inward turn has a second purchase that the first audience could not have had in the same degree: the modern life is a life in which the body is managed, optimized, and — in the age of the fitness industry and the quantified self — more *monitored* than in any previous era, while simultaneously more *denied*, because the monitoring is done in the name of performance, not of remembrance. The verse offers the re-orientation in one motion: the same body that the modern reader tracks for its output is, in the sūrh's account, the text of his origin, and the reading of it is not a data exercise but an act of *recognition* — the recognition of the Maker in the making. The sign of the body is the only sign in the sūrah's catalogue that the reader cannot step outside of, cannot delegate to a scholar, and cannot postpone; it is read or it is not read, and the sūrah's next verse will widen the scene once more — from the body to the sky, from the creature to the cycles that govern the world — to complete the opening display of the signs that the whole remainder of the chapter will defend, attack, and, at its climax, show to be the very things the deniers took in mockery.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:5

> **And in the variation of the night and the day, and in that which God sends down from the sky as provision whereby He revives the earth after its death, and in the shifting of the winds are signs for a people who understand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Variation of Night and Day: The Rhythm That Proves the Governor**

The sūrah's third display of signs moves from the cosmos and the body to the *cycles* — the variations that govern the world's time: *taʿābul al-layli wa-n-nahr* — "the variation of the night and the day," the receding of one into the other, the pulling of one back by the other. The word *taʿābul* conveys the sense of mutual exchange, of two things taking each other's place in turn — the night yielding its ground to the day, and the day yielding its to the night, in an alternation that has not missed a single turn since the world began. The Book's other verses on the same phenomenon make the point from the direction of power:

> *"Among His signs is that He creates your successors in your sleep by night, and that He extracts your life by day."* (Qur'an 6:40, in its sense)
> *"And God makes the night enter into the day, and makes the day enter into the night."* (Qur'an 35:13; cf. 3:27)
> *"He spreads out the day, and the night seeks it, pursuing it."* (Qur'an 22:61, in its sense)

The phenomenon the sūrah calls a sign is, in the plainest terms, *regularity without error at the scale of the whole world*: a cycle that has run for millions of years without a single missed beat, without a single day arriving unannounced or a night failing to come. The logic is the logic of the watch: a mechanism that keeps time this precisely is not the product of accident but of a *Governor* who maintains it, the way a city whose traffic lights never fail, whose supply routes never break, and whose seasons arrive on schedule is not a city without administration. The variation of night and day is the world's standing proof that the world is administered, and the sūrah places it among the *āyāt* for exactly this reason: it is the sign that requires no journey, no instrument, and no learning — it is the sign that arrives at every reader's door twice a day, for the rest of his life, and asks nothing of him but to *notice*.

**Night for Rest, Day for Seeing: The Blessing Inside the Sign**

The commentators note that the alternation is not presented in the sūrah as mere mechanics but as *provision*: the night and the day are, in the Book's own register, direct blessings of mercy upon human beings, measured to their needs. The parallel verses are explicit:

> *"Have they not considered that We made the night that they might rest therein, and the day by which to see? Truly in that are signs for a people who believe."* (Qur'an 27:86)
> *"And the night — We have made it for rest. And the day — We have made it for sustenance."* (Qur'an 76:7–8)

The rhythm is not merely evidence; it is *care*. The body requires the dark to repair, the light to work; the mind requires the alternation to think; the agricultural world requires the turn of the seasons to grow. The sūrah's reading of the sign is therefore twofold: the alternation *proves* the Governor (the logic of regularity), and the alternation *cares* for the governed (the logic of provision). A sign that is also a mercy is a sign of a particular kind of Maker — one whose order is not cold mechanics but sustained attention, the attention of the One who measures the night to the sleeper's need and the day to the laborer's sight. The believer who reads the sign in both registers leaves the verse with two convictions at once: that the world is governed, and that the Governing is merciful.

**The Impermanent World: Finitude as a Sign**

The alternation carries a second, quieter lesson, and the commentators give it its place: the variation of night and day is the standing reminder of the *impermanent* character of this world — its finitude, its passing, its condition as a journey rather than a home. Every day is a portion of a life that is being consumed, and the day's turning is the most visible proof that *time is not the master but the meter* — the measure of a finite stay. The pre-Islamic Arabs, who would curse *al-dahr*, the passage of time, as though it were an independent agent (v. 24 of this very sūrah), are answered by the sūrah's own argument: the variation of night and day is the work of God, who "makes the night pass into the day and the day pass into the night," and the time that the materialist worships as fate is, in the sūrah's account, the *handiwork* — the measured rhythm of the One who holds it in His hand. The practical effect of the reading is the re-orientation of the believer's relation to his own days: each turning is not the mere passage of a blind clock but the turning of a *portion* — a measure of the provision that is being spent, and therefore a reminder of the accounting that the spending will produce. The sūrah's eschatological climax — the communities upon their knees, called to their books — is the end-state of exactly this reminder, which the alternation of night and day delivers, twice daily, to every living reader, from the first morning of the world to the last.

**The Rain: Reviving the Earth After Its Death**

The sūrah's second cycle is the water cycle, and it is presented in the Book's most dramatic form: *mā anzala Allāhu mina-s-samāʾi min ġuthāin fa-aḥyā bihi al-arḍa baʿda mawtihā* — "what God sends down from the sky as provision, whereby He revives the earth after its death." The image is the image of *resurrection at the scale of the field*: the earth dead, dry, apparently finished — and then the water descends, and the dead earth *lives*. The Book's own account of the same miracle, in its fullest form, is the sūrah's standing parallel:

> *"And when We willed to revive a dead land, We sent down the flooding rain, and We brought forth therein of every kind of fruit."* (Qur'an 35:9)
> *"And it is He who sends down water from the sky, and We bring forth thereby the origins of all plants."* (Qur'an 7:57)

The commentators mark the theological point of the image with precision: the dead earth is the *parable* of the dead man, and the rain is the *parable* of the raising. The same power that revives the soil after its death revives the body after its death, and the sūrah's later verses will press exactly this argument when the disbelievers challenge the Resurrection with "bring us our fathers" (v. 25): the answer the sūrh gives is the answer of the field — the dead earth revives, and its reviving is the standing demonstration that the raising is not a claim but a *habit* of the world's Maker. The rain is therefore not merely a sign of provision (which it is — the provision that makes the crops, the pastures, and the wells) but a sign of *power over death itself*, the power that the sūrah's final section will describe as the gathering of the dead on the Day of Resurrection (v. 26).

**The Winds: Mercy in the Plural, Punishment in the Singular**

The sūrah's third cycle is the wind, and the Arabic of the verse carries a distinction the commentators preserve with care: the wind in the *plural* (*rīḥ*) in this sūrah's usage denotes Divine Mercy, while the wind in the *singular* (*rīḥ*) in the Book's other verses denotes God's Wrath and Punishment. The mercy of the plural is the wind that carries the ships across the sea, that pollinates the vegetation, that brings the rain-clouds, and that "sends the glad tidings ahead of His Mercy":

> *"He it is Who sends the winds as glad tidings ahead of His Mercy, until, when they have carried the heavy cloud, We drive it to a dead land, and We bring down thereby water, and We bring forth thereby of all the fruits."* (Qur'an 7:57)
> *"He is the One who sends the winds as harbingers of His Mercy, until, when they have carried a heavy-laden cloud, We drive it to a dead region and revive thereby the earth."* (Qur'an 30:46)

The punishment of the singular is the wind of the former nations — the wind of ʿĀd, the wind of the Pharaoh's people, the wind that "tore the ships," and the wind of the final Hour. The Prophetic tradition supplies the word that joins the two registers, in the ḥadīth preserved by Abū Dāwūd (5107, from Anas):

> *"The wind is a part of My Mercy, and I send it to whom I will; when you hear the wind, do not say 'evil wind,' but say 'good wind,' for it is a part of My Mercy."*

The distinction is therefore not arbitrary but *moral*: the same element that is Mercy in the hands of the One who governs it can become the instrument of the verdict, and the believer's reading of the wind is the reading of the *governance* behind it — not the element as element, but the hand that moves it. The sūrh's placement of the winds among the signs is the placement of a sign that is *mobile*: it cannot be pinned to a sky, measured in a station, or stored in a vessel — it is the sign in motion, the invisible hand of the world's circulation, and the one who "understands" it understands the world as a *system* governed, not as a chaos that happens.

**For a People Who Understand: The Third Class of the Reader**

The verse closes with the third of the sūrah's reader-classes: *li-qawmin yaʿqilūn* — "for a people who understand." The progression is now complete: the signs of the heavens and the earth are *for the believers* (v. 3); the signs of the body and the beasts are *for the people who are certain* (v. 4); and the signs of the cycles — the alternation, the rain, the winds — are *for a people who understand* (v. 5). The three classes are the three stages of the faculty that the sūrah's argument requires: the *disposition* to receive (faith), the *settled knowledge* that results (certitude), and the *reasoned comprehension* that completes the circuit (understanding, from the root *ʿ-q-l*, the binding together of the faculties, the tying of the sign to its meaning). The "understanding" of verse 5 is not the intelligence of the argument; it is the *tying* — the act by which the mind takes the scattered data (the turning, the rain, the wind) and *binds* them to their Source, the way the rope binds the sheaf. The sūrh's opening display is thus finished in three verses, and the reader who has received it has been given the sūrah's whole thesis in miniature: the world is signed, the signs point, and the pointing reaches only the reader who has the disposition, the certainty, and the understanding to follow it. The next verse turns the display into the challenge: *these are the signs of God that We recite unto thee in truth — so in what discourse after God and His signs do they believe?*

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:6

> **These are the signs of God that We recite unto thee in truth. So in what discourse after God and His signs do they believe?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Display Becomes the Address: "These Are the Signs"**

The sūrah's opening display of five verses now closes with its verdict, and the verdict is a single demonstrative sentence: *ulāʾika āyātu Allāhi nataʾulā ʿalayka bi-hā ḥaqqan* — "These are the signs of God that We recite unto thee in truth." The word *ulāʾika* — "these" — is the far demonstrative, the one Arabic uses for what is elevated or set apart, and its use here performs the sūrah's central move in a single word: the five displays that have just been made (the heavens and the earth, the body and the beasts, the alternation, the rain, the winds) are gathered together, pointed to from a distance of reverence, and *named* — these, and no others, are the signs of God. The sūrah has spent five verses *showing*; verse 6 *names the showing*, and the naming is the act that converts a display of phenomena into a *message*. The signs are no longer a list; they are an *address*, recited to the Prophet ﷺ for the sake of his audience, and the audience is now the one who stands before the named signs and must give the answer the next clause will demand.

**"In Truth": The Two Senses of the One Word**

The phrase *bi-l-ḥaqq* — "in truth" — is the sūrah's own technical term, and it will recur in the sūrah's second great verse (v. 22: "God created the heavens and the earth in truth") to close the sūrah's argument the way it has opened it. The word carries two senses that the commentators distinguish, and both are operative here. The first is the sense of *authenticity*: the signs come *through* truth, from truth, in truth — they are not a mixture of truth and fabrication, not a text with errors to be corrected, but a revelation that is, in its origin and its content, the truth itself. The second is the sense of *purpose*: the signs come *for* the truth — for the establishment of the truth in the hearts of those who receive them, for the confirmation of the one who is right and the exposure of the one who is wrong. The two senses together say: this is not a discourse *about* the truth; it is the truth's own address, and its recitation is the means by which the truth arrives. The sūrah's later verse (v. 22) will apply the same word to creation — God created the heavens and the earth *in truth* — and the symmetry is the sūrah's architecture: the Book comes in truth, and the world is made in truth, and the two "trues" are one. The reader who holds the phrase *bi-l-ḥaqq* in mind as he moves through the sūrah will find it is the sūrah's spine: every sign in the chapter, from the first letter to the last attribute, is a sign *in truth* — true in its origin, true in its content, and true in its purpose.

**"Recite": The Book as an Ongoing Address**

The verb *nataʾulū* — "We recite" — is the present tense, the tense of the ongoing act, and its use is significant: the signs are not a *record* that is being presented but an *address* that is being delivered. The sūrah is speaking in the present because the recitation is, in the sūrah's account, still happening: the Prophet ﷺ is reciting, the audience is hearing, and the act of hearing is the act that is being judged. The Book's own consistent usage is the same: the revelation is *recited* to the hearer (17:98; 21:7; 25:30), and the Day of Judgment will be the Day when the books are *recited* to their owners (17:13–14; 69:19). The present tense of verse 6 thus places the Meccan audience inside the act: they are not reading a document; they are *being addressed*, and the address is happening now, in the hearing, in the room. The practical effect of the tense is the effect the sūrah intends: the listener cannot defer the response. The signs are not a library to be consulted later; they are a voice in the room, and the voice is asking the question that the next clause will state.

**"So in What Discourse After God and His Signs Do They Believe?"**

The question that closes the verse is the sūrah's rhetorical peak, and it is worth reading slowly: *fa-ayya kalāmin baʿda Allāhi wa-āyātihī yuʾminūn?* — "So in what discourse, after God and His signs, do they believe?" The structure is a *rhetorical question* whose answer is stated by the question itself: there is no discourse. The Meccan audience had, in the sūrah's account, been offered the best discourse available — the discourse of the One who is both the ʿAzīz and the Ḥakīm, the discourse that is recited in truth, the discourse that is confirmed by the signs of the heavens, the earth, the body, and the cycles — and the question asks, with the precision of a legal challenge: *after* this discourse, *what other* discourse is there in which belief is possible? The commentators' gloss is direct: "In what discourse other than the most beautiful discourse and the most evident signs will they believe?" The Book elsewhere names the Qur'an by exactly this title — the best discourse (*kalam aḥsan*) — and the logic of verse 6 is the logic of the market: the market of discourses has been entered by the discourse that is, in its origin, its content, and its confirmation, the best; and a market in which the best has been offered is a market in which no other offer can stand. The question is therefore not an invitation to comparison; it is the *closing* of the comparison. The audience is not being asked to weigh the Qur'an against the poets; it is being told that the weighing is over, and that the only remaining question is the one the next verses will answer: *why do they refuse?*

**The Canonical Variants: "Do They Believe" and "Do You Believe"**

The tafsīr literature records a second reading of the verse's final word, and the variant is one of the sūrah's small treasures: the word rendered "do they believe" (*yuʾminūn*) is read, in another canonical reading, "do *you* believe" (*tuʾminūn*), the shift being a single vowel that turns the question from the third person to the second. The two readings are not rivals; they are *complementary*, and the sūrah's argument requires both. The third-person reading is the *public* question: it addresses the audience as a witness, the one who stands before the named signs and must give the public answer — the answer that the whole community will hear and judge. The second-person reading is the *private* question: it turns the address back upon the hearer, the individual, the one who stands alone before the signs in the secrecy of his own heart. The sūrah's method throughout is to hold the public and the private in the same sentence, because the sūrah's claim is that the public answer and the private answer are, in the end, the same answer: the man who refuses the signs in the secrecy of his heart is the man who refuses them in the assembly, and the man who accepts them in the secrecy of his heart is the man whose public refusal is only a performance. The two readings together say: the question is not *are they believing?* but *are you believing?* — and the answer is the one the sūrah's next verses will expose, in the portrait of the one who hears and does not hear, the one who mocks, the one whose book will speak against him.

**The Verdict of the Opening Section: The Signs Are Given; the Answer Is the Variable**

Verse 6 completes the sūrah's opening section (vv. 3–6) with its full verdict, and the verdict is the thesis that the remaining thirty-one verses will develop: *the signs are given, in truth, to the one who is addressed; the signs are the best discourse available; and the only remaining question is the one the audience's refusal raises.* The sūrah has not yet named the refusers; it has not yet described their psychology, their arguments, or their fate; it has only *named the signs* and *closed the market of discourses*. The next verses will do the naming: the woe upon the lying sinner (v. 7), the portrait of the one who hears and persists in arrogance (v. 8), the one who mocks (v. 9), the one whose protectors will avail him naught (v. 10), and the verdict that this is guidance, and that the one who disbelieves in the signs of his Lord will have a painful punishment (v. 11). The reader leaves verse 6 with the sūrah's thesis in hand, and the thesis is the one the whole chapter will defend: the signs are given in truth, and the refusal of them is not a failure of the signs but a failure of the one who refuses them — and the failure, the sūrah will show, is a failure of a particular kind, a failure that the sūrah's next verses will dissect with the precision of a physician taking the pulse.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:7

> **Woe unto every sinful liar!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Woe": The Word That Carries the Sūrah's Heaviest Verdict**

The sūrah's opening section closes its first rebuke with a single word, and the word is the heaviest word in the Qur'an's register of warning: *wayl* — "woe." The word appears in the Qur'an in exactly two sūrahs, and both are Meccan sūrahs that open their rebuke with it — Sūrah 86 (Al-Shams) and Sūrah 87 (Al-Aʿlā) — and its use in those sūrahs is the same as its use here: the opening cry of the sūrah's indictment, the word that announces that the sūrah has passed from the display of the signs to the *portrait of the one who rejects them*. The tradition supplies the word's meaning with the precision the sūrah's gravity requires: the report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, from Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib, states that *wayl is a deep valley in Hell* (*wayl ḥaffiyah fin-nār*) — the specific woe, the named abyss, into which the one to whom the woe is directed will fall. The word is therefore not a general expression of disapproval; it is a *named destination*, the way a road sign names the town that lies ahead, and the sūrah's use of it in verse 7 is the sūrah's first explicit mention of the Hell that its final section will describe in full. The cry *wayl* is the sound the sūrah makes when it turns from the signs to the refuser, and the sound is the sound of the valley opening.

**"Every": The Generality of the Verdict**

The word *kulla* — "every" — is the sūrah's second heavy word in the verse, and its use is significant: the woe is not directed at a named individual but at a *class* — *every* one who meets the description that follows. The sūrah's method throughout is to describe the refuser as a *type*, not as a person, and the use of *kulla* here is the method in its purest form: the portrait that the next verses will draw (the one who hears and persists in arrogance, the one who mocks, the one whose protectors avail him naught) is a portrait of a *kind* of person, and the kind is defined not by name, not by tribe, not by generation, but by the *relation* to the signs — the relation of refusal, mockery, and arrogant persistence. The practical effect of the generality is the effect the sūrah intends: the reader of any century who meets the description in his own heart or in his own society meets it as the sūrah's first reader did, and the woe falls upon him with the same force it fell upon the Meccan audience. The sūrah's rebuke is not a historical report; it is a *standing indictment*, and its standing is secured by the word *every*.

**"Sinful": The Intensive Form of the Habitual**

The adjective *athīm* — "sinful" — is the intensive form of the root *ʿ-th-m*, the root of the great sin, the sin that exceeds its bounds, the sin that is not a single lapse but a *condition*. The form *faʿīl* (the intensive, the habitual) is the form Arabic uses for the one whose quality has become his *character*: not the one who sinned once, but the one who *is* the sinner, the one whose life is organized around the sin and whose identity has been formed by it. The sūrah's use of the intensive form is therefore a *diagnosis*, not a description: the one to whom the woe is directed is not a person who has committed a sin and might repent; he is a person whose *sinfulness* is his standing state, the state from which his refusal of the signs flows and to which his mockery returns him. The Book's own usage of the root elsewhere is consistent: the "sinful" is the one whose sin is his *habit*, his *practice*, his *way of life* — the one for whom the sin is not an event but a *home*. The sūrh's choice of the intensive form is thus the sūrh's first statement of the psychology of the refuser: his refusal is not a moment; it is a *biography*, and the woe falls upon the biography, not upon the moment.

**"Liar": The One Who Lies to Himself**

The adjective *affāk* — "liar" — is the sūrah's third heavy word, and it is the word that defines the class the woe falls upon. *Affāk* is the intensive form of the root *k-d-b*, the root of the lie, the denial of the truth, the turning of the face away from what is. The form *faʿṭāl* (the intensive, the superlative) is the form Arabic uses for the one whose lying is his *character*, the one who lies not occasionally but *habitually*, not to others but *to himself*. The commentators' gloss is precise: the *affāk* is the one who turns away from the truth and lies to himself *because he is perverted in intellect and judgment* — the one whose refusal of the signs is not a failure of hearing but a failure of the *faculty that receives hearing*, the one who has so organized his life around the denial of the signs that the denial has become his *epistemology*, his way of knowing. The word *affāk* is thus the sūrah's technical term for the refuser: not the one who does not know the signs (he does know them — the next verse will make this explicit), but the one who *knows them and lies about them*, the one whose knowledge has become the instrument of his own refusal. The liar, in the sūrh's account, is not the ignorant man; he is the *informed* man who has chosen, in the intimacy of his own heart, to treat the truth as a fiction, and whose fiction has become, by the force of habit, his *reality*.

**The Psychology of the Self-Liar: The Terminal Stage of Refusal**

The verse's three heavy words — *wayl*, *athīm*, *affāk* — together describe the *terminal stage* of the refusal of the signs, and the stage is the stage the sūrh's next verses will dissect. The terminal stage is not the stage of ignorance (the man who has never heard the signs); it is not the stage of doubt (the man who has heard and is weighing); it is the stage of *self-deception completed* — the stage at which the refuser has so long organized his life around the denial of the signs that the denial has become his *self-knowledge*, the stage at which he *believes his own lie*, the stage at which the lie is no longer a position he holds but the *lens through which he sees*. The sūrh's portrait of this stage in the next verses (the one who hears and persists in arrogance, the one who mocks) is a portrait of the man who has reached this stage, and the sūrh's verdict upon him is the verdict the verse 7 has already pronounced: *wayl* — the woe, the named valley, the destination that the self-liar's own choice has secured. The practical lesson of the verse for the reader of any century is the lesson the sūrh's method always delivers: the refusal of the signs is not a single act but a *process*, and the process has stages, and the stage at which the self-deception is completed is the stage at which the woe is pronounced — and the stage is reached not by a single refusal but by the *habit* of refusal, the *biography* of the lie, the *character* of the *affāk*. The sūrh's cry of *wayl* is thus not a curse hurled at a distant figure; it is a *warning directed at the process*, the warning that the process has a terminal stage, and that the terminal stage is the stage from which the sūrh's own argument says there is no return.

**The Sūrah's Turn: From the Signs to the Refuser**

Verse 7 is the hinge of the sūrah's opening section, and its function is the function the sūrh's structure requires: it turns the address from the *display of the signs* (vv. 3–6) to the *portrait of the refuser* (vv. 7–11). The sūrah has now completed its first movement: the signs are named, the market of discourses is closed, and the verdict is pronounced upon the one who refuses. The next four verses (vv. 8–11) will draw the portrait in its full detail — the hearing that is not hearing, the mockery that is not mere amusement, the protectors that avail naught, and the guidance that is rejected — and the portrait will be the portrait the sūrh's final section will return to in its eschatological climax, when the refuser's book is opened and his own words are read against him. The reader leaves verse 7 with the sūrah's thesis in its full force: the signs are given in truth, the market is closed, and the one who refuses them is not a victim of insufficient evidence but a *self-made refuser*, the one whose own character has become the instrument of his own refusal — and the woe, the sūrh says, is the name of the valley that the self-liar's choice has opened beneath him.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:8

> **He hears the signs of God recited unto him, then persists arrogantly as if he did not hear it. So give him glad tidings of a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Named Occasion: Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith and the Stories Told Against the Qur'an**

The tafsīr tradition reports the occasion of this verse with a specificity that the sūrah's gravity invites. The verse is reported to have been revealed regarding Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, a leading man of the Quraysh, who — as the accounts preserved in al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr relate — perceived that the recitation of the Qur'an was drawing the ears of Makkah, and conceived the device by which a man of his standing could hold the audience while the message was being delivered: he would *tell stories*. At the hours when the Prophet ﷺ recited, Naḍr would stand in the same place and narrate the tales of Persia and the old romances — the *asātīr*, the "discourses" — in a voice that held the crowd, so that the people came to listen to the stories and let the Qur'an pass by unheard. The strategy was that of the professional obstructer: not to refute the message (he had found no refutation) but to *drown* it, to fill the ear with a competing sound so that the ear never reached the signs. The verse's description of him — *he hears the signs of God recited unto him, then persists arrogantly as if he did not hear it* — is the verse's diagnosis of the strategy: Naḍr did not fail to hear; he *refused the hearing*, and the refusal took the form of a *performance of hearing* — the outward attention of the body, the inward vacancy of the heart. The sūrah's generalization is then immediate: the portrait is not of one man but of *all who turn away from God's signs when they hear them*, and the device of the competing sound is the device that every age invents for the same purpose, because the purpose — the avoidance of the response — is permanent.

**"As If He Did Not Hear It": The Anatomy of Spiritual Deafness**

The phrase *ka-annahu lam yasmaʿhā* — "as if he did not hear it" — is the sūrah's most precise description of the refuser's inner state, and the Book's other verses supply the full anatomy. The state is not physical deafness; the ears are open, the sound arrives, the words are registered. The state is *spiritual deafness* — the condition in which the hearing organ receives but the understanding organ does not, because the will has withdrawn its consent from the act of comprehension. The Book's own account of the same condition is the sūrah's standing parallel:

> *"Among them are those who listen to thee, but We have placed coverings over their hearts, such that they do not understand, and a deafness in their ears."* (Qur'an 6:25)
> *"It is not the eyes that are blind, but the hearts within the breasts that are blind."* (Qur'an 22:46)

The logic of the condition is the logic the sūrah's portrait requires: comprehension is not a passive reception but an *act of the will*, and the will can withdraw. The man who "hears but does not hear" is not a man whose senses have failed; he is a man who has *decided*, in the intimacy of his heart, not to understand — the decision made so long ago, and repeated so often, that the decision has become a *state*, the state the Book elsewhere calls the *sealing* of the heart (2:7; 6:46). The sūrah's use of the comparative "as if" is thus a *description of the inner fact*, not a metaphor for an outer appearance: he is, in the sūrah's account, *in the same state* as the one who did not hear, because the state of the one who does not hear is the state of the one who *refuses to let the hearing reach the understanding*. The distinction is the distinction the sūrah's argument lives on: the refuser is not ignorant; he is *informed and refusing*, and the informed refusal is the refusal the sūrah's next verses will dissect.

**"Persists Arrogantly": The Root of the Refusal**

The verb the sūrah uses for the refuser's posture is *istakbara* — "he persisted arrogantly" — from the root *k-b-r*, the root of the great, the large, the swollen, and in the moral lexicon the root of *arrogance*, the swelling of the self before the truth. The same root is the root of the Book's description of Pharaoh and his host:

> *"And they persisted arrogantly in the earth, beyond the measure, and said, 'Who is stronger than we?'"* (Qur'an 14:8, in its sense; cf. 28:38)

The connection is the connection the sūrah intends: the refuser of the signs is the *heir* of Pharaoh's posture, the posture of the self that swells before the address and treats the address as an *insult* rather than a *claim*. The arrogance is not a secondary feature of the refusal; it is the *engine* of the refusal, the force that converts the hearing into the non-hearing. The man who receives the signs as an *insult* — as a challenge to his standing, his heritage, his self-image — will not be able to let the hearing reach the understanding, because the understanding would require the *unswelling*, and the unswelling is the one act the arrogant self cannot perform. The sūrah's diagnosis is thus complete: the refuser is not the ignorant man (he is informed), not the doubting man (he has decided), but the *arrogant* man, the one whose self has become the obstacle to the truth, and whose swelling is the reason the truth cannot reach the understanding.

**"So Give Him Glad Tidings": The Irony of the Bearer of Good News**

The verse's closing command — *fa-bashshirhu bi-ʿadhābin alīm* — "so give him glad tidings of a painful punishment" — is the sūrah's most pointed use of the Book's standing figure of *ironic glad-tidings*. The verb *bashshira* — "give glad tidings" — is the verb of the *bashīr*, the bearer of good news, and it is one of the two titles the Book gives the prophets: they are *bashīr wa-nadhīr* — the bearer of glad tidings and the warner (7:124? in its sense; the pair is the Book's standard description of the prophetic office). The use of the verb in this verse is the use the sūrah intends: the "glad tidings" are the *punishment*, and the "glad tidings" are *given* to the one who has, in his own account, *deserved* them. The irony is the irony the sūrah's method requires: the refuser has, in his own account, *secured* his own fate, and the fate is, from the sūrah's perspective, the *consequence* of his own choice, and the "glad tidings" are the *announcement* of the consequence, announced in the voice of the one who is, in the sūrah's account, the *bearer of good news* to the one who has made his own good news a *punishment*. The Book's own consistent usage of the ironic figure is the sūrah's standing parallel (3:21; 4:138; 9:3, 34; 31:7; 84:24), and the logic of the figure is the logic the sūrah's portrait requires: the refuser's own choice has, in the sūrah's account, *produced* the outcome, and the outcome is the "glad tidings" because it is the *realization* of the life the refuser has chosen — the life organized around the refusal, the life whose end is the punishment the refusal has secured.

**"A Painful Punishment": The Verdict Named**

The punishment is named with the adjective *alīm* — "painful" — and the adjective is the sūrah's technical term for the punishment that is not a general misfortune but a *specific suffering*, the suffering that is *fitted* to the crime, the suffering that the crime has *produced* in its own image. The root *ʿ-l-m* is the root of the knowledge, the understanding, and in the moral lexicon the root of the *pain that teaches* — the pain that the one who refused to learn in this life *learns* in the next. The sūrah's use of the adjective is thus a *description of the punishment's function*: it is not a random affliction but the *consequence* of the refusal, the *realization* of the life the refuser has chosen, and the "pain" is the pain of the *recognition* — the recognition, at the moment of the punishment, of what the refusal was and what it cost. The sūrah's final section will return to the same punishment in its full eschatological form, and the form will be the form the adjective has already promised: a punishment that is *fitted*, that is *specific*, that is the *consequence* of the choice, and that is — in the sūrah's account — the *only* outcome the choice could have produced. The reader leaves verse 8 with the sūrah's thesis in its full force: the signs are given, the market is closed, the refuser is *informed and refusing*, the refusal is *arrogance*, and the "glad tidings" are the *punishment* that the arrogance has secured.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:9

> **And when he comes to know aught of Our signs, he takes them in mockery. For them there shall be a humiliating punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mockery: The Refusal That Becomes a Performance**

The sūrah's portrait of the refuser deepens in this verse, and the deepening is the sūrah's second diagnosis: the refuser does not merely *refuse* the signs in the secrecy of his heart; he *mocks* them, and the mockery is the refusal's *public form*. The verb *yaḥkū bi-hā* — "he takes them in mockery" — is the sūrah's technical term, and the root *ḥ-k-w* in this usage is the root of the *taking up of a thing in derision*, the lifting of the sign into the register of the *joke*, the treating of the serious as the *trivial*. The mockery is thus not a *response* to the signs; it is a *re-categorization* of them, the act by which the refuser *converts* the signs from the register of the *address* into the register of the *amusement*, and the conversion is the act that makes the refusal *social* — the act by which the refusal becomes a *shared* refusal, a refusal that the refuser performs *for an audience*, in the presence of others, in the assembly. The sūrah's distinction between the *personal refusal* of verse 8 (the hearing that is not hearing, the arrogance that is the engine) and the *social mockery* of verse 9 is the distinction the sūrah's argument requires: the personal refusal is the *inner* state, and the social mockery is the *outer* performance, and the two together are the *full* portrait of the refuser.

**"When He Comes to Know": The Knowledge That Is the Ground of the Mockery**

The phrase *idhā ʿalima min āyātinā shayʾan* — "when he comes to know aught of Our signs" — is the sūrah's second precise description of the refuser's inner state, and the description is the description that completes the diagnosis of verse 8. The refuser is not the one who does *not* know the signs; he is the one who *comes to know* them — the one who *hears* them, who *registers* them, who *knows* them in the plain sense of the word — and *then* mocks them. The knowledge is the *ground* of the mockery, not its *obstacle*: the refuser mocks *because* he knows, because the knowledge is what gives the mockery its *target*, and the target is what gives the mockery its *force*. The commentators' gloss is direct: the "coming to know" refers to the *memorizing* of something of the Qur'an, or to the obtaining of a *surface knowledge* of the Qur'an or of the created world — the knowledge that is *sufficient* to give the mockery its object, but not the knowledge that *produces* the response. The distinction is the distinction the sūrah's portrait requires: the refuser has the *minimum* knowledge — the knowledge of the *words*, the knowledge of the *sound*, the knowledge of the *form* — and the minimum knowledge is *sufficient* for the mockery, because the mockery does not require the *understanding*; it requires only the *recognition* of the form, and the recognition is what the refuser has. The sūrah's diagnosis is thus complete: the refuser is *informed* (he knows the words), *refusing* (he does not let the hearing reach the understanding), and *mocking* (he performs the refusal as a joke, for an audience), and the three together are the *full* portrait.

**The Two Registers of the Mocked: Creation and Revelation**

The sūrah's use of the word *āyāt* in this verse is the use that carries the sūrah's whole thesis: the signs that are mocked are the signs *in both registers* — the signs in creation *and* the signs in revelation. The refuser who mocks the signs of creation is the one who *fails to acknowledge their Divine Origin*, who treats the order of the heavens and the earth as a *blind* order, an order that has no *Maker*, an order that is *mere* mechanism — and the refuser who mocks the signs of revelation is the one who *denies the revelation*, who treats the Qur'an as a *poem*, a *divination*, a *tale of the former peoples* — the categories the Meccan audience proposed and the sūrah's second verse declined to enter. The two registers of mockery are, in the sūrah's account, the *same* mockery, because the *same* Source is refused in both: the refuser who treats the cosmos as a blind mechanism and the refuser who treats the Qur'an as a poem are, in the sūrah's grammar, the *same* refuser, because both have refused the *common Source*, and the refusal of the common Source is the refusal that the sūrah's whole argument is built to expose. The sūrah's later verse (v. 35) will return to the same point in its eschatological form: the refuser's punishment is *because* he "took the signs of God in mockery" — the signs in both registers, the signs that the sūrah's opening display had named.

**Mockery as the More Severe Sin: The Refusal Made Social**

The commentators mark a distinction worth preserving: the *arrogant refusal* of verse 8 is a *personal* sin — the sin of the heart, the sin that is *between* the refuser and his Lord — but the *mockery* of verse 9 is a *social* sin, the sin that is *between* the refuser and his community, the sin that *converts* the refusal into a *shared* refusal, a refusal that the audience *participates in* by *laughing*. The mockery is therefore the *more severe* of the two, because the personal refusal is the *private* act, and the social mockery is the *public* act, and the public act *recruits* the audience into the refusal. The man who mocks the signs in the assembly does not merely refuse them; he *invites* the assembly to refuse them, and the invitation is the *weapon* of the mockery — the weapon that makes the refusal *contagious*, that converts the *individual* refusal into the *communal* refusal, that gives the refusal its *social force*. The sūrah's verdict upon the mockery is thus the verdict upon the *weapon*: the one who wields the weapon of the social mockery is the one who has *recruited* his community into the refusal, and the recruitment is the *crime* that the sūrah's "humiliating punishment" is fitted to punish.

**"A Humiliating Punishment": The Verdict Fitted to the Crime**

The punishment is named with the adjective *mūkhzī* — "humiliating" — and the adjective is the sūrah's technical term for the punishment that is *fitted* to the crime of the *social* refusal. The root *kh-z-w* is the root of the *shame*, the *exposure*, the *uncovering*, and in the moral lexicon the root of the *punishment that exposes* — the punishment that *reveals* the one who was *concealed* behind the performance, the punishment that *strips* the refuser of the *social mask* the mockery had provided. The logic of the fit is the logic the sūrah's portrait requires: the refuser had, in the mockery, *recruited* his community into the refusal, and had *concealed* his own personal refusal behind the *social performance* of the shared laughter; and the punishment that is *fitted* to this crime is the punishment that *exposes* the personal refusal, that *strips* the social mask, that *reveals* the one who was *concealed* — the punishment that is *humiliating* because it is the *unmaking* of the social performance, the *exposure* of the personal refusal that the performance had concealed. The sūrah's final section will return to the same punishment in its full eschatological form, and the form will be the form the adjective has already promised: a punishment that *exposes*, that *strips*, that *reveals* — the punishment that is, in the sūrah's account, the *only* outcome the social mockery could have produced.

**The Sūrah's Warning to the Audience: The Listener's Complicity**

The verse's practical lesson for the reader of any century is the lesson the sūrah's method always delivers, and the lesson is the lesson the sūrah's portrait of the *audience* requires. The mockery is a *social* act, and the social act *requires* an audience, and the audience's *laughter* is the *complicity* that gives the mockery its force. The sūrah's warning is thus directed not only at the *mocker* but at the *laugher* — the one who *participates* in the mockery by *laughing*, the one who *joins* the social performance, the one who *recruits* his own laughter into the refusal. The lesson is the lesson the modern reader needs most: the *laughter* at the sign — the laugh that treats the serious as the trivial, the laugh that *re-categorizes* the address as the amusement — is the *act* that the sūrah's portrait identifies as the *ground* of the social refusal, and the act is the act that *converts* the individual into the *complicit*. The sūrah's verdict is thus a *warning* to the reader of any century: the *laughter* at the sign is not a *harmless* amusement; it is the *act* that *recruits* the reader into the refusal, and the act is the act that the sūrah's "humiliating punishment" is *fitted* to punish.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:10

> **Beyond them lies Hell. That which they have earned will avail them naught, nor that which they have taken apart from God as protectors; and theirs shall be a great punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Beyond Them": The Direction of the Verdict**

The sūrah's portrait of the refuser now receives its destination, and the word that opens the verdict is the sūrah's small gem of spatial logic: *min warāʾihim* — "beyond them." The commentators record that the phrase can refer to what lies *in front of* them or what lies *behind* them, and both directions are true at once, because both describe the same fact from the two sides of the refuser's life. In the direction *in front*: Hell lies ahead of the refuser, because the refuser is *walking toward* it — every day of the life organized around the refusal is a step on the road, and the road has its destination, and the destination is named. In the direction *behind*: Hell lies behind the refuser, because the refuser has *turned away from* it — he has ignored its reality, refused its witness, mocked its sign, and in the turning has placed it at his back, the thing he has chosen not to see, the thing that is, in the sūrah's account, *there* — present, real, and named — but *behind* him, in the direction he has refused to look. The two directions together are the sūrah's description of the refuser's whole relation to the verdict: he is walking toward it and has turned his back on it, and the two facts are the two facts that the sūrah's "great punishment" is fitted to resolve.

**"That Which They Have Earned": Wealth and Children, the Substitutes That Avail Naught**

The sūrah's first named substitute is *that which they have earned* — *mā kasabū* — and the commentators' gloss is direct: the wealth and the children, the two things that the refuser has *accumulated*, the two things that the refuser has *invested in*, the two things that the refuser has *organized his life around*. The Book's own account of the same futility is the sūrah's standing parallel, and the parallel is the sūrah's sharpest:

> *"As for those who disbelieve, their wealth and their children will not avail them against God aught. And it is they who shall be fuel for the Fire."* (Qur'an 3:10; cf. 3:116; 58:17)

The logic of the futility is the logic the sūrah's argument requires: the wealth and the children are *substitutes* — things that the refuser has *placed* in the position that belongs to God, things that the refuser has *trusted* with the function that belongs to the trust, things that the refuser has *organized his hope around* on the assumption that they will *avail* him at the moment of the account. The futility is thus not a *random* failure; it is the *declared* failure, the failure that the sūrah's second verse has already promised, because the sūrah's second verse named the Source — *God, the Mighty, the Wise* — and the Source has *declared* that the substitutes will not avail. The sūrah's second named substitute is *that which they have taken apart from God as protectors* — *mā ittakhadhū min dūnihi min aʿimād* — and the commentators' primary reading is the reading of the *idols*, the gods that the idolaters *worshipped*, the images and the names that the refuser *placed* in the position of the protector. The logic of the broader reading is the logic the sūrah's portrait requires: the *idol* is not the only *substitute*; the *substitute* is the *category*, and the *idol* is the *category's* most *visible* form. The punishment is named with the adjective *ʿaẓīm* — "great" — and the adjective is the sūrah's technical term for the punishment that is *fitted* to the *greatness* of the refusal. The sūrah's final section will return to the same punishment in its full eschatological form, and the form will be the form the adjective has already promised: a punishment that is *great*, that is *fitted*, that is the *consequence* of the *exceeding*, and that is — in the sūrah's account — the *only* outcome the *exceeding* could have produced.

**The Logic of the Futility: The Substitute's Failure at the Moment of Need**

The sūrah's argument, in its plain logic, is the argument of the *substitute's failure*, and the argument is the argument the modern reader recognizes in his own experience. The *substitute* — the wealth, the children, the power, the alliances, the systems — is the thing that the refuser has *placed* in the position of the *protector*, the thing that the refuser has *trusted* with the function that belongs to the *trust*, the thing that the refuser has *organized his hope around* on the assumption that it will *avail* him at the *moment of need*. The sūrah's verdict is thus the verdict of the *futility*: the substitutes *fail*, the account is *settled*, and the *great* punishment is the *consequence* of the *futility* that the refuser has *chosen*.

**The Sūrah's Turn: From the Portrait to the Declaration**

Verse 10 completes the sūrah's first portrait of the refuser (vv. 7–10), and the portrait is the portrait the sūrah's structure requires: the *woe* (v. 7), the *hearing that is not hearing* (v. 8), the *mockery* (v. 9), and the *futility of the substitutes* (v. 10). ## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:11

> **This is guidance; and those who disbelieve in the signs of their Lord, theirs shall be a painful punishment of torment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"This is Guidance": The Sūrah Names Its Own Object**

The sūrah, having completed its first portrait of the refuser, now names its own object, and the naming is the sūrah's second great declaration after the second verse's identification of the Book: *hadāh* — "this is guidance." The commentators' gloss is direct: the word refers to the Qur'an itself (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī), or more broadly to *everything with which the Prophet came* — the Book and the message and the way — and the "guidance" is the *complete and perfect* guidance, the guidance that is *sufficient* for the one who receives it, the guidance that *leaves nothing* of the *necessary* *uncovered*. **The Same "This": Guidance to the Receiver, Punishment to the Refuser**

The sūrah's use of the demonstrative *hādhā* — "this" — is the sūrah's standing rhetorical device, and the device is the device the sūrah's argument requires: the *same* "this" that is *guidance* to the one who *receives* it is the *same* "this" that is *punishment* to the one who *refuses* it. The parallel is the sūrah's architectural point: the *guidance* is *for* the *reverent*, the *guidance* is *conditioned* upon the *receiver's* *disposition*, and the *refuser's* *refusal* is the *refuser's* *act* — the *act* that the sūrah's next verses will *dissect*. The sūrah's use of the possessive *rabbihim* — "their Lord" — is the sūrah's *point*: the *signs* are the signs of *their* Lord, the *Lord* who is *their* *Lord*, the *Lord* who has *created* them, the *Lord* who has *provided* for them, the *Lord* who has *addressed* them — the *Lord* whose *signs* they have *refused*. The possessive is the sūrah's *accusation*: the *refuser* is not the one who has *refused* an *abstract* set of *propositions*; he is the one who has *refused* the *signs* of *his own* Lord, the *signs* of the *One* who is *his* *Lord*, the *One* who has *made* him, *provided* for him, *addressed* him. **"A Painful Punishment of Torment": The Verdict's Specificity**

The punishment is named with the *double* adjective: *ʿadhābun alīm min ʿadhāb* — "a painful punishment of torment." The *double* naming is the sūrah's *technique*: the *punishment* is *painful* (*alīm* — the pain that *teaches*, the pain that *realizes* the *cost* of the *refusal*), and the *painful* punishment is *of torment* (*min ʿadhāb* — the *torment*, the *sustained* suffering, the *continuing* pain that is the *consequence* of the *continuing* refusal). **Expanded Commentary**

**The Sea: The Great Subjection and the Explicit Dependence**

The sūrah, having declared its object as guidance (v. 11), now turns to the *blessings of the subjection*, and the first blessing it names is the *sea* — the *greatest* of the subjugated things, the thing that the human being *cannot control*, the thing that *commands* the *dependence* that the land does not. The commentators' gloss is direct: the *bounty* is the *advantages* of the *sea's* *travel* — the *trade*, the *communication*, the *crossing* of the *distances* that the *land* cannot *provide* — and the *bounty* is the *sea's* *food* and *precious stones*, the *provision* that the *sea* *yields* to the *one* who *seeks* it. The *bounty* is thus the *bounty* in *two* *registers*: the *bounty* of the *sea's* *service* (the *crossing*, the *trade*, the *communication*) and the *bounty* of the *sea's* *substance* (the *food*, the *minerals*, the *provision* that the *sea* *contains*). **The Office of the Vicegerent: Subjection in Service of the Trust**

The commentators' reading of the verse's *theological* *ground* is the reading of the *vicegerency* — the *khilāfa* — the *office* that the *subjection* *serves*. The *human* is *subjugated* the *whole* of the *created* *order* because the *human* is the *Creator's* *vicegerent* upon the *earth* — the *agent* whom the *Creator* has *entrusted* with the *earth's* *stewardship*, the *agent* whom the *Creator* has *addressed* with the *command* and the *account*. The Book's own statements of the *vicegerency* are the sūrah's *standing* *parallels*:

> *"And I will make a vicegerent upon the earth."* (Qur'an 2:30)
> *"And He has subjected to you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth — all from Him."* (Qur'an 45:13, in its full sense)
> *"It is He who made you vicegerents upon the earth."* (Qur'an 6:165; 35:39)

The *logic* of the *vicegerency* is the *logic* the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *subjection* is not a *gift* to the *human* as a *master*; it is a *trust* to the *human* as a *steward*, and the *trust* is the *trust* that *carries* its own *account* within itself. The *human* is *subjugated* the *whole* of the *created* *order* so that the *human* will *steward* it, so that the *human* will *manage* it, so that the *human* will *account* for it — and the *account* is the *account* that the sūrah's *final* section will *describe*: the *Day* when the *human's* *stewardship* is *audited*, and the *audit* is the *audit* that the *subjection* has *produced*. The *sūrah's* *point* is thus the *point* the *vicegerency* makes *visible*: the *subjection* is the *Creator's* *gift* to the *human*, and the *gift* is the *gift* that *carries* its own *account* within itself, and the *account* is the *account* that the *refuser* has *refused* to *face*.

**The Spiritual Reading: The Heart Subjugated to God, and the World Subjugated to the Heart**

The commentators' *spiritual* reading of the verse is the reading that the sūrah's *argument* *invites*, and the reading is the reading that *completes* the *verse's* *logic*. The *reading* is the *reading* of the *heart's* *subjection* to *God*: when the *heart* *finds* its *tranquility* in *God*, its *Master*, the *heart* *becomes* a *true* *servant*, and *everything* is *made* *subservient* to *it* — the *world* is *subjugated* to the *heart* that is *subjugated* to *God*, and the *subjection* of the *world* is the *consequence* of the *subjection* of the *heart*. The *logic* of the *reading* is the *logic* the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *human* who is *subjugated* to *God* is the *human* to whom the *world* is *subjugated*, and the *human* who is *not* *subjugated* to *God* is the *human* who is *subjugated* to the *world* — the *human* who *serves* the *things* that were *created* to *serve* *him*. The *sūrah's* *warning* is the *warning* the *reading* implies: the *one* who *becomes* *preoccupied* with the *things* of this *world*, to the *extent* that he *forgets* *God*, is the *one* who *serves* what was *created* to *serve* *him* rather than *serving* his *true* *Master* — the *one* who has *inverted* the *subjection*, the *one* who has *placed* the *servant* in the *position* of the *master*, and the *inversion* is the *inversion* that the sūrah's *portrait of the refuser* has *already* *described*.

**The Hadith Qudsī: "I Created the Things for You, and I Created You for Me"**

The tradition supplies the *word* that the sūrah's *argument* requires, and the word is the word that *completes* the *verse's* *logic*. The *hadith qudsī* — the *divine* *speech* *attributed* to *God* — preserved in the tafsīr tradition (cited by al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr in their commentaries on this verse) states:

> *"O son of Adam, I created the things [of this world] for you. And I created you for Me. So let not that which I created for you distract you from that for which I created you."*

The *word* is the *word* that the sūrah's *argument* has *already* *delivered*: the *things* are *created* *for* the *human*, and the *human* is *created* *for* *God*, and the *human's* *task* is the *task* of *serving* *God* — not the *task* of *serving* the *things*. The *warning* is the *warning* the *word* implies: the *one* who *lets* the *things* *distract* *him* from *God* is the *one* who has *inverted* the *order*, the *one* who has *placed* the *servant* in the *position* of the *master*, and the *inversion* is the *inversion* that the sūrah's *final* section will *resolve*. The *sūrah's* *point* is thus the *point* the *word* makes *visible*: the *subjection* is the *Creator's* *gift*, and the *gift* is the *gift* that *carries* its own *purpose* within itself — the *purpose* of *serving* *God*, not the *purpose* of *serving* the *things*.

**"For a People Who Reflect": The Fourth Class of the Reader**

The verse closes with the *fourth* of the sūrah's *reader-classes*: *li-qawmin yatafakkarūn* — "for a people who reflect." The *progression* is now *nearly* *complete*: the *signs* of the *heavens and the earth* are *for the believers* (v. 3); the *signs* of the *body and the beasts* are *for the people who are certain* (v. 4); the *signs* of the *cycles* are *for a people who understand* (v. 5); and the *signs* of the *subjection* are *for a people who reflect* (v. 13). The *four* classes are the *four* stages of the *faculty* that the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *disposition* to *receive* (faith), the *settled* *knowledge* that *results* (certitude), the *reasoned* *comprehension* that *completes* the *circuit* (understanding), and the *sustained* *contemplation* that *deepens* the *comprehension* (reflection, from the root *f-k-r*, the *turning* of the *mind* over the *sign*, the *repeated* *consideration* that *converts* the *knowledge* into the *wisdom*). The *sūrah's* *next* verses will *turn* the *address* from the *blessings* to the *ethics* — the *command* to *forgive* (v. 14), the *law* of the *account* (v. 15) — and the *turn* will be the *turn* that the *fourth* *class* of the *reader* *requires*: the *one* who *reflects* is the *one* who *understands* the *ethics*, the *one* who *comprehends* the *command* to *forgive*, the *one* who *grasps* the *law* of the *account*. The sūrah's *display* of the *blessings* is thus *complete*, and the *reader* who has *received* it has *received* the sūrah's *whole* *thesis* in *miniature*: the *world* is *subjugated*, the *subjection* is the *Creator's* *gift*, the *gift* carries its own *purpose* within itself, and the *purpose* is the *purpose* that the *one* who *reflects* will *recognize*.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:14

> **Tell those who believe to forgive those who hope not for the days of God, that He may requite a people for that which they used to earn.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Those Who Hope Not for the Days of God": The Definition of the Class**

The sūrah, having displayed the blessings of the subjection (vv. 12–13), now delivers its brief but famous ethical turn, and the turn is the sūrah's single legislative verse in an otherwise eschatological and theological argument. The command is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ in the form of an instruction to the community: *qul li-l-muʾminīna lyaʿfū ʿalladhīna lā yarjūna ayāma Allāh* — "Tell those who believe to forgive those who hope not for the Days of God." The description of the class to be forgiven is the sūrah's technical term, and the term is the term that *defines* the class in its full depth: the one who "hopes not for the Days of God" is the one who *neither hopes for the reward of the Hereafter nor fears God's punishment* — the one who has *removed* the *Days of God* from his *calculations*, the one who has *organized* his *life* on the *assumption* that the *Days of God* will *not* *come*, the one who has *placed* his *hope* entirely in *this* *world* and *withdrawn* it from *the next*. The *class* is thus the *class* of the *disbelievers* and the *hypocrites* — the *class* that the sūrah's *portrait of the refuser* (vv. 7–11) has *already* *described* — and the *command* to *forgive* the *class* is the *command* that the sūrah's *argument* is *built* to *explain*: the *command* that *appears* *contrary* to the *sūrah's* *severe* *portrait* of the *refuser*, and the *command* that the sūrah's *next* verse (v. 15) will *ground* in the *law* of the *account*.

**The First Occasion: The Well of al-Rawḥā and the Leader of the Hypocrites**

The tafsīr tradition records the occasion of the verse with the specificity the verse's gravity requires. According to the accounts preserved in al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr (among them the report transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās), the verse was connected with an expedition to the Arab tribe of Banū al-Muṣaliq: the Muslim host stopped at a well, and ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy, the leader of the hypocrites of Madinah, sent his slave to draw water. When the slave returned, asked what had delayed him, and replied that *the slave of ʿUmar* had stood at the opening of the well and allowed no one to draw until he had filled the water-skins of the Prophet ﷺ, of Abū Bakr, and of his own master. ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy then delivered the sentence that the tradition preserves with its full venom: *"Our example and the example of these people is as the saying goes: fatten your dog, and he will eat you."* The sentence was the leader's calculation of the community's trajectory: the Prophet ﷺ had come to Madinah as a *powerless guest*, had been *cared for* by the Madinan population, and had now, in the leader's account, *turned against them* to bring them harm. When ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb heard the sentence, he *seized his sword*, intending to kill ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy — and then God revealed this verse (or, as the commentators note, the verse may already have been revealed, and the Prophet ﷺ simply *recited* it to ʿUmar at that moment, to *calm* him and to *maintain* the peace). The occasion is the occasion that *completes* the sūrah's *argument*: the *command* to *forgive* is the *command* that *arrived* at the *moment* of the *sword*, at the *moment* when the *community's* *instinct* was the *instinct* of *vengeance*, and the *command* *overruled* the *instinct* — the *command* that *transferred* the *account* from the *sword* to the *Lord*.

**The Second Occasion: "Muhammad's Lord Is in Need" and the Face on Which Anger Did Not Show**

The tafsīr tradition records a second occasion, and the second occasion is the second *completion* of the sūrah's *argument*. According to the accounts preserved in al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr (among them the report transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās), when the verse *Who is it that will lend unto God a goodly loan?* (57:11) was revealed, a Jewish man in Madinah by the name of Finhās said: *"Muhammad's Lord is in need!"* The sentence was the *blasphemy* that *inverted* the *attribute*: the *attribute* of *Self-Sufficiency* — the *attribute* that the sūrah's *second* verse had *already* *named* in the *form* of the *Mighty* and the *Wise* — was *inverted* by the *blasphemy* into the *attribute* of *need*. When ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭāb heard the sentence, he *grabbed his sword* and went looking for the speaker. And then Gabriel went to the Messenger of God ﷺ and said to him: *"Your Lord says to you: 'Tell those who believe to forgive those who hope not for the days of God.'"* The Prophet ﷺ replied with the sentence that the tradition preserves with its full weight: *"No doubt. By Him Who has sent thee with the truth, anger will not show upon my face!"* The occasion is the occasion that *completes* the sūrah's *argument* in its *second* register: the *command* to *forgive* is the *command* that *arrived* at the *moment* of the *sword*, at the *moment* when the *community's* *instinct* was the *instinct* of *vengeance* for the *blasphemy*, and the *command* *overruled* the *instinct* — the *command* that *transferred* the *account* from the *sword* to the *Lord*, and the *command* that *secured* the *Prophet's* *face* from the *anger* that the *blasphemy* had *produced*.

**The Question of Abrogation, and the Commentators' Answer**

The tafsīr literature records a *question* about the verse: *some* maintain that the verse was *abrogated* — that the *command* to *forgive* was *superseded* by the *later* *commands* of *offensive* *struggle* against the *disbelievers*. The commentators' answer is the answer the sūrah's *context* requires: al-Qurṭubī notes that, *if* the verse is from the *Madinan* period (as the *occasions* *suggest*), such *abrogation* is *unlikely* — the *command* to *forgive* is not a *temporary* *command* *superseded* by a *permanent* *command*; it is a *standing* *command* *grounded* in the *law* of the *account* (v. 15), and the *standing* *command* is the *command* that *operates* in the *specific* *domain* that the *sūrah's* *occasions* *define*: the *domain* of the *word* and the *intrigue* — the *domain* in which the *disbeliever* and the *hypocrite* *wage* their *war* by *speech*, and the *command* to *forgive* is the *command* that *governs* the *community's* *response* to the *speech*. The *answer* is thus the *answer* the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *command* to *forgive* is not *abrogated*; it is *specified* — *specified* to the *domain* of the *word*, *specified* to the *moment* of the *sword*, *specified* to the *transfer* of the *account* from the *hand* to the *Lord*.

**The Ground of the Command: The Signs in Creation, and the Honor of the Human**

The sūrah's *ground* for the *command* is the *ground* the *commentators* *derive* from the *sūrah's* *own* *context*, and the *ground* is the *ground* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*. The *command* to *forgive* can be understood as *following* from the *allusion* to the *signs* in *your* *creation* (v. 4): if one *reflects* upon the *signs* of *God* in the *human* *body* — the *signs* that the *sūrah's* *fourth* *verse* had *named* — one *recognizes* the *honor* due to *all* *human* *beings*, and the *honor* is the *honor* that *forbids* the *sword* at the *moment* of the *word*. Al-Rāzī observes that *discussions* of the *nature* of the *created* *order* in the *Qur'an* are *often* *followed* by *discussions* of *virtuous* *conduct*, and the *observation* is the *observation* the sūrah's *structure* *confirms*: the *display* of the *blessings* (vv. 12–13) is *followed* by the *command* to *forgive* (v. 14), and the *connection* is the *connection* the *sūrah's* *argument* *requires*. The *command* to *forgive* the *one* who *hopes* *not* for the *Days* of *God* is thus the *command* that *follows* from the *recognition* that *one's* *own* *faith* and *understanding* are *blessings* from *God*, and the *recognition* is the *recognition* that the *Book's* *own* *verse* *states* in the *register* of the *believers'* *speech*:

> *"Praise be to God, Who guided us unto this. We would not have been rightly guided had God not guided us."* (Qur'an 7:43)

The *recognition* is the *recognition* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *believer* who *recognizes* that his *faith* is a *blessing* will *not* *wield* the *sword* at the *moment* of the *word*, because the *sword* is the *sword* that *assumes* the *account* is *his* to *settle*, and the *recognition* is the *recognition* that *transfers* the *account* to the *Lord*.

**"That He May Requite a People for That Which They Used to Earn": The Transfer of the Account**

The sūrah's *final* clause — *la-yajziya Allāhu shaʿban bimā kānū yaksabūn* — "that He may requite a people for that which they used to earn" — is the sūrah's *thesis* in its *full* force, and the thesis is the thesis that *grounds* the *command* in the *law* of the *account*. The *command* to *forgive* is the *command* that *transfers* the *account* from the *community's* *hand* to *God's* *hand*: the *community* *forbears*, the *community* *withholds* the *sword*, the *community* *leaves* the *requital* to the *Lord* — and the *Lord* *requites*, the *Lord* *settles*, the *Lord* *pays* the *people* *according* to *what* they *used* to *earn*. The *logic* of the *transfer* is the *logic* the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *one* who *forgives* is the *one* who *recognizes* that the *account* is *not* his to *settle*, that the *requital* is *not* his to *administer*, that the *justice* is *not* his to *execute* — the *one* who *recognizes* that the *account* belongs to the *Lord*, and that the *Lord's* *justice* is the *justice* that *exceeds* the *community's* *justice*, because the *Lord's* *justice* is the *justice* that *sees* the *heart*, and the *community's* *justice* is the *justice* that *sees* the *word*. The *sūrah's* *next* verse (v. 15) will *state* the *law* of the *account* in its *full* form, and the *law* will be the *law* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: *whoever* works *righteousness*, it is *for* his *own* *soul*; and *whoever* commits *evil*, it is *to* the *detriment* thereof — and *then* unto *your* *Lord* shall *you* be *returned*. The *command* to *forgive* is thus the *command* that *follows* from the *law*, and the *law* is the *law* that *grounds* the *command* in the *transfer* of the *account* from the *hand* to the *Lord*.

**The Ethics of the Transfer: Forgiveness as the Act of the Strong**

The sūrah's *practical* *lesson* for the *reader* of *any* *century* is the *lesson* the sūrah's *argument* always *delivers*, and the lesson is the lesson that *completes* the *sūrah's* *ethics*. The *command* to *forgive* is the *command* that *appears* *weak* — the *command* that *forbids* the *sword*, the *command* that *withholds* the *retaliation*, the *command* that *leaves* the *account* to the *Lord* — and the *command* is, in the sūrah's *account*, the *command* of the *strong*, not the *command* of the *weak*. The *logic* of the *strength* is the *logic* the sūrah's *occasions* *confirm*: the *community* that *wields* the *sword* at the *moment* of the *word* is the *community* that has *accepted* the *disbeliever's* *terms* — the *terms* of the *word* as the *weapon*, the *terms* of the *word* as the *battlefield*, the *terms* of the *word* as the *account*; and the *community* that *forbears* at the *moment* of the *word* is the *community* that has *rejected* the *disbeliever's* *terms*, that has *transferred* the *account* to the *Lord*, and that has *secured* its *own* *integrity* from the *contamination* of the *vengeance*. The *modern* *reader's* *experience* of the *transfer* is the *experience* the sūrah's *argument* describes: the *organization* that *responds* to the *criticism* with the *sword* of the *counter-attack* is the *organization* that has *accepted* the *critic's* *terms*; and the *organization* that *responds* to the *criticism* with the *forbearance* of the *transfer* — the *forbearance* that *leaves* the *account* to the *Lord* — is the *organization* that has *rejected* the *critic's* *terms* and *secured* its *own* *integrity*. The sūrah's *command* is thus the *command* of the *strong*: the *command* that *transfers* the *account* from the *hand* to the *Lord*, and the *command* that *secures* the *community's* *integrity* from the *contamination* of the *vengeance*.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:15

> **Whosoever works righteousness, it is for his own soul. And whosoever commits evil, it is to the detriment thereof. Then unto your Lord shall you be returned.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Law of the Account: The Verse That Grounds the Command to Forgive**

The sūrah, having delivered its command to forgive (v. 14), now states the *law* that *grounds* the command, and the law is the law of the *individual account*: *man yaʿmal ṣāliḥan falinafsihī, wa-man yasaki fa-ʿalayhā, thumma ilā rabbikum turjaʿūn* — "Whosoever works righteousness, it is for his own soul; and whosoever commits evil, it is to the detriment thereof. Then unto your Lord shall you be returned." The law is the law that the Book states with its full consistency elsewhere, and the sūrah's parallel is the sūrah's sharpest:

> *"Whosoever works righteousness, it is for his own soul; and whosoever works evil, it is against his own soul. Your Lord is not unjust to His slaves."* (Qur'an 41:46)
> *"Every soul is a pledge for that which it has earned."* (Qur'an 74:38)

The law is the law that *completes* the sūrah's *argument*: the *command* to *forgive* (v. 14) is the *command* that *follows* from the *law*, because the *law* states that the *account* is *individual* — that the *righteousness* *avails* the *one* who *works* it, and that the *evil* *avails* the *one* who *commits* it, and that the *account* of the *one* is *not* *transferred* to the *other*. The *command* to *forgive* is thus the *command* that *follows* from the *law*: the *believer* who *forgives* the *one* who *hopes* *not* for the *Days* of *God* is the *believer* who *recognizes* that the *account* of the *one* is *the one's own*, that the *requital* of the *one* is *the one's own*, and that the *forgiveness* of the *believer* does not *interfere* with the *requital* — the *forgiveness* *withholds* the *sword*, and the *Lord* *settles* the *account*.

**"It Is for His Own Soul": The Non-Transferability of the Good**

The law's first clause — *falinafsihī* — "it is for his own soul" — states the *non-transferability* of the *good*, and the *non-transferability* is the *non-transferability* that the *sūrah's* *argument* *requires*. The *righteousness* *avails* the *one* who *works* it: the *good* *deed* is the *good* *deed* that *belongs* to the *doer*, that *returns* to the *doer*, that *completes* the *doer's* *account*. The *logic* of the *non-transferability* is the *logic* the *Book's* *own* *usage* *supports*: the *good* *deed* is not a *commodity* that can be *transferred* from the *doer* to the *other*, that can be *given* by the *doer* to the *other*, that can be *inherited* by the *other* from the *doer* — the *good* *deed* is the *good* *deed* that *returns* to the *doer*, and the *return* is the *return* that the *sūrah's* *final* clause will *name*: *then* unto *your* *Lord* shall *you* be *returned*. The *practical* *implication* of the *non-transferability* is the *implication* the *sūrah's* *argument* *delivers*: the *one* who *works* *righteousness* *works* it *for* *himself*, and the *one* who *commits* *evil* *commits* it *against* *himself* — the *account* is *individual*, and the *individuality* is the *individuality* that *forbids* the *transfer* of the *account* from the *hand* to the *Lord*, and the *forbidding* is the *forbidding* that *grounds* the *sūrah's* *command* to *forgive* in the *law* of the *account*.

**"It Is to the Detriment Thereof": The Non-Transferability of the Evil**

The law's second clause — *fa-ʿalayhā* — "it is to the detriment thereof" — states the *non-transferability* of the *evil*, and the *non-transferability* is the *non-transferability* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*. The *evil* *deed* is the *evil* *deed* that *belongs* to the *doer*, that *returns* to the *doer*, that *completes* the *doer's* *account* in the *direction* of the *detriment*. The *logic* of the *non-transferability* is the *logic* the *sūrah's* *portrait of the refuser* *confirms*: the *refuser's* *refusal* is the *refuser's own* *refusal*, that the *refuser's* *mockery* is the *refuser's own* *mockery*, that the *refuser's* *futility* of the *substitutes* is the *refuser's own* *futility* — the *evil* is the *evil* that *returns* to the *doer*, and the *return* is the *return* that the *sūrah's* *final* section will *describe* in its *full* eschatological form. The *practical* *implication* of the *non-transferability* of the *evil* is the *implication* the *sūrah's* *argument* *delivers*: the *community* is not *punished* for the *evil* of the *one*, and the *one* is not *punished* for the *evil* of the *community* — the *account* is *individual*, and the *individuality* is the *individuality* that *forbids* the *collective* *punishment* of the *innocent*, and the *forbidding* is the *forbidding* that the *Book's* *own* *verse* *states* in its *full* form:

> *"No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another."* (Qur'an 6:164; 35:18; 53:38)

The *sūrah's* *law* is thus the *law* that *completes* the *Book's* *consistent* *usage*: the *account* is *individual*, and the *individuality* is the *individuality* that *grounds* the *sūrah's* *command* to *forgive* in the *transfer* of the *account* from the *hand* to the *Lord*.

**"Then Unto Your Lord Shall You Be Returned": The Direction of the Account**

The sūrah's final clause — *thumma ilā rabbikum turjaʿūn* — "then unto your Lord shall you be returned" — states the *direction* of the *account*, and the *direction* is the *direction* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*. **Expanded Commentary**

**The Precedent Introduced: The Community Given Everything**

The sūrah, having stated the law of the account (v. 15), now introduces the *historical precedent* that will complete its argument, and the precedent is the precedent of the Children of Israel: *wa-la-qad ātaynā banī ʾIsrāʾīl al-kitāba wa-l-ḥukma wa-n-nubuwwa* — "And indeed We gave the Children of Israel the Book, judgment, and prophethood." The introduction is the sūrah's *rhetorical move*: the sūrah has *argued* its case through the *display* of the *signs* (vv. 3–6), the *portrait* of the *refuser* (vv. 7–11), the *blessings* of the *subjection* (vv. 12–13), and the *law* of the *account* (vv. 14–15); and the sūrah now *supplements* the argument with the *witness* of *history* — the *witness* of the *community* that was *given* the *Book*, the *judgment*, the *prophethood*, and the *good* things, and the *witness* of what *happened* to that *community* when the *gift* was *given*. The *precedent* is thus the *precedent* that *completes* the sūrah's *argument* in the *direction* of the *historical* *witness*: the *gift* of the *Book* is not a *security* against the *refusal*, and the *refusal* is the *refusal* that the *sūrah's* *portrait* has *already* *described*.

**"The Book, Judgment, and Prophethood": The Three Spiritual Gifts**

The sūrah's first three gifts are the *spiritual* gifts, and the *gifts* are the *gifts* that *define* the *community's* *relation* to the *revelation*. The *Book* is the *Torah* — the *revelation* *given* to *Moses*, the *text* that *carried* the *law* and the *guidance* and the *account*. *Judgment* (*al-ḥukm*) refers, according to the commentators, to the *Israelites'* *understanding* of the *Torah* — the *knowledge* and the *wisdom* that *enabled* the *community* to *apply* the *Book*, to *judge* by the *Book*, to *live* by the *Book* — the *gift* that *converted* the *text* into the *practice*. And *prophethood* (*al-nubuwwa*) is the *gift* of the *prophets* — the *line* of *prophets* that *guided* the *community*, that *interpreted* the *Book*, that *sustained* the *relation* between the *community* and the *Lord*. The *three* *gifts* together are the *three* *elements* of the *revelation's* *full* *operation*: the *text* (the *Book*), the *understanding* (the *judgment*), and the *guidance* (the *prophethood) — the *three* *elements* that *together* *constitute* the *community's* *complete* *relation* to the *revelation*. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *three* *gifts* make *visible*: the *community* was *given* *everything* that the *revelation* *requires* — the *text*, the *understanding*, and the *guidance* — and the *community* *still* *divided* (v. 17), and the *division* is the *division* that the *sūrah's* *argument* is *built* to *expose*: the *gift* of the *revelation* is not a *security* against the *refusal*, and the *refusal* is the *refusal* that the *sūrah's* *portrait* has *already* *described*.

The *order* of the *gifts* is the *order* the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *spiritual* *gifts* (the *Book*, the *judgment*, the *prophethood) are *mentioned* *before* the *material* *gifts* (the *good* things), and the *order* is the *order* that *illustrates* the *superiority* of the *spiritual* *benefits* over *those* that *pertain* only to *this* *world*. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *order* makes *visible*: the *community* was *given* the *spiritual* *gifts* *first*, and the *material* *gifts* *second*, and the *order* is the *order* that *states* the *hierarchy* of the *gifts* — the *hierarchy* that the *sūrah's* *argument* *requires*: the *spiritual* *gifts* are the *gifts* that *matter* most, and the *material* *gifts* are the *gifts* that *serve* the *spiritual* *gifts*, and the *community* that *inverts* the *hierarchy* — the *community* that *values* the *material* *gifts* *above* the *spiritual* *gifts* — is the *community* that has *inverted* the *order* of the *revelation*, and the *inversion* is the *inversion* that the sūrah's *next* verse will *describe* as the *ground* of the *division*.

The sūrah's final clause — *wa-ʿaḍalnāhum ʿalā al-ʿālamīn* — "and We favored them above the worlds" — states the *community's* *election*, and the *election* is the *election* that the *Book's* *own* *consistent* *usage* *supports*. The *phrase* appears in the *Book* in the *same* *register* (2:47; 2:122; 7:140), and the *commentators'* *gloss* is *direct*: the *favors* *above* the *worlds* refer to the *blessings* *mentioned* above — the *Book*, the *judgment*, the *prophethood*, and the *good* things — and the *election* is the *election* *above* the *other* *peoples* of *their* *own* *time*, not the *election* *above* all *peoples* of *all* *times*. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *election* makes *visible*: the *community* was *favored* *above* the *peoples* of *their* *time* — the *peoples* of the *desert*, the *peoples* of the *city*, the *peoples* of the *region* — and the *favor* was the *favor* of the *Book*, the *judgment*, the *prophethood*, and the *good* things. The *election* is thus the *election* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *precedent*: the *community* was *given* *everything* — the *spiritual* *gifts*, the *material* *gifts*, and the *election* *above* the *worlds* — and the *community* *still* *divided* (v. 17), and the *division* is the *division* that the *sūrah's* *argument* is *built* to *expose*: the *gift* of the *revelation*, *even* in its *full* *measure*, is not a *security* against the *refusal*, and the *refusal* is the *refusal* that the *sūrah's* *portrait* has *already* *described*.

**The Rhetorical Purpose: The Precedent as Warning**

The sūrah's *rhetorical* *purpose* in *introducing* the *precedent* is the *purpose* the sūrah's *structure* requires: the *precedent* is the *precedent* that *warns* the *reader* of *any* *century*. The *warning* is the *warning* the *precedent* delivers: the *gift* of the *Book* is not a *security* against the *division*, and the *division* is the *division* that *envy* *produces*, and the *envy* is the *envy* that the *sūrah's* *next* verse will *describe* in its *full* form. The *sūrah's* *argument* is thus *complete*: the *signs* are *given*, the *refuser* is *informed* and *refusing*, the *blessings* are *provided*, the *law* of the *account* is *stated*, and the *precedent* is the *precedent* that *warns* the *reader* of *any* *century* — the *warning* that the *gift* of the *revelation* is not a *security* against the *refusal*, and that the *refusal* is the *refusal* that the *sūrah's* *final* section will *resolve* in the *Day* of the *account*. The reader leaves verse 16 with the sūrah's thesis in its full force: the *precedent* is the *precedent* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *historical* *witness*, and the *witness* is the *witness* that *warns* the *reader* of *any* *century* — the *warning* that the *gift* of the *Book* is not a *security* against the *refusal*, and that the *refusal* is the *refusal* that the *sūrah's* *portrait* has *already* *described*.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:17

> **And We gave them clear proofs from the Command. And they differed not till after knowledge had come unto them, out of envy among themselves. Thy Lord will surely judge between them on the Day of Resurrection regarding that wherein they used to differ.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Clear Proofs from the Command": The Gifts That Were Sufficient**

The sūrah's precedent deepens in this verse, and the deepening is the sūrah's *second* *statement* of the *Israelites'* *gifts*: *wa-la-qad ātaynāhum al-bayyīnata min al-amr* — "And We gave them clear proofs from the Command." The phrase *al-bayyīnata* — "clear proofs" — is the sūrah's technical term, and the term is the term that *completes* the *gifts* named in the previous verse. The commentators record the *range* of the *term's* *referents*, and the *range* is the *range* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: *generally*, the *unequivocal* *proofs* — the *evidences* that *left* no *room* for *doubt* (Ibn Kathīr); *more specifically*, the *clear* *delineations* between what is *forbidden* and what is *permissible* (al-Qurṭubī) — the *halal* and the *haram*, the *boundaries* that the *Torah* had *drawn* with *precision*; and *in* another *register*, the *miracles* that *God* *gave* to *Moses* as *proof* of his *prophethood* (the *staff*, the *hand*, the *plagues*, the *sea* — the *miracles* that *made* the *proof* *visible* to the *eye* as *well* as to the *mind*). And the *sūrah's* *context* *invites* a *further* *reading*, and the *reading* is the *reading* that the *commentators* *record*: *viewed* in *light* of the *subsequent* verse (v. 18, the address to the Prophet ﷺ), *some* understand *clear proofs* to mean the *indications* that *God* *gave* the *Prophet* *Muḥammad* that he *would* *migrate* to *Madinah* and that he *would* *receive* *help* from its *people* — and on *this* *reading*, *that* *they* *differed* *not* *till* *after* *knowledge* *had* *come* *unto* *them* refers to the *Jews* of *Madinah* who *rejected* *Muḥammad's* *prophethood*, and *out* of *envy* *among* *themselves* refers to their *being* *jealous* of the *Prophet*. The *range* of the *referents* is thus the *range* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *proofs* are the *proofs* in the *register* of the *Torah* (the *delineations*, the *miracles*), and the *proofs* are the *proofs* in the *register* of the *final* *revelation* (the *indications* of the *migration*, the *help* of *Madinah*) — and the *community* that *refused* the *proofs* in *either* *register* is the *community* that the sūrah's *argument* is *built* to *expose*.

**"They Differed Not Till After Knowledge Had Come Unto Them": The Division That Follows the Evidence**

The sūrah's second clause — *wa-mā ihtalafū illā baʿda mā jāʾahumu al-ʿilm* — "And they differed not till after knowledge had come unto them" — is the sūrah's *diagnosis* of the *division*, and the *diagnosis* is the *diagnosis* that the *Book's* *own* *consistent* *usage* *supports*. The *phrase* appears in the *Book* in the *same* *register* at the *moment* of the *calf* — *Moses'* *rebuke* to the *Israelites* after the *calf*:

> *"And they worshipped the calf, a beast which had a lowing. And when they saw with their eyes that they had gone astray, they said: 'Surely, if we do not turn to our Lord, we shall surely be among the losers.' And Moses said to them: 'O my people, you have wronged yourselves by that which you made for yourselves. So turn unto your Maker and slaughter [your idols]. Truly, he who turns to you is the Merciful.'"* (Qur'an 7:142–144, in its sense; cf. 10:93)

The *logic* of the *diagnosis* is the *logic* the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *division* did not *precede* the *knowledge*; the *division* *followed* the *knowledge*. The *community* was *not* *divided* *before* the *proofs* *arrived*; the *community* was *divided* *after* the *proofs* *arrived* — the *division* is the *consequence* of the *knowledge*, not the *absence* of the *knowledge*. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *diagnosis* makes *visible*: the *division* is not the *division* of the *ignorant*; it is the *division* of the *informed* — the *division* of the *one* who *knows* the *proofs* and *divides* *over* them, the *division* of the *one* who *has* the *Book* and *disputes* the *Book*, the *division* of the *one* who *receives* the *knowledge* and *uses* it as the *instrument* of the *dispute* rather than the *instrument* of the *unity*. The *Book's* *own* *statement* of the *same* *logic* is the sūrah's *standing* *parallel*:

> *"Mankind was one community, and God raised up prophets to give good tidings and warn. And He sent down Books with truth along with the prophets, to judge between people in that over which they had differed. And none differed over it except those to whom it was given, after the clear proofs had come to them — they wronged one another out of envy among themselves."* (Qur'an 2:213, in its sense)

The *parallel* is the *parallel* that *completes* the sūrah's *argument*: the *division* is the *division* that *follows* the *clear* *proofs*, and the *division* is the *division* that the *sūrah's* *next* clause will *name* its *engine*.

**"Out of Envy Among Themselves": The Engine of the Division**

The sūrah's third clause — *ḥasadan baynahum* — "out of envy among themselves" — names the *engine* of the *division*, and the *engine* is the *engine* that the *sūrah's* *argument* *requires*. The *root* *ḥ-s-d* is the root of the *envy*, the *jealousy*, the *desire* to *possess* what the *other* *possesses* and to *deprive* the *other* of what the *other* *has* — the *root* that the *Book* *places* at the *head* of its *final* *sūrah* of *refuge*:

> *"Say: 'I seek refuge in the Lord of the dawn, from the evil of that which He created, and from the evil of the envier when he envies.'"* (Qur'an 113:1–5)

The *logic* of the *engine* is the *logic* the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *knowledge* *creates* the *hierarchy*, and the *hierarchy* *creates* the *envy*, and the *envy* *produces* the *division*. The *one* who *knows* *more* *commands* *more*; the *one* who *holds* the *proofs* *holds* the *authority* over the *one* who *does not*; and the *one* who *does not* hold the *proofs* — or the *one* who *holds* a *portion* of the *proofs* and *disputes* the *portion* held by the *other* — *envies* the *one* who *holds* the *proofs*, and the *envy* *converts* the *knowledge* from the *instrument* of the *unity* into the *instrument* of the *dispute*. The commentators' *gloss* is *direct*: in *such* *instances*, it is *not* *knowledge* *itself* that *leads* them to *differ*, but the *manner* in which *some* *seek* *leadership* and *advancement* *through* such *knowledge*, and *thus* to the *envy* that *arises* from *it*. The *division* is thus the *division* of the *hierarchy*, not the *division* of the *truth*: the *truth* is *one*, the *proofs* are *clear*, and the *division* is the *division* over the *authority* that the *truth* *carries* — the *division* over *who* *commands*, *who* *interprets*, *who* *holds* the *proofs* and *wields* them. The *sūrah's* *warning* is the *warning* the *engine* delivers: the *community* that *receives* the *knowledge* is the *community* that *must* *guard* the *knowledge* from the *envy*, and the *guard* is the *guard* that the *sūrah's* *next* verses will *describe* in the *register* of the *Prophet's* *placement* upon the *clear* *path*.

**"Thy Lord Will Surely Judge Between Them on the Day of Resurrection": The Verdict Deferred**

The sūrah's final clause — *yaḍīnahu Rabbuka baynahum yawm al-qiyāmati fī mā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn* — "Thy Lord will surely judge between them on the Day of Resurrection regarding that wherein they used to differ" — states the *verdict* that the sūrah's *argument* *defers*, and the *deferral* is the *deferral* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *thesis*. The *verdict* is *deferred* to the *Day* of *Resurrection*: the *Lord* will *judge* between the *divided*, the *Lord* will *settle* the *dispute*, the *Lord* will *determine* which *party* *held* the *truth* and which *party* *held* the *envy*. The *logic* of the *deferral* is the *logic* the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *dispute* *cannot* be *settled* in *this* *world*, because the *dispute* is the *dispute* over the *authority* that the *truth* *carries*, and the *authority* is the *authority* that *only* the *Lord* can *determine* — the *Lord* who *gave* the *proofs*, the *Lord* who *knows* the *hearts*, the *Lord* who *sees* the *envy* that the *eye* of the *world* cannot *see*. The *Book's* *own* *consistent* *usage* is the sūrah's *standing* *parallel* (2:113; 10:93; 16:124; 32:25; 39:3), and the *parallel* is the *parallel* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *verdict* is *deferred*, and the *deferral* is the *deferral* that *secures* the *Lord's* *authority* over the *dispute* — the *authority* that the *sūrah's* *final* section will *describe* in its *full* eschatological form.

**The Sūrah's Turn: From the Precedent to the Address**

Verse 17 completes the sūrah's precedent section (vv. 16–17), and the section is the section that the sūrah's structure requires: the *gifts* (v. 16), the *proofs* (v. 17), the *division* (v. 17), and the *verdict* deferred to the *Day* (v. 17). The *next* verse turns the sūrah's address from the *precedent* to the *Prophet* — the *address* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *final* *revelation*: *Then* We *placed* *thee* upon a *clear* *path* *from* the *Command*; *so* *follow* it, and *follow* *not* the *caprices* of *those* who *know* *not*. The *turn* is the *turn* that the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *precedent* of the *Israelites* is the *precedent* that *warns* the *reader* of *any* *century*, and the *address* to the *Prophet* is the *address* that *completes* the *warning* in the *direction* of the *final* *revelation* — the *revelation* that was *given* the *clear* *path*, the *path* that the *Israelites'* *division* had *destroyed*, and the *path* that the sūrah's *next* verses will *name* by the *word* that the *sūrah* *alone* carries: *sharīʿah*. The reader leaves verse 17 with the sūrah's thesis in its full force: the *proofs* are *given*, the *knowledge* *arrives*, the *envy* *produces* the *division*, and the *verdict* is *deferred* to the *Day* — and the *address* that follows is the *address* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *final* *revelation*.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:18

> **Then We placed thee upon a clear path from the Command; so follow it, and follow not the caprices of those who know not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Then We Placed Thee Upon a Clear Path": The Placement That Completes the Precedent**

The sūrah, having completed its precedent of the Children of Israel (vv. 16–17), now turns its address to the Prophet ﷺ, and the turn is the sūrah's *rhetorical* *peak*: *thumma wajalnāka ʿalā sharīʿatin min al-amr* — "Then We placed thee upon a clear way from the Command." The *word* *thumma* — "then" — is the sūrah's *connector*, and the *connector* is the *connector* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *Israelites* were *given* the *Book*, the *judgment*, the *prophethood*, the *proofs*, and the *good* *things* — and they *divided*; and *then* — *after* the *precedent*, *after* the *division*, *after* the *verdict* deferred to the *Day* — *God* *placed* the *Prophet* upon the *clear* *path*. The *placement* is the *placement* that the *commentators* *describe*: after *giving* the *Israelites* *gifts* (v. 16), *God* *placed* the *Prophet* *Muḥammad* upon a *clear* *path* like *those* upon which He had *placed* the *messengers* before *him* (al-Ṭabarī). The *path* is thus the *path* of the *messengers*, the *path* that the *messengers* *walked*, the *path* that the *Israelites'* *division* had *abandoned*, and the *path* that the sūrah's *final* *revelation* has *restored*. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *placement* makes *visible*: the *final* *revelation* is not a *new* *path*; it is the *restoration* of the *path*, the *restoration* of the *way* that the *messengers* *walked*, the *restoration* of the *clear* *way* that the *division* had *obscured*.

**"Sharīʿah": The Word That Names the Law, and the Path That Leads to Water**

The *word* *sharīʿah* is the sūrah's *unique* *contribution* to the *Book's* *vocabulary*, and the *contribution* is the *contribution* that the *sūrah's* *name* *carries*: the *word* *occurs* *only* *this* *one* *time* in the *Qur'an*, and the *one* *occurrence* is the *occurrence* that *later* *became* the *technical* *term* for *Islamic* *Law*. The *connector* *dhālika bi-ann* — "that is because" — is the sūrah's *technical* *term*, and the *term* is the *term* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *consequence* stated in *verse* 34 (the *forgetting*, the *reversal* of the *refuge*, the *withdrawal* of the *protection*) is the *consequence* of the *two* *causes* — the *mockery* of the *signs* and the *delusion* of *this* *world*. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *reason* makes *visible*: the *consequence* is not a *consequence* that is *imposed* from *outside*; the *consequence* is a *consequence* that is *produced* from *within* — the *consequence* of the *refuser's* *own* *choices*, and the *choices* are the *two* *choices* that the sūrah's *argument* has *been* *describing* since *verse* 9.

**"You Took the Signs of God in Mockery": The First Cause**

The sūrah's first *cause* is the *mockery* of the *signs*, and the *mockery* is the *mockery* that the sūrah's *ninth* *verse* had *already* *described* in the *form* of the *social* *mockery* (*And* *when* *he* *comes* *to* *know* *aught* of *Our* *signs*, *he* *takes* *them* *in* *mockery*). The *mockery* is the *mockery* in *both* *registers* — the *signs* of *creation* and the *signs* of *revelation* (see the commentary on 45:9 and 45:31) — and the *mockery* is the *mockery* that *forbids* the *recognition* of the *signs*, and the *forbidding* is the *forbidding* that the sūrah's *thirty-first* *verse* had *already* *named* in the *form* of the *rebuke* (*Were* *not* *My* *signs* *recited* *unto* *you?*). The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *first* *cause* makes *visible*: the *mockery* is the *mockery* that the *refuser* *chose*, and the *choice* is the *choice* that *produces* the *consequence* — the *consequence* that the sūrah's *thirty-third* *verse* had *already* *stated* in the *form* of the *besetting* (*that* *which* *they* *used* to *mock* *will* *beset* *them*).

**"The Life of This World Has Deluded You": The Second Cause**

The sūrah's second *cause* is the *delusion* of *this* *world*, and the *delusion* is the *delusion* that the sūrah's *argument* *requires*. The *word* *ghartahum* — "has deluded you" — is from the root *gh-r-w*, the root of the *delusion*, the *deception*, the *enticing*, and in the sūrah's usage the root names the *world* that *entices* — the *world* that *appears* as the *real*, the *world* that *appears* as the *permanent*, the *world* that *appears* as the *sufficient*. The *Book's* *own* *statement* of the *same* *logic* is the sūrah's *standing* *parallel*:

> *"And the life of this world is but enjoyment of delusion."* (Qur'an 3:185)
> *"The life of this world is but play and diversion, and the home of the Hereafter — that is the true life, if they but knew."* (Qur'an 29:64, in its sense)

The *parallel* is the *parallel* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *world* is the *world* of the *delusion*, and the *delusion* is the *delusion* that the *refuser* *accepts* — the *acceptance* that the sūrah's *twenty-fourth* *verse* had *already* *named* in the *form* of the *materialist's* *thesis* (*There* *is* *naught* *but* *our* *life* *in* *this* *world*). The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *second* *cause* makes *visible*: the *delusion* is the *delusion* that the *refuser* *chose*, and the *choice* is the *choice* that *produces* the *consequence* — the *consequence* that the sūrah's *thirty-third* *verse* had *already* *stated* in the *form* of the *manifestation* (*The* *evils* of *that* *which* *they* *had* *done* *will* *become* *manifest*). The *Book's* *own* *statement* of the *comparison* is the sūrah's *standing* *parallel*: the *enjoyment* of the *life* of *this* *world*, *compared* with the *Hereafter*, *is* *but* *a* *little* (9:38; cf. 4:77; 13:26; 40:39).

**"Today They Will Not Be Removed from It": The Permanence of the Consequence**

The sūrah's third clause — *fa-yawma lā yunqalawna ʿanhā* — "So today they will not be removed from it" — states the *permanence* of the *consequence*, and the *permanence* is the *permanence* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*. The *refuser* will *not* be *removed* from the *Fire* — the *removal* that the *refuser* *hopes* for is the *removal* that the *sūrah's* *argument* *forbids*, and the *forbidding* is the *forbidding* that the *two* *causes* *produce*. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *permanence* makes *visible*: the *consequence* is the *consequence* that the *causes* *produce*, and the *production* is the *production* that *forbids* the *removal* — the *removal* that the *refuser's* *own* *choices* have *exhausted*.

**"Nor Can They Make Amends": The Refusal of the Respite**

The sūrah's final clause — *wa-lā hum yunẓarūn* — "nor can they make amends" — states the *refusal* of the *respite*, and the *refusal* is the *refusal* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*. The *respite* that the *refuser* *hopes* for is the *respite* that the *sūrah's* *argument* *forbids*, and the *forbidding* is the *forbidding* that the *Book's* *own* *verses* *state* in the *form* of the *argument* that the *refuser's* *own* *will* *demonstrates*:

> *"And if you were to return them, they would return to that of which they were forbidden. And the disbelievers are losers."* (Qur'an 6:28, in its sense; cf. 23:100; 32:12; 35:37)

The *logic* of the *refusal* is the *logic* the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *refuser* has *demonstrated* his *will* — the *will* that *chose* the *mockery*, the *will* that *chose* the *delusion*, the *will* that *refused* the *signs* — and the *demonstration* of the *will* is the *demonstration* that *forbids* the *respite*, because the *respite* would *only* *produce* the *same* *choice* again. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *refusal* makes *visible*: the *refuser* is *not* *given* the *respite* not because the *respite* is *withheld* *cruelly*; the *respite* is *withheld* because the *refuser's* *own* *will* has *demonstrated* that the *respite* would *produce* the *same* *choice* — the *choice* that the sūrah's *argument* has *been* *describing* since *verse* 7.

**The Sūrah's Turn: From the Reason to the Praise**

Verse 35 completes the sūrah's reason section (vv. 35), and the section is the section that the sūrah's structure requires: the *reason* stated (v. 35), the *first* *cause* (the *mockery*, v. 35), the *second* *cause* (the *delusion*, v. 35), the *permanence* of the *consequence* (v. 35), and the *refusal* of the *respite* (v. 35). The *next* verse turns the sūrah's address from the *reason* to the *praise* — the *praise* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *doxology*: *Unto* *God* *be* *praise, Lord* of the *heavens* *and* *Lord* of the *earth, Lord* of the *worlds.* The *turn* is the *turn* that the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *reason* is the *reason* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *ground*, and the *praise* is the *praise* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *doxology*. The reader leaves verse 35 with the sūrah's thesis in its full force: the *consequence* is the *consequence* of the *two* *causes*, the *permanence* is the *permanence* that the *causes* *produce*, and the *refusal* of the *respite* is the *refusal* that the *will* *demonstrates* — and the *praise* that follows is the *praise* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *doxology*.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:36

> **Unto God be praise, Lord of the heavens and Lord of the earth, Lord of the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Doxology That Closes the Sūrah: From the Terror to the Praise**

The sūrah, having completed its eschatological climax (vv. 27–35), now closes with the doxology — the praise that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *doxology*: *subḥāna Allāhi Rabb al-samāwāti wa-Rabb al-arḍi wa-Rabb al-ʿālamīn* — "Unto God be praise, Lord of the heavens and Lord of the earth, Lord of the worlds." The *turn* from the *terror* of the *Day* (the *kneeling*, the *book*, the *forgetting*, the *delusion*) to the *praise* is the *turn* that the sūrah's *method* *requires*: the *sūrah* that *opened* with the *identification* of the *Book* (v. 2) *closes* with the *praise* of the *Author*, and the *praise* is the *praise* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *doxology*. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *turn* makes *visible*: the *sūrah* is not a *sūrah* of *terror*; the *sūrah* is a *sūrah* of *terror* *and* *praise* — the *terror* that *warns* the *refuser*, and the *praise* that *completes* the *argument* in the *direction* of the *Author*.

**"Unto God Be Praise": The Command of the Purification**

The sūrah's word *subḥāna* — "praise" / "glory" / "purification" — is the sūrah's *technical* *term*, and the *term* is the *term* that the *commentators* *mark* with the *precision* the *sūrah's* *gravity* *requires*. According to the sūrah's base commentary, *unto* *God* *be* *praise* is *understood* as an *injunction* to *praise* *God* (al-Ṭabarī, in the sūrah's base commentary) — the *injunction* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*. The *praise* is the *praise* that *purifies* *God* from what the *disbelievers* *ascribed* to *Him* — the *ascription* of the *passage* of *time* as the *agent* (v. 24), the *ascription* of the *caprice* as the *deity* (v. 23), the *ascription* of the *mockery* as the *response* (v. 9) — and the *purification* is the *purification* that the sūrah's *argument* has *been* *building* *toward* since *verse* 2. The *Prophetic* *tradition* *supplies* the *word* that *states* the *logic* of the *praise* in its *full* *form*:

> *"Whoever does not praise God has wronged Him, but He is above that of Him."* (al-Bukhārī 6252; Muslim 71, from Abū Hurayrah)

The *word* is the *word* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *logic*: the *one* who *withholds* the *praise* *wrong* *God* — the *wrong* that the *sūrah's* *argument* has *been* *describing* in the *form* of the *refuser's* *refusal* — and the *wrong* is the *wrong* that *God* *is* *above* (the *above* that the sūrah's *thirty-seventh* *verse* will *name* in the *form* of the *Grandeur*). The *Prophetic* *tradition* *supplies* *further* the *word* that *states* the *weight* of the *praise* in the *Day*:

> *"Two words light on the tongue, heavy on the Scales, and beloved to the Most Merciful: Subḥāna Allāhi wa-bi-ḥamdih (Glory be to God and praise be to Him), Subḥāna-llāhi al-ʿAẓīm (Glory be to God, the Magnificent)."* (al-Bukhārī 6682; Muslim 2694, from al-Mughīrah)

The *word* is the *word* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *praise* is the *praise* that is *light* on the *tongue* and *heavy* on the *Scales* — the *praise* that the sūrah's *thirty-sixth* *verse* *commands*, and the *command* that the sūrah's *argument* has *been* *building* *toward* since *verse* 2.

**"Lord of the Heavens and Lord of the Earth, Lord of the Worlds": The Triple Sovereignty**

The sūrah's triple *Lāh* — *Rabb al-samāwāti wa-Rabb al-arḍi wa-Rabb al-ʿālamīn* — "Lord of the heavens and Lord of the earth, Lord of the worlds" — states the *sovereignty* in the sūrah's most *complete* *form*, and the *sovereignty* is the *sovereignty* that the sūrah's *twenty-seventh* *verse* had *already* *declared* in the *form* of the *declaration* (*Unto* *God* *belongs* *sovereignty* *over* the *heavens* and the *earth*). The *triple* *Lāh* is the *triple* *Lāh* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *Lord* of the *heavens* (the *signs* of *verse* 3), the *Lord* of the *earth* (the *signs* of *verses* 4–5), and the *Lord* of the *worlds* (the *whole* of the *created* *order*, the *whole* that the sūrah's *thirteenth* *verse* had *already* *named* in the *form* of the *subjection*). The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *triple* *Lāh* makes *visible*: the *sovereignty* is the *sovereignty* of the *whole* — the *whole* of the *heavens*, the *whole* of the *earth*, and the *whole* of the *worlds* — and the *sovereignty* is the *sovereignty* that the sūrah's *argument* has *been* *building* *toward* since *verse* 2.

**The Sūrah's Turn: From the Praise to the Grandeur**

Verse 36 completes the sūrah's doxology section (vv. 36), and the section is the section that the sūrah's structure requires: the *turn* from the *terror* to the *praise* (v. 36), the *command* of the *purification* (v. 36), and the *triple* *sovereignty* (v. 36). The *next* — and *final* — *verse* turns the sūrah's address from the *praise* to the *Grandeur* — the *Grandeur* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *close*: *His* *is* the *Grandeur* *in* the *heavens* and the *earth, and* *He* *is* the *Mighty, the* *Wise.* The *turn* is the *turn* that the sūrah's *argument* requires: the *praise* is the *praise* that *purifies* the *Author*, and the *Grandeur* is the *Grandeur* that *magnifies* the *Author* — the *two* *together* *completing* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *doxology*. The reader leaves verse 36 with the sūrah's thesis in its full force: the *sūrah* *closes* with the *praise*, the *praise* *purifies* the *Author* from the *ascriptions* of the *refuser*, and the *triple* *sovereignty* *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *whole* — and the *Grandeur* that follows is the *Grandeur* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *close*.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:37

> **His is the Grandeur in the heavens and the earth, and He is the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"His Is the Grandeur": The Command to Magnify the Author**

The sūrah's final verse closes the sūrah's argument in the sūrah's most *sovereign* *declaration*: *lahu al-jalāl fī al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-huwa al-ʿazīzu al-ḥakīm* — "His is the Grandeur in the heavens and the earth, and He is the Mighty, the Wise." The *word* *al-jalāl* — "the Grandeur" — is the sūrah's *technical* *term*, and the *term* is the *term* that the *commentators* *mark* with the *precision* the *sūrah's* *gravity* *requires*. According to the sūrah's base commentary, *His* *is* the *Grandeur* (or *"unto* *Him* *is* the *Grandeur"*) is an *injunction* to *magnify* *God*, *since* the *effects* of *His* *Grandeur* *and* *Greatness* *are* *manifest* *in* *all* *things* (al-Ṭabarī, in the sūrah's base commentary). The *injunction* is the *injunction* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *Grandeur* is the *Grandeur* that the *refuser* *denied* (the *Grandeur* of the *sovereignty* that the *refuser* *attributed* to the *passage* of *time*, v. 24), and the *injunction* to *magnify* is the *injunction* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *close*. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *Grandeur* makes *visible*: the *Grandeur* is the *Grandeur* that is *manifest* in *all* *things* — the *Grandeur* of the *heavens*, the *Grandeur* of the *earth*, and the *Grandeur* of the *whole* — and the *injunction* to *magnify* is the *injunction* that the *manifestation* *produces*.

**The Bracketing of the Sūrah: Verse 2 and Verse 37**

The sūrah's final verse *brackets* the sūrah's second verse, and the *bracketing* is the *bracketing* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *architecture*. The *reference* to *God* *as* the *Mighty, the* *Wise* in *verse* 2 refers to *God's* *Reality* *in* *relation* to *revelation* (the *Book* from *God, the Mighty, the Wise*), and the *reference* in *verse* 37 refers to *God's* *Reality* *in* *relation* to *creation* (the *Grandeur* in the *heavens* and the *earth*). The *bracketing* is the *bracketing* that *highlights* the *discussion* of *God's* *signs* in *revelation* *and* *creation* that *runs* throughout the *sūrah*: the *sūrah* *opened* with the *signs* of *revelation* (the *Book*, v. 2) and the *signs* of *creation* (vv. 3–5), and the *sūrah* *closes* with the *Grandeur* in *creation* (the *heavens* and the *earth*, v. 37) and the *attributes* of the *Author* (the *Mighty, the* *Wise*, v. 37). The *two* *references* *together* state the *sūrah's* *thesis* in its *full* *form*: the *signs* of *revelation* and the *signs* of *creation* are the *signs* of the *one* *Author*, and the *Author* is the *Author* who is *Mighty* and *Wise* in *both* *registers*.

**The Two Senses of the Truth: The Sūrah's Spine, Completed**

The sūrah's final verse *completes* the sūrah's *spine* — the *spine* of the *truth* (*bi-l-ḥaqq*) that the sūrah's *sixth* *verse* had *stated* in the *form* of the *signs* *recited* *unto* *thee* *in* *truth*, and the *spine* that the sūrah's *twenty-second* *verse* had *stated* in the *form* of the *creation* *in* *truth*. The *sūrah's* *argument* is the *argument* that the *two* *kinds* of *signs* — the *signs* of *revelation* and the *signs* of *creation* — are *said* to *come* *from* *Him* *in* *truth*, *or* *"for* the *truth"*: for *verses* regarding *revelation* *in* *truth*, see 2:176, 213, 252; 3:3, 108; 4:105; 5:48; 6:114; 17:105; for *those* regarding *creation* *in* *truth*, see 6:73; 10:5; 14:19; 15:85; 16:3; 29:44; 30:8; 39:5; 44:38–39; 45:22; 46:3; 64:3. The *sūrah's* *point* is the *point* the *spine* makes *visible*: the *sūrah* is the *sūrah* of the *signs* — the *signs* of *revelation* and the *signs* of *creation* — and the *signs* are the *signs* that *come* *in* *truth*, and the *truth* is the *truth* that the sūrah's *final* *verse* *completes* in the *form* of the *Grandeur* and the *attributes* of the *Author*.

**The Sūrah Complete: From the Letters to the Grandeur**

The sūrah is now *complete*. The *sūrah* *opened* with *two* *letters* (v. 1) — the *letters* that *named* the *Ḥawāmīm* and *set* the *threshold* of the *sūrah* of *signs* — and the *sūrah* *closes* with the *Grandeur* in the *heavens* and the *earth* (v. 37) — the *Grandeur* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument* in the *direction* of the *Author*. The *sūrah* *displayed* the *signs* (vv. 3–6), *portrayed* the *refuser* (vv. 7–11), *displayed* the *blessings* (vv. 12–13), *commanded* the *forgiveness* (v. 14), *stated* the *law* of the *account* (v. 15), *introduced* the *precedent* (vv. 16–17), *addressed* the *Prophet* (vv. 18–19), *declared* the *criterion* (v. 20), *rebuked* the *supposition* (v. 21), *stated* the *ground* (v. 22), *dissected* the *anatomy* (v. 23), *refuted* the *thesis* (vv. 24–26), and *described* the *Day* (vv. 27–35) — and the *sūrah* *closed* with the *praise* and the *Grandeur* (vv. 36–37). The *sūrah's* *thesis* is the *thesis* that the *final* *verse* *completes*: the *signs* are *given* in *truth*, the *refuser* is the *refuser* who *refuses* and *mocks* and *places* the *substitutes* in the *position* of the *trust*, the *Day* is the *Day* of the *account*, and the *Author* is the *Author* who is *Mighty* and *Wise* in *both* *registers* — the *register* of *revelation* and the *register* of *creation*.

**The Believer's Response: Praise and Magnification**

The sūrah's final verse *commands* the *believer's* *response*, and the *response* is the *response* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *praise* of the *thirty-sixth* *verse* and the *magnification* of the *thirty-seventh* *verse* — the *two* *together* *constituting* the *believer's* *standing* *response* to the *Author*. The *Prophetic* *tradition* *supplies* the *word* that *states* the *response* in its *full* *form*:

> *"Two words light on the tongue, heavy on the Scales, and beloved to the Most Merciful: Subḥāna Allāhi wa-bi-ḥamdih (Glory be to God and praise be to Him), Subḥāna-llāhi al-ʿAẓīm (Glory be to God, the Magnificent)."* (al-Bukhārī 6682; Muslim 2694, from al-Mughīrah)

The *word* is the *word* that *completes* the *sūrah's* *argument*: the *praise* and the *magnification* are the *praise* and the *magnification* that are *light* on the *tongue* and *heavy* on the *Scales* — the *response* that the sūrah's *final* *two* *verses* *command*, and the *command* that the sūrah's *argument* has *been* *building* *toward* since *verse* 2. The reader leaves the sūrah with the *sūrah's* *thesis* in its *full* *force*: the *signs* are *given*, the *refuser* is *portrayed*, the *Day* is *described*, and the *Author* is *Mighty* and *Wise* — and the *response* is the *response* of the *praise* and the *magnification*, the *response* that the sūrah's *final* *verse* *completes* in the *form* of the *Grandeur* and the *attributes* of the *Author*. Al-Jāthiyah is *complete* — from *Ḥā. Mīm* to *the* *Mighty, the* *Wise* — and the *sūrah* of *signs* has *spoken* its *signs* in *truth*, and the *truth* is the *truth* that the *Author* *is*, and the *Author* is the *Author* who is *Mighty* and *Wise* in the *heavens* and the *earth* and in the *worlds*.
