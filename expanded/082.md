# Sūrah al-Infiṭār (Chapter 82) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter of the Cleaving Sky**

Sūrah al-Infiṭār takes its name from the opening verb *infiṭarat* — "it was cleft asunder" — which pictures the vault of heaven being split open as one of the unmistakable overtures of the Day of Resurrection. The chapter is among the early Makkan revelations, revealed in the period when the Qur'an's central burden was to turn a heedless people toward their Lord by awakening them to the certainty of the Reckoning. It sits, in the arrangement of the written muṣḥaf, immediately before Sūrah 83, al-Muṭaffifīn ("The Defrauders"), and immediately after Sūrah 81, al-Takwīr ("The Enfolding"), and the three form a continuous meditation on the upheaval of the world's end and the moral accounting that follows it. The classical commentators note how these short, powerful sūrahs of the early period work like bells rung at dawn: each is brief, each strikes a single great chord with mounting force, and together they are meant to be recited often, carried in the memory, and turned over in the heart on ordinary days so that the extraordinary Day is never far from a believer's thoughts.

**The Architecture of the Sūrah**

The chapter unfolds in a clear three-part movement, and grasping that movement is the key to understanding every verse that follows. The first five verses paint the cosmic upheaval: the sky is cleft asunder, the stars are scattered, the seas burst forth, the graves are overturned, and in the middle of this collapsing cosmos each soul suddenly knows what it has sent forth and what it has left behind. The opening is deliberately spectacular — the reader is shown the entire familiar order of the universe dismantled — and yet the sūrah's aim is never the spectacle for its own sake. The cataclysm is staged so that a single, shattering recognition can land: *every soul will know what it has done.* The universe is being re-arranged into a courtroom.

The second movement, verses 6–12, turns from the scene of the Resurrection to the moral scandal it exposes: how, the sūrah demands in astonishment, can a human being who was so carefully created, so proportioned and assembled by a noble Lord, so generously served by guardian angels who record everything, nevertheless deny the religion and the judgment? The wonder here is not merely rhetorical; it is an argument from creation to gratitude, from gratitude to obligation, from obligation to the certainty that a Record is being kept.

The final movement, verses 13–19, renders the verdict that the opening upheaval has prepared: the pious shall be in bliss, the profligate shall burn in Hellfire, none shall be absent from that recompense, and — in the chapter's famous closing crescendo — the Day is a day in which no soul will avail another soul in any way, and the Command that Day is with God. The sūrah thus traces the entire moral drama of the human being in nineteen verses: creation, ingratitude, denial, divine guardianship, the Record, the two destinies, and the absolute solitude of the final Judgment. It is a complete theology of human responsibility compressed into the space of a single page.

**The Place of the Sūrah in the Life of the Believer**

Because of its brevity, its rhythm, and its haunting theme, al-Infiṭār has always been a favorite for recitation in the ordinary prayers and at funerals, where its imagery of graves overturned and souls awakened speaks directly to the bereaved. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have encouraged reciting short sūrahs of this kind in the night prayer and in the moments of intense supplication, and many of the narrations that mention al-Infiṭār link it with al-Takwīr (81) and al-Inshiqāq (84) as chapters whose reading keeps the Hereafter vivid. The sūrah performs this function with peculiar efficiency: it does not argue about the Last Day so much as bring its reader, in nineteen swift verses, to stand inside the Day itself — sky splitting above, graves opening beneath, the guardian angels' record spread open, and the soul left alone with what it has sent forth. To recite al-Infiṭār is, in the words of the commentators, to take one's own soul to account before the Account begins.

**How to Read What Follows**

As in the preceding chapters, the commentary below treats each verse as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order. Each verse is quoted individually from the sūrah's canonical text and then expanded through its Arabic roots and morphology, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and the reports traced to Ibn ʿAbbās), authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the internal logic of the teaching, and its bearing on modern life. Verses that the source groups together are here separated and given their own full treatment, so that nothing is compressed because the chapter is short or long. The commentary honors the sūrah's own method: it does not rush. It lingers on each verse as a lesson complete in itself, from the first word — *When the sky is cleft asunder* — to the last — *and the Command that Day is with God.*

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:1

> **When the sky is cleft asunder,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Word: A Verb That Names the Chapter**

The sūrah opens in the manner of so many of the early Makkan revelations — not with a statement about the Hereafter but with an event that draws the listener into the middle of it: *idhā al-samāʾu infaṭarat.* The particle *idhā*, "when," is the particle of sudden, inescapable occurrence; it signals an event that comes without warning and admits of no postponement. The verb *infiṭarat* is built on the eighth form of the root *f-ṭ-r*, and its meaning must be carefully distinguished from the verbs used in neighboring sūrahs for the same cosmic event. In Sūrah 84 the sky "is split apart" (*inshaqqat*, from *sh-q-q*, to cleave lengthwise); in 55:37 it "is rent" (*infaṭarat* also appears, while 69:16 uses *inḥaqqat*, to fall asunder). The root *f-ṭ-r* is rich: it is the root of *fiṭrah* — the original nature with which God created human beings — and of *faṭara*, "He created" or "He brought into being from nothing," as in the verse "Set your face toward the religion, as a ḥanīf, upon the fiṭrah of God upon which He created (faṭara) mankind" (30:30). The verb *infaṭarat* therefore carries, beneath the picture of a sky being split open, the resonance of a thing being returned to the condition of its first making — a breaking open that reveals what lies beneath the familiar surface, just as creation itself was once a breaking open of what was hidden.

**What Is Beneath the Sky**

The commentators explain the picture with reference to the manner in which the sky will be torn: not merely cracked but opened so that its true reality is exposed. Ibn Kathīr gathers the classical descriptions: the sky of this world is not a solid ceiling but a created order of "seven heavens," and on the Day of Resurrection it will be folded, unrolled, and split so that what lies beyond it — the Throne, the angels in their ranks, the very realities of the unseen world — becomes visible to the sight of the gathered. Al-Ṭabarī reports from the early authorities that this cleaving is the splitting that Sūrah 78 describes in another image: "and the sky is opened, and becomes as gates" (78:19), and that Sūrah 25 pictures with the words "on the Day when the sky is cleft asunder with the clouds" (25:25). The same event, then, is depicted from many angles across the Qur'an — rent, unrolled, opened into gates, cleft asunder with clouds — each image emphasizing one aspect of the single reality: the present order, which seems so solid and permanent, is in truth a curtain, and on that Day the curtain is torn away.

**Why the Qur'an Opens With the Collapse of the Visible**

The rhetorical power of beginning the sūrah in this way deserves reflection, because it explains the whole method of the early Makkan revelation. Human beings, left to themselves, tend to treat the visible world as the whole of reality: the sky above and the earth beneath seem as permanent as anything can be, and on that permanence people build their complacency — "the sky will not fall," they say in a dozen languages, meaning the established order is safe. The Qur'an attacks that complacency at its root by showing the sky itself, the very symbol of permanence, being cleft asunder like a garment torn in two. Al-Rāzī notes the pedagogical purpose: the most arrogant man, who believes he has conquered circumstances and arranged his life securely, is being shown that the roof over his entire arrangement — the heavens under which all his plans are made — is itself a thing that will be taken apart. When the frame of the picture is destroyed, the picture cannot remain. The verse thus performs, in a single stroke, what all the philosophy of impermanence attempts: it detaches the soul from its unthinking trust in the durability of the world.

**The Sky in Modern Experience**

The modern reader, who has photographed the heavens with instruments the ancients never imagined, might suppose this imagery belongs to a pre-scientific age. The opposite is the case. The more we have learned of the universe, the more the Qur'an's central claim about the sky is confirmed: the heavens are not a fixed and final ceiling but a contingent, finite, continually rearranging order. Stars are born and die; galaxies drift; the very expansion of the universe points to a beginning and, by implication, to an end. That the Qur'an, revealed in a desert where the night sky seemed the most eternal thing a human being could see, should repeatedly and confidently announce its dissolution, is itself one of its remarkable features. The believer who reads this verse with modern knowledge is not reading a myth that science has outdated; he is reading a description, in the language of its first hearers, of the contingency that physics itself now affirms: the visible universe is not self-sustaining; it depends, moment by moment, on the One who cleft it into being and who will on the appointed Day cleave it asunder.

**A Verse That Prepares the Ear for the Next**

Finally, the verse should be read as an opening chord that resolves only at the end of the sūrah. The sky is cleft asunder — but the sūrah does not yet say why, or for whom. It withholds the answer with the patience of a sermon building toward its point. Verse by verse the scene expands — stars scattered, seas bursting, graves overturned — until the fifth verse reveals the purpose of the entire upheaval: *then shall each soul know what it has sent forth and what it has left behind.* The sky is not torn apart for its own sake; it is torn apart so that nothing can any longer hide. The great cosmic dismantling is, in the end, an act of divine mercy toward justice — the removal of every veil so that the truth of every life may finally be seen. That is the destination toward which the very first word of the sūrah is already traveling.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:2

> **and when the stars are dispersed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Sky-Catastrophe: The Scattering of the Lights**

The scene of the disintegrating cosmos continues with the fate of the stars: *wa idhā al-kawākibu intatharat.* The word *kawākib* (singular *kawkab*) is the Qur'an's general term for the heavenly bodies — stars and planets — while other passages use *nujūm* for the stars in their courses. The verb *intatharat* (from *n-th-r*) carries the meaning of being scattered, strewn, dispersed, and dropped in all directions, like seed cast from a hand or like beads snapped from a string. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr explain that the stars, which once were fixed lights serving travelers and seasons and the reckoning of time, will fall from their places and be scattered across the void — no longer ordered, no longer serving their appointed functions. The verse does not describe an explosion so much as a dispersal: the carefully arranged constellations, which the Arabs had mapped for navigation and for poetry, will be dissolved into a disorder of falling lights.

**The Same Event From Many Angles**

The Qur'an returns to the dissolution of the stars in several sūrahs, and each adds a shade of meaning. Sūrah 81 says "and when the stars are dimmed" (*inkadarat* — they will be extinguished, their light darkened). Sūrah 77 says "when the stars are blotted out" (*kuniṣat* — they will be rubbed out as one rubs out a writing or scatters dust). Here the emphasis is on *intithār* — the scattering and dropping. The commentators connect these pictures: first the stars are dimmed, then blotted, then dropped from their places and scattered. The sequence is the progressive dismantling of the celestial order that men had relied upon and even worshipped — for star-worship and the invocation of the planets were real features of the ancient Arabian and Near Eastern religious landscape, and the Qur'an is at pains to show that these objects of human veneration are themselves dependent creatures that will be cast down on the Day that only their Creator controls.

**The Stars as a Sign Already Given**

There is a subtle irony in the fact that the same heavens that announce the destruction of the sky are themselves among the Qur'an's most repeated proofs of God's power and mercy in the present world. God says elsewhere: "It is He Who made the stars for you, that you may be guided by them in the darkness of land and sea" (6:97), and "We have adorned the lowest heaven with the adornment of the stars" (37:6), and again "And by the stars they are guided" (16:16). In the ordinary course of life, the stars are navigators' lamps and poets' ornaments — instruments of guidance placed in the darkness. Their destruction on the Day of Judgment is therefore the withdrawal of a mercy, a sign that the world of signs itself is being rolled up because the travelers no longer need its guidance: the journey of this world is over, and the reckoning has come. Al-Qurṭubī draws this point out: if even the guidance-lights of the world are extinguished and scattered, how much more will every lesser reliance of the heedless — their patrons, their schemes, their earthly compasses — prove worthless on that Day.

**Order and Dispersal: A Lesson in Gratitude**

For the believer, the verse carries a practical lesson about gratitude and attention that applies long before the Last Day. The stars stand in the heavens as an order the human being did not create and cannot maintain; their regularity is a mercy he takes for granted. The Qur'an repeatedly calls this regularity to attention — "the sun and the moon are by a precise reckoning" (55:5) — and treats the reliable motion of the heavens as a daily evidence of a Sustainer's care. When the Qur'an then announces that this reliable order will one day be dispersed, it is teaching a double truth: first, that the order of the world is a gift, to be received with gratitude rather than taken as an entitlement; and second, that no part of that order is eternal — so that the wise do not build their hopes upon what will be scattered, but upon the One who will remain when the stars have fallen from their places. The same universe that invites the eye upward in wonder is the universe that one day will pour those wonders out like water, and only the soul that looked beyond the lights to their Maker will not find itself, on that Day, staring at an emptied sky.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:3

> **when the seas are burst forth,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Vault of Heaven to the Depths of the Earth**

The sūrah now descends from the sky to the seas: *wa idhā al-biḥāru fujjirat.* The word *biḥār* (singular *baḥr*) denotes the great bodies of water — the seas and oceans — and the verb *fujjirat* (from *f-j-r*) is the same root as *fajr*, "dawn," the breaking forth of light, and *infijār*, "explosion," the violent bursting forth. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī explain the picture: the seas, which in their settled state are held within their bounds, will on that Day be *burst forth* — their barriers broken, their waters released and made to overflow. The image is of the oceans being uncorked: the fountains of the deep are unsealed, and the waters that had been contained now pour out and cover what lies around them. The Qur'an describes the same event elsewhere with the picture of swelling: "and when the seas are set affame" (81:6) — a phrase the commentators read both as the seas being made to boil and overflow and as a sign of the extreme heat of that Day — and "by the sea set aflame" (52:6).

**The Breaking of the Barrier the Qur'an Elsewhere Praises**

The meaning of this bursting is deepened by the Qur'an's own account of the normal state of the seas, which it presents not as a mere fact of geography but as a sign of divine mercy and order. God says: "He released the two seas, meeting one another, between them a barrier they do not overstep" (55:19–20), and "And it is He who has let free the two seas, one palatable and sweet, and the other salty and bitter, and He has made between them a barrier and a forbidding partition" (25:53). The seas are thus depicted as two orders — sweet and salt — held apart by a boundary they "do not overstep." On the Day of Judgment, that boundary, that sign of divine ordering, is itself undone: the seas burst forth, their partitions dissolve, and what was separated is mixed into one overwhelming flood. The verse therefore announces the reversal of one of the Qur'an's own great natural signs: the same mercy that keeps the waters within their bounds and separates the sweet from the salt is the mercy that will, at the appointed hour, release those bounds so that the earth may be turned inside out for the gathering of all souls.

**The Seas as a Mirror of the Human Heart's Boundaries**

The commentators also draw from the root a moral resonance that the believer carries into ordinary life. The same root *f-j-r* gives the Qur'an its word for the *fājir* — the profligate, the wicked one — whose character is named in verse 14 of this very sūrah as the inhabitant of Hellfire. The root meaning is "to break open," and the commentators explain that the *fājir* is so named because he breaks open what should remain closed: the bounds of modesty, the limits of conscience, the barriers of law and decency that keep a life ordered and safe. The word *fujjirat* applied to the seas therefore shares its deepest resonance with the word applied to the wicked in verse 14: on that Day the seas break open because the order that held them has been dismantled, and the wicked are those who, in their lives, broke open the internal barriers that held *their* natures in a godly order. There is a quiet symmetry in the sūrah: the bursting of the seas and the bursting of the wicked man's moral bounds are, in the divine vocabulary, acts of the same kind — the violent release of what ought to have remained contained.

**A Practical Reflection: The Power That Holds the Waters**

For the believer, the verse is a daily invitation to wonder at a mercy so powerful it is normally invisible: the same oceans that can swallow ships and drown cities are kept, by a command, within their shores, so that the human being can walk the beach in safety. Every time the Qur'an speaks of the sea being held, it is pointing to a standing miracle of restraint — an almighty force exercising self-control for the sake of creatures who neither earned nor repay it. And because that restraint is an act of the divine will rather than a property of the water, it can be removed in an instant: the seas burst forth, and the familiar safety is gone. The lesson the believer draws is one of humility before the same power that both sustains and can dissolve the world's order. The sea that today gives fish and carries ships and teaches the child awe at the horizon is, by the word of its Maker, the same sea that on the Day of Judgment will be burst forth to announce that the world's usual mercies have come to their end — and that only the mercy of the Reckoning remains.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:4

> **and when graves are turned inside out,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Overture: The Earth Gives Up Its Dead**

The scene reaches its most personal register with the overturning of the graves: *wa idhā al-qubūru buʿthirat.* The word *qubūr* (singular *qabr*) needs no introduction — the grave, the resting place of the dead. The verb *buʿthirat* (from the root *b-ʿ-th-r*) has a distinctive range of meaning: to turn over, to scatter the earth, to dig up and reverse, and — crucially — the root is related to *baʿth*, the resurrection, the raising of the dead. The commentators describe the picture: the earth over the graves is turned over and scattered, and the dead who were buried within are brought forth, expelled from their resting places, and raised to stand. Al-Ṭabarī reports from the early authorities that *buʿthirat* means the graves are made to "cast out" what is in them — that they are emptied of their occupants, who rise to the summons of their Lord. The verse is the Qur'an's way of announcing the general resurrection: the earth that seemed to hold the dead securely, that swallowed generation after generation, is itself commanded to give them back, and it does so without a single one being lost.

**The Resurrection as a Mercy of Restoration**

The modern mind, shaped by the funeral and the closed casket, often reads the imagery of graves overturned with a shudder of finality — as though the verse were only about death's horror. The classical commentators read it with a different accent: as the announcement of death's defeat. Ibn Kathīr stresses that the same bodies that decayed and mingled with the earth will be restored, for the One who first created them from nothing is able to recreate them, a point the Qur'an makes with the most famous of its agricultural analogies: "Look at the effects of God's mercy — how He revives the earth after its death. Indeed, that is the Reviver of the dead, and He is over all things Powerful" (30:50). The overturned grave is not the last word; it is the penultimate word. What follows the grave's overturning is the standing of the soul before its Lord. The believer therefore does not read this verse with the despair of one who sees only an open pit, but with the awe of one who sees the earth itself commanded to surrender what it held — a surrender that is the necessary prelude to the reunion of every soul with the truth of its own deeds.

**The Gathering of All the Ages**

The overturning of the graves gathers into one image the entire human family across all of time. The sea delivers those who died at sea; the earth delivers those who died in the earth; the graves deliver those who were buried with rites, and the wilderness delivers those who were never buried at all. No one is omitted, because the resurrection is not a partial or selective event: "And the Horn is blown, and behold — from the graves they will hasten to their Lord" (36:51), and "the Day We shall call every people with their imam" (17:71). The Qur'an elsewhere gives a chilling picture of the swiftness of this gathering, comparing the multitudes emerging to "scattered locusts" (54:7) — a swarm beyond numbering, suddenly in motion. The grave, which had seemed the great equalizer that removed people from one another forever, turns out to have been only a waiting room; on the Day this verse announces, all the waiting is over, and the scattered human family is reassembled in the presence of the One to whom every one of them belongs.

**A Lesson in the Constancy of the Account**

For the believer living now, the verse performs a subtle but powerful psychological work. The grave is the one fact every human being prefers to keep at the edge of awareness; cultures build elaborate arrangements to delay, disguise, or beautify the confrontation with it. The Qur'an's method is the opposite — to bring the grave, and what lies beyond its overturning, into the center of daily consciousness, so that the soul conducts its affairs with the awareness that its resting place is not a final destination but a station of waiting. The Prophet ﷺ advised his companions to visit the graves, saying, according to the authentic report recorded by Muslim from Abū Hurayrah: "Visit the graves, for they remind one of death." And the Qur'an itself makes the reminder even sharper by overturning the grave in this verse: not merely reminding of death but showing the death that is not final — the death that gives way to a standing before God. The soul that keeps this verse before it does not grow morbid; it grows honest, because it can no longer pretend that the record of its deeds will end when its breathing ends. The grave is overturned precisely so that nothing the soul "sent forth" will remain buried with it.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:5

> **then shall each soul know what it has sent forth and what it has left behind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose of the Upheaval Finally Declared**

After four verses of cosmic catastrophe — sky cleft, stars scattered, seas burst forth, graves overturned — the sūrah at last reveals why the entire scene has been staged: *ʿalimat nafsun mā qaddamat wa akhkharat.* "Every soul shall know what it has sent forth and what it has left behind." The verb *ʿalimat* (from *ʿ-l-m*, to know) is placed in the past tense as an announcement of what is as good as certain — for God, the Day is already an accomplished reality, and the verse speaks of it with the confidence of a thing already decreed. The words *qaddamat* ("sent forth") and *akhkharat* ("left behind") are built on the roots for "before" (*q-d-m*) and "after" (*-kh-r*), and they set up the verse's central distinction: every soul will know both what it sent ahead of itself — its deeds, which travel before it to the Hereafter — and what it left behind — the deeds and their consequences that it left in this world when it departed.

**Two Glosses on "Sent Forth" and "Left Behind"**

The commentators offer two complementary explanations of the pair, and both are worth preserving because together they cover the whole field of a human life. In the first reading, favored by al-Ṭabarī and reported from the early authorities, "what it has sent forth" means the deeds — good and evil — that the soul actually performed and that therefore travel ahead of it to the Reckoning, while "what it has left behind" means the good deeds it was commanded to perform but left undone — the obligations abandoned, the virtues it never practiced, the good it could have done and did not. On this reading the verse is a terrifying audit of omission as well as commission: the soul will be confronted not only with what it did but with what it failed to do, and both will stand before it as its own.

In the second reading, which al-Ṭabarī also records and which he himself preferred with a subtle refinement, "left behind" refers to the practices and legacies the soul established in this world that continue to work after its death. Al-Ṭabarī argues that a deed cannot properly be said to be "left behind" unless it has actually been performed — one does not leave behind what one never did — and therefore the phrase points to "a good or evil practice that one has established, such that if another performs it after him, he who established it shares equally in the reward or the burden." This is the teaching of the ḥadīth recorded in Muslim from Abū Hurayrah, in which the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever calls to guidance will have a reward like the rewards of those who follow him, without that diminishing anything from their rewards; and whoever calls to misguidance will bear sin like the sins of those who follow him, without that diminishing anything from their sins."* (Muslim 2674)

On this reading, "what it has left behind" is the continuing harvest of influence: the child raised to righteousness, the charity whose stream continues, the example copied by others, the institution founded, the harmful habit normalized, the lie left standing. Every soul, the verse says, will know not only what it personally sent ahead but what it set in motion and left running behind it in the world — and will be credited or burdened accordingly.

**The Two Readings Together: A Complete Accounting**

The two readings are not rivals but complementary, and the mature believer holds both together. On the one hand, the soul will answer for its deeds done and its duties undone — the sum of its moral biography. On the other, it will answer for the continuing consequences of what it established — for the good and evil that outlived it through the lives it touched. This is why the Prophet ﷺ, in the tradition recorded by Muslim from Abū Hurayrah, listed among the deeds whose benefit continues for a believer after death "a continuing charity (ṣadaqah jāriyah), a knowledge from which benefit is drawn, and a righteous child who prays for him." The verse thus extends human responsibility beyond the grave in a way that gives the believer's present actions enormous weight: every good deed is seed planted for a harvest that may outlast its sower, and every evil deed is a fire whose smoke may drift into generations the doer will never see. To live with this verse in mind is to live as one who knows that nothing he does is finally his own private affair.

**The Sudden, Perfect Knowledge of the Day**

The verse's most important word, however, is the quiet verb at its center: *ʿalimat* — the soul *will know.* The commentators stress that on that Day knowledge is no longer a matter of argument, evidence, or faith; it becomes direct, undeniable, total. In this world the soul could argue with its conscience, rationalize its failures, forget its sins, and find excuses for its omissions. On that Day all of that is over: the soul knows, with the immediacy of a wound felt, exactly what it sent forth and what it left behind. Sūrah 75 gives the terrifying mechanics of this knowledge — "Indeed, man shall be a witness against himself, even though he puts forth his excuses" (75:14–15) — and Sūrah 36 makes it a feature of the day of standing: "That Day every soul will be requited for what it earned, and no wrong shall be done" (40:17). The overturning of graves in the previous verse is thus completed here: the bodies rise, but it is the *knowledge* that rises with them — the full, clear, undeniable knowledge of a life, no longer capable of being hidden, denied, or excused. And this is the point toward which the sūrah's opening cosmic collapse has been driving: the sky is torn, the stars scattered, the seas burst, the graves overturned — all so that nothing may any longer stand between the soul and the truth of its own deeds.

**A Practical Discipline for the Present Life**

The believer who takes this verse to heart does not wait for the Day to begin the audit. The early Muslims turned this verse into a discipline of self-examination: ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is famously reported to have said, "Call yourselves to account before you are called to account, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed against you." The verse supplies the very questions such self-accounting must ask, and they are the two questions of the text: *What am I sending forth?* — what deeds am I performing now that will travel ahead of me to the Reckoning — and *What am I leaving behind?* — what legacy, what influence, what continuing stream of good or harm am I setting in motion in the world I will one day leave? A life examined by these two questions cannot easily drift into heedlessness, because the examination itself keeps the soul oriented toward the horizon of the Day. The sūrah's gift to the believer is this: it does not leave the Day in the distant future but brings it into the present as a standing question, so that every action is done in the knowledge that it will one day be *known* — and known, first of all, by the very soul that performed it.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:6

> **O mankind! What has deluded you with regard to your noble Lord,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Scene of Judgment to the Astonishment at Ingratitude**

Having shown the Day in all its upheaval, the sūrah now turns — with a sudden, intimate directness — to address the human being who lived heedless of that Day: *yā ayyuhā al-insānu mā gharraka bi-rabbika al-karīm.* "O man! What has deluded you with regard to your noble Lord?" The address is to *al-insān*, "mankind" in the collective singular — every human being is meant — and the word *gharra* (from *gh-r-r*, to deceive, to beguile, to delude) is the verb of deception, the verb used of Satan himself, who is called *al-Gharūr*, "the Deluder," and of worldly illusion. The verse is a question of astonishment, and its astonishment is the astonishment of a Creator who cannot understand how a creature so favored could turn away: *What has deluded you with regard to your noble Lord?* The implied answer is that nothing — no power, no excuse — could possibly justify such delusion, because the Lord in question is *al-Karīm*, the Noble, the Generous, the One whose very character is bountiful favor.

**The Astonishment of a Loving Lord**

The classical commentators were struck by the affectionate, almost wounded tone of this question. Al-Qurṭubī observes that God does not here say "What has deluded you with regard to your terrible and punishing Lord?" but "with regard to your *noble*, *generous* Lord" — as though the very grounds of the delusion were being removed. The word *al-Karīm* is chosen precisely because it makes the ingratitude more scandalous: it is one thing to defy a tyrant whom one fears and hates; it is another to ignore, deny, and disobey a Lord whose defining attribute is generosity. Ibn Kathīr records that some of the early Muslims, hearing this verse, wept and answered its question with the confession that what had deluded them was their own concealing self — their heedlessness, their worldly hopes, their postponement of repentance. The question is thus not an accusation flung at a stranger; it is a summons issued to the soul that has, in the running of its days, forgotten the One who made it and favored it.

**Who Are the Deluders? The Life of This World and Satan**

The Qur'an elsewhere answers the question this verse poses, and its answers are remarkably precise. Sūrah 31 warns: "Let not the life of this world delude you, nor let the Deluder delude you concerning God" (31:33), and Sūrah 35 repeats the warning nearly word for word (35:5). The commentators identify the two great deluders that this verse's question implies. The first is the life of this world itself (*al-ḥayāt al-dunyā*) — its attractions, its distractions, its capacity to make the present seem all that matters and the Hereafter seem remote or unreal. The second is Satan, *al-Gharūr*, the Arch-Deluder, who whispers to the soul the twin falsehoods of despair and complacency — despair that God will not forgive, or complacency that He will not ask. Between these two, the human being can live a whole life deluded: absorbed in the world's immediate rewards and deaf to the world's ultimate accounting.

**The Delusion of the Modern Soul**

The question has lost none of its force for the modern reader, who lives in an age of unprecedented comfort and unprecedented distraction. If anything, the contemporary world has industrialized the delusion this verse exposes. Whole industries are devoted to keeping the human attention fixed on the immediate — on the screen, the purchase, the status, the pleasure — precisely so that the soul will not lift its eyes to the horizon of death and judgment. The "life of this world" has become a more efficient deluder than ever, because it no longer requires the primitive temptations of the ancient marketplace; it can deliver distraction in infinite quantity to a pocket-sized device. Against this tide, the verse stands as a permanent interruption: *O man, what has deluded you with regard to your noble Lord?* It is a question addressed to the soul in its most secure and comfortable moment, and it is designed to break the spell — to remind the soul that no amount of worldly favor can make it independent of the Lord who favors it, and that the Generosity it enjoys in this life is itself the evidence of the Generous One to whom it will one day return.

**The Answer Every Soul Must Give**

Because the verse is a question, it demands an answer — and the tradition teaches that every soul will, in the end, be made to answer it. The commentators note the connection between this verse and the fact that no delusion, in the final analysis, will stand: when the sky is cleft and the graves overturned (vv. 1–5), the soul that was deluded will suddenly see the truth, and its answer to the question *What has deluded you?* will be a silence of shame. The only answer that spares the soul this shame is given in this life, in the present, by turning from the deluders to the noble Lord — by recognizing, in every blessing of health, provision, and life, the hand of *al-Karīm* who gave it, and by ordering one's days toward the Reckoning that the sūrah's opening verses have made unavoidable. The believer therefore reads this verse not as a mere rhetorical ornament but as a standing summons to ask himself, at the point of every temptation: *What is deluding me with regard to my noble Lord?* And the answer — whether it is the world, the self, or the whispering Deluder — is always a thing that this verse, by the very fact of asking, has already begun to expose.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:7

> **Who created you, then fashioned you, then proportioned you,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Noble Lord Defined by His Acts Toward Man**

The sūrah now answers its own astonished question by describing the noble Lord in terms of what He has done for the very human being who is deluded about Him: *alladhī khalaqaka fa-sawwāka fa-ʿadalaka.* "Who created you, then fashioned you, then proportioned you." The verse lays out a three-stage account of the human being's making, and each stage is a distinct act of divine care. The verb *khalaqa* (from *kh-l-q*) is creation in the broadest sense — the bringing into existence of something from nothing, or the determining and measuring of a thing's nature and destiny; indeed, the root *kh-l-q* is also the root of *qadar*, measure and decree, and *khalaqa* carries the sense of creating with measure and purpose. The verb *sawwā* (from *s-w-y*, to make equal, level, or complete) means to fashion and perfect — to give the created thing its proper, finished, well-ordered shape, so that nothing is left raw or unfinished. The verb *ʿadala* (from the root *ʿ-d-l*) has two readings, and the commentators record both, for the text was recited in two ways.

**The Two Readings of "Proportioned You"**

The most widely followed reading is *fa-ʿaddalaka*, meaning "then He proportioned you" or "made you upright and well-balanced," from the root meaning to make even and correct — giving each limb and faculty its due measure so that the whole stands in harmonious proportion. On this reading the verse traces a complete workmanship: creation out of nothing, then fashioning into a proper form, then the balancing and proportioning of that form so that it functions in perfect order. Al-Rāzī develops this reading at length, observing that the human body is a marvel of proportioned design — the eye fitted to light, the ear to sound, the limbs coordinated, the organs interdependent — a design no artisan could imitate and no accident could produce. A second reading, recorded by al-Ṭabarī and others, is *fa-ʿadalaka*, meaning "then He turned you" or "inclined you" — the idea being that God, having created and fashioned the human being, directs and turns him in whatever way He wills. Both readings were preserved as legitimate, and both serve the verse's argument: whether God proportioned the human form in perfect balance or turns the human being according to His will, the result is the same — a creature utterly dependent on the craft and direction of its Maker, and therefore without excuse for ingratitude.

**The Argument From Craftsmanship**

The rhetorical logic of the verse is a sustained argument from craftsmanship to gratitude — an argument that runs throughout the Qur'an. If a man receives a garment that was cut, sewn, and fitted for him by a master tailor, he does not repay that tailor with contempt; still less does he deny that the tailor exists. The human being is here told: you did not make yourself; you were created, fashioned, and proportioned — your very existence, your upright posture, your working limbs, your perceiving senses are all gifts of a Craftsman who took care over you. The Qur'an drives this argument home elsewhere with an even more pointed question: "Is there, among those He created, any who creates from nothing? God is exalted above what they associate" (7:191), and "O mankind, remember the favor of God upon you. Is there any creator other than God who provides for you from the sky and the earth?" (35:3). The human being who contemplates his own body — its intricacy, its balance, its self-repair — and then denies or ignores his Maker is not exercising intellectual honesty but suppressing an evident conclusion; he is, in the vocabulary of the earlier verses, deluded.

**The Body as a Living Sign**

For the believer, the verse converts the most ordinary experience — looking at one's own hand, one's own face, one's own upright walking — into an act of worship and recognition. The Prophet ﷺ is reported, in a well-known tradition, to have taught a supplication for looking in the mirror that begins with praise for the One who proportioned his form and made it beautiful. And the Qur'an repeatedly calls the human being to contemplate his own making as one of the clearest of all signs: "And in yourselves — do you not then see?" (51:21). The three verbs of this verse — created, fashioned, proportioned — invite the believer to move through his own body with gratitude: to recognize that he did not choose his existence, that his form was not left to chance but perfected, and that the balance of his limbs and faculties is a standing mercy renewed every morning. A soul that habitually reads its own body in this way is a soul that has begun to answer the previous verse's question: it is no longer deluded about its noble Lord, because it sees His craft written in its own frame.

**The Completion That Points to an End**

Finally, the commentators note a subtle implication in the sequence of the verse for the doctrine of the Resurrection. The same God who created the human being from nothing, fashioned him, and proportioned him with such care is — by that very act — the God fully capable of recreating him after death. The human being who was so carefully made once can be made again; indeed, the Qur'an makes this the central reply to the skeptic who doubts the resurrection: "Does man think that We will never assemble his bones? Yes — We are able to proportion even his fingertips" (75:3–4). The care of creation in verse 7 is therefore not merely an argument for gratitude but an argument for hope: the One who proportioned the human being the first time has the power and the knowledge to raise him the second time. The deluded soul that doubts the Hereafter is refuted by its own body, which stands before it as living evidence both of its Maker's generosity and of its Maker's power.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:8

> **assembling you in whatever form He willed?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sovereignty of the Fashioner Over the Form**

The description of the human being's making concludes with the decisive clause: *fī ayyi ṣūratin mā shāʾa rakkabaka.* "In whatever form He willed He assembled you." The verb *rakkaba* (from *r-k-b*, to mount, to set one thing upon another) means to assemble, to compound, to put together part upon part — and it is the perfect verb for the composition of the human body, which is not a single substance but an assembly of bones, sinews, organs, and faculties arranged one upon another in a working whole. The clause *fī ayyi ṣūratin mā shāʾa* — "in whatever form He willed" — places the emphasis on the divine will: the fashioner was not bound to any pattern or compelled by any necessity; He chose, freely and sovereignly, to assemble the human being in the particular form in which each person now exists.

**The Diversity of Human Forms as a Sign**

The commentators, following Ibn ʿAbbās and the early authorities, draw from this clause a lesson in the divine freedom and wisdom that the sheer diversity of human forms displays. No two human faces are exactly alike; no two bodies are assembled in precisely the same proportion; the human family, spread across every land, exhibits a vast range of shapes, colors, sizes, and features. This is not accident but choice — the deliberate, sovereign act of a Fashioner who willed each one. The Qur'an makes the point explicitly: "And of His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth and the diversity of your languages and your colors. Indeed in that are signs for those who know" (30:22). The verse thus teaches the human being to see, in his own particular face and form, the signature of a personal divine will — he was not a mass-produced artifact but a deliberately willed creation, assembled in a form that the Fashioner chose for him and no other.

**The Two Readings of the Verse's Meaning**

Beyond the general teaching of divine sovereignty over form, the classical commentators offered two more specific interpretations of what "whatever form He willed" means, and both were preserved because each carries a distinct truth. In the first reading, the verse refers to the manner in which God chooses, for each individual, a particular human form — tall or short, dark or fair, with the particular features and dispositions that distinguish one person from another. On this reading the verse is a statement about the personal specificity of divine creation: every human being is assembled in a form that God, in His wisdom, chose for him. In the second reading, reported by al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī, the verse points to God's absolute power over even the category of the form — that He is able to assemble human beings in the forms of other creatures as well, whether as a punishment, as in the case of those who transgressed the Sabbath and were told "Be you apes, outcast" (2:65), or simply as a demonstration of His unrestricted power. Both readings serve the verse's argument: whether God chooses among human forms or could assemble in any form at all, the fashioning is His, unconstrained, and the human being is the work of a will he did not originate and cannot control.

**Inner Form and the Two Destinies**

A further dimension, developed particularly by the spiritual commentators whom al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī transmit, reads the "form" not only outwardly but inwardly — referring not merely to the physical shape but to the moral and spiritual states in which God assembles a soul. On this reading, the verse connects with the fact that among human beings are the faithful and the disobedient, the felicitous and the wretched, and that these states — like the physical forms — are ultimately within the scope of the divine will. The commentators who favor this reading cite the verse of Sūrah 11: "On the Day it comes, no soul shall speak save by His leave; among them shall be the wretched and the felicitous" (11:105), and the whole doctrine of the divine determination of states of felicity (*saʿādah*) and wretchedness (*shaqāwah*). But the mature understanding of the tradition holds this in balance with human responsibility: the very next verses of this sūrah go on to blame the human being for denying religion — a blame that would be meaningless if the human being were not accountable for his choices. The divine will over the "form" of the soul, like the divine will over the form of the body, does not erase the human being's responsibility for the deeds that his own will sends forth.

**A Verse That Answers the Delusion Completely**

Read back against verse 6, this verse completes the answer to the sūrah's astonished question. What has deluded you with regard to your noble Lord? The Lord who created you, fashioned you, proportioned you, and assembled you in whatever form He willed — a Lord of such craft, such care, and such sovereign freedom that your very existence and your very shape are His personal act. For a creature so made to deny its Maker, or to treat its Maker's command and its Maker's Day as negligible, is not merely an error of reasoning; it is an ingratitude so profound that the sūrah can only frame it as a question of wonder. And for the believer, the verse becomes a ground of both gratitude and trust: gratitude that his particular form was willed by a wise and generous Fashioner, and trust that the same sovereign will which assembled him once is able to raise him again and to assemble his account with perfect justice. The God who "rakkaba" him in the womb is the God before whom he will one day stand with his deeds.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:9

> **Nay! But you deny religion.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charge Delivered: After All That, You Deny**

The sūrah now delivers the accusation toward which its opening has been building: *kallā bal tukadhdhibūna bi-al-dīn.* "Nay! But you deny the religion." The particle *kallā* is one of the Qur'an's strongest rebukes — "Nay!" — a word of denial and reproach that signals a sharp turning of address, as though to shake the hearer from his complacency. Then the verse adds *bal* ("rather," "but indeed") and delivers the charge: *tukadhdhibūna bi-al-dīn* — "you deny the religion" or, on the alternative rendering that the commentators preserve, "you deny the Judgment." The verb *tukadhdhibūna* is the intensive form of *kadhdhaba*, to cry "lie!" to something, to reject it as false — and its object, *al-dīn*, is one of the richest and most multivalent words in the Qur'anic lexicon.

**The Many Meanings of Dīn**

The Arabic word *dīn* carries within it a whole web of connected meanings, and the commentators note that all of them are intended by the verse. At its most basic, *dīn* means religion, creed, and the way of obedience and worship that a person follows — the sense in which the Qur'an says "your religion for you and my religion for me" (109:6). But *dīn* also carries the sense of judgment and requital, the settling of accounts — the sense in which it appears in verse 15 of this very sūrah ("and truly the profligate shall be in Hellfire... burning therein on the Day of Judgment," where *dīn* is rendered "Judgment"), and in Sūrah 1's famous phrase *māliki yawmi al-dīn*, "Master of the Day of Judgment." Between these two poles lies the full meaning: religion is the way one lives in obedience, and judgment is the requital that way will receive; the two are inseparable, because a religion that does not lead to a judgment is not a religion at all, and a judgment that does not follow from a way of life would not be just.

**Denial of the Religion Is Denial of the Judgment**

By using the single word *dīn* to cover both religion and judgment, the verse makes a profound connection: the denial of religion and the denial of the Judgment are, in the Qur'an's view, one and the same act. The person who refuses to believe in the Day of Requital (verses 1–5) is in reality refusing the whole moral order that religion establishes — the order in which deeds are weighed, accountability is real, and no one escapes the consequences of what he has sent forth. And conversely, the person who lives as though religion were merely optional — a private preference with no binding claim on conduct — is, at bottom, denying the Judgment, because he is denying that his deeds will finally matter. Al-Ṭabarī and the commentators who follow him observe that this denial is the root of all the heedlessness the sūrah has been exposing: it is because men deny the religion and its judgment that they are deluded about their noble Lord (v. 6), neglect gratitude for their creation (vv. 7–8), and imagine that no record is being kept of their deeds (vv. 10–12).

**The Particular Denial of the Pagan Contemporaries**

Historically, the verse was addressed first to the pagan contemporaries of the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah, who denied the resurrection, mocked the idea of a bodily recompense, and asked in derision, as the Qur'an records, "What! When we are dead and have become dust and bones, shall we indeed be raised again?" (56:47), and who said, "There is nothing but our life of this world: we die and we live, and nothing destroys us but time" (45:24). Against this denial the whole early Makkan revelation was marshaled — the resurrection, the Day, the scale, the reward and the punishment — and this sūrah is one of the most concentrated expressions of that marshaling. The denial of the pagans was not merely theoretical; it was practical, for it released them from moral accountability and licensed every appetite. The Qur'an names this consequence explicitly: "Those who deny the Book and that with which We sent Our messengers — soon they will know" (40:70), and it warns that the denial of the Day is the mother of every corruption.

**Denial as a Living Option in Every Age**

The verse, however, is not a report about a vanished people; it is a charge leveled at *every* hearer — "you deny" is in the present and continuous tense — and the modern reader must hear it addressed to his own era. The denial of the religion and the judgment does not always take the crude form of open atheism; more often in the contemporary world it takes the subtle forms the sūrah's own logic exposes: the reduction of religion to private sentiment that must not intrude on public conduct; the assumption that the moral order can be rewritten by majority vote or elite opinion; the practical atheism of the person who believes in God with his lips but orders his life, his money, and his ambitions as though no Reckoning would ever come. The Qur'an's word *tukadhdhibūna* — you keep calling it a lie — catches all of these, because each is a way of denying that the deeds a soul sends forth will be met by a judgment. The verse thus stands as the sūrah's central accusation, and everything that follows — the guardians, the record, the two destinies — is the evidence marshaled to show that the denial is groundless.

**The Believer's Use of the Verse**

For the believer, the verse serves a double purpose. Outwardly, it is a statement about the reality of the denial that surrounds faith and about the certainty that the denial will one day be overcome by the events of the Day. Inwardly, it is a summons to self-examination: the believer must ask himself whether, in the practical ordering of his life, he is among those who "deny the religion" — whether his conduct reflects a real belief in the Judgment or only a nominal one. The Prophet ﷺ warned against the discrepancy between profession and practice in the most searching terms, and the early Muslims applied verses like this one to themselves before applying them to others. The verse that begins with the stinging *kallā* — "Nay!" — is meant to wake the reader from the very heedlessness it condemns, so that he may deny the denial, affirm the religion, and order his sending-forth in the light of the Day that is certainly coming.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:10

> **And yet truly over you there are guardians,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rebuttal of the Denial: You Are Being Watched**

Having delivered the charge of denial, the sūrah now produces its rebuttal in the form of an undeniable fact: *wa inna ʿalaykum la-ḥāfiẓīn.* "And yet truly, over you there are guardians." The construction is emphatic — the particle *inna* together with the emphatic *la-* before the noun — so that the verse reads with the force of an oath-like certainty: *truly, verily, over you there are indeed guardians.* The word *ḥāfiẓīn* (singular *ḥāfiẓ*, from the root *ḥ-f-ẓ*, to preserve, guard, protect, and keep watch) is the same root as *ḥifẓ*, memorization and preservation, and it denotes those who keep watch over a thing so that nothing of it is lost. The verse's force is to answer the denier of verse 9: you may deny the religion and imagine that your deeds are your own private affair, but over you — whether you acknowledge it or not — there stand guardians who see and preserve everything.

**Who Are the Guardians?**

The commentators are unanimous that the guardians are angels — the noble recording angels appointed over every human being. The Qur'an affirms their existence and their function in several places. Sūrah 86 says, with an emphatic parallel to our verse: "There is no soul but that over it there is a guardian" (86:4). Sūrah 50 gives the fullest picture of their station and their industry: "When the two receivers receive, seated on the right and on the left, he utters not a word but that there is by him a watcher, ready" (50:17–18). And Sūrah 43 speaks of their presence in terms that emphasize God's own awareness of even the most private counsel: "Do they think that We hear not their secrets and their private conversation? Nay — and Our messengers are with them, recording" (43:80). The image the Qur'an builds is of a human being who is never, at any moment, without witnesses: two angels attend him constantly, one on his right and one on his left, recording his deeds as he performs them. This is the "guardianship" our verse declares, and it is the direct contradiction of the denier's assumption that his life is unobserved.

**The Recorded Life: Nothing Is Lost**

The purpose of these guardians is not surveillance for its own sake but the preservation of a complete record. Every word, every deed, every hidden intention that issues in action is being written down by angels whom the Qur'an describes in the next verses as "noble, writing, knowing what you do." The guardian angels guarantee that nothing a soul "sends forth" or "leaves behind" (v. 5) will be lost or forgotten — because it is all being preserved in a book that will be opened on the Day of Judgment. The Qur'an describes that book's contents with terrifying completeness in Sūrah 18: "And the Book will be placed, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it, and they will say: 'Oh, woe to us! What is this Book that leaves nothing small or great but has enumerated it?'" (18:49). The guardian angels are thus the instruments of a perfect divine memory: the soul that thought its misdeeds were hidden in the dark, or spoken in secret, or forgotten in the rush of years, will find on that Day that every one of them was guarded and preserved.

**The Comfort of Being Watched**

The doctrine of the guardian angels is double-edged, and the sūrah intends both edges. For the heedless, it is a warning: you are not alone; your deeds are being recorded; the privacy you imagine is an illusion. But for the believer, the same doctrine is a profound comfort — a fact the commentators were careful to draw out. The soul that strives against sin and struggles in solitude is not, in fact, alone: it is guarded and witnessed by angels who will testify on its behalf. The Prophet ﷺ spoke of the angels' witness of the believers' good deeds, and the Qur'an teaches that the angels themselves pray for the believers and stand by them: "Those who bear the Throne and those around it glorify their Lord with praise and believe in Him and ask forgiveness for those who believe" (40:7). Moreover, the presence of the recording angels gives every act of righteousness an eternal weight: the good deed done in secret, seen by no human eye, is nevertheless seen and recorded by the noble guardians — and will therefore be rewarded in full on the Day when the books are opened. The believer who internalizes verse 10 no longer feels that his private goodness is wasted; it is being guarded for him.

**Guardianship and the Nature of True Freedom**

Modern culture prizes privacy as almost a supreme good and often treats the idea of being perpetually observed as a kind of tyranny. The Qur'an's doctrine of the guardian angels offers a deeper and more liberating vision. The soul is not free when it is unobserved; it is free when its observation frees it from the prison of its own secrets and the tyranny of others' opinions. The person who knows that the noble guardians record his deeds is released from the exhausting labor of maintaining a false public image, because his true worth is being recorded by those who cannot be deceived. And the person who knows he is always witnessed is delivered from the moral anarchy of "no one is watching," because he understands that the most important witness is never absent. The verse thus redefines freedom: it is not the license to do as one pleases when unseen, but the liberation to be what one truly is, in the presence of guardians who see truly and of a Lord who will judge justly.

**A Daily Reminder**

Because the verse is short, emphatic, and easily memorized, it has served the believers of every generation as a daily reminder of the reality of divine observation. The early Muslims cultivated an awareness of the recording angels as a discipline of the heart, and the verse itself invites this discipline by its very construction — *over you there are guardians* — spoken to the soul in the second person, as a personal address. When a believer is tempted to sin in a moment of imagined solitude, this verse recalls him to the fact that he is not in solitude at all: the guardians are over him, ready to record. When a believer performs a good deed that no one applauds, this verse reassures him that it is nonetheless being preserved. Between these two poles, the guardianship of verse 10 holds the believer steady — neither despairing at his hidden sins, which are known and can be repented, nor wasting his hidden virtues, which are known and will be rewarded.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:11

> **noble, writing,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Character of the Scribes: Kirāman Kātibīn**

The sūrah now describes the guardians with two adjectives, each carrying a world of meaning: *kirāman kātibīn.* "Noble, writing." The first word, *kirāman* (singular *karīm*, from the root *k-r-m*), is the same root as the description of God in verse 6 as *al-Karīm*, "the Noble, the Generous" — and the commentators note the resonance: the angels who guard the human being are described with the very nobility that belongs to the Lord whose servants they are. The word *karīm* denotes one who is noble, honorable, generous, and dignified — and applied to the angels it conveys that these are beings of exalted honor who do their work with dignity and are themselves honored and respected by God. The second word, *kātibīn* (from *k-t-b*, to write), identifies their function: they are writers, scribes, those who inscribe. Together the phrase names the angels who in the Qur'anic tradition are known precisely by this title — *al-Kirām al-Kātibīn*, the Noble Recording Ones — the two angels appointed over every human being.

**The Nobility That Refuses None**

One of the striking features of the phrase is that it qualifies the scribes as "noble" without exception or condition. The commentators note that the recording angels are noble, honorable, and dignified in their own nature — and that this nobility is not affected by the deeds they record. The angel who records the deeds of a sinner is no less noble than the angel who records the deeds of a saint; the recording is a sacred trust carried out with complete honor regardless of what is being recorded. This has a profound implication for the human being: the very fact that such noble beings are appointed to attend even the most sinful soul is itself a mark of God's care — the soul is so valued by its Creator that it is never abandoned to unobserved oblivion, but is always attended by honorable witnesses. There is a dignity in this that the sūrah intends: even in his sin, even in his heedlessness, the human being is not treated as worthless; he is treated as a creature important enough to be watched and recorded by the noble ones.

**The Writing That Can Never Be Undone by Forgetfulness**

The phrase "writing" fixes the permanence of the record. Human memory is fallible; human witnesses can be bribed, forget, or die. The record kept by the noble recording angels is not subject to any of these failures: they write, and their writing is preserved, and it will be produced in full on the Day of Judgment. The Qur'an's description of that production is among its most striking images: "And every human being — We have fastened his fate to his neck, and We shall bring forth for him on the Day of Resurrection a book which he will find spread open: 'Read your book! Sufficient is your own soul this Day as a reckoner against you'" (17:13–14). The "book" is the writing of the noble scribes, and it is so complete that on the Day the soul itself is told to read it — and the soul, reading the exact record of its deeds, will find it sufficient evidence against itself. No human testimony, no denial, no cleverness of excuse can stand against writing so faithful.

**The Two Recording Angels: A Picture From the Sunnah**

The Prophet ﷺ described the station and work of these two angels in detail, and his description, recorded in the collections of al-Tirmidhī and others from Abū Umāmah, gives a vivid picture that the commentators habitually cite beside this verse:

> *"The two angels, the recorder on the right and the recorder on the left, record [his deeds]. The one on the right records his good deeds and the one on the left records his evil deeds."* (al-Tirmidhī 3461, with a chain that the scholars graded ḥasan)

And in the authentic tradition recorded by al-Bukhārī from Abū Hurayrah, the Prophet ﷺ explained the special honor shown to the writing of good deeds and the grace extended in the writing of evil ones:

> *"If a man intends a good deed and does not do it, it is written for him as a complete good deed; and if he intends it and does it, it is written for him as ten good deeds up to seven hundred times over. And if he intends an evil deed and does not do it, it is written for him as a complete good deed; and if he intends it and does it, it is written against him as one evil deed."* (al-Bukhārī 6491; Muslim 128)

The commentators note how the Prophet's ﷺ description reveals the mercy built even into the machinery of the record: the good deed is written generously, and the evil deed is written at minimum — a mercy whose origin is the *al-Karīm* Lord who appointed the noble scribes in the first place.

**Living in the Light of the Scribes**

The believer who takes seriously that noble, writing angels record his deeds finds his daily conduct invested with a significance it would otherwise lack. Every word spoken is being written; every action performed is being recorded; every hour of the day is passing through the hands of scribes whose writing will one day be opened. The early Muslims internalized this to an extraordinary degree, and the tradition preserves accounts of those who, reminded that the angels on their right and left were recording, would pause before speaking to consider what they were about to say. The Prophet ﷺ recommended the believer make remembrance of the recording angels part of his conduct, and the Qur'an itself, in the verse "he utters not a word but that there is by him a watcher, ready" (50:18), teaches that even speech — the most fleeting of human acts — is inscribed. To live with the noble scribes in mind is to live with the awareness that nothing one does is trivial: the smallest good deed is being guarded for the Day of its reward, and the smallest evil deed is being guarded for the Day of its reckoning.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:12

> **knowing what you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scribes Who Do Not Merely Write but Know**

The description of the guardians culminates in their most decisive attribute: *yaʿlamūna mā tafʿalūn.* "Knowing what you do." The verb *yaʿlamūna* (from *ʿ-l-m*, to know) is placed in the present tense, and the commentators explain its force: the recording angels do not write mechanically, as one might transcribe sounds without understanding them; they *know* what they are writing — they perceive the deeds, the words, the circumstances, and the intentions behind them, and they record with understanding. The clause *mā tafʿalūn*, "what you do," is left general and inclusive: everything you do, in every moment, is not only witnessed and written but understood by the noble scribes.

**The Completeness That Leaves No Loophole**

The verse is deliberately constructed to close every loophole the denier might seek. The soul might imagine that some deeds are done when no one is looking, or that some words are so trivial they escape notice, or that some intentions are so hidden they will never be known. The verse answers all of these at once: the guardians know what you do. Sūrah 50 fills in the detail with a picture of the record's inclusiveness — "he utters not a word but that there is by him a watcher, ready" (50:18) — and Sūrah 36 makes the scope of the divine record total: "Indeed, We give life to the dead, and We record what they have sent before and what they leave behind, and all things We have enumerated in a clear register" (36:12). The claim is not merely that the angels witness actions but that the entire moral content of a human life — its deeds, and the state of the soul from which those deeds issue — falls within a knowledge that nothing escapes.

**The Knowledge of the Angels and the Knowledge of God**

The classical theologians were careful to distinguish the knowledge of the recording angels from the knowledge of God, and the distinction is important for understanding the verse. The angels' knowledge is created, finite, and delegated: they know what they are enabled to know in order to fulfill their appointed function of recording. God's knowledge, by contrast, is uncreated, infinite, and immediate — He knows the secrets of the hearts, the intentions behind every act, and the hidden recesses of every soul, a knowledge the Qur'an states in the most absolute terms: "And God knows what is in your breasts" (3:154), "He knows the secret and what is even more hidden" (20:7), "Indeed, your Lord knows that you stand [in prayer]..." (73:20). The verse's point, then, is not to set the angels' knowledge alongside God's as if they were equal, but to affirm that the record which will be produced on the Day is built upon a real knowledge — that what the scribes write corresponds to what truly happened, because they truly know it — and that above and behind the angels' recording stands the all-knowing God to whom even the angels' own recording is known.

**The Refutation of the Concealment of Evil**

The verse refutes, with special force, the sinner's presumption of concealment. The human being who sins in secret, or who rationalizes his wrongdoings by imagining they are hidden, is here told that his deeds are known — not merely by an abstract, distant deity whom he can ignore, but by the very guardians who stand over him and record with knowledge. This is why the Qur'an, in describing the Day, pictures the guilty being confronted with a record they cannot deny: "On the Day God will raise them all and inform them of what they did — God has enumerated it while they forgot it" (58:6). The concealment of evil is thus a self-deception: the sinner may hide his deed from other human beings, but he cannot hide it from the guardians who know what he does, nor from the God who knows what is in his breast. The only true concealment of evil, the Qur'an teaches, is its erasure through repentance — for God, the Forgiving, the Merciful, is able to forgive even what the angels recorded, blotting it out from the record by His grace.

**A Verse for Both Warning and Reassurance**

Like the two verses before it, this verse serves the believer in two directions. As a warning, it checks the drift toward heedless sin by reminding the soul that its deeds are known and recorded. As a reassurance, it guarantees that no good deed is ever lost — that the charity given in secret, the kindness shown when no one was watching, the patience exercised in solitude, are all known to the guardians and recorded for the Day of reward. The Prophet ﷺ, describing the two angels, taught that the believer's good deeds are written with a generosity that reflects the mercy of the All-Knowing Lord. Between these two uses, the verse holds the believer in a state of watchful confidence: he does not despair over his sins, because he knows they are known and can be repented and forgiven; and he does not waste his virtues, because he knows they are known and will be rewarded. The three verses — 10, 11, 12 — together form a complete doctrine: over you are guardians; they are noble, writing; and they know what you do. The denial of verse 9 is thus fully answered: the human being who denies the religion is nevertheless the object of a perfect, knowing, and recording watchfulness, and the Day will make that watchfulness manifest in a record that cannot be denied.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:13

> **Truly the pious shall be in bliss;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Destinies Declared: The Verdict of the Record**

Having established that every soul's deeds are known and recorded by the noble guardians, the sūrah now draws the inevitable conclusion — the division of the human family according to what its members have sent forth: *inna al-abrāra la-fī naʿīm.* "Truly the pious shall be in bliss." The emphatic construction (inna... la-) marks the certainty of the declaration: there is no doubt, no "perhaps," no condition attached. The *abrār* — the pious, the righteous — shall be in *naʿīm*, bliss, delight, the abounding good of Paradise. The two destinies, verse 13 and verse 14, are set side by side with the stark economy of a verdict being read: the record of the righteous yields bliss; the record of the wicked yields fire.

**The Meaning of Abrār and Birr**

The word *abrār* (singular *barr*, from the root *b-r-r*) is one of the Qur'an's most comprehensive terms for righteousness. The root *b-r-r* carries the meaning of truthfulness, goodness, obedience, and expansive kindness — and the same root gives *al-birr*, which the Qur'an defines in one of its most important verses not as a narrow ritualism but as the whole integrated life of faith and virtue. Sūrah 2 declares: "Birr is not that you turn your faces toward the East and the West, but birr is one who believes in God and the Last Day and the angels and the Book and the prophets, and gives wealth, for love of Him, to kinsfolk, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask, and for the freeing of slaves, and establishes prayer and gives the alms..." (2:177). The *barr* is thus the one whose righteousness is not a surface but a root — a genuine faith that issues in genuine love of God and genuine care for creation. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note that the term comprehends those who are truthful in word, faithful in trust, obedient to God, and heedful of their duties — the people whose lives correspond to the record of good that the guardians have written.

**Bliss as the Just Reward of Righteousness**

The verse presents the bliss of the pious as a consequence grounded in justice: because they were *abrār* — because they believed, obeyed, and did good — they are in *naʿīm*. This is not arbitrary favoritism but the fitting requital of a life rightly lived, and the Qur'an is emphatic that the reward is proportionate and just: "Whoever does righteousness, whether male or female, while being a believer — We will surely cause him to live a good life, and We will surely give them their reward according to the best of what they used to do" (16:97). The commentators draw attention to the sequence of the sūrah: verses 10–12 established that the righteous were known and their good deeds recorded; verse 13 declares that their record yields bliss. There is a perfect symmetry — the deeds the soul "sent forth" (v. 5) return to it on the Day as the bliss it now enters. The naʿīm of Paradise is, in a real sense, the harvest of the birr that was sown in this life.

**The Pious of Every Station**

The classical commentators stress that the *abrār* are not a closed elite of prophets and saints alone, but include the sincere believers of every station. The Qur'an names among the *abrār* the common faithful who worship, give, and endure — and the sūrah of al-Muṭaffifīn, which follows this one and echoes its very first word ("Truly the abrār shall be in bliss," 83:22), goes on to describe their bliss in detail: "upon couches, gazing — you will recognize in their faces the brightness of bliss" (83:23–24). What distinguishes the *abrār* is not worldly rank but the quality of their faith and the fidelity of their deeds. A poor believer who worships his Lord, keeps his trust, and does good to others is among the *abrār*; a rich believer who does the same is among the *abrār*; and the verses of this sūrah hold out the bliss to both without distinction of station, because the guardians recorded their deeds without regard to their wealth or poverty.

**Bliss Not Only Hereafter but a Present State**

The word *naʿīm* points chiefly to the bliss of Paradise, but the commentators also observe that the Qur'an speaks of a foretaste of that bliss in the present life — the *ḥayāh ṭayyibah*, "the good life," which God promises to the righteous in 16:97. The believer whose heart is at peace through faith, whose conscience is clear through obedience, whose wealth is purified through giving, and whose hope is anchored in the Hereafter, experiences a settled well-being that no abundance of worldly goods can supply. The bliss of verse 13 is thus not merely deferred but begun: the soul that lives in birr lives already in a kind of naʿīm — secure, contented, and oriented toward the fullness of bliss to come. The sūrah's declaration is therefore a promise the believer can begin to verify in the present, even as he awaits its complete fulfillment on the Day when the guardians' record is opened and the abrār enter Paradise.

**A Verse That Redirects Ambition**

In an age that measures success by accumulation, status, and pleasure, verse 13 offers a quiet but radical redirection of human ambition. The sūrah has shown the entire cosmic order being dismantled — the sky cleft, the stars scattered, the seas burst, the graves overturned — and out of that dismantling it draws the only ambition that survives it: to be among the *abrār*. Everything else — the wealth, the reputation, the worldly achievement that the heedless spend their lives pursuing — is precisely the "delusion" of verse 6, the thing that will be scattered like the stars when the Day comes. The believer who fixes his aim on the abrār is building what cannot be dismantled, because the guardians are recording it, and verse 13 declares that its reward is certain: *truly the pious shall be in bliss.*

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:14

> **and truly the profligate shall be in Hellfire,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Destiny: The Mirror Image of the First**

The sūrah now completes its verdict by naming the opposite destiny with the same emphatic certainty: *wa inna al-fujjāra la-fī jaḥīm.* "And truly the profligate shall be in Hellfire." The verse is the exact mirror of verse 13 — the same emphatic construction (*inna... la-*), the same definitive declaration — so that the two verses stand side by side as the two columns of a single ledger: the abrār in naʿīm, the fujjār in jaḥīm. There is no middle column, no third destiny, because on the Day of the opened record every soul will fall on one side or the other of the division that its own deeds have drawn. The word *jaḥīm* is one of the Qur'an's names for Hell, and it derives from a root meaning intense heat and burning — the fire whose description the sūrah will develop in the next verses.

**The Meaning of Fujjār and Fujūr**

The word *fujjār* (singular *fājir*, from the root *f-j-r*) is the counterpart of *abrār*, and its meaning was already touched upon in the commentary on verse 3, where the seas are described as "burst forth" (*fujjirat*) from the very same root. The root *f-j-r* means to break open, to split, to breach — and the *fājir* is the one who breaks open what should remain closed. The commentators explain: as the abrār are those who keep the bounds — the bounds of faith, law, modesty, and conscience — so the fujjār are those who breach them. The fājir breaks the barrier of obedience to God, the boundaries of what is lawful and pure, the restraints of modesty and decency; he is the profligate, the libertine, the one who tears through the moral order as the sea on the Day of Judgment bursts through its bounds. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note that the term comprehends the disbelievers and the unrighteous — those who deny the religion and the Judgment (v. 9) and who therefore throw off every restraint that faith would impose.

**The Punishment That Matches the Crime**

There is a profound justice in the pairing of *fujjār* with *jaḥīm*, and the commentators draw it out. The fājir spent his life "breaking open" what should have remained closed — the bounds of decency, the commands of God, the limits of conscience — and his punishment is to be cast into the Fire, the very element of breaking and burning. The imagery is one of perfect correspondence: the one who broke open the barriers of God's order in this world is given, in the next, to the element that breaks and consumes. The Qur'an repeatedly emphasizes this principle of just correspondence — "Is the reward of goodness anything but goodness?" (55:60), and conversely that evil is requited with its like — and verse 14 is a stark instance of it. The fājir who lived as though there were no Guardian recording him and no Judgment awaiting him discovers in the Fire that both were real, and that the breaking he practiced against God's order has returned upon himself.

**The Inversion of the Worldly Assessment**

The verse also performs a quiet inversion of the world's own assessment of its members. In the world's eyes, the fājir is often the successful one — the man who has thrown off moral scruple, taken what he wants, indulged his appetites, and risen by the ruthlessness that faith forbids. He may be admired, envied, and followed. The sūrah, speaking with the certainty of the record, declares the truth the world cannot see: that this admired profligate is bound for jaḥīm, while the humble abrār whom the world may overlook or despise is bound for naʿīm. The verses 13 and 14 together are thus a standing correction of the world's false scale of value — a reminder that the only assessment that finally matters is the one the noble guardians have been recording, and that it will be opened on a Day when all the world's honors and degradations are reversed.

**The Certainty of the Verdict**

Like verse 13, verse 14 is cast in the language of emphatic certainty — *inna al-fujjāra la-fī jaḥīm* — and the certainty is deliberate. The sūrah does not speculate about the fate of the profligate or soften its description with a "perhaps" or a "might be"; it declares, with the finality of a verdict already passed in the divine decree, that the fujjār shall be in Hellfire. This is not because God delights in punishment for its own sake — the Qur'an is emphatic that God is not a wrongdoer to His servants and that He desires mercy for them — but because the fājir has, by his own denial of the religion and his own breach of the bounds, chosen the destiny his record merits. The verse is a warning, and its very certainty is the mercy of the warning: it is spoken while the fājir is still alive, while his record can still be changed by repentance, so that no one who hears it can say he was not told.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:15

> **burning therein on the Day of Judgment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Timing of the Fire: On the Day of Din**

The sūrah now attaches to the profligate's destiny its appointed time: *yaṣlawnahā yawma al-dīn.* "They shall burn therein on the Day of Judgment." The verb *yaṣlawnahā* (from *ṣ-l-w*, a root that also appears in the word for prayer, *ṣalāh*, and here means to enter the fire and endure its heat — to be roasted and burned within it) fixes the punishment in the experience of the fujjār: they do not merely observe the fire from a distance but enter it and suffer its burning. And the timing is named as *yawma al-dīn* — "the Day of Judgment," the same word *dīn* that appeared in verse 9 where the deniers were accused of denying "the religion" (or "the Judgment"). The verse thus closes a circle: those who denied al-dīn in this life will be burned in the Fire on yawm al-dīn — the very Day they denied will come upon them with its requital.

**The Day of Judgment as the Day of the Record's Opening**

The commentators connect this verse with the whole structure of the sūrah. The Day of Judgment is the day when the record kept by the noble guardians (vv. 10–12) is opened, when every soul knows what it has sent forth and what it has left behind (v. 5), and when the verdicts of verses 13–14 are executed. It is the day toward which the entire cosmic upheaval of verses 1–4 was directed — the sky cleft, the stars scattered, the seas burst, the graves overturned, so that all souls could be gathered and their records opened. The burning of the fujjār on the Day of Judgment is therefore not a random catastrophe but the execution of a sentence that the record itself has made certain: the deeds the fujjār sent forth return to them as fire on the very Day whose coming they denied.

**The Reality and Severity of the Fire**

The Qur'an's descriptions of the Fire of Hell are among its most urgent passages, and they are meant to be taken with complete seriousness as descriptions of a real place of real suffering. The Qur'an speaks of a Fire "whose fuel is men and stones" (2:24), of garments of fire and "boiling water poured over their heads" (22:19), of skins that are renewed so that the punishment may be repeated (4:56), and of the plea of its inhabitants: "Our Lord, bring us out of it, and if we return to evil, then indeed we shall be wrongdoers" (23:107). The severity of these descriptions has a clear purpose: the Qur'an is warning the human being of a real consequence in language forceful enough to be remembered, so that he may avoid it while there is still time. The commentators, however, also record the balance of the tradition: the descriptions of the Fire are warnings, and the mercy of God is vast; no soul is cast into it unjustly, and the believer's task is to fear it enough to obey and to hope in God's mercy enough to repent.

**"Burning Therein": The Duration and the Inescapability**

The verb *yaṣlawnahā* and its context in the following verse ("and they will not be absent from it") establish that the burning is not a brief ordeal but an enduring state from which there is no escape. The profligate will burn on the Day of Judgment, and verse 16 will add that they will not be absent from it — that there is no leaving, no intermission, no surcease. The Qur'an elsewhere states the eternity of this punishment for those who die in persistent unbelief and unrepented evil: "they will dwell therein forever" is a refrain attached to the fate of the disbelievers in many verses. The commentators note that this permanence is what gives the warning its final edge: the Fire is not a correction that ends but a state that abides, and therefore the only time to avoid it is now, in this life, where repentance is still possible and where the record can still be changed.

**A Call to Escape While There Is Time**

For the believer who recites this verse, it serves as one of the Qur'an's most powerful calls to repentance and righteousness. The verse does not merely describe a distant punishment; it names the Day — *yawm al-dīn* — as a certain, coming event, and it warns that the fujjār will burn on that Day. The believer who takes the warning to heart is moved to ensure that his name is written among the abrār and not the fujjār — by faith, by obedience, by keeping the bounds that the fājir breaks, and by repentance for every breach. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have warned his companions of the Fire and to have sought refuge from it constantly in his supplications, and the believer who recites this sūrah is taught to do the same — to fear the Day of Judgment enough to prepare for it, and to turn to the Mercy of God, which is the only thing that can erase from the record the deeds that would otherwise lead to the burning.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:16

> **And they will not be absent from it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Locking of the Door: No Absence From the Fire**

The sūrah now seals the fate of the profligate with the most final of its statements: *wa mā hum ʿanhā bi-ghāʾibīn.* "And they will not be absent from it." The Arabic construction is emphatic — *mā... bi-ghāʾibīn*, "they are not going to be absent" — and it denies, absolutely and permanently, any possibility of the fujjār being away from the Fire, even for a moment. The commentators explain the verse against the background of the earlier description of the heedless: in this life, the fujjār had been "absent" from the reality of their deeds — oblivious, distracted, refusing to look at the consequences of what they were doing (a condition the sūrah implied in verse 5 and the Qur'an describes explicitly elsewhere: "you were heedless of this, and We have removed from you your cover, so your sight today is sharp" — 50:22). On the Day of Judgment that oblivion is permanently ended: they will no longer be able to be absent from the Fire, because the fire is now their inescapable reality and their sight is sharp enough to see it.

**The Two Senses of the Verse: Presence and Awareness**

The commentators identify two complementary senses in which the profligate "will not be absent from it." In the first and most direct sense, the verse means they will not be able to leave the Fire or escape from it — there is no exit, no intermission, no moment of relief, no place they can retreat to beyond its reach. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the despair of those who seek escape and cannot find it: "They will want to go forth from the Fire, but they will not be able to go forth from it, and theirs is a lasting punishment" (5:37), and "every time they wish to come forth from it, they are returned into it" (32:20). The second sense, which the spiritual commentators develop, is that they will no longer be able to be *oblivious* to it — they will not be able to distract themselves from the reality of their punishment as they once distracted themselves from the reality of their deeds. In this life the fājir could be "absent" from his conscience, absent from the thought of judgment, absent from the Fire by the anesthesia of worldly pleasure; on that Day all anesthesia is gone, and the soul is fully, permanently, inescapably present to what it has earned.

**The Contrast With the Believer's State**

The commentators draw a striking contrast between the fujjār, who "will not be absent from" the Fire, and the believers, whose relationship to the divine presence is one of nearness and bliss. The same inability to escape that is the fujjār's torment is, for the abrār, transformed into the bliss of unbroken presence: the believers are "near" to their Lord, in the shade and the gardens, enjoying a bliss from which they also will not be absent — but that absence-impossibility is delight rather than torment, because what they are perpetually present to is mercy rather than fire. The symmetry deepens the justice of the sūrah: the profligate, who in life tried always to be absent from God and from the truth of his deeds, is finally made unable to be absent from the consequence of both; while the pious, who in life kept themselves present to God through prayer and remembrance, are made perpetually present to His bliss.

**The Removal of the Cover of Heedlessness**

Sūrah 50's description of the Day supplies the psychology behind this verse: "And We have removed from you your cover, so your sight today is sharp" (50:22). In this life, the Qur'an teaches, the human being is veiled from the full reality of the Hereafter — the veil is partly a mercy, allowing him to live and act and repent, and partly the consequence of his own heedlessness. The fājir exploited that veil, treating the unseen Fire as unreal or remote. On the Day of Judgment the veil is removed, and the soul sees with terrible sharpness the reality it denied. "They will not be absent from it" then carries its full weight: the fujjār, whose whole strategy was absence — absence from the mosque, from the remembrance, from the reality of accountability — is at last and forever denied absence. There is nowhere left to hide, nothing left to distract, no further veil to drop between the soul and the truth.

**The Finality That Makes the Warning Urgent**

The verse's absolute finality is precisely what makes it urgent as a warning in this life. Because the fujjār will not be absent from the Fire on the Day of Judgment, and because that Day is certain, the only rational response is to ensure, while the present life lasts, that one's name is not among the fujjār. The Qur'an is emphatic that the door of repentance is open in this life and closed only at death or at the rising of the sun from the west — the Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever repents before the sun rises from the west, God will accept his repentance" (Muslim 2703). The believer who recites verse 16 is therefore hearing not only a description of the fate of the persistently profligate but a summons to the present: to turn from the fujūr that breaks God's bounds, to repent for what has passed, and to live among the abrār whose destiny verse 13 has already declared. The door that will be locked on the Day — the door of absence from the Fire — is open now, and the sūrah's final verses are about to press this urgency home with a series of questions.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:17

> **And what will apprise thee of the Day of Judgment?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Measures the Human Capacity**

Having described the Day and its two destinies, the sūrah now steps back and poses a question whose very form is a lesson in humility: *wa mā adrāka mā yawmu al-dīn.* "And what will apprise thee of the Day of Judgment?" The construction — *mā adrāka*, "what will make you know," "what will apprise thee" — is one of the Qur'an's most striking rhetorical devices, and it is used repeatedly in the early Makkan sūrahs to introduce realities too vast for ordinary comprehension. The question does not seek an answer from the one addressed; it seeks rather to confess that the Day of Judgment exceeds all human knowledge, that no description, no analogy, no accumulation of words can fully convey what that Day will be. The commentators observe that the question is a way of saying: the Day is so tremendous that you cannot grasp it; nothing in your experience — not the worst catastrophe, not the most solemn moment — can prepare you for it; only God knows its full reality.

**To Whom Is the Question Addressed?**

The verse addresses "thee" — and the commentators, al-Rāzī among them, discuss to whom the question is directed. The most natural and encompassing reading is that it is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, and through him to all human beings: even the Messenger, to whom the Qur'an was revealing the descriptions of the Day, is told that the full reality of the Day exceeds what can be apprised. This is not a denial of the Prophet's knowledge but an affirmation of the Day's transcendence: the descriptions given in revelation are true and sufficient for guidance, but the complete reality of the Day of Judgment remains known fully only to God. Some commentators proposed that the first question (v. 17) is addressed to the pious and the second (v. 18) to the profligate, but the majority understood both questions as rhetorical acknowledgments of the Day's overwhelming greatness addressed to all hearers. The question thus performs a double service: it magnifies the Day beyond all telling, and it humbles the listener before that Day, so that he receives the descriptions that follow not as a curious orator's embellishment but as the grave and only partially expressible truth.

**The Qur'an's Use of "What Will Apprise Thee"**

The phrase *mā adrāka* appears in several early sūrahs, and each use is revealing. In Sūrah 101 it introduces the striking Day: "And what will apprise thee of the Clatterer? — The Day mankind will be like scattered moths" (101:3–4). In Sūrah 97 it introduces the Night of Power: "And what will apprise thee of the Night of Power?" (97:2). In Sūrah 104 it introduces Hell: "And what will apprise thee of the Crusher?" (104:5). In every case the question flags a reality of supreme importance and of correspondingly vast magnitude — a reality whose importance is signaled by the very fact that it cannot be adequately conveyed. The repetition of the device across these sūrahs shows that it is a deliberate pedagogical tool: the Qur'an is teaching its hearers to approach the great truths of the Hereafter with awe, recognizing that human language and human imagination are stretched to their limit, and that the realities described are greater than the words that point to them.

**The Day That Surpasses Every Comparison**

The question's deepest teaching is that the Day of Judgment surpasses every comparison the human mind can make. When the sūrah earlier described the sky being cleft, the stars scattered, the seas burst, and the graves overturned, it was using images drawn from the world the hearers knew — but the question of verse 17 now reveals that even these images, powerful as they are, fall short. The Day is not merely like a storm or a quake or an upheaval; it is a reality of an entirely different order, the day on which the whole of creation is rearranged for the judgment of every soul that ever lived. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the question humbles even the most eloquent describer: no matter how the scholars and preachers of every age attempt to convey the Day, their words remain approximations, and the full truth remains with God. This is why the believer approaches the Day not with the confidence of one who has understood it completely but with the reverence of one who knows it is greater than his understanding — and who therefore prepares for it with a humility proportionate to its greatness.

**A Question That Calls for Preparation, Not Paralysis**

It would be a mistake, however, to read the question as a counsel of despair or a license for indifference — "since I cannot fully know the Day, I need not think about it." The Qur'an poses the question precisely in order to produce the opposite effect: because the Day exceeds all telling, the believer must take it with utmost seriousness, and because no description can fully convey it, the believer must prepare for it with a reverence that no description could fully produce. The sūrah does not leave the listener in bewildered ignorance; it answers its own question in the following verses by adding further description — "a day when no soul will avail another soul in any way, and the Command that Day is with God" (v. 19). The question and its partial answer together teach the correct posture: acknowledge that the Day is beyond full comprehension, and nevertheless act upon what has been revealed of it — for the revealed portion is sufficient for guidance, even if the whole exceeds the telling.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:18

> **Then what will apprise thee of the Day of Judgment?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Repeated: The Device of Redoubled Emphasis**

The sūrah now repeats its question almost word for word: *thumma mā adrāka mā yawmu al-dīn.* "Then what will apprise thee of the Day of Judgment?" The repetition is not accidental; it is a deliberate rhetorical device, and the commentators analyze its force with care. The particle *thumma* ("then," "and again") between the two questions marks a second, redoubled emphasis — as though the first question had not been sufficient to convey the Day's greatness and a second, even more emphatic interrogation were required. The Qur'an uses this device of the doubled "what will apprise thee" in only a few places — most famously in Sūrah 104, where Hell is asked about twice in consecutive verses (104:5 and 104:8) — and in every case the doubling serves to magnify the reality in question beyond the capacity of a single statement. The repetition itself is the message: the Day of Judgment is so great that the Qur'an must ask twice, because one asking cannot convey it.

**The Building Pressure of the Repetition**

The effect of the repetition on the hearer is one of accumulating weight. In verse 17 the question arrested the listener; verse 18 returns to it with a force that leaves no room for distraction. It is as though the sūrah were saying: you have heard that the Day is beyond telling — and now hear it again, because you must not let this truth slip. The doubled question performs the function of a hammer striking the same point twice: it drives the reality of the Day deeper into the consciousness of the one being addressed. The commentators note that this is precisely the method of the early Makkan sūrahs, which return again and again to the Hereafter with increasing urgency because their audience was deeply heedless of it — the very heedlessness this sūrah has been exposing since verse 6. The repetition is a mercy tailored to the disease: the soul that does not respond to one reminder is given another.

**The Two Questions and Their Possible Divisions**

Al-Rāzī, as the source commentary notes, canvassed the possibility that the two questions are addressed to different audiences — that verse 17 is addressed to the pious, to apprise them of the bliss that awaits them, and verse 18 to the profligate, to apprise them of the Fire. On this reading the doubled question serves to announce both destinies described in verses 13–16: the Day holds naʿīm for the abrār and jaḥīm for the fujjār, and the sūrah asks twice because it is announcing two verdicts. The majority of commentators, however, read both questions as addressed generally to all hearers, emphasizing the Day's overwhelming greatness without dividing it between audiences. Whichever reading one adopts, both agree on the essential point: the Day of Judgment is a reality of such magnitude that a single announcement cannot suffice, and the sūrah's doubled question is the measure of the Day's importance.

**The Power of Rhetorical Repetition in the Qur'an**

The verse offers a glimpse into the Qur'an's distinctive method of persuasion, which relies heavily on repetition, rhythm, and the building of emotional and intellectual pressure. Unlike a treatise, which states its point once and moves on, the Qur'an returns to its central truths — the unity of God, the reality of the Hereafter, the accountability of the soul — again and again, in verse after verse, sūrah after sūrah. The repetition is not redundancy but pedagogy: it recognizes that the human heart is heedless and must be reminded, that a single mention of the Day is easily forgotten, and that only persistent, varied, emphatic repetition can keep the soul oriented toward the truth. Verse 18 is a small, perfect example of this method: it takes the truth already stated and restates it with added force, refusing to let the listener move on until the Day's greatness has been driven home.

**The Two Questions as the Sūrah's Own Summons**

Read in the context of the whole sūrah, the doubled question of verses 17–18 serves as the sūrah's own urgent summons to its hearers. The sūrah has shown the Day in all its upheaval (vv. 1–5), exposed the delusion and denial of the heedless (vv. 6–9), established the record of the guardians (vv. 10–12), and declared the two destinies (vv. 13–16). Now it turns directly to the hearer and asks, twice, what can apprise him of the Day — as if to say: after all this, do you still not grasp what is coming? Do you still not understand the stakes of the life you are living? The doubled question is the sūrah's final preparation for its closing verse, which answers the question it has twice asked by naming the Day's most essential features: its absolute solitude and its absolute subjection to the Command of God.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:19

> **A day when no soul will avail another soul in any way, and the Command that Day is with God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Doubled Question**

The sūrah now answers, partially, the question it has twice posed: *yawma lā tamliku nafsun li-nafsin shayʾan, wa al-amru yawmaʾidhin li-llāh.* "A day when no soul will avail another soul in any way, and the Command that Day is with God." The answer does not attempt the impossible task of fully describing the Day; instead it names the Day's two most essential and most consequential features. The first is the total solitude of the Day: no soul will avail another soul in any way — no intercession of the kind the heedless imagined, no payment, no ransom, no exchange, no rescue of one soul by another. The second is the total sovereignty of God on that Day: the Command — *al-amr* — is wholly with God, and every intermediary, every power, every helper that operated in the world of causes is stripped away, leaving God alone as the direct and absolute actor.

**The Solitude of the Day: No Soul Avails Another**

The first clause, *lā tamliku nafsun li-nafsin shayʾan*, literally "no soul possesses for [another] soul anything," paints the Day's absolute isolation. In this world, human beings avail one another constantly: parents provide for children, friends rescue friends, the wealthy relieve the poor, the powerful protect the weak. The fājir, in particular, may have relied on such availings — on patrons, on family, on wealth, on the influence that could smooth over consequences and buy protection. The verse declares that on the Day of Judgment all such availings cease: no soul possesses anything it can give to another; the rich cannot buy, the powerful cannot protect, the beloved cannot intercede in any way that changes the record. The Qur'an states this isolation repeatedly and with mounting detail: "and no friend will ask of a friend" (70:10), "O mankind, fear your Lord and dread a Day when no father will avail his child, nor a child avail his father in anything" (31:33), "and fear a Day when no soul will avail another soul at all, nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor will compensation be taken from it, nor will they be helped" (2:48).

**The End of All Intermediaries**

The second clause, *wa al-amru yawmaʾidhin li-llāh*, "and the Command that Day is with God," is the theological key to the first. In this world, God ordinarily works through the order of causes and intermediaries that He has established: food comes through farmers and merchants, protection through rulers and armies, help through friends and family. Human beings can therefore be deluded (v. 6) into thinking that these intermediaries are independent sources of help — that the wealth, the patron, the influence are themselves powers to be relied upon. On the Day of Judgment, the sūrah declares, all intermediaries are stripped away, and the Command is wholly with God. No angel, no prophet's human power, no created cause operates independently; everything that happens on that Day is the direct, unmediated act of God. The commentators explain that this is why no soul can avail another: all the channels through which one creature might have helped another in this world are gone, and the only hands that act are the hands of God, who acts according to the record of each soul and nothing else.

**The Intercession That Remains: A Point of Balance**

The verse's stark statement that no soul will avail another raises an important question, given the Qur'an's own teaching elsewhere that the prophets and the righteous will intercede on the Day of Judgment. The commentators reconcile the two with care, and the reconciliation is essential for a correct understanding. The intercession that the Qur'an affirms — the intercession of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and of others, by God's permission, for sinners among the believers — is not the "availing" that verse 19 denies. The verse denies the kind of avail in which one soul, by its own independent power, rescues another or changes another's record — the avail the disbelievers and the heedless imagined, by which wealth, family, or patrons would save them regardless of their deeds. The intercession the Qur'an affirms is entirely different: it is a gift that God Himself grants and permits, exercised only by His leave and only for those He wills, and it never operates against justice — it is itself an act of the Command that "that Day is with God." Thus the verse's absolute denial of independent avail stands, while the door of God-granted intercession — which is really an expression of God's own mercy — remains open within the Command that is wholly His.

**The Command Wholly With God: The End of Every False Authority**

The declaration that the Command is with God on that Day is also the final dismantling of every false authority that human beings ever set up in rivalry with God. In this world, men have worshipped and served many masters — tyrants, systems, wealth, self, the "satans" of Sūrah 2:14 — and have imagined that these possessed some real command over their affairs. The Day of Judgment reveals the truth that always was: all command, all authority, all power was and is and will be God's alone. The Qur'an states this with the same finality elsewhere: "To God belongs the Command, before and after" (30:4), "the Command is God's alone" (12:40), and in the most sweeping of all its statements of divine sovereignty: "Everything will perish save His Face. His is the Command, and to Him you will be returned" (28:88). The verse 19 thus brings the sūrah to its climax by naming the reality that the whole sūrah has been teaching: the creature who was created, fashioned, and proportioned by a noble Lord (vv. 7–8), who was guarded and recorded by noble scribes (vv. 10–12), and who denied the religion and the Judgment (v. 9), will on that Day stand stripped of every support and every excuse before the only Commander there is — the God whose Command no soul can alter and before whose justice no soul can avail another.

**The Sūrah's Close as Its Beginning Resolved**

Read as a whole, the closing verse resolves the sūrah's opening. It began with the sky being cleft asunder (v. 1) and the whole cosmos dismantled; it ends with the Command wholly with God. The dismantling was never destruction for its own sake — it was the removal of everything that stood between the soul and the single reality of God's Command. The sky was cleft so that nothing could hide; the stars were scattered so that no false guide remained; the seas were burst so that no barrier stood; the graves were overturned so that every soul would rise; and now, at the end, every soul rises to find itself alone before the God whose Command is absolute. The verse's final words — "and the Command that Day is with God" — are the sūrah's last and greatest teaching: that beneath all the created order, all the causes and intermediaries and powers of this world, there stands the single, sovereign, all-commanding God, to whom every soul will return, and before whom the only thing that will avail a soul is the record of what it sent forth and what it left behind. To live in the light of this verse is to live with the awareness that the Command is God's already — not merely on the Day of Judgment but in every present moment — and that the wise soul orders its life now, while the Command can still be met with repentance and mercy, before the Day when no soul will avail another and the Command is wholly with God.

## Closing Reflection on the Sūrah

**The Unity of the Sūrah's Teaching**

Sūrah al-Infiṭār, in its nineteen verses, presents one of the most complete and concentrated statements of the Qur'anic worldview to be found anywhere in the revelation. It moves from the physical upheaval of the cosmos to the moral accountability of the soul; from the astonishing generosity of the Creator who fashioned every human being to the astonishing ingratitude of the creature who denies Him; from the silent presence of the recording angels to the open record of the Day; from the two destinies of bliss and fire to the absolute solitude and absolute divine sovereignty of the Judgment. Every verse serves the same central truth: that the human being is a creature of a noble and generous Lord, that his deeds are known and recorded, and that he will one day stand alone before the Command of God to receive what he has sent forth.

**The Sūrah's Gift to the Believer**

The sūrah's gift is not merely doctrinal but practical and spiritual. It is a standing invitation to the believer to live with the Day in view — to keep the sky's cleaving, the graves' overturning, and the record's opening before the eyes of the heart, so that no action is done in heedless forgetfulness of its consequences and no moment is wasted in the delusion of the world. It is a summons to gratitude toward the noble Lord who created, fashioned, proportioned, and assembled every human being; to righteousness lived under the gaze of the noble recording angels; to repentance that erases from the record the deeds that would lead to the Fire; and to hope in the mercy of the God whose Command is absolute, for that same God has opened the door of forgiveness to every soul that turns to Him before the Day when the door is shut. To recite this sūrah, in the prayer or in the quiet of reflection, is to stand, for a moment, inside the Day it describes — and to return from that standing resolved to live as one who knows that the Command is with God, and that the record is being written now.
