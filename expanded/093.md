# Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā (Chapter 93) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Short Chapter Revealed as a Personal Consolation**

Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā, "The Morning Brightness," is one of the earliest revelations of the Qur'an and one of the most tender. It belongs to the early Makkan period, when the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was a small persecuted figure surrounded by mockery, and when the revelation itself had briefly paused. Many commentators count it as the fourth or seventh sūrah in chronological order of revelation, and several hold that it was the first complete sūrah to be revealed all at once in a single occasion, though others give that distinction to al-Fātiḥah. Whatever the precise numbering, its atmosphere is unmistakable: this is a chapter spoken by God to a grieving heart. Its eleven short verses move from cosmic oaths, to a direct denial that God has abandoned His servant, to a promise about the future, to a reminder of past favors, to ethical commands that turn received mercy into mercy given. No other chapter of this length combines divine reassurance, personal biography, eschatology, and social ethics so tightly.

**Why the Chapter Is Called al-Ḍuḥā**

The title comes from its first word, *wa'l-ḍuḥā*, "By the morning brightness." The Arabic word *ḍuḥā* most commonly means the bright light of the rising sun after it has fully risen, and by extension the forenoon period from after sunrise until near midday — the time when the supererogatory forenoon prayer (*ṣalāt al-ḍuḥā*) is performed. But the classical lexicons and commentaries record a wider range. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī note that it can mean the heat and full light of the sun, the whole daylight hours illuminated by the sun, and, in a more cosmic reading reported by al-Qurṭubī, the whole of visible creation made manifest by sunlight. The same word appears elsewhere, as in:

> *"As though, on the Day they see it, they had tarried but an evening or its morning (ḍuḥāhā)."*(Qur'an 79:46)

The choice of this word as the chapter's name is itself a commentary: a sūrah revealed in a moment of darkness is named after the brightest part of the morning. The title promises what the verses then prove — that light follows night by divine law.

**The Pause in Revelation and the Mockery That Preceded It**

The occasion of revelation (*sabab al-nuzūl*) is reported with unusual consistency across the early sources, though details vary. After the first revelations, the Prophet ﷺ experienced a pause (*fatrat al-waḥy*) during which no new revelation came for a period reported variously as twelve days, forty days, or longer. During this silence, his enemies seized the moment. Several reports say that the idolaters jeered, "Muhammad's Lord has forsaken him and hates him." One specific account, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Jundub ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Bajalī, states that the Prophet ﷺ fell ill and could not rise for the night vigil (*tahajjud*) for two or three nights, whereupon a woman — identified in the commentaries as Umm Jamīl bint Ḥarb, the wife of Abū Lahab — came and said, "O Muhammad, I hope your satan has forsaken you, for I have not seen him near you for two or three nights." It was then that God revealed:

> *"By the morning brightness, and by the night when still, thy Lord has not forsaken thee; nor does He despise."* (Qur'an 93:1–3)

Al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī all transmit versions of this background. Whether the mockery came from one woman or a group of Quraysh, and whether the pause lasted days or weeks, the human situation is clear and profoundly relatable: a faithful servant passes through a period of divine silence, and the watching world interprets that silence as abandonment. The sūrah answers both the grief within and the mockery without.

**The Architecture of Eleven Verses**

The chapter divides naturally into four movements. Verses 1–3 form an oath and its answer: God swears by the two great alternating signs of morning and night, then delivers the "response to the oath" (*jawāb al-qasam*) — the denial of abandonment. Verses 4–5 turn to the future: the Hereafter and indeed every "later" state will be better than the "earlier" one, and God will give until His servant is content. Verses 6–8 turn to the past: three remembered favors — shelter for the orphan, guidance for the unguided, enrichment for the needy — each phrased as a rhetorical question expecting grateful acknowledgment. Verses 9–11 turn to duty: three commands that mirror the three favors — do not oppress the orphan, do not repel the petitioner, and proclaim the blessing of your Lord. Past mercy becomes present mission. The one who was sheltered must shelter; the one who was guided must teach; the one who was enriched must give and speak gratefully of the gift. This symmetry — three gifts, three duties — is among the most elegant structures in the Qur'an.

**A Chapter Paired With the One After It**

Al-Ḍuḥā is inseparable from the chapter that follows it, Sūrah al-Sharḥ (Chapter 94), "The Expansion." The two share the same second-person address to the Prophet ﷺ, the same retrospective of favors, and the same movement from hardship to relief. Early reports even suggest some Companions regarded them as a single sūrah. The famous promise stands as the seal of both:

> *"So truly with hardship comes ease. Truly with hardship comes ease."* (Qur'an 94:5–6)

Read together, the two chapters teach one doctrine: divine care is not proved by the absence of hardship but by what hardship is made to produce. The morning brightness follows the still night; the expanded breast follows the heavy burden. The pairing also explains a beloved practice: many reciters, following reports that the Prophet ﷺ uttered *Allāhu akbar* in joy when al-Ḍuḥā was revealed, pronounce the *takbīr* ("God is great") at the end of this sūrah and each short sūrah after it to the end of the Qur'an — a small shout of the very gladness this chapter restored.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary below treats each of the eleven verses as an independent discourse, in strict order, exactly as mandated. Verses that the source commentary groups together (notably 93:1–2) are here separated and given full individual treatment. Each verse is quoted, then expanded through Arabic language, classical commentators, authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the logic of its teaching, and its bearing on modern life — including the psychology of grief, despair, gratitude, and generosity. Nothing has been compressed because the chapter is short; indeed, its brevity is the reason for lingering. A chapter revealed to heal one wounded heart has been healing wounded hearts ever since, and it repays slow reading.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:1

> **By the morning brightness,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath Spoken at Daybreak**

The sūrah opens with an oath: *wa'l-ḍuḥā* — "By the morning brightness." The prefixed *wāw* is the *wāw al-qasam*, the "wāw of swearing," by which God calls created things to witness the truth of what follows. The Qur'an swears frequently — by time, by the fig and the olive, by the sun and its brightness, by the night and the day — and the scholars explain that when God swears by a creature, He thereby honors it and appoints it as evidence. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both stress that divine oaths are never decorative; each object sworn by bears an intrinsic connection to the assertion it introduces. Here the connection is luminous: a man sitting in the darkness of suspended revelation is addressed by an oath sworn on morning light itself. Before a single doctrine is stated, the imagery has already preached the sermon — night does not last.

**What *Ḍuḥā* Means in the Arabic Tongue**

The word *ḍuḥā* (from the root *ḍ-ḥ-w*, denoting manifest brightness and exposure to the sun) is defined by the lexicographers as the full brightness of the forenoon, when the sun has risen high and its light spreads completely — distinct from *fajr* (the first crack of dawn) and *shurūq* (the moment of sunrise). It names not the promise of light but light arrived and established. Al-Shawkānī observed a fine point: when *ḍuḥā* is mentioned before night, as here, it encompasses the entire day in its brightness, set in deliberate contrast with the entire night in its stillness. Others, including al-Qurṭubī, widen the meaning further to the whole day illuminated by the sun, or even — in a reading he records — the whole of creation made visible through sunlight, since nothing in the visible world is seen except by light. The verse thus swears by the most universal daily miracle: the world, every morning, is created again out of darkness, and no one finds this strange except the one who has stopped noticing.

**The Morning of Moses and the Prostration of the Magicians**

Beyond the plain sense, the commentaries preserve specific historical readings. Some held that *al-ḍuḥā* here alludes to the particular morning on which God spoke to Moses — the morning of the theophany — while the night of the next verse alludes to the Night Journey and Ascension (*al-isrāʾ wa'l-miʿrāj*) of Muhammad ﷺ. On this reading the two oaths frame the two greatest prophetic encounters with the unseen, one Mosaic and one Muḥammadan, and the sūrah consoles the later prophet by setting him in the company of the earlier. Others, as reported by al-Shawkānī, took *ḍuḥā* as the hour at which the sorcerers of Pharaoh recognized the truth of Moses's sign and fell prostrate, an event the Qur'an itself fixes in the forenoon:

> *"He said: Your appointment is the day of adornment, and that the people be gathered in the forenoon (ḍuḥā)."*(Qur'an 20:59)

That was a morning on which falsehood's champions became truth's martyrs within a single hour — an apt emblem for a sūrah revealed to a prophet whose own vindication would come with the same suddenness. And in the symbolic register, some commentators read *ḍuḥā* as the light of the Garden and night as the lightlessness of the Fire, or *ḍuḥā* as the radiance in the hearts of those who know God (*al-ʿārifūn*) and night as the blackness in the hearts of the deniers. The readings do not cancel one another; the oath is capacious enough to carry the literal day, the remembered mornings of sacred history, and the inward morning of the illuminated heart all at once.

**Why God Swears by the Day to a Man in the Night**

The pastoral logic of the oath deserves lingering attention. The Prophet ﷺ was living through a "night" — revelation withheld, enemies vocal, his own heart heavy. God does not open with argument but with atmosphere: *remember the morning*. Every human being knows that whichever night is darkest, the sun has never once failed to rise. The oath therefore functions as what the theologians would call an argument from customary divine constancy (*sunnat Allāh*): the God who unfailingly brings the physical morning will unfailingly bring the spiritual morning. The Qur'an makes the same move elsewhere on the largest scale:

> *"Say: Have you considered? If God were to make the night endless for you until the Day of Resurrection, what god other than God could bring you light? Will you not hear?"* (Qur'an 28:71)

Light is not the achievement of the sleeper; it is the gift of the Creator to the sleeper. The Prophet ﷺ is thus reminded, before he is promised anything, that relief is not a wage he must earn by anxious effort but a sunrise God will bring by established habit.

**The Psychology of Morning and the Discipline of Hope**

Modern experience confirms what the oath assumes: morning governs mood. Physicians of the soul in every age have observed that grief sharpens at night and softens at dawn; even contemporary studies of mood, sleep, and light find that morning brightness regulates the inner clock and lifts despair. The *sunnah* built a whole discipline around this fact — the dawn prayer, the morning remembrances (*adhkār al-ṣabāḥ*), the Prophet's ﷺ supplication, "O God, bless my community in its early hours" (Abū Dāwūd 2606; al-Tirmidhī 1212, graded ḥasan). The believer is trained to meet each daybreak as a small resurrection:

> *"Praise be to God, who gives us life after having caused us to die, and to Him is the resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī 6312; Muslim 2711)

For anyone passing through a "still night" — depression, unemployment, estrangement, a prayer that seems unanswered — this verse prescribes the same medicine it gave the Prophet ﷺ: anchor hope not in feeling but in pattern. You have survived one hundred percent of your worst nights so far, and every one of them ended in a morning you did not create. The oath of verse 1 asks the sufferer to generalize from cosmology to biography: the Lord of the sunrise is the Lord of your circumstance, and He does not keep one habit of mercy in the heavens while abandoning it in a human heart.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:2

> **and by the night when still,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Half of the Oath**

The second verse completes the oath begun in the first: *wa'l-layli idhā sajā* — "and by the night when still." If verse 1 swore by light arrived, verse 2 swears by darkness settled. The pairing is deliberate and pervades the Qur'an: night and day are the two alternating witnesses to divine lordship, each glorifying God in its own mode. Ibn Kathīr, following the early exegetes, treats the two verses as a single evidentiary act — God calls both phases of time to testify to the single denial that follows in verse 3. The consolation is thus framed by completeness: the God of brightness and the God of darkness is one God, and neither condition escapes His witnessing. The sufferer who feels abandoned in the dark is told that the dark itself is sworn by — it is not a place outside God's oath but an instrument of it.

**What *Sajā* Means: Stillness, Covering, and Calm**

The key word is *sajā*, from the root *s-j-w*, which the lexicographers explain as settling, becoming still, and covering completely. Al-Ṭabarī records its range: the night "when it covers" the day with its darkness, "when it settles" into complete calm, when its darkness extends and creatures grow quiet. Al-Shawkānī adds that some read it as "the night as it approaches" and others as "the night as it departs," but the majority sense is the night at its fullest — dark, motionless, enveloping. The stillness is audible in the word itself: *sajā* is soft, sibilant, almost a hush. Mevlevi-like, the Qur'an elsewhere describes the same mercy of nocturnal stillness:

> *"And among His signs is that He made for you the night and the day, that you may rest therein and seek of His bounty."* (Qur'an 28:73)

And:

> *"He it is who made the night a covering for you, and sleep for rest."* (Qur'an 25:47)

Night in the Qur'an is not evil; it is a garment, a shelter, a time of whispered prayer. The oath therefore dignifies the very condition the Prophet ﷺ was enduring: his "night" of withheld revelation is likened to the night God Himself swears by — a darkness with a function, not a darkness of rejection.

**The Night of the Ascension and the Hours of Intimacy**

Just as commentators found sacred history in the morning of verse 1, several — including al-Qurṭubī — found it in the night of verse 2, reading it as an allusion to the Night Journey and Ascension (*laylat al-miʿrāj*), when the Prophet ﷺ was carried from Makkah to Jerusalem and upward through the heavens. On this reading the two oaths compress the prophet's whole spiritual biography: public vindication in the morning, intimate elevation in the night. Other commentators kept the sense general but drew out the devotional meaning: night is the season of the lovers of God, when the *tahajjud* worshipper rises while the world sleeps, as the Qur'an commands the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"And during the night, keep vigil with it as a supererogatory devotion for thee; it may be that thy Lord will raise thee to a praised station."* (Qur'an 17:79)

The irony is poignant and surely intended: the sūrah was revealed, on one account, precisely because illness had kept the Prophet ﷺ from his night vigil for two or three nights — and God swears, in comforting him, by the night itself. The missed devotion becomes the instrument of devotion's renewal. What the enemy mocked as a sign of desertion ("your satan has left you, since you did not rise"), God elevates into an oath attesting the opposite.

**Why Morning and Night Must Be Sworn Together**

The pairing teaches a theology of alternation. Neither day alone nor night alone could carry the argument; together they declare that change itself is God's signature. The Qur'an returns to this lesson relentlessly:

> *"Thou causest the night to pass into the day, and Thou causest the day to pass into the night."* (Qur'an 3:27)

> *"And He subjected to you the night and the day, and the sun and the moon."* (Qur'an 16:12)

For the grieving heart the logic runs: the same Lord who moves the world from brightness to darkness moves it from darkness to brightness; no phase is permanent, and permanence belongs only to Him. Al-Rāzī-style reasoning would add the metaphysical point: if God can bring the greater alternation — the turning of the entire cosmos twice daily — He can assuredly bring the lesser one, the turning of one man's sorrow into joy. The oath thus answers despair on despair's own terms. Despair says, "This night will never end." The oath replies, "By the night itself — and by every morning that ever followed one — it will."

**Stillness as Mercy: A Lesson for Restless Lives**

There is a further, quieter gift in the verse for modern readers. Contemporary life pathologizes stillness; darkness must be lit, silence must be filled, waiting must be optimized. This verse rehabilitates the night. Stillness is not failure; covering is not abandonment. Just as the body requires sleep to consolidate memory and repair tissue, the soul requires fallow periods — unanswered prayers, unproductive seasons, unexplained pauses — in which growth continues underground. The farmer does not dig up the seed at midnight to check its progress; the believer need not interpret every silent night as divine hatred. The verse invites a practical discipline: when the night of circumstance settles, settle with it — rest, pray briefly rather than anxiously, trust the rotation. The God who swears by stillness will not waste it. As the next verse will declare outright, His silence was never His absence.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:3

> **thy Lord has not forsaken thee; nor does He despise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer the Oaths Were Waiting For**

Verse 3 is the *jawāb al-qasam*, the "response to the oath" — the assertion for which verses 1 and 2 were summoned as witnesses. After the double oath by morning and night, God declares: *mā waddaʿaka rabbuka wa mā qalā* — "Thy Lord has not forsaken thee; nor does He despise." Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī both emphasize the structure: the two cosmic signs testify, and the verdict concerns one man. The grandeur of the witnesses magnifies the tenderness of the verdict. All of day and night are called into court to settle a single question — whether God had abandoned Muhammad ﷺ — and the answer is an absolute negative, twice stated. The verse is the theological center of the sūrah; everything before it prepares it, and everything after it proves it.

**Two Verbs of Abandonment, Each Denied Precisely**

The Arabic is exquisitely exact. *Waddaʿa* (from *w-d-ʿ*) means to bid farewell to a beloved one — to leave someone with a sense of parting and relinquishment. It is a warmer, sadder verb than mere "leaving" (*tark*); it is what one does at a journey's parting. Its denial therefore means: God has not taken leave of you, has not closed the companionship, has not ended the relationship. *Qalā* (from *q-l-y*) means to hate intensely, to detest and reject with loathing. The two denials cover the full spectrum of feared rejection: neither gentle withdrawal (*waddaʿa*) nor hostile repudiation (*qalā*). Al-Qurṭubī notes the tense shift as well: the first denial is in the past (*mā waddaʿa* — "He has not forsaken"), addressing the concrete episode of the pause, while the second is in the timeless present (*wa mā qalā* — "nor does He despise"), addressing the standing disposition. God says, in effect: I did not leave you then, and I am not one who hates you now. Past event and present attitude are both cleared.

**The Missing Pronoun That Widens Into a Universal Promise**

A celebrated grammatical observation attaches to this verse. The first clause includes the object pronoun — *waddaʿa-ka*, "forsaken *thee*" — but the second omits it: not *wa mā qalā-ka* ("nor does He despise *thee*") but simply *wa mā qalā* ("nor does He despise"). The classical rhetoricians, followed by Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī, draw two lessons. First, euphony and emphasis: ending the verse on the open, resonant *qalā* gives the denial a ringing finality. Second, and more beautiful: the omission universalizes the statement. By dropping "thee," God declares not only "He does not hate *you*, O Muhammad" but, as an absolute truth, "it is not in His nature to hate" — to despise His servants capriciously. The verse thus grows from a personal consolation into a statement of divine character. The God of Muhammad ﷺ is not a God who takes men up and casts them off in disgust; He is, as the Qur'an says of His way with believers:

> *"God is the Protector of those who believe; He brings them out of darkness into light."* (Qur'an 2:257)

**Answering the Mockers Without Naming Them**

The verse descends directly upon the mockery recorded in the occasion of revelation. The idolaters had said, "His Lord has forsaken him (*waddaʿahu*) and hates him (*qalāhu*)" — the verse takes up their very verbs and negates them in order. This is the Qur'an's characteristic method with slander against the Prophet ﷺ: it quotes the charge by allusion and refutes it by divine testimony, without dignifying the slanderers with names. The same pattern appears in the defense of the Prophet's ﷺ sanity:

> *"Thou art not, by thy Lord's blessing, a madman."* (Qur'an 68:2)

And in the defense of his receiving revelation:

> *"Your companion has neither strayed nor erred, nor does he speak from caprice."* (Qur'an 53:2–3)

Notice too the choice of *rabbuka* — "*thy* Lord." God does not say "God has not forsaken thee" but "*thy* Lord" — the One who owns thee, rears thee, manages thy affair as a lord manages his charge. The possessive is itself the refutation: a lord does not despise what he has undertaken to nurture. For every believer who has heard the modern equivalents of Umm Jamīl's taunt — "where is your God now?", "your piety has earned you nothing" — this verse models the answer: the verdict on one's standing with God is His to pronounce, not the onlookers', and He has pronounced it.

**When Believers Feel Forsaken: The Psychology of Divine Silence**

Beyond its first occasion, this verse is the Qur'an's charter for everyone who has mistaken divine silence for divine hatred. The feeling is universal: prayers seem to hit the ceiling, devotion dries up, calamity strikes the obedient while the defiant prosper, and the heart whispers the ancient slander against itself — *God has left me*. This verse distinguishes, with divine authority, between *delay* and *desertion*. A teacher's silence during an examination is not the teacher's resignation; a surgeon's silence over the patient is not indifference but concentration; a commander's silence before dawn is not surrender but timing. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the theology explicit:

> *"And your Lord says: Call upon Me; I shall answer you."* (Qur'an 40:60)

— a promise of answer, not of immediacy. And the Prophet ﷺ taught that the supplication of the servant is answered "so long as he does not grow impatient and say, 'I supplicated and was not answered'" (al-Bukhārī 6340; Muslim 2735). Verse 3 is the divine side of that teaching: God's pauses are chapters in the care, not cancellations of it. The practical counsel follows: in seasons of felt abandonment, do not audit God's love by your emotions; audit it by His oath. Morning and night themselves have sworn that He does not forsake — and feelings, however loud, are not competent witnesses against that testimony.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:4

> **And the Hereafter shall be better for thee than this life.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Promise Stated in the Fewest Possible Words**

Verse 4 reads in Arabic: *wa la'l-ākhiratu khayrun laka min al-ūlā* — literally, "And the Last shall be better for thee than the First." The commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, hold the primary meaning to be eschatological: the Hereafter (*al-ākhirah*), the Last Abode, will be better for the Prophet ﷺ than this first life (*al-ūlā*), this world. Stated with sweeping brevity, the verse places the whole of the present hardship inside the whole of eternity and finds it light. The mockers measured Muhammad's ﷺ standing by his immediate circumstances — a pause in revelation, poverty, rejection. God answers by changing the scale of measurement: judge not by the first chapter but by the last, not by the world that passes but by the world that remains. What looks like diminishment in the near frame is increase in the far one.

**The Verse the Whole Qur'an Repeats**

This comparison — Hereafter over world — is among the most reiterated doctrines of the Qur'an, and the commentaries gather the parallels as its proof. The world is "fleeting enjoyment" beside the Hereafter:

> *"Compared to the Hereafter, the life of the world is but fleeting enjoyment."* (Qur'an 13:26)

The Hereafter is living in the true sense:

> *"And this worldly life is but diversion and play; and truly the Abode of the Hereafter — that is the Life, if they but knew."* (Qur'an 29:64)

And the Qur'an rebukes those who invert the judgment:

> *"But you prefer the life of the world, though the Hereafter is better and more lasting."* (Qur'an 87:16–17)

The Prophet's ﷺ own life embodied the verse. Ibn Kathīr records the report that when the Prophet ﷺ was given the choice between remaining in this world until its end and then entering Paradise, or passing on to be with God, he chose companionship with God — the event alluded to in his final illness, when he said, "O God, the Highest Companionship," choosing the Last over the First (al-Bukhārī 4463; Muslim 2444). The verse thus describes both a destiny and a preference: the Hereafter *will be* better for him, and he *chose* it as better.

**The Second Reading: Every Tomorrow Better Than Every Yesterday**

Alongside the eschatological sense, many commentators — among them al-Qurṭubī — preserve a complementary reading: *al-ākhirah* as "the later" and *al-ūlā* as "the earlier," so that the verse promises a continuously improving trajectory *within* this life as well. Every later stage of the Prophet's ﷺ affair would be better than the earlier: the mission wider, the community larger, the victory nearer, the light fuller. History vindicated the reading spectacularly. The man comforted in this verse as a grieving, mocked preacher in Makkah would within two decades receive the allegiance of Arabia, establish a polity in Madinah, see his persecutors enter his religion in crowds, and leave a Book recited by billions. The Qur'an itself elsewhere describes this pattern of increasing triumph:

> *"He it is who sent His Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth, to make it prevail over all religion."* (Qur'an 48:28)

For the individual believer the two readings merge into one law of spiritual arithmetic: with God, the end exceeds the beginning — whether the "end" is death, the Hereafter, or simply the next season of a faithful life. Despair, which insists the best is behind, is arithmetically refuted.

**Why the Promise Is Logically Necessary, Not Merely Comforting**

The verse is not wishful thinking; it states a moral necessity. If the righteous suffer and the wicked flourish in this world — as they observably do — then either the universe is ultimately unjust, or there exists a "Last" that redresses the "First." The Qur'an presses exactly this argument:

> *"Or shall We make those who believe and perform righteous deeds like those who work corruption upon the earth? Or shall We make the reverent like the profligate?"* (Qur'an 38:28)

A universe in which Pharaoh drowns only after enslaving generations, in which prophets are mocked and martyrs slain, *requires* a Hereafter for the moral account to balance; otherwise cruelty would be the final word and virtue a failed strategy. Verse 4 announces that the balance exists and favors the faithful. This is why the verse consoles not by minimizing present pain — it does not say "thy suffering is nothing" — but by *outweighing* it. The scale has two pans; the mockers weighed only one.

**Living the Verse in an Age of Immediate Measurement**

Modern life is militantly *ūlā*-minded: it measures persons by quarterly returns, follower counts, visible success by thirty, and treats any present obscurity as verdict. This verse retrains the eye. The young believer struggling in obscurity, the convert rejected by family, the honest trader outsold by the unscrupulous, the mother whose labor leaves no résumé — all are judged failures by first-pan accounting. Verse 4 commands second-pan vision: *what will this be worth at the Last?* Practically, it counsels three disciplines. First, lengthen the horizon before judging any life, especially your own; no faithful story is a tragedy that ends before death. Second, invest where the verse says the return lies — in deeds that survive the world, since:

> *"Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of the world; but the lasting righteous deeds are better with thy Lord in reward, and better in hope."* (Qur'an 18:46)

Third, receive present hardship as the early pages of a book whose ending God has already written and sworn to be better. The Prophet ﷺ lived this arithmetic so completely that he could be hungry, bereaved, and wounded — and still be, by his own testimony, the most contented of men. The verse does not promise believers an easy First; it promises them a better Last, and asks whether they believe the Promiser.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:5

> **And surely thy Lord shall give unto thee, and thou shalt be content.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Gift Promised With Double Emphasis**

Verse 5 intensifies the promise: *wa la-sawfa yuʿṭīka rabbuka fa-tarḍā* — "And surely thy Lord shall give unto thee, and thou shalt be content." The Arabic piles assurance upon assurance: the emphatic *lām* (*la-*), the particle of futurity (*sawfa*), and the verb of giving (*yuʿṭī*) whose object is left magnificently unspecified. God does not say *what* He will give — victory, community, Paradise, intercession, the Kawthar — because the gift exceeds enumeration. The verse ends not with the gift but with its effect: *fa-tarḍā*, "and thou shalt be content." The promise is not merely that giving will occur, but that it will continue until the receiver's contentment is complete. Ibn Kathīr gathers the early reports that this verse encompasses everything God bestowed on His Prophet ﷺ in this world — triumph, the spread of the message, the love of the believers — and everything stored for him in the next, chiefly the station of intercession and the supreme felicity of Paradise.

**The Prophet's Request and God's Enumeration of Favors**

Ibn ʿAbbās transmitted a luminous report concerning these verses, preserved by al-Qurṭubī, al-Shawkānī, and al-Wāḥidī: the Prophet ﷺ said, "I asked my Lord for something I wish I had not asked. I said: O my Lord, there were prophets before me — to some of whom You subjected the wind" — mentioning Solomon son of David — "and some of whom could bring the dead to life" — mentioning Jesus son of Mary — "and some of whom..." Thereupon God answered by enumerating His favors: "Did I not find thee an orphan and shelter thee?" He said, "Indeed, O my Lord." "Did I not find thee astray and guide thee?" "Indeed, O my Lord." "Did I not find thee in need and enrich thee?" "Indeed, O my Lord." "Have I not expanded thy breast and lifted from thee thy burden?" — referring to the opening of Sūrah al-Sharḥ — "Indeed, O my Lord." The report, whether taken as the direct occasion of verse 5 or as its companion illumination, reveals the verse's logic: the Prophet ﷺ measured himself against the spectacular miracles of earlier prophets and felt his portion small — and God answered by displaying a greater miracle, the transformation of an orphaned, unguided, needy man into the mercy to the worlds. Verse 5 then seals the exchange: *and there is still more to come, until thou art content.*

**What the Giving Includes: Intercession, the River, and the Community's Salvation**

The classical commentaries delight in specifying portions of the unspecified gift. Foremost is the Great Intercession (*al-shafāʿah al-ʿuẓmā*), when all mankind will seek an intercessor on the Day of Resurrection and the Prophet ﷺ will prostrate beneath the Throne, be told "Raise thy head; ask and thou shalt be given; intercede and thy intercession shall be accepted," and will intercede until — as the authentic ḥadīth in al-Bukhārī (7510) and Muslim (193) from Anas details — multitudes are brought out of the Fire. Closely joined to it is the report that the Prophet ﷺ, reflecting on this verse, said of his community that he would not be content while a single one of them remained in the Fire — a tradition the commentators cite to show that his *riḍā* (contentment) is bound up with his followers' salvation. Also included is al-Kawthar, the river of Paradise granted to him:

> *"Truly We have given thee al-Kawthar."* (Qur'an 108:1)

— whose basin his community will drink from on the Day of Thirst. And in this world, the giving included the conquest of Makkah, the entry of the tribes into Islam, and the establishment of a revelation that would outlive every empire contemporary with it. The verse's open-ended "giving" thus spans both worlds and gathers them into one promise: *nothing good will be withheld from thee, until thou declarest thyself sufficed.*

**Contentment as the True Measure of Gift**

The verse's ending — *fa-tarḍā*, "and thou shalt be content" — redefines abundance. Human beings typically measure gifts by quantity; God measures them by *effect*: the gift is complete when contentment (*riḍā*) is produced. This is a profound psychological truth embedded in theology. Wealth that leaves its owner anxious is not yet "giving" in the Qur'anic sense; power that leaves its holder grasping is not gift but trial. True giving terminates in the quiet of the heart. The Qur'an elsewhere makes *riḍwān* — God's contentment with the servant and the servant's contentment with God — the highest of all rewards, above even the Gardens:

> *"And contentment from God is greater."* (Qur'an 9:72)

And it describes the soul that has attained it:

> *"O soul at peace, return unto thy Lord, content, contenting."* (Qur'an 89:27–28)

Verse 5 thus promises the Prophet ﷺ the summit of human experience: a heart so filled by divine generosity that wanting itself comes to rest. The scholars note the beautiful reciprocity: in verse 3 God denied abandoning His servant; here He promises to fill His servant — from the removal of fear to the establishment of joy.

**From the Prophet's Contentment to Ours**

Though addressed to Muhammad ﷺ, the verse establishes a pattern every believer may enter. God's way with His servants is giving-until-contentment for all who walk the path of trust. The Qur'an generalizes the promise:

> *"And whosoever is reverent toward God, He will make for him a way out, and provide for him whence he reckons not."* (Qur'an 65:2–3)

The practical application is twofold. First, ask largely and expectantly: the God of this verse gives without a stated limit, and the Prophet ﷺ taught, "When one of you asks God, let him be earnest in asking, for nothing is too great for God" (paraphrased from al-Bukhārī 6339; Muslim 2679). Second, recognize contentment itself as the gift to seek above the gifts: pray not only "give me this and that" but "make me content with what Thou givest." The discontented rich man and the contented poor man have already exchanged places in the only accounting that matters. And for those who love the Prophet ﷺ, the verse is a summons to contribute to his contentment as described by the commentators — by being among the community whose salvation completes his joy, through faith, repentance, and love of his way. His *riḍā* was tied to ours; ours may be tied to his.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:6

> **Did He not find thee an orphan and shelter,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Remembered Favor**

With verse 6 the sūrah turns from future promise to past proof: *a-lam yajidka yatīman fa-āwā* — "Did He not find thee an orphan and shelter?" The interrogative expects the answer "Yes, indeed" — it is God's way of making the servant testify to grace already received. The logic is judicial: the One who sheltered the orphaned child is the same One who now promises the grieving prophet; past care is entered as evidence for future care. Three such favors follow in verses 6–8 — shelter, guidance, enrichment — each matching one of the three commands of verses 9–11. The structure teaches that gratitude is not nostalgia; it is the premise from which obedience is deduced. Because He sheltered thee, shelter; because He guided thee, teach; because He enriched thee, give and proclaim.

**An Orphanhood the Historians Record in Detail**

The Prophet's ﷺ orphanhood was stark and historically precise. His father, ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, died while his mother Āminah bint Wahb was still pregnant with him; he never saw his father's face. His mother died when he was six years old, on the return journey from visiting his uncles in Yathrib — the commentators dwell on the image of the small boy standing at her grave at al-Abwāʾ. His grandfather ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, the chief of Quraysh, then took him in with exceptional tenderness, seating the boy on his own couch at the Kaʿbah — but died when the Prophet ﷺ was eight. Guardianship then passed to his uncle Abū Ṭālib, a man of limited means but immense loyalty, who protected him, restrained the Quraysh from harming him after the mission began, and endured the boycott in the ravine for his sake. Later, God gave him refuge of another kind in his marriage to Khadījah al-Kubrā, his first believer, his counselor, and his shelter in the earliest and hardest days of revelation. Every stage of shelter arrived exactly when the previous one failed — a relay of care with a single Runner behind it.

**What *Āwā* Means: Shelter as More Than Housing**

The verb *āwā* (from *ʾ-w-y*) means to give shelter, refuge, lodging — to take in and enfold. It is the verb used for giving refuge to the vulnerable, and the Qur'an uses it for God's sheltering of believers in their weakness:

> *"And remember when you were few, deemed weak in the land, fearing lest people snatch you away; then He sheltered you (fa-āwākum) and strengthened you with His help."* (Qur'an 8:26)

Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both stress that the sheltering here included not only physical guardianship but honor, love, and placement: the orphan was not warehoused but cherished — by his grandfather beyond custom, by his uncle beyond interest, by his wife beyond measure, and finally by a community that would love him more than their own fathers. The theology is tender: God's care for orphans is not bureaucratic provision but personal enfolding. The One who "finds" (*yajid*) — the verb implies God's attentive noticing of the child's state — is the One who shelters. No orphan is overlooked before he is sheltered; the finding precedes the enfolding.

**Why the Orphan Stands First Among the Favors**

Orphanhood heads the list for reasons both biographical and universal. Biographically, it was the Prophet's ﷺ earliest wound and therefore the earliest theater of divine care; to begin the enumeration here is to say, *My care for thee predates thy awareness of it — it began before thou couldst ask.* Universally, the orphan is the Qur'an's supreme emblem of human vulnerability — the one who cannot repay, cannot protect himself, and cannot choose his condition. By opening with the orphan, God declares His special solicitude for the defenseless as the very signature of His lordship. The Qur'an returns to orphans with extraordinary insistence — their property, their dignity, their marriage, their emancipation into independence (see 4:2–10; 6:152; 17:34) — and the Prophet ﷺ made their care a measure of communal righteousness. Beginning the favors with orphanhood thus prepares the first command of verse 9 with perfect symmetry: *thou who knowest orphanhood from the inside — oppress no orphan.*

**Shelter Given and Shelter Owed**

For modern readers the verse works in two directions at once. Inwardly, it invites every person who has known early loss — dead parents, broken homes, absent protectors — to reread their biography for the hidden *āwā*: the grandmother who raised them, the teacher who noticed them, the stranger whose help arrived precisely on time. Faith is the discipline of tracing those relays back to their single Sender. Outwardly, the verse converts every recipient of shelter into its provider. The orphanages, foster systems, sponsorships, and guardianships of the modern world are the institutional forms of *fa-āwā*; but the verse demands more than institutions — it demands the tenderness the Prophet ﷺ himself received and then commanded. He said:

> *"I and the caretaker (kāfil) of an orphan are like this in the Garden"* — and he held his index and middle fingers together. (al-Bukhārī 6005)

The promise is staggering: care for an orphan buys nearness to the Prophet ﷺ in eternity. Verse 6 is where that economy begins — with God's own sheltering of the orphan who would become the shelter of orphans.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:7

> **find thee astray and guide,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Delicate Verse in the Chapter**

Verse 7 — *wa wajadaka ḍāllan fa-hadā*, "and find thee astray and guide" — is the most carefully handled verse in the sūrah, for it touches the Prophet's ﷺ pre-revelation state. Read crudely, "astray" (*ḍāll*) might suggest misguidance in sin or unbelief, and generations of commentators have labored to state precisely what it does and does not mean. The labor itself is instructive: the verse shows Islam holding two truths together — that Muhammad ﷺ was always the noblest of men in character, and that his prophetic knowledge was pure divine gift, not human attainment. He was never corrupt, but neither was he self-guided; the guidance he brought to the world was guidance he had first to receive.

**What *Ḍāll* Can and Cannot Mean Here**

The root *ḍ-l-l* means to lose the way, to stray from the road — whether by the traveler's error, the road's obscurity, or the absence of any marked path at all. Al-Baghawī records the main interpretations. Some understood the verse plainly: before revelation, the Prophet ﷺ did not know the detailed realities of *tawḥīd* in its revealed form or the institution of prophethood, and God guided him to both — a reading supported by:

> *"We recount unto thee the most beautiful of stories, by Our having revealed unto thee this Qur'an, though before it thou wert among the heedless."* (Qur'an 12:3)

Others refined the sense: he had never worshipped idols and never doubted God's oneness — his *fiṭrah* (primordial nature) was sound — but he followed no revealed *sharīʿah* (law), since none was known among his people, and possessed no scripture until the Qur'an came. This reading leans on the decisive parallel:

> *"Thus have We revealed unto thee a Spirit from Our Command. Thou knewest not what scripture was, nor faith. But We made it a light whereby We guide whomsoever We will among Our servants."* (Qur'an 42:52)

Still others, wishing to distance the Prophet ﷺ from any religious sense of straying, read *ḍāll* literally and biographically: the time he was lost as a child in the streets of Makkah and Gabriel guided him home, or the journey on which his mount strayed from the road and Gabriel set him right. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr present these views without forcing an exclusive choice; the verse's capacious wording accommodates the outward lostness and the inward unreadiness together. What all sound readings agree upon: the verse ascribes no sin, no idolatry, no moral fault to the Prophet ﷺ — only the absence of what only revelation could supply.

**The Prophet's Pre-Revelation Life: Soundness Without Scripture**

The historical record supports the refined reading. Before revelation the Prophet ﷺ was known among Quraysh as *al-Amīn*, the Trustworthy; he never bowed to an idol, never drank wine, never participated in the pagan rites' obscenities. He withdrew to the cave of Ḥirāʾ for contemplation (*taḥannuth*), feeding the poor who came to him, seeking the God his heart knew behind a world his people misread. He possessed, in other words, the sound instinct without the revealed instruction — the hunger without the feast. The Qur'an describes the moment the feast arrived:

> *"Read! In the Name of thy Lord who created."* (Qur'an 96:1)

Verse 7 names that arrival *hudā* — guidance: not mere information but the placed-upon-the-road gift by which a sound heart finally learns the road's name, direction, and destination. The great theologians draw the universal moral: if even Muhammad ﷺ — the soundest heart ever created — needed revealed guidance to reach the truth in full, then no intellect, however brilliant, is self-sufficient. Reason is the eye; revelation is the light. The soundest eye sees nothing in darkness.

**Guidance as God's Supreme Gift**

The verse ranks guidance (*hudā*) among the three supreme favors — beside shelter and enrichment — and the Qur'an everywhere treats it as the greatest gift of all. Wealth perishes, shelter crumbles, but guidance determines eternity:

> *"Whomsoever God guides, he is rightly guided; and whomsoever He sends astray — those are the losers."* (Qur'an 7:178)

The Prophet's ﷺ guidance was moreover vicarious: God guided him *in order to* guide the world through him. He is called:

> *"A light and a clear Book... guiding them to the paths of peace."* (Qur'an 5:15–16)

Every prayer performed, every idol abandoned, every orphan fed, every verse of this very commentary written — all of it flows downstream from the *fa-hadā* ("and He guided") of this verse. The commentators note the verb's form: God does not say "and thou foundest guidance" but "and *He* guided" — guidance is entirely His act. The doctrine preserves both gratitude and humility in the guided: gratitude, because the gift is unmerited; humility, because the guided one can claim no credit over the unguided except God's favor.

**For Every Seeker Who Feels Lost**

The verse's pastoral power extends to every person conscious of being *ḍāll* — lost, uncertain, without direction. Three counsels flow from it. First, lostness honestly acknowledged is the precondition of guidance, not its disqualification: God found His prophet in that state and guided him; He finds seekers in it still. The Qur'an promises:

> *"And those who strive for Us — We shall surely guide them to Our paths."* (Qur'an 29:69)

Second, seek guidance where the Prophet ﷺ found it — in revelation, prayer, and the company of the guided — rather than in the echo chamber of unguided opinion. The age offers infinite information and little direction; this verse distinguishes the two permanently. Third, having been guided, guide: just as verse 6's shelter issues in verse 9's command to shelter, verse 7's guidance issues in verse 10's command (on one reading) to share knowledge with seekers, and verse 11's command to proclaim the blessing. The lost-and-found must become the finder of the lost. No one who remembers their own *ḍalāl* — their years of confusion, error, or emptiness before guidance — should despise the currently lost; they should recognize in them their former selves, and in the *fa-hadā* of their own story the promise of what God can do in another's.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:8

> **and find thee in need and enrich?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Favor: From Need to Sufficiency**

Verse 8 completes the triad: *wa wajadaka ʿāʾilan fa-aghnā* — "and find thee in need and enrich?" The question form continues — "Did He not...?" — expecting grateful assent. Three states of lack (orphanhood, lack of guidance, poverty) have been met by three acts of divine fullness (shelter, guidance, enrichment). Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr treat the three as ascending: the first concerns protection, the second concerns the soul's greatest need, the third concerns worldly provision. Together they declare God's lordship comprehensive: He noticed the infant without parents, the man without scripture, and the household without wealth — and supplied each lack from His own treasury. The verse closes the proof: a Lord who enriched the needy prophet will not abandon the grieving one.

**What *ʿĀʾil* and *Aghnā* Precisely Mean**

The word *ʿāʾil* (from *ʿ-y-l*) describes not mere poverty but poverty with dependents — the poor provider, whose need is multiplied by those who depend on him. It is need felt on behalf of others, the distinctive ache of the father, guardian, or husband who has mouths to feed and empty hands. The Prophet ﷺ knew this state: raised in his uncle Abū Ṭālib's crowded, modest household, later a young husband and father with no great capital, working in trade and shepherding. The verb *aghnā* (from *gh-n-y*, richness, sufficiency, independence) means to make rich, to render free of need. The Qur'an uses it for the divine act of sufficing:

> *"And that He it is who enriches and suffices."* (Qur'an 53:48)

The pairing is exact: God met need-with-dependents with sufficiency-with-dignity. He did not merely drop coins into a palm; He established His prophet beyond dependence — first through lawful means, then through contentment that made little feel like much.

**Through What Means God Enriched Him**

The commentators identify layers of enrichment. The outward layer, noted by al-Baghawī and others, was Khadījah's wealth: his marriage to the successful merchant Khadījah — whose trade he had faithfully conducted to Syria — brought him partnership in prosperity and freed him from economic anxiety during the decisive early years of the mission. Later came the sustenance of the community: the gifts of the Companions, the shared resources of Madinah, and eventually the lawful spoils by which the young polity sustained itself. But the commentators insist on a deeper layer: the true enrichment was *ghinā al-nafs*, the richness of the soul — contentment. They cite the famous ḥadīth:

> *"Wealth comes not from abundance of goods; rather, wealth is the wealth of the soul (ghinā al-nafs)."* (al-Bukhārī 6446; Muslim 1051, from Abū Hurayrah)

The Prophet ﷺ lived this definition to the letter: at the height of his authority, when Arabia's wealth flowed to Madinah, his household often passed months without kindling a cooking fire, living on dates and water (al-Bukhārī 2567; Muslim 2972, from ʿĀʾishah) — yet he was, by unanimous testimony, the most generous and least anxious of men. God had enriched him in the only way enrichment cannot be stolen: by detaching his heart from need itself.

**The Theology of Provision Behind the Verse**

Verse 8 rests on the Qur'anic doctrine of *rizq* (provision): every creature's sustenance is apportioned by God, guaranteed in its arrival though entrusted to human effort in its pursuit:

> *"And there is no creature upon the earth but that its provision is upon God."* (Qur'an 11:6)

The Prophet's ﷺ biography is the doctrine's exhibit: an orphan with no inheritance, raised in a poor household, became — through marriage, work, community, and conquest — the distributor of Arabia's wealth, who gave like one who feared no poverty. His companion Ḥakīm ibn Ḥizām reported his teaching:

> *"The upper hand is better than the lower hand... and whosoever refrains from asking, God will make him self-sufficient; and whosoever seeks independence, God will make him independent."* (al-Bukhārī 1477; Muslim 1035)

Enrichment in this economy is a spiritual transaction before it is a material one: the heart's independence from creatures precedes and outlasts the hand's possession of goods. The verse thus promises more than money; it promises *freedom from humiliating need* — the dignity of the sufficed.

**Need, Wealth, and Contentment in Modern Life**

Few verses speak more directly to the modern condition of anxious abundance. Contemporary societies produce a new species of *ʿāʾil*: persons with incomes their grandparents would have called riches, yet consumed by need — mortgaged wants, comparative cravings, dependents' expectations inflated by advertisement. Verse 8 diagnoses the disease and prescribes the cure together. The diagnosis: need is not abolished by income; the poor provider and the indebted executive share one ailment, insufficiency of the soul. The cure: seek *aghna* — divine enrichment — in its proper order. First, lawful effort without humiliation: work, trade, and provide as the young Muhammad ﷺ did before any miracle relieved him. Second, gratitude and generosity as technologies of sufficiency: the Qur'an promises increase to the thankful (14:7), and experience confirms that the giving hand feels richer than the grasping one. Third, keep Khadījah's lesson: righteous partnership — a spouse, a community, a friendship of faith — is itself a form of wealth no salary replaces. And finally, measure enrichment by the verse's own implicit standard: *art thou content?* The one who can answer yes has received the *fa-aghnā*; the one who cannot, whatever his balance, is still awaiting it.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:9

> **So as for the orphan, scorn not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Favor Received to Duty Owed**

Verse 9 opens the sūrah's final movement — three commands answering the three favors. The particle *fa-ammā* ("So as for...") draws the moral inference: *because* He sheltered thee as an orphan, *therefore* — *fa-lā taqhar*, "scorn not" the orphan. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both stress the symmetry: verse 6's gift issues in verse 9's law. This is the Qur'an's characteristic ethics of gratitude: mercy received must become mercy extended. The one who has known vulnerability from the inside is appointed its guardian on the outside. The verse addresses the Prophet ﷺ first — who needed no correction, being already the orphans' protector — and through him every believer, and through them, as al-Baghawī notes, the Quraysh themselves, who were notorious for devouring orphans' property and despising their persons.

**What *Qahr* Means: More Than Mere Oppression**

The prohibition uses the verb *qahara* (*q-h-r*), which means to overpower, subdue, dominate with harshness — to treat someone with the contemptuous mastery of the strong over the helpless. It is stronger than merely wronging; it includes scorning, humiliating, speaking roughly, and making the orphan *feel* his orphanhood. The Qur'an elsewhere condemns precisely this attitude toward the vulnerable:

> *"Nay! But you honor not the orphan."* (Qur'an 89:17)

> *"Hast thou seen him who denies the Recompense? That is he who repulses the orphan."* (Qur'an 107:1–2)

Al-Qurṭubī explains that the prohibition covers every form of *qahr*: seizing the orphan's wealth, refusing him his rights, rebuking him harshly, or receiving him with the frown that announces he is a burden. The positive counterpart is stated across the Qur'an: approach the orphan's property only "in the best manner" (6:152; 17:34), deal with orphans in equity (4:127), feed them from what God has given (4:8; 76:8). The verse thus legislates not only justice but *tenderness*: the orphan must not merely be uncheated — he must be un-despised.

**The Qur'an's Sustained Legislation for Orphans**

This single Makkan prohibition would unfold in Madinah into one of the most detailed bodies of protective law in ancient times. The commentaries gather the Madinan elaborations as this verse's progeny: the command to restore orphans' property and not exchange the bad for the good (4:2); the permission of guardianship hedged with accountability (4:3, 6); the terrible warning against consuming orphans' wealth:

> *"Truly those who consume the property of orphans wrongfully — they only consume fire in their bellies, and they shall burn in a Blaze."* (Qur'an 4:10)

The Prophet ﷺ enforced the ethic with unmatched vividness. He said:

> *"The best house among the Muslims is a house in which an orphan is treated well; and the worst house among the Muslims is a house in which an orphan is treated poorly."* (Ibn Mājah 3679, from Abū Hurayrah)

And, in the celebrated promise recorded by al-Bukhārī:

> *"I and the caretaker of an orphan are like this in the Garden"* — joining his index and middle fingers. (al-Bukhārī 6005)

The logic connecting verse 6 to verse 9 is thus cosmic in scope: God sheltered one orphan, who sheltered the orphans, whose followers were promised eternity beside him for sheltering them in turn. A single act of divine *āwā* propagates through history as an endless relay of shelter.

**Why the Orphan Tests a Civilization**

The orphan is the Qur'an's litmus case for social righteousness because the orphan cannot reciprocate. Powerful men honor those who can repay them; only the God-fearing honor those who cannot. How a society treats its orphans — and by extension its foundlings, its foster children, its refugees' unaccompanied minors, its quiet neglected children in crowded homes — reveals whether its morality is transactional or transcendent. Pre-Islamic Arabia failed the test flagrantly: guardians married orphan girls to control their property, consumed their inheritances, and regarded them as encumbrances. Islam's reversal was total: the orphan became the neighbor of the Prophet ﷺ in Paradise. The modern world, for all its institutions, still fails the test wherever children in care are processed without love, shuttled without stability, or aged out without belonging. Verse 9 judges every such system: efficiency without tenderness is another form of *qahr*.

**Practicing the Verse Today**

The verse's application is concrete and graduated. At the highest level: *kafālah* — taking an orphan into one's home and raising him as one's own, the act the Prophet ﷺ equated with his own companionship in eternity. Below it: sponsoring orphans financially, supporting orphanages and foster systems, mentoring fatherless children, ensuring their education and their property's integrity. Below even that — available to everyone: the *manner* of treatment the verse directly commands. Receive the vulnerable child with warmth; never use his condition as an insult or a lever; let no orphan in your presence feel his lack as shame. The Prophet ﷺ modeled the tenderness physically: he wiped the heads of orphans, and taught that God records a good deed for every hair the hand passes over (Aḥmad 22259, from Abū Umāmah; its chain is discussed, its meaning consonant with the whole). Every believer who was ever sheltered — by parents, teachers, a community, a timely stranger — stands in the Prophet's ﷺ position in verse 6 and is therefore addressed by verse 9: *thou wert sheltered; shelter.*

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:10

> **And as for one who requests, repel not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Command: Honoring the Seeker**

Verse 10 pairs with verse 9: *wa ammā al-sāʾila fa-lā tanhar* — "And as for one who requests, repel not." Just as verse 9 answers verse 6 (shelter received, shelter commanded), this verse answers verses 7 and 8 together — guidance and enrichment received, generosity of knowledge and wealth commanded. Al-Baghawī and Ibn Kathīr record the two principal readings of *al-sāʾil* (the requester): the beggar who asks for material help, and the seeker who asks for knowledge. The verse's wording embraces both, and the classical commentaries refuse to confine it: the one who knocks at your door and the one who knocks at your understanding are both *sāʾil*, and neither is to be repelled. The sūrah that began by consoling a prophet ends by commissioning a benefactor — of bread and of wisdom alike.

**What *Nahr* Prohibits: The Harshness, Not the Refusal**

The prohibition turns on *nahr* (from *n-h-r*), meaning to repel harshly, to rebuke, to drive away with roughness and contempt — the raised voice, the slammed door, the shaming word. The scholars are precise: the verse does not command giving to every requester on every occasion — means are limited, and the Qur'an elsewhere instructs measured, sustainable generosity (17:29; 25:67). What it prohibits absolutely is *the manner of harshness*. If you give, give graciously; if you cannot give, decline gently. Al-Baghawī summarizes the early teaching: feed the beggar who comes to the door, or turn him away kindly without rebuke. The Qur'an states the principle universally:

> *"Kind speech and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury."* (Qur'an 2:263)

And it ranks the gentle word to a petitioner above charity that wounds. The Prophet ﷺ embodied the verse: he was never known to turn a requester away empty if he possessed anything to give, and when he had nothing, he promised kindly or directed to another — "never did he say 'no' when asked for anything in Islam," as Jābir reported (Muslim 2311, paraphrased). Dignity, the verse teaches, is owed even when dirhams are not available.

**The Beggar at the Door: The Primary Reading**

Most commentators take the material beggar as the verse's first referent. In the Makkan context — and in every age — the petitioner at the door tests the householder's memory of his own need. The Prophet ﷺ, reminded in verse 8 that he was once *ʿāʾil* (needy with dependents), is commanded never to make a needy one feel despised. The Qur'an reinforces the ethic repeatedly, listing "the petitioner and the deprived" among the rightful claimants upon wealth:

> *"And in their wealth is a rightful share for the petitioner and the deprived."* (Qur'an 51:19; cf. 70:24–25)

Note the word *ḥaqq* — a *right*, not a favor. The petitioner's request is framed as collection, not begging; the householder's giving as payment, not condescension. The great jurists and ethicists built upon this verse a whole etiquette of charity: give promptly, give privately, give the good (not the cast-off — cf. 2:267), give with a cheerful face, and never follow the gift with reminder or reproach. Modern believers meet the *sāʾil* in new forms — the homeless person at the intersection, the crowdfunding appeal, the refugee family, the relative too proud to ask plainly — and the verse governs them all: *repel not.* Even suspicion of fraud does not license harshness; scholars advise that the doubtful case be met with a small kindness or a gentle word, since the sin of wrongly repelling a genuine needy one outweighs the cost of occasionally aiding an unworthy one.

**The Seeker of Knowledge: The Second Reading**

Al-Baghawī and Ibn Kathīr record the further reading: *al-sāʾil* is the one who asks for knowledge of the religion, and *lā tanhar* commands the learned never to repulse him. This reading answers verse 7 (guidance received) with perfect symmetry: *thou wert guided; guide.* Its implications built Islamic civilization's ethic of teaching: the scholar's door, like the rich man's bread, must stay open. The Prophet ﷺ warned:

> *"Whoever is asked about knowledge and conceals it will be bridled on the Day of Resurrection with a bridle of fire."* (Abū Dāwūd 3658; al-Tirmidhī 2649, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

And he commanded the knowledgeable to receive seekers with the welcome, "Welcome to the Prophet's ﷺ bequest," instructing that seekers of understanding be made room for and taught with patience (Ibn Mājah 249; al-Ṭabarānī). The etiquette flows both ways: teachers must not humiliate sincere questions — no question asked in earnest is contemptible — and the verse rebukes every form of intellectual *nahr*: the teacher who mocks the slow student, the scholar who hoards learning as status, the community that shames new Muslims for basic questions. The one who was guided from *ḍalāl* must remember his own former ignorance whenever ignorance approaches him.

**Generosity as the Proof of Enrichment**

Verses 9 and 10 together state a law of the spiritual economy: *gifts retained are gifts denied.* Shelter that does not shelter, guidance that does not guide, wealth that does not give — these are favors aborted, blessings that died in transit. The Qur'an elsewhere makes generosity the verification of every claim to faith:

> *"You shall not attain righteousness until you spend of what you love."* (Qur'an 3:92)

The psychology is exact: harshness toward requesters (*nahr*) typically springs not from principle but from fear — fear of depletion, of being used, of becoming needy again. This sūrah answers that fear with its whole argument: the God who enriched thee when thou wert *ʿāʾil* is not impoverished by thy giving; the hand that opens in His path is refilled from His treasury:

> *"And whatever you spend of anything, He shall replace it; and He is the best of providers."* (Qur'an 34:39)

Practice follows: set a fixed charity that leaves the hand before fear can object; keep small means ready for the unexpected petitioner; answer questions — religious or worldly — as one paying a debt to those who once answered yours. The sūrah's arc is complete when the consoled becomes the consoler: the orphan shelters, the guided guides, the enriched gives — and the morning brightness that dawned on one man spreads through him to all who ask.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:11

> **And as for the blessing of thy Lord, proclaim!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Command: Speak the Blessing**

The sūrah closes with its crown: *wa ammā bi-niʿmati rabbika fa-ḥaddith* — "And as for the blessing of thy Lord, proclaim!" The verb *ḥaddith* (from *ḥ-d-th*, speech, narration, occurrence) commands telling, narrating, speaking forth — making the blessing *ḥadīth*, spoken news. After prohibitions (scorn not, repel not), the chapter ends with a positive act: *speak.* Al-Qurṭubī glosses the meaning: "Spread that with which God has blessed thee, through thanks and praise." Ibn Kathīr, al-Baghawī, and al-Shawkānī record the breadth of *niʿmah* (blessing) here: it includes the Qur'an itself, the gift of prophethood (*nubuwwah*), the guidance that followed wandering (v. 7), and — in the widest reading — every blessing God has bestowed upon a person. The verse is thus at once a commission to preach and a command to give thanks: proclaim the message because it is the blessing, and proclaim the blessing because gratitude spoken is gratitude completed.

**What the Blessing Encompasses**

The commentaries refuse to narrow *niʿmat rabbika*. At its summit stands the revelation: the Qur'an, named elsewhere "a healing and a mercy" (17:82), is the blessing whose proclamation is the prophetic mission itself —

> *"O Messenger, proclaim what has been sent down unto thee from thy Lord."* (Qur'an 5:67)

Beside it stands prophethood: to be chosen as the seal of messengers, the mercy to the worlds (21:107), is a blessing whose "proclamation" is the mission's public discharge — teaching, warning, bearing glad tidings. Beneath these heights extends the plain of ordinary mercies: shelter, guidance, enrichment (vv. 6–8), health, family, daily bread — each a *niʿmah* owed its *taḥdīth*. Al-Shawkānī notes the particular link to verse 7: the blessing of guidance-after-straying especially demands proclamation, since guidance unshared stagnates. The verse's possessive — "the blessing of *thy Lord*" — repeats the intimate *rabbuka* of verse 3, closing the sūrah's circle: the Lord who did not forsake (v. 3) enumerated His lordly care (vv. 6–8) and now asks only that His care be spoken of (v. 11). Testimony is the rent gratitude pays.

**Proclamation as Thanks, Silence as Ingratitude**

The Prophet ﷺ himself expounded this verse's theology of speech in a sermon preserved across the commentaries (al-Baghawī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Shawkānī):

> *"Whosoever does not give thanks for that which is little will not give thanks for that which is much. And whosoever does not thank people does not thank God. And proclaiming God's blessings (al-taḥdīth bi-niʿam Allāh) is thanks (shukr), while abandoning it is disbelief — ingratitude (kufr)."* (Aḥmad 18467; its wording is transmitted with variations)

The equation is startling: to speak of blessings *is* thanks; to suppress them *is* a form of *kufr* — the word meaning both disbelief and, at its root, covering-over, ingratitude. The scholars explain: blessings concealed simulate absence; the silent rich man preaches, by his complaint and gloom, that God has given him nothing, and thus bears false witness against his Lord. Conversely, the grateful tongue — "Praise be to God who has blessed me with..." — preaches true witness, encouraging the hearer to trust the same Giver. This is why the Qur'an commands remembrance of blessings as worship:

> *"And remember God's blessing upon you."* (Qur'an 3:103; 5:7, among many)

Gratitude in Islam is therefore irreducibly *vocal*: the heart's thanks must ascend to the tongue, and the tongue's thanks must descend to the limbs in obedience and generosity. Verse 11 legislates the middle passage — the spoken testimony without which gratitude remains private and incomplete.

**Preaching the Message: The Prophetic Fulfillment**

On its grandest scale, the verse commissioned the public preaching that changed history. The Prophet ﷺ, consoled from grief and reminded of favor, is sent back to his people as a proclaimer. He fulfilled the command exhaustively: thirteen years of public and private invitation in Makkah, ten years of teaching, judging, and training in Madinah, a Farewell Sermon charging his followers — "Let the present convey to the absent" (al-Bukhārī 1741; Muslim 1679) — so that the *taḥdīth* would never cease. Every sermon, every Qur'an class, every parent teaching a child *al-Fātiḥah*, every act of *daʿwah* is verse 11 still being obeyed. The commentators note the wisdom of placing this commission *after* the ethical commands: the proclaimer is first purified (scorn not, repel not) and then empowered (proclaim). Character authenticates message; the orphan-scorning preacher and the petitioner-repelling teacher falsify with their conduct what their tongues announce. Mercy first, then microphone.

**Gratitude Spoken Aloud in an Age of Complaint**

No verse corrects the modern tongue more precisely. Contemporary culture monetizes complaint: feeds reward grievance, comparison manufactures lack, and even the blessed curate dissatisfaction. Against this economy of ingratitude, verse 11 prescribes a counter-discipline: *narrate thy blessings.* Practically, the scholars and ethicists recommend its forms: begin supplications by naming favors before presenting requests; speak of God's kindness to your family so children inherit a theology of generosity rather than scarcity; thank people openly and specifically, since thanks to creatures is thanks to the Creator ("whosoever does not thank people does not thank God"); and testify to guidance — tell, where fitting, how you were lost and found, for such testimony is *taḥdīth* in its most potent form. Two cautions complete the practice. First, proclaim without boasting: the speaker is the witness, not the hero — "this is from the bounty of my Lord" (27:40), as Solomon said. Second, conceal at times from envy what wisdom advises concealing; proclamation is the rule, discretion its prudent exception. It is reported that when this sūrah was revealed, the Prophet ﷺ was overjoyed, proclaimed *Allāhu akbar*, and counseled his followers to magnify God with every recitation of it — the first *taḥdīth* of the blessing this very chapter bestowed. Eleven verses that began with an oath on the morning end by turning the consoled heart into a sunrise for others: *speak, and let thy speech be light.*
