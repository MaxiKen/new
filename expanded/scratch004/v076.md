## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:76

> **Those who believe fight in the way of God, and those who disbelieve fight in the way of false deities. Fight, therefore, against the allies of Satan; surely the scheme of Satan is ever feeble.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Alladhīna Āmanū Yuqātilūna fī Sabīli Allāh: The Battle-Line Drawn by Allegiance**

The verse opens the sūrah's fighting-file with a classification so concise it has become the standing geography of all combat in the Book: *alladhīna āmanū yuqātilūna fī sabīli Allāh* — "those who believe fight in the way of God": *fī sabīli Allāh* — the standing battle-formula (its register through the sūrah: 4:74-75 above; 4:84; 4:95; and across the Book: 2:154; 2:190 — "fight in the way of God those who fight you"; 2:218; 2:244; 2:246; 3:146; 3:157; 4:76; 9:20; 9:81; 47:4; 61:11; 73:20) — and the commentators (al-Ṭabarī's standing chains; al-Rāzī's standing doctrinal note which Ibn al-Qayyim later canonizes) define the phrase by its *intentionality-structure*: the Book ordains in fighting neither terrain nor tactic but the *aim* — fighting must be *for God alone*, in obedience to His command and within His Law; al-Rāzī's standing formulation: fighting for any other end — pride, spoils, reputation, conquest — is not a defective version of fighting in God's way but a fighting for *false deities* outright, because the aim of an act is its deity: what you fight *for* is what you serve at the moment of fighting. The verse thereby splits the battlefield not by uniform but by destination: on one side believers whose sword is lifted *for* justice-and-obedience; on the other, disbelievers — *wa alladhīna kafarū yuqātilūna fī sabīli al-ṭāghūt* — "and those who disbelieve fight in the way of false deities": *al-ṭāghūt* — the standing word of the overreaching rebel — from ṭ-gh-y — to transgress bounds (its standing file: 2:256 — "whoever disbelieves in the ṭāghūt and believes in God has grasped the firmest handhold"; 4:51-52 — the sūrah's own ṭāghūt-file just above; 4:60 — "they wish to take their disputes to the ṭāghūt"; 5:60; 16:36; 39:17) — glossed here by the standing traditions of the tafsīr-canon (al-Ṭabarī's standing collection) as **Satan himself**: the enemy's war-effort filed under the direct command-chain of Shayṭān — Idolatry's wars and rebellion's campaigns registered not as autonomous politics but as the Shayṭān-file in military dress.

**Fa-Qātilū Awliyāʾa al-Shayṭān: The Command Speaks Names**

Then the imperative with the alliance-register already established in the verse's own vocabulary: *fa-qātilū awliyāʾa al-Shayṭān* — "fight, therefore, against the allies of Satan": *awliyāʾ* — the walāyah-word again (the sūrah's standing protector-file: 4:45; 4:76; 4:89; 4:123's null-pair; 4:139; 4:144) — and the commentators mark the stark symmetry the verse thereby erects: the disbelievers are *awliyāʾ al-Shayṭān* — the allies of Satan — while the believers' standing register supplies its own counterpart: *Allāhu waliyyu alladhīna āmanū* — "God is the Protector of those who believe" (2:257; 3:68's "those most worthy of Abraham are those who followed him — and this Prophet and those who believe — and God is the Protector of the believers"; 4:45's own file; 5:55's standing triply-posted walāyah — "your Protector is but God and His Messenger and those who believe") — the battlefield therefore a war of *two protectorates*: every combatant fighting under a walī — the only question being *which* — the verse's anatomy of war thereby theological before it is military: whose banner, whose protection, whose scheme.

**Inna Kayda al-Shayṭāni Kāna Ḍaʿīfan: The Feebleness Prophecy of the Enemy's General**

The verse closes with the Book's most quoted military-theological assessment: *inna kayda al-Shayṭāni kāna ḍaʿīfan* — "surely the scheme of Satan is ever feeble": *kayd* — scheming, stratagem (its standing register: 3:120; 4:76; 8:18 — "and verily God weakens the scheme of the disbelievers"; 12:5's own Joseph-file; 21:57 — "I shall surely scheme against your idols"; 52:46 — "the day their scheme avails them naught"; 77:39; 86:15-16 — "verily they scheme a scheme — and I scheme a scheme") — and the attribute assigned the enemy's *whole* strategic apparatus: *ḍaʿīf* — weak, feeble — the commentators (al-Ṭabarī's standing gloss with the early registers) unfolding the feebleness in four standing causes: (1) motivational poverty — Satan's allies fight out of fear and jealousy of the believers, their armies' hearts divided between profit and preservation, while the believers fight for God with hope of Paradise and, if they shrink, with fear of divine reckoning — the moral quality of motive an actual battle-variable; (2) eschatological poverty — the disbeliever fights in doubt, fearing death as terminus, with no afterworld-reward to make courage rational; the believer battles toward a Hereafter whose harvest 4:74 has just priced ("whosoever fights in the way of God, whether he is slain or is victorious — We shall grant him a great reward"), so that calculation itself fights on the believing side; (3) ontological poverty — Satan's power is by nature *suggestion*, not coercion ("verily he has no authority over those who believe and trust in their Lord — his authority is only over those who take him as protector and associate partners with Him" — 16:99-100; its standing twin: 14:22 — the Satan-confession at the Judgment: "I had no authority over you, save that I called you and you answered" — and 15:42 — "verily over My servants thou hast no authority"); (4) providential poverty — God Himself occupies the counter-strategist post: the kayd of Satan is not merely intrinsically weak but *rendered* ineffectual — 8:18's standing formula; 3:120's standing "God is ever encompassing of what they do"; 86:15-16's counter-scheme. Al-Rāzī's standing synthesis makes the verse's assessment a standing law of morale: the side that knows its enemy's general is feeble fights a different war — fear defects to the other camp.

**Ibn ʿAjībah and the Inner Battlefield: The Ṣūfī File of the Verse**

The standing spiritual comment-canon (al-Sulamī's register; Ibn ʿAjībah's standing inward reading) accredits the verse its second, permanent battlefield: the soul at war with itself. In the jihad-of-the-nafs file the polarity reads identically: the true believer fights *with* God against the lower elements of his own soul — its appetite-legions filed under the awliyāʾ-of-Shayṭān register — while the heedless man fights as the *ally* of his own base passions against God's claim upon him. Ibn ʿAjībah's standing refinement reaches further than the ascetic minimum: fighting "in the way of God" in its perfect register means striving only to attain God's own Presence — not even the otherworldly wage of Paradise as terminal aim — for to strive for a wage, even a celestial one, is to trade within the economy of the self; it leaves one a believer (the wage of 4:74 is real, the promise keeps), but short of the rank of the awliyāʾ — those who strive for God alone. The inner application preserves the verse's comfort in its exactness: *the scheme of Satan is ever feeble* holds equally in the chamber of temptation as on the field of battle — the whispering retreater (al-khannās — 114:4) works by suggestion alone, and suggestion, met by remembrance, dissolves — "when a thought from Satan touches those who fear God, they remember — and behold, they see clearly" (7:201); "resist him — and lo, he flees from you" being the standing ethical summary the commentators draw from the whole kayd-dossier: for the feeble schemer's one non-feeble instrument is the human invitation — fight his allies, and the general himself has nothing left to field.
