## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:148

> **God loves not the open speaking of evil — save on behalf of one who has been wronged. And God is ever Hearing, Knowing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lā Yuḥibbu Allāhu al-Jahra bi-al-Sūʾi min al-Qawl: Speech as Moral Dignity's Frontier**

Immediately after the dossier's mercy-floodgate, the Book legislates the ethics of the tongue: *lā yuḥibbu Allāhu al-jahra bi-al-sūʾi min al-qawl illā man ẓulim* — "God loves not the open speaking of evil — save on behalf of one who has been wronged": *jahr* — from j-h-r — audible publicness (its standing register: 6:3 — "He knows the secret and the open"; 13:10 — "he who whispers speech and he who proclaims it"; 21:110 — "He knows what is proclaimed of speech and what you conceal"; 87:7) — the sin located not in the thought but in its *broadcast*: the Book granting that resentment, anger, even condemnation may arise in the chest, but forbidding the mouth's conversion of them into public currency. The commentators register the verse's twin-context: (1) its local occasion in the dossier — the repentance of the former hypocrites (v. 146): once returned, their past falseness is no longer open-season for public mention — according to al-Rāzī's contextual reading: the repentant are not to be vilified by reminders of their former hypocrisy — the community forbidden from keeping old files open on souls God has newly sealed into the believing register — the standing ethic that public memory must obey divine amnesty; (2) its universal scope — the standing principle of Islamic ethics that faults — others' and even one's own — are to be *concealed*, not merchandised: the tradition's standing hadith-cluster (Muslim's register 2990: "all my ummah shall be pardoned except those who sin openly — and among open sinning is that a man does a deed by night, God covers him, and in the morning he says: O so-and-so — last night I did such-and-such — his Lord having veiled him through the night, and by morning he removes God's own veil from himself") — the veil of God over the night-sinner being treated as a divine courtesy the dawn-confessor criminally declines: public evil-speech unwinding God's own covering. Exceptions the commentators carefully hold: the witness summoned in justice (v. 135's standing command — witnesses for God even against the self), the warning against genuine harm (the hadith-sciences' own public impeachment of unreliable transmitters), and the ruling-clause the verse itself immediately legislates.

**Illā Man Ẓulim: The Wronged One's Licensed Complaint**

The exception stands in a single compressed relative clause — *illā man ẓulim* — "save one who has been wronged": the verb passive — *he was wronged* — and the license belongs to the wronged as such: the victim may speak the evil *against* the wrongdoer — publicly accusing, complaining, redress-seeking — this publicness reclassified from slander to claim-of-right. Al-Ṭabarī chains the standing glosses: the wronged may invoke against the oppressor (the duʿāʾ of the maẓlūm being the tradition's most feared projectile — Bukhārī's standing register 2448: "fear the supplication of the wronged — for between it and God there is no veil"), may report him to authority, may publicize his conduct to obtain justice or protection — 42:41 standing as the Book's own parallel license: "whoever defends himself after being wronged — upon those there is no way." The jurists built the verse's standing legal compartments: complaint-filing, the petitioner's description of injuries, and the standing boundary that the license is measured by its purpose — redress, not relish: the wronged one who converts his license into a banquet of defamation overshoots the exceptional warrant (al-Qurṭubī's standing boundary-notes gathering the early cautions).

**The Abū Bakr Scene: The Angel, Satan, and the Gathering**

Al-Rāzī transmits the occasion-report that fixes the verse in lived memory: a man abused Abū Bakr in the Prophet's presence; Abū Bakr sat silent — once, twice — then began to answer; and the Prophet rose to leave. Abū Bakr followed him out asking: you sat while I was abused, yet rose when I and the Prophet's standing reply (its standing register in Abū Dāwūd 4896 and Aḥmad's Musnad): "an angel was answering on your behalf — but when you began to answer him back, Satan entered — and I do not sit in a gathering where Satan enters" — and the verse, the report concludes, descended: the subtlest of calibrations — the *silent* wronged one defended by heaven itself (the angel rebutting in his stead), the *answering* wronged one descending from angelic custody into the quarrel's own arena — permission undenied (the exception-clause granted) yet the higher path marked: concealment-with-dignity outranking licensed retaliation. Its standing Book-kin gathers the same gradation: 42:40-43 — "the recompense of an evil is an evil like it — but whoever pardons and makes amends — his wage falls upon God... and whoever is patient and forgives — verily that is of the firmness-of-purpose of affairs"; 23:96 — "repel evil with that which is better"; 13:22; 28:54 — "they shall be given their reward twice over for that they were patient, and they repel evil with good"; 41:34 — "repel with that which is better — and lo, the one between whom and you is enmity becomes as a bosom-friend" — the ethic of *supersession* standing as the sūrah's own moral sky above the license's floor.

**Wa Kāna Allāhu Samīʿan ʿAlīman: The Two Names That Settle Every Silence**

The verse seals with the two perceiver-Names — *wa kāna Allāhu samīʿan ʿalīman* — "and God is ever Hearing, Knowing": the Samīʿ as the wronged one's standing solicitor: whatever he does not broadcast, the Hearing One has already heard — silence therefore never a forfeiture of evidence; the broadcast withheld is not data lost but data *deposited* — and the ʿAlīm as the abuser's standing dossier: the open speaker of evil building a record in a court he forgets is sitting. The two Names thereby adjudicate both the command and the exception: you may conceal (He hears anyway), and you may complain (He knows it is true) — the ethics of the tongue in the sūrah of justice resting finally, like all its ethics, upon the standing doctrine that freedom of concealment is freedom's loan against an audit already complete (6:3; 21:110; 87:7's standing confirmations: concealed and proclaimed alike known). The hypocrisy-dossier of vv. 137–148 thereby closes where it always intended to: after the anatomy of those who *performed* religion before men, the Book legislates the community's own speech: no merchandising of faults, no archival cruelty toward the repentant, no banquet of scandal — save the cry of the wronged, which heaven itself hears before it reaches a human ear — the sūrah of the Watcher (4:1) proving to the end that its deepest laws are about who is doing the listening: God, always God — Samīʿan ʿAlīman.
