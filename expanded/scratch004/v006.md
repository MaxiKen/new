## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:6

> **And test the orphans until they reach the age of maturity; then if you perceive in them sound judgment, deliver unto them their property, and consume it not wastefully and in haste before they come of age. Whosoever is rich should abstain, but whosoever is poor should partake thereof in an honorable way. And when you deliver unto them their property, bring witnesses on their behalf, and God suffices as a Reckoner.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa-btalū: The Guardianship as Examination**

The verse's first verb sets the whole pedagogy of the trust-years: *wa-btalū al-yatāmā* — "and test the orphans": *ibtilāʾ*, testing, for once here a trial administered *by the guardian* to the ward. God's law does not permit the vault's key to pass on a birthday's dusk; the estate of the fatherless is released only upon *measured* competence. Al-Qurṭubī preserves the classroom of the salaf: the testing included advancing the young heir small sums of his own money and watching his commerce — whether he bought and sold with judgment, preserved his capital, paid his workers, kept his accounts. The verse thereby founded, centuries before its appearance in the codes of other nations, the transitional instrument of supervised financial apprenticeship: guardians had been told at v. 5 not to hand wealth to the incompetent; v. 6 now teaches them *how competence is manufactured* — by degrees, with the child inside the household's commerce long before the vault is his.

**Balughū al-Nikāḥ... al-Rushd: The Two Credentials of Release**

The two gates of release stand in sequence: maturity and rectitude. *Ḥattā idhā balaghū al-nikāh* — "until they reach marriage-age" — the idiom for puberty, set by the majority of the jurists between fifteen and eighteen years for males; and *fa-in ānastum minhum rushdan* — "then if you perceive in them sound judgment": *rushd*, the guardian's certification, defined by al-Ṭabarī's authorities as the compound of a functioning rational faculty, upright religion, and proven property-sense — the three of which a guardian has been observing at close quarters since the parent's grave. The schools sharpened the clause: the Mālikīs and Ḥanbalīs require both rushd in religion and rushd in wealth; the Ḥanafīs allow additional time — to twenty-five in Abū Ḥanīfah's ceiling — where financial ripeness lags the body's; the reports of Ibn ʿUmar capture the practice: his father ʿUmar was tested by the Prophet's own household-principle that the youth who shows judgment early may be handed his charge early. The law's architecture is merciful realism: puberty opens the *possibility* of release; rushd alone *commands* it — *fa-dfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum* — "then deliver to them their property."

**Wastefully and in Haste: The Guardian's Two Temptations**

Against the relapse into consumption, two adverbs seal the statute: *wa lā taʾkulūhā isrāfan wa bidāran an yakbarū* — "consume it not wastefully nor in haste, anticipating their coming of age." *Isrāf* — waste — and *bidār* — hurried squandering — each name a distinct sin of the trustee: the slow bleed of the careless administrator, and the sudden gutting of the one who hears the heir's majority approaching like a bailiff and spends the trust before the audit. Al-Qurṭubī's masters read the second with special poignancy: the verse anticipates the oldest maneuver of corrupt custodianship — the end-of-term raid — and forbids it in its own name. Between v. 2's *do not exchange* and v. 6's *do not waste and do not rush*, the Book closes every valve by which trusts leak into guardians' pockets before their owners' hands are strong enough to hold them.

**The Rich Abstains, the Poor Eats Honorably: The Wage of Care**

The guardians themselves are next admitted into the statute's justice: *man kāna ghaniyyan fa-l-yastaʿfif, wa man kāna faqīran fa-l-yaʾkul bi-l-maʿrūf* — "whoso is rich, let him abstain; whoso is poor, let him partake honorably." The rich guardian — abstinence as his honor-budge — keeps his hands wholly off the ward's estate, his service counted to God. The poor guardian's licensed share the commentators disciplined by two lights: Ibn ʿAbbās and the strict school — a loan repayable, food of necessity only; the majority of the jurists — a wage measured by his labor upon the estate, *bi-l-maʿrūf*: as the shepherd of another's flock drinks of the milk and rides no beast to its hurt, the administrator of property draws reason's fee. Al-Qurṭubī, answering the charge that v. 10's hellfire abolishes all consumption, distinguishes with the register of all the schools: dishonest consumption is the fire of the tenth verse; *maʿrūf* compensation earns the guardian's bread — the law that forbids embezzlement likewise refuses to make charity compulsory of the poor who serve orphans.

**Witnesses at the Hand-over, and the Reckoner Above**

The statute's closing machinery is procedural and theological in a single seal: *fa-idhā dafaʿtum ilayhim amwālahum fa-ashhidū ʿalayhim, wa kafā bi-Allāhi ḥasībā* — "and when you deliver to them their property, take witnesses over them; God suffices as Reckoner." The witnesses (two, per the courts' extending of the law of testimony) protect both parties forever after: the guardian against the grown heir's later charge of shortfall, the heir against the guardian's false claim of delivery — the ancestor of every inventory-and-receipt practice in Islamic probate. Then the verse lifts the room: *ḥasīb* — the Reckoner — the same root-word Lady Mary's mother used presenting her newborn ("God is my Reckoner," in the ḥadīth of the name-recital), the same audit-attribute sealing the borrowed-property verses: *custodies end in witnessed papers on earth, but the audit that cannot be bribed is kept Above*. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's saying, treasured by the commentators in this pericope, lays the guardian's working creed in one line: *"the orphan's property is fire, and the poor man's consumption from it is per God's measure"* — between the two clauses the orphan-law of Islam stands complete, and the Watcher of v. 1 adds at its gate His final title: Reckoner over the reckoners.
