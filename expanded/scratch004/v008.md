## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:8

> **And when kinsfolk, orphans, and the indigent are present at the division, make provision from it for them and speak unto them in an honorable way.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥaḍara al-Qismah: The Gallery at the Probate Table**

The Book moves from the fixed seats of v. 7 to the gallery of the excluded: *wa idhā ḥaḍara al-qismata ulū al-qurbā wa-l-yatāmā wa-l-masākīn* — "and when the near kin, the orphans, and the indigent are present at the division": the funeral division was a *social* event in Madinah as in every society since — the kin gathered, the neighbors watching, the poor attracted by the rumor of distribution — and the revealed law refuses to disperse that gallery with empty hands. *Ulū al-qurbā* here denotes the kin *outside* the ordained shares — the aunt, the cousin, the kin-disabled — for the named heirs have already found their seats in v. 7 and vv. 11–12; alongside them stand the two standing clients of the sūrah's whole economy: the orphans and the *masākīn*, the indigent, from the root of motionlessness — those whom want has stilled. The command: *fa-rzuqūhum minhu* — "make provision for them from it" — clothe the excluded from the same table at which the heirs now sit.

**Abrogation's Dispute: A Statute the Scholars Refused to Bury**

The commentators' debate over this clause is one of the elegant cases in the science of *naskh*, and al-Qurṭubī presides over it with his usual fullness. Three opinions came down from the early generations: (1) the verse is *binding* (muḥkam) — the very position of Ibn ʿAbbās, who the report says used to recite it and declare it never abrogated — a gift owed at every division, its measure at the heirs' discretion; (2) it was *abrogated* by the fixed shares of vv. 11–13 — the economy of distribution henceforth exhausted by the ordained seats; (3) it governs the *bequest-third* — the dying person's disposable third, from which the unseated may be remembered. Ibn Kathīr marshals the witnesses and inclines, with characteristic balance, to the first: the verse addresses the *living* division-ceremony as a standing courtesy-obligation, one the community often neglects — his own era already lamenting it — without its law-claim ever being formally lifted. The Shiʿi tradition through the Imams of the Prophet's house reads the same: al-Bāqir taking the *provision* as recommended in every case, greatest where the division is public. The lesson beneath the disagreement is permanent: whether as duty or as generosity, revelation insists that the room of distribution never void of kindness — the excluded watch God's portions dealt, and they are fed with the dealing itself.

**Qulan Maʿrūfan: Speech as the Second Alms**

The clause's twin-command is the verse's charm and its severest instrument: *wa qūlū lahum qawlan maʿrūfan* — "and speak to them an honorable word." For the Book knows the economy of funerals: the poor who linger around probate tables carry the *double* burden — want of the hand and lowliness of the eye — and a distribution that feeds no palms can still wound them at the tongue's edge. The maʿrūf-command, the same speech-standard as v. 5's to the feeble-minded, addresses the second wound *as law*, and al-Rāzī pauses on the structure: the verses' pairing of *provision* and *honorable speech* — material gift and verbal gift — instructs the house of Islam that charity corrupts into insult wherever its manner forgets its soul: the Book itself has already laid the principle across the entire economy of giving — "kind speech and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury" (2:263) — and the Prophet ﷺ extended it to the smallest coin of dignity:

> *"Do not belittle any good deed — even meeting your brother with a cheerful face"* and *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good or keep silent."* (Muslim 2626, 48; al-Bukhārī 6018 — from Abū Dharr and Abū Hurayrah)

At the probate table, the polite sentence is counted among the shares distributed.

**The Funeral-Table as a School of the Afterlife**

Why should a *law* reach so far into the etiquette of the death-chamber? Because, the commentators answer, the division-scene is *the one hour* in which the clan beholds the whole motion of wealth openly: the owner gone, the estate lying in the tray, the heirs approaching with their names. It is the hour of maximal greed — and therefore revelation's chosen classroom. By commanding provision-for-the-excluded and beautiful speech *at precisely that table*, God plants His remedies where the disease flowers, and models forever what the Prophet's house practiced: ʿĀʾishah's report of the Prophet's own dividing of what arrived — *"he would take a portion and say: give this to so-and-so"* — immediately accessible to those at the door. The verse closes its case gently, without a seal-clause — no Watcher, no Reckoner named this once — as though the Lord of the arḥām knew that the gallery at the division needs no threat: the orphans there are the very ones whose trusts the previous six verses have ringed with fire, and no reader of the sūrah's opening page will look at a poor child's face across the tray and need the tenth verse explained to him. Provide them from it; speak to them beautifully; the court is in session and the Reckoner is still in the room.
