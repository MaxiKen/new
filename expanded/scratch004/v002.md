## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:2

> **Give orphans their property, and exchange not the bad for the good, nor consume their property with your own. Truly that would be a great sin.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Uncle and the Orphan: The Case That Opened the Law**

The commentators of the occasions of revelation bring us into a Madinan courtyard: an orphan, come of age, demanded his inheritance from the uncle who had held it in trust; the uncle refused. The young man carried his complaint to the Messenger of God ﷺ, and heaven's answer descended — *ātu al-yatāmā amwālahum* — "give the orphans their property" (al-Wāḥidī; the report is preserved by al-Qurṭubī and underlies the whole pericope ff. in Ibn Kathīr's collection). The related case in al-Ṭabarī's transmission — a man of the tribe of Ghaṭafān who held a great sum belonging to his orphaned nephew and withheld it when the boy reached maturity — shows the temptation in its classic form: not naked theft but *prolonged custody*, the guardian's gradual inability to distinguish between what he administers and what he owns. Revelation's response cuts at exactly that confusion, which is why the imperative is *give* — *ātu* — and not "return": the property was the orphan's all along; the guardian's vault never altered the title. Custody is administration, not possession — the first axiom of the whole law of wards.

**Yatāmā: The Word of the Fatherless**

*Yatīm* (plural *yatāmā*) derives from a root of singleness and bereavement: in the usage of the Arabs and the law alike, the orphan is the child who has lost his father before reaching maturity — for the father was, in that world as in most worlds of provision, the roof of the household. The Book's own biography of the word reaches its tenderest point in the Prophet's ﷺ person: "Did He not find you an orphan and shelter you?" (93:6) — God reminding His Messenger of his own fatherless boyhood, and thereby consecrating orphan-care as an act performed upon the Prophet's own childhood in every later child. His ﷺ famous gesture, made with two fingers, fixed the station of the guardian for all generations:

> *"I and the one who sponsors an orphan will be in Paradise like these two" — and he raised his index and middle fingers together.* (al-Bukhārī 6005; Muslim 2983, from Sahl ibn Saʿd)

Proximity to Muhammad in Paradise measured by two adjoining fingers — such is the estate held out to the guardian who fulfills this verse.

**Exchange Not the Bad for the Good: The Embezzler's First Skill**

The first prohibition anatomizes the craft of subtle robbery: *wa lā tatabaddalū al-khabītha bi-l-ṭayyib* — "do not exchange the bad for the good." Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr define the classic operation: the guardian, distributing the orphan's estate, returns it in debased coin — the emaciated camel for the fat one, the moth-eaten cloth for the bolt of fine weave, the inferior land-parcel for the prime — valuing each substitution at the value of what it replaces, and calling the ledger square. The verse names the practice *tabdīl* — swapping — because its essence is not confiscation but substitution, robbery wearing the mask of accounting. A second reading, transmitted from the early exegetes, widens the clause: do not exchange the *bad practice* — consuming the orphan's wealth — for the *good practice* of preserving it. Both readings stand, for both are one sin seen from two sides: the debasing of a trust.

**Consume Not Their Property with Your Own: The Dangerous Illness of Mingling**

*Wa lā taʾkulū amwālahum ilā amwālikum* — "nor consume their property with your own": the preposition *ilā* (with/alongside) is, Ibn Kathīr points out, deployed in the sense of *ka* — *as* — as though the orphan's estate were a mere extension of the guardian's own table. The danger it names is the most realistic in all fiduciary law: not the dramatic embezzlement but the daily confusion — one purse, one pot, one account — out of which wrong invisibly grows. So searching was the command upon the first community that the Companions went to extremes of separation: al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī report that after this verse, guardians would keep the orphan's water apart from their water and his flour from their flour — until God relieved their hardship with the revealed concession of 2:220: "if you intermingle with them, they are your brothers" — communion permitted where *iṣlāḥ*, betterment, governs the household. The two verses together draw the permanent map: separation is the safe default; mixing is lawful only under proven good intent and scrupulous accounting, for "God knows the corrupter from the improver" (2:220's own protocol clause).

**Ḥūb Kabīr: The Naming of the Sin's Weight**

The verse's verdict — *innahū kāna ḥūban kabīrā* — "truly that is a great *ḥūb*" — selects one of the gravest nouns in the Book's lexicon of guilt. *Ḥawb* denotes a sin of crushing weight — al-Qurṭubī glosses it as the mighty transgression that burdens its bearer until it bears him down — and its pairing with *kabīr* (great) heaps emphasis upon emphasis: this is no peccadillo of accounting but a destroyer. The Prophet ﷺ enrolled the deed in his census of the fatal seven:

> *"Avoid the seven destroyers (al-mūbiqāt): ascribing partners to God; sorcery; killing the soul God has sanctified except by right; consuming usury; consuming the property of the orphan; fleeing on the day of battle; and slandering chaste, heedless, believing women."* (al-Bukhārī 2766; Muslim 89, from Abū Hurayrah)

Between idolatry and murder sits the embezzlement of a child — the Lord of the orphan counts His ledger in the company of the gravest crimes. The sūrah will deepen the sentence at its tenth verse into the fires of the belly; and the Muslim of every age inherits the warrant plainly: the wealth of the fatherless is guarded from the strong by a Guard stronger than them all, and the two fingers raised beside the Prophet in Paradise await only those who gave *all*, on time, untouched.
