## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:74

> **Let them fight in the way of God, those who would sell the life of this world for the Hereafter. And whosoever fights in the way of God — whether he is slain or victorious — We shall grant him a great reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-l-Yuqātil fī Sabīli Allāh: The Counter-Muster After the Tarriers**

Immediately after the twin-portrait of absence (vv. 72–73: the man who blessed God over the Muslims' wounds and grew wretched at their spoils), the sūrah issues its counter-call: *fa-l-yuqātil fī sabīli Allāhi alladhīna yashrūna al-ḥayāta al-dunyā bi-l-ākhirah* — "let them fight in the way of God — those who sell the life of this world for the Hereafter": *fa-l-yuqātil* — let them fight, the imperative-lām of commission; *fī sabīli Allāh* — *in the way of God* — the battle-clause the Book forever pins to legitimacy (the commentators' standing law-note, al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī concurring: the way of God is the cause God appointed — the defense of His religion, its upholding, and the call to it — never pride, never clan-glory, never plunder: aim governs act, so that an identical combat severed from this intention loses the sabīl-qualification and reverts to *fī sabīli al-ṭāghūt* per v. 76 immediately below) — and *alladhīna yashrūna* — *those who sell* — the mercantile lexicon the Book reserves for its most radical exchanges (from sh-r-y: to sell and in some registers to purchase, the same market-voice heard at 9:111's celebrated purchase-verse: "God has purchased from the believers their souls and their wealth — for that the Garden is theirs — they fight in the way of God, so they slay and are slain — a promise binding upon Him in the Torah, the Gospel, and the Quran") — war recast as a *transaction*: the worldly life offered as the sale-price, the heart liquidating everything perishable, with the counter-price everlasting — al-ākhirah — al-Qurṛṭubī's note observing that the two sides of the exchange have no ratio in any finance: the perishable swapped for the Imperishable, a trade conceivable only because the divine Purchaser is generous past arithmetic — the traders' own ledger-clause pinned beside it in the most-cited jihād-hadith of the Sahīh corpus:

> *"Standing guard at the frontier in the way of God for a single day is better than the world and all that is in it."* (al-Bukhārī 2892; Muslim 1880)

— one day of battlefield vigil outweighing the whole commodity of earth.

**Fa-man Yuqātil fī Sabīli Allāh: Win or Die — Both Are Wages**

Then follows the soldier's guarantee — the clause armies of believers have recited before battle release: *fa-man yuqātil fī sabīli Allāhi fa-yuqtal aw yaghlib fa-sawfa nuʾtīhi ajran ʿaẓīmā* — "whoever fights in the way of God — then is slain, or prevails — We shall grant him a mighty reward": the grammar lists both battle-outcomes, no third arm needed — *yuqtal*, he is killed — *aw yaghlib*, or he is victorious — Heaven pronouncing both gain: if he falls, shahādah and its wages; if he prevails, victory and its wages — no battlefield exit goes unpriced; *fa-sawfa nuʾtīhi* — We shall surely give him, the deferred-certain of majesty — *ajran ʿaẓīmā* — a majestic reward — the ʿaẓīm-noun of vv. 40 and 67's promised payments returning as the regular currency of God's own contracts. And over it the tradition suspends the standing qualification — the hadith that has defined "in the way of God" for every campaign of Islamic history, the Prophet's own sieve upon intentions when the asker named the warrior-cases (al-Bukhārī 2810; Muslim 1904 — narrated by Abū Mūsā):

> *"A man said: Messenger of God! One man fights out of bravery, one out of zeal, and one to be seen — which of these is in the way of God? He said: Whoever fights so that the word of God may be the highest — his is the way of God."*

— sincerity of aim being the full enlistment-paper, and the classification of every other combat instantly following: whatever fights for the name of tribe, glory, or worldly gain — lies outside the sabīl.

**The Two Economies Side by Side**

Set against vv. 72–73, the clause is the pericope's great divide (the commentators sensing it as one of the Book's starkest either-ors): the tarrier's economy — abstain; then whether your side bleeds or feasts, you measure your private ledger alone; the seller's economy — enlist; then *either* ledger prints *ajran ʿaẓīmā* — two outcomes, one profit-class: battle robbed by God of its risk-asymmetry — death versus plunder as the world counts it — dissolved into the heavenly arithmetic where death is the *larger* half of success. The sages' summary fixes the verse's historical substance: *Islam needed an army unbuyable by spoils and undissuadable by graves — this clause recruited it*; and the Prophet's own heart gave the doctrine its most human expression, in the famous wish he disclosed at the departure of a detachment (al-Bukhārī 36 and 2797; Muslim 1876–1878):

> *"Were it not that it would grieve some of my ummah, I would never lag behind any detachment; but I have not the mounts to give them, they have naught to ride, and it grieves them to stay behind when I set out. By Him in Whose hand is my soul, I wish I were killed in the way of God, then revived, then killed, then revived, then killed again."*

— the Messenger himself craving the very outcome his soldiers feared, the merchant having long ago settled the price: no ledger-fright stands between the seller and the field — and the reader of every later age inherits the same both-ways wages, slain or victorious, great reward already written, God's own hand upon the contract.
