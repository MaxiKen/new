## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:34

> **Men are the upholders and maintainers of women by virtue of that in which God has favored some of them above others and by virtue of their spending from their wealth. Therefore the righteous women are devoutly obedient, guarding in [their husbands'] absence what God has guarded. As for those from whom you fear discord and animosity, admonish them, then leave them in their beds, then strike them. Then if they obey you, seek not a way against them. Truly God is Exalted, Great.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qawwāmūn: The Leader-Steward of the Household**

The most debated sentence in the sūrah begins with the Book's own loaded participle: *al-rijālu qawwāmūna ʿalā al-nisāʾ* — "men are *qawwāmūn* over women": *qawwām*, the intensive of *qāma ʿalā* — standing over, standing *for* — the one who stands guard, administers, and above all *stands the cost*: the standing-provider, standing-guardian, standing-bearer of the family's affairs. The whole classical tradition from al-Ṭabarī through al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr reads the word at the *household* level — the man as the accountable head of his family — with the two grounds the verse itself immediately pledges: *bi-mā faḍḍala Allāhu baʿḍahum ʿalā baʿḍin wa bi-mā anfaqū min amwālihim* — "because of what God has favored some of them over others with, and because they spend of their wealth." The two grounds divide precisely: the *first favor* — the greater estate assigned men in the inheritance-architecture of vv. 11–12 — and the *spending* — the mahr of v. 4, the nafaqah-command of house, food, and clothing — al-Qurṭubī's jurist's-eye fixing the doctrine in the sentence most quoted from his commentary: the qawwāmūnah *is staked to performance* — he who withholds provision has no standing over her (the Shāfiʿīs and Mālikīs binding authority to maintenance the same) — so that the verse reads, by its own syntax, not as a coronation but as *an employment-contract under God*: leadership *because of responsibility*; and the title's much-quoted danger dissolves when the first pillar of the favor-word is genuinely read as *provision*, not person. The learned modern redactions — from the salaf-mindful — therefore never lose the household *function* in the in the now-present debate: the Book made the man*answerable* *for*, not merely *master of*, and tied his claim exactly to his spending-string of law.

**Qānitāt Ḥāfiẓāt: The Righteous Wife Under the Verse**

The reciprocal virtue runs parallel: *fa-l-ṣāliḥātu qānitātun ḥāfiẓātun li-l-ghaybi bi-mā ḥafiẓa Allāh* — "the righteous women are devout-ones, guarding the hidden [in their husbands' absence] by God's guarding": *qānitāt*, standing-devoted — whose every Qur'anic use otherwise points at divine devotion (2:238; 66:12 — Mary of ʿImrān its queen) — the majority of the tradition taking it here as devotion to God flowing into household-faithfulness (Ibn Kathīr's middle-way: *devout to God, therefore dutiful in the house*), and *ḥāfiẓāt li-l-ghayb* — guarding the unseen: the Prophet's own dictum defining the wife who pleased him:

> *"The best of women is she who, when you look at her, pleases you; when you command her, obeys you; and when you are absent, guards you in herself and your property."* (al-Nasāʾī among the Sunan, from Ibn ʿUmar)

— *ḥāfiẓah* defined by the Prophet himself as the keeper of *nafs and māl* — chastity and stewardship in the husband's absence, the exact two treasuries the qawwām bears in presence. The couplet of roles is thus not master and servant but the two-sided guardianship of a house — his outward accountability for provision and protection, her inward guardianship of home, honor, and and assets: each a *ḥāfiẓ* / *qawwām* of the side the other cannot reach — and its aim not hierarchy for its own sake but the tranquility the Book names *sakīnah* at 30:21: *garments to each other* (2:187), *mawaddah and raḥmah* between them.

**Nushūz: The Crisis-Protocol, Fenced at Every Door**

Then the verse's storm-clause: *wa-llātī takhāfūna nushūzahunna fa-ʿiẓūhunna wa-hjurūhunna fī al-maḍājiʿi wa-ḍribūhunna* — "those from whom you fear nushūz: admonish them, then leave them in beds, then strike them." *Nushūz* — rising-up, from *nashaza*, the raised ground — is the state of *declared, ongoing defiance and contempt*: the wife's flagrant rising against the legitimate structure — al-Ṭabarī and the authorities distinguishing it from momentary strife and from any dislike (v. 19 covers mere dislike by *patience*), and the tradition underlining its *relational*, not merely legal, register in 4:128-130's reciprocals where the husband's nushūz opens the wife's own remedies — the protocol's rungs then ascending by obligation-made-mercy: (1) *ʿiẓūhunna* — admonish: counsel and reminder — words, only words, first; (2) *wa-hjurūhunna fī al-maḍājiʿi* — withdrawal *in the beds*: the bed-fellowship's suspension, *inside the house* (the Prophet's own instruction marking its limit: "not leaving the not abandoning her save in the house" — Sunan-reporting's discipline in Abū Dāwūd 2142 at the ḥajj-sermon's series, *"clothe and feed them, strike not the face, do not revile, and abandon not save in the house"*), time and pride both given room to melt; (3) *wa-ḍribūhunna* — and strike: the third rung hedged by the whole law-tradition in hands the in the hedges the tradition never tired of citing: *ghayr mubarriḥ* — non-injurious (the ḥadīth-standard in Muslim 1218's farewell-sermon register); *ghayr muʾaththir* — mark-less (the gloss of the early police of every school); not the face (Abū Dāwūd 2142 again — the face singled out absolutely); al-Shāfiʿī's own maxim counting the step as *makrūh*, near-forbidden by distaste; Ibn ʿAbbās's antique image of the twig-*siwāk* — the token by which severity could never exceed a signal; and the Prophet's own body, the ummah's walking tafsīr — "the best of you will not strike" (Abū Dāwūd 2149's wording), and never in his life did he hit wife or servant (Muslim 2328, ʿĀʾishah's own report). The classical record is unanimous — the tradition never licensed violence: it *narrowed* a violence-ridden world to a no-pain tap, fenced first by sermons and silences and the Prophet's perfect absence of the act.

**Occasion, Wisdom, and the Clause That Closes the Door**

The verse's own occasion anchors this reading in the Prophet's particular mercy: the reports (al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī's chains, Ibn Kathīr with the fullest choreography) remember the woman of the Ansār — Saʿd ibn al-Rabīʿ's wife, struck by her husband — coming with her people to the Prophet ﷺ; his first instinct was *qiṣāṣ* — retaliation against the husband — and God's verse stayed him: *the Prophet said, "I desired something, and God desired something else, and what God desires is best."* — himself then placing the three-rung protocol, in which the lawyers see the law's own medicine: *remedy, not vengeance* — each rung designed to *stop* before the next (bed-separation re-heals; the tap signals; reconciliation follows) and the whole section aimed at *avoiding divorce* (v. 35's arbitration-system arriving immediately as the institutional pair). The seal then bars the house-door itself: *fa-in aṭaʿnakum fa-lā tabghū ʿalayhinna sabīlan* — "if they obey you, seek no way against them": no resenting-tactics, no reopened files, no accountancy of old wrongs — with the eternal with the attribute-seal *inna Allāha kāna ʿaliyyan kabīrā* — "God is Exalted, Great" — warning the towering husband that his station itself stands beneath a Station higher and greater: strength shown to the weaker is measured by the Greatest of all; and the qawwām's and the qawwām bears his office under that exalted review. The Muslim household read beneath this verse is finally and always a *reconciliation-architecture*: its hinges counsel, withdrawal, and the feigned strike; its summit the arbiters of v. 35; its seal the Knowledge of the Exalted — with mercy laid at every rung, and the Book set over all: *the prophets' house, the swordless house, the listening house.*
