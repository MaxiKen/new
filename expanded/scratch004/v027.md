## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:27

> **God desires to relent unto you, but those who follow lusts desire that you go tremendously astray.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yurīdu Allāhu an Yatūba: The Will of God Above the Wills of Men**

The verse plants the two contesting wills side by side — *yurīdu Allāhu an yatūba ʿalaykum, wa yurīdu alladhīna yattabiʿūna al-shahawāti an tamīlū maylan ʿaẓīmā* — "God wills to relent toward you, while those who follow passions will that you incline with tremendous inclination" — and the juxtaposition is the whole conflict-file of revelation: above every soul hover two designs, one from the Lord building the return-road toward Him (tawbah as His willed gift, repeated from v. 26's third lamp in emphatic foreground now), the other from the passion-merchants angling to *tilt* the soul (mīl, from the weighing-scale's lean) — not merely an inch into sin but *maylan ʿaẓīman*, a great career-deviation. Al-Ṭabarī and the early school named the tilting-party from the occasions: sections of the People of the Book, whose own scriptures showed tolerances the new law now abjured, pressing the Madinans to take the easier marriage-contracts and slackened morality-roads — whether the verse's arm reaches exactly them (the common reading) or the whole species of corrupters whose trade is comfort-sin licensed (the majority's wider take: al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī) — the lesson stands twofold: (1) religious relaxations pressed with fervor are rarely neutral — each has a *design* behind it; (2) God's design answers, and His is *acceptance of the repenting*: God desires your *return*, not your ruin.

**Yatūb: The God Who Turns — Before You Do**

The verb the sūrah assigns God is from the same root as the servant's repentance — *tāba / yatūb* — but turned: God *turns-toward the turning*, and in the Arabs' great philosophical coincidence the two verbs mirror: man returns to God; God returns *to* man — the Holy One also "repenting," in the only sense that honors Him: mercy coming out to meet the approacher. The Prophet's report on this verse's theme — that God stretches out His hand through the night and the day for the day-sinner and the night-sinner — runs beneath it (Muslim 2759, Abū Mūsā's standing hadith, as at v. 17-18): the readiness precedes the repentant, which is precisely the difference from *the other will* — desire-partisans' welcome comes *after* you tilt, to keep you tilted. Your allies shape your direction, Ibn Kathīr's commentators return constantly: the first will-embraced is usually the will owned — and the verse puts both hands before you at the same signage: *God's: turn; theirs: tilt.*

**Maylan ʿAẓīman: No Small Tilt in the Desires-Plan**

The measuring-phrase deserves its exact reading: not merely that men "sin" but that the tempter's industry pushes *to a great inclining* — *a catastrophic career of the soul*, the commentators glossing the ʿaẓīm along a spectrum from the technically theological (deviation into disbelief itself, by the road of licensed sins) to the morally social: licensed looseness never stops at Zulaykhā's porch; looseness granted becomes looseness organized — until marriage-maps crumble, pedigrees wobble, and the orphan-files of vv. 2–10 are re-opened by the back way: everything the sūrah legislated against expands through the passion-road. That is why the Book's wariness of loose license is structural, and seen across the whole chapter: God fortifies *the law's locks* (prohibited degrees, dower-seatings, shares) while the tempters market *the keys* — and one more theological subtlety the schools noted: God's "will" here (*yurīdu*) is His prescriptive will — His command and His love of obedience-repentance — not necessitating: man remains addressed, fearing, choosing — lest anyone read the desire-clauses as fate.

**The Contest of the Two Intendants at Every Door**

The believer's daily spiritual intelligence derives directly from this verse: before each open doorway of ease-and-license there stand invisibly *two Intendants* — one who desires your relenting, another who desires your tilt — and the religious life consists substantially in reading which attendant holds which invitation. The followers of desires may be flesh-people — seducers, corrupters, profiteers of laxity — or market-ideas, fashions, cutting-edge permissions, "everyone does," "times have changed" — but their will is the same: *maylan ʿaẓīman*, always the farther-than-you-meant, never the small drop. The Muslim's own position under v. 27 is therefore surveillance over his own concessions: at each relaxation, ask the verse's question: *whose desire is this?* — if it inclines massively, its author is not God. Thus the sūrah's mercy-verses (Ibn ʿAbbās's eight) protect the *sinner-penitent* from despair of God's door — and this companion-verse protects *the waverer-tempter's prey* from entry into sin in the first place, the scheme uncovered at the threshold and named *by desire*.
