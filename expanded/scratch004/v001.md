## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:1

> **O mankind! Reverence your Lord, Who created you from a single soul and from it created its mate, and from the two has spread abroad a multitude of men and women. Reverence God, through Whom you demand your rights of one another, and family relations. Truly God is a Watcher over you.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yā Ayyuhā al-Nās: The Address That Enfolds Every Human Ear**

The sūrah of legislation par excellence opens not with *"O you who believe"* — the address that will recur nearly a dozen times within its own pages — but with the widest summons in the Book's repertoire: *yā ayyuhā al-nās* — "O mankind." The distinction is the first lesson of the chapter's jurisprudence: the laws about to descend upon women and orphans, spouses and heirs, guardians and wards, are not tribal customs binding one polity but ordinances addressed to the entire human family — for their foundation, laid in this very verse, is the kinship of all flesh. Ibn Kathīr notes that this opening returns the Madinan community — and every community after it — to the primordial fact: before there were Muslims and non-Muslims, there was one soul and its mate, and the multitude scattered from them both. The legislation of the family must therefore begin with the origin of the family; God legislates for households as their Creator, not as a foreign lawgiver.

**Ittaqū: The Shield-Verb at the Head of the Law**

The first imperative of the sūrah is *ittaqū rabbakum* — "reverence your Lord" — from the root *w-q-y*, to guard, to shield: taqwā is the heart's buckler against the displeasure of its Lord, raised through obedience and maintained through vigilance. That the longest body of social legislation in the Qur'an should be prefaced by taqwā is no ornamental juxtaposition: al-Rāzī draws the connection plainly — law without God-consciousness degenerates into technique, manipulated by the clever against the weak; the orphan's rights, the widow's share, the wife's bridewealth are defended in the last instance not by courts but by the watcher-consciousness named at the verse's close. Ibn Taymiyyah's celebrated definition gathers the thread: *taqwā is acting in obedience to God, upon light from God, hoping for God's mercy; and abandoning disobedience to God, upon light from God, fearing God's punishment.* The light comes first in both clauses — for the sūrah that follows *is* that light, statute by statute.

The community's worship enshrined this very verse in the canonical sermon of need (*khuṭbat al-ḥājah*) — the Prophet ﷺ taught ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd the opening with which he commenced every address, and the jurists fixed it as the introduction to the marriage sermon:

> *"Truly the best speech is the Book of God... O mankind! Reverence your Lord, Who created you from a single soul... O you who believe! Reverence God as He deserves to be reverenced... O you who believe! Reverence God, and speak a word directed to the right..."* (Abū Dāwūd 2118; al-Tirmidhī 1105; al-Nasāʾī 3279)

Three taqwā-verses — 4:1, 3:102, and 33:70–71 — open every wedding in Islam to this day: the sūrah of the family, the sūrah's first word, spoken first over the family's founding.

**Nafs Wāḥidah: The Single Soul and the Grammar of Reciprocity**

*Khalaqakum min nafsin wāḥidatin* — "created you from a single soul" — is the chapter's founding anthropology, affirmed across the Book: "He it is Who produced you from a single soul" (6:98); "It is He Who created you from a single soul, and made from it its mate, that he might find comfort in her" (7:189); "your creation and your resurrection are only as a single soul" (31:28). The single soul is Adam, upon him be peace; *minhā khalaqa zawjahā* — "from it He created its mate" — and the commentators of every century have inquired into the *min*: from it, or of its kind? The transmitted report of the rib — "treat women kindly, for woman was created from a rib; and the most curved part of the rib is its topmost part" (al-Bukhārī 3331; Muslim 1468) — underwrote the Genesis-echoing reading favored by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī: made from Adam, for Adam. Al-Rāzī, reasoning from the Arabic *min* of kind (as in "mates from among yourselves," 30:21), argues the woman's creation *of the same nature* — and the two readings shelter beneath one canopy: same substance, same dignity, no second clay. The Shiʿi tradition transmits from Imam Muḥammad al-Bāqir that she was formed of Adam's own superior clay. And the verse's own grammar gossips its secret to the attentive: *nafs* is feminine, *zawj* masculine — soul and mate trading grammatical gender in the single sentence — which the rhetoricians, and al-Zamakhsharī among them, took as the text's own signet of reciprocity: each sex carries the other's form within the language itself, as 2:187 declares of the spouses, "they are a garment for you, and you are a garment for them."

**Baththa: The Scattering That Became the Nations**

*Wa baththa minhumā rijālan kathīran wa nisāʾ* — "and from the two He spread abroad a multitude of men and women": the verb *baththa* is the scattering of seed into prepared earth, deployed here for the human sowing that populated the earth. From two — to "a multitude," uncounted and uncountable — runs the genealogy of every reader; the verse quietly razes every aristocracy of blood: all lineages terminate at one pair, so *boasting of fathers is boasting of nothing* — the very lesson the Prophet ﷺ pressed at the Farewell Sermon's plum-line: "No Arab has superiority over a non-Arab... all of you are from Adam, and Adam is from dust." Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī both detect in the clause the sūrah's abiding social command: universal siblinghood is not a metaphor but a pedigree — the orphan of v. 2 is your brother's child; the widow of v. 3 is your father's daughter. Kin is not sentiment; it is fact.

**Tasāʾalūna Bihi wa-l-Arḥām: The Oath and the Womb**

The verse's second taqwā-command is aimed at two objects joined in one breath: *wa-ttaqu Allāha alladhī tasāʾalūna bihi wa-l-arḥām* — "reverence God, through Whom you demand of one another, and the wombs." The Arabs of paganism swore their claims *by God and by kinship*; revelation did not strike the custom but re-consecrated it: the God invoked between claimants is watching the claim, and the kinship invoked is a trust with rights of its own. *Raḥim* — womb — shares its root with *raḥmah*, mercy, and with the Divine Name *al-Raḥmān*, the Compassionate — the Book's etymology made audible in the ḥadīth qudsī the community has never ceased repeating:

> *"God created creation, and when He had finished, the womb (al-raḥim) rose and took hold of the waist of the Compassionate. He said: What is it? It said: This is the station of one who seeks refuge with You against severance. He said: Are you not content that I should connect whoever connects you, and sever whoever severs you? It said: I am content, my Lord."* (al-Bukhārī 5988; Muslim 2554, from Abū Hurayrah)

The womb's own name is thus a fragment of the Name of the Compassionate; to sever it is to tug at what hangs from His Throne. This is why the sūrah of women and orphans opens with arḥām: every statute that follows — the shares, the gifts, the guardianships — is an application of *do not sever.*

**Raqīb: The Watcher Above Every Court**

The seal — *inna Allāha kāna ʿalaykum raqībā* — "truly God is ever a Watcher over you" — supplies the sūrah its great enforcement clause. *Raqīb* (root *r-q-b*, the stationed lookout) is the sentinel who neither blinks nor is bribed: over the guardian tempted by the orphan's estate, over the husband weighing equity, over the executor dividing shares, over the judge and the witness — the Book posts, before any human inspector, the divine Inspection itself. Al-Qurṭubī remarks that the chapter's threats to embezzlers and its commands to judges all budget with this attribute; and the believer reads the seal the way the chapter intends: the Lawgiver who teaches the law in the assembly is the Watcher who keeps its ledger in the privacy of every heart. Law opened by kinship is closed by surveillance — not of men, whose watching fails, but of the Lord who made us of one soul, and watches what we do one with another.
