## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:64

> **We sent no messenger, save that he should be obeyed by God's Leave. If, when they had wronged themselves, they had but come to thee and sought forgiveness of God, and the Messenger had sought forgiveness for them, they would surely have found God Relenting, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa Mā Arsalnā min Rasūlin illā Li-Yuṭāʿa bi-Idhni Allāh: Prophets Sent for Obedience Alone**

The pericope now plants the warrant beneath the whole tribunal-obligation: *wa mā arsalnā min rasūlin illā li-yuṭāʿa bi-idhni Allāh* — "We sent no messenger except to be obeyed, by God's leave": the absolute *mā... illā* — *none... except* — universalizing the commission across the prophetic corps (the commentators' broad note: from the earliest warner to the Seal — each one dispatched *for obedience*, his authority never private property but God's franchise — *bi-idhni Allāh*, by God's leave) — and the two classical readings the kalām-masters developed on the clause are both permanently indexed to our verse: (1) the Muʿtazilite emphasis — God *wills the messengers be obeyed by all*; none is ever misguided *by divine intent against them* — the verse proving heaven's goodwill toward every audience (their reading setting it alongside the hadith-famous their distinctive defense of human choice); and (2) the Ashʿarite reading — al-Rāzī's own celebrated use: *bi-idhni Allāh* — by God's leave — is the operative governor — obedience and disobedience alike occur within His encompassing decree, none slipping past His idhn — so the very warrant itself equals proof of the Sovereignty-of-leave (the school fight here being over who authors the *walking*, never over whose command the messengers carry — both houses agreeing on the latter absolutely) — the pastoral yield of the clause outlasting the school-grammar: in every age God's appointed guidance is *obediently receivable*; the bypass of the Prophet's court (v. 60's ṭāghūt-litigants) thereby stands measured against the whole architecture: to skirt *this* Messenger is to skirt the universal covenant of messenger-ship itself.

**Wa Law Annahum Idh Ẓalamū Anfusahum: The Counterfactual of the Door Left Open**

Then comes the verse that the entire tradition has treasured as the charter of prophetic intercession for sinners: *wa law annahum idh ẓalamū anfusahum jāʾūka fa-istaghfarū Allāha wa-staghfara lahumu al-rasūlu la-wajadū Allāha tawwāban raḥīman* — "and if, when they wronged themselves, they had but come to thee, sought God's forgiveness, and the Messenger sought forgiveness for them — they would have found God Relenting, Merciful": the *law* — *had they...* — a counterfactual of yearning (the same tender subjunctive-conditional the Book uses when holding open roads not taken: the door not closed even to the ṭāghūt-litigants — had they come, forgiveness *would have* issued — ṭab heaven lamenting the missed appointment, not celebrating their loss) — and the triple choreography of the scene: the wrongers *come to the Prophet* (admission of guilt at the living court); the wrongers *seek God's forgiveness* (their own istighfār — direct address to God); and then *the Messenger seeks forgiveness for them* (his ku his intercession completing the double-appeal of the weak party by himself and by the sanctioned patron) — find with the verdict certain: *la-wajadū* — they would surely have found — tawwāb, the Ever-Returner — raḥīm, Ever-Merciful. The doctrine sequenced here is the ummah's standing istighfār-triangle: person to Prophet, person to God, Prophet for person — with the reverse-side gospel preserved in its sister-cases the commentators always line up beside it (9:80 — "seek forgiveness for them or seek it not — if you seek seventy times God will not forgive them"; 63:6 — "it is alike for them whether you seek forgiveness or not"; 9:113 — "it is not for the Prophet and the believers to seek forgiveness for the polytheists, even if near of kin" — the intercession conditional upon the *repentant approach* itself, never a limitless blank check) — so that al-Rāzī's capsule stands: *the Prophet's prayers open only the doors the runners themselves run to.*

**The Station at His Grave and the Verse's Living Afterlife**

Upon our verse stands one of the most cherished devotional practices and theological conversations of Islam at once. The famous accounts in the transmitted registers (the maghāzī and āthār channels, with Ibn Kathīr's citation through Ibn ʿAsākir's chains) preserve the community's memory of this verse at work: the well-known story-report, transmitted through Mālik al-Dār's anecdote, tells of a needy man coming to the Prophet's grave in Madinah and praying the clause's own formula: "My Lord, I have wronged myself; You said: *if they came to thee and sought forgiveness...* — so I have come to Thy Prophet" — and receiving the answer in a dream: *forgiven*. The report's chains have been graded and criticized in the technical books, yet it is beloved of the devotional community, serving ever since as the verse's living pronunciation-guide: the dead Prophet in the believers' heart never exited his intercessory office (its law underwritten by the authentic the authentic proclamation of the Prophet's ongoing life in his grave — Muslim's reports of the prophets greeting each other alive in the night-journey, Muslim 237, and Bukhārī's multiple prophetic-life attestations) — with the scholarly boundary-lines the doctors guarded at equal strength: the verse's *coming* remains the sinner's own motion, its *istighfār* his own lips, its Messenger-intercession God's own gift of agency — not magical proximity but covenant fidelity — tawwāban raḥīman sealing the door as always-open *only to the seeker* — and the last word the Book leaves lying at the perjurer's feet being precisely this verse's kindness: *had they but come... they would have found* — the pericope's oath-theater answered not by a stake but by a road-sign — a road that every reader this day may still walk.
