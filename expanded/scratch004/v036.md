## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:36

> **Worship God, and ascribe not partners unto Him. And be virtuous toward parents and kinsfolk, toward orphans and the indigent, toward the neighbor who is of kin and the neighbor who is not of kin, toward the companion at your side and the traveler, and toward those whom your right hands possess. Truly God loves not one who is a vainglorious boaster,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa-bʿudū Allāha wa Lā Tushrikū Bihi Shayʾan: The Vertical Axis First**

After the household-architecture, the sūrah plants its creed-pillar mid-l mid-law with the grand * with the two commands that govern every clause that follows: *waʿbudū Allāha wa lā tushrikū bihi shayʾan* — "worship God, and ascribe nothing as partner to Him": *ʿibādah*, total devoted service — from *ʿabd*, servant, the self-surrender that is worship's root — and the absolute *shayʾan*, *a thing* — nothing, however small: no icon, no saint-share, no self-share, no star. The commentators note the verse's placement: it is the sūrah's central axis (the ~72nd verse of 176), and, and the two half-surahs of law turn upon it — for without tawḥīd all the orphan-statutes and justice-commands would be *ethics*; with it they are *worship*. Al-Rāzī draws the structural the thread: what God commands next (iḥsān through seven human classes) flows *from* worship of Him, for service to people is here declared the immediate exterior of service to God — the second clause obeyed *because* of the first, the whole Book resolving its law into the two imperatives of 4:36 — worship the Creator; beautify conduct to the created — the same twin-pillars the Prophet's own compressed: *"the best of you is the best to his family"* extended at last to all mankind.

**Wa bi-l-Wālidayni Iḥsānan: Immediately After God, the Parents**

The first addressees of the iḥsān-command are always the parents — *wa bi-l-wālidayni iḥsānan* — and the Book's architecture of the clause is itself an unbroken sermon assembled from its five parallel planks (2:83; 6:151; 17:23–24; 31:14; 46:15): *right after the shirk-prohibition, the parents* — no other creature ever placed between these two clauses, not prophets, not angels. Ibn Kathīr's commentary at this verse's sister (17:23) carries the tradition's deepest note, the celebrated report of Ibn Masʿūd:

> *"I asked the Prophet ﷺ: which deed is most beloved to God? He said: prayer in its time. I said: then which? He said: goodness to parents (birr al-wālidayn). I said: then which? He said: striving in the way of God. He (Ibn Masʿūd) said: these he told me, and had I asked more, he would have given more."* (al-Bukhārī 527; Muslim 85)

— *iḥsān*, from *ḥusn*, beauty — naming thereby not mere non-harm but *beautiful conduct*: gentled speech, lightening of their affairs, patience with their age, and remembrance of their dead with prayer and release of their debts — all this the Book then binds with half a with the List it now spreads around the compass — *kinsfolk; orphans; the indigent (masākīn, the stilled-by-want); the near neighbor; the distant neighbor; the companion beside; the wayfarer; and the owned*: a concentric outpouring of charity from the nearest blood to the uttermost dependent — so that no human being on earth falls outside its arc.

**The Two Neighbors, the Companion, the Wayfarer, and the Owned**

The compass-points deserve their separate naming, for the Prophet expanded them into standing libraries of instruction: *the near neighbor* — al-jār dhī al-qurbā, kin-neighbor, holding thereby *three* rights (neighbor, kin, co-religionist per the finest gloss) — and *the far neighbor* — al-jār al-junub, distant or alien — on whom the hadith of this office is unrelenting:

> *"Gabriel kept counseling me about the neighbor until I thought he would make him an heir; whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him honor his guest; whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him not harm his neighbor."* (al-Bukhārī 6014–16; Muslim 2624, from Abū Shurayḥ and Abū Hurayrah)

*the companion at your side* — al-ṣāḥib bi-l-janb — the seated partner, the travel-mate, the wife at the commentators' chief reading (al-Qurṭubī's remembering the married life after the whole pericope), and any beside-one in the company or the road; *ibn al-sabīl*, the son of the road — the stranded traveler, whose hunger and distance make him God's special ward (the Prophet's repeated practice, lodging strangers at the mosque's suffah-benches as the standing office of Islam) — and *mā malakat aymānukum*, those your right hands possess — the owned of the old world, over whom the sūrah ended every hierarchy with iḥsān, the Prophet's own last-days admonition still hot — *"your brothers whom God placed in your hands... do not burden them with what overwhelms them, and if you do, help them"* (al-Bukhārī 30; Muslim 1661). Across the whole register runs one engine: *iḥsān* — beauty without account-book — unaccountable kindness flowing down every gradient of power.

**Man Kāna Mukhtālan Fakhūrā: The Two Nouns of the Self-Viewer**

The seal names the character God does *not* love, in two antique nouns of intensity: *mukhtāl* — from *ikhtiyāl*, strutting, gait-arrogance — the one puffed by his own self-assessment; *fakhūr* — the loud self-celebrator, from *fakhr*, boast, vaunting over others with the goods of his hand — the pairing deliberate: concealed swagger of the interior, public swagger of the tongue — both God-loathed in the Book's long roster (57:23's *God loves not any vainglorious boaster* — same doublet; 31:18's "do not turn your cheek from people..."). The commentators connect the seal to the verse's own logic of placement: iḥsān-downward — kindness exactly into the weaker classes — is the precise antidote of the two named diseases: for strutting and boasting both feed on withholding before inferiors, on the posture of *looking down* at orphan and wayfarer and slave — hence the Book ordered its care-register *descending*, and closed with the two sins that grade exactly power abused: *arrogance of heart, boasting of mouth.* The believer has now his full map in one verse — worship God alone, and beautify toward everyone beneath his reach — and divine love is withheld from one character only: the man who made his rank a mirror. *That alone God does not love.*
