## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:3

> **If you fear that you will not deal fairly with the orphans, then marry such women as seem good to you, two, three, or four; but if you fear that you will not deal justly, then only one, or those whom your right hands possess. Thus it is more likely that you will not commit injustice.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿĀʾishah's Key: The Orphan Girl Behind the Marriage Verse**

No verse of the sūrah has been so misread at a distance — and so precisely explained at its source — as this one. The mother of the believers, ʿĀʾishah, may God be pleased with her, preserved its occasion and its logic for all generations, in the very verses' own custody-chain:

> *She said: "It concerns the orphan girl who is in the care of her guardian, and her property is joined to his; her wealth and her beauty please him, and he wishes to marry her with less than the standard bridal gift that any other would give her. So they were forbidden to marry those orphan girls unless they did them justice and gave them the highest bridal gift of their peers — and they were commanded to marry, apart from them, whatever women pleased them."* She said: "The people then asked the Messenger of God ﷺ about the matter after this verse, and God revealed: *They ask you for a ruling about women. Say: God gives you the ruling about them, and what is recited to you in the Book concerning orphan girls to whom you do not give what is prescribed for them, yet you desire to marry them* (4:127)." ʿĀʾishah added: "God's saying about 'other women' shows that they were forbidden to marry them merely because of their beauty and wealth — unless they treated them justly — on account of their being drawn to them by beauty and wealth." (al-Bukhārī 4573; Muslim 3018)

The verse's architecture is thereby made clear: it opens exactly where v. 2 left off — the guardian's conscience — and diagnoses the next abuse after embezzlement: the *marriage of exploitation*, the ward married by her own warden for her own money, at a discounted dowry no outsider could write for her. God's ordinance: *keep away from the vulnerable; if your fear of God cannot secure even their dowry, go to the open market of marriage where women can bargain full.*

**Mathnā wa Thulātha wa Rubāʿa: Restriction, Not License**

*"Then marry such women as seem good to you, two, three, or four"* — and here the whole scholarly tradition speaks with one voice, from al-Ṭabarī to al-Qurṭubī to Ibn Kathīr: this clause did not open polygamy; it *closed* it at four. Pre-Islamic Arabia knew no ceiling — men circled women as wealth allowed and conscience permitted, and the early community included companions with many more than four wives upon Islam; the reports are famous that Ghaylān al-Thaqafī and others embraced Islam with ten wives, and the Prophet ﷺ commanded: *"keep four, and part from the rest"* (reported by al-Tirmidhī 1128 and Ibn Mājah 1953 from several channels; the ruling unanimous in the books). The revealed number-words — *mathnā* (two by two), *thulāth* (three by three), *rubāʿ* (four by four) — thus fixed a maximum where none stood, bound to a clause the legislatures of the age never dreamed of: *but if you fear that you will not deal justly, then only one.* Divine law capped the custom, then chained the cap to justice — and chained justice to the husband's own fear of inability. The license has a guardian: conscience measured against capacity — financial, physical, moral.

**ʿAdl: The Justice the Marriage Market Owes**

*Fa-in khiftum allā taʿdilū fa-wāḥidah* — "but if you fear you will not deal justly, then one": the justice stipulated, explain al-Qurṭubī and the jurists with him, is the *measurable* equity: maintenance at like standards, lodging, clothing, and the nights' rotation — the outer ledger every wife can audit. The inner ledger — love's tilt, the heart's lean — the sūrah itself exempts at v. 129: "you will never be able to deal fully equitably between your wives, even if you strive" — the Prophet ﷺ himself living under that dispensation and teaching his ummah its prayer:

> *"O God, this is my allotment in what I control; so do not hold me to account for what You control and I do not control"* — meaning the heart's inclination (Abū Dāwūd 2134; al-Tirmidhī 1140, who graded it ḥasan; from Abū Hurayrah).

And his ﷺ sternest warning stands sentinel beside it:

> *"Whoever has two wives and inclines to one of them will come on the Day of Resurrection with one side of his body hanging down paralyzed."* (Abū Dāwūd 2133; al-Tirmidhī 1141; al-Nasāʾī 33942 — from Abū Hurayrah)

The leaning side drags into the next life: so precisely does the verse's justice-verb weigh husbands who multiply vows.

**Adnā Allā Taʿūlū: Tilting Away from Justice**

The seal — *dhālika adnā allā taʿūlū* — "thus it is likelier that you will not incline [to injustice]" — closes the statute with the Book's precise jurisprudential idiom: revelation aims at the *likelier* justice, legislating probabilities into structures. *ʿŪl*, from the root of inclination and deviation, is the tilt of the scale-beam; the verse's entire edifice — cap of four, condition of equity, retreat-option to one — is an instrument for keeping the beam level. Al-Rāzī observes that the legislation thereby binds the marriage law to the orphan law by a single thread of *preventive* wisdom: a man overburdened with mouths he cannot honestly feed is a man driven toward the orphan's vault; house-laws that keep his dependents within his honest means are orphan-protection by another door. The sūrah's pericope is thus one fabric: ward the orphan's property (v. 2), ward the orphan girl's dowry and person (v. 3), ward the wife's gift outright (v. 4) — three fences around one garden, whose gate is taqwā and whose watchman is the Raqīb of v. 1.

**The Post-Uḥud Prudence and the Modern Reading**

Read against its battlefield background — seventy widows made in a single morning, their daughters of marriageable age — the verse performs one more office: it makes room, within strict equity, for the sheltering of the war's surplus of women, a sociological mercy the European wars of later centuries would render unintelligible and necessary alike. The scholars therefore refuse both distortions: the zealotry that reads v. 3 as a standing encouragement to multiply households, and the apologetics that erase its license altogether. It is, say al-Qurṭubī's words, a law of *permissibility within bounds, conditioned by justice, measured by fear of God* — marriage, whatever its number, remaining what the whole sūrah declared it: a trust before the Watcher, in which the woman's rights are as revealed as the man's, and likelier to be defended by heaven than by any human court of her age.
