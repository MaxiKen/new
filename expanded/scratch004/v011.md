## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:11

> **God enjoins upon you concerning your children: unto the male a share equal to that of two females; but if there are only daughters, two or more, then unto them is two-thirds of what he leaves; if only one, then unto her a half. And unto his parents—each of the two—a sixth of what he leaves if he has a child; but if he has no child and his parents are his [only] heirs, then unto his mother a third; and if he has brothers, then unto his mother a sixth, after paying any bequest he may have bequeathed or any debt. Your parents or your children—you know not which of them are nearer to you in benefit. [This is] an obligation ordained by God. Truly God is Knowing, Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yūṣīkumu Allāh: The Testator Is God**

The inheritance-code opens in a construction of singular majesty: *yūṣīkumu Allāhu fī awlādikum* — "God enjoins upon you concerning your children": *yuṣī* — He testates, He gives the final charge — the same root as the dying man's *waṣiyyah* (bequest). The rhetorical reversal is the entire doctrine: the dead neither own nor allocate anymore; upon decease the estate reverts to the real Owner — "We it is Who give life and death, and We are the Inheritors" (15:23) — and God now *makes His own will known* concerning His returned property. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both mark how the *faṣl*, the legal clause, arrives therefore without negotiation-address: not "O you who believe, apportion thus," but a declaration over the dead man's most sensitive dependency, his children: the Lord of the family legislates direct. The occasion-reports set the scene back in the wake of Umm Kujjah's complaint: when this verse descended confirming the daughters' portions, the shock ran through even the Companions' assembly, long habituated to cavalry-law — the women and minors allocated the lion's share — until the Prophet's teaching habituated them to the arithmetic of mercy.

**Li-l-Dhakari Mithlu Ḥaẓẓi al-Unthayayn: The Ratio and Its Rationale**

The first ratio, most quoted and most polemicized of the code's clauses, assigns son over daughter at two-to-one: *li-l-dhakari mithlu ḥaẓẓi al-unthayayn* — "unto the male the like of the allotment of two females." The commentators fix the ratio's logic within the law's own architecture, for it is *cost-accounting, not honor-accounting*: upon the male heir, revealed law lays the total financial burden of his household — the bridewealth he must pay to marry (v. 4), the maintenance he owes his wife and children wholly (v. 34's *because they spend of their property*; 65:6–7's nursing-houses and wage obligations), his unmarried sisters' and widowed mother's support — while the female heir bears *none* of these, her own mahr, earnings, and inheritance locked to her sole name (al-Qurṭubī's spelling out of the design, with the full juristic register). Ibn Taymiyyah's summary is the schools' common one: Islam assigned each sex a financial *role* and funded the role: the man is made provider at law — his double portion is his equipment, carried in trust for dependents — the woman is relieved of provision-duty — her single portion is *freehold*, hers to keep or spend. The same Book that distributed mahr entirely to the bride and maintenance wholly to the husband made the probate shares balance the ledger: two-to-one measured against lifetime liabilities, not against souls, whose full equality the Book seals at its standing address: "whoever works righteousness, male or female, being a believer..." (16:97).

**The Daughter-Shares: Half and Two-Thirds at Law**

The verse's middle clauses give the single daughter half, two-or-more daughters two-thirds — and here the commentators record the first-era's astonishment this way: in a world where daughters inherited *nothing*, God's probate handed them the estate's largest ordinary fractions — half and two-thirds — and then *completed* their advantage through the residue-rules the jurists opened from the verse (with a son, she shares as one-of-two-parts; with no sons, the sole daughter takes half and the residue reverts by established maxims — Ibn ʿAbbās's school confirming the majority's rule in a maxim the masajid still teach: *a daughter is never without her fixed chair*). ʿUmar's caliphate already witnessed qāḍīs processing daughter-estates by the verse exactly; the Shiʿi transmission through Imams al-Bāqir and al-Ṣādiq affirms the same clauses (with their own doctrine on the daughter's return of residue where no father-brother competes); and the clause *fa-in kānat wāḥidatan fa-lahā al-niṣf* — "if one, to her a half" — is the single sentence by which, thirteen centuries ago, the world was informed that a lone girl-child is *heir of half a man's wealth*, full and personal.

**The Parents' Third and Sixth: Filial Debt Paid at the Grave**

The parents' clauses — *wa li-abawayhi li-kulli wāḥidin minhumā al-sudus* with child-heirs; the mother's *third* where no children and no crowd of siblings; her *sixth* under the brothers' reduction (the celebrated *ʿawl*-adjacent question for which ʿUmar, in the case known as *al-ḥimāriyyah*, consulted the assembly before conceding the siblings-rule) — enact, at the point of deepest test, the Book's one abiding symmetry: the parents who spent are *repaid at last from their own child's grave-goods*, the mother with her named chair in every scenario, the father riding his named-sixth plus his residue-position as ʿaṣabah (nearest male kin — the majority's reading). Al-Qurṭubī's inheritance chapters and Ibn Kathīr's tafsīr beneath these clauses stand as the direct fountainheads of the entire Muslim probate-practice — and the structure is transparent about its pedagogy: "your parents *or* your children — *lā tadrūna ayyuhum aqrabu lakum nafʿan* — you know not which of them are nearer to you in benefit": benefit in this world and the next, unknown to dying men; hence the shares were left *revealed*, not elected — so that where affection misjudges, and cost miscalculates, the fraction from the Knower's hand stands correct. The verse's seal ascends therefore to its proper attributes: *farīḍatan min Allāh* — an obligation from God — *inna Allāha kāna ʿalīman ḥakīman* — Knowing, Wise: Knows what men owe; is Wise in what men cannot see; and settles, between the mother and the child of every dead householder, the estate's story without passion, error, or greed.
