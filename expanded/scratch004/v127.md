## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:127

> **And they consult thee concerning the women. Say: God gives you the ruling concerning them, and what has been recited to you in the Book concerning the orphan-girls to whom you do not give what is prescribed for them, while you desire to marry them — and concerning the oppressed among the children, and that you stand firm for the orphans with justice. And whatever good you do — God is ever Knowing of it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa Yastaftūnaka fī al-Nisāʾ: The Consultation That Returned**

The family-file of the sūrah's opening now reopens with a live question: *wa yastaftūnaka fī al-nisāʾ* — "and they consult thee concerning the women": *yastaftūnaka* — from f-t-y — the verb of seeking a fatwā, a considered ruling (its standing question-cadence appearing across the Book whenever the community is at a practical loss: "they consult thee concerning the sacred month — fighting therein" — 2:217; "they consult thee what they should spend" — 2:219; "they consult thee concerning the spoils" — 8:1; and already within this sūrah: "they consult thee — say: God gives you the ruling concerning the heirless" — 4:176 below, closing the sūrah with the same formula). The occasion — reported in the standing collections: in the standing Sabāb-material (Muslim's standing narration from ʿĀʾishah and the tafsīr tradition generally) — the question came after the earlier rulings: the women of Madīnah themselves, stirred by the inheritance-verses and the orphan-guardianship ordinances, came asking: what now of us — where do we stand in this new order? And the verse's standing lesson is already in its first word: the community's questions are not interruptions of revelation but its *occasions* — God answers the asking, and the asking of women about women is written into the Book's own text forever — a standing testament that the sūrah named "the Women" was itself composed partly in response to women who asked.

**Quli Allāhu Yuftīkum Fīhinna: The Answer Above the Answer**

The reply-form is itself a ruling about rulings: *quli Allāhu yuftīkum fīhinna* — "say: God gives you the ruling concerning them": the Prophet is made to answer — but to answer by deferring upward: *say: God rules on this* — the standing deferential pattern (4:176 reprises it: "they consult thee — say: God gives you the ruling"). Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr remark on the humility encoded in the grammar: although rulings could have issued through the Prophet in his own words, the Book teaches the questioners before teaching them the rule — the teaching being: fatwā is not opinion; it is transmission from the One who owns the estate of v. 126 — hence the verse then recites the actual content, beginning with the most arresting phrase: *wa mā yutlā ʿalaykum fī al-kitāb* — "and what has been recited to you in the Book."

**Wa Mā Yutlā ʿAlaykum fī al-Kitāb: The Recited-Again Ruling — Orphan-Girls and the Cheated Dowry**

*Wa mā yutlā ʿalaykum fī al-kitābi fī yatāmā al-nisāʾi allātī lā tuʾtūnahunna mā kutiba lahunna wa targhabūna an tankiḥūhunna* — "and what has been recited to you in the Book concerning the orphan-girls to whom you do not give what is prescribed for them, while you desire to marry them": the commentators recognize the double-reference: (1) "what is recited to you in the Book" = the earlier passage of this very sūrah — vv. 2–3 above ("give the orphans their property... and if you fear you cannot deal justly with the orphans — then marry whom you please of women") — God ruling now by *pointing back* to His own prior ruling: the answer to the women's consultation is partly: *you have already been told* — reread vv. 2–3. And (2) the precise abuse identified: guardians of orphan-girls — controlling both the girl's property and her marriage — would either marry her themselves for her wealth, giving her less than her due dowry ("you do not give what is prescribed for them" — her proper mahr and her inheritance-share), or prevent her marriage to others so that her wealth might remain in the household, or marry her while having no true desire for her. The standing report of the first hearers' attitude — noted in the tradition of Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and others: the Companions, hearing these verses, wished to leave off marrying orphan-girls altogether; and the Prophet — in Muslim's standing narration when asked about the orphan-girl verse — defined the target precisely: it is the guardian who, seeing her beauty and wealth, marries her for her money and does not treat her fairly in the dowry — injustice forbidden, not marriage itself.

**Raghiba: The Two Readings and the Two Abuses**

The pivot-word of the verse carries a celebrated double-reading: *wa targhabūna an tankiḥūhunna* — most read it "while you desire to marry them" (the guardian drawn to the dowered girl for her property) — and another standing transmission (reported from ʿĀʾishah's circle) reads with the implied negative understood from the context: that you are *averse* to marrying them — the guardian turning away from the plain or poor orphan-girl, yet neither marrying her fairly nor releasing her to others, holding her suspended while her property is consumed. Ibn Kathīr gathers both and remarks that the verse embraces both abuses: the greedy marriage that takes the girl without her rights, and the neglectful detention that takes her rights without the marriage — the word *raghiba* (from r-gh-b — inclining, desiring) covering the two directions in which desire, unmanaged by piety, wrongs the dependent: excessive desire that exploits, and withheld desire that abandons. Between them stands the only lawful posture: the marriage of fairness, or the release with kindness — the very alternatives Mercy continues to elaborate in vv. 128–130 below.

**Wa al-Mustaḍʿafīna min al-Wildān: From Orphan-Girls to the Oppressed Children**

The verse then enlarges its protectorate: *wa al-mustaḍʿafīna min al-wildān* — "and concerning the oppressed among the children": *mustaḍʿaf* — the passive-participle of al-ḍuʿf — weakness — the standing Qur'ānic category of the socially weakened (4:75's standing plea on behalf of "the oppressed men, women, and children crying: our Lord — take us out of this city of the wrongdoing people"; 4:98's excuse-boundary for "the oppressed among men, women, and children who can devise no plan and are not guided to a way"). The position — noted by the commentators — unfolds the sūrah's deepest logic: the child is here not a lineage-asset but a *trust*, and the law of the sūrah consistently locates holiness at the point of weakness: where a person is small, dependent, propertyless, and voiceless — there the Book posts its strongest guards: the orphan-girl first (the most defrauded of the vulnerable), then oppressed children generally, then — in the sweeping clause — *wa an taqūmū li-l-yatāmā bi-l-qisṭ* — "and that you stand firm for the orphans with justice": *qūmū* — standing — the posture of vigilance and service (the same verb that 4:135 will raise to its summit: "be standing-firm for justice — witnesses for God — even against yourselves") — and *qisṭ* — the portioning justice, equal shares without desert-distinctions. The entire verse is thus the sūrah's thesis restated as gerund: God has spoken — concerning women, the recited ruling; concerning orphan-girls, the full share; concerning children, protection; concerning orphans, standing justice — and then the closing assurance unseals everything: *wa mā tafʿalū min khayrin fa-inna Allāha kāna bihi ʿalīman* — "and whatever good you do — God is ever Knowing of it": khayr in the indefinite — *any* good — and the ʿalīm's knowledge the guarantor that no kindness shown to a voiceless dependent is ever spent where it cannot be recorded — the pattern the Book seals elsewhere with the same broad surety ("so whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it" — 99:7; "God does not waste the wage of the good-doers" — 9:120; "whatever good you send before you for your souls — you shall find it with God — better and greater in reward" — 73:20): the family-law of heaven audited by the Knowing One Himself.
