## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:25

> **And whoever of you has not the means to marry believing free women, then [let him marry] from among those believing slave girls whom your right hands possess. And God knows best your faith. You are of one another. So marry them by the permission of their people, and give them their bridewealth honorably, as married women, not as fornicators, nor as takers of paramours. And once they are married, should they commit an indecency, then upon them is half the punishment of free married women. This is for those among you who fear hardship. But to be patient is better for you. And God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Man Lam Yastaṭiʿ: The Poor Man's Door God Himself Cut**

The verse answers a distressed question of the new community's young men — its occasion standing in the Companion-reports as the plea of those without property for marriage, falling to desire's danger — with an escorted alternative: *wa man lam yastaṭiʿ minkum ṭawlan an yankiḥa al-muḥṣanāti al-muʾmināti fa-min mā malakat aymānukum min fatayātikumu al-muʾmināt* — "whoever of you lacks the amplitude (*ṭawl* — wealth's stretch) to marry free believing women, then from among your right-hand-possessed believing slave-girls." The clause erects upon the ancient world's most hardened status-wall — the slave's body as commodity — a wholly new estate of humanity: the slave-girl is *mu'minah*, a believer — marriageable by God's law, dowered by statute, wed by ceremony — entering marriage not as used goods but as bride. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī labor the significance in every matter the verse furnishes: the Prophet's own the Prophet's own counsel over the class had set the theology: Abū Dharr's report of the prophetic instruction — *"your brothers whom God placed in your hands: feed them of what you eat, clothe them of what you wear, and do not burden them with what overwhelms them"* (al-Bukhārī 30, 2545; Muslim 1661) — and the emancipation-hedges of the Book in every expiation verse — from this statute the believers learned slavery was a temporary legal estate of persons, never a metaphysics of persons, with the bridal-verse its first and loudly humanizing proof.

**Allāhu Aʿlamu bi-Īmānikum / Baʿḍukum min Baʿḍ: The Great Leveling Sentence**

Then the verse reaches what its commentators have repeatedly called its social trump: *wa-Allāhu aʿlamu bi-īmānikum, baʿḍukum min baʿḍ* — "God knows best your faith; you are of one another." *Baʿḍukum min baʿḍ* — a clause of three Arabic words voluminous as a manifesto: *each of you is from the other* — free and slave from a single clay, a single nafs wāḥidah of 4:1 — spiritual status never purchasable and never chattel-ized, for the title-deed of worth is the faith God alone measures. Al-Rāzī takes it to its full consequence: outward inequalities are the world's business; inner rank is God's; and the slave-wife's soul may stand, at Judgment, rows above the free woman's. The clause stands at the summit of those compressed Qur'anic sentences in which a social system is quietly annulled: between v. 1's one-soul and v. 25's *you are of one another*, the sūrah's whole anthropology of women-and-wards comes to speech.

**By the Permission of Their People, Muḥṣanāt: The Slave-Bride's Full Honors**

The statute then reproduces, for the slave-bride, every safeguard the free bride received: *fa-nkiḥūhunna bi-idhni ahlihinna wa ātūhunna ujūrahunna bi-l-maʿrūf muḥṣanātin ghayra musāfiḥātin wa lā muttakhidhāti akhdān* — "marry them with their people's permission, and give them their rewards honorably: fortress-women, not pourers-out, nor takers of secret lovers." Three honors, bestowed in sequence: hers is a wali-witnessed contract (the *ahl* — her legal holders, the jurists refining how much passes to her and how much to the estate); hers is her own *ujūr*, her *mahr*, in the Book's same wage-word measured to rank; hers *also* is the conduct-word *muḥṣanāt* — the fortress-title once married: once wed she is *fortress-woman*, and marriage is to be built against both ancient shapes of the pour (the open *sifāḥ*-fornication and the secret *khidn* — the paramour kept hidden) — the Book barring both its ages' two exits from covenant-honor: public shamelessness and private arrangements. The commentators' distinction — *musāfiḥāt* as the open marketplace-women and *akhhdān*-takers as veiled alliances (Ibn ʿAbbās's full triad-description: marriage public, fornication open, paramours hidden, as Ibn Masʿūd-explicated — in al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr's preservation) — has become a paradigm for later marital law-book: Islam recognizes the *announced* house alone.

**Half the Punishment of the Fortress, and a Concluding Mercy**

The verse's legal tail — half the free woman's penalty upon the slave-wife's proven offense — the jurists read within God's distributive realism: the slave-wife's walls stood lower, her protection thinner in the world's actual sociology; so her accountability is mercifully measured, while (33:30) the Prophet's own household stands its inverse: double for doubling of station: heaven's equity runs both directions of status. The verse then seals, against the pressure-brooding man himself: *dhālika li-man khashiya al-ʿanata minkum* — "this is for him who fears hardship (al-ʿanat — the crush of desire-hardship)" — *wa-an taṣbirū khayrun lakum* — "but that you be patient is better for you": release provided, patience crowned — God's law easing and exhorting in the same paragraph — with the twin-attributes of the marriage-pericope returning at the close: *wa kānā Allāhu ghafūran raḥīman*: the living God of this entire legislative block, forgiving the lapses of the law's subjects, merciful in the tailoring of the law itself — statutes sewn, from orphan to slave-bride, to the measurements of mercy.
