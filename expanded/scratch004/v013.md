## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:13

> **Those are the limits set by God: whosoever obeys God and His Messenger, He will cause him to enter Gardens with rivers running below, abiding therein. And that is the great triumph.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tilka Ḥudūd Allāh: Boundaries Named for the Ledger**

The crowding pronoun *tilka* — "those" — gathers six verses of statutes — orphan-trust, wardship, the men's and women's shares, the provision-word, the arithmetic of children, parents, spouses, and siblings — into a single legal noun: *ḥudūd Allāh*, the limits of God. *Ḥadd*, plural *ḥudūd*, is before all else a *boundary-line*: the hedge, the pale, the fence-post at the field's edge — named in God's law therefore *both* the ordained penalties and every ruling placed like a marker between lawful and unlawful (2:187's twin-clause: "those are God's limits; so approach them not"). Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr lean on the clause's majesty quietly: the Book could have summed vv. 1–12 as "the inheritance rules"; it chose *the limits of God* — elevating the family's ledger into sacred geography — so that a man moving a sixth to a seventh feels it in his bones as *moving a fence*, the oldest violation in every covenant of God (with 58:4's consequence for posts removed: a clod's weight of wrong to the clan, the full law-claim of heaven). The distribution-paper becomes thus as grave as the temple-stone: boundaries are boundaries, in pastures or percentages.

**Man Yuṭiʿi Allāha wa Rasūlahu: Obedience Again at the Ledger**

Then the promise: *wa man yuṭiʿi Allāha wa rasūlahu yudkhilhu jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru khālidīna fīhā* — "whoso obeys God and His Messenger, He admits him into Gardens, rivers flowing below, abiding therein." Commentators of the schools — al-Qurṭubī pre-eminently at this clause — have always paused at the *placement*: the paired obedience-command — God and Messenger, in the creed's binding-canonical form, the very formula of 3:32, 4:59, 5:92 — is issued here *not at prayer or pilgrimage but at a probate schedule*. The lesson stands as one of the sūrah's quiet definitions of Islam: obedience is not a liturgy-department but a way of answering a revealed ratio — the sixth given as sixth, the eighth as eighth, the widow seated, the orphan full-handed — whole religion at work in pen, ink, and scale. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's school pressed exactly such pointings from this verse-family: these are limits *carried out*, not merely recited — for the promise's verb, *yudkhilhu* — "He will cause him to enter" — is awarded to the *doer*. The garden-rivers' image — water under the pavilions, permanence above the water — answers the grave's drying: *dividing duly in death's chamber, the believer hires rivers for eternity's chamber.*

**Al-Fawz al-ʿAẓīm: Triumph's Superlative on a Probate Line**

The verse's seal — *wa dhālika al-fawzu al-ʿaẓīm* — "and that is the great triumph" — takes the Book's supreme success-title and nails it to inheritance-obedience. The phrase *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm*, with its fixed throne in ten verses across the Book (5:119's "God content with them... that is the great triumph"; 9:72's again above the Gardens; 44:57's and 64:9's "that is the great triumph"), names the *summit* of every divine scale — and here furnishes the clause the scholars bundle with the sūrah's chapter-meaning: God's ledger of triumph numbers honesty-with-shares among its peak-pass entries. Al-Rāzī's note in the companions' spirit: the greed that guards dinars at probate and the glory-love that guards fame in speeches are *one* interior foe; letting go at God's named fraction is fawz of the exact sort because it is the here-located victory over that foe. The community's practice answered with its feet: Islam's first centuries read v. 13 in the mosques at the teaching-circles of farāʾiḍ — teachers of fractions beginning lectures with the *ḥudūd*-recitation, a habit al-Qurṭubī's own age still kept — as if to teach every student of the reckoning-science: the syllabus's stakes are *garden and fire*; the ratios are a pilgrimage; the pen that writes them right walks, its holder with it, *into the garden where rivers run below — the triumph beside which the estates men died for are nothing, and beside which nothing else is anything.*

**The Standing Word for the Household Ledger: Farāʾiḍ as Devotion**

The jurists named the whole science of this law *ʿilm al-farāʾiḍ* — the knowledge of the appointed portions — and the tradition's standing commendation of it flows directly from this verse: the Prophet's celebrated designation of inheritance-knowledge (its standing register in the Sunan-collections — "learn the farāʾiḍ and teach them, for they are half of knowledge" — the report transmitted through the ḥadīth-collections of Ibn Mājah and al-Dāraquṭnī) fastening the probate-science to half of all knowledge because it orders half of human anxiety: what becomes of what we leave. The verse's own logic justifies the superlative: a law revealed by God with shares of His own fixing, breached or kept in a chamber no camera will ever see, tests precisely that unseen-fearing (*al-ghayb*-fearing) piety the Book everywhere crowns — for the man dividing his father's land knows that only God watches the ledger, and verse 13 tells him the Watcher has named the task a triumph: *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm* on a probate line, the Garden through a guardian's honest pen.
