## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:12

> **And unto you a half of what your wives leave, if they have no child, but if they have a child, then unto you a fourth of what they leave, after paying any bequest they may have bequeathed or any debt. And unto them a fourth of what you leave if you have no child, but if you have a child, then unto them an eighth of what you leave, after paying any bequest you may have bequeathed or any debt. If a man or woman leaves no direct heir, but has a brother or sister, then unto each of the two a sixth; but if they are more than two, they share equally a third, after paying any bequest he may have bequeathed or any debt, without harm to anyone—a duty ordained by God, and God is Knowing, Clement.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Marriage-Bond at the Probate Table**

The revealed arithmetic now seats the two spouses at the grave-side: the husband receiving half his wife's estate childless, a quarter where children exist; the wife a quarter of his childless, an eighth with children. The asymmetry — half/quarter answered by quarter/eighth — distills in miniature the code's whole logic laid bare at v. 11: the husband, bearer of mahr and maintenance through life, is likewise the closer claimant in death; the wife, never charged to provide, steps back one rank — and al-Qurṭubī is careful, as at v. 11, to keep the table's philosophy in view: the entire scheme is the *cost-ledger of the family*, distributed across two sexes the law has made financially asymmetric by deliberate mercy, not any scale of souls. That the wife's bond is named *in* her death as well as his teaches the code's own doctrine of marriage: the house that was two governments for the budget — his for spending, hers for holding — is nonetheless *one* corporation before God in both graves. The reports from the days of the statute's descent tell of the Anṣār's wives receiving what pagan Islam's own mothers never dreamed: a pitched, written share of the husband's caravan and palm-grove, no fraction removable by the sons of his brother.

**Each Clause a Shelter: The Double-Repetition of Debts and Bequests**

Twice in the verse the apparatus-clause is rung — *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin yūṣīna bihā aw dayn* — "after the bequest they made and debt": again the code's unvarying sequence: funeral-claims (under jurists' extension), then creditors, up to the bequest-limited third, and only then the seats. The doubling is itself a guardianship of the dying: the Book refuses to let the grieving glance skip the settled order — each party's share is calculated on the net, every loan of the two lifetimes honored before a single heir is served, as the Prophet's own teaching had cemented the rule in tthe assembled dictum of the law: *debts to God before debts to men, and debts to men before bequests, and bequests before shares* — the Prophetic consensus-sequence as al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr register as unanimous in the law-books. The clause stands likewise as the law's first anti-fraud fence: *no* inventing generous "bequests" at the bedside to starve specific heirs, no conveniently remembered "debts" — subjects the *without harm* clause comes, at the verse's end, to seal with a special lock.

**Kalālah: The Word ʿUmar Carried to His Grave**

The verse introduces the code's famous difficulty — *wa in kāna rajulun yūrathu kalālatan aw imraʾatun* — "if a man inherits by kalālah, or a woman": *kalālah*, the deceased *without parent or child* — the descendant-chain snapped at both ends, the estate left looking sideways to siblings. The word weighed upon no one more than the second caliph; the compilations of the *Sunan* and the Musnad-gatherers preserve his pulpit-grief:

> *"There are three matters — I would rather that the Messenger of God ﷺ had left us a decisive word upon them than that I should possess this world entire: the kalālah, the grandfather [in inheritance], and of ribā certain dealings."* (transmitted by ʿUmar's son and others — its substance running through al-Bayhaqī's Sunan, al-Tirmidhī's registers on the grandfather, and the tafsīr-collections at this verse)

And it was for kalālah alone that God would send a *second* seat of legislation — verse 176's closing statute of the sūrah, answering ʿUmar's own envoy with the Prophet himself months before his ﷺ death, "Have the summer's verse not come, with the sūrah's own end?" — the completion which set maternal and paternal siblings in distinct seats: this verse, per the consensus of the law's doctors from Ibn ʿAbbās forward, addressing the *maternal* brothers and sisters (sixth each; more than two sharing a third *equally*, male and female — tied at the womb's own tie), and v. 176 the *full or paternal* (male two-to-one over female), the difference itself teaching the two ships' names the Arabs kept distinct: a brother of one mother shares the kinship of the birth-waters alone, ranked with women equally as children of one merciful channel — a brother of one father shares the father's line, riding the v. 11 ratio.

**Ghayra Muḍārr: The No-Harm Lock on Every Testament**

Then the seal with which no other statute in the Book's corpus closes: *ghayra muḍāran bi-waṣiyyatin* — "no bequest-worked harm" — with its Prophetic hardening into statute:

> *"No bequest to an heir"* (Abū Dāwūd 2870; al-Tirmidhī 2120, graded ṣaḥīḥ; al-Nasāʾī 3639, from Abū Umāmah — the ruling of the conquest-year pilgrimage-address)

and with the Prophet's separate maxim — *"harm is not permitted in Islam"* (Ibn Mājah 2340) — sealing the same gate. The clause thereby posts at every dying man's desk the law's anti-malice rule: one may love whom one likes, and owe whom one lists — *but the probate floor is God's; no last letter may kick seats away.* Al-Qurṭubī's masters classify the harms the clause blocks: bequest to one heir above others (the express Prophetic bar), false debt-acknowledgments on the deathbed, and bequests just under the third worked with an accomplice-heir. The verse then takes its attributes: *farīḍatan min Allāh* — an ordinance from God — *wa kānā Allāhu ʿalīman ḥalīman* — Knowing, Clement: the Knowing-of-estates couples here with forbearance-toward-the-faulty — and the probate's two offices are complete, one verse for children and parents, one for spouses and siblings, all calculated under heaven's own patient, sinless accountancy: death unties every purse-string except these.
