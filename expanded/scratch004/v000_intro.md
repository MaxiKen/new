# Sūrah al-Nisāʾ (Chapter 4) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ — "The Women" — is the fourth chapter of the Qur'an and the second longest of the Madinan sūrahs, comprising one hundred and seventy-six verses. It was revealed in Madinah in the years immediately following the Battle of Uḥud (3 AH / 625 CE), when the young Muslim community was confronting simultaneously the wounds of that defeat, the treachery of internal hypocrisy, the open hostility of certain Jewish tribes of Madinah, and — most pressingly for this sūrah's distinctive subject matter — the social wreckage war leaves behind: widows without provision, orphans without guardians, households fractured along lines of loyalty and belief.

The sūrah takes its name from the prominence of its legislation concerning women — their marriage rights, their bridewealth, their inheritance shares, their protection from the predations of guardians — the first corpus of revealed law in history to establish, categorically and by divine command, the independent legal personality of women in property, marriage, and succession. Alongside this, however, the chapter builds an equally weighty architecture on other foundations: the sanctity of family relations and the duty of parents and children to one another; the administration of justice and the obligation of equity even against one's own soul; the crime of usury and the ethics of commerce; the status of the People of the Book — their distortions of scripture, their rejection of the Prophet ﷺ, their claims concerning Jesus son of Mary — culminating in the Qur'an's definitive address of the crucifixion question (vv. 157–159); the exposure of the hypocrites and the mechanics of their double-dealing; the laws of war, of the fear-prayer, of emigration, and of the protection of the weak; and, woven through every section, the demand that faith be complete — that entering Islam means entering it wholly, in conduct as in creed.

Commentators have long noted the sūrah's architectural symmetry: it opens with an address to all mankind — *Reverence your Lord, Who created you from a single soul* — placing the entire human family under one Creator and one womb, and it closes, at verse 176, with a question asked and answered about inheritance, so that the chapter that begins with the origin of human kinship ends with the law that governs kinship's final earthly transaction. Between these poles stands one of the most consequential bodies of legislation and exhortation in the Qur'an: family law, commercial law, penal law, the law of war and peace, and above all the law of the heart — reverence (*taqwā*) — commanded at both the sūrah's first verse and repeated through its course as the single thread binding every statute to its Lord.

## The Occasions of Its Revelation

The Battle of Uḥud (Shawwāl, 3 AH) looms over this sūrah as the Shadow over its opening sections. Seventy Muslims had been martyred; households were suddenly headed by grieving widows raising fatherless children; and the question thrust upon the community — *who now stands guardian over the orphan's property and the widow's future?* — is answered in the sūrah's celebrated opening pericope (vv. 1–14), where the orphans' sacred trust, the women's marriage rights, and the inheritance ordinance are legislated together in a single sweep. The Prophet's ﷺ own era of consolidation in Madinah — the expulsion of Banū al-Naḍīr after their treason (4–5 AH), the crisis of the slander against ʿĀʾishah and its exonerating revelation, the expeditions of the middle Madinan years, and the Jews of Khaybar and Madinah contending with the new polity — supplies the occasions for verse after verse recorded in the works of al-Wāḥidī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Suyūṭī, many of which are noted in the commentary that follows.

## Distinguished Contents

Among the matters for which Sūrah al-Nisāʾ is especially renowned:

- **The inheritance code** (vv. 7–14, 176) — the revealed mathematics of succession that founded the science of *ʿilm al-farāʾiḍ*, which the Prophet ﷺ urged his companions to learn and teach, calling it "half of knowledge."
- **The women's charter** (vv. 3–4, 19–25, 34–35, 127–130) — bridewealth as the woman's own property, prohibition of inheriting women against their will, the obligations of maintenance and kindness, and the machinery of arbitration in marital discord.
- **Justice as testimony to God** (v. 135) — *O you who believe! Be custodians of justice, witnesses for God, even if against yourselves* — a verse the scholars have called the supreme statute of judicial integrity.
- **The verse of universal trust** (v. 58) — *God commands you to deliver trusts to those entitled to them* — which the commentators, citing the Prophet's own precedent at the conquest of Makkah, have made the charter of all governance and office-holding in Islam.
- **The crucifixion passage** (vv. 157–159) — *They did not slay him, nor did they crucify him, but it was made to appear so unto them* — the Qur'an's own account of the end of Jesus's earthly mission, around which the whole theology of the People of the Book section (vv. 44–55, 153–173) is arranged.
- **The hypocrites' anatomy** (vv. 60–63, 78–83, 137–146) — the chapter's systematic diagnosis of the double-hearted: their speech, their hesitation, their terror, and their end.
- **The fear-prayer** (vv. 101–104) — revealed for the battlefield's exigency, and the divine condescension of shortening prayer in travel.

## Structure of the Commentary

What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary in which every one of the one hundred and seventy-six verses receives its own complete treatment, in the manner of the earlier volumes of this series on Sūrah al-Fātiḥah, Sūrah al-Baqarah, and Sūrah Āl ʿImrān. Where the source groups verses together (as with vv. 13–14, 27–28, 57–58, and others), the verses have been separated and treated individually, as the method of this series demands. Each commentary draws upon the Arabic language and its roots, the transmitted views of the classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and the reports of Ibn ʿAbbās among them — the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, the occasions of revelation where they are soundly transmitted, and the living reasoning that connects this Madinan legislation to the households, marketplaces, courts, and battlefields of every age that inherits it.
