## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:21

> **And how can you take it back, when you have lain with one another and they have made with you a solemn covenant?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa Kayfa Taʾkhudhūnahu: The Question That Silences the Courtroom**

After v. 20's waving hundredweight, God's voice closes around the plaintiff-husband with one of the Book's most intimate rhetorical devices — *wa kayfa taʾkhudhūnahu?* — "and how could you take it?" — the *kayfa* of scandalized impossibility (its sisters: 2:28's *how do you disbelieve in God?*, 3:101's *how could you deny?*) — a question asked not for lack of mechanism but as exposure of *moral impossibility*: the thing can be imagined, and the question denies it could be *imagined without shame*. The two grounds the verse lays out follow into the heart of the marriage-mystery: one of the *body*, one of the *word*: proximity past undoing, pledge past recalling. Between them God answers — no juristic heavy-studied code — with the simple amazement of decency: *how, with what face, at which reckoning?*

**Qad Afḍā Baʿḍukum ilā Baʿḍ: The Closet Congress of Two Lives**

*Qad afḍā baʿḍukum ilā baʿḍ* — "you have already gone in one unto another" — the commentators take in the full company of its shading: from *ifḍāʾ*, arriving unto, emptying into, coming upon — a phrase including the directest meaning the law-speech of the Arabs permitted the Book to use without loss of its veil: lying together (Ibn Kathīr's authorities, with immediate juristic explication: full entitlement of the wife's mahr on consummation drops clear of every schools' dispute), stretched by others of the Salaf, al-Ḥasan at their front, to *khalwah* — privacy accomplished, door-shut alone-ness — a reading by which Abū Ḥanīfah rested full dower-title upon the proven privacy alone (al-Shāfiʿī allotting half — the disputation al-Qurṭubī transcribes at full scope with the reasoning of both schools). The argument of the verse either way overwhelms by delicacy: *yours was the secret sharing that emptied her before you* — the Book mindful of everything public society cannot see, demanding at bar the memory of what each husband saw. The law's protection of the *used* relationship is thereby founded upon its protection of shared *memory*: what you alone knew cannot be un-known for a price.

**Mīthāq Ghalīẓ: The Covenant Word That Marriages Share with Prophethood**

*Wa akhadhna minkum mīthāqan ghalīẓan* — "and they have taken from you a weighty covenant" — is, in the company of the women-commentators' treasuries, the most celebrated grammatical gift of the verse: the covenant exacted is said to be taken *from you, by them* — the wife and her house standing at contract as *requirement-issuers* over the groom — and the weight-word *ghalīẓ*, thick and grievous, is otherwise reserved in the Book for God's own bonds: the covenant of the prophets (33:7), the covenant of Israel (4:154), the believers' works-side of heaven's contract. Marriage alone among human compactions enters that register — al-Rāzī documenting theologians at this sharpened point: the nikāḥ is *ʿaqd* — binding — not bargain: its desk is the mosque's, its witnesses God's, its unpicking mortally serious. Ibn Kathīr's transmitters give the *mīthāq* its content from the Prophet's own formula in the farewell year's company-instruction: *"You have taken them as a trust from God, and intimacy with them was made lawful to you by the word of God"* — the amānah-word and the kalimah-word together forming, in the scholars' later development, the precise instruments of every nikāḥ-khuṭbah before every qāḍī's assembly to our time: the mannered *ʿaqd* every Muslim still hears read from this verse's lineage: *"give her unto me upon the Book of God and the way of His Messenger."*

**The Three-Sentence Citadel: Vv. 19–21 as the Wife's Bill of Rights**

The cumulative fortification of vv. 19–21 stands measured at its third wall: *consort honorably* (v. 19), *take nothing back though a qintār* (v. 20), *remember what you shared and swore* (v. 21) — the Muslim woman defended at law by affection's memory, by gift's permanence, and by covenant's gravity in one breath. The Prophet's own record in such causes sealed the principle in courtroom-flesh: the reports stand in the books — the accuser-husband of the liʿān cycle asking the restitution of his mahr after the curse-divorce, answered by the verdict he heard rendered from God's message: she keeps it (Ibn Kathīr relates beneath these clauses) — and the new groom whose bride was found gravid by another, likewise fined no recovery of his gift. In all three history-forges the statute holds: the mahr never rides back; the covenant never thins; the *how can you?* of v. 21 remains unanswered by any husband in thirteen centuries of qāḍīs' courts — because no answer could be framed that respects either of the verse's two unanswerable witnesses: the body that was trusted, and the word that was bound.
