## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:7

> **Unto the men a share of what parents and kinsfolk leave, and unto the women a share of what parents and kinsfolk leave, be it little or much—a share ordained.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Umm Kujjah at the Prophet's Door**

The scholars of the occasions of revelation — al-Wāḥidī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Suyūṭī in their registers — carry us to the Prophet's ﷺ assembly for the case that forced the legislation: Aws ibn Thābit al-Anṣārī died in Madinah, leaving a widow, Umm Kujjah, and three small daughters. His two cousins — Suwayd and ʿArfajah, men of the martial generation — took the estate entire, and left the mother and her girls nothing. She came to the Messenger of God ﷺ with her complaint; he said, *"go home until I see what God reveals about you,"* and summoned the two men. They answered with the creed of the old world:

> *"O Messenger of God — her children cannot ride a horse, nor bear arms, nor gain booty!"*

— that is, they are not *heirs*, by the only definition pagan Arabia knew: heirs were those who bled for the tribe. God's answer descended: *li-l-rijāli naṣībun... wa lil-nisāʾi naṣībun* — unto men a share, and unto women a share — and with it the whole cavalry-theory of inheritance fell; the specific apportionment followed in v. 11, and the cousins' temporary possession was reversed by the Prophet's command. In one Madinan morning, the sword forfeited its seat at the probate table.

**Naṣīb: The Appointed Portion as a New Legal Idea**

The word around which the statute turns is *naṣīb* — from the root *n-ṣ-b*, to set up, appoint, fix — a portion *installed* by authority, not wrested by strength. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss is doctrinally exact: a share *mafrūḍ*, made binding by God — the verse's own final word — so that no agreement of the clan, no custom of the century, no affection or aversion of the dying could add or erase an ounce. The weight of this idea upon the history of law can hardly be overstated: inheritance ceases to be the tribe's redistribution of the strong man's gear and becomes a *revealed right* vesting automatically in named persons — in *women* as a listed class, in children, in the old. The sūrah's whole architecture of succession — the science the Muslims would name ʿ*ilm al-farāʾiḍ*, "the knowledge of the ordained portions," from this very verse's *mafrūḍan* — rises on the foundation laid here: the grave opens a court; the will of God is its code; the heirs walk to fixed seats.

**Mā Qalla Minhu aw Kathura: No Estate Too Small for Justice**

*Mimmā taraka al-wālidāni wa-l-aqrabūn mā qalla minhu aw kathura* — "of whatsoever parents and near kin leave, be it little or much": the clause forecloses the lawyer's first loophole before it opens — that trivial legacies fall beneath the law's regard. Al-Qurṭubī's scholars understood: one palm-tree distributes its shares as exactly as one oasis; a widow's needlework inheritance obeys the same fractions as a merchant's caravan-stock. The universality extends equally to the lecturers' other point: neither does greatness exempt — no estate is so vast that the heirs may convene and, by *consensus*, vary the ordinance. The clause thereby teaches the community its first lesson in the precision-fiqh of family property: revelation measures justice not in totals but in *ratios*, and the written shares hold in the cottage as in the castle — the arithmetic of the Unseen falling the same everywhere, the sixth to the mother, the residue to the sons of the house, in every tent from Yathrib's walls to the ends of every later empire.

**Half of Knowledge: The Prophet's Blessing on the Fractions**

The Prophet ﷺ singled out this body of law with an honor he gave few subjects — counting its very transmission among the trusts of the nations:

> *"Learn the ordained shares (al-farāʾiḍ) and teach them; for they are half of knowledge, and they will be forgotten, and they are the first thing that will be taken from my community."* (reported by Ibn Mājah 2719 and al-Ḥākim from Abū Hurayrah and Ibn Masʿūd — much-cited by the jurists as the charter-tradition of the science, though some of its channels were disputed)

— and at his direction the commanders of the early generations set the teaching of the shares in the mosques beside the teaching of the prayer itself. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib would address the people from the pulpit: *"whoever among you can recite the Qur'an, let him recite — and let him learn the laws of inheritance"* (al-Khaṭīb's transmission from the caliphal registers), and ʿUmar's school counted the computist of farāʾiḍ equal to the scholar of memorization. From that pulpit-climate descended the science's wonders: the algebra-works of al-Khwārizmī — the man whose own name became the modern world's word for the algorithm — opening his treatise with the inheritance-problems as their natural first client; the great registers of *farāʾiḍ* rulings beside every qāḍī's seat in every Islamic century; and the quiet persistence, in every Muslim funeral till today, of the distributors working the shares pen-in-hand at the graveside, lest the burial sun set on an unsettled estate.

**And the Women a Share: The First Statute of Female Property in History**

The clause itself is the marvel, read against its century: *wa lil-nisāʾi naṣībun* — "and unto the women is a share." The legal systems of the age — Roman patria potestas, near-eastern custom, Arab tribalism — treated women variously as dependents transmitted *with* estates rather than recipients *of* them; and here, by revealed statute, the female heir walks into fixed, enforceable, unmediated ownership — *her* name, *her* portion, *her* disposal thereafter; for what a woman inherited became, like her dower of v. 4, her sole property, to sell, endow, or bequeath. Al-Qurṭubī's remark captures the verse's placement logic: separated from v. 2's orphan-protection only by the guardianship-chain, the law here places its second fence — the *mothers* of the orphans are themselves propertied, so that widowhood cease to mean destitution. A commentary tradition of women commentators through the ages — from the learned ladies of Ṣafiyyah's circle reading *farāʾiḍ* to the female muftis of later capitals — springs directly from this sentence: the Woman's Charter began, in the Qur'an's own chronology, with the grave and the ledger: *unto the women a share — be it little or much — a share ordained.*
