## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:20

> **If you desire to take one wife in place of another, even if you have given to one of them a great sum, take back nothing from it. Would you take it by way of calumny and manifest sin?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qinṭār: The Hundredweight You Cannot Carry Back**

The law of the irrevocable gift is now fixed at its maximum setting: *wa in aradtum istibdāla zawjin makāna zawjin wa ātaytum iḥdāhunna qinṭāran fa-lā taʾkhudhū minhu shayʾan* — "if you wish to exchange one wife for another, though you had given one of them a *qinṭār*, take not back anything of it." The Quran borrows the weight-word deliberately: *qinṭār*, of foreign extraction, the merchant's hundredweight — commentators calculate it variously between forty and twelve hundred ūqiyyah, and a host of silver over a man's two hands — chosen so that no husband could plead *smallness*: the gifts between man and wife are no reclaimable deposits, once given at any scale. Al-Qurṭubī's axiom from the clause runs into every code of the schools: the bridewealth has *vested*, contract-delivered, remainder a debt divorce cannot void — so that in the lawbooks of every century the mahr stands in the wife's estate ahead of wills, ahead of creditor-jostlings, where even the husband's poverty pleads in vain. The occasion-frame shows the law taming a tactic: the downtrodden "replacement-marriage" circuit — soured husband, fresh bride-interest, engineered recovery of the old's dower as the new's stake — named here and retired at the full force of the concluding clause.

**Calumny and Manifest Sin: The Two Enemies of the Cold Husband**

*A-taʾkhudhūnahu bi-buhtānin wa ithmin mubīn* — "would you take it by calumny and manifest sin?" — and the commentators expose the mechanism: to claw the mahr back lawfully, the husband needed a crime — hence the manufactured one. *Buhtān* — calumny-astonishing, the lie that stuns the hearer, from its root of dumbfounding — is here, per al-Ṭabarī's authorities and al-Qurṭubī's juristic eye, the fabricated charge of the *fāḥishah* or of *nushūz*: accusations alone opening v. 19's little exception-gate; *ithm mubīn* — clear sin — is the rest of the toolkit: cruelty staged so she might *offer* the ransom herself. That the Book should speak in this interrogative-mocking register — *would you take it?!* — seizes every generation's attention from al-Rāzī on: revelation adopts the bench-sarcasm of judges who have watched it all, the crooked petition named before it is filed: *the clause which could legalize the theft is here declared the theft.* And 2:229 — the Book's twin ruling at divorce — completes the statute from the other side, with its khulʿ-mercy alone held out for the *wife's own* unhurried choice: the husband can never even *ask back*; she alone, and only freely, may offer.

**ʿUmar's Pulpit and the Woman Who Knew Her Verse**

The community's proudest vignette of *female* competence in the Book centers precisely on this verse's hundredweight-word, in the story the commentators — Ibn Kathīr first among them — pass down from the days of the Caliph ʿUmar: he stood at his minbar regulating a society swollen with bride-price bargains, and moved to cap the mahr at four hundred dirhams — whereupon a Qurayshī woman got to her feet and recited: *"though you had given one of them a qinṭār — take nothing of it back"* — and added, in a session every century after has quoted: *"Umar, God has given us more and you would lessen? Forbid men from nothing that God has permitted!"* The Caliph's retraction was historic courtroom-dictum:

> *"The woman is right and ʿUmar is wrong — everyone is more knowing than ʿUmar."* (variant readings of the retraction run through the Sunan-compilations and the tafsīr of 4:20, being among the most quoted aṣḥābi-precedents of the shariʿa)

The jurisprudential treasure of the tale — that even a caliph's *public-welfare* ordinance perishes against a woman's recitation of her revealed share — is weighed as heavily by scholars as its social treasure: the first marriage-gift statute of Islam did not merely *defend* the woman's bounty; it *taught her* to defend it, pulpit against pulpit, the Book in her mouth and the Caliph bowing to it.

**Istibdāl Zawj Makāna Zawj: Beneath the Swap-Word, the Whole House**

Beneath the legal surface the verse's own choice of phrase — *istibdāla zawjin makāna zawjin*, "exchanging a spouse in place of a spouse" — exposes the act's deeper nature: the verb *istabdala* is the Book's word for swaps and trade-ups (2:61, 61:11's *do you prefer the world's life?*) — marriage-cycling drawn for once in the idiom of *merchandise* — and legislators in every school have read the noun-swap as the verse's interior rebuke: the renewed bride at the old dowry is a discount-store marriage, and God forbade its discount. The Muslim household has retained the verse's lesson at two poles ever since: men *may* divorce — the gate is not barred — but they may never *mine* a marriage; and a woman's dower is hers as the sight in her eyes is hers — transfer absolute, un-cancelable by his weariness, his newer love, or his economizing intent. The hundredweight the Arabs knew well: God's measure of *irretrievability*. A man delivers it in the wedding — he hears, at the parting, in the same chapter, the only reply of heaven: *take back nothing. Nothing. Though it be a qinṭār.*
