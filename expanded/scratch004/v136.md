## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:136

> **O you who believe! Believe in God and His Messenger, and in the Book which He has sent down upon His Messenger, and in the Book which He sent down aforetime. And whosoever disbelieves in God, and in His angels, and in His books, and in His messengers, and in the Last Day, has indeed gone far astray.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yā Ayyuhā Alladhīna Āmanū... Āminū: "Believe, You Who Believe" — The Command to Be What You Are**

The verse opens with one of the Book's most dislocating vocatives: *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū — āminū* — "O you who believe! — Believe": those already named believers are commanded to believe — an address-form the commentators have pondered from the beginning. Al-Ṭabarī records the first reading historically: the primary addressees were those among the People of the Book of Madīnah who had come to believe in their own prophet(s) but had not yet borne full witness to Muḥammad and his Book — the Book calling them "you who believe" by the belief they legitimately held, and then ordering them to complete the act: believe *in God and His Messenger* — the second belief not a repetition but an arrival: faith's fulfillment in the bearer God now actually addresses them through. Yet the commentators extend the form to every hearer in every generation — al-Rāzī's register gathering the enduring expositions: the command to the believing is the command to *increase* in belief — moving from belief-by-report to belief-by-investigation, from outer assent to the settled certainty the tradition elsewhere names (ʿĀʾishah's standing theology-of-certainty preserved in the tafsīr-collections: her celebrated remark in the tafsīr-collections: "if you knew what I know, you would laugh little and weep much" — certainty arriving as knowledge increases); or — as others phrase it — the command to *hold fast* to belief, renewing it as the standing devotional formula commands: the Prophet's standing exhortation recorded in the Sunan (its standing register in Aḥmad's Musnad and the ḥadīth-collections generally): "renew your faith" — asked, "how do we renew it, O Messenger of God?" — answered: "increase in saying: lā ilāha illā Allāh." Faith in this register is not a past event but a standing ordinance: the believer lives under a present-tense command to keep being what his name declares.

**The Five-Point Archipelago: The Verse's Own Creed-List**

Then the verse erects the five-point schema of īmān — the positive command of two articles (God and His Messenger) doubled into a negative inventory of five: *wa man yakfur bi-llāhi wa malāʾikatihi wa kutubihi wa rusulihi wa al-yawmi al-ākhiri* — "and whosoever disbelieves in God, and His angels, and His books, and His messengers, and the Last Day": the commentators immediately note the architecture — the command names two (God, Messenger) because in them are contained all five, and then the forensic clause enumerates the five because in them sits the definition of kufr — disbelief not as a vague posture but as a breach at enumerated points. The standing Book-register of the list is its own commentary: 2:285 — "the Messenger believes in what has been sent down to him from his Lord — and the believers: each one believes in God and His angels and His books and His messengers — we make no distinction between any of His messengers"; 2:177 — the sūrah of the Cow's standing creed-verse ("righteousness is not that you turn your faces to the east and west, but righteousness is: whoever believes in God and the Last Day and the angels and the Book and the prophets — and gives of his wealth..."); and the sūrah's own later row — 4:150–151 below — where those who "disbelieve in God and His messengers, and wish to make a distinction between God and His messengers, saying: we believe in some and disbelieve in some" are declared "the disbelievers in truth" — the archipelago-principle standing: the articles are inseparable — a broken link voids the chain.

**Al-Wāḥid li-l-Jamīʿ: Gabriel's Question as the Standing Gloss**

The tradition's master-text for this entire lexical territory is the celebrated hadith of Gabriel — the standing source in the two Ṣaḥīḥs (Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8): the stranger with intensely white garments and intensely black hair, sitting knee-to-knee with the Prophet before the Companions, asking: "tell me about faith" — and the Prophet answering with the sixfold formula that glosses this verse forever: "that you believe in God, and His angels, and His meeting, and His messengers, and the Last Day, and that you believe in the decree — its good and its evil" — the present verse's five enlarged to six by the meeting-decree pair, the whole tradition's catechism of islām-īmān-iḥsān descending from that single sitting. The mufassirūn (Ibn Kathīr here as elsewhere) treat the hadith as the verse's living exegesis: belief as *taṣdīq* — inward assent-attestation — not merely lip-service; and belief as one seamless fabric — the angel-belief because the angels are the couriers of revelation; the book-belief because the books are its cargo; the messenger-belief because the messengers are its bearers; the Last Day because it is its horizon — God at the origin and the End, the entire middle furnished by His envoys and their texts.

**Fa-Qad Ḍalla Ḍalālan Baʿīdan: The Far Error and Its Register**

The verse closes with the standing verdict-form for total apostasy: *fa-qad ḍalla ḍalālan baʿīdan* — "has indeed gone far astray": *ḍalāl* — from ḍ-l-l — losing the way, error — with its standing superlative-doublet *baʿīd* — far, remote: error measured by distance from the Road (its standing register: 2:108 — "whosoever exchanges belief for disbelief has strayed from the evenness of the way"; 4:60 above — Satan desiring "to lead them far astray" — ḍalālan baʿīdan — the identical phrase there in Satan's wish, here in its realized state; 4:116 — "whoever associates partners with God has gone far astray"; 4:167 — "those who disbelieve and bar from God's way have gone far astray"; 14:18; 22:12; 34:8; 42:18); and the doubled cognate-accusative (*ḍalla ḍalālan*) intensifying as Arabic intensifies — the act named and then its cognate object naming its kind: not a stumble but an *error-of-the-far-kind*. Ibn Taymiyyah's standing principle orders the scene: since īmān is the condition of every deed's acceptance and every promise's arrival, the public loss announced here is not one item among others but the dissolution of the contract itself — the far error being precisely the stepping outside the archipelago within which alone meaning, covenant, and hope are legally alive. And the verse's zoom-structure teaches its own lesson: the sūrah of the household and the court of justice (vv. 127–135) now anchors both in eternal geography — spouses and orphans, witnesses and judges, all acting inside an īmān whose poles are God and the Last Day — the family-law of the sūrah fenced at each end by the two worlds in which it is lived.
