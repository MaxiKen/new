## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:44

> **Hast thou not considered those who were given a portion of the Book, who purchase error, and wish that you too should stray from the path?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Lam Tara: The Prophet Shown His Opposition**

A new movement within the sūrah opens with the Book's familiar reveille: *alam tara ilā alladhīna ūtū naṣīban mina al-kitāb* — "Hast thou not seen those who were given a portion of the Book?" — *alam tara*, "have you not considered" — a gaze-address to the Prophet ﷺ that initiates the sūrah's long confrontation-sequence with the scripture-holders of Madinah (vv. 44–55: the portion-bearers; then the Torah-quarrel of v. 60–65's arbitration-refusers; and its aftermath through v. 76) — the formula itself belonging to the Qur'an's spectacle-lanterns (its sisters: "Have you not seen your Lord with the shadow" — 25:45; "Have you not seen the ships" — 17:66 and scores more), whereby the recipient is made to *look* at what has been organized before him as evidence. The addressees: *alladhīna ūtū naṣīban mina al-kitāb* — "those given a portion of the Book" — not the whole bearer-class of revelation, but its *share-division*: al-Ṭabarī's polls settle its address upon the Torah-bearing community of the oasis-towns (the Jews of Madinah, no Gentiles then standing in Arabia with comparable Book-claims) — with the *naṣīb*, portion-word itself carrying inside it the criticism to come: a *part* of the heavenly ledger was granted them (and preserved as such — a covenant-asset), but the portion-sense likewise recalls their resistance to anything beyond it — holding to the share they had while declining the Share now revealed upon the Arabian prophet. The commentators well carefully note how gently the campaign opens — no names, no curses: first only *look*.

**Yashtarūna al-Ḍalālah: Error Purchased as Merchandise**

The first charge is delivered in a shop in the Book's fierce mercantile vocabulary: *yashtarūna al-Ḍalālata* — "they purchase error" — the verb *ishtarā* — to buy, exchange-in-sale — the same coin-verb as 2:16 ("they purchased error at the price of guidance — so their commerce profited not") and 3:177 ("whoever bought unbelief for belief has not harmed God at all"): revelation here describing rejection not as blindness but as *a transaction* — the reasoner paying over what he possesses (guidance-offered, scripture-understood) in order to receive *error* as the deliverable. Al-Rāzī's unpacking of the grammar is the standing classical lesson: purchase-language presupposes preference — they *chose* — not ignorance — and thereby ruins the age-old apology of custom ("we found our fathers so!") with which Part-holders everywhere defend themselves (cf. 2:170; 5:104; 31:21); the merchant-metaphor furthermore implies accountability — a bargain consented to, a price reckoned, a receipt held — all the while the exchange is *negative*: gold traded for fog. The sūrah's own immediate context teaches what was purchased: verse-by-verse before this it had legislated justice upon orphans, women, and the hour of testimony — and the portion-bearers' error-purchase is their turning from the same standards to private advantage in the wards of their clans (the verse pairing this conduct, in the next breath, with its wish).

**Wa Yurīdūna an Taḍillū al-Sabīl: The Recruitment-Hatred of the Erring**

The second charge descends from commerce to poaching: *wa yurīdūna an taḍillū al-sabīl* — "and they wish that you too stray from the path": the purchase of error never stays contained — error markets itself: the path-losers wishing the Muslims to lose it likewise (the same social physics the Book maps at 2:109 — "many of the People of the Book wish they could restore you to unbelief after your belief, out of envy from their own souls" — and 4:89's blunt demand within Madinah's hypocrite-ring: *"they wish that you disbelieve as they disbelieve, that you may be all alike"*). Al-Qurṭubī's note on this clause discloses the ancient mechanism the scholars observed at work in the Prophet's own courts and alleys: courteous flattery in public, doctrinal sabotage in private — pointed challenges devised to embarrass the Messenger before converts and waverers, Torah-questions contrived for scandal (cf. their ruses listed in 4:46 and 5:41), the missionary-envy aimed at detaching someone — anyone — from the new fellowship so the portion-bearers' own standing at Arabia's revelation-market might be preserved. The wisdom spiritually, the clause is a principle of all ages: the erring do not rest until the walking have strayed — not from intellectual care but from interior vindication-need; misguidance *hires* company the way guilt does — and believers are thereby warned about the circle a man keeps: the Prophet had already named the rule in the proverb-traditions (Abū Dāwūd 4833 — "a man follows the religion of his intimate; so let each of you look to whom he befriends" — al-Tirmidhī grading its cousin 2378 upon the simile of the musk-carrier and bellows).

**The Placement Beneath v. 43: From Purity to War**

The sūrah's own sequence here deserves its note before the storm grows: after purity-laws (v. 43) and before battle-commands (vv. 71–76 ff.), it plants *this* — the announcement that knowledge-holders themselves have entered error's market and hunt the Muslims' path — so that the two halves of the early Medinese defens e are taught together: *ritual* defense of the worshipping body and *doctrinal* defense of the believing mind; the enemy of the second is first the bought-learned — the portion that turned to merchandising. The Prophet receives the campaign's intel with the gaze-command — *alam tara* — and thereby so does every believer ever after: watch the portion-sellers, read their invoices, refuse their bargains — an for God in the very next verse names their true status — "God knows best your enemies" (v. 45) — and appoints Himself the only patron sufficient for the ones pursued along the path.
