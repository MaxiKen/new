## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:26

> **God desires to make [this] clear unto you, and to guide you to the traditions of those who went before you, and to relent unto you. And God is Knowing, Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yurīdu: The Legislator Who Announces His Desires**

The great marriage-pericope — prohibited degrees, dower-statutes, the slave-bride's charter — closes with a curtain-speech the commentators hold among the tenderest in the codex: *yurīdu Allāhu li-yubayyina lakum wa yahdiyakum sunana alladhīna min qablikum wa yatūba ʿalaykum, wa-Allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm* — "God wills to make clear to you, and to guide you in the ways of those before you, and to relent toward you; and God is Knowing, Wise." The Book rarely unveils the lawmaker's *intention* at the moment of the lawmaking — and here it unveils three, in succession, as lamps over the just-finished statutes: *clarification*, *guidance*, *relenting* — so that the household of faith understands its new laws not as decrees descending from a sealed heaven but as chapters in a companionship the Lawgiver Himself explains. Al-Rāzī marks the stylistic tenderness: the discourse had been *command-and-forbid*; the curtain is *desire-and-promise* — as though God, handing the marriage-map to a nervous community, kneels to say what He was *doing for them* the whole time: unveiling their confusion, walking them down roads walked by prophets before them, and keeping the return-door of repentance behind them at every experiment of their implementation.

**Li-Yubayyina Lakum: Clarification as Worship's First Need**

The first divine desire is clarification — *li-yubayyina lakum* — and the commentators' theology of the clause runs wide: no religion of God has ever asked *blind* obedience — the statutes must be settled first in the understanding (*bayān*), then enacted — with the precise content of the clarification here being the whole just-finished pericope: which women stand prohibited and why, what belongs to the bride and what may be remitted, how the marriage is contracted and how it is honored, how much depends on *consent* and how little on *custom*. Al-Ṭabarī reads *li-yubayyina* — "to make clear" — against the *opposite* competitive practice of revelation's enemies at 3:78 and 2:75–79: God *clarifies*; the corrupting among the ancients *twist*; the community of Muhammad thereby receives its constituting epistemological grace — the Book closed in their hands with nothing reserved for a priestly caste, the degrees of marriage advertised to every suitor, the dower-law placed in the bride's own memory. Clarification-outsourced ends here: henceforth the creed of Islam knows no mystery the layman may not read.

**Sunanan Alladhīna min Qablikum: The Old Roads, Now Lit**

The second desire is guidance into the roads of the ancients — *sunan alladhīna min qablikum* — the trodden ways of the prophets and their faithful before Islam — and the mufassirūn of the early generations read the clause as the marriage-map's *pedigree*: the degrees now forbidden in Madinah were forbidden of old to Abraham's house and the purified among the nations; the the marriage-covenant with its truth-gift descends from the primordial law; so that in receiving vv. 22–25 the Muslims are not enrolled in novelty but *re-enrolled* in an ancient sunnah of God with mankind (7:38's "our wont of those whom We sent before you"; 17:77's "no change in Our wont" — in the sunans of God), and the pagan charge of innovation against the Prophet's community is answered from within its own legislation: *your-forefathers', too, was this constitution; you are but its latest court.* Ibn Kathīr's anchoring of the clause goes to its first-teacher: the prophets' communities are held in v. 26 as one continuous brotherhood walking *one* road of God in institutions and ethics — a claim the chapter will pick up again in its great census of prophets at vv. 163–165 — with the family-law now joined to faith, creed, and monotheism as one unbroken chain.

**Tawbah Behind Every Statute**

The third desire is relenting — *wa yatūba ʿalaykum* — and its placement *behind* the laws is the Book's preventative against the legalistic despair of the sinning implementer: knowing the statutes, these Muslims would break them often enough (vv. 31, 48, 110 below will keep the subject), so God announces the compliance-safety: repentance keeps working during implementation — God's triple intention never forgets its third lamp. Ibn ʿAbbās's celebrated selection — preserved in al-Rāzī's own transmission at this verse — counts vv. 26–28 among the *eight verses of mercy in this sūrah* (with vv. 31, 40, 48, 110, 147) that should be of the Muslim's treasures — evidence the first disciples themselves treated the trilogy as a jewel-engraving on the legislative block. And the attribute-seal — *wa-Allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīman* — crowns the ceremony dogmatically: the Clarifier is Knowing of all the statute-hidden temptations; the Guide is Wise in what He uncovers and what He leaves to reason; the Merciful One has appointed repentance with knowledge of the with knowledge of how much mud the feet that walk His roads will gather. Laws were revealed; the heart of their Reveler is now read — and the Muslim comes out of vv. 22–26 with both in hand: a marriage-map, and a Companion upon it.
