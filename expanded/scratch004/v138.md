## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:138

> **Give glad tidings to the hypocrites that theirs shall be a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bashshiri al-Munāfiqīna: The Glad Tidings That Are Not Glad**

The hypocrisy-dossier's second verse delivers one of the Book's most chilling rhetorical strokes: *bashshiri al-munāfiqīna bi-anna lahum ʿadhāban alīman* — "give glad tidings to the hypocrites that theirs shall be a painful punishment": *bashshir* — the imperative of b-sh-r — the very verb of Gospel good-news (its own standing register across the Book: "We give thee glad tidings of a boy" — 3:45; "give glad tidings to the patient" — 2:155; the word *bushrā* being the standing name of prophetic gladness) — here commandeered into its exact opposite: news of punishment announced in the vocabulary of joy. The commentators recognize the device instantly — the standing Qur'ānic register of ironic mockery: 3:21 — "give those who disbelieve in God's signs and slay the prophets... glad tidings of a painful punishment"; 9:3 — "give glad tidings of a painful punishment to those who disbelieve"; 9:34 — to the gold-and-silver hoarders "give them glad tidings of a painful punishment"; 31:7; 45:8; 84:24 — each time the annunciation-vocabulary announcing doom, as though the Book were saying: you traded the true glad tidings; here are the only tidings your conduct has purchased. Al-Zamakhsharī's literary note marks the sting's exact location: the irony lands hardest on *munāfiqīn* — the hypocrites — because their entire career had been the pursuit of advantageous appearances; in contempt of appearance-worship the Book dresses their doom in their favorite costume: outward good-news wrapping inward ruin — the mirror-image of their lives, which wrapped inward ruin in outward good-news. And the double-edge of the vocative itself: the Prophet is commanded to pronounce the sentence — making the exposure public, stripping the concealment they labored to maintain; the very announcement a punishment before the punishment.

**The Inversion-Logic: Joy-Vocabulary as Final Sarcasm**

The Book elsewhere explains why the guilty receive no glad tidings at all: 25:22 — "the day they see the angels — no glad tidings that day for the guilty" — the Day's joy-vocabulary entirely reserved for the upright; the hypocrites, by contrast, are addressed *in this life* with the glad-word precisely because in the next there is none for them: the wardrobe of celebration lent one final time, parodically, before being closed forever. And the counter-note the commentators add (16:58–59 carrying the standing parallel of impious grief): the pre-Islamic Arabs, receiving the "glad tidings" of a newborn daughter, had their faces darken — the Book wielding the same joy-word to shame the joyless; here the hypocrites, who considered themselves the cleverest joy-brokers of Madīnah, receive their last headlines in the style they always craved — *bashshir* — and the content undoes the word. Al-Biqāʿī's rhetorical tradition gathers the device under standing irony (the standing register of the rhetoricians: the "announcement-irony" — *al-tabshīr al-tahakkumī* — mockery through the form of felicitation).

**ʿAdhābun Alīm: The Painful Punishment**

The content of the faux-tidings is stated plainly: *ʿadhāban alīman* — "a painful punishment": *alīm* — from ʾ-l-m — pain: the standing punishment-adjective across the Book (its register in this sūrah alone: 4:18; 4:161 below; the several standing stations throughout) — and its descriptive minimalism deliberate: no detail of kind or duration — the darkness of the unspecified letting the horror do its own work, the standing method of the Book's briefest doom-verses. Yet the commentators locate its reality precisely within this sūrah's own map: v. 145 below will name the destination — "verily the hypocrites are in the lowest depth of the Fire" — *al-dark al-asfal* — the basement-basement beneath even the open disbelievers — the hypocrites outranked in punishment by those they privately agreed with, because they added to unbelief the artistry of pretense. And the place of all this within the chapter-structure: v. 137 diagnosed the oscillation; v. 138 announces the wages; v. 139 will expose the false alliances that the hypocritical heart arranges as insurance — taking disbelievers as protectors "instead of the believers" — the glad tidings of v. 138 therefore read as the answer to the insurance-policy of v. 139 before it is even described: insure yourself with whomever you like — your underwriter has already announced His settlement. The spiritual commentators (Ibn ʿAjībah's standing inward register) extend the application to every pretense-managing heart: every soul that maintains a public face in commerce with God while banking a private face against Him lives inside this verse's irony — receiving good news daily (health, provision, reputation) while the account being settled is of another kind. The verse, six words long, thereby stands as one of the Book's purest exhibitions of style-as-judgment: the sentence itself is the sentence.
