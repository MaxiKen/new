## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:30

> **And whosoever does that in enmity and injustice, We shall cause him to burn in a Fire; and that is easy for God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa Man Yafʿal Dhālika: The Sentence Clause That Follows the Mercy**

The law of v. 29 — wealth not consumed falsely, souls not slain — ends in that verse's astonishing mercy-seal, *God is ever Merciful toward you*; and the very next sentence stands in the deliberate anatomy of legislation: *wa man yafʿal dhālika ʿudwānan wa ẓulman fa-sawfa nuṣlīhi nārā* — "whoever does that, in enmity and injustice, We shall roast him in a Fire": the promise of mercy guarded by the sword-edge of threat, so that the lightened law of v. 28 would never be read as fiction or the mercy of v. 29 as license. Al-Ṭabarī's register of the pronoun *dhālika* — "that" — is decisive for the whole juristic architecture: it reaches back to the verse's *two* great prohibitions together — the false-eating of property and the slaying of souls — and many commentators extend it further to *the sum of the established forbiddance in the pericope* (v. 29's two majors, with the orphan-embezzlement and indecency chapters still neighbor-standing) — the verse thus functioning as the seal of penalty upon the entire first legislative slab of the sūrah, from orphan to marketplace-marriage, an architecture no attentive reciter can miss.

**ʿUdwānan wa Ẓulman: The Pair That Doubles the Indictment**

The two accusatives of manner — *ʿudwānan wa ẓulman* — "in enmity and injustice" — rank among the Book's formula-pairs of aggravated guilt, and the commentators parse the doubling with the precision its Lawgiver demands: *ʿudwān*, enmity-transgression — overstepping into the property and blood that another also breathes behind, the trespass *against the neighbor*; *ẓulm*, injustice — the misplacement of thing from its right, trespass *against the order* — so that the verse damns the deed under two arraignments: aggression against persons, disorder against God. Al-Rāzī's reading of the pair is worth its courtroom care: *ʿudwān* is the *intention-in-motion* — the hostile spirit that *meant* the crossing — *ẓulm* the deed *as legally misordered* — so that no defense survives the pair: neither "I never meant" (the enmity supplies the meaning), nor "it was accidental" (the injustice fixes the deed). The commentators of the occasions found the pair exact to the first audience's behavior — the the Ansari faction-disputes and the Jāhiliyya blood-tempers, wealth-grudges waked at every distribution — the verse teaching them in one breath: God seals His law's door with the the with both the motive and the measure.

**Sa-Nuṣlīhi Nāran: The Fire-Clause of Specific Application**

*Fa-sawfa nuṣlīhi nāran* — "We shall broil him in Fire" — the verb *aṣlā*, to roast against flame-direct, from the same image-set as v. 10's orphan-eaters' broiling and 4:56's skins renewed for flaming — chosen for the sharpest tactile force: not merely entering fire but roasting at it — the verb of spit-contact that nothing in the Book mitigates afterward. And the the law's wisdom in appending the clause just here, the commentators of the schools observed through generations: *threats of specific fire-clauses attach traditionally to gravest sins* — Ibn ʿAbbās's kabaʾir-counting principle stands this way: whatever God threatened with His fire, cursed, or apportioned a fixed doom, belongs to the majors — so that v. 30 itself is listed among the evidences the early doctors used to catalog the kabāʾir: consuming-by-fraud, slaying, and orphan-devouring all grade within the seal's range.

**Wa Kāna Dhālika ʿAlā Allāhi Yasīran: The Ease of Omnipotence as Deterrent**

The verse's darkness-clause closes at last on the terrifying theology of *ease*: *wa kāna dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīrā* — "and that, for God, is easy": the deterrent-word of the Book's God-address to the armed and the confident: your walls, your cash, your alliances shall not add one atom to the difficulty of your broiling — the doing is *yasīr*, light — and the righteousness-minded commentators (al-Qurṭubī pre-eminently here) hear the clause's counter-melody for the believing pair of ears: *God's ease is also in the reward-gate* — for the faithful guardian and honest trader, heaven is equally effort-free for Him — the chapter's fire-reference recurring at v. 55's *kafā*, where the *sufficiency of Hell* balances balances ablangs of threat and refuge. The Muslim reader exits vv. 29–31's trinity as from a courtroom where all three words were spoken: *fear*, *mercy*, *appointment* — and the statute-table of verses 14–31 stands sealed at its pivot-point: wealth's lanes fenced, lives inviolate, each sealed by broiling and forgiveness — and the hearer relieved into the paired safer door that opens immediately after: the absolution-clause of v. 31, the very next recited sentence, whereby *avoid, and be forgiven* — mercy as always stationed nearest the threat.
