## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:4

> **And give the women their bridewealth as a free gift, but if they are pleased to remit unto you any part thereof, then consume it with wholesome enjoyment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ṣaduqāt: The Gift Called Truth**

*Wa ātu al-nisāʾa ṣaduqātihinna niḥlah* — "and give the women their bridewealth as a free gift": the Book names the dower *ṣadūqāt* (singular *ṣadūqah* or *ṣidāq*) from the root *ṣ-d-q*, truth and sincerity — a bride-gift stamped by its very name as the *proof* of a covenant-claimant's sincerity, not a purchase-price nor a toll. Al-Qurṭubī lingers on the fit between name and thing: the Arabs said *ṣadaqa al-khāṭib* for the suitor who honored his proposal with substance — and the Qur'an ratified the idiom into statute, making the gift the husband's first installment of truth in the marriage-files. And *niḥlah* — the glossators dispute its etymology between *naḥl*, unconditioned largesse, and the bee's poured-out honey — fixes the manner: given outright, without strings, without "loan" or "return on divorce," a transfer of ownership as clean as the word itself. The Book's choice of *give* (*ātū*) against Jāhiliyya's vocabulary of buying and selling announces the reclassification: the woman is no chattel priced between men; the gift is *hers*, flowing past her father, past her warden, to her own hand.

**Breaking the Chain of the Fathers**

For the statute abolished an economy: in pre-Islamic Arabia the *mahr* of daughters and wards was customarily pocketed by guardians as a house-revenue, and the *shighār* marriage — exchange of daughters between two men in lieu of any dowry at all — had built the practice into a system, which the Prophet ﷺ struck with a single prohibition:

> *"There is no shighār in Islam"* — the shighār being that a man marries his daughter to a man on condition the other marries him his daughter, with no bridal gift between them (al-Bukhārī 5112; Muslim 1415, from Ibn ʿUmar).

Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī enumerate the abolished forms re-appearing in later ages under new etiquette — the guardian taking his daughter's mahr "for her own keeping," the uncle marrying off the orphan for her gift, the father-in-law consuming the payment as "compensation for raising her" — and the verse's own sealings forbid them all, with v. 19 and v. 20 soon reinforcing its fortress from two more sides. Under heaven's charter, title vests in the bride at contract; her lien against a deferred remainder ranks before the husband's estate's other debts.

**Ṭibna Nafsan: The Soul's Signature on Every Remission**

The verse's second clause governs the delicate case of the bride's own generosity: *fa-in ṭibna lakum ʿan shayʾin minhu nafsan fa-kulūhu hanīʾan marīʾ* — "if they are pleased to remit to you any of it, with a willing soul, then consume it in wholesome enjoyment." Three verbs and two adverbs stand guard around her consent: *ṭāba* — to be goodly-willing at heart's ease; *nafsan* — the accusative of state, "with her soul" — stipulating that the soul itself sign the waiver, not the browbeaten face; and the paired *hanīʾan marīʾan* — roots of sweet digestion and nourishment — the Book's own culinary idiom for lawfulness enjoyed without hidden rot (as at 2:168's ḥalālan ṭayyibā). Al-Qurṭubī records the juristic distillate: the remission extracted by shame, family pressure, or the husband's sulk is *null beneath the verse's condition* — and judges have annulled waiver-receipts signed under the father-in-law's roof ever since. God's registry refuses phantom gifts, for the same Watcher of v. 1 stands over the signing.

**The Dower's Measure: Between Extravagance and Miserliness**

How much, then? The Prophet's own household marked the middle path: the mahr of his daughters and most of his wives stood near five hundred dirhams — the silver of a working man's year — and he would bless the contracted pairs with *"may God bless you both, and bless upon you, and unite you in good"* (Abū Dāwūd 2130; al-Tirmidhī 1091), calling likewise for the feast of walīmah even with a single sheep (al-Bukhārī 5167; Muslim 1427). Yet he refused to fence out the poor: when a suitor owned nothing — not even an iron ring — he accepted the farthest descent of the scale:

> *"Seek something, though it be an iron ring."* He could find none; so the Prophet ﷺ said: *"What have you of the Qur'an?"* He named certain sūrahs. *"I marry her to you for what you have of the Qur'an"* — that he should teach it to her (al-Bukhārī 5149; Muslim 1425, from Sahl ibn Saʿd).

Against the other extreme, ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb's platform from the caliphal pulpit — "do not overmultiply the women's bridewealth, for if it were an honor in this world or taqwā with God, the Prophet ﷺ would have been first to it" (al-Tirmidhī 1114; Abū Dāwūd 2106 — and the report continues with the woman who answered him with 4:20's *qintār*, whereupon he conceded: "the woman is right and ʿUmar is wrong") — fixed the jurisprudential prudence of the community: neither impoverishing suitors nor selling daughters, the gift measured by *ability, custom, dignity* — but given, in every case, as *niḥlah*: a free truth-token into the bride's own treasury, written in the Book beside the orphan's vault and the widow's share, within the sūrah's uninterrupted argument: *the weakest signature in the marriage-file shall be guarded by the strongest seal in the heavens.*
