## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:31

> **If you shun the grave sins that you are forbidden, We shall absolve you of your evil deeds and cause you to enter at a noble gate.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kabāʾir and Sayyiʾāt: The Two Ledgers of the Record-Books**

The adjacent-threat of v. 30 now gives way to one of the Muslim's standing promises-of-relief, and the first sister in Ibn ʿAbbās's canon of this sūrah's eight mercy-verses — *in tajtanibū kabāʾira mā tunhawna ʿanhu nukaffir ʿankum sayyiʾātikum wa nudkhilkum mudkhalan karīmā* — "if you shun the major sins you have been forbidden, We shall absolve you of your evil deeds and enter you by a noble entrance": the Book holding out, to the community it has just finished fencing around with fire-clauses, a treaty-structure of staggering generosity: **avoid the majors, and the minors are struck off**. The commentators at once set about *defining the registers*, for the Book nowhere prints the list — and the tradition's reportage here forms its own garden of studying: al-Ṭabarī records the Companion-generations' differing counts — ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib's list of seven (ascribing partners to God; killing the soul unlawfully; slandering the chaste; consuming the orphan's wealth; usury; fleeing the battlefield; and the peculiar item *returning to Bedouinry after the settlement*, seeing the settled Muslim life as covenant) — with variants adding false testimony, undutifulness to parents, despairing of God's mercy, sorcery; and the Prophet's own census, kept in the two Sittings: the four-catalogue *"the greatest of the great sins (akbar al-kabāʾir): associating with God, undutifulness to parents, killing the soul, and the false oath (al-yamīn al-ghamūs)"* (al-Bukhārī 2511, 6675 many; Muslim 87) beside the and the seven-destroyers canon (mūbiqāt: shirk, sorcery, soul, ribā, orphan, flight, slander — al-Bukhārī 2766; Muslim 89 — attached above at v. 2). The line of doctrine the schools then settled: majors are *whatever God has attached a fixed ḥadd, a threat of fire, a curse, or a closing of the gates* — the sayyiʾāt, the slips-of-day, the lapses of temper and eye — with the with Ibn ʿAbbās' own memorial word giving the believer his perpetual crowbar: *"no sin is minor while persisted in; and no sin is major with repentance following."*

**Nukaffir ʿAnkum: Expiation as the Book's Weight-Word**

The promise-verb — *nukaffir* — is from the Book's great root *k-f-r*: *kaffāra*, the covering-over, erasure — God's own verb for washed guilt — and its role in the verse is structural: the absolution is an *act of God upon the ledger* (cf. 3:193's "expunge our sins"; 2:271's "and expiation of your sins" for open giving; 2:271-2's word) — not the minor-sinner's flimsy earning but a *divine bookkeeping-off*, which is why many commentators — with the deep law-mind al-Qurṭubī on the clauses — taught it as *by His bounty*, the avoidance-of-majors itself no equivalence of worth but *a condition He honored*, like the ḥadīth's prayer-promise it echoes full-circle with echoes full-formed in the Prophet's own mosque-prophesy — Prime among its ḥadīth-sisters:

> *"The five prayers, and Friday to Friday, and Ramadan to Ramadan, are expiations (kaffārah) for what is between them — so long as the major sins are avoided."* (Muslim 233, from Abū Hurayrah — same covenant-formula, same condition)

so that v. 31 is the treaty-text and the several-fasts-and-prayers canons are its named instruments: expiation *runs continuously* through the believing week upon the one stipulation the verse seals in bold — *kabāʾir abstained*.

**Mudkhalan Karīman: The Entrance as Honor-Gift**

Then the clause of destination — *wa nudkhilkum mudkhalan karīman* — "We shall enter you by a noble entrance": the phrase is read in the text's own two channels, both drawn by the commentators forward into Paradise: *mudkhal* as *the gate of entrance* — the gate described as noble: dignified, untroubled filing past the benches of reproach into the Mansions — and as *the entry-state itself* — an arriving honored and unblamed. Both mean one beatitude: the abstainer-of-majors not squeaking past the border but *welcomed* — and the angel-walī testimony from al-Bukhārī's and Muslim's hadiths of arrival — "peace upon you; you were pure; so enter it, abiding" (39:73's peer-verse) — is the day-scene the verse promises the steady. The theologians of the mercy-schools (al-Rāzī the very chapter here) guard carefully the guard carefully the non-arithmetic of the covenant: not that avoidance *buys* the minors — for God's forgiveness is never barter — but that the Lord of the ledger *honors the will of avoidance with the covering of the rest*: *faḍl*, bounty, from start to finish.

**Between Despair and Insolence: Where the Muslim's Fiqh-of-Heart Lives**

The verse's design teaches the believer exactly where to stand: *above despair* (the minors are manageable; God carries them), *below insolence* (the majors are non-negotiable; you have been warned) — the standing-position of the middle community. The Sufi commentators, whose tradition runs long at this clause (al-Qushayrī and the rest of the older masters), carried the clause one layer deeper: the ultimate *kabīrah* is whatever veils from God — shirk's last dress being the self-regarding soul — and the Rābiʿian maxim sounding beneath it: *"your own existence is a sin beside which no sin compares"* — hyperbole of the gnostics, not law — while the street-Qur'an of the verse stays at the register named: grave sins avoided, misdeeds expunged, noble entry achieved. The sūrah from this point moves to internal disputes of roles (vv. 32–36), but the mercy-keystone now stands inspected: the whole of vv. 1–30's severe law closes at v. 31 with an *exit-hall* — the ummah walks into the remaining statutes knowing that the ledger it carries can be washed weekly, upon one condition the Lawgiver never blurred: *the majors are not for you.*
