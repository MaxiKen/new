## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:19

> **O you who believe! It is not lawful for you to inherit women through coercion, nor to prevent them from marrying [again], that you may take away some of what you have given them, unless they commit a flagrant indecency. And consort with them in a kind and honorable way; for if you dislike them, it may be that you dislike a thing in which God has placed much good.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Stepson Who Draped His Cloak: The End of Wife-Inheritance**

The verse struck at one of pagan Arabia's strangest customs, and the reports of its occasion — preserved by al-Ṭabarī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī in their several chains — bring it before us: a Madinan man died; his stepson walked to his stepmother — the famous case attaching to the household of Abū Qays ibn al-Aslat's son — and threw his cloak over her, the tribe's own ceremony of claim: *by this draping I inherit her as I inherit the camels.* Thereafter she was his chattel: married to him unconsulted, or married off at bridewealth he consumed, or confined a widow till death so that her wealth fall where he wished. And the woman came to the Messenger of God ﷺ: *"I am inherited, Messenger of God — neither married nor left alone."* Heaven answered: *lā yaḥillu lakum an tarithū al-nisāʾa karhan* — "it is not lawful for you to inherit women *against their will.*" Ibn Kathīr takes the clause's full measure: the verse abolishes at a stroke the woman-as-estate — with one clause transferring her from the *inventory* of inheritance to its *registry of heirs*, as vv. 11–12 have since sealed: she is seated at the table now, no longer laid upon it. The *karhan* — by coercion — is itself a second teaching: where heiresses' consent might have sanctified *nothing* in the old law, in Islam marriage never exits consent's door — the Prophet's own documentation standing in the celebrated report of Khansāʾ bint Khidhām, given by her father against her liking, whereupon the marriage was invalidated and the maxim descended to the entire law: *"the previously-married woman has more right to herself than her guardian"* (al-Bukhārī 5138; Muslim 1421 — from Ibn ʿAbbās, who told of the case directly).

**ʿAḍūl and Mīmār: The Two Ways of Stealing Back a Gift**

The statute's second arm — *wa lā taʿḍulūhunna li-tadhhabū bi-baʿḍi mā ātaytumūhunna* — "nor bar them [from remarriage] in order to take back part of what you gave them" — forges the two locks of the ancient dowry-theft. *Taʿḍilūhinn* — the verb of obstruction — names the Jāhili weapon exactly: the heir barring the widow's remarriage until she ransom her freedom from her own dower; and the companion-form, the husband's *hostility-tactic*: the man who sours against a wife — beating, starving, humiliating — not to divorce but to *force her hand* at the return of her bridewealth for release. The exception-gate — *illā an yaʾtīna bi-fāḥishatin mubayyinatin* — "unless they come with a flagrant indecency" — the commentators distribute with care: for those who read *fāḥishah* as adultery, no gift-recovery is re-licensed thereby, since the public ḥadd answers the public wrong; for those receiving *nushūz* — the wife's declared defiance, perpetual abandonment — the clause opens the road to the khulʿ proceeding of 2:229, in which *she*, uninjured yet seeking release, may buy her exit with what she freely returns. Al-Qurṭubī's juristic sum: no taking-back may begin in a husband's force — the one legal channel is her un-coerced wish.

**ʿĀshirūhunna bi-l-Maʿrūf: Companionship as Worship-Grade Conduct**

Then the verse's positive heart, which generations of preachers have made the charter-verse of Muslim marriage: *wa ʿāshirūhunna bi-l-maʿrūf* — "and consort with them in the honorable way": *ʿashara* — to consort, to keep daily company, from the root of *ʿāshirah*, intimate clan — names marriage not as contract-law but as *companionship*, a shared living; and *bi-l-maʿrūf* — by the known-and-recognized good —delegates its content to the moral conscience of every age and house while setting its direction infallibly. The commentators fill the clause with the Prophet's own household as its prime gloss — he ﷺ the "best of you to his wives, and I am the best of you to my wives" (al-Tirmidhī 3895, ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ) — serving in his house, mending, listening long into night at Ḥudaybiyyah-eve to ʿĀʾishah's tale of the eleven women, racing with her on the road. From God-and-angel-witnessed sermons the same command went down the Farewell Pilgrimage's standing tiers: *"Fear God concerning women! You have taken them as a trust from God... and they have rights over you that you feed them and clothe them honorably"* (Muslim 1218, the ḥajjat al-wadāʿ sermon-text) — and in the equity-counsel preserved against the strong-willed: *"the best of you is the best to his family."* Muʿāsharah bi-l-maʿrūf is thus no decorative mercy but the household's own *act of worship*, the dawning discipline by which the sūrah's whole women's-charter becomes a lived interior.

**ʿAsā an Takrahū Shayʾan: The Inscrutable Good Hidden in Dislike**

The verse seals with one of the Book's deepest counsels on endurance, repeated to warriors at 2:216 and now issued to husbands: *fa-in karihtumūhunna fa-ʿasā an takrahū shayʾan wa yajʿala Allāhu fīhi khayran kathīrā* — "if you dislike them, it may be you dislike a thing in which God has placed much good." Al-Rāzī loves the clause's psychology: the dislike here is the uncaused kind — no cruelty, no defect — the itch of *nafs* before God's verdict has matured; the command stands against divorcing-in-haste against v. 20's own calumny-trap; and the consolation-word *ʿasā* — "perhaps, it may be" — in God's dialect is near-promise: *he waits, and finds the good.* The school of patience drew from the clause its marriage-rule of thumb: the unblamed wife often bears children who become blessing, supports at hours history later reveals, builds patience itself — the patience of the #1 household virtue — and that houses survived by one partner's forbearance fill God's reading with *khayran kathīran*, much good. The whole verse thus runs the gamut of a woman's passage: no inheriting of her; no obstructing of her; no clawback of her gift; companionship with her in kindness; and in the last refinement — even where nature recoils — a standing invitation to *look again at what God has hidden in the disliked*, the same surprise reserved for every saddled patience in the Book: *"bear patiently, that God may bring after the hardship ease"*.
