## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:75

> **And what ails you that you fight not in the way of God, and for the weak and oppressed — men, women, and children — who cry out, "Our Lord! Bring us forth from this town whose people are oppressors, and appoint for us from Thee a protector, and appoint for us from Thee a helper."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa Mā Lakum Lā Tuqātilūna: The Indignation-Question of Heaven**

The battle-block pivots from the tarriers' self-interest to the oppressed's cry — and opens with the Book's indignant interrogative: *wa mā lakum lā tuqātilūna fī sabīli Allāhi wa al-mustaḍʿafīna mina al-rijāli wa al-nisāʾi wa al-wildān* — "and what is with you, that you fight not in the way of God and for the oppressed — of men, women, and children?": *wa mā lakum* — what ails you! — the interrogative of astonished reproach? of softened indignation (its rank among the Book's wake-up formats: 22:46's "have they not hearts to understand", most poignant being its own 9:38's exact pairing — *"what ails you that when it is said: march in the way of God, you sink heavily to the earth"* — with al-Rāzī's reading out of it the standing rule-on? out of it the standing rule: *hesitation invited into the heart becomes the resisting? becomes the inhabitant of the next the heart grows lounges takes slack for its nature — so heaven keeps posing the question until the slack is ashamed) — and the object of the fighting is doubled by *wa*: *in the way of God* AND *for the oppressed* — two causes grammatically joined (the commentators' standing jurisprudence-note, al-Qurṛṭubī's spearhead? nj. — anchor: here is one of the Book's clearest legitimations of war *for third parties* — combat in defense not of the Self but of wronged others: the *mustaḍʿaf* not being able to defend themselves, the strong-arm the able-bodied ummah is drafted on their account) — war-theology thereby emerging from its deepest ethical root: *relief of the oppressed.*

**Al-Mustaḍʿafīn: The Voiceless Three Named — Men, Women, Children**

Who are the named classes? *al-mustaḍʿafīn*, from *ḍuʿf*, weakness — the made-weak, the the stripped-of-power — imper Islam's own political social category for the chronically overpowered (its twin-family across the Book: 4:97–98 below, where some excuse their staying some excuse their ignorance of religion by *kunna mustaḍʿafīna*: the earth was spacious for your emigration; 28:4–5's revolutionary charter for the down-trodden of Pharaoh-earth "We wished to favor the oppressed in the land and make them leaders and heirs"; with its with its inner-s īrah anchorage in Mecca's suffering underclass: slaves like Bilāl and Khabbāb, houseless women, disowned converts) — and the enumeration *mina al-rijāli wa al-nisāʾi wa al-wildān* — of men, women, and children — is the commentators' own pointing of the extent of Meccan cruelty (al-Rāzī's and al-Zamakhsharī's registers: children specifically included *because Quraysh tortured children* to break parents' faith — so heaven's cause-list excludes the emptiness ascends makes children a casus bellorum beyond the ordinary), with the historical reports the asbāb-roll nourishes bearing the verse's own self-implicating descendants: Ibn Ibn ʿAbbās recounting that he and his mother were among the *mustaḍʿafūn* of Mecca — he a child of the subjugated class — his own later his own after-honor forever tethered thus to the verse (al-Bukhārī's the biography-record of him simply saying: I was of those referred to — the excused-of v. 98) — the whole ummah thereby learning its first great lesson in solidarity: the weak, if they cannot fight for themselves, *are the cause for which the strong fight*.

**Rabbanā Akhrijnā min Hādhihi al-Qaryati al-Ẓālimi Ahluhā: The Prayer Forged in Mecca**

The quoted cry itself is one of the most forlorn passages in the Book: *"Our Lord! Bring us forth from this town whose people are oppressors"* — *akhrijnā* — bring us out — the plea for exit from a town not named yet unmistakable: Mecca — house of the first sanctuary and the present oppressors — the the commentary-lineage (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṛṭubī's register — sustained by later asbāb-collections) reading the verse against the post-hijrah interlude: believers slow of the emigrants long-gone, but a remnant still trapped — kin-dragged, tortured, some hostage-converts on the Qurayshian leash — whose prayer the Book here immortalizes; and its two twofold petitions ran always as the formula of the persecuted-ever-after: *wa-jʿal lanā min ladunka walīyan* — appoint for us from Thyself a *walī* — protector-guardian — *wa-jʿal lanā min ladunka naṣīran* — appoint for us from Thyself a *naṣīr* — helper: the doubled *min ladunka* — from Thy own Presence — the ladunnī-preposition repeating the nearness-token the sūrah already struck at v. 54 at vv. 67's *min ladunnā* — protection and victory sought not from neutral providence but from God's own side — the whole pericope bracketed by the very walī/naṣīr pair of v. 45 (God suffices as Protector, God suffices as Helper) answered now at street level: *the oppressed prayed the pair; the faithful army was sent as its answer as its embodiment* — fulfillment arriving at the conquest of Mecca — the classical chain noting the Prophet's post-conquest commission of ʿAttāb ibn Asīd the young ʿAttāb ibn Asīd installed as its governor — and, in the commentaries' frame, the day answering their prayer forever: the town whose people were oppressors became the very garrison of God's religion.

**The Standing Law: The Oppressed's Prayer and the Believer's Obligation**

The verse thereby established in the ummah's conscience a two-part order that outlasts Mecca (the commentators and preachers' standing twin-summary): (1) *oppression anywhere recruits the believers to its side under the sabīl-sub-clause* — the war-justification clause treasure valued by al-Qurṛṭubī's school precisely as the mandate of *defensive-solidarity* war — the strong may not salt may not stay safely home while the wordless wordless of their own communion are crushed (the Book's twin roots with the twin pillar of asylum at vv. 97–100 below closing the alternative avenue — him who *can* migrate should; the escaping and him who fights, fights *for* them) — and (2) *the oppressed's prayer is itself a weapon heaven receives* — the whole asbāb-history proving it: they could not rescue themselves, so they prayed — and God drafted a nation to liberate them — with the Tradition lodging the theological weight of such prayer in its highest registers:

> *"Fear the prayer of the oppressed, for there is no barrier between it and God."* (al-Bukhārī 2448; Muslim 19)

— no veil on that line — the mighty sitting therefore in double fear: his own prayers may queue; the wronged man's does not — and the believer surveying the verse is left with its daily audit: *whose mustaḍʿaf-neighborhood do I keep walking past while my hands are strong?* — for the question-wah the question posed to the the question heaven kept posing to the first community never stopped being asked: *what ails you that you do not move for the oppressed of your own town — men, women, and children — who still cry: Our Lord, appoint for us from Thyself a protector, and appoint for us from Thyself a helper?*
