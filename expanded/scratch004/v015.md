## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:15

> **As for those of your women who commit an indecency, call four witnesses among you to bear witness against them. And if they bear witness, then confine them to their houses until death takes them, or until God appoints for them another way.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fāḥishah: The Named and the Unnamed**

The sūrah's journey from the graves of property-law to the bedrooms of chastity-law begins with the Book's grave-euphemism: *fāḥishah* — indecency, monstrosity, from the root of the *fāḥish*: the act that exceeds all bounds of the seemly. The transmitted consensus of every school names it precisely, as Ibn Kathīr records from the first exegetes: *zinā* — adultery and fornication — the sin the Book assaults with the peculiar ferocity of a legislator defending two sanctities at once: the bed and the pedigree, since by this one breach both the spouses' covenant and the children's lineage are effaced. The community's memory has preserved the Prophet's own ranking of the crime among the destroyers — his list of the seven mortal sins binds *zinā with the neighbor's wife* beside idolatry and murder (al-Bukhārī 2766; Muslim 89) — and among the three at whose account there is no chance of acceptance, his searching of the ummah's conscience: "three with whom God will not speak on the Day of Resurrection... nor purify them: the aged adulterer, the lying king, and the arrogant poor man" (Muslim 107). In the present verse the assault begins with the process of proof — and proof here is armor for the town, not artillery against the woman.

**Four Witnesses: The Near-Impossible Standard**

*Fa-stashhidū ʿalayhinna arbaʿatan minkum* — "and summon against them four witnesses from among you": four — not two, not three — eyewitnesses to the act itself, in a unanimity no other crime in the Book requires. Al-Qurṭubī's jurist-reasoning: the legislator thereby armored domestic rumor, woman's honor, and the household's peace simultaneously — for adultery, the injury that unbloods the most households, is also the charge most ruinously cheap to allege. Islam's law made the charge *expensive*: eyewitness-quartet or nothing, with 24:4's complement striking the other side — the unproven *accuser* is himself flogged eighty and his testimony voided forever: *"those who accuse chaste women, then do not bring four witnesses — flog them with eighty lashes, and do not accept their testimony ever after."* The Prophet's own invocation in the hadith that defines the standard confronts the accruer's hunger plainly, Māʿiz's case being the great witness alongside: Māʿiz ibn Mālik came confessing, again and again, the Prophet ﷺ turning away from him, sending him back — "perhaps you only kissed... perhaps you only touched" — until the fourth time of insistence, and then only upon confession unforced (al-Bukhārī 6824; Muslim 1691). Proof of dishonor, in this law, is walled about with the utmost difficulty; confession is refused thrice before heard — for what is at issue is not the sin's excuse but *its holiness-guard* of the social fabric: God veils; the fourfold door stands between His veiling and men's exposure.

**Confinement Until Death: The First Ruling, Meant to Be Replaced**

The first-appointed consequence is domestic: *fa-amsikūhunna fī al-buyūti ḥattā yatawaffāhunna al-mawtu aw yajʿala Allāhu lahunna sabīlā* — "confine them in houses until death takes them, or God appoints for them a way." The clause holds, inside itself, the philosophy of gradual law, which the commentators — Ibn Kathīr with his usual clarity — draw in two strokes: (1) Islam found chastity-law in a society to whom its stringency was unknown; the discipline began with confinement matched to no public execution, no bloodstream — the sinner *warehoused* until straightening, until God's "other way"; (2) and that *way* arrived on the Prophet's own lips, in the celebrated subscription through ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit:

> *"Take from me, take from me: God has now appointed a way for them: the unmarried with the unmarried — a hundred lashes and exile for a year; and the married with the married — a hundred lashes and stoning."* (Muslim 1690; al-Bukhārī 6827 — at whose wording jurists founded the ḥadd of 24:2 in the Book and the stoning in the Sunna)

— *"a way"* the earlier verse had left deliberately unnamed. The verse is thus read today, across the entire tradition, as preserved text and archived ruling — its recitation cautioning ages how mercy-of-process is *had while waiting for justice*: the household's honor was repaired domestically first; the state's sword-arm unsheathed only at the last revelation.
