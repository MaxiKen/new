## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:5

> **And give not the feeble-minded your property, which God has placed in your hands to manage, but provide for them and clothe them from it, and speak unto them in an honorable way.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sufahāʾ: Incompetence as a Legal Category**

After the orphan and the bride, the sūrah's guardianship-litany reaches its third protected class: *al-sufahāʾ* — the feeble-minded, from the root *s-f-h*, lightness, the lightness of a judgment that cannot hold weight. The Arabs used *safīh* for the man of unstable reason, and the revealed law adopted the idiom as a precise juristic category: the person incapable of preserving property — the child before discernment, the insane, the chronically profligate who consumes capital as though it were appetite — is placed under *ḥajr*, interdiction, his signature suspended to spare him and society his own hand. The learned disciples of Ibn ʿAbbās transmitted his wider flavor of the term — every persistent sinner has a *safah* of soul — yet the verse's legal register is firm, and al-Qurṭubī polices its boundaries with particular care against the misreading that would enroll *women* among the sufahāʾ: the noun stands in the masculine plural covering both sexes, and the sūrah itself has already commanded that women and orphans be *given* their property (vv. 2, 4); Islamic law vests women with complete financial competence in dower, inheritance, gift, and earnings. The incapacity intended is demonstrable, functional, and individual — not assigned by gender or class, but proven by conduct, exactly as the courts of Islam have administered it, from the interdiction registers of Kūfa to the hospital-adjacent guardianships of our own day.

**Amwālakum Allatī Jaʿalahā Allāhu Lakum Qiyāman: Property as Standing-Provision**

*Wa lā tuʾtu al-sufahāʾa amwālakumu allatī jaʿala Allāhu lakum qiyāman* — "and give not the feeble-minded your property, which God has made for you a means of standing": the possessive *your* with money is swiftly reinterpreted by the clause following: this wealth is *qiyām* — the thing by which a society *stands*, its provision-structure, its standing-energy — "made for you" as stewardship, not as desert. Al-Rāzī notes the profound reclassification hidden in the phrase: property is here defined *functionally* — by the support it renders to persons and orders — so that handing it to one who cannot preserve it is an offense against the wealth's own purpose, not merely against the ward. The Book repeats the doctrine in its management-verses (57:7's "He has made you trustees in it [khulafāʾa fīhi]"; 24:33's "give them of the wealth of God which He has given you"), and al-Qurṭubī records the jurists' corollary: this verse is among the foundations of the law of *ṣulḥ*, *waqf*, and interdiction together — the legal machinery by which wealth is preserved for its qiyām-function across generations incompetent or unborn. God calls the community's patrimony *its standing*; the hand that squanders or endangers it saws at the beam beneath the whole house.

**Provision, Clothing, Speech: The Triple Charge of the Guardian**

The ward under interdiction is not thereby reduced to a ledger entry. The verse assigns his guardian three offices in one breath: *wa-rzuqūhum fīhā wa-ksūhum wa qūlū lahum qawlan maʿrūfan* — "provide for them from it, and clothe them, and speak to them in an honorable way." The first two discharge the body's account: the feeble-minded person eats *from his own property* — *fīhā*, "from it" — by the guardian's administration, housed and clothed at the standard of his peers. The third guards the subtler treasury: *qawlan maʿrūfan* — speech *maʿrūf*, known-good, recognizable-to-the-heart as kindness — the standard the sūrah fixes again at v. 8 for the indigent at the division, and the Book rings wherever power towers over weakness: "say to My servants that they speak what is best" (17:53); "kind speech and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury" (2:263, *qawlun maʿrūf* in the same formula); culminated in the parent-clause of 17:23, where even *uff* is barred. The jurist-theologians — al-Rāzī among them — remark that incompetence to handle property never suspends the right to be addressed as a person: wealth can be interdicted; dignity cannot. The mad, the simple, the spendthrift son — all remain *mukallaf* of respect: every forbiddance that shields their purse concludes, in the same verse, with a commandment for their ears.

**The Fiduciary Mind as a Religious Formation**

The sūrah's triad across three verses — orphan (v. 2), sufahāʾ (v. 5), and the absent heirs of v. 8 — assembles Islam's complete wardship-ethic: hold; do not consume; administer; provide; clothe; and *speak honorably*. The Muslim societies arrived early, through these verses, at the mature legal institution the later ages would call fiduciary duty: the guardian bound to the ward's interest as though it were his own, audited by heaven where no earthly auditor sits. The Prophet's own teaching set the same standard for trustees of every description — guardians of estates, executors, treasurers of mosques, deputies of absent persons:

> *"Each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock: the ruler is a shepherd and is responsible for his flock; the man is a shepherd of his household and is responsible for his flock; the woman is a shepherd of her husband's house and children and is responsible for her flock."* (al-Bukhārī 893; Muslim 1829, from Ibn ʿUmar)

The shepherd-law of v. 5 thus trains the believer in the disposition the whole sūrah demands: wealth is qiyām entrusted; mouths under one's authority are trusts in kind; and speech — the cheapest good and the easiest wrong — is *maʿrūf* or it is sin. Between these three, guardianship becomes what the verse-medicine intended: not power over the weak, but a pastors' office under the Watcher's post.
