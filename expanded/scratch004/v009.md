## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:9

> **Let those who would dread if they left behind their own helpless progeny have fear; let them reverence God and speak justly.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dread Transposed: Empathy Written as Statute**

The sūrah's orphan-pericope closes its opening movement with one of the Book's most beautifully engineered clauses: *wa-l-yakhsha alladhīna law tarakū min khalfihim dhurriyyatan ḍiʿāfan khāfū ʿalayhim* — "let those fear, who, had they left behind weak offspring, would have feared for them." The construction performs its own meaning: it takes the deepest animal terror in any parent — *what becomes of my little ones when I am gone?* — and transposes it, by grammatical sleight, onto everyone standing in an orphaned child's vicinity. Al-Ṭabarī's transmission-grid catches the clause's two complementary readings from the Companions' school: it addresses the deathbed's *witnesses and counselors* — those who gather round a dying man with advice about his estate — bidding them fear for his helpless children the very fear they would feel at a like scene over their own deathbeds; and it addresses every guardian and *trustee* — deal with this ward as you would pray that a strange guardian deal with *your* child, in the morning after your grave. Ibn Kathīr, gathering both threads, makes of them one rope: the verse is the orphan-law's *empathy-engine* — for law's clauses can be gnawed by clever counsel, but conscience armed with a father's own dread does not misread a child's face.

**Saʿd's Hospital-Chamber: The Third That Is Much**

The verse's companion in the Prophet's practice is the celebrated bedside of Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ at the conquest-illness of Makkah — the chamber that fixed forever the limits of generosity over inheritance:

> *Saʿd said: "The Messenger of God ﷺ came to visit me in my illness, the year of the Conquest. I said: Messenger of God, I am wealthy, and my only heir is one daughter — shall I bequeath two-thirds of my property in charity?" He said: "No." I said: "A half?" He said: "No." He said: "A third — and a third is much. To leave your heirs wealthy is better than to leave them destitute, begging of people; nor will you spend anything, seeking God's face, but that you are rewarded — even the morsel you put in your wife's mouth."* (al-Bukhārī 2742, 2745; Muslim 1628)

The ceiling of the bequest-third, fixed in that chamber, is v. 9's law in action: deathbed-counsel cooled to protect the small; the dying told, with prophetic gentleness, that their heirs' bread outranks their worries about the surplus man's applause. Al-Qurṭubī obliges the connection expressly: the verse was sent for every comforter who whispers to a wasting man *"give, give, and empty the tray before your sons arrive"* — let such counselor fear for the children as for his own; and the pious press the same clause upon the executor at the desk of the legacy, and the notary at the pen.

**Dhurriyyatan Ḍiʿāfan: Weakness as the Measure of Duty**

The verse chooses its noun with precision: *dhurriyyah ḍiʿāf* — offspring *weak* — the plural *ḍuʿafāʾ* of the helplessness-class the Book names in every protection-verse (the weak of the earth at 4:75 and 8:26; the formerly-weak of Pharaoh's Egypt made heirs themselves at 28:5) — because duty is measured in the Book by the very variable the strong erase: the *weakness* of the dependent. The more helpless the progeny, the heavier the obligation-onlookers bear; and God's citation of *ḍiʿāfan* at exactly this clause fixes the calibration-rule of the entire orphan-pericope: the presumption is always of the child's incapacity, never of the trustee's benignity — an inversion of presumption, al-Rāzī notes, from the Jāhiliyya order in which the minor's *defenselessness* was the reason to consume his estate. Under the sunna's architecture, vulnerability is not opportunity but summons: the weak one's weakness is read as the strongest mandate for his protection.

**Qawlan Sadīdan: The Just Word at the Death-Chamber**

The seal joins fear of God to speech — *fa-l-yattaqu Allāha wa-l-yaqūlū qawlan sadīdā* — "let them reverence God, and speak a word that hits the mark": *sadīd*, the arrow-word (its root giving the archer's *straightness* — the shaft that reaches its target), a speech that neither deflects toward flattery of the dying nor curves toward interest of the counselor; the same word God will bind to taqwā for our very public tongue at 33:70–71 — *"reverence God, and speak a directed word; He shall set right your deeds for you."* The commentators of the pericope hold the meant *word* to include the entire service-language around the succession: what the counsel says at the bed, what the guardian tells the child, what the executor reads in the assembly — each word weighed against what its speakers would ask, at their own beds, of the same functionaries. And the sūrah will now pass, the verse having planted empathy at the heart of its orphan-law, to the inheritance-code's own mathematics — as if to say: the shares that follow are not arithmetic merely but the very answer, by statute, to every fearful prayer a dying father offered: *God, keep my little ones in feeding and in dignity after me* — to which heaven's reply comes mandatory, in numbers.
