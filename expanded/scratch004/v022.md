## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:22

> **And marry not those women whom your fathers married, save for what is past. Truly it was indecent and loathsome, an evil way.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa Lā Tankiḥū Mā Nakaḥa Ābāʾukum: The Forbidden Enterprise of the Sons**

The prohibition arrives with terrible plainness: *wa lā tankiḥū mā nakaḥa ābāʾukum min al-nisāʾi illā mā qad salaf* — "and marry not such women as your fathers married — except what has passed": the statute of the stepmother, closing the door the plume-and-cloak code of v. 19 had left ajar: for whereas wife-inheritance *by coercion* had been banned at v. 19, some of the early Madinans continued seeking the father's widows *with consent* — the cloak-claim run under new gentleness — until this verse gave the practice its absolute burial. The transmitters of its occasion (al-Ṭabarī, al-Wāḥidī, Ibn Kathīr in their magisterial chains, with the fullest narrative in al-Qurṭubī) attach it to the four sons of the to a cluster of four questions of the same kind — the crystal-case being the son of Abū Qays ibn al-Aṣlat, seeking marriage with his father's widow, who herself found the trade unseemly and carried it to the Prophet ﷺ; God's answer removed the matter from desire altogether. And the law's precision knows its own fences: the prohibition holds whether the father's marriage was consummated or not (al-Ṭabarī's register); the jurists extend it likewise to any woman of the father's private intimacy (marriage-form or not, per the per the broader reading of the verb *nakaḥa*); while *illā mā qad salaf* — "save what is past" — covers the pre-Islamic estate of everything: the God of the new community does not unpick the pagan past, He interdicts the future.

**Innahū Kāna Fāḥishatan wa Maqtan: The God's Own Insult-Vocabulary**

The sentence God renders on the practice is composed of His strongest censure-language, three nouns deep: *fāḥishatan* — flagrant indecency, the name already established at vv. 15–16 as the category of maximum sexual breach; *wa maqtan* — *maqt*, loathing, abomination-one-hateful — the Book's rare grave-accent (used for God's loathing of the disobedient at 40:10 and 35:39, for the sabbath-breakers' apportionment at 2:65) — and *wa sāʾa sabīlā* — "evil as a way," the malway-word the Book bestows on courses in entirety evil. Al-Rāzī dwells on the communion of the three: the practice is censured intellectually (*it repugns the nature of fatherhood*), legally (*it intrudes the son into the father's chamber-past*), and customarily (the God's-made fiṭrah of the Arabs of the Arabs themselves already knew its shame in parts, as their own poets reviled the one dubbed the *dayzan* — and Islam made universal what instinct half-knew). The theologians of the schools note the weight of *maqt* in God's own mouth: when loathing is attributed to the Creator of souls, the practice is bound to an interior horror deeper than community-custom — fathers' chambers are not inheritable goods because fatherhood is one of the human estates that survive the grave.

**Mā Qad Salaf: The Habit of God with Dead Pasts**

The little saving-clause — *illā mā qad salaf* — deserves its own dignity in the map of revealed forgiveness: it is the Qur'an's standing refusal to prosecute yesterdays under today's statute (paired threefold with its sisters in these very verses: v. 23's *illā mā qad salaf* on the two-sister marriages and v. 24's similar framing), and the commentators lay down from it one of the great principles of conversion-jurisprudence: Islam accepts the person *in the condition Islam finds him* — Ghaylān with ten wives choosing four (the reports of al-Tirmidhī 1128 already noted), the two-sister husbands choosing one — because accounting begins at hearing, not at birth. Al-Qurṭubī's jurist-pastoral note generalizes still more softly: the clause also teaches *how revelation legislates continuity*: its bans are not certificates against ancestors — the fathers of the first Muslims had lived under no dispensation known to them — but *frontier-posts*, beyond which the new road runs. So, too, the family of Islam instructs every convert, every young Muslim confronting family history: the practice of forefathers belongs to their own ledger; what is *ours* to choose is the road from here — and in the matter of fathers' wives, that road closed at this verse for every generation to come: *evil as a way.*
