## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:23

> **Forbidden unto you [as wives] are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your fathers' sisters, your mothers' sisters, your brothers' daughters, your sisters' daughters, your milk-mothers and milk-sisters, the mothers of your wives, the stepdaughters in your care—born of your wives with whom you have consummated marriage, but if you have not consummated the marriage with them, then there is no blame on you—and the wives of your sons who are from your loins, and two sisters together, save for what is past. Truly God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥurrimat: The Table of the Seven Bloods**

The verse finally unrolls the marriage-map's dark side — *maḥārim*, the prohibitions-of-nearness — in the statute-form *ḥurrimat ʿalaykum* — "forbidden to you are..." — a roll-call the jurists memorize as seven blood-ties: mother (and through her every grandmother upward, al-Qurṭubī's hereditary rule without gaps), daughter (and every granddaughter downward), sister (full, paternal, maternal alike), the two aunts of both parents, and the two lines' nieces — brother's daughters, sister's daughters — *both downwards and backwards, the tree's rings barricaded in all directions*. The commentators pause over the map's own *philosophy of blood*: the prohibited seven are exactly the degrees in which Islamic society is commanded *unveiled intimacy* — the circle within which a woman may relax her dress, travel with a male, and live in household freedom — so that the single statute draws both the safe-room and its boundary at one stroke: those forbidden in marriage are, by the same text, the permitted of domestic trust (24:31's paired roster proving the correspondence). God closes what He opens: the nearness-love was never meant to complete itself in the bed; the household's sanctity is built precisely on the union it forbids.

**The Womb-Match of Milk: Raḍāʿah, the Suckling Prohibition**

Then comes the statute that most displays revelation's independence of biology alone: *wa ummahātukumu allātī arḍaʿnakum wa akhawātukum min al-raḍāʿah* — "and your milk-mothers who nursed you, and your milk-sisters": the bond of nursing raised to the full rank of the bond of blood — five sucklings in the leading legal schools (al-Shāfiʿī fixing the count from the traditions; the Ḥanafīs content with one; ʿĀʾishah's report marking the earlier ten-then-five stages of the ordinance), and the mouth stretched to the breast within the first two or three years of life. The Prophet's own code was enacted in court-scenes the age memorized: Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ's brother ʿUtbah's the Prophet instructing ʿĀʾishah's nephews through Sahlah bint Suhayl against Salīm the freedman (that particular case the special exception the books preserve), and the general maxim of Islam writing the statute in his own mother's voice:

> *"What is forbidden through milk-kin is as what is forbidden through blood-kin."* (al-Bukhārī 2645, 5102; Muslim 1444–45 — the milk-law's corner-hadith)

— a maxim its teachers and mothers still hold before every every suckling-arrangement in every Muslim home: wet-nursing is a legal act, binding houses together as surely as marriage, confining them therewith as surely as blood.

**Rabāʾib and Daughters of the Son: The Affinity-Chain**

The statute then turns to the *in-law* degrees — maṣāhir — with the care of a lawyer of families: the mothers of wives — forbidden by the very contract, consummation or not (the consensus the schools affirm against the minority read); the stepdaughters — *rabāʾibukumu allātī fī ḥujūrikum min nisāʾikumu allātī dakhaltum bihinna* — forbidden where the mother's marriage was consummated, *fa-in lam takūnū dakhaltum bihinna fa-lā junāḥa ʿalaykum* — otherwise no blame — the accompanying gloss of *fī ḥujūrikum* ("in your care") read by most as description of the household's closeness, not a condition of the ban (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Z); and the wives of loins-sons — judgment eternally separated from the Prophet's Zayd-affair's shadow through 33:37, since adoptive sons are no loins-sons — *ulāʾika banūkum min aṣlābikum* deliberately qualified, the commentators reminding every generation the statute fenced the son's divorcée from his *father*, as the son's mother had been fenced at v. 22 from her *son*: the house of Muhammad, al-Rāzī remarks, *needed the clearest statute-book on this planet, for his own doors were its object-lessons.*

**And Two Sisters Together: The Verse's Closing Combination**

The table's last clause bars the *combination*: *wa an tajmaʿū bayna al-ukhtayn illā mā qad salaf* — "that you take in wedlock two sisters together, save what is past" — and the jurists' reasoning completes its wisdom with its its sister-clause: aunt-with-niece, by the Prophet's still-extant courtroom-ruling — *"God and His Messenger have forbidden a woman and her father's sister together, and a woman and her mother's sister together"* (al-Bukhārī 5109; Muslim 1408; al-Tirmidhī 1157; Abū Dāwūd 2066). Jealousies at the blood-line of sisters was the commentators' standard note; furthermore the Book's seal — *inna Allāha kāna ghafūran raḥīman* — Forgiving, Merciful — stretches over the whole table of of prohibitions-of-marriage its own grace-banner: what was crossed before hearing falls under the old *illā mā qad salaf*; what is crossed after, under repair-of-repentance — for the statute-draws of God are matched, line for line, by mercy measured for the man who weeps. Thus the *maḥram*-table stands finished — one of the oldest intact marriage-maps of mankind — and every Muslim wedding since that Madinan noon has been walked through it, ring by ring.
