## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:24

> **And married women [are forbidden unto you] save those whom your right hands possess; thus has God prescribed for you. Lawful unto you are all others whom you would seek in marriage with gifts from your property, in honest wedlock and not in fornication. And those women whom you enjoy thereby, give unto them their bridewealth, as an obligation; but there is no blame on you for what you agree to after the obligation. God is Knowing, Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Muḥṣanāt: Fortress-Women and the Rule of Another's Covenant**

The verse resumes the marriage-map at its largest perimeter: *wa-l-muḥṣanātu min al-nisāʾi illā mā malakat aymānukum* — "and the fortress-women among women are forbidden — save those your right hands possess": *muḥṣanāt*, from *ḥiṣn*, fortress — the women made inaccessible by standing covenant — married women, according to the consensus of the tradition, as are the nearest words of al-Ṭabarī and the great congregations of schools after him: another man's wife is forbidden absolutely, her covenant his *ḥiṣn* about her; the Book's own protected integrity thereby advanced from blood (v. 23) to parchment-and-honor — the marriage-bond itself entered into the list of holies. The reports of its comprehensive occasion — women of Ḥunayn's battlefield within this within the statute's ancient exception — boxed provisionally within the then-standing law of captives, which the law later disciplined afresh; but the mosque-law scholars of every generation have kept first its standing clause: *no man may approach, flirt with, or negotiate around the covenant of another* — the law of brotherly reverence at the last door, where every house is a fortress against every hand save the one the door was built for.

**An Tabtaghū bi-Amwālikum: Marriage by Gift-Money, Not by Whim**

The verse then re-opens the lawful wide world with a complete methodology in a single sentence: *uḥilla lakum mā warāʾa dhālikum an tabtaghū bi-amwālikum muḥṣinīna ghayra musāfiḥīna* — "lawful for you is all beyond that, to seek with your property, as chastity-keepers, not as fornicators." The clause instructs *how* one marries, threefold: (1) *an tabtaghū bi-amwālikum* — seek *by means of your property* — the mahr-way, public, priced, documented — against the Jāhili roads that cost no one anything: al-Qurṭubī reading the reference to property as demanding quantifiable bridal-gift, not vague service; (2) *muḥṣinīna* — *as chaste ones* — the man himself fortress-warded *within* the taking, the ḥiṣn built this time round the seeker: marriage as the chastity-instrument, its own contract a a wall; (3) *ghayra musāfiḥīna* — not *sifāḥ*-practicers: *sifāḥ*, the poured-out way — pouring seed into unguarded channels — the Book's own image for fornication-as-spillage: against every "connection" the age invents, God contrasts a house keyed with property, witnesses, naming, and the bride's own treasury. The *muḥṣanīn / musāfiḥīn* polarity is exactly the Qur'an's double-register of marriage against every since: *chastity-builders against spillers*: where chastity holds, contract and payment; where spilling rules, no contract, no account.

**Fa-Mā-stamtaʿtum Bihi Minhunna: The Bridewealth-Anchor and Its Echoes**

The verse's crown-clause — *fa-mā-stamtaʿtum bihi minhunna fa-ātūhunna ujūrahunna farīḍah* — "so those whom you enjoy thereby, give them their rewards, an obligation" — resumes the sūrah's central women's-right (v. 4's *ṣaduqāt*; v. 20's qintār) with its juristic synonym *ujūr* — wages/fee — set in the divine-payroll voice. The eternal the eternal statement: her due is a *farīḍah*, statute-assigned — distant now from the dispute of later centuries over the clause's over the famous *mutʿah*-question, on which the tradition itself preserves the entire debate honestly: the memory of Ibn ʿAbbās's reading *"ila ajalin musamman"* — "for a term appointed" — the Shiʿi schools cleaving to the verse as mutʿah's own text, the Sunna-schools holding to the Prophet's final prohibition at Khaybar and Mecca's year — "God and His Messenger forbade the mutʿah to the Day of Resurrection" — with the ruling of the Caliph ʿUmar sealing the community's operational consensus since. The honest exegete transcribes both — as al-Ṭabarī himself does in his catalogs — and the believer notices that where tradition broke, the *verse's own central content* stands identical in every school: *whatsoever be the creature's contract, the woman's gift is God's statute, never the man's benevolence* — upon which note all sects and centuries still unite, the wage-word *farīḍah* striking the same settled chime it did at the grave's ledger: marriage without the farīḍah is a denied marriage here.

**Wa Lā Junāḥa ʿAlaykum Fīmā Tarāḍaytum Bihi: Freedom After the Obligation**

The statute then closes — exquisitely — with a liberty: *wa lā junāḥa ʿalaykum fīmā tarāḍaytum bihi min baʿdi al-farīḍah* — "no blame upon you in what you mutually consent to, after the fixed obligation": after the farīḍah is *safely fixed* — registered, owed, sealed — the spouses' consenting commerce of increase or remission lies open: he may add; she may yield (v. 4's ṭibna); installments may be reshaped — the law stepping out of a bedroom it has furnished with rights. Al-Qurṭubī draws the seal-wisdom of the sequence: Islam writes marriage as *statute-floor plus consent-ceiling*: the floor is immutable — God-forged: mahr, consent-of-brides-past, prohibited degrees; above the floor, two adults live as free souls under God. And the verse's attribute-closure gathers the whole pericope again — *inna Allāha kāna ʿalīman ḥakīman* — Knowing, Wise — Every exception weighed against every house: the He knows what marriages build and what marriages conceal; He is wise in what He closed and what He left open; and the family of believers, its map now complete within the pericope vv. 22–25, may walk from the statute-table into the courts and bedrooms of the world carrying, like a passport, the signature of the Knowing and the Wise.
