# Sūrah al-ʿAṣr (Chapter 103) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Three-Verse Summary of the Entire Qur'an**

Sūrah al-ʿAṣr is the 103rd chapter in the written order of the Qur'an and one of the shortest chapters in the entire Book — three verses, fourteen Arabic words, and yet, in the estimation of the earliest community, a chapter that contains the whole message of religion. It is a Makkan sūrah by consensus of the biographers of revelation. In the chronological ordering reconstructed by scholars such as al-Suyūṭī and Nöldeke, it falls very early, most likely after Sūrah al-Sharḥ (94) and before the cluster of short oath sūrahs that characterize the first years of the Prophet's call in Makkah. Its brevity is itself a sign: it was revealed to a small community that needed to memorize, to carry, and to transmit under persecution, and therefore the most essential truths were compressed into a form that could be held in the heart without a parchment.

The title al-ʿAṣr is taken from its first word, as is the custom for many short sūrahs. The word itself carries a cluster of meanings that the classical lexicons preserve together rather than choosing between. In its most immediate sense it means the declining day — the late afternoon, the time after Ẓuhr when the sun has passed its zenith and begins its visible descent. In a second sense it means time in its absolute sense — dahr, zamān, the flowing epoch in which human lives occur. In a third sense it means the age or era of a people. In a fourth sense, by established usage among the Companions, it means the ʿAṣr prayer itself. The sūrah's power comes in part from holding all these senses together at once: the afternoon as daily reminder of ending, time as the capital of life, the era as the stage of history, and prayer as the act that redeems time.

**The Practice of the Companions With This Sūrah**

The spiritual weight this sūrah carried for the first generation is preserved in a report transmitted by al-Ṭabarānī in al-Awsaṭ and authenticated by al-Albānī in al-Silsilah al-Ṣaḥīḥah, from ʿUbayd Allāh ibn Ḥuṣayn, who said that Abū Madīnah al-Dārimī — a Companion — reported:

> *“When two men from among the Companions of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, would meet, they would not part from one another until one of them recited Sūrat al-ʿAṣr to the other, then one would give the greeting of peace to the other.”* (al-Ṭabarānī, al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ 5120; al-Silsilah al-Ṣaḥīḥah 2648)

This was not ritual formalism. It was a mutual reminder: we have just spent time together; time is the subject of our accounting; let us not part until we have reminded each other why we met and what saves us from loss. The practice shows how the Companions understood the sūrah as a charter for companionship itself — that every meeting must end with truth and patience.

**Al-Shāfiʿī’s Famous Assessment**

The most cited assessment of the sūrah's comprehensiveness comes from the founder of one of the four schools of law, Imām Muḥammad ibn Idrīs al-Shāfiʿī (d. 204/820). Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and others transmit his statement:

> *“If the people were to reflect upon Sūrat al-ʿAṣr alone, it would suffice them.”* (Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, introduction to Sūrah al-ʿAṣr)

Al-Shāfiʿī did not mean that the rest of the Qur'an is superfluous. He meant that this sūrah contains, in germ, the four foundations that the rest of the Qur'an elaborates at length: correct belief, righteous action, calling to truth, and steadfastness. A community that lived by these three verses would have lived by Islam.

**Its Place Between Two Sūrahs**

The placement of al-ʿAṣr in the mushaf after Sūrah al-Takāthur (102) and before Sūrah al-Humazah (104) is deeply instructive. Sūrah al-Takāthur warned:

> *“Rivalry in worldly increase distracts you, until you visit the graves.”* (Qur'an 102:1-2)

And it closed with:

> *“Then on that Day you will surely be questioned about the blessing.”* (Qur'an 102:8)

What is the blessing that will be questioned? The exegetes say: among the first blessings is time itself. Then immediately comes al-ʿAṣr: By Time — you are in loss unless you use it rightly. Then comes al-Humazah, which describes one archetype of loss: the hoarder who thinks wealth will make him immortal. The three sūrahs together form a logical chain: distraction → time as capital → the portrait of the distracted loser. To read them in order is to experience the Qur'an's argument as a single discourse.

**The Basmalah Before the Oath**

Like all sūrahs except al-Tawbah, al-ʿAṣr is preceded in the mushaf by:

> *“In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful.”* (Qur'an 1:1 and opening of 112 other sūrahs)

The basmalah here is not counted as a verse of al-ʿAṣr in the Kufan counting, but it is still recited as a threshold. It reminds the reader that the severe oath that follows — by time, that man is in loss — comes from the Compassionate, the Merciful. The warning itself is mercy. A God who did not care would not warn.

## Sūrah al-ʿAṣr 103:1

> **By the declining day,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Root That Squeezes: ʿA-Ṣa-Ra and the Image of Pressing**

The word al-ʿaṣr comes from the triliteral root ʿayn-ṣād-rā (ʿ-ṣ-r), whose core sense in Arabic is to press, to squeeze, to wring out. ʿAṣara al-thawb means he wrung the garment to extract water. ʿAṣara al-ʿinab means he pressed grapes to extract juice — from which comes ʿaṣīr, juice. Al-iʿtiṣār is to extract. The same root gives maʿṣarah, the place of pressing, and ʿuṣārah, the extract or essence.

What does squeezing have to do with afternoon? The Arab linguists, among them al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad and Ibn Manẓūr in Lisān al-ʿArab, explained that the late afternoon is called al-ʿaṣr because it is the time when the day is being squeezed, pressed to its end. Light is being extracted. The day, which at noon seemed abundant, is now being wrung out, drop by drop. Anyone who has watched the late afternoon sun in a desert or on a workday knows the feeling: the shadows lengthen, the heat softens, the day that felt endless is now visibly leaving. The word itself therefore contains a warning: time is not a static container; it is something being pressed out of your hands while you hold it.

This linguistic image also explains why ʿaṣr can mean era or epoch. An era is the juice pressed out of many years, the concentrated essence of a generation's deeds. History is what remains after time has squeezed a people.

**Why Does God Swear By Time? The Logic of Divine Oaths**

The verse opens with wa — the particle of oath — and al-ʿaṣr in the genitive: wa'l-ʿaṣr. God is swearing by al-ʿaṣr. The Qur'an swears frequently, but never idly. The pattern, as al-Rāzī systematized in his Tafsīr al-Kabīr, is that God swears by something to draw attention to its rank as a sign (āyah) and to prepare the listener for a truth that concerns that thing.

He swears by the sun and its brightness (91:1), by the night when it covers (92:1), by the fig and the olive (95:1), by the Mount (52:1), by the Pen (68:1). In each case, the object of the oath is itself a witness to the statement that follows it. Here, the statement that follows is that mankind is in loss — and what better witness to human loss than time itself? Time sees every loss. Time measures every waste. Time is the courtroom in which loss is proven.

Ibn al-Qayyim in al-Tibyān fī Aqsām al-Qur'ān observed that when God swears by something created, He is elevating it in the listener's estimation so that he will not treat it cheaply. To swear by time is to tell the listener: you treat time as cheap, as empty filler between events, but in My sight it is so weighty that I swear by it. The oath therefore performs two functions at once: it authenticates the coming message, and it revalues time in the listener's heart.

**The Afternoon Prayer Reading: Why So Many Scholars Said al-ʿAṣr Means Ṣalāt al-ʿAṣr**

Among the interpretations preserved by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, and al-Bayḍāwī, one of the strongest is that al-ʿaṣr means the ʿAṣr prayer itself. Several evidences support this.

First, the Qur'an elsewhere singles out a prayer for special mention as al-ṣalāt al-wusṭā, the middle or most excellent prayer:

> *“Guard strictly the prayers, and the middle prayer, and stand before God in devotion.”* (Qur'an 2:238)

The majority of Companions and their students, including ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Ibn Masʿūd, Abū Hurayrah, and Ibn ʿUmar, interpreted the middle prayer as ʿAṣr. The Prophet ﷺ himself clarified this on the Day of the Confederates (Yawm al-Aḥzāb), when the polytheists distracted the Muslims from prayer:

> *“They distracted us from the middle prayer, the ʿAṣr prayer — may God fill their houses and graves with fire.”* (al-Bukhārī 2931; Muslim 627, from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib)

Second, the ʿAṣr prayer occurs at the time when commerce is busiest, when the day's earnings are being concluded, when fatigue tempts a person to delay. To guard it therefore requires deliberate prioritization of God over worldly gain — precisely the theme of being saved from loss.

Third, the Prophet ﷺ attached an unusually severe warning to its neglect:

> *“Whoever misses the ʿAṣr prayer, it is as though he has been deprived of his family and his wealth.”* (al-Bukhārī 552; Muslim 626, from Ibn ʿUmar)

> *“Whoever abandons the ʿAṣr prayer, his deeds are rendered void.”* (al-Bukhārī 553, from Buraydah al-Aslamī)

If al-ʿaṣr in this sūrah means the ʿAṣr prayer, then the oath becomes: By the prayer that, if you miss it, you have lost everything — truly mankind is in loss except those who hold fast. The linguistic and the ritual meanings reinforce each other.

Al-Qurṭubī in al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qur'ān preserves both views without forcing a choice, as did al-Ṭabarī: the word can bear both, and the Qur'an often intends a breadth of meaning rather than a single narrow referent. Time in general is being sworn by, and the afternoon prayer is the most time-sensitive, loss-revealing portion of time.

**The Era of the Prophet and the Arc of History**

A less common but profound reading, preserved by al-Qurṭubī and attributed to some of the exegetes, is that al-ʿaṣr means the era (ʿaṣr) of the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ itself — his generation — because of its bounty through the renewal of prophethood in it. In this reading, God swears by the prophetic era as the most blessed era, and then declares that humanity outside it is in loss.

This connects to a larger Qur'anic view of history as moral descent interrupted by prophetic renewal. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“The best of generations is my generation, then those who follow them, then those who follow them.”* (al-Bukhārī 2652; Muslim 2533, from Ibn Masʿūd and ʿImrān ibn Ḥuṣayn)

Each successive generation, without revelation to renew it, drifts further. The late afternoon of humanity is therefore an apt image for the time distant from prophetic light — light is declining, shadows are long, judgment is near. Sūrah al-ʿAṣr, revealed in the full light of the prophetic era, warns all later eras that their natural trajectory is loss unless they reconnect to the four saving acts.

**The Psychological Power of Declining Light**

There is a reason the Qur'an chose the declining day rather than simply saying “by time.” The declining day is time that you can see leaving. At noon, time feels infinite. At late afternoon, it feels finite. The worker looks at the sun and knows his shift ends. The traveler knows he must find lodging. The student knows the exam approaches.

Modern psychology calls this scarcity awareness — humans value what they perceive as running out. The Qur'an uses the daily, visible scarcity of light to teach the scarcity of life. Every single day, without a sermon, the sun preaches this sūrah: I rose, I peaked, I am now declining; so will you. The oath by al-ʿaṣr is therefore an oath by a daily memento mori that no one can avoid seeing.

Consider how people actually behave at ʿAṣr time. In many Muslim lands, the markets pause. In office life today, it is the time of the afternoon slump, the second coffee, the checking of how much was accomplished. It is the moment when the question “what did I do with my day?” naturally arises. The sūrah takes that natural human accounting and elevates it to eternal accounting.

**Taqwā as Driving Through a Narrowing Road**

The image of taqwā as careful driving is especially apt here. To drive at noon on an empty highway is easy; to drive at declining day, when light is fading, when you are tired, when the road narrows toward night, requires heightened awareness. The believer who hears wa'l-ʿaṣr is being told: you are not driving at noon. You are driving in the late afternoon of your life, of your day, of your era. Drive accordingly — with lamps on, with focus, with awareness that the destination is near.

**Cross-Reference: Time as the Material of Oaths Elsewhere**

The Qur'an's oaths by time cluster in the final thirtieth of the Book, the portion most recited and most memorized, as though God wanted the most frequent reciters to be most reminded of time:

> *“By the morning brightness, and by the night when it is still.”* (Qur'an 93:1-2)

> *“By the night as it envelops, and by the day as it reveals.”* (Qur'an 92:1-2)

> *“By the dawn, and by ten nights.”* (Qur'an 89:1-2)

Each time, the created phenomenon of time is called as a witness. Al-ʿAṣr is the most concentrated of them: not morning, not night, but the hinge between day and night, the moment of transition — because human life is itself a hinge between beginning and ending.

## Sūrah al-ʿAṣr 103:2

> **truly mankind is in loss,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grammar of Certainty: Inna, La, and the Definite Form**

The Arabic of this verse is extraordinarily emphatic for its brevity: Inna al-insāna lafī khusr. Three devices of emphasis converge.

First, inna — “truly, indeed” — is a particle that establishes a fact and removes doubt. It is used when the listener might resist or be heedless. Second, la — the lām of emphasis — “surely, indeed” — reinforces inna: inna ... la ... is the strongest affirmative construction in Arabic rhetoric, used for oaths and decisive news. Third, the noun khusr is preceded by fī — “in” — not merely “is losing” but “is *in* loss,” enveloped by it, immersed in it, as one is in water or in darkness. And al-insān is generic, with the definite article denoting the species: not “a man” but “mankind,” humanity as such.

The verse therefore does not say “some people sometimes lose.” It says: the human being, by the very fact of being human in time, is enveloped in loss — certainly, truly, inevitably — unless something interrupts that envelopment. The oath in verse 1 is now answered with a verdict.

Al-Zamakhsharī in al-Kashshāf noted the rhetorical force: the listener who heard “By the declining day” expects something momentous; the answer comes with doubled certainty to match the solemnity of the oath.

**What Khusr Means: Losing Capital, Not Just Missing Profit**

The word khusr (and its longer form khusrān, khusār) does not mean simple failure or sin. Its root khā-sīn-rā (kh-s-r) means to lose capital, to go bankrupt in trade, to diminish. In commercial Arabic, khasira al-rajul means the man lost his investment — he put capital in and it was consumed. Bāʿa bi-khusr means he sold at a loss. Al-khāsir is the bankrupt trader.

This commercial metaphor is central to the Qur'an's moral psychology. Life itself is presented as capital — ra's al-māl — given by God. Time, health, intellect, opportunity are the investment. The human being is a trader who must invest that capital. If he invests it in what perishes, or leaves it idle, he loses the capital itself, not merely the profit.

The Qur'an uses this metaphor repeatedly:

> *“O you who believe, shall I guide you to a commerce that will save you from a painful punishment?”* (Qur'an 61:10)

> *“Indeed, those who recite the Book of God and establish prayer and spend from what We have provided them, secretly and openly, hope for a commerce that will never perish.”* (Qur'an 35:29)

And of the opposite:

> *“They are the ones who have purchased error in exchange for guidance, so their commerce has brought no profit, nor were they guided.”* (Qur'an 2:16)

> *“Truly the losers are those who lose their own souls and their families on the Day of Resurrection. Unquestionably, that is the manifest loss.”* (Qur'an 39:15)

> *“He has lost this world and the Hereafter; that is the manifest loss.”* (Qur'an 22:11)

Al-insān lafī khusr therefore means: humanity is engaged in a trade in which, by default, it is losing its principal. Time passes whether used or not; every day that passes without being invested in the four saving acts is capital that can never be recovered.

**Mankind in General or Disbelievers in Particular? Two Classical Readings**

The exegetes differed on whether al-insān here means all humanity without exception, or disbelievers specifically. Al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī report both:

- The first view, attributed to the majority, is that al-insān is generic — all humans are in loss by the nature of time's passage and heedlessness, except those exempted in the next verse. This reading matches the next verse's illā — “save those who...” — which grammatically requires that the preceding statement was general.

- The second view, attributed to some early exegetes, is that al-insān means the disbelieving human, or the human who is ungrateful (kafūr). In this reading, the verse is a condemnation of the disbeliever's state.

Al-Ṭabarī reconciles them by noting that even the believer, insofar as he is merely “human” without the four qualities, shares in the default loss. The believer is not exempt by label but by practice. This is why the exception is not “save the Muslims” but “save those who believe and do...” — faith must be actual, not nominal.

Ibn Taymiyyah in Majmūʿ al-Fatāwā emphasized this: imān in the Qur'an is not a tribal identity but a living reality that must be renewed. Without it, even a person born in a Muslim family remains within the general rule of loss.

**The Parallel in Sūrah al-Tīn: Low and High**

The closest parallel to this verse is in Sūrah al-Tīn, revealed around the same period:

> *“We have certainly created man in the best of stature; then We return him to the lowest of the low, except for those who believe and do righteous deeds, for they will have a reward unending.”* (Qur'an 95:4-6)

The structure is identical: creation in excellence → descent into loss/lowness → exception by faith and righteous deeds. Sūrah al-ʿAṣr states the descent as khusr (commercial loss); Sūrah al-Tīn states it as asfal sāfilīn (the lowest of the low). Both teach that human excellence is not self-sustaining; it must be maintained by conscious connection to God. Left to entropy, man falls.

This is a profound anthropology. The Qur'an does not teach that man is inherently evil; it teaches that he is created in the best form (aḥsan taqwīm) but that time, desire, and heedlessness pull him downward unless he actively climbs. Loss is not an accident; it is the default trajectory of a soul that does not strive upward — just as a stone left in the hand falls downward unless held up.

**Time as Capital: The Prophetic Teaching on Two Wasted Blessings**

The Prophet ﷺ made the concept of time-as-capital explicit in a hadith that every Muslim who reflects on Sūrah al-ʿAṣr should memorize:

> *“There are two blessings which many people lose: health and free time.”* (al-Bukhārī 6412, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The word used is maghbūn — one who is cheated in trade, who had valuable goods and sold them cheaply. The hadith uses the same commercial metaphor as khusr. Most people possess health and free time — two forms of capital — and trade them for what is worthless: endless scrolling, argument, heedless entertainment, or even permissible but aimless busyness.

Another hadith sharpens the accounting:

> *“Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your sickness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your preoccupation, and your life before your death.”* (al-Ḥākim in al-Mustadrak 7846; al-Bayhaqī in Shuʿab al-Īmān 10248, from Ibn ʿAbbās; authenticated by al-Albānī)

Each pair is capital and its exhaustion. Youth is capital that old age will liquidate. Health is capital that sickness will bankrupt. Free time is capital that preoccupation will consume. Life itself is capital that death will close. The person who does not invest before exhaustion is, by definition, in khusr.

**The Modern Economy of Distraction**

The verse has acquired new vividness in the modern attention economy. The business model of many contemporary platforms is precisely to convert human time — the ra's al-māl — into corporate profit, while the user experiences the loss as entertainment. A person can spend three hours in the evening watching short videos, each individually trivial, and feel that nothing was lost because nothing was spent — but time was spent, the only non-renewable capital. The Qur'anic term khusr captures this better than modern terms like “waste” because waste suggests something extra; khusr says you lost your principal.

Consider a simple analogy: a man is given $86,400 each morning — the number of seconds in a day — with the condition that whatever he does not invest wisely by night is gone, and tomorrow he receives $86,400 again, but never more. If he spends it carelessly, he is not merely failing to profit; he is losing the daily grant. This is exactly how time works. The verse says mankind lives inside that daily loss.

**Why Inna al-Insāna La-fī Khusr Is Mercy, Not Despair**

At first glance, a universal declaration of loss sounds harsh. Why would a merciful revelation begin with such severity? The classical scholars answered: because diagnosing the disease is the first mercy. A physician who tells a patient “you are healthy” when he is hemorrhaging is not merciful; the physician who says “you are losing blood and must act now” is merciful, even if the news is frightening.

By declaring that loss is the default, the verse frees the listener from the illusion that coasting is safe. Many people assume that if they do nothing particularly evil, they are safe. The verse says: no, doing nothing is itself loss, because time itself is moving. You are on a conveyor belt moving toward the edge; standing still is moving backward.

This is also why the verse uses al-insān rather than al-kāfirūn. If it had said “disbelievers are in loss,” a listener could say, “I am not a disbeliever, so I am safe.” By saying “mankind is in loss,” it includes everyone and forces self-examination. The believer must ask: am I among the exempted? The answer comes not from identity but from the four criteria that follow.

**The Logical Principle: Entropy Requires Work**

There is a logical principle embedded here that modern science echoes in thermodynamics: ordered systems tend toward disorder unless energy is expended to maintain them. A garden left alone does not stay a garden; it becomes weeds. A body left without exercise does not stay fit; it atrophies. A language not practiced is forgotten. A relationship not nurtured decays.

The soul follows the same law. Faith not renewed fades. Knowledge not acted upon is lost. Character not disciplined regresses. Sūrah al-ʿAṣr states the spiritual version of entropy: human excellence decays into loss unless actively maintained by belief, righteous deeds, mutual truth, and mutual patience. The exception in verse 3 is therefore not arbitrary; it is the exact energy input required to reverse entropy.

## Sūrah al-ʿAṣr 103:3

> **save those who believe, perform righteous deeds, exhort one another to truth, and exhort one another to patience.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception That Saves: Illā and the Grammar of Hope**

After the sweeping declaration of loss, the Qur'an says illā — “save, except.” In Arabic rhetoric, an exception after a universal condemnation is the most powerful form of hope. The listener has just been told that his entire species is enveloped in loss. The natural response is despair: then who can be saved? The answer comes immediately: there is an exception, and it is defined not by lineage, tribe, wealth, or even by a mere label, but by four active qualities.

Grammatically, illā here is muttaṣil — a connected exception — meaning the exempted group is part of the original group (mankind). They are not angels or a different species; they are humans who have chosen a different trade. This is crucial for aqidah: salvation is not for a different kind of being; it is for human beings who fulfill the conditions.

The four qualities are conjoined by wa — “and” — not aw — “or.” They are cumulative, not alternative. One is not saved by belief alone without deeds, nor by deeds without belief, nor by personal piety without social responsibility. The four form a complete system.

**First Pillar: Belief — The Root of the Tree**

> *“save those who believe”* — illā alladhīna āmanū

The first exemption is īmān — belief, faith. The verb āmana from the root a-m-n means to be secure, to be safe, to trust. Al-mu'min is one who is secure and who grants security. Faith is not merely assent to a proposition; it is entrusting oneself to God, finding security in Him, and therefore becoming secure.

What must be believed? The Qur'an elsewhere details the objects of faith:

> *“The Messenger has believed in what was revealed to him from his Lord, and so have the believers. All of them have believed in God and His angels and His books and His messengers.”* (Qur'an 2:285)

And:

> *“Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but righteousness is in one who believes in God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets.”* (Qur'an 2:177)

But in this sūrah, the object is left absolute — āmanū — they believed. This absoluteness is intentional, as al-Rāzī noted: it allows the verse to encompass all that must be believed, without limiting. It is the root.

Psychologically, belief is the reorientation of the heart's trust. The human heart must trust something. If it does not trust God, it will trust wealth, status, intellect, or other people — all of which are themselves in loss. Belief transfers trust from the perishable to the Permanent. This is why the Qur'an calls faith a light:

> *“Is one who was dead and We gave him life and made for him light by which to walk among the people like one who is in darkness, never to emerge therefrom?”* (Qur'an 6:122)

Without this light, deeds have no direction. A person can be busy with righteous-looking deeds but without belief they are like a fast car with no destination — movement without guidance. Hence belief is mentioned first.

Ibn Taymiyyah emphasized that īmān in the Qur'anic usage includes belief of the heart, statement of the tongue, and action of the limbs — it is not a static inner state but a living reality that increases and decreases. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“Faith wears out in the heart of any one of you just as a garment wears out, so ask God to renew faith in your hearts.”* (al-Ḥākim in al-Mustadrak 5, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr; authenticated by al-Albānī)

Thus even the first pillar requires maintenance — which is provided by the next three.

**Second Pillar: Righteous Deeds — The Fruit That Proves the Root**

> *“and perform righteous deeds”* — wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt

Faith without deeds is a claim without evidence. The Qur'an almost invariably pairs īmān with ʿamal ṣāliḥ — belief and righteous deeds — in over seventy places, establishing that one is the necessary manifestation of the other.

What is al-ṣāliḥāt? From the root ṣ-l-ḥ, meaning to be sound, wholesome, upright, in good order, beneficial. A ṣāliḥ deed is one that is sound in itself, done with sound intention, and productive of soundness in the world. It is the opposite of fasād — corruption. The righteous deed repairs, heals, sets right.

Classical scholars divided righteous deeds into three circles:

1.  Deeds toward God: prayer, fasting, remembrance, recitation, supplication, pilgrimage, reliance, repentance.

2.  Deeds toward people: honesty in trade, kindness to parents, maintaining kinship, charity, justice, removing harm from the road, speaking truthfully.

3.  Deeds toward the self: purifying the heart, seeking knowledge, disciplining desire, guarding the tongue and gaze.

The Prophet ﷺ gave a comprehensive definition of the second circle when he said:

> *“Every joint of a person must give charity each day the sun rises: to judge justly between two people is charity, to help a man with his mount is charity, a good word is charity, every step you take to prayer is charity, and removing harmful things from the road is charity.”* (al-Bukhārī 2989; Muslim 1009, from Abū Hurayrah)

And:

> *“Do not belittle any good deed, even meeting your brother with a cheerful face.”* (Muslim 2626, from Abū Dharr)

Thus al-ṣāliḥāt is not limited to grand acts. A cheerful face, a removed thorn, a just judgment between two colleagues — all are capital invested against loss.

The relationship between faith and deeds is like that between root and fruit, or between tree and shade. Al-Qurṭubī quotes the early exegete al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī:

> *“Faith is not by embellishment nor by wishing, but it is what settles in the heart and is proven by deeds.”*

Modern analogy: a person who says he believes exercise is healthy but never exercises does not truly believe in a way that affects his life; his “belief” is information, not conviction. True belief reorganizes behavior. Hence the sūrah's second condition.

**Third Pillar: Exhorting One Another to Truth — Faith Becomes Social**

> *“and exhort one another to truth”* — wa tawāṣaw bi'l-ḥaqq

The first two pillars could be practiced alone. The next two make salvation communal. Tawāṣaw is from wa-ṣā, meaning to enjoin, to exhort, to counsel, to make a will (waṣiyyah). It is in the form tafāʿala — tawāṣaw — which denotes mutual action: they exhort *one another*. Not one group preaching to another, but reciprocal counseling. Each believer is both advisor and advised.

What is al-ḥaqq? The word comes from ḥ-q-q, meaning to be true, real, established, binding. Al-Ḥaqq is one of the Names of God:

> *“That is because God — He is the Truth, and what they call upon besides Him is falsehood.”* (Qur'an 22:62)

> *“And say, Truth has come and falsehood has perished; indeed falsehood is bound to perish.”* (Qur'an 17:81)

In exegesis, al-ḥaqq here has been interpreted at several levels, all valid and complementary:

- Al-Ḥaqq as God Himself — exhorting one another to turn to God (al-Qurṭubī).

- Al-Ḥaqq as the Qur'an and revelation — exhorting to hold fast to the Book (al-Baghawī).

- Al-Ḥaqq as tawḥīd — the oneness of God — and all that it entails (al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabarī).

- Al-Ḥaqq as truthfulness in speech and fidelity in action — the opposite of lying and betrayal.

- Al-Ḥaqq as justice — establishing rights where they belong.

Al-Rāzī summarized that al-ḥaqq includes both correct belief (to know the truth) and correct speech and action in accordance with it (to live the truth). Tawāṣī bi'l-ḥaqq is therefore the social practice of truth-preservation.

Why is mutual exhortation necessary? Because truth is not self-sustaining in a community without effort. Falsehood is loud, well-funded, and entertaining; truth is often quiet and demanding. Without a community that actively reminds, corrects, and encourages, truth recedes. The Qur'an commands:

> *“Let there arise from you a community inviting to good, enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong, and those are the successful.”* (Qur'an 3:104)

And:

> *“You are the best community brought forth for mankind: you enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong and believe in God.”* (Qur'an 3:110)

Psychologically, tawāṣī bi'l-ḥaqq protects against the normalization of deviance. A person alone can convince himself that a small compromise is acceptable; a community that practices mutual truth-telling prevents that drift. It is like mountain climbers roped together: one person's slip is caught by the other's hold.

In modern life, this takes forms such as: reminding a friend to pray when work is busy, advising a colleague against a dishonest deal, sharing beneficial knowledge, correcting misinformation gently, encouraging a family member to seek forgiveness rather than harbor resentment. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“Religion is sincere advice (naṣīḥah).” They said, “To whom?” He said, “To God, to His Book, to His Messenger, to the leaders of the Muslims and their common folk.”* (Muslim 55, from Tamīm al-Dārī)

And:

> *“The believer is a mirror to his brother.”* (Abū Dāwūd 4918, from Abū Hurayrah; graded ḥasan by al-Albānī)

A mirror does not flatter; it shows what is there, with care.

**Fourth Pillar: Exhorting One Another to Patience — The Glue That Holds Truth**

> *“and exhort one another to patience.”* — wa tawāṣaw bi'l-ṣabr

The final pillar is ṣabr — patience, steadfastness, endurance. From the root ṣ-b-r, meaning to restrain, to hold back, to bind. The ṣābir is one who holds himself back from complaint, from hasty reaction, from abandoning the path when it becomes difficult.

If tawāṣī bi'l-ḥaqq is knowing and doing what is right, tawāṣī bi'l-ṣabr is continuing to do it when it is hard. Truth without patience is a spark that dies in wind. Patience without truth is endurance in the wrong direction. Together they are complete.

The classical scholars, following Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid, divided ṣabr into three types, all needed:

1.  Ṣabr ʿalā al-ṭāʿah — patience in obedience: continuing to pray, fast, give, be honest, even when the soul is lazy or the environment is hostile.

2.  Ṣabr ʿan al-maʿṣiyah — patience against disobedience: restraining oneself from what God forbade, even when desire is strong and opportunity is easy.

3.  Ṣabr ʿalā al-balā' — patience in tribulation: enduring loss, illness, injustice, bereavement, without losing faith or resorting to prohibited means.

The Qur'an elevates ṣabr to a rank few other virtues attain:

> *“Indeed, the patient will be given their reward without account.”* (Qur'an 39:10)

> *“O you who have believed, seek help through patience and prayer; indeed God is with the patient.”* (Qur'an 2:153)

> *“And be patient, for indeed God does not allow to be lost the reward of those who do good.”* (Qur'an 11:115)

> *“And We made from among them leaders guiding by Our command when they were patient and were certain of Our signs.”* (Qur'an 32:24)

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“Amazing is the affair of the believer: when something good happens to him he is grateful, and that is good for him; and when something harmful befalls him he is patient, and that is good for him.”* (Muslim 2999, from Ṣuhayb ibn Sinān)

And:

> *“No one has been given a gift better and more encompassing than patience.”* (al-Bukhārī 1469; Muslim 1053, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

Tawāṣī bi'l-ṣabr is therefore mutual coaching in resilience. It is the friend who says, “Hold on, this hardship will pass, do not sell your principles for relief.” It is the community that visits the sick, supports the bereaved, helps the unemployed without humiliating him, and reminds the successful not to become arrogant. It is the practice that makes truth sustainable over a lifetime.

**The Four Pillars as a Complete System of Salvation**

Al-Shāfiʿī's insight that this sūrah suffices becomes clear when the four are seen as a system:

- Belief (īmān) is the foundation — the inner compass.

- Righteous deeds (ʿamal ṣāliḥ) is the structure — the compass translated into movement.

- Exhortation to truth (tawāṣī bi'l-ḥaqq) is the social correction — ensuring the compass points to true north, not magnetic deviation.

- Exhortation to patience (tawāṣī bi'l-ṣabr) is the endurance — ensuring the journey continues through storm.

Remove one, and loss returns. Belief without deeds is wishful thinking. Deeds without belief are rootless. Belief and deeds without mutual truth become isolated and prone to self-deception. Truth without patience burns out. Patience without truth is stubbornness.

Ibn al-Qayyim in Zād al-Maʿād and Madārij al-Sālikīn described the path to God as requiring both knowledge and will, and both need community. Al-ʿAṣr provides both: truth addresses knowledge, patience addresses will, and tawāṣī makes both communal.

**Why the Sūrah Ends With Patience: The Final Word**

It is significant that the sūrah's last word is al-ṣabr. The Qur'an often ends passages with what is most needed for what preceded. After diagnosing universal loss, after demanding faith, deeds, and truth, it ends with the virtue that makes all others last: patience. As though to say: you will be tempted to abandon this program when it becomes difficult, when truth is unpopular, when deeds are tiring, when belief is tested. So I leave you with patience as your final provision.

The companions who recited this sūrah to each other before parting were, in effect, renewing a pact: I remind you of truth, you remind me of patience, and together we escape loss. It is a social contract in fourteen words.

**A Practical Program for Today**

How does a modern person live Sūrah al-ʿAṣr today, in a world of notifications and endless demands on time?

- Begin the day by investing time before it is squeezed: Fajr prayer, a portion of Qur'an, intention for the day — this is īmān renewed.

- Convert time into ṣāliḥāt: schedule at least one deed that benefits beyond yourself — a helpful email, charity, teaching a child, removing harm — not left to chance but placed in the calendar.

- Practice tawāṣī bi'l-ḥaqq: once a day, gently remind someone of something true and beneficial, and be open to being reminded. Make truth-telling a habit, not an occasional lecture.

- Practice tawāṣī bi'l-ṣabr: when faced with delay, insult, or difficulty, consciously choose restraint, and help someone else do the same. Send a message of encouragement to someone enduring hardship.

This daily cycle turns time from an enemy that erodes into a friend that builds. The person who does this has reversed khusr: his capital is not lost but multiplied.

> *“By time, indeed mankind is in loss, except those who believe and do righteous deeds and exhort one another to truth and exhort one another to patience.”* (Qur'an 103:1-3)

This is the entire religion in three verses. Whoever lives by it has been saved from loss, and whoever is saved from loss has been given everything.

## Closing Reflection: Why Three Verses Suffice

**A Book in Miniature**

If Sūrah al-Fātiḥah is the mother of the Book (Umm al-Kitāb) because it contains its themes in summary, Sūrah al-ʿAṣr is its pocket companion because it contains its program in practice. Al-Fātiḥah asks for guidance to the straight path; al-ʿAṣr defines the travelers on that path. Al-Baqarah, the longest sūrah, elaborates law, history, and theology; al-ʿAṣr distills the condition for benefiting from all of it.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“Make use of your time before you have no time.”* (Paraphrase of authentic hadiths on five before five, al-Ḥākim 7846)

Sūrah al-ʿAṣr is the divine articulation of that prophetic urgency. It does not threaten with graphic imagery of Hell, nor does it promise with detailed gardens of Paradise. It does something more immediate: it swears by the afternoon light that is fading even as you read, that you are losing, unless you do four things. The brevity is itself eloquence — balāghah — because a truth about time must not waste time.

May God make us among those who believe, do righteous deeds, exhort one another to truth, and exhort one another to patience, and may He make our declining days better than our rising ones, and our last deeds better than our first.

> *“And the end of their call will be, Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds.”* (Qur'an 10:10)
