# Sūrah Ṣād (Chapter 38) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Letter, a Book, and a Court of Kings**

Sūrah Ṣād is a Makkan chapter of eighty-eight verses. The soundest reports place its revelation after Sūrah 54, al-Qamar, in the middle Makkan period, when the opposition of Quraysh had hardened from mockery into organized refusal. It takes its name from the isolated Arabic letter with which it opens — Ṣād — one of the disconnected letters (*al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*) that stand at the head of twenty-nine sūrahs. Some of the early readers also knew it as *Dāwūd*, “David,” because it contains the Qur’an’s most concentrated portrait of that prophet: his night prayer, his kingdom, his trial in judgment, his repentance, and the office of vicegerency that God then confirmed upon him.

The sūrah is not a biography. It is a courtroom. The accused in the opening movement are the notables of Makkah, who cannot bear that a man from among themselves should be sent as a warner, and who cannot bear still more that he should reduce their many gods to One. The witnesses called against them are the destroyed generations — Noah’s people, ʿĀd, Pharaoh of the tent-poles, Thamūd, the people of Lot, and the dwellers of the thicket. The living examples then brought forward are not warriors of conquest first, but servants who turned: David, Solomon, Job, then Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Ishmael, Elisha, and Dhū’l-Kifl. After their remembrance the sūrah opens two doors: a beautiful return whose gates stand unlocked, and an evil return of boiling drink and icy murk. It closes by taking the dispute back to its first origin — the assembly of the Highest Host, the creation of Adam from clay, the breathing of the Spirit, the prostration of the angels, and the arrogance of Iblīs. The last three verses return the Prophet ﷺ to his proper office: he asks no wage, he is no pretender, and the Book is only a reminder for the worlds, whose tiding will be known after a time.

**Why This Sūrah Follows What Came Before**

The commentators who read the Qur’an as a continuous address, among them al-Biqāʿī, note that Sūrah 37 ended with the promise:

> *“And Our host, surely they will be the victors.”* (Qur’an 37:173)

Sūrah Ṣād opens by naming the opposing host and declaring it already routed (38:11). What 37 promised as outcome, 38 exhibits as pattern: every party that denied the messengers was broken, and the present party will not be an exception. Another inner hinge is 37:168–169, where the Makkan objectors claimed:

> *“If only we had a reminder from those of old, then we would indeed be God’s sincere servants.”*

Verse 1 answers that claim with an oath: the Reminder has arrived, possessed of remembrance. Verse 2 then diagnoses why they still refuse it: not for lack of a Book, but because of vainglory and schism. The sūrah thus begins as a moral X-ray of rejection. The problem is not information. The problem is pride that will not share the sky with a messenger, and a social fracture that treats tawḥīd as an attack on inherited identity.

**The Architecture of the Chapter**

The chapter moves in six large arcs, each of which must be read as serving the next.

Verses 1–11 confront Quraysh: the oath by the Qur’an, the diagnosis of pride, the warning from destroyed generations, the mockery of the Prophet ﷺ as “a lying sorcerer,” the scandal of One God, the notables’ counsel of stubbornness, the jealousy that the Reminder should descend upon “him among all of us,” and the two rhetorical questions that strip them of any claim to control mercy or the heavens. Verse 11 then names them a host among the parties, already routed.

Verses 12–16 list the earlier parties and close with the disbelievers’ sarcastic prayer that their share be hastened before the Day of Reckoning. The tone is legal: each denied the messengers, so retribution came due. There is no delay in the single cry that awaits them.

Verses 17–48 turn from the doomed to the patient. The Prophet ﷺ is told to bear what they say and to remember David. The David cycle (17–26) is the theological heart of the first half: strength joined to turning, mountains and birds in glorification, sovereignty joined to wisdom and decisive speech, a trial in the sanctuary, a judgment, a falling down, forgiveness, nearness, and the office of vicegerent with the warning not to follow caprice. Verses 27–29 then lift the lens from one king to the whole cosmos: heaven and earth were not created in vain; believers are not like corrupters; the Book is sent down so that those possessed of intellect may reflect. The Solomon cycle (30–40) and the Job cycle (41–44) then show two further faces of servanthood: the one given a kingdom such as would not befit anyone after him, and the one given patience under weariness and a spring of cool water. Verses 45–48 gather a constellation of other servants so that no reader imagines that turning, strength, and sight belonged to one house alone.

Verses 49–64 set the two returns side by side: Gardens of Eden with open gates, modest companions, a provision that is never spent; then Hell as an evil resting place, boiling and murk, troops that curse one another, and the stunned question of the damned when they do not see those they had counted as evil. Verse 64 seals it: that disputing is true.

Verses 65–70 restore the Prophet’s limits: he is only a warner; God is the One, the Paramount; the message is a great tiding from which they turn away; he has no knowledge of the Highest Assembly except what is revealed.

Verses 71–85 narrate the primordial case of arrogance, so that the vainglory of verse 2 is seen to be older than Makkah. Iblīs refuses to prostrate to what God created with His two Hands, claims superiority by origin (fire over clay), is outcast, is granted respite, and vows to mislead all except the sincere. God answers with truth and with the filling of Hell.

Verses 86–88 close the brief: no wage, no pretension, a reminder for the worlds, and certain knowledge of its tiding after a time.

**The Moral Thread That Ties Kings to Clay**

If one sentence could name the sūrah’s argument, it would be this: *greatness before God is turning, not rank; and rank without turning becomes Pharaoh’s tent-poles.* David is “possessed of might,” yet the verse that names his might immediately names that he turned oft. Solomon is “how excellent a servant,” and the excellence is again that he turned. Job is “what an excellent servant,” and again he turned. The refrain *innahu awwāb* — truly he was one who turns back — is the sūrah’s definition of a servant. Against this stand two refusals to turn: the notables of Makkah, who say “be steadfast to your gods,” and Iblīs, who says “I am better than him.” The first refusal is social and political; the second is metaphysical. The sūrah insists they are the same disease.

This is why the trial of David in the sanctuary is not a scandal to be hurried past. It is the proof that even a vicegerent is safe only by returning. The command that follows — “judge among the people with truth and follow not caprice” — is addressed to David, but it is hung as a lamp over every later office: parent, judge, employer, scholar, ruler, and the private soul that sits in judgment on its own excuses. Caprice is the inner idol that corresponds to the outer idols of verse 5. The man who cannot accept One God in the public square is often the man who cannot accept one truth in the private court of his own desire.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary treats each of the eighty-eight verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order. Passages that the source commentary groups (5–7, 13–14, 21–24, 31–33, 37–38, 45–47, 53–54, 55–58, 59–60, 62–63, 65–66, 67–68, 69–70, 73–74, 77–78, 79–83) are here separated and given full individual treatment. Arabic roots are explained in plain English. Qur’anic cross-references are quoted. Authentic ḥadīth are given with their wording and collections. The views of al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Rāzī, and others are used as foundation, then expanded with reasoning, psychology, social implication, and application to ordinary modern life. Nothing is compressed because the chapter is long. The sūrah’s own counsel — “bear patiently that which they say, and remember Our servant David” — is honored in the manner of the reading: unhurried, sequential, and complete, from the letter Ṣād to the promise that its tiding will be known after a time.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:1

> **Ṣād. By the Quran possessed of remembrance.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Single Letter Set as an Oath’s Threshold**

The sūrah does not ease the listener in with narrative. It places one isolated letter — *Ṣād* — then an oath: *wa’l-Qurʾāni dhī al-dhikr*, “By the Qur’an possessed of remembrance.” Twenty-nine sūrahs open with such disconnected letters. The majority of the commentators, following the caution of the early generations, hold that their precise meaning is with God. Abū Bakr is reported to have said:

> *“Every book has a mystery, and the mystery of the Qur’an is in the openings of the sūrahs.”*

That confession is not an excuse for emptiness. It is a discipline. Before the argument against pride begins, the reciter is made to pronounce a sound that he does not own. The first word of the chapter already trains the soul that will later be told, in the story of Iblīs, that claiming to know one’s own rank is the beginning of ruin.

**What the Letter Has Been Taken to Carry**

Alongside the confession of mystery, the tradition gathered possible meanings without forcing any one of them as dogma. Some read *Ṣād* as an allusion to the Divine Names that begin with this letter: *al-Ṣamad*, the Eternally Sufficient unto Himself; *al-Ṣādiq*, the Truthful; and, in the language of later theologians, *al-Ṣāniʿ*, the Maker. Ibn Jarīr and others transmitted that it may abbreviate a phrase: *ṣadaqa Allāh*, “God has spoken the truth,” or *al-Ṣādiq Allāh*, “the Truthful One is God,” or *ṣadaqa Muḥammad*, “Muhammad has spoken the truth.” Still others took it as a name of the Qur’an itself, or simply as the name of this sūrah.

These readings are not in conflict if they are held as openings rather than as cages. A letter that can point to God’s sufficiency, God’s truthfulness, the truthfulness of the Messenger, and the Book itself is a letter standing at the exact intersection this chapter will occupy: a truthful Book, from a truthful God, on the tongue of a truthful prophet, refused by people who prefer their own rank to that truth.

**The Oath by a Book That Owns Remembrance**

God swears by the Qur’an. In Arabic rhetoric an oath elevates the object sworn by and prepares the listener for a grave statement. Here the object is described as *dhī al-dhikr* — “possessor of *dhikr*.” The word *dhikr* is among the richest in the revelation. It means remembrance, mention, reminder, and, in several places, honor. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note that in this verse many of the early authorities understood *dhikr* as *sharaf*, honor or renown, as it is understood in:

> *“And indeed it is a reminder for you and your people, and you will be questioned.”* (Qur’an 43:44)

and in:

> *“And We raised high your remembrance.”* (Qur’an 94:4)

The Book confers honor because it attaches a people to God. A tribe that recites it is no longer merely a tribe; it becomes a community of reminder. Others, including reports from Ibn ʿAbbās’s school, took *dhikr* as *bayān*, clarification, because the Book makes plain what, when reflected upon, leads to the truth.

Both senses are alive in the verse. The Qur’an is possessed of remembrance because it contains what reminds: tawḥīd, the Names and Attributes, the stories of the prophets, the recovered memory of earlier communities, the Resurrection, and the Decree. Al-Ṭūsī and others connected this to:

> *“We have neglected nothing in the Book.”* (Qur’an 6:38)

It is also possessed of honor because the one who holds to it is given a dignity that wealth and clan cannot give. The Makkans wanted honor from lineage and from the House they served with many gods. The oath answers: true honor is in the Reminder you are rejecting.

**Why an Oath Is Needed at the Door of Pride**

The next verse will diagnose the disbelievers as being in vainglory and schism. An oath before a diagnosis is medically precise. Pride does not yield to a casual remark. It yields, if it yields at all, to something that stands above it. By swearing by the Book, God places the revelation in a rank the notables cannot outtalk. They may call the Messenger a sorcerer; they cannot unswear the oath of the Lord of the worlds.

The same logic governs ordinary life. A person entrenched in self-importance is not moved by another person’s opinion. He is sometimes moved when a standard he already claims to respect is brought against him — a contract he signed, a principle he teaches his children, a truth he uses to judge others. The Qur’an, for those who still conceded that it was extraordinary speech even while denying its source, was that standard. For the believer who recites this verse now, the oath still functions: before you argue with anyone, including yourself, remember that you stand under a Book that owns remembrance. You do not own it.

**Remembrance as the Opposite of the Sūrah’s Diseases**

This chapter will speak of forgetting the Day of Reckoning (38:26), of creation supposedly made in vain (38:27), of Iblīs forgetting his place, and of people who will know the tiding only “after a time.” The first verse plants the cure before the diseases are named. *Dhikr* is not a rosary counted while the heart sleeps. It is the recovery of what the soul already knows and has covered: that God is One, that the messenger is true, that the world is not a toy, and that every host is already routed if it sets itself against the Reminder.

A driver on a mountain road at night does not need more scenery. He needs the white line and the memory of the drop. Taqwā, as the tradition has always said in other words, is driving that road awake. This verse hangs the line at the start of the chapter: *By the Qur’an possessed of remembrance.* Everything that follows — David’s turning, Solomon’s prayer, Job’s patience, Adam’s creation, Iblīs’s refusal — is that remembrance unfolding. The letter Ṣād remains, like a sealed initial on a royal decree, to remind the reader that the speech about to judge kings and satans begins in a mystery no king and no satan can counterfeit.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:2

> **Nay, but those who disbelieve are in vainglory and schism.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Particle That Refuses Their Excuse**

*Bal* — “Nay, but” — cuts off a false implication. Verse 1 has sworn by a Book full of the very reminder the Makkans claimed to want. The expected human response would be acceptance. *Bal* announces that the expected response will not come, and that the reason is not a defect in the Book. Al-Rāzī and others read the turn as: it is not that the Qur’an lacks remembrance; it is that those who disbelieve are locked in two states, *ʿizzah* and *shiqāq*.

This is a diagnostic verse, not a merely polemical one. It tells the Prophet ﷺ, and every later caller, where to look when a clear reminder is refused. Do not first assume that the wording was unclear. Look at pride and at faction.

**Vainglory as a Counterfeit of Honor**

*ʿIzzah* in praiseworthy use means honor, might, and the dignity that God gives. The Qur’an says:

> *“Whoever desires honor — then to God belongs honor altogether.”* (Qur’an 35:10)

and:

> *“Honor belongs to God, and to His Messenger, and to the believers.”* (Qur’an 63:8)

When the same word describes the disbelievers here, it has soured into vainglory: a self-bestowed exemption from being addressed. Al-Rāzī explains that, used of a human attitude, it is the belief that one is too high to follow anyone other than oneself. The portrait is completed in:

> *“And when it is said to him, ‘Reverence God,’ vainglory seizes him in sin.”* (Qur’an 2:206)

The man of *ʿizzah* in this diseased sense does not merely disagree. He takes the very invitation to God as an insult. Advice feels like humiliation. A warner “from among themselves” (38:4) feels like a social demotion. Tawḥīd feels like the dismantling of the family’s brand.

Modern life is thick with this *ʿizzah*. The executive who cannot apologize because the apology would rearrange the hierarchy; the scholar who cannot say “I do not know” because his platform is built on knowing; the young person who cannot accept a parent’s or teacher’s correction because identity has been fused with being uncorrectable — all are reciting, in secular grammar, the same verse. Vainglory is honor that has cut its cord to God and now feeds on comparison.

**Schism as Honor’s Social Form**

*Shiqāq* comes from *shaqqa*, to split. The Qur’an uses it for rupture that has become a stance:

> *“And if they believe in the like of what you believe in, then they are rightly guided; but if they turn away, then they are in schism.”* (Qur’an 2:137)

Al-Ṭūsī notes that it connotes enmity, disobedience, opposition, and defiance. Al-Rāzī adds a precise psychological hinge: a person enters *shiqāq* when he deems himself equal or superior to the one he opposes. In this verse the opposed one is the Messenger of God. The notables cannot share a public square in which Muhammad ﷺ speaks from above them by revelation. If they accept the message, their ranking system collapses. So they choose fracture over truth.

Schism is therefore not a mere difference of opinion. Difference of opinion can be sincere. Schism is difference maintained as identity. “We are the people of the old gods; he is the inventor” is easier to live with than “we were wrong.” Communities, families, and even online tribes still prefer a clean enemy to a costly correction. The verse names that preference as unbelief’s climate: they are *in* vainglory and schism, as a man is in a room. It is not a passing mood. It is an atmosphere they inhabit.

**Why the Two Diseases Travel Together**

Pride that stays private is already ruinous; pride that recruits a faction becomes a historical force. Vainglory supplies the inner refusal; schism supplies the committee, the slogan, and the social punishment for anyone who might convert. Verses 6–7 will show the notables walking out of Abū Ṭālib’s presence with a party line: “Go! Be steadfast to your gods.” That is *shiqāq* organized. The individual who might have listened in secret is now policed by the group’s honor.

The logical principle is simple. Truth is a single direction; ego is a private compass. When many private compasses are sworn to one another rather than to the north, they call their agreement “loyalty” and call the north “an astounding thing” (38:5). The sūrah will later show the same structure in Hell, where troops rush in together and then curse one another (38:59–61). Faction feels like warmth on the way down. It feels like betrayal at the bottom.

**The Pastoral Use of a Hard Verse**

The verse is addressed as a description of “those who disbelieve,” yet the believer is not forbidden to find its warning in himself. Ibn Taymiyyah’s school repeatedly noted that the diseases named in the Qur’an as kufr’s garments can appear as stains on īmān: refusing advice, loving to split, needing to win. The Prophet ﷺ said, in a report recorded by Muslim:

> *“He who has in his heart a mustard seed of pride will not enter the Garden.”* (Muslim 91)

Pride here is the pride of rejecting the truth and looking down on people, as the same ḥadīth explains. Verse 2 is that ḥadīth in Makkan weather. The Book has come. The obstacle is not the Book. The obstacle is a heart that would rather be glorious and split than small and joined to God.

A practical test follows. When a clear reminder arrives — from the Qur’an, from a friend, from a consequence — does the chest expand or armor itself? If it armors itself, *ʿizzah* has entered. If it then seeks allies to justify the armor, *shiqāq* has entered. The cure is the *dhikr* of verse 1: to remember that honor is God’s to give, and that the party worth belonging to is the party of those who turn, not the party of those who cannot be told.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:3

> **How many a generation before them have We destroyed? And they called out when the time for escape had passed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is Already an Answer**

*Kam ahlaknā min qablihim min qarn* — “How many a generation before them have We destroyed?” — is not a request for a number. *Kam* here is the *kam* of abundance. God asks the Makkans to look down the well of history they themselves recited in evening talk: ʿĀd of the lofty pillars, Thamūd of the rock-hewn houses, Pharaoh of the tents and the river. The Qur’an repeats this tour in many places, not because God needs to rehearse, but because human beings remember ruins as scenery and forget them as verdicts.

> *“How many a generation have We destroyed before them? Can you perceive of them a single one, or hear from them a murmur?”* (Qur’an 19:98)

> *“Have they not seen how many a generation We destroyed before them?”* (Qur’an 36:31)

Verse 3 of Ṣād belongs to that family, but it adds a sound: they called out. Destruction in this verse is not mute. It has a last cry.

**The Cry That Comes After the Door Has Shut**

*Fanādaw wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣ* — they called out, and it was not the moment of escape. *Manāṣ* is a place or time of flight, a way out. *Lāta* is a particle of negation used for time: it is not the hour. They did not fail to cry. They failed to cry when crying was still a door. Al-Ṭabarī and the commentators on parallel passages point to towns that, when they felt Our might:

> *“they ran from it. ‘Do not run! Return to that in which you were made to live luxuriously, and to your dwellings, that you may be questioned.’”* (Qur’an 21:12–13)

and to those who, when the punishment came, cried for help while still arguing:

> *“Do not cry out today. Indeed, you will not be helped from Us.”* (Qur’an 23:65)

The most famous individual instance is Pharaoh at the sea:

> *“Until, when drowning overtook him, he said, ‘I believe that there is no god but He in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am of those who submit.’”* (Qur’an 10:90)

The reply came that this was not the hour:

> *“Now? When you had disobeyed before and were of the corrupters?”* (Qur’an 10:91)

Verse 3 compresses all such scenes into one law: there is a time when warning is mercy, and a time when the same words are only noise in a collapsing house.

**Why God Destroys Generations at All**

The verse must be read with the sūrah’s later insistence that heaven and earth were not created in vain (38:27) and that the reverent are not like the profligate (38:28). Destruction of a *qarn* — a generation, a people bound by a time — is not cosmic moodiness. It is the moral structure of a world that has meaning. If communities can deny messengers, devour the weak, and mock the Reminder without remainder, then history is a game and verse 27 is false. The ruined generations are the evidence that the world is not a game.

Human responsibility is not cancelled by Divine decree in this verse. They were destroyed *after* warning and *after* a period in which escape was possible. The cry proves they understood at the end what they had refused in the middle. Understanding that arrives only as the roof falls is still understanding — but it is not repentance that the law of this world will treat as a new beginning. The door of *tawbah* is wide while the sun is up. At a certain noon known to God, for a people or a person, the sun of that particular chance sets.

**The Psychology of Too-Late Knowledge**

Everyone knows a smaller version of this verse. The student who begins the book the night after the exam; the patient who believes the doctor only in the ambulance; the spouse who discovers tenderness after the other has gone; the nation that legislates safety after the preventable disaster. The knowledge was not unavailable. The will to use it was. Pride (verse 2) delays the cry until delay itself becomes the punishment.

The analogy of taqwā as careful driving is exact here. A driver who ignores the barrier on a mountain pass may scream when the car leaves the road. The scream is sincere. It is also useless as steering. The verse is not mocking the scream. It is warning the living not to postpone their scream until it can only echo.

**What the Verse Does to the Listener Still Alive**

For Quraysh, the verse said: you are not the first notables, and ruins older than your Kaʿbah already answered your experiment. For a reciter now, it says two further things.

First, do not confuse God’s patience with God’s absence. The generations had time. That time was the trap of those who thought delay meant immunity. The Prophet ﷺ said, in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *“God gives respite to the wrongdoer until, when He seizes him, He does not let him go.”* Then he recited: *“Such is the seizing of your Lord when He seizes the towns while they are doing wrong. Indeed His seizing is painful, severe.”* (al-Bukhārī 4686; Muslim 2583; the verse is 11:102)

Second, call out now. The whole religion of turning — David’s *awwāb*, Solomon’s *awwāb*, Job’s *awwāb* — is the opposite of this verse’s last clause. They called when there was no escape. The servants remembered in this sūrah called while the door was open, and some of them called from under the weight of their own mistakes. That is why their stories are placed after this warning. Verse 3 is the locked gate. Verse 17 is the living man who still knows how to turn the handle.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:4

> **Now they marvel that a warner from among themselves has come unto them, and the disbelievers say, “This is a lying sorcerer.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wonder Aimed at the Wrong Object**

*Wa-ʿajibū* — they marveled, they found it strange. Human beings are built to wonder; revelation often honors wonder when it is aimed at the heavens, the self, or the Book. Here wonder is misfired. They do not marvel that God should speak, or that idols of stone cannot hear. They marvel that a warner has come *minhum*, from among themselves: an orphan of Banū Hāshim, who eats food and walks the markets, who was known among them as al-Amīn before he was known as a prophet.

The same astonishment is recorded almost identically in:

> *“Nay, they marvel that a warner from among them has come to them, so the disbelievers say, ‘This is a strange thing.’”* (Qur’an 50:2)

and in the complaint:

> *“What ails this Messenger, who eats food and walks in the markets? Why is there not an angel sent down unto him to be a warner with him?”* (Qur’an 25:7)

They wanted either a being who did not share their flesh, so that they would not have to measure themselves by him, or a man already sitting on their highest seat, so that revelation would confirm the existing ladder. A warner from among themselves does neither. He is close enough to be compared, and unofficial enough to rearrange comparison.

**Why a Human Warner Is Mercy, Not a Defect**

The Qur’an elsewhere explains the wisdom they refused:

> *“Say: ‘Had there been in the earth angels walking in tranquility, We would have sent down upon them from the heaven an angel as messenger.’”* (Qur’an 17:95)

A messenger is a model, not a spectacle. Angels do not marry, hunger, grieve, or negotiate with uncles. If the warner is not from among the warned, the warned can always say, “He does not know how this feels.” God closed that excuse by sending a man who buried children, carried goods, suffered hunger in the siege, bled at Uḥud, and still stood to pray at night until his feet swelled. Marveling at his humanity is like marveling that a physician has a body. The body is how he enters the ward.

**The Twin Smear: Sorcery and Lying**

When marveling does not suffice as a defense, they name him: *sāḥirun kadhdhāb*, a lying sorcerer. The pairing is instructive. A sorcerer, in their usage, is one whose words and acts affect people by hidden means; a liar is one whose report does not correspond to reality. Together the two charges try to cover both the power of the Qur’an and its truth-claim. If hearts are moved, it is magic. If news of resurrection is given, it is invention. They will not allow a third category: truthful speech that changes people because it is from God.

The same file was opened against earlier messengers. Pharaoh’s court said of Moses:

> *“This is a knowing sorcerer.”* (Qur’an 26:34)

The chiefs of Noah’s people used other words for the same move: not a prophet, but a man who wants preference. Of Jesus the Qur’an records that he was accused of sorcery after clear signs (5:110; 61:6). The repetition is itself a proof. A smear that must be recycled across centuries is not an analysis; it is a reflex of threatened power.

**The Social Function of a Label**

Calling a man a lying sorcerer does work that argument cannot do. Argument would require sitting with the Qur’an. A label allows a notable to warn his dependents without thinking: do not listen; it is sorcery; you will be enchanted. It also protects the notable from his own heart. If a verse strikes him in private, he can tell himself that the strike is a spell, not a proof.

This mechanism is familiar wherever inconvenient truth appears. Reformers, whistleblowers, and even ordinary family members who name a harm are quickly given a psychiatric or moral diagnosis that makes listening unnecessary. The verse teaches the listener to inspect the function of the name. Does “sorcerer” explain the speech, or does it exempt the hearer from the speech?

**From Among Themselves: The Test of Nearness**

There is a further sting in *minhum*. It is easier to accept a sage from a distant land than a warner who knows one’s childhood name. Distance supplies romance; nearness supplies embarrassment. “We know his father, his uncle, his former trade” is treated as a refutation, when it is in fact a credential: you know he was not a pretender (38:86). The Prophet ﷺ lived among them until forty without claiming a throne or a rhyme. Their knowledge of him was evidence. They converted it into an objection.

In daily life the same nearness-test continues. People will take advice from a distant lecture and refuse it from a spouse. They will quote a dead scholar and ignore a living parent. They will accept a principle in a book and deny it when it arrives in the mouth of a sibling. Verse 4 says that this is an old unbelief, not a personal quirk. God sends the reminder through someone close enough to cost pride. The marvel is not that the warner is human. The marvel, if there is to be one, is that God should care enough to warn at all — and that some hearts would rather invent sorcery than accept a gift that arrives in a familiar face.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:5

> **Has he made the gods one God? Truly this is an astounding thing!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence in Which Their Theology Collapses**

The notables do not first complain of the Prophet’s character, nor of the Qur’an’s language. They complain of arithmetic: *a-jaʿala al-ālihata ilāhan wāḥidan* — has he made the gods one God? The verb *jaʿala* here means to render, to appoint, to turn something into something. In their hearing, Muhammad ﷺ is not reporting what is. He is performing a reduction, as if tawḥīd were a political downsizing of the pantheon. What is in fact discovery of the Real they experience as demolition of their world.

*Inna hādhā la-shayʾun ʿujāb* — truly this is a thing *ʿujāb*, intensely astonishing. Arabic intensifies *ʿajīb* into *ʿujāb* to mark wonder that has gone beyond curiosity into outrage. They are not puzzled. They are scandalized.

**Why One God Felt Like a Catastrophe**

Al-Ṭūsī records that some among Quraysh feared that abandoning the idols would bring famine and distress. The idols were not only statues. They were insurance, tourism, clan badges, and the gravestones of ancestors. To make the gods one God threatened the economic rites around the Kaʿbah, the pride of each tribe in its patron, and the comforting idea that divinity could be negotiated by gift and proximity. One God who needs no partners cannot be flattered with a share. One God who sends a Book cannot be managed by a priesthood of custom.

Their reasoning, as al-Rāzī notes, was also genealogical: if we did not hear this from our predecessors, it must be false. The creed of the fathers is treated as a closed canon. Verse 7 will say this explicitly. Verse 5 is the cry that precedes the argument: the ear has heard *lā ilāha illā Allāh* and the chest has heard a funeral for the old self.

**The Historical Scene Behind the Cry**

The commentaries, drawing on Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī, preserve the report of the meeting with Abū Ṭālib. The leaders came to complain of the nephew’s preaching. Abū Ṭālib asked the Prophet ﷺ what he wanted of his people. He answered that he wanted from them a single word which, if they said it, the Arabs would follow them and the non-Arabs would pay them tribute. They, astonished at the cheapness of the price, offered ten words. Abū Ṭālib asked what the word was. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“There is no god but God.”*

They rose and said, in the wording this verse records, that he had made the gods one God, and that this was astounding. Other reports make this exchange the occasion of verses 1–7. Whether every chain is of equal strength, the fit with the text is tight: a single declaration, a council of notables, a walkout.

**The Logic They Would Not Face**

If there are many gods, they must either cooperate or compete. If they cooperate perfectly, they function as one will, and the many are theater. If they compete, the cosmos should show rifts of command. The Qur’an makes that argument elsewhere:

> *“Had there been in them gods besides God, they would have been ruined.”* (Qur’an 21:22)

> *“God has not taken any son, nor is there any god with Him, for then each god would have taken what it created, and some would have overcome others.”* (Qur’an 23:91)

Verse 5 is the sound of people refusing that logic because their lives were built on the opposite. They needed many doors so that no one Door could command the whole of them — money, ritual, vendetta, and desire. One God means one judgment. That is the astounding thing.

**Astounding as a Mirror**

The verse lets them speak, and in speaking they indict themselves. What ought to be astounding is that a piece of wood or stone should be called a god. What they find astounding is unity. The inversion is the signature of *jāhiliyyah*: the normal is shocking, the shocking is normal. Pharaoh found Moses’ call astounding. Thamūd found the she-camel astounding. Every age has its *ʿujāb*. In one century it is tawḥīd against idols; in another it is moral limits against appetite; in another it is the resurrection against a closed material world. The test is not whether a teaching startles. The test is whether the startle is the startle of truth entering a crowded house.

For the reciter, the verse is also a caution against a pious counterfeit of their cry. It is possible to say “God is One” and still keep a drawer of smaller gods — career, image, party, nation, the self. Ibn ʿAbbās and later commentators on *taghūt* repeatedly warned that anything obeyed against God functions as a god. The notables of Makkah at least knew that tawḥīd would cost them. A Muslim who finds One God unsurprising may not yet have let the sentence into the places where he still says, in practice, “Has He made my loves one Love?”

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:6

> **And the notables among them set out, [saying], “Go! Be steadfast to your gods. Truly this is a thing desired.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**When the Elite Become a Procession of Refusal**

*Wa-inṭalaqa al-malaʾu minhum* — the *malaʾ*, the notables, the filled assembly, set out. The verb *inṭalaqa* is used of people who launch forth, who leave a gathering with a decision already made. They did not remain to ask, to compare the Qur’an with what they knew of Abraham, or to test the Prophet’s truthfulness by the life they had watched for forty years. They departed. Movement here is a verdict.

The *malaʾ* is a Qur’anic type. Noah’s notables said he was only a man who wanted preference. Pharaoh’s notables said Moses was a sorcerer. The notables of ʿĀd and Thamūd mocked the idea that a human like themselves should be followed. Sūrah 34 states the pattern as a law of cities:

> *“We sent no warner to a town but that its affluent said, ‘We disbelieve in that with which you have been sent.’”* (Qur’an 34:34)

Verse 6 is that law in Makkan dress. Those with the most to lose from a reordering of sacred capital become the shepherds of other people’s steadfastness — not steadfastness toward truth, but toward the gods that keep the capital in place.

**“Go” as a Command to Stop Thinking**

*Ani’mshū* — go, walk on, keep moving. It is the language of crowd management. A person standing still might hear the next verse of the Qur’an. A person walking in a group with a slogan will hear the slogan. The notables do not say “refute him.” They say “leave.” Distancing is easier than disproof.

*Iṣbirū ʿalā ālihatikum* — be steadfast upon your gods. *Ṣabr* is one of the Qur’an’s great virtues. Here it is commandeered. Patience, which in David and Job will mean holding to God under pain, is made to mean holding to idols under argument. Every false cause eventually steals a virtue’s name. People are told to be loyal, brave, authentic, or free — when the act required is simply not to think. The verse trains the ear to ask: steadfast toward what?

**“A Thing Desired” and the Fear of Losing a World**

*Inna hādhā la-shayʾun yurād* — truly this is a thing desired, a thing intended. The commentaries differ slightly on who is the desirer. Some take it as: this unification of the gods is a thing Muhammad desires, a bid for mastery over us. Some take it as: this is a thing desired against our gods, a plot. Some hear in it: this is a thing God has willed to test us, so hold fast. In all readings the notables interpret tawḥīd as a project rather than as a report. If the message is a man’s desire, it can be resisted as ambition. If it is God’s speech, resistance is war with the Real.

The social logic is transparent. If the many gods fall, the notables’ role as custodians falls. “Be steadfast to your gods” means, at the same time, “be steadfast to us.” Religion here is identity-protection. The verse is therefore a lesson in reading religious language for its payload. Not every call to conserve the old is piety. Sometimes it is the old privilege asking to be loved as if it were God.

**How Committees Manufacture Unbelief**

An individual Makkan might have been moved. The *malaʾ* make sure that movement is costly. They leave together, speak in the plural, and issue a marching order. Unbelief becomes a public good they supply to one another. Modern analogues are not hard: a board that decides a fraud is “how business is done”; a family that agrees never to mention a crime; a circle of friends that mocks the one member who begins to pray. The words differ. The structure is verse 6: the notable few set out, telling the rest to go and to be steadfast to the local gods.

The believer who recites this is warned twice. Do not be surprised when truth is opposed by people with titles. And do not, if you yourself become a notable in some small realm — an older sibling, a manager, a popular voice — use your *ṣabr*-language to keep others from God. Steadfastness is owed to the One who does not need your committee. The gods that need you to defend them are already dead.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:7

> **We did not hear of this in the creed of latter days. This is naught but an invention.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Appeal to a Recent Orthodoxy**

*Mā samiʿnā bi-hādhā fī al-millati al-ākhirah* — we have not heard of this in the latter creed, the religion of recent times. The commentators mention two main identifications of that *millah*. One is the inherited paganism of Quraysh themselves, the “religion of the fathers” as currently practiced. The other, reported from several early authorities, is the Christianity known to them in the region — a creed they associated, wrongly or loosely, with a complex of persons around God — so that a stark “one God” without a son and without their idols sounded like a novelty against both the local and the neighboring “last religions.”

In either case the argument is the same: what we have not heard, in the version of religion that reached us last, cannot be true. Recency and familiarity are made into proofs. The verse captures a universal fallacy: the last thing we were taught is treated as the first thing God said.

**Invention as the Only Remaining Category**

*In hādhā illā ikhtilāq* — this is nothing but an invention, a fabrication, something made up. The same charge is scattered across the Makkan Qur’an:

> *“Those who disbelieve say, ‘This is only a lie he has invented.’”* (Qur’an 25:4)

> *“Or do they say, ‘He has invented it’? Say: ‘If I have invented it, then you have no power for me against God at all.’”* (Qur’an 46:8)

Noah heard it. Moses heard it from Pharaoh. It is the cheapest way to close a revelation: if it is new to *my* ear, it is new to the world, and if it is new to the world, a man made it.

They did not ask whether Abraham, whose house they claimed, had heard of One God. They did not ask whether “the creed of latter days” might itself be the invention — layers of custom poured over an older tawḥīd. The Qur’an will later remind them that they are to follow the millah of Abraham the upright, who was not of the idolaters. Verse 7 is the sound of people measuring the primordial by the provincial.

**Why “We Did Not Hear” Is Not Evidence**

Absence of hearing is a fact about the hearer. It is not a fact about the thing. A man who has never left his valley has not heard of the sea; the sea is not therefore an *ikhtilāq*. Quraysh had heard of Abraham, of a House built for one God, of ḥanīfs among them who rejected idols. What they meant was: we have not heard this as the public creed of our establishment. That is a confession of the establishment, not a refutation of the Prophet.

Al-Rāzī notes that they reasoned: if this were true, our predecessors would have had it. The hidden premise is that predecessors cannot err in clusters. The sūrah has already smashed that premise in verse 3: generations were destroyed. The dead are not a proof merely by being many and merely by being ours.

**The Modern Face of the Latter Creed**

Every age has a *millah ākhirah* — the latest consensus that calls itself simply “how things are.” It may be the religion of the ancestors, or the religion of progress, or the religion of the market, or the religion of the nation. When a reminder from God arrives that does not match that latest creed, the reflex is identical: we did not hear this in the respectable conversation; therefore it is invented. The verse asks the reciter to reverse the test. Do not ask first whether tawḥīd matches the age. Ask whether the age matches tawḥīd.

There is a personal version as well. A Muslim raised in slogans may meet, in adult reading, a verse that contradicts the family tone or the party line — on sincerity, on wealth, on the tongue, on the rights of women or neighbors or the poor. “We did not hear this” is then a temptation. The right reply is David’s reply later in the sūrah: to fall down and turn, not to call the Book an invention because the house was quiet about it.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:8

> **Has the Reminder been sent down upon him among all of us? Nay, but they are in doubt regarding My Reminder! Nay, they have not yet tasted My Punishment!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jealousy Speaking in the Grammar of Theology**

Verse 4 was astonishment that a human warner had come. Verse 8 is astonishment that *this* human was chosen: *a-unzila ʿalayhi al-dhikru min bayninā* — has the Reminder been sent down upon him from among us? The objection has shifted from species to status. If God must send a man, they imply, let Him send a man we would have elected.

The parallel is explicit in:

> *“And they said, ‘Why was this Qur’an not sent down upon a great man from the two towns?’”* (Qur’an 43:31)

Makkah and Ṭāʾif had their magnates. An orphan under an uncle’s protection did not look like a “great man” in the ledger of caravans. The verse exposes the ledger. They are not defending a theory of prophecy. They are defending a theory of themselves.

**God’s “Nay” Twice**

The reply is not a committee explanation of why Muhammad ﷺ was chosen. It is two *bal*s from God.

First: *bal hum fī shakkin min dhikrī* — rather, they are in doubt regarding My Reminder. The problem is not the recipient. The problem is the gift. Even if the Reminder had fallen on the man of their choice, the same hearts would have found another clause to dispute — as they did with Moses, who had palace access, and with Jesus, who had signs they called magic. Doubt toward the Reminder dresses itself as a personnel question so that it need not become a repentance question.

Second: *bal lammā yadhūqū ʿadhābī* — rather, they have not yet tasted My punishment. Al-Rāzī notes that their doubt continues because the consequence has not yet been sensed. Taste is a verb of direct experience. Argument has reached them; pain has not. Some commentators allow *bal* here as confirmatory rather than adversative: indeed they are in doubt; indeed they will taste. Either way the verse binds doubt to delay. They treat the absence of punishment as the absence of truth, which is the same error verse 3 corrected by pointing to generations who also cried too late.

**Election Is Mercy’s Prerogative**

The next verse will ask whether they hold the treasuries of the Lord’s mercy. Verse 8 prepares that question. Prophethood is not a civic appointment. God says:

> *“God knows best where He places His message.”* (Qur’an 6:124)

The choice of Muhammad ﷺ — known truthfulness, isolation from the corrupt incentives of the very rich, a heart prepared in orphanhood for dependence on God — is wisdom. But the verse does not even grant them the debate on terms of résumé. To demand that revelation ratify the existing ranking is to want a god who is chaplain to the clan.

**Doubt, Taste, and the Education of the Heart**

There is a mercy in “they have not yet tasted.” The door is still open. The punishment is spoken of as unsampled, not as impossible to avoid. The warning is therefore still a warning. For the reciter the verse has a second edge. Spiritual doubt is sometimes an intellectual problem. Often it is a moral one: the will does not want this reminder to be from God because of what life would have to become. Jealousy, status, and delayed consequence keep the doubt warm.

The Prophet ﷺ did not answer their personnel objection by listing his virtues. He continued to warn. The believer who is passed over for a role, or who sees a younger or poorer person given a trust, is being examined by verse 8 in miniature. Will he say, “Has the reminder been sent down upon him among all of us?” or will he remember that mercy’s treasuries are not in his pocket? The notables’ question is the Iblīs-question early: why him, not me? The sūrah will end by letting Iblīs say it in the language of fire and clay. Here it is said in the language of Makkan rank. Same root. Same cure: submit to the Chooser.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:9

> **Or have they the treasuries of thy Lord’s Mercy, the Mighty, the Bestower?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Empties Their Hands**

*Am ʿindahum khazāʾinu raḥmati rabbika* — or are the treasuries of your Lord’s mercy with them? The *am* continues the rebuke of verse 8. If they object to the descent of the Reminder upon Muhammad ﷺ, they must be claiming a right to apportion prophecy. That right would mean they hold the keys of *raḥmah*. The verse asks them to produce the keys.

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Ṭūsī take the treasuries here as, first, the stores of prophethood: do they decide upon whom revelation alights? The verse also opens, by its own wording, onto every mercy — livelihood, talent, beauty, authority, the next breath. The parallel is:

> *“Is it they who apportion the mercy of your Lord? We have apportioned among them their livelihood in the life of this world and have raised some of them above others in ranks, that some of them may take others in service. And the mercy of your Lord is better than what they amass.”* (Qur’an 43:32)

They cannot distribute rain. They distribute, at most, goods God already placed in their hands. From that tiny agency they infer a right to distribute prophecy. The inference is a child’s.

**The Mighty and the Bestower: Two Names That End the Debate**

The treasuries belong to *al-ʿAzīz al-Wahhāb*. *Al-ʿAzīz* is the Mighty, the One who cannot be thwarted, whose honor does not borrow from voters. *Al-Wahhāb* is the Bestower, the One who gives again and again without being compelled and without exhausting the store. The pairing is exact. Might without giving would be a closed fortress; giving without might would be a well that anyone could seize. God is both: no one forces Him to give, and no one limits what He gives.

Ibn ʿAbbās’s tradition and the later theologians treated such pairings as tafsīr of one another. Mercy is not a weak kindness. It is the gift of the One who could withhold. Prophethood given to an orphan is not an administrative error. It is *al-Wahhāb* displaying that the treasuries are His.

**The Human Instinct to Hoard Mercy**

The Prophet ﷺ is told in another verse to say:

> *“If you possessed the treasuries of my Lord’s mercy, you would withhold them for fear of spending. And man is ever miserly.”* (Qur’an 17:100)

Verse 9 of Ṣād and 17:100 are one teaching. If the notables held the keys, they would not have opened them upon Muhammad ﷺ. They would have opened them upon themselves. The fear of spending is the economic form of the jealousy of verse 8. Mercy, in their hands, would become a ration for the already important.

This is not only a Makkan vice. Anyone who treats knowledge as a club, who withholds a job from the more able out of threat, who cannot rejoice at another’s gift, is handling a treasury as if it were his. Parents do it when they try to script a child’s path as if guidance were an inheritance they own. Scholars do it when they speak as if understanding were a private well. The Names in the verse correct all of this: the Mighty does not need your permission; the Bestower does not need your supply chain.

**Living Under a Sky You Do Not Own**

The practical yield is contentment without passivity. If the treasuries are God’s, then another’s gift is not your theft, and your gift is not your essence. You work, you ask, you take means — and you do not appoint yourself distributor of prophethood, talent, or love. The Makkans failed a theological exam about revelation. Most people fail a smaller exam about envy. Both exams are passed by the same sentence: the keys are with *al-ʿAzīz al-Wahhāb*.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:10

> **Or have they sovereignty over the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them? Then let them ascend by some means.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Keys of Mercy to the Throne Itself**

The questions climb. Verse 9 asked if they have the treasuries of mercy. Verse 10 asks if they have *mulk* — sovereignty, kingship, ownership — over the heavens and the earth and what is between them. If they object to God’s placement of revelation, perhaps they imagine themselves landlords of the cosmos. The verse offers them their claim back as a dare.

Similar dares appear in:

> *“Or have they a share in the kingdom?”* (Qur’an 4:53)

and in the scorn of those who would legislate a religion God did not permit (42:21). Here the share claimed would be total: heaven, earth, and the interval of air, cloud, death, and time.

**“Then Let Them Ascend” as Unmasking**

*Fal-yartaqū fī al-asbāb* — then let them ascend by the means, the cords, the ways. *Asbāb* are ropes, causes, connections, the ladders a person might climb. Ibn Jarīr and others take it as: let them rise through the gates of heaven by their own devices, and then send down revelation upon whom they choose. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī hear the same: if the kingdom is theirs, let them mount and administer it.

The image is close to:

> *“Whoever thinks that God will not help him in this world and the Hereafter, let him stretch a rope to the heaven, then cut it, and see whether his stratagem removes what enrages him.”* (Qur’an 22:15)

Both verses reduce rage against God’s disposal to a physical experiment the angry cannot perform. The man who cannot climb the sky should not claim to staff the office of prophecy.

**Sovereignty as the Hidden Claim Inside Every Shirk**

Idolatry is often discussed as a mistake about statues. Verse 10 says it is also a mistake about *mulk*. To assign partners to God, or to veto His messenger, is to behave as if shareholding in the universe were possible. The Qur’an answers:

> *“Blessed is He in whose hand is the kingdom, and He is over all things Powerful.”* (Qur’an 67:1)

> *“That is God, your Lord. To Him belongs the kingdom. There is no god but He. So how are you turned away?”* (Qur’an 39:6)

The notables of Makkah did not say, in so many words, “we own the heavens.” They only wanted a veto. The verse tells them that a veto over revelation is a claim on the throne. Small shirk is large shirk in seed.

**Means Without Mastery**

*Asbāb* is a word that also dignifies ordinary causation. God runs the world by means: rain by wind, birth by marriage, knowledge by study. Human beings are commanded to take means. They are forbidden to worship means. The verse’s sarcasm falls on those who would take means as a ladder into God’s prerogative. You may ascend a mountain by a path. You may not ascend into the distribution of mercy.

The modern form of the dare is technological. Human beings climb high — orbits, genomes, markets. Each climb is still inside “what is between the heavens and the earth.” None of it is *mulk* over the heavens and the earth. A culture that can photograph a galaxy and still not own tomorrow is being addressed by verse 10. Let them ascend by some means. When they have finished ascending, they will still need the Reminder, and they will still not choose the prophet.

For the individual, the verse cuts a smaller boast: “I run my life.” The reciter who cannot command sleep, cannot command the heart of a child, cannot command the hour of his death, should not rage when God places a gift or a command where He wills. David will be given *mulk* later in this sūrah, and even that *mulk* will be a loan with a warning against caprice. The only unborrowed sovereignty is the one this verse denies to the objectors.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:11

> **a host among the parties yonder routed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Like a Banner Already Fallen**

The grammar of verse 11 hangs as a predicate upon the people just described. They are *jundun mā hunālika mahzūm min al-aḥzāb* — a host, some host yonder, routed from among the parties. The wording is compressed, almost like a field dispatch. *Jund* is an army. *Mā* here diminishes: some host, a mere host. *Hunālika*, “yonder,” points away — to a place or a time not identical with the speaker’s present. *Mahzūm* is already defeated, broken, its banners down. *Al-aḥzāb* are the confederate parties, the word this sūrah will use in verse 13 for the destroyed nations, and the word other sūrahs use for the confederates who besieged Madinah.

The effect is to refuse Quraysh the dignity of uniqueness. They are not the exception in history. They are one more regiment in a file whose end is known.

**Yonder as Future Defeat and as Distant Pattern**

Ibn Jarīr and others transmitted that some of the early commentators heard in *hunālika* the Battle of Badr, years later in 2/624, when a small Muslim host met the “great men” of Makkah and the notables of verses 5–8 fell or returned stripped. Others, recorded by al-Ṭūsī, pointed to the Battle of the Trench in 5/627, when the *aḥzāb* in the later, technical sense — the confederates — were turned back in the wind and the cold. Both readings treat the verse as a promise: the host that now boasts will be the host that breaks.

Another reading, equally native to the Arabic, treats *yonder* as the already-visible field of the past: look there, at those parties, a host routed. Verses 12–14 then name them. In that reading verse 11 is a caption over the ruins, and Quraysh are invited to see themselves in the caption.

The two readings do not fight. The past is the pattern; Badr and the Trench are the pattern arriving. God’s host will be the victors (37:173). This host among the parties is routed. The sūrah’s opening movement ends not with a plea but with a result.

**Why Naming Them a “Host” Is a Mercy of Scale**

They thought of themselves as the guardians of the House, the heart of the Arabs, the unconquerable town. The verse calls them a *jund*, a unit, and then less than that: *jundun mā*, some host or other. Pride is treated with proportion. A man who has never left his city thinks the city is the world. A verse that places him among “the parties” returns him to a map on which ʿĀd and Pharaoh are already ink.

This is how taqwā works at the level of nations. Taqwā in a person is driving the mountain road awake. Taqwā in a people is reading the wrecks in the valley and not repeating the speed. Verse 11 is a wreck-sign planted before verses 12–14 list the wrecks.

**Rout as Spiritual Condition Before It Is Military**

*Mahzūm* can be heard, even before Badr, as a description of their argument. A host that has already lost the logic of verse 5 — that cannot answer One God except with walkout and slogan — is routed in meaning. Military loss, when it comes, only makes the inner loss visible. Many groups in later history have remained rich and armed while being *mahzūm* in the only sense that lasts: they cannot tell the truth about God or about themselves.

The reciter who is not of Quraysh still needs the verse. Every ego assembles a small host — excuses, allies, stored grievances — and calls it invincible. The verse says: a host among the parties yonder, routed. Better to join the servants who turn (38:17, 30, 44) than to keep drilling a regiment that has already been named defeated in the speech of God.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:12

> **The people of Noah denied before them, as did ʿĀd and Pharaoh of the tent poles.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The File of Denial Is Opened by Name**

After calling Quraysh a routed host among the parties, the revelation names the parties. First: *kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥ* — the people of Noah denied before them. Denial (*takdhīb*) is the opposite of the Book “possessed of remembrance.” Remembrance says: this has happened. Denial says: this has not, and the man who says it is a liar.

Noah’s story is told at length in Sūrahs 7, 10, 11, 23, 26, 54, and 71. He remained among his people, by one Qur’anic number, a thousand years less fifty, calling them by night and day. Their notables said he was only a man like them who wanted preference. They were drowned. The first name in verse 12 is therefore the longest patience and the most total water.

**ʿĀd: Arabian Memory, Not Distant Myth**

Second: *wa-ʿĀd*. ʿĀd were not a Mesopotamian tale. They were an Arabian memory, the people of Hūd, possessors of strength and lofty constructions, who said:

> *“Who is stronger than we in might?”* (Qur’an 41:15)

God answered with a wind. By placing ʿĀd immediately after Noah, the verse brings the ruin closer to the listener’s sand. Quraysh could treat the Flood as another people’s story. They could not treat ʿĀd as foreign. Their own poets knew the name.

**Pharaoh of the Tent-Poles**

Third: *wa-Firʿawnu dhū al-awtād*. The epithet is used again in 89:10. *Awtād* are tent-pegs, stakes, poles. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that the poles refer to the size of Pharaoh’s army: many tents mean many stakes, and many stakes mean a host that holds a land as a tent holds to earth. Others related that he tortured with four stakes, spreading a victim and leaving him to die. Others heard in *awtād* the Arabic idiom of “firm pillars,” a man of terrible stability in oppression.

All three glosses serve one portrait. Pharaoh is not named here for his river or his palaces first, but for the apparatus that pinned his power — army, torture, the claim of being the high lord. He denied Moses as the people of Noah denied Noah and as ʿĀd denied Hūd. The technologies of denial differ. The verb *kadhdhabat* is the same.

**Why These Three Open the List**

Noah is the warning after which a world was washed. ʿĀd is the warning in Arabia’s own genealogy of might. Pharaoh is the warning in the register of state power, the one who said “I am your lord most high.” Together they tell Quraysh: neither ancient time, nor tribal strength, nor royal administration saved the deniers. Your town is smaller than all three.

The ethical yield is not antiquarian. Denial of messengers is not an academic error. It is a people’s decision to keep their *awtād* — the stakes of their system — when God sends a hand to pull them up. Modern Pharaohs still have tent-poles: security services, propaganda, the sacralizing of the ruler. Modern ʿĀd still ask who is stronger than they. Modern Noah-peoples still laugh at a man who builds an ark on dry land, which is to say, who prepares for a world their market cannot price. Verse 12 is not only history. It is a classification of the stakes we still drive into the ground.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:13

> **Thamūd, the people of Lot, and the inhabitants of the thicket; they are the parties.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The List Continues Toward the Listener’s Neighborhood**

Thamūd, who denied Ṣāliḥ, hewed houses from rock and hamstrung the she-camel that was God’s sign. Their ruin was a cry, a blast. The people of Lot denied a prophet who forbade their violence and their sexual injustice; their towns were turned upside down. The inhabitants of the thicket — *aṣḥāb al-aykah* — are, in the dominant identification, the people of Shuʿayb, dwellers among dense trees, perhaps near Madyan, who cheated in measure and mocked the man who called them to fair dealing and to God.

The three names complete a moral map. Thamūd: strength in stone, contempt for a sacred limit. Lot’s people: desire without covenant, hostility to the guest and to the prophet who sheltered guests. The thicket: commerce without justice, piety of the grove without piety of the scale. Quraysh, a trading town proud of a stone House and not innocent of the other two diseases, could find themselves in more than one mirror.

**“They Are the Parties”**

*Ulāʾika al-aḥzāb* — those are the parties. The demonstrative gathers Noah’s people, ʿĀd, Pharaoh, Thamūd, Lot’s people, and the thicket into one confederacy across time. They never signed a treaty with one another. God classifies them as one because they shared a deed: denial of messengers. Ibn Jarīr and al-Rāzī note that the phrase also tells Quraysh: you are another of these parties. Ibn Kathīr and others add that it can mean “they were *the* parties” — the true coalitions of power in their days, wealthier and mightier than Makkah — and they were still routed. Either you join that file, or you learn from it.

The word *aḥzāb* will later name the confederates around Madinah. The Qur’an is teaching its first audience, before any trench is dug, that confederacy against a prophet is an old international. It does not need simultaneous calendars. It needs a shared refusal.

**Classification as a Moral Science**

Human beings classify by race, market, and flag. This verse classifies by response to revelation. A poor believer in Makkah is not of Pharaoh’s party. A rich denier in Makkah is. That re-sorting is among the Qur’an’s most socially explosive acts. It unmakes the *malaʾ* of verse 6 as the true “us.” The true parties are two: those who deny, and those who turn.

For a modern reader the thicket is especially close. Few live in ʿĀd’s pillars. Many live among trees of consumption, manipulating measures — advertising, debt, the thumb on the scale — while keeping a decoration of religion. Shuʿayb’s people thought the grove and the trade were enough. The verse sets them in the same line as Pharaoh. Injustice in the market and tyranny in the palace are not different religions. They are different tents of the same camp.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:14

> **Each of them did naught but deny the messengers; so My Retribution came due.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Crime, Many Addresses**

*In kullun illā kadhdhaba al-rusul* — there was not one of them but that he denied the messengers. The plural *al-rusul* is important. A people may have been sent one messenger, yet to deny him is to deny the office, and thus to deny all who carried it. The Qur’an says of this solidarity of revelation:

> *“We make no distinction between any of His messengers.”* (Qur’an 2:285)

and of those who pick and choose:

> *“Those who disbelieve in God and His messengers and wish to make a distinction between God and His messengers… it is they who are the disbelievers in truth.”* (Qur’an 4:150–151)

Verse 14 therefore does not say they denied “their man.” It says they denied the messengers. Quraysh, by calling Muhammad ﷺ a lying sorcerer, enter the same legal category as those who called Noah a liar and Moses a sorcerer. The file is unified.

**“So My Retribution Came Due”**

*Fa-ḥaqqa ʿiqāb* — so My punishment became ḥaqq, due, established, binding. *Ḥaqqa* is the verb of a right that has matured, like a debt whose term has arrived. God is not impulsive in this verse. He is a Judge whose sentence has come into effect after the condition — denial after warning — was met.

The first-person *ʿiqāb* (“My retribution”) refuses the idea of a blind fate. The ruin of ʿĀd is not weather. The drowning of Noah’s people is not hydrology. The blast upon Thamūd is not geology. They are *ʿiqāb*, the consequence that belongs to God as Judge. This is among the verse’s doctrinal loads: history is morally owned.

**Justice Without Haste and Without Failure**

The sūrah will later say that heaven and earth were not created in vain (38:27). Verse 14 is that doctrine in the past tense. If denial of messengers had no due retribution, the creation would be a joke and the reverent would be fools. God pays. He does not always pay on the schedule of the mocked prophet’s enemies, who ask to have their share hastened (38:16). He pays.

Human courts fail by delay, by bribery, by incomplete evidence. This verse places a court that does not fail, and it names the single evidence that matured the sentence: *takdhīb al-rusul*. For a society, the implication is grave. You may negotiate many policies. You may not deny the messengers and then call your prosperity a proof of safety. Prosperity can be the term of the loan. *Ḥaqqa ʿiqāb* is the day the loan is collected.

For a person, the verse is a call to check the mouth. Denial is not only a public press conference. It is every treatment of a known command as if it were optional decoration. The messengers are denied when their message is denied. Retribution, for a believer, may begin as the smaller collapses of a life built on a lie. The wise do not wait to see which form of *ʿiqāb* will fit them. They do what the later servants in this sūrah do: they turn before it comes due.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:15

> **They await naught but a single cry for which there is no delay.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Waiting Without Knowing They Are Waiting**

*Wa-mā yanẓuru hāʾulāʾi illā ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan* — these await nothing except a single cry. *Yanẓuru* can mean to wait, to look for. The subject *hāʾulāʾi*, “these,” may be Quraysh, or the gathered parties, or both as one type. They think they are waiting for the Prophet to fail, for the old creed to reassert itself, for death to be the end. The verse says that what is actually on the calendar is a *ṣayḥah*, one shout.

The Qur’an uses the single cry for the destruction of towns and for the eschatological blast:

> *“It was but a single cry, and behold, they were extinguished.”* (Qur’an 36:29)

> *“They await only a single cry that will seize them while they are disputing.”* (Qur’an 36:49)

> *“It will be only a single shout, and at once they will be seeing.”* (Qur’an 37:19)

Commentators on 38:15 differ on whether the cry here is the first blast of the trumpet, which levels the living world, or the second, which raises the dead. Some assign the first to Quraysh’s awaited national catastrophe, the second to all the parties’ resurrection. The relation of the two blasts is discussed at 39:68. What the verse itself stresses is not the numbering of blasts but the quality: *wāḥidah*, one, and *mā lahā min fawāq*.

**No Pause in Which to Renegotiate**

*Fawāq* is the interval in milking when the milk is allowed to collect again, a pause, a delay, a catching of breath. *Mā lahā min fawāq* — there is no such interval for this cry. No time to fetch an argument, no time to send a delegation to Abū Ṭālib, no time to say “ten words, not one.” The cry that ends the term does not include a recess.

This is the opposite of the long patience in Noah’s thousand years, of the years of Makkan warning, of the respite implied in “they have not yet tasted My punishment” (38:8). Respite is real. It is also finite, and its end is not a negotiation.

**The Psychology of Living as If the Cry Were Fiction**

People can hear a verse about a single cry and then return to a market as if the market were the eternal. That numbness is not always loud denial. It is often the habit of treating the loudest present noise as the most real. The cry is quiet now; the caravan is loud. Verse 15 inverts the volumes. The only thing truly being awaited, whether they know it or not, is the shout for which there is no milking-pause.

A practical taqwā follows. If the end arrives as one cry, then the work of turning cannot be scheduled for “after this season.” David prayed at the ends of the day. Job called from the midst of weariness. The notables said “go, be steadfast to your gods.” The verse asks which of those postures can survive a shout that has no *fawāq*. Only the posture that was already facing God.

The Prophet ﷺ would often seek refuge from a sudden, evil death and would remember the last hour even in ease. The believer who recites 38:15 is not asked to live in panic. Panic is another self-absorption. He is asked to live without the fantasy of an interval that God has said will not be given. Finish the duty now. Repair the injustice now. Say the word of tawḥīd now. The cry is single. The time to be double-minded is already a stolen interval.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:16

> **And they say, “Our Lord, hasten our share unto us before the Day of Reckoning.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mockery Dressed as a Prayer**

They speak to “our Lord,” and they ask for haste. The form is supplication; the soul of it is taunt. Across the Makkan Qur’an the deniers ask that punishment be brought quickly, as if delay proved absence:

> *“They ask you to hasten the punishment.”* (Qur’an 22:47)

> *“They say, ‘When will this promise be, if you are truthful?’”* (Qur’an 36:48)

Here the thing they ask to be hastened is *qiṭṭanā*, “our share.” *Qiṭṭ* in Arabic can mean a portion, a script, a written lot, a voucher of what is due. Ibn Jarīr records that it was understood as their book of deeds, their account, their decree, or their portion of punishment — or, in another report, their portion of the Garden when the Prophet ﷺ described it, asked for in sarcasm, as if to say: give us our pages now; we are not afraid of what is on them.

Both edges of *qiṭṭ* may be in the word at once. They do not believe in the Day of Reckoning, so they ask for their share *before* it, which is a way of saying that the Day is optional theater. If they believed, they would ask for delay and for pardon, as the believers do. The prayer for haste is the fingerprint of disbelief.

**The Self-Image Under the Joke**

Ibn Kathīr and others connect this to the type in:

> *“And when it is said to them, ‘Do not work corruption upon the earth,’ they say, ‘We are only putting things right.’”* (Qur’an 2:11)

People who think themselves the righteous of the land can ask for the account to be brought, because they have already written their own report. Quraysh saw themselves as guardians of the House, feeders of pilgrims, the clan of honor. A Day of Reckoning, if it existed, would surely confirm them. So they can afford the joke. The joke is their creed.

**Why God Does Not Hasten as They Wish**

Other verses answer the request:

> *“If God were to take people to task for what they have earned, He would not leave on the back of the earth a single creature. But He grants them respite until a named term.”* (Qur’an 35:45)

Respite is not forgetfulness. It is the space in which a notable of verse 6 might still become a servant of verse 17. To hasten the *qiṭṭ* upon a people who ask for it in mockery would be to close the door they have not yet fully understood is a door. God is *al-Wahhāb* (38:9) and *al-Ghafūr* (38:66). He does not accept the dare as a child accepts a dare.

Yet the asking itself is a crime of speech. To demand one’s account as a taunt is to treat the Lord as a stage opponent. The Day of Reckoning (*yawm al-ḥisāb*), named here and again in David’s charge (38:26), is not a metaphor for conscience. It is a day. Asking to have it early, while denying it, is like a debtor demanding the court while claiming the court is fiction.

**How the Believer Asks for a Share**

The opposite prayer is taught elsewhere: *rabbanā ātinā fī al-dunyā ḥasanatan wa-fī al-ākhirati ḥasanatan* — our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the next. The believer also asks for *qiṭṭ*, but the share wanted is mercy, not a showdown. When a person in pain says “let me die” in despair, or a person in sin says “let Him punish me if He exists,” they are near the grammar of verse 16. The sūrah will soon show Job calling upon his Lord from weariness without demanding that the Hour be jumped forward, and David seeking forgiveness rather than a hasty verdict.

In ordinary life, the verse warns against using religious language as sarcasm, and against the confidence that one’s ledger is already beautiful. The people who most need the Day to be delayed are often the people who talk as if they would welcome it. The people who will be at peace on it are those who, like the servants remembered next, spent the delay in turning.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:17

> **Bear patiently that which they say, and remember Our servant David, possessed of might; truly he turned oft [unto God].**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Patience Named as the First Prophetic Craft**

*Iṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūn* — bear patiently that which they say. The command is addressed first to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, as Ibn Jarīr and al-Qurṭubī note, and it belongs to a family of Makkan counsels:

> *“So be patient over what they say, and glorify the praise of your Lord before the rising of the sun and before its setting.”* (Qur’an 50:39)

> *“And be patient over what they say, and leave them with a gracious leaving.”* (Qur’an 73:10)

What they say has just been recorded: lying sorcerer, astounding reduction of the gods, invention, the jealous question “why him,” the taunt about hastening the share. The Prophet ﷺ is not told to answer each clause with a sharper clause. He is told to *ṣabr*. Some later jurists claimed that this patience was abrogated by the sword-verse (9:5). Al-Qurṭubī and others record that claim and also the sounder practice of the tradition: forbearance remains whenever circumstances permit, and the believer’s default toward insult is not a hair-trigger. A command can have a wartime specification without being emptied as a virtue.

**Patience Is Not Silence of the Dead**

The verse does not stop at “bear patiently.” It adds *wa-dhkur ʿabdanā Dāwūd* — and remember Our servant David. Patience here is fed by remembrance. The insulted messenger is to occupy the heart with a predecessor who was insulted, tried, given a kingdom, and kept turning. Al-Qurṭubī observes that telling these stories may persuade some of the deniers, and will in any case console the Prophet ﷺ: you are not the first to be called a liar, and the servants who were called liars were given might and a beautiful return.

This is how patience works psychologically. An empty “be patient” is a pressure without a picture. A patience that remembers David has a shape: strength without vainglory, office without caprice, sin-if-sin there was followed by falling down, not by schism. The Makkans are in *ʿizzah* and *shiqāq* (38:2). David is in *quwwah* and *awbah*. Might that turns is the cure for might that splits.

**“Our Servant” Before “Possessed of Might”**

God does not first say “Our king” or “Our prophet.” He says *ʿabdanā*, Our servant. The honorific of this whole sūrah is servanthood. Solomon will be “how excellent a servant” (38:30). Job will be “what an excellent servant” (38:44). Iblīs will refuse to be a servant of the command. The notables will refuse to be servants of One God. David is introduced as owned by God, and only then as *dhā al-ayd*, possessed of might, of hands, of power.

The commentators split the might along two complementary lines. Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Suyūṭī record that it is strength in obedience and in worship: a body and a will that could carry night prayer and fasting in a way most people cannot. Others, including al-Ālūsī’s line, take it as combined prophethood and kingship, power in both the sanctuary and the court. The two readings should be held together. A king who cannot stand at night is only Pharaoh of the tent-poles. A worshipper who is given no public trust may still be a saint, but David’s file is the file of a man who had both, and who did not let the court eat the sanctuary.

**The Refrain of the Sūrah: He Turned Oft**

*Innahu awwāb* — truly he was one who turns, again and again. *Awwāb* is an intensive form from *āba*, to return. It is used of David, of Solomon, of Job in this sūrah. Al-Qurṭubī connects it to the Prophet Muhammad’s own istighfār a hundred times a day. Al-Ālūsī and Ibn Kathīr take it as turning to God in all affairs, not only after a fall. Ibn ʿArabī, in the *Futūḥāt*, hears in it a perpetual servanthood, a soul that does not take up residence in any station except as a visitor who must go back to God.

The intensive form matters. David is not described as a man who once repented. He is described as a returning type. Strength that does not return becomes Iblīs. Weakness that returns is still *ʿabd*. The sūrah will soon show David falling down; the introduction has already told you that falling down is not the interruption of his rank but the content of his rank.

**The Prayer and Fast God Loved**

The Prophet ﷺ said, in a ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *“The prayer most beloved to God is the prayer of David, and the fast most beloved to God is the fast of David. He would sleep half the night, stand a third of it, and sleep a sixth of it. And he would fast one day and break the fast one day.”* (al-Bukhārī 1131; Muslim 1159)

The rhythm is humane and severe at once. He slept, so the body was a trust. He stood, so the night was not wasted. He slept again, so dawn worship would not be a corpse’s worship. He fasted and broke, so that the fast was not a stunt and the feast was not an idol. Might in this ḥadīth is measured as stewardship of time and appetite. The Makkan notable measured might as tents and gods. The verse places David against them as a different species of power.

**What the Insulted Believer Is to Do with the Verse**

When people say of a Muslim what Quraysh said of the Prophet ﷺ — fraud, sorcery, novelty, ambition — the verse’s sequence is the method: patience upon the saying, then remembrance of a servant, not rumination on the insult. Remembering David means remembering that God has had employees before who were stronger than their enemies and still spent the night returning. It means refusing the *shiqāq* of answering faction with faction as a first move. And it means checking whether one’s own might — office, knowledge, income, physical force — is *ayd* in David’s sense or *ʿizzah* in verse 2’s sense. The difference is one word: *awwāb*.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:18

> **Truly We compelled the mountains to join him in glorifying at eventide and at the break of day.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation Already Praises; David Makes It a Choir**

The Qur’an is clear that praise is not a human monopoly:

> *“Whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth glorifies God.”* (Qur’an 59:1; cf. 61:1; 62:1; 64:1; 57:1)

Mountains, in their own mode, already *sabbaḥa*. What this verse adds is *sakhkharnā al-jibāla maʿahu yusabbiḥna* — We compelled, We made subservient, the mountains to glorify *with him*. The same is said in:

> *“And We subjected the mountains to glorify with him at evening and sunrise.”* (Qur’an 21:79)

> *“O mountains, echo with him, and the birds.”* (Qur’an 34:10)

David’s tasbīḥ does not invent the mountains’ worship. It gathers it. A vicegerent (38:26) is not a being whose praise replaces the world’s; he is a being in whose praise the world finds its conductor. When the human khalīfah is rightly turned, the landscape is not mute stone around him. It is a congregation.

**Compulsion as Honor, Not as Violence**

*Sakhkharnā* is the verb used of the sun and moon made of service to mankind, of ships on the sea, of Solomon’s wind. It means that God assigned them a task they cannot refuse. The mountains’ joining David is therefore not a poetic flourish about echoes in a canyon, though the physical echo may be one of its signs. It is a Divine assignment: at the hours of his glorification, the mountains were commanded to be with him.

The honor is David’s, and it is also the mountains’. Stone is given a share in a prophet’s liturgy. The modern habit of treating matter as dead inventory is already judged. If mountains can be a choir, then a human who treats the earth as garbage has not only failed ecology. He has failed metaphysics.

**Eventide and the Break of Day**

*Bi-l-ʿashiyyi wa-l-ishrāq* — at the evening time and at the shining of the sun. The two ends of the day stand for the whole, as “east and west” stand for the earth. They also name the hours when the soul most easily forgets: the tiredness of dusk, the rush of morning. David’s might in worship is timed against those leaks.

Many commentators, among them those cited by Ibn Jarīr, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī, heard in *ishrāq* the time after sunrise, associated with the supererogatory *ḍuḥā* prayer, after the obligatory *fajr* has been prayed in its darkness. If so, the verse commends not only the obligatory poles of the day but the extra standing that fills the brightening. The Prophet ﷺ said of the *ḍuḥā*:

> *“In the morning, charity is due from every joint of one of you… and two rakʿahs which he prays in the forenoon suffice for that.”* (Muslim 720)

David’s mountains at *ishrāq* are that charity of being, written in a king’s register.

**What It Means to Have the Landscape With You**

There is a loneliness in calling to God in a hostile town. The Prophet ﷺ in Makkah had few human voices to join him. Verse 18 tells him, through David, that the choir is larger than the town. If the notables will not say One God, the mountains will still say Glory. This is not an invitation to abandon people. It is an invitation not to let people define whether worship is real.

A person who prays *fajr* in a city that sleeps, or who remembers God in a workplace that mocks, is in David’s hours. The walls do not have to sing audibly for the teaching to hold. God is able to make stone echo a prophet. He is able to make a commuter train a place of *dhikr*. The compulsion is God’s; the joining is the servant’s work. When the servant turns, he discovers he was never the only one glorifying. He had only been deaf.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:19

> **And the birds gathered, each turning oft unto Him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Congregation, This One of Wings**

After mountains, birds: *wa-l-ṭayra maḥshūrah* — and the birds assembled, gathered, mustered. *Ḥashr* is a strong word. It is used of the gathering of resurrection. Here it is used of birds taking their places around a prophet’s voice as if around a standard. The commentaries, including Ibn Kathīr and al-Ālūsī, say they gathered because of the beauty of David’s recitation and singing of the psalms, and they turned to God with their own praise.

The Qur’an elsewhere confirms the bird as a creature of tasbīḥ:

> *“Do you not see that God is glorified by whosoever is in the heavens and the earth, and the birds spreading their wings? Each knows its prayer and its glorification.”* (Qur’an 24:41)

Verse 19 of Ṣād is that general truth becoming a scene. The birds do not merely know their own prayer. They know how to join a human who is praying as he should.

**“Each Turning Oft” — The Pronoun’s Two Directions**

*Kullun lahu awwāb* — each was *awwāb* to Him, or to him. If the pronoun is God, the birds share David’s epithet from verse 17: they too are oft-returning. If the pronoun is David, as some commentators allow, the birds are responsive to him, following his lead in glorification, as al-Qurṭubī notes. The two readings meet. A prophet who is *awwāb* to God becomes a qiblah for creatures who are *awwāb* with him. Leadership in Islam is this before it is administration: to be so turned toward God that others, even the winged, find it easier to turn.

**Beauty as a Cause of Gathering**

The Islamic tradition remembered David’s voice as a gift. Beauty here is not entertainment. It is a means by which hearts — and birds — are collected from scattering. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“He is not of us who does not make his voice beautiful with the Qur’an.”* (al-Bukhārī 7527, in this meaning)

and he listened to Abū Mūsā’s recitation and told him he had been given a flute from the flutes of the family of David. Verse 19 therefore quietly legislates an ethic of sound. If a prophet’s voice can muster birds, a careless or showy recitation that scatters human hearts is a betrayal of the gift. The aim is *ḥashr*, gathering toward God, not gathering of fans.

**The Smallness of Human Isolation**

A man may think, in grief or in a godless room, that nothing in the environment is with him. This verse says that the default of creation is to gather around true *dhikr*. The exception is the human who, like Iblīs later, refuses. Birds and mountains do not have that Iblīs-option in the same way. Their gathering judges our scattering.

Practically, the verse invites two acts. First, beautify remembrance so that it is fit to be joined — not as performance, but as care. Second, notice the non-human world as fellow glorifiers, not as decoration. A bird on a morning wire is already *awwāb* in its kind. The human who hears only noise has not yet entered David’s sanctuary. The human who hears a reminder has begun to.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:20

> **And We strengthened his sovereignty and gave him wisdom and decisive speech.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kingship as Something God Tightens**

*Wa-shadadnā mulkahu* — We strengthened, We made firm, his sovereignty. Ibn Kathīr reports the tradition’s sense that David’s kingdom was the most powerful of its world. Al-Rāzī immediately adds the theological correction: its strength was from Divine aid, not from the metaphysics of iron and army alone, though iron is named as a gift to David elsewhere (34:10). *Mulk* in this sūrah is never a natural right. It is a tightened rope in God’s hand. Later, Solomon will ask for a kingdom that will not befit anyone after him, and he will receive it as gift (38:35–39). David receives the strengthening without that particular request being mentioned here. God gave, then God tightened.

This matters for the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah, who has no *mulk* in the Qurayshī sense. The verse says that true sovereignty is an act of God upon a servant, not a condition for being a servant. It also matters for anyone who holds a small *mulk* now — a household, a company, an office. If God did not *shadda* it, it is a tent without pegs, however loud.

**Wisdom: Prophethood, Understanding, or Both**

*Wa-ātaynāhu al-ḥikmata* — and We gave him wisdom. Ibn Jarīr, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī record that *ḥikmah* here is taken as prophethood, or as understanding and sound judgment, or as both, because a prophet’s wisdom is not a separate hobby from his office. The Qur’an says of David and Solomon together:

> *“And We gave them both judgment and knowledge.”* (Qur’an 21:79)

Wisdom in the Arabic of revelation is not cleverness. It is the putting of things in their places: this is God’s, this is the servant’s; this is the plaintiff’s right; this is the hour of night prayer; this is the enemy at the border. A king without *ḥikmah* is only a tent-pole Pharaoh. A mystic without *ḥikmah* may know states and still ruin a household. David is given *mulk* and *ḥikmah* as a pair so that neither eats the other.

**Decisive Speech: The Gift of Cutting Clearly**

*Wa-faṣl al-khiṭāb* — and the separating of address, decisive speech. *Faṣl* is cutting, dividing, deciding. *Khiṭāb* is address, discourse, the matter spoken. Ibn Jarīr gathers the early meanings: clarity and truth of speech; knowledge of how to give decrees and administer justice; the ability to decide between plaintiff and defendant. Some of the pious early figures said it was the gift of saying *ammā baʿd* — “to proceed” — the hinge that separates preface from judgment, so that speech does not drown in its own courtesy. Others related *faṣl* to the revelation’s own *tafṣīl*, the expounding of signs:

> *“A Book whose signs have been expounded as an Arabic Qur’an for a people who know.”* (Qur’an 41:3)

David’s wisdom includes the capacity to differentiate (*faṣṣala*) aspects of reality and to expound them so that a court, or a people, can walk. Decisive speech is the opposite of the notables’ slogans in verses 6–7. They said “go” and “invention.” He is given speech that cuts between right and wrong.

Sahl al-Tustarī’s line, that the kingdom was strengthened by truthful ministers, belongs here too. *Faṣl al-khiṭāb* in a ruler is not only his own tongue. It is a court that can still hear a distinction. When a government, a family, or a scholarly circle loses the ability to say “this is not that,” the *mulk* may still look tight and already be rotten.

**Why the Three Gifts Arrive Together**

Sovereignty without wisdom is tyranny. Wisdom without decisive speech is a light under a bowl: true, but unshared, and therefore unjust to the wronged who need a sentence. Decisive speech without wisdom is cruelty with a gavel. God gave David all three, and the next verses will test all three. The disputants will scale the sanctuary. If the *mulk* is weak, he cannot judge. If wisdom is absent, he will follow caprice (38:26). If *faṣl al-khiṭāb* is missing, he will speak before he has separated the case.

For a reciter without a throne, the verse still assigns a craft. Strengthen what God has given you to govern — your tongue first. Ask for *ḥikmah*, as the Qur’an says God gives it to whom He wills, and “whosoever is given wisdom has been given much good” (2:269). Practice *faṣl* in speech: distinguish evidence from rumor, apology from excuse, the other person’s right from your fatigue. The mountains and birds joined David’s glorification. Human beings join, or flee, a person’s speech. Decisive, wise speech is how a servant’s *mulk*, however small, becomes a place of safety rather than another thicket of the parties.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:21

> **Has the report of the dispute come unto thee, when they scaled the sanctuary?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Draws the Prophet into a Courtroom**

*Wa-hal atāka nabaʾu al-khaṣm* — has the report of the disputants come to you? The address is to Muhammad ﷺ, in the same intimate style as “has the report of Moses come to you?” The story is not gossip about an old king. It is *nabaʾ*, news that carries weight, delivered to a prophet who has just been told to be patient and to remember David. What he is to remember is not only night prayer and birds. It is a day when the sanctuary was climbed.

*Idh tasawwarū al-miḥrāb* — when they scaled the *miḥrāb*, the sanctuary, the elevated chamber of prayer and judgment. *Tasawwur* is coming over the wall. They did not wait at the door. They arrived as if from the sky or from the roof, in a space David had reason to think sealed.

**Why the Entry Itself Is Part of the Trial**

Ibn Kathīr notes that the sanctuary was difficult of access and that David had ordered that none should enter upon him in that hour. Al-Rāzī and al-Ālūsī mention that he was frightened because the manner of entry looked like the manner of assassination. A king at prayer, surprised by bodies coming over the wall, is being tested before any sheep are mentioned. Will fear make him unjust? Will the violation of his private worship make him refuse the duty of public judgment?

The verse therefore teaches that trials do not always arrive through the official gate. They scale. They come at the hour you had scheduled for God alone. A parent is tested in the night. A judge is tested on the way to prayer. A scholar is tested when a question climbs into his rest. The *miḥrāb* is not a bunker. If it were, David would be a monk, not a vicegerent.

**Dispute as a Gift That Looks Like a Break-In**

*Al-khaṣm* can be the two sides of a dispute treated as one event. God calls it a report that “comes” to the Prophet ﷺ, as if the important thing were that the Messenger of the last ummah should have this file. Why? Because the last ummah will have judges, and because the Prophet ﷺ himself judges among people, and because every believer judges in smaller *miḥrābs* all day: whose story do I believe, whose ewe am I taking, whose wall am I climbing?

The scaling also hints, in the reading of many commentators who take the visitors as angels, that the unseen may enter the seen without permission of chamberlains. If they are human litigants, the hint is different and equally useful: the weak may have to arrive irregularly because the regular doors are owned by the ninety-nine. Either way, the vicegerent’s sanctuary is not allowed to become a fortress against the claim of the one ewe.

**How to Hear the Verse Without Turning It into a Novel**

The Qur’an, unlike the Biblical sequel some commentators imported, does not here name a woman, a general, or a crime of the flesh. It names a report, a dispute, a wall, a sanctuary. Scholarly discipline, which Ibn Kathīr insists on later in the passage, begins already at verse 21: stay with what God told. The scene is already severe without embroidery. A prophet-king at worship is interrupted by a case that will interrupt his view of himself. That is enough to start the heart’s work. The reciter is being asked, “Has the report come to you?” — not “have you collected the extra tales?” If the report has come, the next verses will show what a servant does when the wall is climbed.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:22

> **When they entered upon David, he was frightened of them. They said, “Fear not! We are two disputants: one of us has transgressed against the other; so judge between us with truth. Be not unjust, and guide us unto the right path.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fear in a Prophet Is Not a Defect of Trust**

*Fafaziʿa minhum* — he was frightened of them. The Qur’an does not hide this. Moses was afraid. Mary was afraid. The Prophet ﷺ in the cave heard a heart that pounded. Fear at an unexplained entry is the body’s honesty. Al-Rāzī will later suggest that David’s seeking of forgiveness (38:24) may be related to this first suspicion, if he thought ill of men who only wanted a judgment. Whether or not that is the cause of the istighfār, the fear itself is recorded with respect. A vicegerent is not a stone. The mountains glorify with him; he is not himself a mountain.

**“Fear Not” as the First Human — or Angelic — Word**

*Lā takhaf* — fear not. If the two are angels in human form, as many commentators including Ibn Jarīr and al-Qurṭubī held, then they speak as angels have spoken to prophets and to Mary. If they are men, they speak as litigants who must first calm the judge they have startled. In both readings the case cannot begin until fear is told to stand down. Justice requires a heart that is not fighting for its life. A judge who is still in *fazaʿ* will either strike out or submit too quickly. They give him the second he needs.

**The Self-Description of a Just Complaint**

They do not begin with names or with insults. They begin with a structure: *khaṣmāni baghā baʿḍunā ʿalā baʿḍ* — two disputants; one of us has transgressed against the other. *Baghy* is transgression, the overflow of a right beyond its bank. Then they ask for four things that are in fact one: judge between us *bi-l-ḥaqq*; do not *tushtiṭ*, do not go to excess or deviate; and guide us to *sawāʾ al-ṣirāṭ*, the evenness of the path.

The litigants teach the court. They define justice as truth, as refusal of excess, as guidance to a level road. Even if, in some interpretations, one of them is acting a parable, the words remain a charter. A plaintiff who asks the judge not to be unjust is already conceding that the judge might be tempted. They speak to David as if a prophet could err in office. The sūrah will soon confirm that office is a trial.

**The Ethics of How to Enter Upon the Powerful**

They scaled a wall, which is irregular, but their speech is meticulously regular. They identify the relationship (two disputants), the problem (transgression), the request (judgment in truth), the prohibition (do not be unjust), and the aim (the right path). People who approach power with only rage, or only flattery, have not yet learned verse 22. People in power who punish the frightened manner of entry and refuse to hear the clear speech have not learned it either.

For ordinary life the verse is almost embarrassingly practical. A family argument that began with fear — a slammed door, a child walking in on adults — can still be saved if someone says, in effect, “fear not; one of us has transgressed; judge by truth; do not be excessive; put us on a level path.” Most domestic *baghy* continues because no one dares to offer that frame, or because the frightened party never comes down from the fear enough to hear it.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:23

> **Behold, this, my brother, has ninety-nine ewes, and I have one ewe. Yet he has said, ‘Place her under my care,’ and has overpowered me in speech.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Brotherhood Named Before the Wound**

*Inna hādhā akhī* — this, my brother. Al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabarī mention both blood brotherhood and brotherhood in religion. The word is placed first so that the injustice cannot be anonymized. The one ewe is not being taken by a stranger of another nation. It is being taken by *akhī*. The Qur’an elsewhere says that believers are brothers, and that brothers of Yusuf threw him in a well. Nearness is not safety. Nearness is often the condition of the worst *baghy*, because the ninety-nine already have access.

**Ninety-Nine and One: The Mathematics of Greed**

Ninety-nine ewes is already abundance. One ewe is already a livelihood, a name, a warmth in the night. The request is *akfilnīhā* — place her under my care, let me be her *kafīl*, her sponsor, her owner in effect. Greed rarely says “I steal.” It says “let me manage,” “it would be more efficient,” “she will be better in the larger flock.” The language of care is the costume of absorption.

This is why the story, even on its plainest reading, needs no further scandal to be grave. A society in which the large flock has a legal tongue and the single ewe has none is already a society of *baghy*. The Qur’an elsewhere forbids the devouring of the small:

> *“Do not consume one another’s wealth in vanity.”* (Qur’an 2:188)

> *“Give the orphans their property, and do not exchange the bad for the good, and do not consume their property with your own.”* (Qur’an 4:2)

Verse 23 is those laws as a scene.

**Overpowered in Speech**

*Wa-ʿazzanī fī al-khiṭāb* — and he has overpowered me in the address. *ʿAzza* here is to prevail, to be stronger. Al-Ālūsī takes it as overcoming by argument; al-Ṭabarī as clearer, more eloquent speech. The man of ninety-nine can hire the better lawyer, speak with more confidence, use the dialect of the court. The man of one is not necessarily wrong. He is out-talked.

David was given *faṣl al-khiṭāb* in verse 20. The plaintiff now tells him that *khiṭāb* itself can be a weapon. Decisive speech is a gift; overpowering speech is a temptation. The judge must know the difference. A fluent injustice is still injustice. In modern courts, media, and family negotiations, the ninety-nine still *ʿazza* in *khiṭāb*: they have the vocabulary, the connections, the calm of people who will eat tonight either way. The verse commands the listener to hear the one ewe under the louder sentence.

**The Parable-Reading and Its Limits**

Many commentators, drawing a line to Nathan’s parable in the Biblical book of Samuel, read the ninety-nine and the one as a figure for a powerful man taking the poor man’s only wife, and then connected that figure to David and Bathsheba. Ibn Kathīr states plainly that the reports which bind this Qur’anic scene to that story are not grounded in sound chains. Al-Rāzī argues that even a sanitized version — desire without adultery — fights the qualities God has just attributed to David. The Qur’an itself does not make the identification.

What can be taken without borrowing weak narrative is the moral structure: abundance plus speech-power plus the request to absorb the last remaining thing of a brother. That structure judges kings and it judges us. The extra room in the house taken from a sibling, the credit stolen from a colleague, the public honor taken from a quieter person, the second glance at what is already another’s — all are ninety-nine reaching for one. Whether or not David’s own life contained a shadow of this, the words he is about to speak will condemn the structure, and the sūrah will make him a man who seeks forgiveness. Both the condemnation and the seeking are what the ummah needs. The extra tale is not required for the medicine.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:24

> **He said, “He has indeed wronged you in asking that your ewe be added to his ewes. Truly many associates transgress against one another, save those who believe and perform righteous deeds. Yet how few are they!” And David understood that We had tried him; so he sought forgiveness from his Lord, fell down kneeling, and repented.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Correct Verdict Spoken Quickly**

David answers: *la-qad ẓalamaka bi-suʾāli naʿjatika ilā niʿājih* — he has wronged you by asking that your ewe be added to his ewes. The verdict matches the plain sense. Asking to absorb the last of a brother’s property is *ẓulm*. The king who was given wisdom and decisive speech uses both: he names the wrong and he names it as adding, *ilā niʿājih*, the small to the many.

Al-Rāzī notes a judicial problem that several commentators felt: did he speak before hearing the other side? If he did, the haste itself may be part of what he later seeks forgiveness for, and verse 26’s “judge among the people with truth and follow not caprice” would fit as a correction of method. If the case was a parable whose meaning was meant to flash, the single speech is the point, as Nathan’s single speech was the point. The Qur’an does not slow the scene with cross-examination. It lets the prophet’s moral intelligence appear at once — and then it lets that same prophet fall down.

**The General Law He Draws from the Case**

He does not stop at the two brothers. *Wa-inna kathīran min al-khulaṭāʾi la-yabghī baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍ* — truly many associates, partners, people mixed in a common life, transgress against one another. *Khulaṭāʾ* are those whose affairs are blended: partners in trade, kin in an inheritance, spouses, co-rulers, even worshippers in a shared town. Nearness creates opportunity for *baghy*. The exception is named with the Qur’an’s great pair: *illā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* — except those who believe and do righteous deeds.

Then the sigh that is itself revelation: *wa-qalīlun mā hum* — and how few they are. The Qur’an repeats this paucity:

> *“But most of mankind do not give thanks.”* (Qur’an 2:243; 40:61)

> *“But most of mankind believe not.”* (Qur’an 11:17)

David, a king, is not flattering his population. He is telling the truth about mixed life. Partnership without faith and good work tends toward the ninety-nine. Anyone who has sat on a board, divided an estate, or shared a kitchen knows the verse without a commentary.

**“David Understood That We Had Tried Him”**

*Wa-ẓanna Dāwūdu annamā fatannāhu* — David understood, thought, realized that We had tried him. *Ẓanna* in Qur’anic Arabic is often a thought that has weight, not a guess. *Fitanāhu* is We tested him. What was the test? The commentaries offer a disciplined range, not a single novel.

Those who take the disputants as angels say that he knew when they smiled and ascended, as Ibn Jarīr and al-Qurṭubī relate. The test was a mirror: his own life flashed in the ninety-nine and the one. Those who refuse the Bathsheba link, as al-Rāzī prefers, say the test was the judgment itself — wisdom under surprise, fear, and incomplete procedure — or that he had suspected them of evil when they only wanted a ruling. Some say he asked to be raised to the rank of the most tried prophets, and God answered by sending a trial in the form of a case.

What the verse itself requires is that David *understood* it as trial and then moved. The mark of *awwāb* is not that he is never tested. It is that he reads the test as from God, not as an accident of palace scheduling.

**Forgiveness, Kneeling, Return**

*Fa-staghfara rabbahu wa-kharra rākiʿan wa-anāba* — he sought forgiveness of his Lord, fell down kneeling, and turned in repentance. Three verbs, three motions of the servant. Istighfār with the tongue and heart; *kharra* with the body, a falling that is not collapse but worship; *inābah* with the will, the return that matches his epithet *awwāb*.

This is one of the fifteen places of Qur’anic recitation at which a prostration is made. Ibn Kathīr notes that it is treated as a prostration of gratitude, and that the Prophet ﷺ usually prostrated at it but told the people it was not an obligation of the same kind as the recitation-prostrations that are more firmly required. The body’s falling is part of how the ummah keeps David’s understanding alive. We do not only analyze the case. We go down.

The doctrinal caution remains: prophets are protected from the sins that would nullify their office, and the Qur’an does not here charge David with the crime the Biblical text charges. Seeking forgiveness is the work of the near, as the Prophet ﷺ himself sought forgiveness continually. Al-Rāzī’s alternatives — suspicion of the visitors, judging before hearing, seeking pardon for the disputants themselves — all preserve a prophet who turns without needing to make him an adulterer and a murderer. The lesson for the reciter does not depend on the worst version. If a prophet falls down when he understands a trial, what is the excuse of a person who understands and only straightens his crown?

**The Few Who Do Not Transgress in Partnership**

David’s sigh — how few they are — is the social heart of the verse. Belief plus righteous action is the only proven solvent of *baghy* among *khulaṭāʾ*. Contracts help. Courts help. They do not replace the inner exception. A Muslim partnership, marriage, or community that relies on documents without *īmān* and *ʿamal* will reproduce the ninety-nine. The verse sends the reciter not first into David’s psychology but into his own mixed lives: where am I the brother with ninety-nine, fluent in *khiṭāb*? Where am I the one ewe? Where, if I am the judge, have I spoken too fast? And when the understanding comes — *annamā fatannāhu* — do I argue, or do I fall?

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:25

> **Thus did We forgive him that. Truly nearness unto Us shall be his, and a beautiful return.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Forgiveness Stated as a Divine Act Already Done**

*Fa-ghafarnā lahu dhālika* — so We forgave him that. The demonstrative *dhālika* is deliberately unspecified in its content: “that” which he sought pardon for. God does not publish the file in the verse of pardon. He publishes the pardon. This is a mercy to David and a legislation for the ummah. The believer is not required to reconstruct a prophet’s fault with amateur archaeology. He is required to know that God forgives the servant who falls down and turns.

*Ghafr* is covering and erasing in the sense of not holding the fault as a barrier. The same Lord who will curse Iblīs till the Day of Judgment (38:78) here covers His *awwāb*. The difference is not that one was strong and one was weak. Iblīs was strong in a kind. The difference is turning.

**Nearness as the Rank That Follows Return**

*Wa-inna lahu ʿindanā la-zulfā* — truly for him, with Us, is nearness. *Zulfā* is proximity, a station of being brought nigh. The Qur’an uses “those brought nigh” (*al-muqarrabūn*) for the highest human rank in the next world (56:11, 88). Nearness is tied to what people send forward:

> *“And the righteous deeds that last are better with your Lord in reward, and better in hope.”* (Qur’an 18:46, in meaning)

> *“It is not your wealth or your children that bring you near to Us in proximity, but whoever believes and works righteousness.”* (Qur’an 34:37)

David’s *zulfā* is not the nearness of his throne to a capital. It is nearness *ʿindanā*, with Us. The sanctuary he fell in becomes, by forgiveness, a door. Those who seek nearness through other gods are rebuked in 39:3 and 46:28. This verse shows the legal way: mistake, understanding, istighfār, falling, *inābah*, then *zulfā*.

**A Beautiful Return**

*Wa-ḥusna maʾāb* — and a beautiful return. *Maʾāb* is a place of returning, the end of the road home. The same phrase will be given to Solomon (38:40) and promised to the reverent (38:49). David’s personal pardon is thus written in the same ink as the general promise of Paradise. The sūrah’s eschatology begins in a kneeling king.

*Ḥusn* makes the return beautiful, not merely safe. Forgiveness could have been only the dropping of a charge. God adds a beauty. The servant who feared when the wall was scaled is given a homecoming that has no scaling enemies and no ninety-nine.

**Why Pardon Is Announced in Public Revelation**

God could have forgiven David in the unseen and never told Makkah. He told Makkah, and told Muhammad ﷺ, so that the insulted Prophet would know that the remembered servant was a forgiven servant, and so that every later reciter would not despair at a trial in his own *miḥrāb*. Ibn Taymiyyah’s writings on repentance return often to this structure: the one who repents is not merely restored to zero. He may be raised. David is not described after the trial as “back to duty.” He is described as near, with a beautiful return. Then verse 26 will re-appoint him. Pardon is not retirement. Pardon is how vicegerency becomes possible again.

The reciter who has fallen — in a judgment, in a partnership, in a private *baghy* — is being taught the sequence. Do not explain the sin into a virtue. Do not publicize what God has not asked you to publicize. Do seek forgiveness, fall, return. The *zulfā* is God’s to give. The *maʾāb* is God’s to make beautiful. Your part is the going down.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:26

> **O David! Truly We have appointed thee as a vicegerent upon the earth; so judge among the people with truth and follow not caprice, lest it lead thee astray from the way of God. Truly those who stray from the way of God, theirs shall be a severe punishment for having forgotten the Day of Reckoning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Vicegerency Spoken After the Fall, Not Before It**

*Yā Dāwūdu innā jaʿalnāka khalīfatan fī al-arḍ* — O David, We have made you a vicegerent upon the earth. The appointment is announced after the sanctuary trial and the pardon. This order is itself tafsīr. *Khilāfah* is not a certificate of impeccable taste. It is a trust given by God to a servant who knows how to return. Adam will be named in this sūrah’s last movement as the one into whom God breathed of His Spirit, and the angels will be told to prostrate. David is Adam’s child in office: to act in the earth on terms God sets, not as a second god.

The meaning of *khalīfah* is discussed at 6:165: successors who inherit the earth in turns, tested by what they are given. David’s share of that meaning is concentrated: judge, do not follow *hawā*, remember the Day.

**Judge Among the People with Truth**

*Fa-ḥkum bayna al-nāsi bi-l-ḥaqq*. The *fa* makes judgment the first consequence of vicegerency. A khalīfah who will not judge, or who judges by other than truth, is a steward who will not unlock the store. *Al-ḥaqq* is God’s truth, the real, the just portion. It is the opposite of the notables’ slogans and of the ninety-nine’s fluent *khiṭāb*. The litigants had already asked for this in verse 22. God now makes their request a Divine command.

**“Follow Not Caprice”**

*Wa-lā tattabiʿ al-hawā* — and do not follow caprice, the wind of the self, that which the soul loves because it loves it. Ibn ʿArabī, in the *Futūḥāt* (II 336), glosses *hawā* as everything a person loves, and reads the command: follow not what you love; follow what I love. Then: lest caprice confound you, ruin you, and make you blind to the way I have laid down as law.

This is among the most portable sentences in the sūrah. *Hawā* is the inner idol that matches the outer gods of verse 5. The notables could not accept One God because their loves were many. A judge cannot accept one truth if his loves are many. Caprice in a court is a bribe the judge pays himself: the face he likes, the tribe he fears, the story that flatters his self-image, the haste that protects his nap. David is told this after a case that involved brothers, flocks, and speech. The warning is not theoretical.

**The Chain: Caprice, Straying, Forgetting the Day**

*Fa-yuḍillaka ʿan sabīl Allāh* — lest it lead you astray from the way of God. Caprice is not a private room. It is a path-changer. Then the general law: those who stray from God’s way have a severe punishment *bi-mā nasū yawm al-ḥisāb* — for that they forgot the Day of Reckoning.

Ibn Kathīr takes the forgetting as denial, or as heedlessness of what must be prepared. Al-Ṭūsī and others note that the Arabic can also mean “for what they forgot on the Day of Reckoning,” the neglected duties meeting them as emptiness. It can mean they forgot the reminder (5:13–14), forgot God so that He made them forget themselves (59:19), forgot their own creation (36:78). All these forgettings are one family. The Day is not remembered; therefore the way is left; therefore caprice drives.

The people who asked to hasten their share before the Day (38:16) are the public face of this forgetting. David is warned not to become, in the robes of a prophet-king, a private version of the same amnesia.

**The Verse as a Charter for Every Small Throne**

Few reciters will sit on David’s throne. All reciters judge: between children, between employees, between their own excuses and their duties, between a rumor and a reputation. The charter is the same. You are a *khalīfah* in a plot of earth God named for you. Judge by truth. Do not follow what you love when what you love and what is true are not the same. Remember that a Day will unpack the files you closed too quickly.

Taqwā here is again careful motion on a high road. Caprice is taking the hands off the wheel because the view is pretty. The severe punishment is not an arbitrary sting. It is the crash that belongs to forgotten gravity. David’s might, his choir of mountains, his decisive speech, his pardon, his nearness — none of these cancel the charter. They make the charter possible. A forgiven vicegerent is the only kind the earth can bear without becoming Pharaoh’s camp.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:27

> **And We did not create Heaven and earth and whatsoever is between them in vain; that is the conjecture of those who disbelieve. So woe unto those who disbelieve in the Fire!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation Declared Incapable of Being a Joke**

*Wa-mā khalaqnā al-samāʾa wa-l-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā bāṭilan* — We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them *bāṭil*, in vain, as a nullity, as play without remainder. The verse lifts the camera from David’s court to the whole interval of existence. A king has been told not to follow caprice. Now the cosmos is declared free of caprice. God’s act of creating is the opposite of *hawā*.

The sister verses are explicit:

> *“And We did not create the heavens and the earth and what is between them in play.”* (Qur’an 44:38)

> *“We did not create the heavens and the earth and what is between them except in truth.”* (Qur’an 15:85; cf. 46:3)

*Bāṭil* is the hollow, the false, the wasted. A universe that is *bāṭil* would be a stage on which David’s repentance and Pharaoh’s poles have the same weight, which is to say none. This sūrah refuses that. Vicegerency (38:26) only makes sense if the earth is a trust. The Day of Reckoning only makes sense if the world was billed as meaningful when it was made.

**“That Is the Conjecture of Those Who Disbelieve”**

*Dhālika ẓannu alladhīna kafarū* — that, the idea of vain creation, is the conjecture of those who covered the truth. Ibn Kathīr ties the conjecture to denial of resurrection and of a final abode. If there is no return, then the long making of heavens and earth is a firework that no one meant. The Qur’an quotes their slogan:

> *“There is nothing but our life of this world; we die and we live, and we will not be resurrected.”* (Qur’an 23:37; cf. 45:24)

*Ẓann* here is not a humble “I do not know.” It is a settled guess treated as a worldview. They needed the world to be vain so that their gods, their *ʿizzah*, and their mockery of the Reminder would not be called to account. A meaningful world is a dangerous world for pride.

**Woe in the Fire as the Match of a Meaningful Cosmos**

*Fa-waylun lilladhīna kafarū min al-nār* — so woe to those who disbelieve, from the Fire. The *fa* is consequential. If creation is not vain, then covering its meaning is not a lifestyle choice. It is a collision with what is. The Fire is not an extra cruelty pasted onto a neutral universe. It is the universe’s refusal, at the last, to remain *bāṭil*.

**Why This Verse Stands Between David and Solomon**

David has been warned not to forget the Day. Solomon will be given a kingdom. Job will suffer. If a reciter thought these were only national epics, verse 27 relocates them inside a cosmic legal order. The stories are exhibits in the case that the world has a Judge.

The modern form of *ẓann* is not always the spoken creed “there is no resurrection.” It is the practiced creed that Sunday’s injustice will not meet a Monday of God; that private *baghy* among partners (38:24) is only strategy; that the earth is raw material without trust. Environmental waste, sexual consumption without covenant, and speech without truth are all ways of treating what is between heaven and earth as *bāṭil*. The verse calls that treatment kufr’s conjecture, even when the tongue still recites.

A believer’s application is gratitude plus caution. Gratitude: the sky is not an accident, so praise is rational. Caution: if nothing was made in vain, then my day was not made in vain, and caprice is a lie against my own hours. Woe is for those who insist on the lie until the Fire explains the truth they would not infer from the sky.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:28

> **Or shall We make those who believe and perform righteous deeds like those who work corruption upon the earth? Or shall We make the reverent like the profligate?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Rhetorical Questions That Are One Moral Law**

The verse asks what a meaningful creation (38:27) requires of moral classification. *Am najʿalu alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti ka-l-mufsidīna fī al-arḍ* — shall We make those who believe and do righteous deeds like those who work corruption in the earth? Then: *am najʿalu al-muttaqīna ka-l-fujjār* — shall We make the people of taqwā like the profligate?

The expected answer is no. God does not run a cosmos in which these pairs are equivalent. Sister questions confirm:

> *“Shall We then make those who submit like the guilty?”* (Qur’an 68:35)

> *“Is one who believes like one who is iniquitous? They are not equal.”* (Qur’an 32:18)

Al-Rāzī notes that this can be read as continuation of the counsel of verse 17: bear patiently what they say, because the classification will be made. The Makkans’ taunt, asking for their share now (38:16), assumed either that there is no classification or that they are already on the right side of it. Verse 28 removes both assumptions.

**Corruption’s Opposite Is Not Only “Belief” as a Label**

The Qur’an usually opposes *ifsād* with *iṣlāḥ*, setting things right. Here the opposite of working corruption is “those who believe and perform righteous deeds.” The substitution is instructive. True *iṣlāḥ* is not a temperament. It is faith plus work. A person may speak the language of reform and still be of the *mufsidūn* if the work is devouring the one ewe. David has just condemned *baghy* among partners. This verse universalizes: the earth has two work-forces, and God will not pay them the same.

**The Reverent and the Profligate**

*Al-muttaqūn* are those who walk the dangerous road awake — the driving analogy the instructions themselves honor. *Al-fujjār* are those who split the limits, from *fajara*, to burst forth, to gush over a bank. Taqwā keeps the river in its bed. Fujūr is flood. Shall the careful driver and the drunk driver be given the same certificate because they shared a road? The verse’s logic is that simple, and it is the logic of the Fire and the Garden that will soon be described.

**Equality Talk That Is Not Justice**

A certain modern moral instinct flinches at unequal ends. The verse answers that unequal lives with equal ends would be the true injustice. To make the corrupter like the righteous would be to insult the righteous and to lie about the earth. God’s justice is not the equality of outcomes regardless of *ifsād*. It is the equality of a scale that actually weighs.

For the reciter the questions become examinations. In my house, are the truthful and the manipulative treated as the same because both are “family”? In my work, is the one who refuses a bribe the fool, and the *mufsid* the realist? If so, I have answered God’s “shall We make” with a practical yes, and I have taken a share in the conjecture that creation is vain. Patience under that inverted system is part of *ṣabr* in verse 17. Certainty that the inversion is not the last word is part of īmān in this verse.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:29

> **A blessed Book that We have sent down upon thee, that they may contemplate His signs and that those possessed of intellect may reflect.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Book Named as Blessing, Not as Burden**

The verse opens, as Ibn Jarīr and al-Qurṭubī note, with an implied “This is”: *kitābun anzalnāhu ilayka mubārak* — a Book We have sent down upon you, blessed. *Mubārak* is that in which increase is placed, good that multiplies in the thing and around it. Commentators say the Qur’an contains good of religion and of worldly affairs, for the ordinary believer and for the elite. It is blessed because it does not sit as ink. It grows in a life that holds it.

The purpose clauses follow: *li-yaddabbarū āyātihi* — that they may *tadabbur*, look to the hinder side of its signs, turn them over until the back of the meaning appears; *wa-li-yatadhakkara ulū al-albāb* — and that those of kernels, those possessed of intellect, may remember and take heed.

**Tadabbur as the Opposite of the Notables’ Walkout**

The *malaʾ* of verse 6 said “go.” Tadabbur says “stay.” To contemplate a sign is to refuse slogan-speed. The Qur’an asks elsewhere:

> *“Do they not contemplate the Qur’an? Or have hearts their locks upon them?”* (Qur’an 47:24)

> *“Do they not contemplate the Qur’an? Had it been from other than God, they would have found in it much discrepancy.”* (Qur’an 4:82)

Contemplation is therefore both a spiritual act and a test of origin. The Book claims to be from God and then invites the inspection that would ruin a false book. The notables’ “this is invention” (38:7) is a refusal of the invitation this verse describes as the Book’s reason for being sent.

**Those of the Kernel**

*Ulū al-albāb* — possessors of *lubb*, the kernel, the inner essence of the nut as opposed to the shell. The phrase recurs (2:179, 197, 269; 3:7, 190; 5:100; 13:19; 39:9, 18, 21; 40:54; 65:10). It does not mean the professionally clever. It means those whose intelligence has reached the inside of things. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī’s line, that when people in general contemplate they are guided, and when those of intellect reflect they are guided by proofs and by the Book’s making-clear, respects a difference of depth without making the Book two books.

The seat of this intellect in Qur’anic anthropology is not a cold brain cut off from the chest:

> *“Indeed in that is a reminder for whoever has a heart.”* (Qur’an 50:37)

Tadabbur without *ḥuḍūr al-qalb*, presence of heart, is only the shell of study. The kernel people are those in whom understanding and remembrance meet.

**Why a Blessed Book Is Placed Here**

David was given wisdom and decisive speech. The ummah of Muhammad ﷺ is given a Book that performs, for every vicegerent of a smaller earth, what *ḥikmah* and *faṣl al-khiṭāb* performed for David. You contemplate so that you may judge by truth and not follow caprice. You reflect so that you do not join the conjecture of vain creation. The stories of Solomon and Job that follow are themselves part of the *āyāt* to be turned over. The verse is a user’s instruction printed in the middle of the sūrah: do not consume these reports as entertainment. They were sent down *mubārak* so that kernels would form in you.

Practically, this condemns both the abandoned muṣḥaf and the recitation that never leaves the throat. Blessing is tied to purposes. If there is no tadabbur and no tadhakkur, the user has used a lamp as furniture. A short daily passage turned over — one dispute, one Name, one command — is closer to verse 29 than a complete recitation sped through to finish a chart. The mountains joined David at two hours of the day. The Book asks for two acts at whatever hour: contemplate, and let the kernel remember.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:30

> **And We bestowed Solomon upon David; how excellent a servant! Truly he turned oft [unto God].**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Son Named as a Gift, Not as a Dynasty’s Automatic Right**

*Wa-wahabnā li-Dāwūda Sulaymān* — and We bestowed Solomon upon David. The verb is *wahaba*, the same root as *al-Wahhāb* in verse 9. A prophet-son is not an heir produced by the father’s *mulk*. He is a bestowal. The treasuries of mercy open again, this time inside a house that has just been shown kneeling.

The Qur’an says:

> *“And Solomon inherited from David.”* (Qur’an 27:16)

The inheritance, the commentators agree in substance, is of prophethood and of a righteous sovereignty, not the automatic passing of prophecy as if it were furniture. Other sons of David are not named here. The one named is the one who will also be *awwāb*.

**The Refrain Repeated Without Dilution**

*Niʿma al-ʿabd* — how excellent a servant. *Innahu awwāb* — truly he turned oft. The epithets are the same music as David’s, and later Job’s. The sūrah is building a type. Excellence is not measured first by the wind, the jinn, or the horses that will appear. Excellence is servanthood that returns. Solomon’s later request for a kingdom unlike any after him (38:35) will be misunderstood if this verse is forgotten. He is introduced as *ʿabd*, not as emperor. Whatever kingdom comes must fit inside that word.

Both father and son were given judgment and knowledge (21:79; 27:15). The shared gift does not make the shared turning redundant. Knowledge that does not return becomes Iblīs’s argument from origin. *Mulk* that does not return becomes Pharaoh of the poles. The excellent servant is the one who, having been given, faces the Giver.

**What It Means to Be Given a Child Like This**

David’s trial in the sanctuary included, in the parable-reading some favored, the taking of what is another’s. Verse 30 is a different taking: God giving him a son who is himself a servant. The contrast is pedagogic. What you seize as ninety-nine is *ẓulm*. What you receive as *hibah* is *niʿmah*. Parents who treat children as extensions of their *ʿizzah* are in verse 2. Parents who receive children as Solomon was received — a servant of God first, a son second — are in verse 30.

For the Prophet ﷺ the verse continues the consolation. You are opposed by the sons of notables. Remember that God gives sons to servants, and that the excellence of a house is turning. For every reciter who fears that a father’s mistake (whatever David’s “that” was) has closed the future, the verse is a door: We bestowed Solomon upon David. Forgiveness (38:25) is fertile. It can be followed by a child who is himself *awwāb*.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:31

> **When at eventide noble steeds swift of foot were displayed before him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Scene of Beauty Brought at the Hour of Turning**

*Idh ʿuriḍa ʿalayhi bi-l-ʿashiyyi al-ṣāfinātu al-jiyād* — when there were displayed before him, at eventide, the horses standing with one hoof raised, swift and excellent. *Al-ṣāfināt* are horses that stand in that poised way the Arabs admired, a sign of breeding and readiness. *Al-jiyād* are the excellent, the swift of stock. *Al-ʿashiyy* is the same evening named in David’s glorification with the mountains (38:18). The hour that was a choir-hour for the father becomes a review-hour for the son.

The commentaries differ on whose horses they were: inherited from David, assembled for jihād, or a gift of kingship. The verse itself cares about display. Something beautiful, useful, and related to power is *ʿuriḍa*, spread before the servant’s eyes, at the time when remembrance is due.

**The Test Hidden in a Legitimate Good**

Horses in the Qur’an are not a vice. God even swears by charging steeds (100:1–5), and the Prophet ﷺ said, in a ḥadīth of al-Bukhārī:

> *“Goodness is tied to the forelocks of horses until the Day of Resurrection.”* (al-Bukhārī 2849)

Solomon is not being shown wine or an idol. He is being shown a good. The sūrah’s trials are often of this kind. David’s trial came as a case of justice, which is his office. Solomon’s trial comes as a review of strength, which is his office. The excellent servant is not the one who is only tested by filth. He is the one who can be tested by glory.

**Eventide as the Time When the Gaze Lengthens**

Evening is when the day’s work is reviewed and when the soul wants a reward for having worked. Displaying *al-jiyād* at that hour is like placing a feast at the door of the mosque. Will the servant remember the Lord of the horses or the horses of the Lord? Verses 32–33 will give his speech and his gesture. Verse 31 only sets the table, and it is already a complete warning for anyone who has a “review” at dusk: screens, accounts, cars, children performing, the self performing. The *ṣāfināt* still stand, one hoof raised, waiting to see whether they will be a means or a veil.


**Display as a Ritual of Power**

Kings review horses the way later states review missiles: to feel that the future is saddled. The Qur’an does not forbid the review. It timestamps it. *Bi-l-ʿashiyyi* is the hour David’s mountains glorified. A nation can hold a parade at the hour of *dhikr* and not notice that the parade has become the liturgy. Solomon is a prophet, so the verse can risk showing him in that hour without making the horses haram. The ummah is to learn the timestamp.

The Prophet ﷺ raced horses, encouraged skill at riding, and still left the night for standing. The difference is not the animal. It is whether the display is inside remembrance or on top of it. A believer’s “eventide review” may be messages, news, the day’s intake. Verse 31 asks only that you notice what is being *ʿuriḍa* before your eyes when the light slants.

**Swift and Standing Still**

The paradox of *al-ṣāfināt al-jiyād* is that they are at once still and bred for speed. The poised hoof is potential. Most of our veils are like that: not yet a sin in motion, a good held in a beautiful stance. The trial is potential. What you do when the still thing is displayed decides whether it remains *khayr*.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:32

> **he said, “I have loved the love of good things over the remembrance of my Lord, until [the sun] was taken behind the veil.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Servant Diagnosing His Own Gaze**

*Fa-qāla innī aḥbabtu ḥubba al-khayri ʿan dhikri rabbī* — he said: I have loved the love of the good, of the horses, of wealth, in a way that has stood *ʿan* — away from, or in preference to, or, in al-Rāzī’s reading, on account of — the remembrance of my Lord. The majority of the commentators took the line as a confession: the display absorbed him until the time of remembrance passed. *Al-khayr* in Arabic can mean horses specifically, as in some early usage, and good things generally. Until *tawārat bi-l-ḥijāb* — until it (the sun, or the horses) disappeared behind the veil of night or of distance.

The sentence is rare: a king catching his heart in the act. Whether one follows the majority and hears regret, or follows al-Rāzī and hears “I have loved the love of good things because of my Lord’s remembrance” — i.e., I love these steeds as instruments of a commanded good — the verse still places love, good, and *dhikr* in one breath. That triangulation is the point.

**The Sun Behind the Veil**

If the pronoun is the sun, Solomon has remained with the horses until prayer time at sunset was gone. That reading, common in the classical tafsīr, makes the trial calendrical: a duty has a door, and beauty made him miss the door. If the pronoun is the horses, he watched them until they left his sight, and the confession is about the length of the gaze, not necessarily a missed farḍ. Al-Ṭūsī records both.

The image of a veil remains either way. Something that should stay a means stepped in front of the Lord and became a *ḥijāb*. Every lawful love is capable of this. A child, a craft, a congregation, a steed — *khayr* all — can stand between the servant and the *dhikr* that makes them *khayr*. The excellent servant is not the one who has no loves. He is the one who notices when love has moved in front of the Named.

**Al-Rāzī’s Reading and the Honor of Prophets**

Al-Rāzī, jealous for ʿiṣmah, prefers: I loved these goods *because of* remembrance of my Lord; they passed from sight; bring them back. The horses are then instruments of a prophet’s jihād, loved for God’s sake. This reading avoids a missed prayer and a slaughter (see 38:33). It also fits *niʿma al-ʿabd*. The majority reading, however, has a different pedagogic power, and it does not require a major sin: a prophet can be absorbed by a good and then speak about it as David spoke, with return. The Qur’an has already shown David seeking forgiveness. A Solomon who confesses that love of *khayr* ran ahead of *dhikr* is still *awwāb*. The ummah needs that picture at least as much as it needs an unstained timetable.

**The Modern Veil**

The verse is almost too easy to update, which is why it must be updated carefully. Screens at eventide, the review of metrics, the love of “good things” that are truly good — health data, children’s success, charitable projects — can run until *tawārat bi-l-ḥijāb*, until the sun of presence is gone. The confession to practice is Solomon’s sentence, not a self-hate. Name the love. Name the remembrance. Notice the veil. Then verse 33 will show what he did with the horses when he saw.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:33

> **Bring them back unto me!” Then he began to stroke their legs and necks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pronoun That Divides the Commentators**

*Ruddūhā ʿalayya* — bring them (or it) back to me. If the pronoun is the horses, he calls the display to return. If some who take the sun as having set, he is asking that the sun be brought back so that the prayer may be in its time — a miracle-reading al-Ṭūsī mentions. The next clause decides the more natural grammar: *fa-ṭafiqa masḥan bi-l-sūqi wa-l-aʿnāq* — then he set about stroking, wiping, passing the hand over the shanks and the necks.

**Three Meanings of the Hand on the Horse**

*Masaḥa* is to wipe or stroke. The majority of classical commentators, including Ibn Jarīr, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Jalālayn, took it as the idiom of striking with the sword: he hamstrung and slaughtered the horses, either as atonement for having been distracted from remembrance, or as dedicating them by sacrifice, then giving the meat to the poor. Others said he stroked them with his hand in love and honor. Ibn Jarīr also records that he marked them, branding legs and necks as devoted to God.

Al-Rāzī rejects the slaughter as unjust to animals for a prophet’s own slip, and reads a consistent scene: the horses passed, he asked them back, he rubbed them to honor them and to check their fitness for battle, his love of them being for God’s sake. Al-Ṭabarī’s hesitation is honest: slaughter for one’s own neglect looks unfitting. Love-stroking alone may look too thin for the solemnity. Branding as endowment to God, or inspecting as a commander, sit between.

**What Can Be Held Without a Weak Tale**

The Qur’an reports a command to bring back and a wiping of legs and necks. It does not report blood. The safest teaching is therefore about relation to the good thing that veiled. Solomon does not leave the horses in the unseen of the evening and merely regret. He calls them back into a deliberate relation. Whether that relation is sacrifice, endowment, inspection, or affectionate mastery under God, the structure is: the veil is not denied, and the object is not allowed to remain an ungoverned love.

This is the practical fiqh of the verse. If a lawful good has eaten *dhikr*, the answer is not always to kill the good. Often it is to bring it back and put the hand on it in a new way: to assign it, to limit it, to give from it, to inspect whether it still serves the Lord or now serves the self. A person whose work has become the sun-veil may need to cut hours (a kind of slaughter of excess). A person whose love of children has become anxiety without remembrance may need to “stroke” — to return to presence with them as trusts, not as trophies. A person whose wealth distracted may need to brand it as God’s by zakāh and extra alms.

**The Servant’s Hand**

Legs and necks are the points of power and of yielding in a horse. To pass the hand there is to take responsibility for what carries you and what can throw you. Solomon the king is not above the animal. He touches the animal. The excellent servant’s turning includes the body of the world — wind, jinn, horses — brought back under a hand that has remembered. Verse 34 will say he was tried more darkly. Verse 33 is the lighter, brighter trial: can you touch your goods again without the veil?

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:34

> **And We did indeed try Solomon, and We cast a corpse upon his throne. He then repented.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Trial Named Without a Novel**

*Wa-la-qad fatannā Sulaymān* — We did indeed try Solomon. The same verb of David’s understanding (*fatannāhu*) is now used of the son. Then a picture that has generated more weak storytelling than almost any verse in the sūrah: *wa-alqaynā ʿalā kursiyyihi jasadan* — and We cast upon his throne a body, a corpse, a form.

Ibn Jarīr collects reports: a jinn who stole the ring of authority and sat on the throne while Solomon wandered unrecognized; a dead son he had tried to hide from the jinn; Solomon’s own body so wasted by illness that it was as a corpse upon the seat. Al-Rāzī dismisses the mutually contradictory extras as unsubstantiated. The Qur’an gives a throne, a body, and a turning. That is the skeleton, and it is enough.

**What a Corpse on a Throne Says**

A *kursī* is the visible sign of *mulk*. A *jasad* is a body without the command that makes a body a king. Placing one on the other is a parable even if it was also an event: sovereignty without the living secret of God’s permission is a carcass in a chair. Every nation has seen this — a title still printed, a man already gone, a bureaucracy running on a dead center. Solomon, who will ask in the next verse for a kingdom that cannot be stolen in that way, is taught first that thrones can bear corpses.

If the trial was illness, the teaching is that the king and the corpse can be the same person. Might does not exempt from the body’s collapse. If the trial was a loss of control to rebellious jinn, the teaching is that even a prophet’s administration can be penetrated when God tries him. If the trial was a child’s death, the teaching is that the heir on the chair can be taken. In all sound readings, *mulk* is shown as loaned.

**“Then He Turned”**

*Thumma anāba* — then he returned, repented, came back. The verb is the same family as David’s *anāba*. Some commentators, including al-Ṭūsī, allow “then he returned” as return to health or return to the throne. The sūrah’s refrain prefers the richer sense: he turned to God, and in turning he returned to what God had given. Al-Ṭabarī notes that some accounts have him regain the throne as a result of repentance. That sequence matches David: understanding of trial, then return, then confirmation of office.

**Allegory Without Irresponsibility**

Al-Rāzī’s caution against fanciful sources is a scholarly taqwā. It is also possible, as some have said, to read the verse as an allegory of what befalls a kingdom when the king’s desire sits on the chair instead of the king’s turning. Desire is a corpse. It looks like a body of wants and cannot rule. The reciter does not need the name of a particular jinn to apply this. Whenever a dead thing sits on your *kursī* — an old grudge running the house, a past status running the heart, an empty routine running the prayer — Solomon’s trial is in session. The prescribed act is not a better story about the jinn. It is *inābah*.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:35

> **saying, “My Lord, forgive me and bestow a kingdom upon me such as shall not befit anyone after me. Truly Thou art the Bestower.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Forgiveness Asked Before Kingdom**

*Qāla rabbi ighfir lī* — he said, my Lord, forgive me. The order is the sūrah’s theology in a mouth. He does not first say “restore my chair.” He says “cover me.” David sought forgiveness and fell down, then was given nearness and vicegerency. Solomon seeks forgiveness, then asks for *mulk*. The kingdom that is asked without pardon is Pharaoh’s. The kingdom asked after pardon is a servant’s tool.

**A Kingdom That Will Not Befit Anyone After Him**

*Wa-hab lī mulkan lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī* — and bestow upon me a sovereignty that is not appropriate, not seeming, not to be for anyone after me. *Min baʿdī* can mean after me in time, or besides me, other than me, as al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ṭūsī allow. In the context of a stolen or corpse-occupied throne, many commentators heard: a kingdom no satan can snatch by a ring, no rival can sit in by trick. Historically, Solomon’s united rule was not successfully held after him in that form; the request, if taken of outward kingdom, was granted in the unrepeatable sense.

Al-Maybudī’s inward reading should not be lost: he asked for dominion over the inner kingdom, the soul, so that it would not obey other than God. *Lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī* then means a mastery of the self that is his prophetic portion. The two readings can live together if the outward *mulk* is seen as a sign of the inward. God did give him wind, jinn, and a unique court. God also gave him, as this verse’s first clause shows, the knowledge to ask for pardon while asking for power.

**“Truly Thou Art the Bestower”**

*Innaka anta al-Wahhāb*. The Name of verse 9 returns on a servant’s tongue. The notables were asked whether they had the treasuries of mercy of the Mighty, the Bestower. Solomon confesses who has them. A request for unique kingdom would be *ʿizzah* if it were made as a claim. It is ʿubūdiyyah when it is made to *al-Wahhāb*. He asks the One who can give what no committee of Makkah, and no jinn of the throne-tale, can allocate.

The Prophet ﷺ taught his ummah to ask God for kings’ gifts in a different wording — the good of this world and the next — and he forbade competing for *mulk* as a worldly race. Solomon’s prayer is not a template for every believer’s ambition. It is a template for the order of asking: pardon, then need, then the Name. A student who asks for unmatched knowledge, a merchant who asks for unmatched provision, must hear the first clause or the prayer becomes Iblīs’s “I am better.”

**After Me, Barring the Door of Pretenders**

There is a mercy to later people in “not befitting anyone after me.” The unique kingdom, once given, cannot be honestly claimed by a later magician, a later tyrant, or a later pretender who would say “I sit where Solomon sat, therefore I am what Solomon was.” Verse 86 will have Muhammad ﷺ deny being of the *mutakallifūn*, the pretenders. Solomon’s prayer, granted, closes a door of false imitation. You may inherit wisdom. You may not inherit his wind.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:36

> **So We made the wind subservient unto him, coursing gently by his command whither he wished.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer in a Creature of Air**

*Fa-sakhkharnā lahu al-rīḥ* — so We made the wind subservient to him. The *fa* ties the subjection to the prayer. He asked the Bestower; the Bestower assigned him a creature that cannot be sat on as a corpse and cannot be stolen as a ring in the ordinary way. The parallel is:

> *“So We subjected to him the wind, running by his command wherever he directed.”* (Qur’an 21:81)

*Tajrī bi-amrihi rukhāʾan ḥaythu aṣāba* — running by his command, gently, wherever he aimed. *Rukhāʾ* is a soft wind, not a punishing gale. The king who might have been tempted to rule by storm is given a wind that courses *gently*. Power in this gift is yoked to mildness.

**Compensation and Proportionality**

Those who read the horse-scene as distraction from prayer say that when Solomon gave up or dedicated the swift horses, God gave him something swifter: the wind (Ibn Kathīr, al-Jalālayn). The exchange, even as a homiletic, is sound. What is surrendered for *dhikr* is not lost. It is replaced from a higher store. The man who hamstrings his own excess, or who brands his goods for God, may find himself carried by means he did not saddle.

**“Whither He Wished” and the Limit Inside the Wish**

*Ḥaythu aṣāba* — wherever he intended, wherever he struck his aim. The wish is real. It is also *bi-amrihi* in a double sense: by Solomon’s command, and never outside God’s command who subjected the wind. A prophet’s irādah is not Iblīs’s. The wind does not obey a caprice that would make creation *bāṭil*. It obeys a servant whose last named act was *inābah* and *istighfār*.

For a reciter who will never steer a wind, the verse still maps a hope. God can subject to a turning servant the very element that wrecked ʿĀd. The wind that was punishment in verse 12’s people is service in verse 36. The difference is not the air. It is the one to whom the air is assigned. Ask, after pardon, for the means you need. Take them gently. Aim them where a servant aims, not where a pretender aims.


**Gentleness as a Condition of Subjected Force**

*Rukhāʾan* is easy to skip. God could have given Solomon a hurricane and still have been the Bestower. He gave a wind that runs gently. Power that can only arrive as a gale is already close to ʿĀd. The prophets’ miracles in this sūrah are repeatedly usable without being wild: cool water, a bundle of rushes, a soft wind. The servant who asks God for means should notice which climate he is asking for.

**Command Without Cosmic Theft**

*Bi-amrihi* protects tawḥīd. The wind does not acquire a second lord. Solomon’s wish is a locus of God’s command, as the staff of Moses was. People who want “manifestation” of power without the ethics of *amr* are asking for Iblīs’s ʿizzah, not Solomon’s wind. The practical test is simple: if the means you seek would not obey a revealed command, it is not this verse’s gift.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:37

> **as well as the satans, every builder and diver.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hosts That Are Not Human**

*Wa-l-shayāṭīn* — and the satans, the rebellious of the jinn. Solomon’s court in 27:17 includes jinn, men, and birds marshaled in ranks. Here the satans are specified by labor: *kulla bannāʾin wa-ghawwāṣ* — every builder and every diver. Al-Ṭabarī: they would do what he willed in building upon the earth and in extracting from the sea. Combined with the wind, the sovereignty covers air, land, and water. The three realms of ancient kingship are gathered without Solomon having to become a god of any of them.

**Work Assigned to the Rebellious**

It is a striking mercy and a striking warning that *shayāṭīn* are made to build and to dive. Their nature in this sūrah’s ending is to mislead. Their labor in Solomon’s term is to construct and to bring up wealth from below. God can compel even a rebellious species to useful work under a prophet’s command. That does not baptize rebellion. Verse 38 will show others bound in fetters. The point is that *mulk* given by God includes the management of dangerous strengths, not the pretense that dangerous strengths do not exist.

**Builders and Divers in the Human Register**

Every society has builders and divers: those who raise the visible, those who fetch what is hidden — miners, researchers, spies, treasure-hunters of the market. Unruled, they become satanic in the moral sense: construction as pride, extraction as plunder. Under a just *awwāb*, building and diving serve. The verse is not an invitation to employ evil as evil. It is a statement that power must include a policy for the restless talents that would otherwise tunnel the throne. Solomon’s gift is unique. The need to govern builders and divers is not.


**The Sea and the Wall as Trusts**

A builder raises what will outlast a life. A diver fetches what the surface cannot see. Both can serve a prophet; both can serve Pharaoh. The Qur’an in other places condemns those who build as if they will live forever, and those who take the sea’s wealth as if there were no *ḥisāb*. Solomon’s satans do both labors under a command that has already asked for pardon (38:35). That sequence is the license. Without it, building is ʿizzah in stone, and diving is *baghy* in water.

**Unseen Labor, Seen King**

Most of a kingdom’s work is invisible. The verse refuses the sentimental idea that a just ruler deals only with pleasant helpers. He must have a policy for the restless. The believer’s unseen laborers are thoughts. Some can build. Some can dive into secrets that are not theirs. Subject them or they will subject the chair.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:38

> **and others, bound together in fetters.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Remainder Who Would Not Work**

*Wa-ākharīna muqarranīna fī al-aṣfād* — and others, coupled, bound together in shackles. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Ṭūsī: these are the recalcitrant among the jinn, those who would not accept the labor of building and diving. Solomon’s *mulk* is not only employment. It is restraint. A king who can only hire and cannot bind will be eaten by the ones who refuse the contract.

**Fetters as Justice, Not as the Style of Pharaoh**

Pharaoh was *dhū al-awtād*, of the stakes. Solomon has *aṣfād*, fetters, upon a rebellious unseen. The difference is moral object. Pharaoh staked the innocent and the dissenting believer. Solomon binds the satanic. The Qur’an does not treat all restraint as tyranny. It treats restraint of *baghy* as part of *ḥukm bi-l-ḥaqq*. David was told to judge with truth. Solomon’s fetters are that judgment in the unseen workforce.

**The Inner Shackling**

For the servant without a court of jinn, the verse still speaks. There are impulses that can be put to work — anger that becomes courage in a just cause, desire that becomes love in a covenant. There are impulses that must be fettered — envy, the glance, the lie. A spirituality that only “employs” and never “binds” will find a corpse on the chair. *Taqwā* is, again, knowing which powers in the self are builders and which are wreckers, and not confusing the two because both are strong.


**Binding Without Becoming Pharaoh**

The stakes of Pharaoh and the fetters of Solomon differ by object and by warrant. Islamic law later developed ḥudūd, taʿzīr, and rules of prisoners because a community cannot live on employment of the willing alone. The verse is not a blank check for shackles. It is a refusal of naïveté. Recalcitrance exists, including in the unseen. A spirituality that denies this becomes prey.

**Coupled in Fetters**

*Muqarranīn* — bound together. Evil prefers company, as the troop of 38:59 will show. Binding “together” is sometimes the only way to stop a chain of *baghy*. In the self, vices are also coupled: pride with envy, lust with lying. Fetter them as a pair or they will open each other’s doors.


**The Unseen Requires Law, Not Mood**

A religion that believes in jinn and then has no category for restraint would be a superstition. Solomon’s fetters are part of the same *ḥikmah* David received: put things in their places. Some beings, and some impulses, are not in their place unless bound. The modern habit of treating every limit as oppression is Pharaoh’s opposite error: Pharaoh bound the innocent; this error refuses to bind the predatory. Verse 38 stands in the middle, where justice stands.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:39

> **“This is Our Gift; so bestow graciously or withhold without reckoning.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gift Named as Gift While It Is Still in the Hand**

*Hādhā ʿaṭāʾunā* — this is Our gift. God speaks so that Solomon cannot misread wind, jinn, and fetters as wages he extracted. *ʿAṭāʾ* is a granting from above. Al-Ṭabarī records that “this” was taken as the kingdom, or the power over jinn, or all that he was given. The wider reading is safer. Everything in verses 36–38 is placed back in God’s hand even as it remains in Solomon’s.

**Leave to Give and to Withhold**

*Fa-umnun aw amsik bi-ghayri ḥisāb* — so bestow, do kindness, or hold back, without reckoning. He is permitted to give of these powers and to withhold, and he will not be called to a petty account on the manner of distribution, because God has authorized the discretion. This is not a license to injustice. It is a removal of niggardly fear in a prophet who has already asked for pardon and has been given a unique *mulk*. Al-Ṭabarī: he may give or withhold as he deems best.

The phrase *bi-ghayri ḥisāb* elsewhere describes God’s own giving (2:212; 24:38). Here a servant is allowed a shadow of that freedom as a gift, not as an attribute of his essence. The Bestower sometimes gives a servant the role of bestower.

**The Danger and the Dignity of Discretion**

Dignity: a vicegerent is not a machine. He may show favor, may withhold, may read cases. Danger: a non-prophet who quotes this verse as a blank check for nepotism has stolen Solomon’s charter without Solomon’s *inābah*. The ummah’s rulers are under *ḥisāb*, as ʿUmar knew. The verse is descriptive of Solomon’s unique gift. Its transferable core is smaller and still large: what you have is *ʿaṭāʾ*; give from it without the miser’s terror; withhold without the hoarder’s glee; do not imagine the treasury is yours by species.


**Discretion Under the Word “Gift”**

Every permission in this verse is preceded by *hādhā ʿaṭāʾunā*. If a ruler, a parent, or a wealthy person hears only “give or withhold without reckoning,” they have cut the verse in half. The ʿAbbāsid and every later court that quoted Solomon for luxury without quoting 38:35’s *ighfir lī* was performing that surgery.

**Without Reckoning Is Not Without the Day**

*Bi-ghayri ḥisāb* here is about the petty audit of a prophet’s authorized favors. It is not a cancellation of *yawm al-ḥisāb*. Solomon will still stand. The believer who gives freely because God gave freely is in the spirit of the verse. The believer who withholds cruelly and says “this is my gift to do with as I please” has put a corpse on the chair again.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:40

> **Truly nearness unto Us shall be his, and a beautiful return.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Sentence That Closed David’s File**

Verse 25 said of David: *wa-inna lahu ʿindanā la-zulfā wa-ḥusna maʾāb*. Verse 40 says it of Solomon without changing a word of the promise. The repetition is the theology. Unique wind and unique fetters do not buy a different nearness. Father and son meet in *zulfā* and a beautiful return. The Garden will not have two castes called “those who had kingdoms” and “those who had sores.” Job is about to be mentioned. He will not be given the wind. He will be given the same kind of praise: excellent servant, *awwāb*.

**Nearness Is Not the Wind**

A reciter might envy Solomon’s *mulk* and miss Solomon’s *zulfā*. The verse puts the envy in its place. What is announced as his, in the end, is what was announced as David’s: proximity with God and a homecoming that is beautiful. The wind stops. The jinn are not the companions of the next world. *Zulfā* remains.

**A Beautiful Return After a Corpse on a Chair**

Solomon was tried. A body was thrown on his throne. He turned, asked pardon, asked kingdom, received gift and discretion. The last word is not the gift. The last word is return. All *mulk* in this world, even his, is a road. The *maʾāb* is the house. The sūrah will soon describe that house as Gardens of Eden with open gates (38:50). David and Solomon are promised it by name. The reverent will be promised it as a class (38:49). The structure tells the poor believer: you are not locked out of the sentence that was spoken over kings. You are invited into the same last line, if you are of those who turn.


**Why the Formula Must Repeat**

If the promise to Solomon differed from the promise to David, the sūrah would have taught that wind buys a better nearness. It does not. The poor reciter is being protected from a theology of spiritual classes based on worldly *mulk*. Nearness is *ʿindanā*. The beautiful return is God’s to decorate.

**Return as the Meaning of Mulk**

All of Solomon’s subjected forces are movements: wind coursing, divers descending, builders raising. The last word is *maʾāb*, a place of stopping with God. Motion without return is Iblīs’s respite. Motion with return is prophethood. Hold the last line tighter than the wind.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:41

> **And remember Our servant Job, when he called upon his Lord, “Truly Satan has afflicted me with weariness and punishment.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Remember Moves to a Different Kind of Might**

*Wa-dhkur ʿabdanā Ayyūb* — and remember Our servant Job. The same imperative that opened David’s file opens Job’s. The Prophet ﷺ, still under the insult of “lying sorcerer,” is given a servant whose trial is not a courtroom and not a throne but a body and a house stripped. The Qur’an’s longest account of Job is this passage (with 21:83–84). He is listed among those God guided (6:84) and those to whom revelation was given (4:163). He is not, in the Qur’an, a long debate on theodicy. He is a caller.

**The Call That Does Not Curse the Lord**

*Idh nādā rabbahu annī massaniya al-shayṭānu bi-nuṣbin wa-ʿadhāb* — when he called his Lord: Satan has touched me with weariness and with punishment. *Nuṣb* is fatigue, toil, the used-upness of a person. *ʿAdhāb* is pain, punishment, torment. Al-Ṭabarī: the loss of property and family and a painful disease, said by some to have lasted seven years and seven months. He attributes the touch to Satan, as 21:83 also records that he cried, “Harm has touched me, and You are the most merciful of the merciful.”

He does not say God is unjust. He does not adopt the Makkan joke of asking that the share be hastened. He names a touch, names its agent as Satan, and names his Lord as the one to call. This is ʿaqīdah in pain. Secondary causes are real enough to mention; they are not last enough to worship or to despair under.

**Why Satan Is Named in a Believer’s Illness**

The Qur’an does not require that every fever be a satanic possession. It does teach that Satan loves to tie hardship to whisper: “If you were valued, this would not have happened; curse your Lord; throw away patience.” Job’s sentence cuts the whisper from the Lord. The weariness is a touch of the enemy. The address is to the Friend. Many patients need this grammar. They may use medicine, as Job will use water, and still refuse to let the pain become a proof against God.

**Patience Without the Bible’s Long Argument**

The Biblical book of Job is a sustained argument about innocent suffering. The Qur’an, as the source commentary notes, does not turn Job into that seminar. It gives the cry, the command to strike the ground, the restoration, the oath, and the praise. The difference is not that Islam has no interest in evil’s problem. It is that this sūrah’s interest is *inābah* under evil. David turned from a trial of office. Solomon turned from a trial of throne. Job turns from a trial of depletion. The insulted Prophet ﷺ is to remember all three, because Makkah was dealing him a mixture of all three: office denied, power absent, body and companions hurt.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:42

> **“Strike with thy foot; this is cool water wherewith to wash and to drink.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command That Makes the Earth a Well**

*Urkuḍ bi-rijlika* — stamp, strike, run your foot. The healing does not begin with a lecture. It begins with a movement of the tried body. *Hādhā mughtasalun bāridun wa-sharāb* — this is a cool washing-place and a drink. Al-Ṭabarī: a spring for healing. Most commentators, including Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī, speak of two springs, or of water that served two functions: washing that healed the outer, drinking that healed the inner. 21:84 is the parallel of relief after the cry.

**Coolness as the Opposite of His ʿAdhāb**

He had named weariness and punishment. God names *bārid*, cool. The gift matches the wound without being a replica of the wound. Cool water is among the Qur’an’s quiet mercies, the element people remember in fever and in the grave’s asking. Here it is given before death, as a *mughtasal* and a *sharāb*, so that Job’s remaining life is not only endurance but washing.

**Means After Crying, Not Instead of Crying**

He called; then he was told to strike the ground. Tawakkul in this verse is not stillness. It is a foot. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the meaning recorded in the Sunan, that the believer ties the camel and trusts. Job’s camel is a foot-strike. Those who leave medicine in the name of reliance, and those who leave prayer in the name of medicine, both miss the verse. The spring is God’s; the stamp is Job’s.

Ibn Kathīr records reports that rain filled his fields, and the ḥadīth in which golden locusts fell upon Job as he bathed, and he gathered them, and his Lord asked whether He had not made him rich enough, and Job said:

> *“Yes, my Lord; but I am not without need of Your blessing.”* (recorded by al-Bukhārī 279)

Whether that ḥadīth is mapped onto this exact spring or onto the restoration in general, it paints the same servant: healed, and still collecting God’s gifts without apology, because need of *barakah* does not end when sores end.

**Washing and Drinking as a Picture of Tawbah**

Outer and inner, wash and drink: the same water, two uses. Repentance and healing in this religion often have that double. The body has a law; the heart has a law. Job is not told to wash only, as if religion were a bath, nor to drink only, as if it were a secret without limbs. The excellent servant uses what God opened with both skin and throat.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:43

> **And We bestowed upon him his family and their like along with them as a mercy from Us and a reminder for possessors of intellect.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb of Solomon’s Birth Used in Job’s Restoration**

*Wa-wahabnā lahu ahlahu* — We bestowed upon him his family. The same *wahaba*. Commentators differ on the mode: they were brought back from death (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī), or healed, or returned from absence (al-Rāzī). *Wa-mithlahum maʿahum* — and their like with them: an increase, a doubling of the household’s joy if not always of the Biblical head-count. The Biblical account doubles livestock and keeps the number of sons and daughters; the Qur’an stresses family plus their like. Mercy is not only replacement. It is surplus.

**Mercy and Reminder, Two Reasons for One Gift**

*Raḥmatan minnā wa-dhikrā li-ulī al-albāb* — as a mercy from Us and as a reminder for those of intellect. Job’s healing is not only for Job. It is a *dhikrā*, a remembrance, for kernel-people. Verse 29 said the Book was sent so that those of intellect might remember. Verse 43 says Job’s restored house is itself a verse inside the Book. The possessors of *albāb* are to see: crying to God in weariness is not a failed strategy; the Bestower can return a family and add their like.

**What the Kernel Understands That the Shell Does Not**

The shell sees a story with a happy ending and calls it folklore. The kernel sees a law of *raḥmah*: God may take, and God may give more than was taken, and neither the taking nor the giving is *bāṭil*. The kernel also sees a warning: restoration is *from Us*, not from Satan who touched, not from the water as water, not from the foot as foot. Means remain means.

For a person who has lost — a child, a marriage, a country, a health — the verse is permission to ask for *ahl* and their like, and a caution not to measure God by the year of *nuṣb* alone. Mercy may be delayed so that it can become a reminder for many intellects, not only a private relief. Job’s years of sores, if the reports of length are even roughly true, were long enough to make the return a public *āyah*. Some mercies are slow because they are meant to be read, not only received.


**Family as Rizq, Not as Possession**

*Ahlahu* is people, household, those one is at home with. They are *wahabnā*, bestowed. A restored family is therefore not a trophy proving Job “won.” It is a mercy that could have been withheld without God ceasing to be just. That is why the verse adds *raḥmatan minnā*. The possessors of intellect remember both the sores and the surplus as from one Lord.

**Their Like Along With Them**

Increase after loss is one of God’s habits with those He loves, though not a contract He owes on our timetable. The kernel-people do not make it a law that every Job in the ward will go home with doubled children next year. They make it a law that God may, and that asking, as Job asked, is not bad manners. Despair is bad manners.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:44

> **And, “Take with thy hand a bundle of rushes and strike therewith, and break not thine oath.” Truly We found him to be steadfast. What an excellent servant! Truly he turned oft [unto God].**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command Whose Popular Tafsīr Is Stronger Than Its Text**

*Wa-khudh bi-yadika ḍighthan fa-ḍrib bihi wa-lā taḥnath* — take in your hand a bundle, a handful of mixed stalks, and strike with it, and do not break your oath. The widespread story in the commentaries is that Job had sworn to strike his wife, after she had listened to Satan’s suggestion that he say a word or do an act to end the trial, and God then told him to fulfill the oath with a bundle of rushes so that the blow would be symbolic and she would not be harmed. Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Zamakhsharī all transmit versions. The source commentary rightly notes that the Qur’an and the sound Ḥadīth do not supply this narrative.

A reading closer to the wording, and more consistent with the healing of verse 42, is that the bundle is medicinal — herbs, rushes, a sheaf used in treatment — and “do not break your oath” is either a general order to keep his vows to God while using means, or an assurance that using the sheaf is not perjury of some vow of reliance. This respects the text’s silence about beating a wife.

**The Praise That Does Not Depend on That Tale**

What God makes unambiguous is the evaluation: *innā wajadnāhu ṣābiran* — We found him steadfast. *Niʿma al-ʿabd* — how excellent a servant. *Innahu awwāb* — truly he turned oft. *Wajadnāhu* is “We found him,” the language of a result after examination. Patience here is not a slogan Job recited. It is what God found when He looked.

Ṣabr in Job is not passivity. He called (38:41), stamped (38:42), took a bundle (38:44). Steadfastness is remaining turned toward God while using the foot and the hand. The excellent servant is the one in whom *ṣabr* and *awbah* are one motion: he does not leave, and he does not stop returning.

**Oaths, Hands, and the Refusal of Cruel Fulfillment**

Even if one keeps the common story, the legal yield that Muslim jurists sometimes drew — that an oath to harm can be fulfilled in a way that avoids harm, or expiated — must be handled with care. The Prophet’s Sharīʿah has rules of kaffārah for broken oaths and forbids harming the innocent. The spirit that can be saved from the common tale is God’s dislike of using His name to ratify a cruelty. “Do not break your oath” does not mean “do not break her.” A bundle of rushes, in that reading, is a Divine joke against our wish to be both angry and pious.

The cleaner yield, without the tale: keep your word to God; take the means in your hand; be found patient; be an excellent servant; turn. That is already a complete ethics for illness, for marriage, for work, and for the Prophet ﷺ under insult. David was *awwāb* with might. Solomon was *awwāb* with wind. Job is *awwāb* with sores. The sūrah’s definition of excellence is now undeniable.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:45

> **And remember Our servants Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, possessed of strength and sight.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Cluster of Servants After the Three Long Files**

David, Solomon, and Job have been given scenes. Now the command *wa-dhkur ʿibādanā* — remember Our servants — opens a constellation: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. They are not given anecdotes here. They are given two qualities: *ulī al-aydī wa-l-abṣār* — possessors of hands (strength) and of eyes (sights, insights).

Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī: strength in acts of worship, and insight in the affairs of religion. Al-Ṭabarī adds that their vision was the vision of the heart. *Aydī* without *abṣār* is the might of ʿĀd. *Abṣār* without *aydī* is a knowledge that will not carry a sacrifice, a night prayer, or a journey. Abraham has both: the hand that held the knife in the trial, the eye that saw the stars and said “I love not things that set.” Isaac and Jacob inherit a house where both are expected.

**Why Strength and Sight Are Paired After Job**

Job’s body lost strength; his sight toward his Lord did not. Abraham’s house is now named as the place where strength and sight are a lineage. The Prophet ﷺ, of Abraham’s prayer, is being told that his own people, who claim Abraham’s House in Makkah, have dropped the *abṣār* while keeping a certain *ayd* of caravan and clan. Remember the servants who had both.

**Servants, Plural, Owned by the Same Lord**

*ʿIbādanā* — Our servants. The possessive is the honor. They are not “great men of the two towns” (43:31). They are owned. Quraysh wanted a prophet who looked like their notables. God says: remember men whose definition is that We own them, and they had hands and eyes. If you have hands without eyes, you are ʿĀd. If you have neither, you are the corpse on the chair. The ummah’s educational ideal is this pair: power of obedience, vision of truth.


**Hands and Eyes Against Makkah’s Split**

Quraysh had hands: trade, arms, the hanging of poems. They were short of *abṣār* in the Qur’anic sense: insight that sees the idol as stone and the orphan as messenger. Abraham’s house is remembered as the repair of that split. Strength without sight persecutes. Sight without strength pities and does not move. The Prophet ﷺ had both: the eye of revelation and the hand that dug at the Trench.

**A Lineage of Seeing**

Isaac and Jacob are not extras. They prove that *aydī* and *abṣār* can be a house style, not a single genius. Parents who want “Abrahamic” children should ask what hands and what eyes they actually train: thumbs for screens, or hands for prayer and giving; eyes for fault in others, or eyes for the Abode.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:46

> **Truly We purified them with that which is pure—remembrance of the Abode.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Purification Named as a Divine Act**

*Innā akhlaṣnāhum bi-khāliṣatin* — We purified them, We made them devoted, with a *khāliṣah*, a thing purely itself, unmixed. The instrument of their ikhlāṣ is then named: *dhikrā al-dār* — remembrance of the Abode, the last house, the home.

Ibn Kathīr: God removed love of this world from their hearts and made them mindful of the Hereafter. Sahl al-Tustarī, as reported, said God purified them for remembrance of God for God’s sake, not for the sake of wages; they remembered Him through Him and for Him. The two comments are not at war. Remembrance of the Abode is how love of the present house is cleaned; the highest ikhlāṣ then remembers the Owner of the Abode even more than the Abode.

**The Abode as What the Notables Forgot**

Quraysh asked for their share before the Day of Reckoning (38:16). David was warned against forgetting that Day (38:26). Abraham’s house is described as having been washed with the memory of the *dār*. Purification is not first a ritual of water — though Abraham’s millah is full of ritual. It is a ritual of attention. What you remember as home decides what you will dirty yourself for.

**A Pure Thing, Unmixed**

*Khāliṣah* is quality without alloy. Many religious lives are mixed: some remembrance of the Abode, some worship of the market. God says He purified these servants with an unmixed remembrance. The reciter cannot claim Abraham by blood or by a building in Makkah without submitting to this wash. If the last home is a vague decoration, the ikhlāṣ has not been given. Ask for it. It is a bestowal, *akhlaṣnāhum*, We did this to them. Then act as people act who know they are moving house.


**The Abode Cleans the Present House**

You cannot wash a heart with a place you do not think is real. Remembrance of the *dār* is not a morbid hobby. It is detergent. It removes the stain of treating Tuesday as the only room. Al-Tustarī’s report — they remembered Him for Him, not for wages — raises the purification still higher: even the Abode is not the idol; the Owner is the point. Most of us need the first step before the second: remember that you are moving.

**Ikhlāṣ Is Given and Then Inhabited**

*Akhlaṣnāhum* is God’s act. People who boast of their own unmixed sincerity have already mixed it. Ask for the wash. Then live as the washed live: less panic in loss, less drunkenness in gift, less need to take the one ewe.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:47

> **And truly in Our Sight they are among the chosen, the elect.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rank Stated from the Only Side That Confers It**

*Wa-innahum ʿindanā la-min al-muṣṭafayna al-akhyār* — and truly they, with Us, are of the chosen, the excellent. *ʿIndanā* again, as in David’s and Solomon’s *zulfā*. Chosenness is not a vote of the *malaʾ*. It is a status *with Us*. *Muṣṭafayn* is the same root as Muṣṭafā, the Prophet’s epithet: those God has selected. *Al-akhyār* are the good as a class, the better, the excellent.

**Election After Purification, After Strength and Sight**

The order of 45–47 is a curriculum. Possess strength and sight; be purified by remembrance of the Abode; then know that with God you are among the chosen. People want verse 47 without 45 and 46. They want to be elect without hands, eyes, or the wash of the last house. The sūrah does not sell that.

**Consolation for a Chosen Prophet Among the Unchosen Notables**

Muhammad ﷺ is of this Abrahamic election. The notables who said “has the Reminder been sent down upon him among all of us?” (38:8) are answered without being named: chosenness is *ʿindanā*. Their treasury does not include it. The believer who is mocked as backward for remembering the Abode is placed, if he is true, nearer to Abraham than the brilliant *fājur* who has forgotten the Day. Verse 28 asked whether God would make them alike. Verse 47 names one side of the unlike.


**Chosenness That Cannot Be Campaigning**

*Muṣṭafayn* is a passive. They were chosen. The *malaʾ* of verse 6 choose themselves and then choose their gods. Election in this verse sits *ʿindanā*, where envy cannot campaign (38:8). The consolation for the unchosen by the town is the same as the warning for the town’s elect: the only list that holds is the list God keeps.

**Akhyār as a Plural Mercy**

He does not say “the one excellent man.” The good are a people. Isolation is not a condition of being chosen. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are together in the sentence as they were together in a house. Seek the excellent as company, not only as a private medal.


**With Us: the Only Audience of Rank**

*ʿIndanā* is the cure of verse 8’s jealousy. If chosenness is with God, then being unseen by Quraysh is not being unseen. Many servants of later times will do their *aydī* and *abṣār* in rooms no historian will enter. Verse 47 is written for them as much as for Abraham. Do the work of 45–46. Leave the list of 47 to the only Sight that confers it.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:48

> **And remember Ishmael, Elisha, and Dhu’l-Kifl; each is among the excellent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three More Names, One Class**

*Wa-dhkur Ismāʿīla wa-l-Yasaʿa wa-Dhā l-Kifl* — and remember Ishmael, Elisha, and Dhū’l-Kifl. *Wa-kullun min al-akhyār* — and each is of the excellent. The same *akhyār* as verse 47. The list of the good is not closed with Abraham’s immediate line. It includes Ishmael, who is Abraham’s other son, the one tied to the Makkan House; Elisha, named only here and in 6:86 among those God favored above the worlds; and Dhū’l-Kifl, named here and in 21:85 among the patient.

**Ishmael at the Door of Quraysh**

To command a Makkan audience to remember Ishmael is to command them to remember their own claimed father as *min al-akhyār*, not as a mascot of idols. Ishmael’s Qur’anic file includes the building of the House (2:125–128) and the patience of the sacrifice-narrative in Sūrah 37, just before this sūrah. If they honored him, they would honor the tawḥīd of the House, not the gods of verse 5.

**Elisha: A Name That Ties the Book to the Earlier Book**

Al-Yasaʿ is widely identified with Elisha, disciple of Elijah, who received the mantle in the Biblical history. The Qur’an does not tell his episodes. It places him in the mercy-list with Ishmael, Jonah, and Lot (6:86). The point in Ṣād is inclusion: the excellent are not only the household names of Arabia. Revelation’s memory is international. A people who say “we have not heard this in the latter creed” (38:7) are told to remember a prophet they may barely have heard, because God’s *akhyār* are not limited to local hearing.

**Dhū’l-Kifl: The Possessor of a Portion**

The name is a title: possessor of *kifl*, a share, a portion, a doubling, as in:

> *“He will give you a double portion (*kiflayn*) of His mercy.”* (Qur’an 57:28)

From *k-f-l*, to assume responsibility, to guarantee. Commentators said he had a double share of prophetic labor, or pledged himself to a prophet, or pledged to fast daily, pray nightly, and judge without anger. Some identified him with Elijah, Joshua, or Zechariah (the last because God *kaffala* Mary to Zechariah in 3:37). The majority on 21:85, al-Qurṭubī notes, did not even insist he was a prophet. Linking him to Ezekiel via “bearing the house of Israel’s punishment” is, as the source says, not justified.

What the verse requires is that a man known as the possessor of a responsible share is among *al-akhyār*. Excellence can be a name the public does not know how to parse. God still says: remember him. The reciter who cannot settle the identification can still take the root: be a person of *kifl* — of a pledged share of duty, of responsibility taken and kept — and ask to be among the excellent. The sūrah has now filled the sky with servants so that Quraysh cannot say they were not given models, and so that the believer cannot say excellence had only one shape: king, or invalid, or father of nations, or forgotten title. Each is among the good.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:49

> **This is a reminder. And for the reverent there will surely be a beautiful return.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Stories Declared to Be Dhikr Before They Are History**

*Hādhā dhikrun* — this is a reminder. Ibn Kathīr takes “this” as the Qur’an; al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī allow the stories just told. The sūrah began by swearing by the Qur’an possessed of *dhikr* (38:1). Midway it says of the Book that those of intellect may *tadhakkur* (38:29). Job’s restoration was a *dhikrā* for them (38:43). Now the whole preceding movement is stamped: reminder, not epic for sport.

A reminder is something you already needed and might have dropped. David, Solomon, Job, Abraham’s house, Ishmael, Elisha, Dhū’l-Kifl — these are not information to fill a gap in Arabian antiquarianism. They are the memory that pride erases.

**The Reverent Are Given the Kings’ Last Sentence**

*Wa-inna li-l-muttaqīna la-ḥusna maʾāb* — and truly for the people of taqwā is a beautiful return. The exact promise given to David (38:25) and Solomon (38:40) is now a class promise. Taqwā, not throne, is the ticket. The *muttaqī* is the one who walks the high road awake, who does not treat creation as vain, who does not follow *hawā*. He may never have had wind or a choir of mountains. He is given the same *ḥusnā maʾāb*.

This is the sūrah’s justice in one line. Verse 28 asked whether the reverent would be made like the profligate. Verse 49 begins the long answer: no, and here is their return. Verses 55 and following will give the profligate theirs.

**Reminder as What Makes Return Beautiful**

The beautiful return is announced in the same breath as “this is a reminder,” because no one reaches the *maʾāb* without *dhikr* on the road. The Garden is not a surprise party for the amnesiac. It is home for people who remembered the Abode (38:46) enough to be washed by it. Practically, the verse tells the reciter how to use the first half of Ṣād: not to admire prophets as one admires statues, but to become of the *muttaqīn* whose last line matches theirs.


**From Named Servants to a Path Anyone May Walk**

The sūrah could have closed the first half with “how excellent these men were” and left the reciter as a spectator. *Li-l-muttaqīn* opens the gate. Taqwā is available to the unprophetable, the unkinged, the untested by sores of Job’s degree. The beautiful return is classed by reverence, which is a way of walking, not a passport.

**Reminder Before Reward**

*Hādhā dhikrun* precedes the promise so that no one treats Paradise as a prize detached from memory. The Garden in the next verses is the future of *dhikr*, not a replacement for it. If you want 38:50–54, you want 38:49a first: let this be reminder.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:50

> **Gardens of Eden, the gates whereof are open unto them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Eden Named as a Place Inside the Promise**

*Jannātu ʿAdn* — Gardens of Eden, of lasting settlement. The phrase recurs (9:72; 13:23; 16:31; 18:31; 19:61; 20:76; 35:33; 40:8) and is taken as an especially blessed region of Paradise, or as Paradise named by its quality of ʿadn, perpetual dwelling. After “beautiful return,” the sūrah does not leave the return abstract. It gives it geography of a revealed kind.

**Gates Open, Not Climbed**

*Mufattaḥatan lahum al-abwāb* — the gates opened for them. David’s disputants scaled a sanctuary. Iblīs will refuse a command in a garden of another order. The reverent do not scale. For them the doors are already *mufattaḥah*, thrown open. The ḥadīth of the eight gates is famous in this connection. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the meaning recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *“In Paradise there are eight gates. There is a gate called al-Rayyān, through which those who fast will enter, and none other than they will enter it.”* (al-Bukhārī 3257; Muslim 1152)

And in other reports, people of prayer, of charity, of fasting, of jihād are called from corresponding gates, and some are called from all. Sūrah 39 says:

> *“Those who were reverent to their Lord will be driven to the Garden in throngs, until, when they reach it, and its gates are opened, its keepers will say, ‘Peace be upon you; you have done well; so enter it, to abide.’”* (Qur’an 39:73)

And:

> *“Angels shall enter upon them from every gate: ‘Peace be upon you for what you patiently endured.’ How excellent is the Ultimate Abode!”* (Qur’an 13:23–24)

Open gates are the opposite of *lāta ḥīna manāṣ* (38:3). There, the cry came when escape’s time had passed. Here, arrival finds doors that do not need to be forced. Taqwā in the world is walking as if some doors may close; taqwā’s wage is doors that do not.

**Openness as Honor**

A closed palace is Pharaoh. An open garden is gift. The reverent are not petitioners at a notable’s door in Makkah. They are people for whom the next world has already unlocked. A believer who feels locked out of every room in this life is meant to hear *mufattaḥatan lahum*. The opening is timed to the return, not always to the Tuesday of the dunya.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:51

> **reclining therein, they call therein for abundant fruit and drink.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Recline: The End of the Stance of Alert**

*Muttakiʾīna fīhā* — reclining therein. The posture of taqwā in the world is upright, like a driver on the mountain road. The posture of the Garden is reclining, because the drop beside the road is gone. Other verses add couches arrayed, facing one another, shade, spouses (52:20; 37:44; 56:16; 36:56). Rest is not laziness here. It is the arrival of people who did not rest in the wrong place.

**They Call for Fruit and Drink**

*Yadʿūna fīhā bi-fākihatin kathīratin wa-sharāb* — they call, they summon, therein for fruit in abundance and drink. Asking in the Garden is not the needy cry of Job, nor the mocking cry of verse 16. It is the call of honored guests whose request is itself part of the hospitality. 52:22: fruits and meat as they desire. 47:15: rivers of water, milk, wine, honey. 52:23: a cup passed, no idle talk, no incitement to sin.

Job was given cool water to wash and drink as healing. Here drink is delight. The element is related; the state is other. God is not poor in water. He gives it as medicine and as feast according to the house.

**Abundance Without the Ninety-Nine**

*Kathīrah*, abundant fruit, in a sūrah that condemned the man of ninety-nine ewes taking the one. In the Garden, abundance does not require *baghy*. The structure of scarcity that tempts partners to transgress (38:24) is gone. This is part of why the return is beautiful. People who learned not to devour the one ewe are given a table at which devouring the other is impossible and unnecessary.

The reciter’s work is to practice that economy now, in relative scarcity, as training for a place where calling for fruit is not theft. If you cannot ask God for a lawful apple without cheating a brother, you have not yet understood either this verse or verse 23.


**Calling Without Neediness, Need Without Shame**

*Yadʿūna* is the same root as duʿāʾ. In the world, calling is the poor man’s act and the prophet’s act — Job called. In the Garden, calling is still there, because a servant remains a servant even on a couch. What dies is the ache of scarcity and the sin of idle talk around the cup (52:23). The religion does not end in a silence of proud self-sufficiency. It ends in a call that is immediately hospitality.

**Fruit as the Reversal of the First Garden’s Trial**

Adam’s story, coming later in this sūrah, includes a tree and a slip. The believers recline calling for fruit without a forbidden branch hanging over the feast. Abundance here is safe. That safety was purchased by taqwā in a world where some fruit was not yours (the brother’s ewe, the unlawful glance, the orphan’s property). Learn the lawful call now.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:52

> **And theirs shall be maidens of modest gaze, of like age.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Companionship Named After Food**

The Garden’s hospitality includes persons, not only fruit. *Wa-ʿindahum qāṣirātu al-ṭarf* — and with them are ones whose gaze is shortened, restrained. Al-Qurṭubī and others: they look upon none but their spouses. *Atrāb*, of like age, peers, matched. Other verses of the maidens of Paradise (44:54; 52:20; 55:56, 72; 56:22; 78:33) fill the picture with a reserve the Qur’an itself chooses. The point here is not a catalogue for appetite. It is that the beautiful return includes a love that does not wander.

**Modest Gaze as the Healed Eye**

The world of *fujjār* is a world of unshortened glances — at the brother’s ewe, at the neighbor’s spouse, at every screen. *Qaṣr al-ṭarf* is the opposite of that flood. Even the companions of Paradise are described first by a virtue of the eye, not by a virtue of display. The believer who wants this verse as a consumer and will not lower his gaze in the street has tried to buy the Garden’s companionship with the fujjār’s currency.

**Like Age, Like Meeting**

*Atrāb* suggests harmony, not the predatory mismatch of power that 38:23 condemned. The Garden’s sociality is peer and peace. For married believers the verse also transfigures earthly companionship: modest gaze and suitability are already a law of this world (24:30–31). Paradise is that law made climate.


**The Gaze as a Moral Organ**

The Qur’an legislates lowering the gaze in this world (24:30–31) and then describes the next world’s companions by that same organ. Continuity, not contradiction. People who treat Paradise’s spouses as a license for a filthy imagination have inverted the verse. *Qāṣirāt al-ṭarf* is a praise of limit. Limit is the mother of the joy the verse is actually giving.

**Like Age and the End of Predation**

So much injustice in desire is a mismatch of power, age, and need — the ninety-nine taking the one. *Atrāb* is harmony. The sūrah that condemned overpowering speech in a property case also refuses overpowering in love. If that is the climate of the end, it should be the ethic of the road.


**Companionship Without the Market**

In a world that sells bodies, this verse is often misread as more market. It is the opposite: a gaze that has a home, an age that matches, a presence *ʿindahum* that is withness, not consumption. Train for it by keeping covenants now, by not treating people as fruit that exists to be called for without a name. The Garden’s loves are named by virtue first.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:53

> **This is that which you are promised for the Day of Reckoning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Garden Tied Back to the Day David Was Told Not to Forget**

*Hādhā mā tūʿadūna li-yawm al-ḥisāb* — this is what you are promised for the Day of Reckoning. The Day that the notables asked to hasten as a taunt (38:16), and that the straying forget (38:26), is the Day for which this table is dated. The Garden is not a weekend. It is a promise timed to ḥisāb.

**Promise, Not Guess**

*Tūʿadūn*, you are promised. The conjecture of those who disbelieve (38:27) is that creation is vain. A promise of Gardens of Eden is the contradiction of that conjecture. If the promise is true, the world is not *bāṭil*. If the world is not *bāṭil*, woe to those who disbelieve, from the Fire (38:27) — and joy to those who were reverent, from the open gates.

**How a Promise Educates a Day**

A person who believes he is promised this for a Day of Reckoning lives Tuesday differently. He can lose a ewe without becoming Iblīs. He can miss a throne without a corpse of envy sitting on his chest. He can take Job’s water without demanding Job’s surplus on the same schedule. The promise is not opium. It is the reason justice is not stupid. Verse 54 will add that the provision does not run out, so that the promise cannot be suspected of being a limited ration.


**Dated Joy**

Promises without dates become slogans. This joy is dated to *yawm al-ḥisāb*. That does two things. It keeps the Garden from being confused with a utopian political Tuesday. And it keeps the Day from being only a terror. For the reverent the Day is the day the table is set. David was told not to forget it. This verse tells you why forgetting would be stupid as well as sinful: you would forget your own appointment.


**The Day as Appointment, Not as Abstract**

*Li-yawm al-ḥisāb* attaches furniture to a calendar of God. Live toward an appointment and the hours arrange themselves: less *baghy*, more *dhikr*, less mockery, more lowering of the gaze. People who say they believe in the Day but arrange nothing toward it are treating verse 53 as literature. It is a date.


**Promise as the Structure of Hope**

Hope in this religion is not a mood. It is a promise dated to a Day. If the date is real, hope is rational. If it is not, the couches of the previous verses are fiction, and the notables were right to laugh. Verse 53 is therefore ʿaqīdah in one line: you are promised; the Day is the when; this — Gardens, gates, fruit, modest companionship — is the what. Hold the line when the troop of mockers invites you to their census of the “evil.”

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:54

> **This indeed is Our provision which shall never be spent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Provision That Does Not Behave Like Dunya**

*Inna hādhā la-rizqunā mā lahu min nafād* — truly this is Our provision; there is no depletion for it. *Nafād* is running out, being exhausted. Worldly rizq, even Solomon’s, has an end. The Qur’an says:

> *“What is with you runs out, but what is with God remains.”* (Qur’an 16:96)

and of the Garden’s hospitality:

> *“Its food is everlasting, as is its shade.”* (Qur’an 13:35)

**“Our Provision” as a Seal of Source**

*Rizqunā* — Our provision. The same Lord whose treasuries of mercy the notables do not hold (38:9) now names the feast as His. No ninety-nine, no satans diving for pearls, no fear of spending (17:100) applies here. God is not miserly. Man is. The Garden is where God’s manner of giving becomes the climate, and human miserliness has no object.

**Why Inexhaustibility Had to Be Stated**

People might imagine Paradise as a large but finite store, a better Makkah. *Mā lahu min nafād* kills the market-imagination. You cannot hoard what does not thin. You cannot take a brother’s ewe when the flock is infinite. The moral education of the Garden begins in this world when a person already gives as if God’s store does not thin — which is the point of infāq. The miser is training for a world that will not exist. The spender in the right is practicing the climate of verse 54.


**The Economics That Train the Soul**

A provision that never thins cannot be understood by a miser, because the miser’s whole intelligence is built on thinning. Zakāh, charity, feeding, the prohibition of ribā — these are not only social policies. They are rehearsals of verse 54. You behave, a little, as if God’s store does not empty. The Garden then does not feel like a foreign language.

**Our Provision, Not Your Capture**

*Rizqunā* blocks the last ʿizzah: “I earned Paradise.” The open gates are gift, as Solomon’s wind was gift. Works are required; they are not a purchase that empties God. Hold both. Drop either and you become a pretender or a fatalist.


**Never Spent, Therefore Never Worth Stealing**

If the provision of the end does not thin, then taking a brother’s ewe is not only cruel. It is stupid in the long math. You steal what runs out and risk what does not. The possessors of intellect (38:29, 43) can do this arithmetic. The ninety-nine cannot, because their intelligence is the intelligence of piles.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:55

> **This it shall be! Yet for the rebellious there will surely be a most evil return.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Turn in the Text Like a Turn in the Road**

*Hādhā* — this, thus it is — then *wa-inna li-l-ṭāghīna la-sharra maʾāb* — and truly for the rebellious, the overflowing, is an evil return. The *muttaqūn* had *ḥusnā maʾāb*. The *ṭāghūn* have *sharr maʾāb*. The parallelism is the answer to 38:28. God will not make them alike even in the grammar of their ends.

**Who the Ṭāghūn Are**

*Ṭaghā* is to overflow, to exceed the limit, to tyrannize. The sea ṭaghā in Noah’s flood. Pharaoh is the *ṭāghiyah* in other verses. Ibn Kathīr: those who disobey God and oppose the messengers. In this sūrah the type has been built: notables who walk out, generations who deny, Iblīs who will refuse. Rebellion is not teenage mood. It is the overflow of ʿizzah against the Reminder.

**Evil Return as a Place, Not Only a Mood**

*Maʾāb* is a return-place. The rebellious do not vanish. They arrive. The next verse will name the address. For the reciter, the verse is a refusal of the modern hope that the worst people simply “stop” at death. Overflow in this world, if unrepented, arrives as an overflow of harm in the next. The beautiful return and the evil return are both arrivals.


**Overflow Named as a People**

*Al-ṭāghūn* are not “the slightly too lively.” Pharaoh, Iblīs, the notables who would not shrink to One God — overflow is their habit. The verse’s *wa* after the Garden is a hinge of justice. To stop the sūrah at the couches would be to lie about the world God just said was not created in vain.

**Evil Return as a Destination You Practice**

People practice their *maʾāb*. A life of overflow — of appetite, of speech, of power — is already living toward *sharr maʾāb*. The change of house at death is less a surprise than a consistency. That is why tawbah is an emergency change of practice, not a wish about furniture.


**This It Shall Be — Then the Hinge**

*Hādhā* closes the Garden as complete. The *wa* opens the other complete thing. God is not embarrassed to mention both in one breath. A daʿwah that only recites couches produces *fujjār* who think they are *muttaqūn* because they felt warmth. A daʿwah that only recites Hell produces despair. The sūrah refuses both reductions.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:56

> **Hell, wherein they will burn. What an evil resting place!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name After the Description**

*Jahannam* — Hell, Gehenna. *Yaṣlawnahā* — they will roast in it, burn in it as fuel and as sufferers. *Fa-biʾsa al-mihād* — and what an evil resting-place, an evil bed, an evil mattress. The Garden had reclining. Hell has a *mihād* too, a place one lies, and the lying is ruin.

**Rest Named in Order to Be Denied**

*Mihād* is what you spread to rest upon. Calling Hell an evil *mihād* is a terrible irony the Qur’an uses elsewhere (2:206). They wanted a bed. They get a bed that is fire. The human need to rest does not disappear at death. It is either met by couches in 38:51 or mocked by this mattress.

**Burning as the Opposite of Job’s Cool Water**

Job was given *bārid*, cool. The ṭāghūn are given *yaṣlawnahā*. The sūrah’s elements remember their users. Water for the patient servant; fire for the overflowing rebel. This is not because God lacks coolness. Verse 57 will show that Hell also has a cold, and the cold is not mercy. The point is that both climates of Hell are climates of *ṭughyān* returned to its people.


**Hell Spoken in One Breath of Place, Act, and Bed**

Three strokes: the name Jahannam, the verb of burning, the mattress. The Qur’an often refuses to let Hell be only a temperature. It is a dwelling. Humans are creatures of dwelling; they will dwell somewhere. The horror is not annihilation. The horror is a home that hates the homed.

**Biʾsa as a Moral Taste**

The language of *biʾsa* (how evil) is the language of value, not only of pain. Hell is not merely hot. It is a bad place in the sense that a lie is a bad sentence. The reciter is being trained to find ṭughyān *biʾs* before he finds it hot.


**Burning and Lying Down**

The verb of fire and the noun of a bed should not fit. Their fit is the punishment of people who wanted rest without *taqwā*. They lay down on the wrong things in life — on wealth as mattress, on clan as mattress. Hell says: you will have a *mihād*. It will match what you spread for yourselves (see 2:206). Spread *dhikr* now if you want a different bed.


**Resting Place as the Last Irony of ʿIzzah**

They wanted honor that does not bow. They receive a bed that does not rest. *Mihād* in Arabic is what a mother spreads for a child. Hell as *mihād* is the anti-mercy of people who refused mercy’s conditions. The word should end the romance of rebellion. Rebellion is not a standing forever. It is a lying down in a bad place.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:57

> **This it shall be! So let them taste it, a boiling liquid and a cold, murky fluid.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Taste, the Verb Used of Punishment Not Yet Sampled**

Verse 8 said they have not yet tasted My punishment. Verse 57 says *fa-l-yadhūqūhu* — so let them taste it. The delay ends. What they taste is *ḥamīm*, water at the extreme of heat, used across the Qur’an as the drink of the Fire (6:70; 10:4; 37:67; 44:46; 47:15; 56:54; 88:5), poured over heads in 22:19. And *ghassāq*, a cold, dark, foul fluid (cf. 78:25), so cold it cannot be borne, or the ooze of the damned.

**Extremes as the Climate of Overflow**

Ibn Kathīr: Hell is a place of extremes; every time one torture is escaped, its opposite is met. The rebellious overflowed limits. Their drink overflows limits in both directions, too hot, too cold. The Garden’s drink was simply drink, abundant, without idle talk. Hell’s drink is pedagogy of the body: you would not stay within the riverbanks of the law; now the river is scald and ice.

**Murk Against the Clear Spring**

Job’s water was cool and clean enough to wash and to drink. *Ghassāq* is murky, stained. Purity of water followed purity of turning. Filth of drink follows filth of *ṭughyān*. The reciter who treats small corruptions of the tongue and the market as flavorless should notice that the Qur’an gives moral filth a taste.


**Hot and Cold After a Life of Avoiding the Middle**

The straight path of the Fātiḥah is a middle. The fujjār burst the banks. Their drink is banks burst: ḥamīm and ghassāq. Ibn Kathīr’s remark that one flees one torture into its opposite is the psychology of people who could not stay within a limit. They still cannot. The climate has become their habit made external.

**Taste as the End of Debate**

Verse 8 used taste as what they had not yet met. Here taste is command: *fal-yadhūqūhu*. Argument is over. The great tiding (38:67) has become tongue and throat. This is why the warning is a mercy. A person who “tastes” the warning now, in fear and tawbah, is using the safer of the two tastings.


**Two Drinks, One Refusal of the Spring**

Job drank cool water because he called and stamped. The ṭāghūn drink ḥamīm and ghassāq because they asked, in verse 16, to hasten a share they did not believe in. The element of drink runs through the sūrah as a test of how you ask. Ask as Job. Do not ask as the mockers. Do not refuse to ask at all.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:58

> **coupled with other [punishments] similar in kind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No Single Form, No Getting Used To**

*Wa-ākharu min shaklihi azwāj* — and other of its shape, in pairs, in kinds. The punishments come as *azwāj*, couples, sorts. Ibn Kathīr’s reading of 55–58 is that the pairing of hot and cold is the model: similar in being unbearable, different in quality, so that the self cannot adapt. Adaptation is a mercy of the dunya; even pain can dull. The Fire’s “others of like shape” refuse that mercy.

**Pairs as a Dark Mirror of the Garden’s Pairs**

The Garden had pairs too: fruit and drink, reclining and calling, spouses of modest gaze. Hell has pairs of another order. Creation’s pairing (the world was made in pairs in other verses) is not itself evil. What you chose as your pair in the world — the *muttaqūn* or the *fujjār* — decides which pairing you inherit.

**The End of Bargaining**

A person in pain in this world bargains: let it be this rather than that. Verse 58 says the “that” is also prepared, of the same shape of misery. There is no negotiation into a milder department by the skill of complaint. The only negotiation was earlier, in the time of *dhikr*, when David fell down and Job stamped the ground. After arrival, there are only kinds.


**Kinds, So That No One Hopes in Monotony**

Human pain in the dunya sometimes becomes bearable by sameness. The verse says Hell’s “others” are of the same *shakl* — shape of being unbearable — in pairs. Hope that “I will get used to it” is part of what is stripped. The only getting-used-to offered in this religion is getting used to *dhikr* until the Garden’s abundance feels like the right climate.


**Similarity Without Relief**

*Min shaklihi* means you will not find a department of Hell that is “not really like Hell.” The kinds differ as hot and cold differ; they do not differ as pain and ease differ. This kills the gambler’s hope of a better corner. The better corner was verse 50, and its gate was taqwā.


**Pairs That Teach What Pairing Is For**

God made the world in pairs as a mercy of complementarity. Hell’s *azwāj* of punishments are complementarity without mercy: each kind completing the other so that no escape-clause remains. Learn pairing in the world as the Garden uses it — fruit and drink, rest and call, two that help — not as overflow uses it, two that corner.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:59

> **This troop rushing in with you—no greeting for them! They will surely burn in the Fire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Arrival as a Crowd, Not as a Solitary Tragic Hero**

*Hādhā fawjun muqtaḥimun maʿakum* — this is a troop plunging, rushing in, with you. Hell receives factions, as the world produced *aḥzāb*. The notables who said “go, be steadfast to your gods” (38:6) liked troops. They get a troop still, and the troop is *muqtaḥim*, forcing itself in, crowding through a gate that is not the open gate of Eden.

**No Greeting**

*Lā marḥaban bihim* — no welcome for them, no “marḥabā.” The Garden’s keepers say peace (39:73). The Fire’s climate is the refusal of greeting. In 7:38 every community that enters curses its sister. The social pleasure of being a *fawj* in kufr — we are many, we walked out together — is stripped at the door. Many, and unwelcome.

**They Will Burn**

*Innahum ṣālū al-nār* — they are burners in the Fire, surely. The verse may be spoken by the keepers, or by a group already inside about a group arriving, or as God’s caption. In all cases the troop’s togetherness does not dilute the burning. 38:14 said each denied, so retribution came due. 38:59 says the due is not commuted for arriving in company.

The practical warning is for every us-versus-him piety of the wrong kind. If your group’s warmth depends on refusing the Reminder, you are rehearsing *lā marḥaban bihim*. Choose a troop whose greeting in the next world is *salām*.


**The Gate of the Troop**

*Muqtaḥim* is to throw oneself in, to crowd a gap. They entered sin as a crowd; they enter the Fire as a crowd. The greeting that would have made them a community — *salām* — is withheld. A group without peace is not a community. It is a *fawj*. Check whether your group can greet with peace or only with slogans of verse 6.

**No Marḥabā as the End of Brand**

Brands welcome their own. Hell’s troop discovers that the brand does not own the door. The keepers of the Fire are not a club committee. If you chose your troop to be welcomed by men, you may be unwelcomed by the only door that remains.


**Rushing In: the Habit of Not Stopping**

*Iqtiḥām* is the motion of people who never practiced stopping at a limit. They rushed into the walkout (38:6), rushed into mockery, rushed into the cry that had no escape. The troop is the social form of haste. Taqwā is the social form of stopping. Choose which group-motion you train in a family and a city.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:60

> **They will say, “Nay, but no greeting for you! You have sent it forth for us. What an evil dwelling place!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Troop Answers Its Leaders**

*Qālū bal antum lā marḥaban bikum* — they say: nay, you — no welcome for you. The arriving or the following group throws the greeting-refusal back. *Antum qaddamtumūhu lanā* — you sent this ahead for us, you prepared it for us, you brought us to this. *Fa-biʾsa al-qarār* — what an evil dwelling, what an evil settlement.

The *qarār* they wanted was the Garden’s ʿAdn, or at least the world’s palaces. They get a settlement that is evil, and they know whom to blame second: the ones who went before in misguidance.

**Leadership’s Afterlife**

The Qur’an is consistent: followers will accuse leaders.

> *“Our Lord, it was they who led us astray, so give them a double punishment in the Fire.”* (Qur’an 7:38)

The notables of verse 6 told people to be steadfast to gods. Verse 60 is those people, in the only meeting that cannot be adjourned, saying: you sent this in advance for us. Worldly leadership that sells a religion of walkout will be paid in the coin of this sentence.

**Evil Dwelling as a Confession**

They do not, in this verse, deny the evil of the place. The conjecture that creation was vain (38:27) is finished. They know a *qarār* can be *biʾs*. Knowledge without the time of escape (38:3) is the Hell of the mind as well as of the skin. The reciter should prefer to say “what an evil dwelling” about sin now, while changing house, rather than about Jahannam later, when the house will not change.


**You Sent It Ahead**

*Qaddamtumūhu lanā* is among the Qur’an’s most precise political sentences. Leaders send ahead a climate: laws, jokes, markets, fatwas of ease, cultures of mockery. Followers arrive into a Fire that feels “prepared.” The preparation was sunnah of evil. The Prophet ﷺ said that whoever starts a bad sunnah bears its burden and the burden of those who act on it, without theirs diminishing (Muslim 1017). Verse 60 is that ḥadīth as dialogue.

**Qarār: They Wanted to Settle**

Humans want a *qarār*, a place to settle. They settled in the wrong creed. The evil settlement is the truth of that choice, not an arbitrary hotel assignment. Change settlement now: from *ʿizzah* to *ʿubūdiyyah*.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:61

> **They will say, “O Lord! Whosoever has sent this forth for us, increase him twofold in punishment in the Fire.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prayer in Hell That Is Not Repentance**

They now address the Lord, as they did in mockery in 38:16, but the request has changed from “hasten our share” to “double his punishment.” *Man qaddama lanā hādhā fa-zidhu ʿadhāban ḍiʿfan fī al-nār*. 7:38 gives God’s reply in a similar scene: for each it is doubled, but you do not know. 33:67–68 is the same request against leaders. 29:13: they will bear their own loads and others’ loads with their own. The followers’ punishment is not cancelled by the leaders’ extra.

**Double for Astray and for Leading Astray**

The fiqh of the request, even if God is the one who knows how it is applied, matches a moral intuition: to err is one crime; to organize error as a party line is two. The *malaʾ* who manufacture unbelief (38:6) are in the sights of this verse. So is every later caller to *ṭughyān* — the one who makes the lie fluent, who *ʿazza fī al-khiṭāb* (38:23) on behalf of the ninety-nine against God.

**The Follower Is Not Thereby Innocent**

The verse records their prayer; it does not grant them the status of victims only. They chose to go (38:6). They asked for the share in taunt (38:16). They denied messengers (38:14). Blaming the one who “sent it forth” is true as far as extra crime goes, and false as an acquittal. The Prophet ﷺ warned, in the meaning of the ḥadīth of those who start a bad sunnah, that they carry its burden and the burden of those who act on it, without diminishing the actors’ burdens (Muslim 1017). Verse 61 is that ḥadīth’s eschatology.


**Double, and Still Not Empty of Your Own**

The request for twofold punishment is morally intelligible and spiritually insufficient. God may well double the leader’s load. 7:38 says each is doubled and you do not know. The follower who recites this as his hope in the Fire has still not done tawbah; he has only done blame. The time to refuse the leader is before the troop plunges.

**Who “Sends Forth” in Ordinary Life**

A parent who teaches contempt of people, a celebrity who makes indecency stylish, a scholar who sells ʿizzah as religion — these *qaddama*. The verse should make such people tremble more than it makes their audiences enjoy imagining doubles. If you have sent even a small evil ahead of others, retrieve it with a good sunnah while retrieval exists.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:62

> **And they will say, “How is that we do not see men here whom we had counted among the evil?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Missing Faces**

*Wa-qālū mā lanā lā narā rijālan kunnā naʿudduhum min al-ashrār* — and they say: what is the matter with us that we do not see men we used to count among the evil? The damned have a census in their heads. Certain people “must” be here: the poor believers, the Prophet’s weak companions, the ones they mocked as sorcerer’s followers, the ones they swore God would never have mercy on.

7:49 turns that census around when the people of the heights say:

> *“Are these the ones you swore God would never reach with mercy?”*

The shock of verse 62 is the empty place in Hell where the mocked should be, in their arithmetic, and are not.

**Evil as a Social Verdict**

*Naʿudduhum min al-ashrār* — we used to count them, number them, among the wicked. Evil was a count they performed. They had the ledger. The ledger was pride’s ledger: whoever did not share our gods and our *ʿizzah* was *sharr*. The Day of Reckoning uses another ledger. The missing men are missing because they were of the *muttaqīn*, whose gates were open (38:50).

**The Persistence of the Old Eyes**

That they still ask the question in the Fire, as the source commentary notes, means punishment has not yet given them true sight. They understand that they burn. They do not yet understand the map of who burns. Hell is not a seminar that automatically graduates its inmates in tawḥīd. Some veils burn; some judgments cling. The reciter is warned to correct the census now. Whom do you count as *ashrār* because they are weak, or poor, or not of your party, or too religious for your taste? Check the count before the count is checked.


**The Census of Spite**

Hell’s inmates are still counting. Their religion in the world was a count of whom they despised. That count continues as a question: why are the despised not here? 7:49 is the Divine mockery of that census. The weak Muslims of Makkah — Bilāl, the poor, the young — were *ashrār* in the notables’ ledger. The ledger was the crime.

**Not Seeing as a Continuation of Not Seeing**

They did not see those men correctly in the world. They do not see them in the Fire because they are not there. Blindness had two stages; the second only reveals the first. Ask now: whom do I not see as they are?

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:63

> **Did we take them in mockery? Or have our eyes swerved away from them?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Explanations for an Absence**

*Attakhadhnāhum sikhriyyan am zāghat ʿanhumu al-abṣār* — did we take them as mockery, as a joke? Or have the eyes swerved, slipped, from them? Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī note that the first clause can also be read as a statement: we did take them in mockery. Either they ask whether their own ridicule was the error, or they wonder whether the missing people are here but invisible because their sight is crooked.

**Mockery as a Makkan Craft**

The sūrah opened with marveling and labels: lying sorcerer, astounding thing, invention. Mockery is the cheap form of *khiṭāb* that overpowers (38:23) without argument. The Prophet ﷺ was told to bear patiently what they say (38:17). Verse 63 is those sayings returning as a question the speakers cannot answer well. If we mocked them, and they are not here, then mockery was not knowledge. It was *ẓann*.

**Eyes That Swerve**

*Zāghat al-abṣār* — the eyes deviate. Abraham’s house had *abṣār* as a praise (38:45). These have *abṣār* that swerve. The same organ, two uses. In the Fire they consider that their seeing might be the problem. That consideration, had it come in Makkah, would have been tawbah. Coming now, it is only anatomy of ruin.

For a culture of comedy that lives on taking the pious, the poor, and the awkward as *sikhriyy*, the verse is a freeze-frame. You may discover, in a place you do not wish to be, that the people you used for laughter were the people of open gates, and that your eyes had been swerving for years. Lower the gaze from mockery as you lower it from indecency. Both are unshortened glances at what is not yours to consume.


**Mockery as a Form of ʿIzzah**

To take someone as *sikhriyy* is to use him to feel higher. It is verse 2 as entertainment. The Prophet ﷺ was mocked; the Qur’an forbids mocking people lest they be better than you (49:11). Verse 63 is 49:11 after the door has shut. The question “did we?” is the beginning of knowledge and the end of its usefulness.

**Swerving Eyes**

*Zaygh* of the eyes is cousin to *zaygh* of the hearts (3:7–8). They ask whether their eyes swerved from the missing men — perhaps those men are here and we just cannot see them. Even their alternative explanation is still about optics, not about repentance. Train the eye now: look at the weak as possible people of open gates.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:64

> **Surely that is true: the disputing of the people of the Fire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Seals the Dialogue as Real**

*Inna dhālika la-ḥaqqun takhāṣumu ahli al-nār* — truly that is true, the disputing of the people of the Fire. The *khaṣm* that scaled David’s sanctuary (38:21) was a dispute that could still end in justice and kneeling. This *takhāṣum* is dispute without a court that will reverse the house. God affirms it as *ḥaqq*, real, so that no listener may treat the preceding dialogue as parable only. The quarrel of Hell is part of the news of the Unseen that the Prophet ﷺ is about to say he would not know except by revelation (38:69–70).

**What They Dispute**

They dispute with one another about who sent the punishment forth, about the missing “evil” men, about mockery and sight. Other passages show them speaking to the people of the Garden, asking for water or light (7:50; 57:13), and being refused because those blessings are forbidden to them. They do not, this verse implies, win a case. They only produce *takhāṣum*. Argument, which they refused to use honestly in the world, becomes their climate without fruit.

**A Warning to Lovers of Dispute**

Some temperaments enjoy *khiṣām*. The Qur’an elsewhere says that man is the most disputatious of things (18:54). This verse shows the end of disputatiousness without taqwā: an eternal file that never closes. Learn David’s dispute — truth, no excess, a level path, then falling down if you were wrong. Avoid the Fire’s dispute — blame, census of enemies, mockery remembered too late. The sūrah is about to leave Hell’s quarrel for the Highest Assembly’s. Two assemblies, two kinds of speech.


**True Dispute Versus Blessed Dispute**

David’s *khaṣm* could end in *ḥaqq* and a falling down. The Fire’s *takhāṣum* is *ḥaqq* as fact and barren as fruit. The Qur’an does not condemn all argument; it condemns argument that cannot repent. A scholarly disagreement for truth is closer to David. A comment-thread of blame is closer to 38:64. Leave a dispute when it has become a home rather than a path.


**God Calls Their Quarrel True So You Will Not Call It Legend**

The Makkans called the Reminder invention. God calls the Fire’s dispute *ḥaqq*. That inversion is the last word on their literary criticism. If this quarrel is true, then the warner is not a poet of threats. Arrange your disputes in the world so they can end like David’s, in kneeling, not like this, in a sealed file.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:65

> **Say, “I am only a warner, and there is no god but God, the One, the Paramount.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet Put Back Within His Office**

*Qul innamā anā mundhir* — say: I am only a warner. After Gardens, Fire, and the quarrel of the damned, the Messenger is not allowed to become a third deity or a manager of outcomes. *Innamā* restricts. His function is *indhār*, warning, the same office the Makkans marveled at (38:4). Other verses: he is naught but a warner (11:12; 13:7; 34:46; 35:23; 79:45). He does not own the treasuries (38:9). He does not make the single cry (38:15). He delivers.

**The Creed in the Mouth of the Warner**

*Wa-mā min ilāhin illā Allāhu al-wāḥidu al-qahhār* — and there is no god but God, the One, the Paramount. The sentence they found *ʿujāb* (38:5) is now given as the content of the warning. *Al-Wāḥid* answers “has he made the gods one God?” *Al-Qahhār*, the Paramount, the Overwhelming, from the same root as *al-Qāhir*, always in the Qur’an paired with *al-Wāḥid* (12:39; 13:16; 14:48; 39:4; 40:16). Oneness is not smallness. The One is the One who subdues.

**Why Paramount After the Fire’s Dispute**

People in Hell still talk as if their factions matter. *Al-Qahhār* is the Name that ends factions. There is no god to appeal to against God, no second throne, no confederacy that can outvote the cry. The warner’s job is to say this while the tasting has not yet begun (38:8). Once the Paramount is tasted as Paramount, the warning has become the event.

For the reciter, “I am only a warner” is also a limit on daʿwah-ego. You are not *al-Qahhār*. You do not open the gates of Eden or stoke Jahannam. You say: no god but the One, the Paramount. Then you bear patiently what they say (38:17).


**Only a Warner, After So Much Unseen**

The sūrah has just given furniture of Paradise and temperatures of Hell. The speaker in Makkah must not be mistaken for the interior decorator of either house. *Innamā anā mundhir* is a wall around tawḥīd. Christians who lifted ʿĪsā beyond warning, and any Muslim who lifts a shaykh or a scholar into a mini-qahhār, are being addressed.

**The One, the Paramount — Why Both**

Oneness without qahr could be imagined as a lonely god among options. Qahr without oneness could be a tyrant among tyrants. Together they are: there is no other, and there is no resistance that holds. The notables’ walkout does not dent *al-Qahhār*. It only sorts them into the troop of 38:59.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:66

> **Lord of the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them, the Mighty, the Forgiving.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One Identified by Kingdom and by Names**

*Rabbu al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-mā baynahumā* — Lord of the heavens and the earth and what is between them. Verse 10 asked if they had sovereignty over that interval. Verse 66 answers who does. *Rabb* is the owner who fosters. The interval that was not created in vain (38:27) has a Lord.

**The Mighty and the Forgiving**

*Al-ʿAzīz al-Ghafūr*. Verse 9 had *al-ʿAzīz al-Wahhāb*. Here might is paired with forgiveness, as in 39:5, 40:42, 67:2. Al-Ālūsī: threat to the disbelievers and promise to the believers, akin to the frequent pair Mighty and Merciful. Might without forgiveness would make David’s kneeling useless and Job’s cry pointless. Forgiveness without might would make the Fire a rumor. The Names together are the whole sūrah: Iblīs will meet the Mighty; David has met the Forgiving; Quraysh are offered both while the warner warns.

**What Is Between Them**

The phrase gathers winds, birds, horses, satans, springs, human speech, the Highest Assembly about to be mentioned. Nothing in the interval is ownerless. Therefore nothing in the interval is a safe idol. The Lord of what is between is the only one to warn toward, and the only one who can cover what happened in the interval when a servant turns.


**Lord of the Interval**

“What is between them” is where we actually live — air, history, speech, pain. A god of the sky who does not own the interval would be a holiday god. *Rabb* of the interval is the God of Job’s sores and Solomon’s wind. Worship is therefore not escape from the between. It is addressing the Lord of the between while you are in it.

**Mighty, Forgiving — the Pair David Needed**

If God were only ʿAzīz, David’s falling would be theater before a closed door. If only Ghafūr, Iblīs’s “I am better” would have no court. The pair is why a sinner should not despair and why a notable should not swagger. Live between the two Names: fear the Mighty, ask the Forgiving, do not use either Name to cancel the other.


**Heavens, Earth, Between — No Cracks for a Partner**

Idolatry needs a crack: a zone God does not run. This verse names all three zones. Partners have nowhere to sit. The Mighty closes the crack of force; the Forgiving closes the crack of despair. Worship in the between with both Names on the tongue, as the Prophet ﷺ often joined majesty and mercy in one breath of duʿāʾ.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:67

> **Say, “It is a great tiding.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Message Compressed to a Nabaʾ**

*Qul huwa nabaʾun ʿaẓīm* — say: it is a great tiding, a great report. *Nabaʾ* was used of the dispute that came to the Prophet about David (38:21). Now the whole matter — Oneness, warning, Garden, Fire, the coming story of Adam — is a *nabaʾ ʿaẓīm*. Al-Ālūsī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī: the Qur’an, or the Resurrection as in 78:1, or the message of tawḥīd issuing from God. The greatness is not rhetorical inflation. A tiding is great when what it reports changes every smaller tiding.

**Greatness Against Their “Astounding”**

They said of tawḥīd: *shayʾun ʿujāb*, an astounding thing. God says of the message: *nabaʾun ʿaẓīm*, a great report. Two kinds of astonishment: theirs, which is scandal; His, which is rank. The warner is told to name the rank even when the town names the scandal.

**How to Carry a Great Tiding**

If it is great, it is not a hobby, not a cultural ornament, not a debate-club topic. It is news. News is delivered. News is not improved by the messenger’s pretension (38:86). The next verse will say they turn away from it. The messenger’s job is not to shrink the tiding until they do not turn. It is to keep saying it is great.


**Nabaʾ ʿAẓīm as a Test of Speech**

When people reduce the religion to culture, genealogy, or politics, they have denied the adjective *ʿaẓīm*. A great tiding makes small talk small. It also makes the warner’s voice tremble rather than preen. If you can advertise the Day as you advertise a product, you have not felt verse 67. Say it as news. News does not need your branding.


**Greatness Measured by What It Costs to Deny**

A tiding is great if denying it ruins a world. Tawḥīd, the Day, this Book: deny them and you must treat creation as *bāṭil* (38:27). That cost is the proof of greatness, not the volume of the preacher. Speak of it in a size that matches the cost.


**Say It: the Command to Name the Size**

*Qul* — say. The Prophet ﷺ is not allowed to shrink the tiding to make the *muʿriḍūn* more comfortable. Size is part of honesty. A doctor who names a cancer a cold is not kind. The great tiding includes the Fire’s dispute and the Garden’s gates. Say both. That is how a warning stays *mubīn*.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:68

> **from which you turn away.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Tragedy in a Relative Clause**

*Antum ʿanhu muʿriḍūn* — you are, from it, turning aside. The great tiding is not failed as tiding. It is refused as orientation. *Iʿrāḍ* is to turn the face, to put the thing beside you rather than before you. The notables’ “go” (38:6) is iʿrāḍ as group policy.

**Turning Away as the Human Contribution**

God sent a blessed Book for tadabbur (38:29). The mountains did not turn away from David’s tasbīḥ. Birds gathered. Humans *muʿriḍūn*. The sūrah’s anthropology is here: the only creature being asked to face the great tiding is the one most able to turn the face.

**The Cure Is the Opposite Verb**

David, Solomon, Job: *awwāb*, turning toward. Quraysh: *muʿriḍūn*, turning away. The whole moral map of Ṣād is two turnings. The reciter checks his face. When the great tiding appears — in a verse, in a funeral, in a loss, in a dawn — does he *anāba* or *aʿraḍa*? There is no third direction for long.


**Turning Aside as a Posture of the Neck**

*Iʿrāḍ* is physical in Arabic: the side of the face. Hearts follow necks. A person can sit in a recitation and be *muʿriḍ* because the neck of attention is turned toward a plan, a resentment, a phone. The verse is not only about pagans of the seventh century. It is about the direction of the face when the great tiding is in the room. Turn it back. That turning back is *awbah* in the smallest coin.


**The Grammar of You**

*Antum ʿanhu muʿriḍūn* — you, from it, turning. The pronoun is second person. The sūrah does not only discuss “them.” At the end it faces the seated listener. You. The great tiding is in the room. The neck turns or it does not. This is the most intimate verse of Ṣād, and among the shortest, because a turned neck does not need a long description. It needs to be named.


**Turning Away While Remaining in Town**

Iʿrāḍ does not require leaving Makkah. It is possible in the same market, the same family, the same prayer-space of later centuries. The body stays; the face of the heart leaves. The sūrah’s last movement will offer the story of Adam as one more reason to turn the face back. If you are still reading, you are being given the chance the notables walked out of.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:69

> **I have no knowledge of the Highest Assembly, when they dispute.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Confession That Authenticates the Coming Story**

*Mā kāna liya min ʿilmin bi-l-malaʾi al-aʿlā idh yakhtaṣimūn* — I had no knowledge of the Highest Assembly when they disputed. The *malaʾ aʿlā* are the angels, the assembly on high, as Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī say. Their *khiṣām*, in the reading of most on this verse, is the exchange in 2:30:

> *“Will You place therein one who will work corruption and shed blood, while we hymn Your praise and call You holy?”*

The Prophet ﷺ is instructed to say that he would not know that dispute except by revelation. Verses 71–85 are therefore not his guess, not an old tale he improved. They are news from the only One who was there.

**The Ḥadīth of the Highest Assembly**

Al-Qurṭubī and others also relate the ḥadīth in which the Prophet ﷺ said that his Lord came to him in the most beautiful form and asked what the Highest Assembly were disputing about, and he said he did not know, until God placed His palm between his shoulders and he felt its coolness, and all things were disclosed, and he answered that they were disputing about expiations — going on foot to the congregations, sitting in the mosque after prayer, completing wudūʾ in difficulty — and about ranks, and he then asked God for good deeds, leaving bad deeds, love of the poor, and to be taken without a trial if a trial were decreed, and for God’s love, the love of those who love Him, and love of deeds that bring near to His love. The ḥadīth is of the reports of the night-vision, taken by the imams with their discussions of grading. It does not cancel the tafsīr that 38:69 points to the Adam-dispute. It confirms the Prophet’s *mā kāna liya min ʿilm* until God taught him.

**Dispute on High and Dispute in Hell**

Verse 64: the disputing of the people of the Fire is true. Verse 69: the Highest Assembly also disputed, in a way proper to them — questions of wisdom, not of rebellion (except Iblīs, who was among them and not of their obedience). Two *takhāṣum*s. One is kufr’s quarrel. One is the angels’ wonder at a vicegerent who will shed blood. The Prophet ﷺ knows neither by himself. Revelation is the only bridge. This cuts off both the soothsayer and the philosopher who would speak of heaven’s council from a private ladder (38:10).

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:70

> **Naught is revealed unto me but that I am only a clear warner.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Revelation’s Content Restricted to the Office**

*In yūḥā ilayya illā annamā anā nadhīrun mubīn* — it is not revealed to me except that I am only a clear warner. The exception can mean: the sum of what is revealed is that my role is clear warning; or: I receive revelation only in my capacity as a clear warner, not as a knower of the Unseen by nature. Both serve the polemic. He has just denied independent knowledge of the Highest Assembly. He now denies that revelation makes him other than *nadhīr mubīn*.

**Clear, Not Cloudy**

*Mubīn* is that which makes plain, or that which is itself plain. The notables called the message invention and sorcery — unclear origins, hidden craft. A *nadhīr mubīn* is the opposite: a warning whose source is named (the One, the Paramount) and whose content is named (Garden, Fire, tawḥīd, the story of Adam next). Clarity is part of mercy. People who perish after a clear warner perish from iʿrāḍ (38:68), not from fog.

**The Last Limit Before the First Story**

The sūrah is about to tell the making of the human from clay, the breathing of the Spirit, the prostration, the arrogance. Before that primordial narrative, the human who tells it in Makkah must be known as a warner only. Otherwise the story of Adam’s honor would be mistaken for the story of Muhammad’s self-exaltation. He is a descendant of that honored clay. He is not the Lord who said “I am creating.” Verse 70 is humility as method. Then verse 71 can be heard as God’s speech, not as the orphan’s.


**Revelation Does Not Make a Demigod**

People who receive a little knowledge often become *mutakallifūn* (38:86). The Prophet ﷺ receives the highest knowledge and is told to say: what is revealed to me is that I am a clear warner. The more the unseen is opened, the tighter the office. That inverse is a sign of authenticity. Pretenders swell with scraps. Messengers shrink into *indhār*.

**Clarity as Kindness**

A warning that is not *mubīn* is a cruelty, because the warned can say they did not understand. God will not let that be said of this message. If someone perishes, it is after plainness. That raises the follower’s responsibility and lowers his excuses.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:71

> **[Remember] when thy Lord said unto the angels, “Behold! I am creating a human being from clay.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Adam-Narrative in the Muṣḥaf, Perhaps the First in Time**

Verses 71–85 are the last account of Adam’s creation in the written order of the Qur’an, and, as the source notes, likely among the first in revelation’s chronology. The other accounts are 2:30–39; 7:11–25; 15:28–43; 17:61–65; 20:115–24. Each telling chooses a face of the event. Ṣād’s face is arrogance versus command, fire versus clay, respite versus sincerity — because this sūrah opened with *ʿizzah* and *shiqāq*.

**“Thy Lord Said” as the Source of the Nabaʾ**

*Idh qāla rabbuka li-l-malāʾikah* — when your Lord said to the angels. The Prophet who has no knowledge of the Highest Assembly (38:69) now recites what the Lord said there. *Rabbuka*, your Lord, ties the Makkan warner to the primordial speaker. The God of the interval of heaven and earth (38:66) is the God of the hour before Adam walked.

**A Human from Clay**

*Innī khāliqun basharan min ṭīn* — I am creating a human, a *bashar*, from clay. 15:28 adds dried clay of molded mud. 2:30 says: I am placing a vicegerent upon the earth. Ṣād chooses *bashar* and *ṭīn*: skin, surface, the wet earth. The honor that will come in verse 72 is therefore an honor given to mud, not to a creature who was already luminous. Iblīs will not forgive this.

**Why the Angels Are Told Before the Making**

God does not need consultants. He tells the angels because the making of the human is a public act in the unseen, a teaching of the Highest Assembly. They will be commanded to prostrate. They must know what they prostrate toward: a creation from clay, not a second god. The notables of Makkah refused a warner from among themselves because he was too much *bashar*. Verse 71 says that *bashar* from *ṭīn* is God’s project. To scorn the human form of the messenger is to scorn the material God announced to the angels.

Clay is also a moral reminder. David’s vicegerency (38:26) is clay in office. Solomon’s throne can bear a corpse (38:34) because the king is clay. Job’s sores are clay in pain. The one who remembers he is *ṭīn* can be *awwāb*. The one who forgets it says “I am better.”


**Clay Announced in the Presence of Light**

The angels are creatures of light in the famous ḥadīth of Muslim: angels created from light, jinn from smokeless fire, Adam from what was described to you (Muslim 2996). God announces clay in that company on purpose. The honor of the human will not be that he outshines angels by matter. It will be the Spirit, the vicegerency, the knowledge of names in other tellings. Quraysh’s scorn of a human warner is already answered: God was not ashamed of clay in front of the Highest Assembly. Why are you ashamed of a man from among yourselves?

**Creating, Not Consulting**

*Innī khāliq* — I am creating. The angels’ later question in 2:30 is not a vote. The project is God’s. Human worth is not a human invention and cannot be a human veto. Iblīs will try to veto. The sentence of verse 71 is the veto of the veto.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:72

> **When I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My Spirit, fall down before him prostrating.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Completions: Form and Breath**

*Fa-idhā sawwaytuhu* — when I have proportioned him, made him even, balanced his form. *Wa-nafakhtu fīhi min rūḥī* — and breathed into him of My Spirit. *Fa-qaʿū lahu sājidīn* — then fall down to him, prostrating. 15:29 is nearly identical. 32:9 tells the same in the third person. Al-Rāzī notes that the human is complete only with both: body formed, spirit breathed. A body without breath is the *jasad* on a throne. A spirit without a proportioned body is not the *bashar* of this story.

**“My Spirit” and the Guard of Tawḥīd**

Most commentators, jealous for God’s transcendence, read *min rūḥī* in a way that does not make a piece of the Divine Essence enter clay. Al-Ṭabarī glosses with power. Ibn Kathīr and al-Jalālayn: God ascribes the spirit to Himself as honor, as “My House” and “My she-camel” are ascriptions of honor. Al-Qurṭubī: the spirit that I own, that no one else owns. Al-Rāzī: the soul is a noble, heavenly, holy substance, yet created, subtle, luminous; the human is body and soul. Some Sufi readers, including Rūzbihān, hear a clothing of the clay in the lights of God’s attributes, a life through His life, while still not making the servant the Lord. The command to prostrate is, in that hearing, intelligible: the angels bow to a locus of manifestation, not to mud as mud.

The reciter need not settle the kalam of the spirit in order to take the verse’s work. Something in the human is so honored by ascription to God that the angels are commanded to fall. That honor is why murder is grave, why mockery of the weak believer is grave, why Iblīs’s refusal is not a small etiquette.

**Prostration of Honor, Not of Worship**

The angels’ sujūd is, by the consensus of the commentators in meaning, a prostration of greeting and obedience to God, not ʿibādah of Adam. Worship is God’s alone, as 38:65 just restated. God can command a bow toward a creature as He commanded the children of Israel to enter the gate bowing. The direction of the body is Adam; the worship is God. Iblīs will confuse this on purpose, as if God had asked him to take a rival.

**Fall Down: The Verb David Also Lived**

*Qaʿū*, fall. David *kharra rākiʿan* (38:24). The good of this sūrah go down. The angels go down on command. Iblīs will stay up. The spiritual physics of Ṣād is gravity toward God. Pride is the attempt to reverse gravity.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:73

> **Then the angels prostrated, all of them together.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Unanimity in the Highest Assembly**

*Fa-sajada al-malāʾikatu kulluhum ajmaʿūn* — then the angels prostrated, all of them, all together. Two emphases of totality: *kulluhum* and *ajmaʿūn*. There is no party among the angels, no *shiqāq*, no notables who walk out. The Highest Assembly, when the command is clear, is not the *malaʾ* of Makkah.

**Obedience Without the Clay-Argument**

They do not say “he is from ṭīn.” They have already, in 2:30, asked about corruption and blood. Once the command to prostrate is given, the question is over. This is ʿilm that has entered ʿamal. The kernel people of verse 29 are to learn: tadabbur is not an excuse to delay sujūd once the command is clear. The angels contemplated, in their way, then fell.

**A Picture of a Healthy Community**

*Kulluhum ajmaʿūn* is what Quraysh are not, and what the ummah is asked to be in the ṣalāh: bodies going down together because God said so, not because the notable said “go.” The angels’ unison judges every congregation that cannot bow until a faction gives permission.


**Together as the Definition of Angelic Intelligence**

Intelligence in Iblīs will mean comparison of elements. Intelligence in the angels here means unison in obedience. The sūrah has praised *ulū al-albāb*. The Highest Assembly shows that a kernel of intellect, at its highest, does not delay sujūd. Some cleverness is only a more articulate *istakbara*. Measure your intellect by whether it can fall with others when God says fall.


**All, Together — Against the Romance of the Lone Rebel**

Culture often loves the one who will not bow with the rest. Verse 73 loves the rest who will bow. Iblīs will be the lone rebel in the next verse, and he will not be a hero. If your instinct is always to stand when a congregation falls in obedience to a clear command, inspect the instinct. It may be intelligence. It may be 38:74 rehearsing.


**The Sound of Unanimous Falling**

The verse is almost only a verb and two words of totality. That brevity is the point. When God has spoken, the Highest Assembly does not need a paragraph. Our need for long excuses before a clear sujūd is already closer to the next verse than to this one. Practice unison in the lawful: the row of prayer, the lowering together at a recitation-sajdah such as 38:24. The body learns what the angels already knew.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:74

> **Not so Iblīs. He waxed arrogant, and was among the disbelievers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception Named**

*Illā Iblīs* — except Iblīs. The exception is not from the species of angels in every scholar’s view; many say he was of the jinn, present in the assembly (18:50). The grammatical exception is from those who prostrated. He was there. He did not go down.

**Arrogance as the Inner Act**

*Istakbara* — he waxed arrogant, he sought greatness, he considered himself too great. In 7:13 God tells him: it is not for you to wax arrogant here; go forth, you are of the humbled. *Istikbār* is the verbal match of the Makkans’ *ʿizzah* (38:2). The sūrah has been waiting to show the first instance of the disease it diagnosed in the town.

**“And Was of the Disbelievers”**

*Wa-kāna min al-kāfirīn* — and he was of those who cover the truth. *Kāna* may mean he became of them by this act, or that he was, in God’s knowledge, of them, and this act displayed it. The doctrinal point is that arrogance before a clear command is not a small character flaw. It is kufr’s door. The ḥadīth of the mustard seed of kibr (Muslim 91) belongs here. Iblīs does not “also” disbelieve. His arrogance is the disbelief: he refuses the truth of God’s ranking.

**The First Schism**

The angels were *ajmaʿūn*. Iblīs makes *shiqāq*. A single will against the command splits the assembly. All later parties of verse 13 are children of this split in meaning: denial of what God sent, elevation of the self’s origin-story. Quraysh saying “we did not hear this in the latter creed” is a pale echo of Iblīs saying, in the next verses, “I am better.”


**Kufr Named at the First Refusal**

The verse does not wait for Iblīs’s argument of fire and clay. It names kufr at *istakbara*. The argument in 38:76 is a costume. The deed is already unbelief because the command was already clear. Many of our arguments — after we have refused a known duty — are costumes. Stop the costume by going down.

**Was of the Disbelievers**

*Kāna min al-kāfirīn* warns against a romantic Satan who is “tragic, almost right.” The Qur’an classes him. Compassion for human sinners who may turn is one thing. Softness toward the outcast who has chosen the hunting season is another. Seek refuge and do not admire the refusal.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:75

> **[God] said, “O Iblīs! What has prevented thee from prostrating unto that which I created with My two Hands? Dost thou wax arrogant, or art thou among the exalted?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is Already a Verdict**

*Yā Iblīsu mā manaʿaka an tasjuda li-mā khalaqtu bi-yadayya* — O Iblīs, what prevented you from prostrating to what I created with My two Hands? In 7:12: what prevented you from prostrating when I commanded you? Here the object is described by its making: *bi-yadayya*.

**“My Two Hands” and the Rule of Attributes**

God’s command, when He desires a thing, is to say “Be,” and it is (36:82). The “two Hands” are therefore not a need for limbs. The scholars of the Sunnah affirm what God affirmed without likening and without stripping. Other commentators take the dual as a sign of special care, or as an allusion to two kinds of attributes — majesty and beauty, qahr and luṭf, as some of the later theosophers said — meeting in the human as their locus. Al-Qurṭubī: an allusion to the human’s greatness. The rhetorical question to Iblīs then means: do you truly think yourself a more exalted creation than this?

The verse’s work for the reciter is not to invent a body for God. It is to feel the weight of Adam’s making. Something God ascribes to His two Hands is not to be scorned as “only clay” without scorning the Maker’s honor.

**Two Diagnoses Offered**

*Astakbarta am kunta min al-ʿālīn* — did you wax arrogant, or were you of the exalted? God offers Iblīs the chance to claim a rank that would exempt him, knowing he has none. *Al-ʿālīn* would be those whose exaltation is given, not seized. Iblīs’s exaltation is seized. The question separates given height (the angels who bowed are in fact higher in obedience) from claimed height. Quraysh are in the same exam: are you truly *ʿālīn*, or only *mustakbirūn*?


**Prevention as the Question of Every Sin**

*Mā manaʿaka* — what prevented you? The question assumes that the default after a command is motion, and that refusal needs a blocker. For Iblīs the blocker was kibr. For us it may be shame, laziness, the *malaʾ*, love of a ewe we do not own. God asking Iblīs is God teaching us to locate the blocker. Once named, it can be fought. Unnamed, it calls itself “I am better.”

**Two Hands, Special Care**

Even those who interpret the Hands as “care without limb” agree that Adam is singled out. Other creatures are “Be.” This one is narrated as made with two Hands. The human who then destroys humans, or despises the poor believer, is at war with that care. The verse is a foundation of honor ethics, not only of Iblīs-studies.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:76

> **He said, “I am better than him. Thou hast created me from fire, while Thou hast created him from clay.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Racist Sentence**

*Anā khayrun minhu* — I am better than him. Then the proof: *khalaqtanī min nārin wa-khalaqtahu min ṭīn*. 7:12 is the same argument. 15:33 and 17:61 are its cousins. Fire is subtle, rising, luminous; clay is dense, falling, dark. Iblīs makes origin a moral rank. This is the mother of every later boast of blood, color, caste, passport, and school.

**The Logical Error**

Better, in God’s court, is more obedient, more *awwāb*, more *muttaqī*. Material of origin is what God chose. To use God’s choice of material as a proof against God’s command is to contradict oneself. If fire is “better” because God made it so, then the God who made it so is the one to obey when He ranks clay above fire for this bow. Iblīs wants God’s creative decision in his own making, and refuses God’s legislative decision in Adam’s honor. That is *hawā* (38:26) in an unseen mouth.

**Fire and Clay in the Sūrah’s Economy**

This sūrah will fill Hell with Iblīs and his followers (38:85). Fire’s child will have fire as *mihād*. Clay’s children who turn will have gardens and cool water. The element is not destiny. The response to command is destiny. Job was clay in pain and was found patient. Pharaoh was clay in palaces and was of the tent-poles. Iblīs’s elemental argument is already refuted by the human stories above it, if he would have heard them; they are told to us so that we hear them before we repeat his sentence in our smaller fires and clays: I am better because my degree, my wealth, my eloquence, my *khiṭāb*.


**Khayr Misdefined**

*Khayr* in Solomon’s mouth (38:32) was horses, goods, possibly a confession about misplaced love. *Khayr* in Iblīs’s mouth is himself. That is the fork. Goods may veil. Self-as-better is kufr. The reciter should hear his own “I am better than him” — better than a sibling, a colleague, a poorer worshipper — as a quotation, not as an insight.

**Fire’s Children and Clay’s Children**

Later racisms are this verse in new costumes: blood, language, GPA, gym, genealogy of scholars. The reply is always 38:26 and 38:28: judgment by truth, not making the muttaqī like the fājir. Origin is God’s affair. Ending is by taqwā.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:77

> **He said, “Go forth from it! Surely thou art outcast!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Expulsion as the First Answer to Istikbār**

*Fa-ukhruj minhā fa-innaka rajīm* — go forth from it, for you are *rajīm*, pelted, accursed, expelled, a thing from which one casts stones. 15:34 is identical. 7:18: go forth, disgraced and banished. *Minhā*, from it: from the Garden, or from the exalted assembly, or from the place of nearness. Arrogance does not get to stay in the room where the angels fell.

**Outcast as the Opposite of Zulfā**

David and Solomon were promised nearness (38:25, 40). Iblīs is named *rajīm*. The sūrah’s two ends of space: *zulfā* for those who go down, *rajm* for the one who will not. The Prophet ﷺ taught the istīʿādhah, seeking refuge from the *shayṭān rajīm*, so that every recitation of the Qur’an begins by naming this expulsion. The Book possessed of remembrance (38:1) is read after throwing the outcast away from the heart’s door.

**“Go Forth” in a Makkan Ear**

Quraysh tried to make the Prophet ﷺ go forth from the Ḥaram. God tells Iblīs to go forth from the holy precinct of the unseen. The true expelled one is not the warner they drove to the ravine of Abū Ṭālib’s clan. The true expelled one is the arrogance that would not bow. History will show who remains at the House.


**Space Cannot Host This Pride**

*Ukhruj minhā* teaches that some inner states are incompatible with some spaces. A mosque can host a sinner who wants to turn. It cannot host a program of istikbār. A heart that wants to be a *miḥrāb* must expel the Iblīs-sentence. The expulsion is God’s in the unseen; in the heart it is istīʿādhah, tawbah, and refusing the elemental boast.

**Rajīm and the Stoning of the Jamarāt**

The ritual of stoning in ḥajj is a later embodiment of this naming: the outcast is not debated at the pillar; he is pelted as rejected. The verse is the metaphysics of that gesture. Do not stand and negotiate the “I am better.” Go forth, to him, is God’s word. Ours is: I take refuge.


**Exit as Mercy to the Place**

The holy place is cleansed by Iblīs’s exit. Some removals are mercy to everyone who remains. A community that cannot tell anyone to go forth from a program of arrogance will watch the *miḥrāb* become a stage. The verse is God’s own removal policy. Humans imitate it only with justice, proof, and without becoming Pharaoh of the poles.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:78

> **And surely My Curse shall be upon thee till the Day of Judgment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Curse with a Term Named**

*Wa-inna ʿalayka laʿnatī ilā yawm al-dīn* — and truly upon you is My curse until the Day of Recompense. 15:35 is identical. *Laʿnah* is expulsion from mercy. *Yawm al-dīn* is the Day when debts are paid, the same root as the Dīn the Fātiḥah names. The curse is not a mood. It is a sentence with an eschatological horizon. What happens after that Day to Iblīs is the Fire of verse 85, not a lifting of the curse into Paradise.

**God’s Curse and Human Cursing**

The believer is not licensed by this verse to curse as a hobby. He is informed that the enemy of Adam is already under a Divine laʿnah. Wasting the tongue on endless malediction while repeating Iblīs’s “I am better” in practice is a poor trade. The useful act is not to rival God’s curse but to refuse Iblīs’s argument.

**Until the Day, Not Until Tomorrow**

The term tells humans that Iblīs will be in the interval of history as a cursed being, not as a retired one. Respite (38:79–81) is coming; the curse and the respite will run together. He will be present, and rejected. Taqwā is walking the road knowing a cursed, outcast whisperer still has a permit to call, not a permit to compel (14:22).


**Curse Until the Day, Work Until the Day**

The term of the curse is the term of history. Therefore history is not morally neutral weather. An enemy under laʿnah is at work in it. The believer’s work is not to out-curse but to out-remember: *dhikr* of verse 1 against the whisper of the cursed. When you remember, you are using the Book that Iblīs most wants you to treat as *bāṭil*.


**Laʿnah Is Not a Human Hobby but a Divine Fact**

To know that God’s curse is on Iblīs until the Day is to know which side not to decorate. Films, jokes, and “complex” sympathies that make the outcast admirable are a strange use of a life. Use the name *rajīm* as the revelation uses it: a rejected whisperer, still delayed, never a muse.


**Until the Day of Dīn**

*Yawm al-dīn* is the day religion becomes visible as recompense. The curse runs until that visibility. Therefore every earlier day is a day when the cursed one still calls and the Book still reminds. Do not wait for the Day of Dīn to take a side. The side is already named.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:79

> **He said, “My Lord! Grant me respite till the Day they are resurrected.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Request That Still Says “My Lord”**

*Qāla rabbi fa-anẓirnī ilā yawmi yubʿathūn* — he said, my Lord, so grant me delay until the Day they are raised. 7:14 is the same. Iblīs does not become an atheist. He becomes a rebel who still knows who owns time. This is a warning: knowledge of Lordship (*rubūbiyyah*) without obedience of worship (*ulūhiyyah*) is Iblīs’s own creed. Quraysh knew the Lord of the House in a fashion and still kept the gods. The sūrah will not let that knowledge count as tawḥīd.

**Respite Until the Resurrection**

He asks to remain until *yawm yubʿathūn*, the Day Adam’s children are sent up from clay again. Al-Bayḍāwī: that he may not die or be punished until then. He wants the whole human story as his hunting season. The request is hateful. It is also a confession of the Resurrection. The deniers of Makkah are less consistent than Iblīs. He plans around a Day they say is invention.

**Why He Wants Time**

The next verses will tell: to mislead. Time, for the outcast, is not for tawbah. God would have accepted turning — that is the sūrah’s refrain for servants — but Iblīs wants time for *ighwāʾ*. When a human asks for a long life in order to do more *baghy*, he repeats this prayer in a smaller key. When he asks for a long life in order to *anāba*, he is in David’s key.


**Lordship on the Tongue of Rebellion**

He says *rabbī* as the drowning Pharaoh will say belief at the last minute, except that Iblīs says it at the beginning of the hunting season. Knowing God as Rabb is not salvation. The notables of Makkah conceded a high god and kept partners. Iblīs concedes a Lord and keeps refusal. Tawḥīd is command accepted, not only a metaphysical compliment.


**Respite Requested for Harm**

Compare his request with Solomon’s: forgive me and bestow. Compare with Job’s: harm has touched me. Iblīs asks for time to harm. God sometimes gives people what they ask as a further trial. If your hidden request for more years is for more ṭughyān, you have quoted 38:79. Change the request.


**Raised: He Believes in the Resurrection He Will Use**

*Yubʿathūn* — they will be raised. Iblīs builds his career on a doctrine Quraysh denied. Atheism is not required for ruin. Ruin requires only that knowledge not become sujūd. Many who can recite eschatology still ask, in practice, for time to ighwāʾ their own souls. Rewrite the request: grant me respite to return, not to mislead.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:80

> **He said, “Then verily thou art among those granted respite.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Permit Without the Pleasure**

*Qāla fa-innaka min al-munẓarīn* — He said: then you are of those granted delay. 15:37 is identical. God answers the request. The answer is a fact of the moral cosmos, not a friendship. Some of the theologians say this respite is part of the trial of Adam’s children: a whispering enemy whose power is call, not compulsion. 14:22 will have Iblīs himself admit:

> *“I had no authority over you except that I called you and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves.”*

**“Among Those Granted Respite”**

*Min al-munẓarīn* — of the class of the delayed. He is not the only one whose term is extended. Quraysh are of the delayed (38:8: they have not yet tasted). Pharaoh was delayed until the sea. Delay is a Divine habit of warning, and a Divine rope for the arrogant. The same Name that is *al-Ghafūr* (38:66) is patient. Patience toward Iblīs is not pardon of Iblīs. It is time for humans to choose.

**How to Live in a World Where Iblīs Is Munẓar**

Do not imagine the road is empty of a caller. Do not imagine the caller is a second god. He is delayed, cursed, outcast, and permitted to speak. The *muttaqī* drives awake. The *awwāb* returns when he has listened too long. The arrogant says “I am better” and thus becomes a student of the delayed one. Verse 80 explains why the sūrah had to be sent as reminder: the hunting season is on, and the Book is the refuge.


**Delay as the Air We Breathe**

We do not live in the hour before Iblīs’s request. We live inside the permit. That should make us serious without making us haunted. The Prophet ﷺ would seek refuge morning and evening, not because God had lost control, but because the *munẓar* is real. Verse 80 is why those adhkār are not folklore. They are policy for a delayed enemy.


**Of Those Delayed — a Crowd of Deadlines**

He is not uniquely delayed. Pharaoh was delayed, Quraysh are delayed, we are delayed. The class *al-munẓarīn* includes every person still breathing after a sin. The difference is what the delay is for. Iblīs’s delay is for ighwāʾ. Yours is for *awbah*. Do not waste a servant’s delay on a satan’s project.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:81

> **till the Day of the Moment Known.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Respite Has a Clock God Owns**

*Ilā yawmi al-waqti al-maʿlūm* — until the Day of the time that is known. 15:38 is identical. Known to whom? To God, first. The angels may be taught of it; Iblīs is not made its timekeeper. He is given a term whose date is not in his mouth. The Makkans who asked when the promise would be are answered here in Iblīs’s file: there is a known time, and it is not yours to hasten or to postpone.

**The Moment and the Cry**

Verse 15: they await a single cry for which there is no *fawāq*. Verse 81: Iblīs is delayed until the known moment. The cry and the moment meet. The hunting season does not run forever. People who live as if the whisperer were a permanent landlord have mistaken a lease.

**Known, Therefore Not Vain**

A known moment is the enemy of *bāṭil* creation (38:27). If there is a *waqt maʿlūm*, the interval is a timed exam. Job’s years, David’s night, Solomon’s wind, Quraysh’s caravans — all sit inside a known time. The intellects of verse 29 are those who live as if the date, though unseen, is real. That is taqwā as driving toward a wall you have been told exists, even when the fog hides it.


**The Known Time as Anti-Anxiety and Anti-Heedlessness**

If the moment is known to God, anxiety that tries to steal tomorrow from God’s knowledge is wasted ʿizzah. If the moment is known, heedlessness that lives as if there is no moment is a lie. The verse sits between those two. Work, return, do not appoint yourself timekeeper. Iblīs wanted the calendar. He got a term without a date in his pocket. You will not get a better deal. You got a Book instead.


**Known to God, Unknown to the Hunter**

Iblīs hunts without knowing which hour the hunt ends. That ignorance is part of his punishment and part of our test. We also do not know our hour. The shared ignorance should not produce a shared project. He uses ignorance to hurry error. We should use it to hurry return, because the cry has no *fawāq* (38:15).


**Maʿlūm as Comfort for the Warned**

The warner does not know the date; God does. That is enough to warn without panic and without postponement. Panic pretends we must become timekeepers. Postponement pretends there is no known time. Verse 81 forbids both. Work in the interval. The Moment is already in a knowledge that does not slip.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:82

> **He said, “Then, by Thy Might, I shall cause them to err all together.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath by the Might He Does Not Obey**

*Qāla fa-bi-ʿizzatika la-ughwiyannahum ajmaʿīn* — he said: then by Your ʿizzah, I shall surely mislead them all together. He swears by the Might of the One whose command he refused. This is the most twisted piety in the Qur’an: using God’s Name as the energy of rebellion. Quraysh’s *ʿizzah* (38:2) was a counterfeit of this ʿizzah. Iblīs at least ascribes might to God. He then volunteers to be the opponent of God’s servants by that might.

**Ighwaʾ as His Entire Craft**

*Ughwi* is to send off the path, to make err. His tools, elsewhere, are making things seem fair (6:43; 8:48; 16:63), whispering (114:4), deluding (31:33), enjoining indecency (2:268), promising (4:120; 14:22). 17:64: incite whom you can with your voice, bear down with horse and foot, be partner in wealth and children, promise them. Verse 82 is the mission statement. The rest is method.

**“All Together” as the Wish to Undo the Angels’ All Together**

The angels prostrated *kulluhum ajmaʿūn* (38:73). Iblīs wants the children of clay *ajmaʿīn* in error. He is trying to reverse a scene. He will not get them all, verse 83 will concede. The wish, however, explains history’s pressure. The whisper is not modest. It wants the species.

**By Thy Might: A Doctrine of Permission**

Some of the gnostics and theologians noted that Iblīs knows he cannot mislead by an independent power. He swears by God’s might, which is, in a dark way, tawḥīd of *qudrah* without tawḥīd of *ṭāʿah*. God will say in 17:64, after listing his methods, that he will have no authority over the servants. The oath is permitted as speech; the result is limited as fact. Humans who say “I cannot help myself” give Iblīs more ʿizzah than God gave him.


**Misleading as Reverse Vicegerency**

Adam is khalīfah to set the earth right under God. Iblīs wants a reverse khilāfah: to set the children of clay wrong, all together. He swears by ʿizzah to do it. The sūrah opened with human ʿizzah as vainglory. It ends with Divine ʿizzah sworn by a rebel. Do not lend him your ʿizzah. Every time you say “I am better,” you have volunteered for his *ajmaʿīn*.


**All Together: the Wish Against Ikhlāṣ**

He wants the species. He will not get the *mukhlaṣīn*. The wish still explains why error feels organized: fashions, markets, jokes, the *malaʾ*. Seeing the organization is not paranoia. It is 38:82. Seeing it without seeing verse 83 is despair. Hold both verses as one sentence, because they are.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:83

> **save Thy sincere servants among them.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Iblīs’s Own Exception**

*Illā ʿibādaka minhumu al-mukhlaṣīn* — except Your servants among them, the sincere, the purified, the ones made purely Yours. He knows a class he cannot take. The word is read *mukhlaṣīn* (made sincere by God) and *mukhliṣīn* (who make their worship sincere). Both are in the sūrah already: Abraham’s house was purified with a pure remembrance of the Abode (38:46). The *awwāb* is *mukhlaṣ* in motion.

**Servanthood as the Firewall**

*ʿIbādaka* — Your servants. Not “the strong,” not “the fiery,” not “those of ninety-nine.” Servants. David, Solomon, Job were introduced as *ʿabd*. Iblīs excepts the ʿabd. Therefore the path of safety is not becoming fire. It is remaining owned. Ikhlāṣ is the inner of that ownership: a worship not mixed with the audience of jinn or men.

**The Enemy’s Testimony to a Doctrine**

It is a mercy that the Qur’an has Iblīs teach, against himself, that sincere servants are exempt from his authority. 17:65: “My servants — you have no authority over them.” 15:40 is this same exception. When a believer doubts whether sincerity does anything in a world of whisper, he is to remember that Iblīs, who would not bow, bowed in speech to this fact. The firewall is real. Ask to be of those God has purified, as 38:46; work at unmixed remembrance; when you fail, fall down as David, stamp as Job, ask pardon as Solomon. That is how the exception stays an exception.


**The Exception That Organizes the Whole Sūrah**

Every servant praised above — David, Solomon, Job, Abraham’s house, Ishmael, Elisha, Dhū’l-Kifl — is a commentary on *illā ʿibādaka al-mukhlaṣīn*. Iblīs excepts them because God excepted them. Your life’s work is to be clearly in the exception: owned, purified, returning. Not sinless like an angel — the sūrah showed kneeling and sores — but unmixed in direction.


**Sincere Servants: Made So, and Making Themselves So**

The two readings — *mukhlaṣ* and *mukhliṣ* — are a door and a walking. God purifies; the servant purifies his worship from mixed motives. Neither reading allows a person to sit still and claim exemption. The exception is a class you enter by being *ʿabd*, the word this sūrah loves more than *malik*.


**Among Them: Sincerity Inside the Threatened Species**

*Minhum* — among them, among the children he just swore to mislead. The exception is not a different species. It is clay that stayed ʿabd. That is why Iblīs hates this class: they prove that clay was never the problem. Fire was never the superiority. Command and ikhlāṣ were the whole exam.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:84

> **He said, “This is the truth, and the truth I speak.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse That Is Almost Only Truth’s Name**

*Qāla fa-al-ḥaqqu wa-al-ḥaqqa aqūl* — He said: then the truth, and the truth I say. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr gather readings: I am the Truth and I speak the truth; the truth is from Me and I speak the truth; I establish the truth and the truth that is spoken; God is the Truth and I speak the truth; to God belongs the truth. Al-Jalālayn and others take it as introducing verse 85: it is true that I shall fill Hell… and I speak the truth. The literal compression — “the truth, and the truth I speak” — is itself a teaching. After Iblīs’s oath, God answers not with a matching oath of ego but with *ḥaqq*.

**Truth as God’s Manner of Speech**

David was told to judge *bi-l-ḥaqq* (38:26). The litigants asked for truth (38:22). The disputing of the Fire is *ḥaqq* as event (38:64). Now God names His own speech as truth. The sūrah that began with an oath by the Qur’an ends its primordial scene by saying that what God says is the real. Iblīs swore by might to mislead. God speaks truth to judge.

**Why the Compression Fits the Moment**

There is no need, at this point, for a long disproof of fire-versus-clay. The truth is that God commands and God reports. Verse 85 will be the content. Verse 84 is the seal: unlike the notables’ slogans and Iblīs’s elemental boast, this speech cannot be invention (38:7). The Prophet ﷺ, who is only a warner, is transmitting a sentence that is truth twice — as essence and as utterance.


**Truth Spoken as the Opposite of Overpowering Speech**

The brother of the one ewe was overpowered *fī al-khiṭāb* (38:23). God’s *khiṭāb* is *ḥaqq* twice. Power in this speech is not fluency. It is correspondence to the real. The Prophet ﷺ’s warning is under this seal. That is why calling it sorcery (38:4) or invention (38:7) is not a literary opinion. It is a collision with 38:84.


**Truth Twice, So That Oaths of Rebellion Do Not Set the Tone**

Iblīs swore by might. God answers with truth and truth. Might without truth is Pharaoh. Truth is the climate of the Throne. The ummah’s speech should rhyme with 38:84 more than with 38:82. If your oaths, even pious ones, are engines of ighwāʾ, they have the wrong model.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:85

> **‘I shall surely fill Hell with thee and with such of them as follow thee, all together.’”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Filling of the Evil Return**

*La-amlaʾanna jahannama minka wa-mimman tabiʿaka minhum ajmaʿīn* — I shall surely fill Hell with you and with whoever follows you of them, all together. 11:119 and 32:13 speak of filling Hell with jinn and men. 7:18 is the promise made to Satan in another telling. Here the first content of the filled Fire is Iblīs himself, then the followers.

**Followers, Not Mere Victims**

*Mimman tabiʿaka* — of those who follow you. The verb is following, the same social motion as the troop plunging in (38:59). Iblīs called; they walked. 14:22 remains: blame yourselves. The filling is justice, not a harvest of the unwilling. The *mukhlaṣīn* are not in this load.

**All Together: The Inverse of the Angels**

*Ajmaʿīn* again. Angels all together in sujūd. Followers of Iblīs all together in Jahannam. The sūrah loves this word because it is about parties (38:11–13). Choose your *ajmaʿūn*. The Garden’s gates open for the reverent as a people. The Fire fills with a leader and his file.

**Hell as the End of the Elemental Boast**

He who said “you created me from fire” is promised a filling of Fire. The punishment fits the myth. Those who followed the myth of being better — Quraysh by clan, every later racist and arrogant scholar and cruel notable — enter by the same door. Verse 55’s evil return now has its population named in the first of all time.


**Fill: Justice as Completeness**

God does not say He will put Iblīs in a corner of Hell. He says He will fill Hell with him and his followers. The filling is the answer to empty, vain creation. The Fire is not a leftover. It has a population that chose its leader. The word *ajmaʿīn* here is terrible because it is voluntary assembly completed.

**Follow: the Verb You Can Still Stop**

*Tabiʿaka* is a present-life verb. Stop following. Change troops. The warner of 38:65 is still speaking. After the filling, the verb will have been conjugated for the last time.


**Hell’s Population Is a Following**

The verse does not fill Hell with those Iblīs merely annoyed. It fills it with him and those who *followed*. Following is the controllable act. In a hundred smaller followings — of a trend, a boss, a desire — you rehearse the big following or you rehearse its refusal. The filling is future. The following is now.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:86

> **Say, “I ask not of you any reward for it, nor am I among the pretenders.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mark of the True Prophet**

*Qul mā asʾalukum ʿalayhi min ajrin* — say: I do not ask you for any wage upon it. The “it” is the reminder, the great tiding, the delivery. Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, Shuʿayb say the same in Sūrah 26. Muhammad ﷺ says it in 6:90, 25:57, 42:23. 10:72: if you turn away, I have not asked you for a wage; my wage is only upon God. A mark of prophethood is that the caller is not on a salary from the called. The notables understood power as a thing that collects. The warner is a thing that spends.

**Not of the Mutakallifūn**

*Wa-mā anā min al-mutakallifīn* — and I am not of the pretenders, the affecters, those who impose themselves, who take on what is not theirs. Al-Zamakhsharī: he never feigned a rank that was not his, nor claimed what was not given. A saying attributed to the Prophet ﷺ, not in the canonical Six, and recorded in some commentaries: the pretender has three signs — he contends with those above him, he claims what was not conferred, and he speaks of what he does not know. The verse’s plain sense is enough: the man they knew for forty years is not a man of *takalluf*. He did not seek the seat of the *malaʾ*. He was given a *nabaʾ* and he warned.

**Against Iblīs and Against Quraysh at Once**

Iblīs claimed a rank God did not confer: better than Adam. Quraysh claimed a right to distribute prophecy (38:8–10). The Prophet ﷺ claims neither a wage nor a false rank. This is why he is a clear warner (38:70) and not a sorcerer (38:4). Sorcery and pretension collect. Warning spends.

**A Law for Every Later Caller**

Scholars, preachers, activists who turn the reminder into a wage-machine or into a costume of ranks have left verse 86. Taking a living from teaching can be lawful. *Takalluf* is another thing: speaking of the Highest Assembly as if one had been there, claiming spiritual thrones, contending for ʿizzah in the name of daʿwah. The last three verses of Ṣād are a purification of the office that began in verse 4. Be a warner. Do not be a pretender. Leave the wage with the Bestower.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:87

> **It is naught but a reminder for the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Book Reduced to Its Function, Which Is Vast**

*In huwa illā dhikrun li-l-ʿālamīn* — it is only a reminder for the worlds. The sūrah’s first oath was by the Qur’an possessed of *dhikr*. The middle said “this is a reminder” (38:49). The end says it is nothing else, and that the nothing-else is for all worlds. 6:90, 12:104, 68:52, 81:27 say the same. 53:4: it is nothing but a revelation revealed.

**For the Worlds, Not for a Clan**

*Li-l-ʿālamīn* refuses Quraysh’s jealousy (38:8) and their latter creed (38:7). A reminder for the worlds cannot be owned by the two towns. It cannot be an invention of one orphan for his tribe’s prestige. Worlds include jinn and men, the builders and divers, the people of Noah and the people of the thicket, the later nations who will read Ṣād in tongues not yet born.

**Only a Reminder, Yet Enough**

*In huwa illā* looks like a diminishment. It is a concentration. The Book is not a wage, not a pretension, not a ladder to the Highest Assembly for sport. It is *dhikr*. If you want honor, it has *dhikr* as honor (38:1). If you want to judge, it has tadabbur (38:29). If you want to be excepted from Iblīs, it has the path of the *mukhlaṣīn*. A reminder for the worlds is larger than a kingdom that does not befit anyone after Solomon. Kingdoms end. Dhikr, if taken, does not.


**Worlds: the Scale That Humiliates Faction**

A reminder for the worlds cannot be reduced to an Arabian quarrel, though it entered through one. Your party is not the ʿālamīn. When you treat the Qur’an as a weapon of a small ʿizzah, you have made a Book for the worlds into a slogan for a tent. Return it to its size. It will return you to your size, which is *ʿabd*.


**Naught but Dhikr — a Mercy to Pretenders in Training**

If the Book were a ladder for *mutakallifūn*, it would feed verse 86’s disease. It is reminder for the worlds: it returns you to what you knew on the day of *alastu* (7:172) and what you forget in ʿizzah. Take it as reminder and you become small enough to enter the exception of 38:83. Take it as a throne and you have already begun to pretend.

## Sūrah Ṣād 38:88

> **and you will certainly know its tiding after a time.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Promise Is Knowledge on a Delay**

*Wa-la-taʿlamunna nabaʾahu baʿda ḥīn* — and you will surely know its tiding after a time. *Nabaʾ* again: the great tiding of 38:67, the report they turned from (38:68). They will know it. The verb is not “you may guess.” It is *taʿlamunna*, you will know, with the nūn of emphasis. Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī: after death, after resurrection, or after the victory and spread of Islam. All three are “after a time.” Badr was a *ḥīn*. The grave is a *ḥīn*. The cry of verse 15 is a *ḥīn*.

**Knowing Too Late and Knowing in Time**

Verse 3: they called out when the time of escape had passed. Verse 88 is the same structure as warning, not yet as lock. There will be knowledge. The question is whether the knowledge comes while *inābah* is possible, or as the tasting of 38:57. The sūrah has spent eighty-seven verses trying to pull the knowledge forward: ruins of parties, kneeling of David, wind of Solomon, water of Job, open gates, evil bed, clay and Spirit, Iblīs’s oath, God’s filling of Hell. If after all that the tiding is still postponed by the hearer, the hearer has chosen to know *baʿda ḥīn*.

**After a Time, Not Never**

The phrase is a mercy and a threat. Mercy: God still speaks of a future in which they know, which means the story is not over in the hour of walkout. Threat: the knowledge is certain, so delay is not escape. The routed host (38:11) will know why it was routed. The pretenders will know they were pretenders. The sincere servants will know the *zulfā* as sight, not as report.

**How a Sūrah of Kings and of Clay Should End**

It ends without another king. It ends with the worlds and a time. Muhammad ﷺ is not given Solomon’s wind in this closing. He is given a reminder and a delay in which the reminder will be verified. That is enough for a warner. It is enough for us. The letter Ṣād opened in mystery. The last verse opens in certainty: you will know. Between the mystery and the knowledge lie the two turnings — *awbah* and *iʿrāḍ* — and the two returns — beautiful and evil.

The practical last word is therefore the sūrah’s first practical word: bear patiently what they say, and remember the servants who turned, and be of those who treat the Book as *dhikr* before the *ḥīn* arrives. When it arrives, the tiding will not have changed. Only the chance to take it as reminder rather than as verdict will have changed. That is why the verse is spoken now, in the world of clay and horses and open gates still unseen, by a prophet who asks no wage and does not pretend, to worlds that will certainly know.
