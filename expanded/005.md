# Sūrah al-Mā'idah (Chapter 5) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Table Spread and the Charter Sealed**

Sūrah al-Mā'idah, "The Table Spread," takes its name from an event narrated near its end: the apostles of Jesus ask for a table of food to be sent down from Heaven, and God sends it — with a warning attached (5:112–115). A sūrah could have been named after its laws, its covenants, or its celebrated declaration that religion has been "perfected." Instead it is named after a meal requested as a sign. The classical commentators saw wisdom in this. A table spread is where people gather, eat together, and become bound to one another; to eat at someone's table and then betray him is, in Arab custom as in most human customs, among the ugliest of treasons. This sūrah is, from first verse to last, about the covenant as a shared table: God spreads before His servants lawful food, clear law, and confirmed guidance, and then asks whether they will honor the company they keep at that table or betray it. The name is thus a quiet thesis statement for all 120 verses.

The sūrah is Madinan, and by broad agreement among the scholars it belongs to the very last phase of revelation. Al-Qurṭubī records that all of its verses came after the conquest of Makkah (8/630); Ibn Kathīr counts it among the last complete sūrahs revealed, alongside al-Fatḥ (48), with only al-Naṣr (110) coming later in the widely accepted ordering. Several of its passages — including the opening verses and the momentous third verse — are reported to have descended during the Farewell Pilgrimage in 10/632, weeks before the Prophet's death ﷺ. Multiple reports describe the physical weight of its descent: the Prophet ﷺ received it while riding his camel, and the animal could not bear the weight, so that he had to dismount (IK). Whether one reads this as literal or as the Companions' awestruck idiom for the gravity of revelation, the point stands: the earliest community felt that what was coming down in this sūrah carried the density of final things.

**Why the Last Laws Carry Special Weight**

Because of its lateness, this sūrah was treated by the first generation as the Book's last word on the matters it addresses. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said:

> *"O people! Sūrat al-Mā'idah is among the last that came down, so whatever it makes licit, you should consider licit, and whatever it makes forbidden, you should consider forbidden."* (Reported in the commentary tradition; cf. al-Qurṭubī)

A similar statement is attributed to ʿĀ'ishah, the Prophet's wife (IK, Q). Some scholars therefore held that nothing in this sūrah was ever abrogated (Q), though others excepted portions of verses 2 and 106 (IK). The logic is straightforward and worth grasping: in any extended legislation, the latest enactment clarifies the earlier ones. Just as a later clause in a contract governs the reading of an earlier clause, the rulings here — on food, pilgrimage, ritual purity, wine and gambling, bequests, and the grave penalties for violent corruption and theft — arrive as settled law. A modern reader might compare it to the final edition of a constitution: the earlier articles stand, but where this text speaks, its speech is decisive.

**Two Great Concerns: Sacred Law and the People of the Book**

The sūrah's content moves between two great concerns, and recognizing them is the key to reading it without getting lost. The first is the establishment of definitive ritual and legal rulings: pilgrimage conduct (vv. 1–2, 95–97), dietary law (vv. 3–5, 87–88), ablution and purification (v. 6), wine and gambling (vv. 90–91), bequests and testimony (vv. 106–108), the penalty for those who wage war against God and His Messenger (vv. 33–34), and the penalty for theft (vv. 38–39). Even the call to prayer, the adhān, is alluded to here alone among all verses that mention it (v. 58).

The second, larger concern is the definitive settlement of relations between the Muslim community and the People of the Book. Here the sūrah holds two truths in balance that a hasty reader might mistake for contradiction. On one side stands sharp criticism: the breaking of covenants (vv. 12–14), exaggerated claims about Jesus and Mary (vv. 17–18, 72–77, 116), the Israelites' refusal to obey Moses (vv. 20–26). On the other side stands normalization: Muslims may eat the food of the People of the Book and marry their chaste women (v. 5); Jews and Christians are told to judge by their own scriptures and are assured those scriptures still contain guidance and light (vv. 43–47); Christians are portrayed with striking tenderness in verses 82–85; and the sūrah gives Jesus' miracles — including the Table itself — some of their fullest Qur'anic narration (vv. 110–115). Between these poles run the political boundaries of verses 51–57, warning Muslims not to take as protecting allies those who mock or oppose their religion. The sūrah thus draws a complete map: theological clarity without dehumanization, social openness without political naivety, and judgment left finally to God (vv. 48, 69).

**How This Commentary Proceeds**

What follows treats each of the 120 verses as an independent, fully developed discourse, in strict order from the command to fulfill pacts (v. 1) to the closing declaration of God's sovereignty over the heavens, the earth, and all therein (v. 120). Where the source commentary groups verses — 9–10, 25–26, 36–37, 38–39, 43–44, 80–81, 82–83, 85–86, 87–88, 90–91, 101–102, 112–113, 114–115, 117–118 — each verse is separated and given its own complete treatment, as the method requires. Nothing is compressed because the chapter is long. The Table is spread; the reader is invited to sit with each verse in turn.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:1

> **O you who believe! Fulfill your pacts. Lawful unto you are grazing cattle, save that which is recited unto you, hunted game being unlawful when you are in the state of pilgrim sanctity. Truly God decrees whatsoever He desires.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why the Sūrah Opens with Contracts, Not Creed**

Most sūrahs open by glorifying God, telling a story, or swearing an oath. This one opens with a command about keeping promises: *awfū bi'l-ʿuqūd* — "fulfill your pacts." The abruptness is deliberate. Al-Mā'idah is a sūrah of the covenant in its practical dress: not the covenant as an idea to admire, but as a set of bonds to honor. Al-Ṭabarī observes that the verse most likely addresses religious pacts with God generally — the whole structure of obligation the believer has entered — while al-Qurṭubī notes that this naturally extends to marital, commercial, and treaty agreements, since these too are governed by religious Law. The same phrase appears where social transactions are in view:

> *"And fulfill the pact; surely the pact shall be questioned about."* (Qur'an 17:34)

The logic is worth pausing over. A community that has just received, in verse 3, the declaration that its religion is "perfected" must understand that perfection expresses itself first in trustworthiness. Before the sūrah legislates food, pilgrimage, or punishments, it legislates the moral precondition of all legislation: that words bind. A society whose contracts are honored can build markets, marriages, and peace treaties; a society whose contracts are decorative cannot build anything. The verse thus functions the way a foundation functions for a building — invisible in the finished architecture, but load-bearing for everything above it.

**The Word ʿUqūd: Knots That Hold a Society Together**

The Arabic *ʿuqūd* (singular *ʿaqd*) comes from the root *ʿayn–qāf–dāl*, meaning to tie, to knot. An *ʿaqd* is literally a tying — of two parties to each other, of the present to the future, of a promise to its performance. The same root gives the "knots" of magic blown upon in Sūrah al-Falaq (113:4), which shows how concrete the image is: a contract is a knot in the rope of social life. Ibn ʿAbbās (d. 68/687), the Companion most famed for Qur'anic learning, is reported to have understood *ʿuqūd* here broadly — the covenants God took from His servants regarding what He made lawful and unlawful. Some early commentators extended the address beyond Muslims to the People of the Book as well, since they too stand under covenant (cf. 2:40); Ibn Shihāb al-Zuhrī (d. 124/741) even claimed to have seen a letter from the Prophet ﷺ to the Christians of Najrān containing the first four verses of this sūrah (Q). Whether one accepts that report's details or not, its thrust matches the sūrah's own movement: covenant is the shared grammar of all who claim revelation, and this verse sets the examination in which every claimant will be tested.

**Lawful Cattle and the Exception That Proves Divine Authorship**

*Bahīmat al-anʿām* — "grazing cattle" — designates, for al-Ṭabarī, camels, cows, and sheep, the grazing animals commonly eaten in Arabia, including their young and even the unborn young in the womb when the mother is slaughtered. Some extended it to wild grazing animals such as deer; others assigned wild game to the hunting rules (Q). Then comes the exception: *save that which is recited unto you* — a forward reference to the detailed prohibitions of verses 3–4 (carrion, blood, swine, idolatrous slaughter, improperly killed animals; cf. 6:118–19, 121, 145). The structure teaches a principle of Qur'anic law: the general rule is liberality — God's earth is a table spread with provision — and prohibition is the marked, recited, deliberate exception. This matters psychologically. A religion imagined as a wall of "no" produces either rebellion or despair; a religion framed as a wide "yes" with fenced exceptions produces gratitude and attentiveness. The believer learns to ask not "why is anything allowed?" but "why is this particular thing fenced?" — and the fence then becomes a teacher rather than a prison.

**No Hunting in Pilgrim Sanctity: Practicing Harmlessness**

The verse then suspends even the lawful: while in *iḥrām*, the state of pilgrim sanctity, hunting game is forbidden (see 5:95–96 for the full treatment). Consider the wisdom. The pilgrim has entered a condition that rehearses the soul's standing before God — unarmed, unadorned, forbidden to harm even an animal. For a few days, the human being who spends eleven months as a taker practices being a leaver-alone. Modern readers may recognize the logic from nature reserves and ceasefires: there are times and places where the normal economy of taking is suspended so that life can breathe and the taker can remember he is not the owner. The pilgrimage thus trains the hand in restraint before it returns to ordinary life — and a hand trained in restraint at the Kaʿbah is less likely to snatch unjustly in the marketplace.

**"God Decrees Whatsoever He Desires": Sovereignty as Comfort**

The verse closes with a sentence that governs the entire sūrah: *inna'Llāha yaḥkumu mā yurīd* — "Truly God decrees whatsoever He desires." Al-Rāzī and the theologians read here the Ashʿarī principle in miniature: God's commands rest ultimately on His will and wisdom, not on human ratification. But this is not arbitrariness; it is authorship. The One who designed the human constitution — body, psyche, and society — prescribes for it the way a physician prescribes for a patient: the patient need not grasp the pharmacology to benefit from the medicine, though grasping it deepens his trust. Ibn Taymiyyah (d. 728/1328) insisted in this connection that God's laws always contain wisdom (*ḥikmah*) and mercy, whether or not human reason has yet uncovered them. For everyday life the sentence yields a practical fruit: when a ruling puzzles us, the response of faith is not suspension of reason but patience with it — study, ask, reflect, and meanwhile obey, the way a traveler follows his guide through unfamiliar terrain. The guide's authority does not insult the traveler's intelligence; it compensates for his unfamiliarity.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:2

> **O you who believe! Do not violate the sanctity of the rituals of God, nor the sacred month, nor the offerings, nor the garlands, nor those bound for the Sacred House, seeking the Bounty of their Lord, and [His] Contentment. But when you have left the state of pilgrim sanctity, then hunt for game. And let not hatred for a people who once turned you away from the Sacred Mosque lead you to transgress. Help one another toward piety and reverence; do not help one another toward sin and enmity. And reverence God. Truly God is severe in retribution.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Honoring What God Has Declared Off-Limits**

The verse lists five inviolabilities: the *shaʿā'ir* of God (His rites and ritual places), the sacred month, the sacrificial offerings, the garlands marking those offerings, and the pilgrims journeying to the Sacred House. *Shaʿā'ir* (singular *shaʿīrah*) comes from a root meaning to sense or perceive (*shaʿara*); the rites are "perceptible signs," landmarks of devotion one can see and touch. In Qur'anic usage the term belongs exclusively to the pilgrimage complex — Ṣafā and Marwah are called among "the rituals of God" (2:158), as are the sacrificial animals (22:32, 36). The command, as al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī explain, is to recognize these rites, fulfill them, and avoid everything prohibited in pilgrim sanctity. Behind the list stands a civilizational achievement easily missed: in a land with no police and no standing courts, the sanctuary system — sacred places, sacred months, marked offerings, protected travelers — was the peace treaty of Arabia. To violate it was not rudeness; it was the collapse of the only neutral ground a stateless society possessed. Islam did not abolish this inherited sanctuary; it purified and confirmed it, showing that revelation completes sound custom rather than always razing it.

**The Sacred Months: Time Itself Can Be a Sanctuary**

The singular "sacred month" is read by most commentators as denoting all four sacred months — Rajab, Dhū'l-Qaʿdah, Dhū'l-Ḥijjah, and Muḥarram — the seasons when Arabia traditionally abstained from violence (Q, R; cf. 2:217; 9:36). Some read it as Dhū'l-Ḥijjah specifically (Z), others as Rajab or Dhū'l-Qaʿdah (R, Ṭ). The underlying idea deserves reflection: just as space can be consecrated (the ḥaram), so can time. Human beings who fight all year without pause become incapable of peace; the calendar itself must contain rooms where the sword is hung up. The Qur'an upheld these months even through the years of warfare with Makkah, permitting fighting in them only under defined conditions (2:194, 217). Some later held their sanctity abrogated for idolaters by 9:5, while others pointed to this very verse, verse 97, 9:36, and the Farewell address as confirmation that the months' sanctity endured (IK). The practical lesson for any age: build ceasefires into the rhythm of life — truces in feuding families at holidays, pauses in litigation for mediation, Sabbaths of rest in labor. Peace needs scheduled space or it never arrives.

**The Stolen Camels and the Man from Yamāmah**

The early reports attach a vivid occasion to this verse. After the conquest of Makkah but before idolaters were barred from the pilgrimage (9:1–28), some Muslims spotted a man from Yamāmah — who had earlier feigned interest in Islam, then left Madinah driving off some Muslims' camels — now coming on pilgrimage with those same camels wearing sacrificial garlands. The Muslims asked whether to seize him and the animals. The verse answered: do not interfere with the offerings, the garlands, or pilgrims bound for the Sacred House (Q, Ṭ, W). The ruling stings in the best way: even a thief's sacrificial camels, once dedicated, are protected, and even a suspect traveler, once a pilgrim, is safe. Law that bends for outrage is not law but mood; the verse insists that sacred procedure outranks private grievance. Later, 9:1–28 barred idolaters from the pilgrimage altogether — a clear instance of *naskh*, where a later ruling narrows an earlier universal protection (Ṭ).

**When the Wounded Become the Rulers: Hatred Must Not Legislate**

*Those who once turned you away from the Sacred Mosque* were the Makkan idolaters who had driven Muslims from their homes and blocked their ʿumrah in 6/628 (cf. 48:25; 2:114; 8:34; 22:25). Now those same Makkans had embraced Islam en masse — yesterday's persecutors were today's brethren — and the verse warns the victors: *let not hatred for a people lead you to transgress*. Al-Rāzī's logic here is razor-edged: transgressing God's limits is itself a form of injustice, and injustice against former enemies poisons the victor more than the victim. The Prophet ﷺ embodied the verse immediately: after the conquest of Makkah, following a victorious battle joined by the new Makkan converts, he distributed the entire spoils to those new converts, binding their hearts to the community and dissolving lingering resentments on both sides. Every post-conflict society faces this verse's test — truth commissions, lustration laws, the treatment of collaborators. The Qur'anic answer: justice without vengeance, accountability without humiliation. Hatred may knock at the door of judgment; it must never be given a seat at the table.

**"Help One Another": The Shortest Social Contract in Scripture**

*Wa taʿāwanū ʿala'l-birri wa'l-taqwā, wa lā taʿāwanū ʿala'l-ithmi wa'l-ʿudwān* — "Help one another toward piety and reverence; do not help one another toward sin and enmity." Few sentences in any scripture have done more work. *Birr* is expansive goodness — the word the Qur'an uses for the fullest righteousness (2:177); *taqwā* is reverent self-guarding, the careful walk through a thorn-covered path. Their pairing means: cooperate in everything that builds souls and societies, and refuse cooperation — politely, firmly — with everything that damages them. The Prophet ﷺ gave the principle its famous social image:

> *"Help your brother, whether he is an oppressor or oppressed." They said, "O Messenger of God, we help the oppressed — but how do we help an oppressor?" He said, "By restraining him from oppression; that is helping him."* (al-Bukhārī 2444; Muslim 2584)

Notice the reversal: true solidarity with a wrongdoer is stopping him, not joining him. Applied today, the verse governs boardrooms (refuse the fraudulent scheme), families (refuse to cover the abuse), and politics (refuse the sectarian bargain). Cooperation is sacred — but only in the right direction. The verse ends, fittingly, with reverence and a warning that God is severe in retribution: solidarity in sin is never costless, however popular it feels at the moment.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:3

> **Forbidden unto you are carrion and blood, the flesh of swine and that which has been offered to other than God, that which has been strangled or beaten to death, that which has been killed by falling or has been gored to death, that which has been mangled by beasts of prey — save that which you may purify — and that which is sacrificed on stone altars, and that which you allot with divining arrows; that is iniquity. This day those who disbelieve have despaired of your religion. So fear them not, but fear Me! This day I have perfected for you your religion, and completed My Blessing upon you, and have approved for you as religion, Submission (Islām). But whosoever is compelled by hunger, without inclining toward sin, then surely God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sermon on a Mountain: The Setting of the Perfection Verse**

Most commentators place the heart of this verse — *This day I have perfected for you your religion* — on the plain of ʿArafāt, on a Friday, as the Prophet ﷺ delivered his Farewell Sermon (Q, Ṭ, W). A minor opinion traces it to the conquest of Makkah (Q, via Mujāhid). The scene matters: over a hundred thousand pilgrims, the rites of ḥajj finally established in their complete form, the mission weeks from its close. A famous report in al-Bukhārī and Muslim captures what the verse meant to its first hearers: a Jewish scholar told ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb that had such a verse come down upon the Jews, they would have taken its day as a festival. ʿUmar replied:

> *"I know the day on which it was revealed and the place: it was revealed upon the Messenger of God ﷺ at ʿArafah on a Friday — and both are festivals for us."* (al-Bukhārī 45; Muslim 3017)

ʿUmar is also reported to have wept upon hearing it, explaining that nothing is perfected but that it begins to wane — perfection announced completion, and completion announced farewell. The verse is thus joy and grief braided together: the religion stands complete, and its bringer will soon depart.

**Ten Prohibitions: Reading the Dietary List as a Moral Education**

The verse lists ten forbidden categories: carrion, blood, swine, meat dedicated to other than God, strangled animals, beaten animals, animals killed by falling, gored animals, animals mangled by predators, and meat sacrificed on stone altars or apportioned by divining arrows. Earlier verses (2:173; 6:145; 16:115) gave the shorter list; this late verse adds the modes of death in detail. Each category teaches something. Carrion and blood concern wholesomeness and respect for life — one does not eat what one found rotting, nor drink the fluid of life itself. Swine and idol-dedicated meat concern spiritual allegiance — the eater's table declares his lord. The strangled, beaten, fallen, gored, and mangled concern the manner of death: an animal that died in violence, neglect, or accident, without the deliberate, God-invoking act of slaughter, is not food but a casualty. A ḥadīth applies the same logic to hunting: game struck with a blunt arrow that kills without piercing is "beaten to death" and may not be eaten (Q). The eater is thereby trained to care how his food died — a discipline of attention that industrial food systems, with their hidden slaughterhouses, have almost entirely dissolved.

**Dhakāh: Purification Through the Throat of Mercy**

*Save that which you may purify* (*mā dhakkaytum*) — from the root *dhāl–kāf–wāw*, meaning fragrant, delicious, pure — exempts any injured animal that is still alive enough to be properly slaughtered (Q, Ṭ). The exception pointedly does not cover carrion or swine, which are forbidden absolutely (Ṭ). Proper slaughter (*dhabīḥah*/*dhakāh*) requires slitting the throat, preferably severing the jugular vein, for a swift death with the blood drained; mentioning God's Name; facing the qiblah; using a sharp knife; sparing the animal struggle and fright; and turning one's face away, remembering that killing is permitted only by God's leave (Q). The cumulative effect, as the source commentary beautifully notes, is to strip the slaughterer of any sense of absolute power over the animal. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"God has prescribed excellence in all things. So when you kill, kill well, and when you slaughter, slaughter well. Let one of you sharpen his blade and give rest to his animal."* (Muslim 1955)

A religion that legislates the comfort of a sheep at slaughter is a religion that takes mercy as a universal operating system, not a sentiment reserved for the likeable.

**"This Day the Disbelievers Have Despaired": The End of the War on Islam's Survival**

*This day those who disbelieve have despaired of your religion* — the idolaters, who had spent twenty years trying to strangle Islam in its cradle, finally lost hope of turning the Muslims back (Q, Ṭ). *So fear them not, but fear Me!* (cf. 2:150; 3:175; 5:44). The psychology is precise: communities born in persecution often keep living in fear long after the danger passes, and fear curdles into either aggression or compromise. The verse retires the fear. From now on, the community's only fear must be God — which is to say, its only anxiety must be about its own fidelity, not its enemies' plots. When a people fears only God, it becomes both unbullyable and unprovokable: unbullyable because no threat outweighs His command, unprovokable because no insult outweighs His prohibition.

**Perfected, Completed, Approved: Three Words for One Triumph**

*Perfected your religion* (*akmaltu*), *completed My Blessing* (*atmamtu*), *approved for you Submission as religion* (*raḍītu*) — three verbs, three facets. Most commentators read "perfection" as the establishment of the final ritual obligation, the ḥajj, or as the completion of revelation itself; some held that no further ritual or legal rulings came after this day (Ṭ, Ṭū, Z), others that some legal verses still followed (Q). "Completion of blessing" was read as victory over idolatry and the banishment of idolaters from the pilgrimage (Ṭ, Z). And *Islām* here, most held, names the confessional religion revealed through Muḥammad ﷺ, not merely generic submission (though al-Ṭabarī preserves the reading of islām as the heart's full surrender to tawḥīd — a level above verbal affirmation). Twelver Shīʿī traditions connect this verse to Ghadīr Khumm, where the Prophet ﷺ declared, "For whomever I am his master (*mawlā*), ʿAlī is [also] his master" — reading the perfection as the establishment of spiritual authority (*walāyah*/*imāmah*) in ʿAlī's line (Qm, Ṭū); Sunnīs accept the Ghadīr event while reading the verse's perfection as the completion of law and rite. Both readings agree on the verse's core claim: after this day, nothing essential is missing. The believer who learns, practices, and transmits what was completed at ʿArafāt holds the whole religion in his hands.

**Necessity Softens Law Without Breaking It**

The verse returns at its close to food: *whosoever is compelled by hunger, without inclining toward sin — God is Forgiving, Merciful* (cf. 2:173; 6:119, 145; 16:115; and the general compulsion exemption, 16:106). The jurists built a whole jurisprudence of necessity (*ḍarūrah*) on such clauses: necessity renders the forbidden permissible, but only to the measure of the necessity. Starvation may license carrion; it does not license feasting. The profound point is that mercy is internal to the Law, not an apology for it. A legal system with no emergency exit is brittle and will be shattered by real life; a legal system whose exits are everywhere is mush and will be exploited. The Qur'anic balance — strict rule, narrow compassionate exception, God watching the heart's inclination — models legislation that is both principled and humane.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:4

> **They ask thee what is lawful unto them. Say, "Lawful unto you are all good things." And as for the hunting animals you have taught, teaching them from that which God has taught you, eat of what they catch for you, and mention the Name of God over it. And reverence God; truly God is swift in reckoning!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"All Good Things": The Default Setting of Divine Law**

*They ask thee what is lawful* — and the answer comes back wider than the question: *uḥilla lakumu'l-ṭayyibāt*, "lawful unto you are all good things." *Ṭayyib* (plural *ṭayyibāt*) means good, pure, wholesome, pleasant — the same word the Qur'an uses for good speech (14:24), good land (7:58), and the "good life" promised to the righteous (16:97). The jurists derived from this verse and its sisters a foundational maxim: *al-aṣl fī'l-ashyā' al-ibāḥah* — the default ruling on things is permissibility. God is not a miser with His world; prohibition is the exception requiring evidence, not permission. The Qur'an elsewhere ties this liberality to spiritual standing: manna and quails came with the command *eat of the good things We have provided you* (2:57; 20:81), while the Israelites' later wrongdoing narrowed what was lawful for them (4:160; 7:160). The pattern suggests a spiritual law of appetite: purity of heart widens the table; corruption narrows it. And the verse equally rebukes the reverse error — those who forbid what God allowed (5:87; 7:32; 10:59; 16:116). Monastic self-denial imposed as law is as much a trespass as gluttonous indulgence; both rewrite the menu the Host has set.

**The Hunting Dog: When an "Unclean" Animal Becomes a Partner**

The verse permits game caught by trained hunting animals — dogs, falcons, and the like — despite traditions describing dogs as unclean. The commentators are explicit: the trained hunting dog, and by extension dogs providing protection or valuable service, are exempted from that status by this verse (Ṭ, W). The training itself is described with theological precision: *teaching them from that which God has taught you* — human mastery over animals is borrowed mastery, a sub-loan from the Divine Teacher. The conditions complete the picture: the animal must be genuinely trained (it catches for its master, not for itself — if it eats from the prey, the prey is suspect (Ṭ)); God's Name must be pronounced before releasing the hound or over the living prey; and blood must flow in the killing. If the prey reaches the hunter alive, it must be slaughtered properly under the verse-3 exception (Ṭ). The Prophet ﷺ detailed these rules for ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim:

> *"When you release your trained dog and mention God's Name, then eat what it catches for you. But if the dog has eaten from it, do not eat, for it caught it for itself."* (al-Bukhārī 5478; Muslim 1929)

A whole ethic hides here: even predation must be disciplined, purposeful, and God-remembering. Sport killing for amusement — the very thing modern trophy hunting celebrates — finds no shelter in this verse.

**Why "Swift in Reckoning" Follows a Hunting Law**

The verse ends: *inna'Llāha sarīʿu'l-ḥisāb* — "truly God is swift in reckoning." Why append eschatology to game laws? Because small permissions are where reverence is tested. No one watches the hunter in the empty valley; only God counts whether His Name was mentioned and whether the hound was truly trained. Al-Rāzī notes the pattern throughout the sūrah: legal detail sealed with a Divine Name that turns the outward act inward. Swiftness of reckoning does not mean haste — it means inescapability without delay once the term arrives, and effortlessness: God reckons all creatures as easily as one. For the believer, the phrase converts every unwatched moment into a watched one — not with the paranoia of surveillance, but with the comfort of significance: nothing you do correctly in secret is wasted, and nothing you do wrongly in secret is lost. The lonely hunter who still says *bismi'Llāh* over his arrow has understood the verse better than the scholar who merely parses it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:5

> **This day, all good things are made lawful unto you. The food of those who have been given the Book is lawful unto you, and your food is lawful unto them. And likewise the chaste women of the believers, and the chaste women of those who were given the Book before you, when you have given them their bridewealth as married women, not as fornicators, nor as paramours. And whosoever rejects belief, his deeds have come to naught, and in the Hereafter he shall be among the losers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"This Day": Triumph as the Context of Liberality**

*This day* (*al-yawma*) echoes the triple "this day" of verse 3 — the day of despair for disbelievers, of perfection, of completion — and some commentators read it as naming not a single day but the entire era of Islam's establishment (Q). The placement is theologically eloquent: liberality in food and marriage arrives as the fruit of triumph, not as a concession wrung from weakness. A community unsure of itself builds walls; a community secure in its covenant can open doors. The verse opens two of the most intimate doors human beings possess — the kitchen and the bedroom — to the People of the Book. Whatever else this sūrah will say in criticism of Jewish and Christian doctrines, it says here, unambiguously, that Jews and Christians are not idolaters: their slaughter may be eaten (unlike the idolaters', v. 3) and their women may be married (unlike the idolaters', 2:221; 60:10–11). Social coexistence is not a reluctant footnote to this religion; in its final legislation, it is part of the main text.

**Eating Together: The Table as a Bridge Between Faiths**

*The food of those who have been given the Book is lawful unto you* — read by the majority as the meat slaughtered by Jews and Christians (with the early Shīʿī tradition dissenting, limiting it to non-slaughtered foods like grain and vegetables (Qm, Ṭū)). Some Companions and Successors read the license broadly — even food offered at a church (Ṭ) or slaughtered in the Messiah's name (Q); ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and ʿĀ'ishah drew a line at meat one actually heard dedicated to other than God, citing *eat not of that over which God's Name has not been invoked* (6:121; Q). Beneath the jurisprudence lies a social vision: peoples who eat together cannot easily dehumanize each other. The shared meal is humanity's oldest diplomacy, and the verse institutionalizes it across the deepest divide its audience knew — the divide of religion. Most commentators extended the license to all Jews and Christians generally, not only the strictly observant (Q, Ṭ), and debated whether Zoroastrians and others counted as "People of the Book" — a debate that would later stretch, in places like India, to cover Hindus and others under protected status. The trajectory of the verse is expansive: begin with kitchen-table peace among Abraham's children, and let the circle widen by analogy.

**Marriage Across the Divide: Permission with Guardrails**

The permission for Muslim men to marry chaste (*muḥṣanāt* — "chaste" or "free") Jewish and Christian women comes fenced with three conditions: the bridewealth (*ṣadāq*/*mahr*) must be paid, the union must be marriage rather than open fornication, and rather than secret paramours (cf. 4:25). *Muḥṣanāt* was parsed both ways — "chaste," referring to present status (a repentant woman regains chaste status, and ʿUmar reportedly forbade raising her past against her (Ṭ)), and "free," restricting the license to free women since only believing slave women are explicitly permitted elsewhere (2:221; 4:25; Ṭ). The great schools nevertheless counseled caution: ʿUmar discouraged such marriages where Muslim women were available, fearing both the neglect of Muslim women and the pull of the non-Muslim household on children; Twelver Shīʿī tradition generally discouraged them except by necessity or as temporary marriage (Ṭū). The wisdom is two-sided and both sides must be heard. Permission says: love and family can cross religious lines without sin. Caution says: count the cost first — the children's upbringing, the household's spiritual climate, the community's cohesion. A permission is not a recommendation; the Law opens the door and wisdom decides whether to walk through it.

**"His Deeds Have Come to Naught": Belief as the Currency of Deeds**

The verse closes with a theological axiom repeated across the Qur'an (2:217; 3:21–22; 6:88; 7:147; 9:17; 18:105; 33:19; 39:65): *whosoever rejects belief, his deeds have come to naught* (*ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhu*). *Ḥubuṭ* originally described a swollen, worthless udder — deeds puffed up with quantity but empty of the only substance that preserves them into the next life. The logic is not cruelty but coherence: deeds are addressed to Someone, and if the agent denies the Addressee, the address fails. A letter of exquisite prose sent to "nobody" arrives nowhere. This does not mean unbelievers' good deeds go unrewarded in this world — Islamic theology widely holds they may be recompensed here — but that eternity requires an eternal orientation. The warning lands with special force right after the verses of openness: social generosity toward the People of the Book must never blur into religious indifferentism. The Muslim may eat at his Christian neighbor's table and marry his Jewish neighbor's daughter, while still holding, clearly and without apology, that conscious rejection of God's final message voids the account. Warmth of dealings, clarity of doctrine — the verse refuses to sacrifice either.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:6

> **O you who believe! When you rise to perform the prayer, wash your faces, and your hands up to the elbows, and wipe your heads and your feet up to the ankles. If you are in a state of major ritual impurity, then purify yourselves. But if you are ill, or on a journey, or one of you has come from satisfying a call of nature, or you have touched women, and you find no water, then resort to clean earth, and wipe therewith your faces and your hands. God desires not to place a burden upon you, but He desires to purify you, and to complete His Blessing upon you, that haply you may give thanks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Purifications for Three States of the Body**

This verse is the ablution charter of Islam, detailing all three modes of ritual purification: *wuḍū'* (the lesser ablution — face, arms to the elbows, head, feet to the ankles) for minor impurity such as sleep or bodily discharge; *ghusl* (the full bath) for major impurity such as intercourse, seminal discharge, or menstruation; and *tayammum* (dry ablution with clean earth on face and hands) when water is absent or harmful. The schools agree that *wuḍū'* is obligatory before the five prayers and recommended before Qur'an recitation, entering mosques, eating, and sleep (with Shāfiʿīs adding occasions like carrying the dead). *When you rise to prayer, wash* initially suggested a fresh ablution for every prayer; the Prophet's ﷺ practice after the conquest of Makkah — praying several prayers with one ablution — established that renewal is required only when impurity intervenes, though renewal remains meritorious (Q, Ṭ). Beneath the rulings lies an anthropology: the human being approaches God not as pure spirit but as embodied soul, and the body must be gathered, washed, and readied — the way one dresses carefully before meeting a king. Prayer five times daily means the believer rehearses this readying five times daily; cleanliness becomes not an occasional effort but a rhythm of life.

**Wash or Wipe the Feet? A Grammar Lesson with Legal Consequences**

*Wa arjulakum ilā'l-kaʿbayn* — "and your feet up to the ankles" — sparked one of the most famous grammatical debates in Qur'anic law. Read as governed by *wipe* (*imsaḥū*), it commands wiping; read as governed by the distant *wash* (*ighsilū*), it commands washing. Ibn ʿAbbās and other Companions, along with Twelver Shīʿī commentators, preferred wiping (Ṭs); the major Sunni schools required washing, citing the predominant Prophetic practice and communal consensus (Q). Anas ibn Mālik reportedly reconciled them: wiping is the verse's wording, washing is the Prophet's Sunnah (Q, Ṭ). Mālikīs added rubbing, Shāfiʿīs, Ḥanbalīs, and Jaʿfarīs added intention, order, and succession (Q). The modern reader should not mistake this for pedantry. What is being negotiated with such care is the exact boundary between God's text and the Prophet's practice — between what is recited and what is demonstrated. Fourteen centuries later, a billion people wash or wipe their feet daily because of how a preposition was parsed. Few facts illustrate more vividly how seriously this civilization takes getting revelation right.

**Dry Earth as Water: The Law That Bends Without Breaking**

When illness, travel, or the absence of water makes washing impossible, the verse permits *tayammum*: striking clean earth and wiping face and hands (cf. 4:43). The symbolism is profound. Water is the great purifier of nature; when it is gone, God accepts the very dust of the earth — the substance of Adam's creation — as its stand-in. The gesture says: purity before God is ultimately about the heart's turning, and the body's gesture follows what is possible. Then comes the verse's theological seal: *God desires not to place a burden upon you, but desires to purify you and complete His Blessing upon you, that haply you may give thanks* (cf. 2:185, 280). *Complete His Blessing* echoes verse 3 — ablution itself is part of the completed favor. The Prophet ﷺ gave the inward dimension:

> *"Purity is half of faith."* (Muslim 223)

And:

> *"Whosoever performs ablution, and does it beautifully, his sins leave his body, even from beneath his fingernails."* (Muslim 245, paraphrased in the commentaries; Q, Ṭ)

Water that washes limbs becomes, by intention, water that washes sins. The daily chore is thus revealed as a daily baptism of mercy — five small drownings of the old self, five small risings of the new.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:7

> **And remember God's Blessing upon you, and His covenant by which He bound you, when you said, "We hear and we obey." And reverence God. Truly God knows what lies within breasts.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Gratitude as the Engine of Obedience**

*Remember God's Blessing upon you* — the same call sounds in verses 3, 6, and 11, stitching the sūrah's opening movement into a single tapestry of triumphal gratitude. Al-Rāzī makes a subtle point: the command is not to *recover* a forgotten blessing after neglect, but to *maintain* remembrance perpetually, lest prosperity anesthetize the community into imagining its blessings are self-made. Every thriving community faces this amnesia — the second generation inherits ease and mistakes it for the natural state of things. The verse prescribes deliberate, repeated recollection as the antidote. Modern psychology has stumbled upon the same mechanism: gratitude journaling measurably increases well-being and generosity. The Qur'an commanded the practice fourteen centuries earlier, with God Himself as the object — because gratitude directed upward becomes obedience outward, while gratitude directed nowhere curdles into self-congratulation.

**"We Hear and We Obey": The Pledge That Made a Community**

*His covenant by which He bound you, when you said, "We hear and we obey"* (*samiʿnā wa aṭaʿnā*) — most commentators read this as the pledges of allegiance (*bayʿah*) the community gave the Prophet ﷺ: before the Hijrah with the future Emigrants and Helpers, renewed at Ḥudaybiyah in 6/628, and in individual pledges of obedience (R, Ṭ, Z). Such pledges to the Prophet count as covenant with God because:

> *"Whosoever obeys the Messenger obeys God."* (Qur'an 4:80)

And of Ḥudaybiyah specifically:

> *"Truly those who pledge allegiance unto thee pledge allegiance only unto God. The Hand of God is over their hands."* (Qur'an 48:10)

A minority view (Mujāhid) reads the covenant as the pretemporal testimony of all Adam's children (7:172), but al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī object that this verse addresses Muslims specifically, and verse 12 immediately contrasts it with Israel's covenant (R, Ṭ). The deeper lesson: *samiʿnā wa aṭaʿnā* is Islam's master-sentence of discipleship — hearing that terminates in doing. The Qur'an contrasts it with the disobedient formula *samiʿnā wa ʿaṣaynā*, "we hear and we disobey" (2:93; 4:46). Between those two sentences lies the entire difference between knowledge that saves and knowledge that condemns. Information without obedience is, in this sūrah's accounting, not neutral — it is a covenant signed and then dishonored.

**Hearts Have Interiors, and God Reads Them**

*Truly God knows what lies within breasts* (*dhāt al-ṣudūr*) — a refrain sounding across the Qur'an (3:119, 154; 8:43; 11:5; 29:10; 31:23; 35:38; 39:7; 42:24; 57:6; 64:4; 67:13). Al-Rāzī reads it here as a warning against breaking covenant secretly while keeping it outwardly (R). The hypocrite's strategy — public pledge, private sabotage — is bankrupt before it begins, because the Witness audits interiors. For the sincere believer the same sentence is pure consolation: the good intention nobody noticed, the quiet loyalty nobody applauded, the inward struggle nobody witnessed — all are known, itemized, and valued by the One whose knowledge constitutes reward. Live, then, for the audit of the Breast-Knower rather than the applause of the crowd; the crowd sees the performance, but only He sees the rehearsal.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:8

> **O you who believe! Be steadfast for God, bearing witness to justice, and let not hatred for a people lead you to be unjust. Be just; that is nearer to reverence. And reverence God. Surely God is Aware of whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Standing for God by Standing for Justice**

*Kūnū qawwāmīna li'Llāhi shuhadā'a bi'l-qisṭ* — "Be steadfast for God, bearing witness to justice." The construction mirrors, in inverted form, the great injunction of 4:135: "Be steadfast maintainers of justice, witnesses for God." Read together, the two verses weld worship to ethics with an unbreakable joint: to stand for God *is* to stand for justice, and justice detached from God-remembering withers into ideology. Al-Rāzī explains that standing for God means bearing witness to His Oneness and Lordship — and since God Himself "upholds justice" (3:18: *qā'iman bi'l-qisṭ*), the witness to God must embody what God embodies. *Qawwām* is an intensive form — not one who stands occasionally, but one whose standing is his defining posture, like a pillar that does nothing but hold. The verse thus defines the believer's public identity: the person others can count on to be fair even when fairness costs him. In an age of partisan truth and tribal courts of opinion, this single sentence is a complete reform program.

**Hatred on Trial: The Hardest Command in the Sūrah**

*Let not hatred for a people lead you to be unjust* (*wa lā yajrimannakum shana'ānu qawmin ʿalā allā taʿdilū*) — the verb *jarama* means to incite or impel to crime; hatred is portrayed as a criminal urging the soul toward its own offense. The verse echoes verse 2's warning against hatred-driven transgression, now sharpened from battlefield conduct to the universal courtroom of judgment. Note what is *not* commanded: the verse does not order the heart to stop feeling hatred — emotions are not directly legislated. It orders the will to stop hatred from *legislating*. You may feel enmity toward persecutors; you may not let that feeling author your verdicts. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī draw the shattering corollary: if injustice is forbidden even against disbelievers and enemies — no killing their non-combatants, no breaking treaties with them — then injustice against fellow believers is inconceivably graver (R, Z). Apply the test honestly: do my judgments of the other political party, the other sect, the other nation, use the same evidence standard I demand for my own? If not, this verse is addressing me by name.

**"Be Just; That Is Nearer to Reverence": Justice as the Road to God**

*Iʿdilū — huwa aqrabu li'l-taqwā*: "Be just; that is nearer to reverence." Justice is not merely the fruit of piety here; it is its *road*. The outward discipline of fairness trains the inward disposition of God-awareness, the way practicing scales trains the musician's ear. Ibn Taymiyyah extended the principle to the fate of nations in a statement that became proverbial among later scholars:

> *"God aids the just state even if it is disbelieving, and He does not aid the unjust state even if it is believing."* (Ibn Taymiyyah, *al-Ḥisbah* and *Majmūʿ al-Fatāwā*, paraphrased)

History has kept confirming it: empires that institutionalize fairness outlast empires that institutionalize favoritism, regardless of their creeds. The verse closes with *God is Aware of whatsoever you do* — *khabīr*, the All-Aware, whose knowledge penetrates motives. Every biased judgment will be re-judged by the Judge who watched the judging. That is either terrifying or exhilarating, depending on which side of justice one stood.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:9

> **To those who believe and perform righteous deeds, God has promised forgiveness and a great reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Promise, Not a Possibility**

*Waʿada'Llāhu* — "God has promised." The verb matters enormously. Forgiveness and reward are not dangled as possibilities contingent on mood or merit beyond reach; they are *promised* by the One who, as the Qur'an insists elsewhere, never breaks His promise (3:9; 39:20). Al-Rāzī notes that the previous verse's ending — *God is Aware of whatsoever you do* — functions simultaneously as promise to the obedient and threat to the sinful; this verse and the next unpack those two faces explicitly (R). The pairing *believe and perform righteous deeds* (*āmanū wa ʿamilū'l-ṣāliḥāt*) is the Qur'an's master formula for salvation, repeated dozens of times: inward assent wedded to outward action. Faith without deeds is a tree without fruit; deeds without faith, as verse 5 warned, are fruit with severed roots. The promise binds the two together the way a covenant binds two parties — neither element alone triggers the clause.

**Forgiveness First, Then Reward: The Order of Mercy**

*Maghfirah* (forgiveness) precedes *ajr ʿaẓīm* (great reward), and the order is itself a mercy. *Maghfirah* comes from *ghafara*, to cover — like a helmet (*mighfar*) covering the head in battle. The believer's record is not merely balanced but *covered*, shielded from exposure. Only then comes reward — and it is *great* (*ʿaẓīm*), a word the Qur'an reserves for things whose magnitude exceeds measure. The sequence mirrors the psychology of repentance: first the burden lifts (forgiveness), then the joy arrives (reward). A king who merely pardons a criminal has shown mercy; a king who pardons and then enriches him has shown love. The verse promises both, in that order, to everyone who meets the twofold condition — no lineage required, no intercessor needed, no spiritual aristocracy interposed. Divine forgiveness for fundamental righteousness is likewise assured in 4:31 and 25:70. The door stands open; the verse is the invitation nailed to it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:10

> **And those who disbelieve and deny Our signs, they shall be the inhabitants of Hellfire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Side of the Promise**

Every promise in this religion has a shadow, and the Qur'an never hides it. Verse 9 gave the promise; verse 10 gives the warning, in perfect parallel structure: belief-plus-deeds earns forgiveness-plus-reward; disbelief-plus-denial earns the Fire. *Kaffarū wa kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* — "disbelieve and deny Our signs." The doubling is precise: *kufr* is the inward covering-over of truth (the root means to cover, as a farmer "covers" seed with soil), while *takdhīb* is the outward act of calling it a lie. The verse thus condemns not mere ignorance or weakness, but the settled, active rejection of recognized signs — truth seen and then refused. This is why the Qur'an elsewhere distinguishes those who never received the message from those who received and rejected it; the warning here targets the latter, the conscious deniers.

**"Inhabitants" of the Fire: The Horror of Belonging There**

*Aṣḥāb al-jaḥīm* — "companions/inhabitants of Hellfire." *Ṣāḥib* means a companion, one who keeps company; the damned are not visitors to the Fire but its *residents*, its company-keepers, as though they belong to it the way a citizen belongs to a city. *Jaḥīm* (from a root denoting intense blazing) is one of the Qur'an's proper names for Hell. The image completes the verse's quiet logic: human beings become companions of what they keep company with. Those who kept company with God's signs in this life keep company with His mercy in the next (v. 9); those who kept company with denial keep company with the Fire. Eternity, in this vision, is not arbitrary assignment but the ripening of association — the harvest of whatever the soul planted itself beside. The verse is severe, but its severity is the severity of truth-telling: a physician who hides a fatal diagnosis is not kind. The Qur'an diagnoses, warns, and — in the very next verse — returns to reminding believers of the blessings that make the promised end attainable.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:11

> **O you who believe! Remember God's Blessing upon you, when a people were prepared to stretch forth their hands against you, but He withheld their hands from you. Reverence God, and in God let the believers trust.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hands Stretched and Hands Withheld: Providence in the Archive**

This is the final *remember God's Blessing* in the sūrah's opening movement (cf. vv. 3, 6, 7), and it commemorates deliverance: *a people were prepared to stretch forth their hands against you* — the idiom for violent assault — *but He withheld their hands from you*. The early reports offer several occasions. One tells of a Bedouin who seized the Prophet's ﷺ own sword as he rested unarmed beneath a tree and tried to kill him, but was thwarted (R, W). Another — favored by al-Ṭabarī for its fit with the following verses on Israel — tells of the Banū Naḍīr, a Jewish clan of Madinah, who plotted to drop a stone on the unarmed Prophet ﷺ when he came seeking help with blood-money, until Gabriel warned him and he withdrew (R, Ṭ, W). Yet the verse's plural *you* suggests the broadest reading is intended: God's standing protection of the fledgling community against enemies who outnumbered and outmatched it (R). The infant Muslim community survived its cradle years the way a child survives infancy — not by its own strength but by being carried. Communities, like individuals, should keep a written archive of deliverances; memory of past rescue is the raw material of future courage.

**From Memory to Trust: The Verse's Two-Step Logic**

The verse moves in two steps: *remember* the blessing, then *trust* (*tawakkal*) in God. The order is the argument. Trust is not a mood one summons from nothing; it is memory projected forward. The believer who rehearses how God withheld hostile hands yesterday finds it rational — not merely pious — to expect His help tomorrow. This is why the Qur'an returns obsessively to sacred history: every narrated deliverance (Moses at the sea, Muḥammad at Badr, the community against its plotters) is a precedent filed as evidence for trust. And trust, the theologians insist, is not passivity — the Prophet ﷺ who trusted God also posted guards, dug trenches, and wore armor. Trust means the heart's reliance rests on God while the limbs take the means; it is the opposite both of anxious self-reliance (means without God) and of lazy fatalism (God without means). *Reverence God, and in God let the believers trust* — piety in conduct, reliance in outcome. Do your part impeccably; sleep peacefully about the rest.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:12

> **God had made a covenant with the Children of Israel, and We raised among them twelve chieftains. And God said, "I am with you!" Surely, if you perform the prayer, and give alms, and believe in My messengers and support them, and lend unto God a goodly loan, I shall surely absolve you of your evil deeds, and shall cause you to enter Gardens with rivers running below. But whosoever among you disbelieves thereafter, surely he has strayed from the right way.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Cautionary Tale Begins: The Covenant's Other History**

With this verse the sūrah pivots from Muslim blessings to Israelite warnings — a cautionary history for a community that has just been told to fulfill *its* pacts (v. 1) and remember *its* covenant (v. 7). Al-Rāzī frames the logic: the Jews and Christians also held a covenant with God and broke it despite His blessings, and their story is exhibited so the new covenant-bearers can see the disease before catching it (R). The Israelite covenant echoes across the Qur'an (2:40, 63, 83, 93; 4:154; 5:70; 7:169), nearly always followed by an account of unfaithfulness — the pattern itself is the sermon. History in the Qur'an is never antiquarian; it is always diagnostic. A wise community studies the failures of its predecessors the way a physician studies case histories: not to gloat over the dead, but to recognize the symptoms in the living.

**Twelve Chieftains: Representation and Responsibility**

*Twelve chieftains* (*naqīban* — *naqīb*, a guarantor-chief) for twelve tribes (cf. 7:159–60; 2:60). The early reports connect them to the scouting mission before entering the Holy Land (vv. 21–25): twelve trusted men sent ahead, of whom ten returned with terror and two — Joshua (Yūshaʿ ibn Nūn, of Joseph's line) and Caleb — with faith (R, Ṭ, Z). The *naqīb* was a witness-guarantor for his tribe, answerable for what his people did with what he reported. Here lies a leadership lesson of permanent value: representatives shape the morale of those they represent. Ten chieftains spoke fear and produced forty years of wandering; two spoke trust and were vindicated by history. Every committee, cabinet, and council replicates this scene: the report brought back from the frontier — of markets, of enemies, of possibilities — becomes the reality the community inhabits. Choose scouts for courage as well as competence, and weigh fearful reports with special skepticism.

**"I Am with You": The Conditional Promise**

*Inn Allāha maʿakum* — "I am with you!" Then the conditions: prayer, alms, belief in *all* My messengers with active support (*ʿazzartumūhum* — to honor, aid, and defend), and lending God "a goodly loan" (*qarḍan ḥasanan* — spending wealth and effort for religion's cause; cf. 2:245; 57:11, 18; 64:17; 73:20). The reward: absolution of evil deeds and Gardens with rivers. Note the covenant's architecture — ritual (prayer, alms), doctrinal (all messengers, which later generations violated by rejecting some, including Muḥammad ﷺ; cf. 2:89–91; 4:155–57; 5:70), and financial (the goodly loan). God wants the whole person — body, belief, and purse — because partial surrender is the seed of total relapse. And the closing warning — *whosoever disbelieves thereafter has strayed from the right way* — establishes the principle of proportionate accountability: the more light given, the graver the darkness chosen. Twelve tribes, twelve guarantors, one promise, one warning. The mathematics of covenant has never changed.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:13

> **Then for their breaking of their covenant, We cursed them and hardened their hearts. They distort the meaning of the Word, and have forgotten part of that whereof they were reminded. Thou wilt not cease to discover their treachery, from all save a few of them. So pardon them, and forbear. Truly God loves the virtuous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Anatomy of a Broken Covenant: Curse, Hardness, Distortion, Forgetting**

The verse lays out a four-stage pathology of covenant-breaking, and its order repays study. First, *laʿannāhum* — "We cursed them," understood as exile from God's Mercy (Z; cf. 2:88; 4:46–47; 5:64, 78). Second, *hardened their hearts* (*qasāwah*) — the heart that repeatedly refuses soft truth grows calluses, like hands that grip too long without gloves (cf. 2:74; 6:43; 39:22; 57:16). Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī preserve a striking metallurgical image: pure gold is soft and malleable, but alloyed with base metals it hardens — so the heart alloyed with hypocrisy hardens against the imprint of truth (R, Ṭ, Z). Third, *taḥrīf* — distorting the Word, whether by rearranging scripture's words or by twisting its meanings (cf. 2:75; 4:46; 5:41). Fourth, *forgetting part of the reminder* — the neglected portion of revelation quietly slips from memory (cf. 20:115 on Adam; 6:44; 7:165; 20:126). Notice the causation runs both ways: sin hardens the heart, and the hardened heart sins more easily. This is the spiritual equivalent of addiction medicine — each indulgence rewires the organ of choice itself. The only exit is the one the verse's ending provides: pardon, forbearance, and virtue — the softeners of hearts, human and divine both.

**"Pardon Them, and Forbear": Mercy After Treachery**

*Thou wilt not cease to discover their treachery, from all save a few* — likely recalling plots such as the Banū Naḍīr's (v. 11; Ṭ). And yet: *fa'ʿfu ʿanhum wa'ṣfaḥ* — "So pardon them, and forbear" (cf. 2:109; 3:159; 7:199; 24:22). The command astonishes precisely because it follows confirmed treachery rather than preceding it. Pardon before knowledge of the offense is naivety; pardon after full knowledge is mastery. Qatādah (d. early 2nd/8th c.) held this pardon abrogated by 9:29's command to fight hostile People of the Book until they pay the jizyah (IK, Ṭ, Th) — but al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī object that this sūrah likely postdates Sūrah 9 (revealed 9/631 versus the Farewell Pilgrimage of 10/632), so the pardon stands: once opponents accept lawful terms, past treachery is forgiven rather than nursed (R, Ṭ). Read beside verse 5's table-fellowship and marriage license, the verse reveals this late sūrah's trajectory: toward normalized, peaceful relations with the People of the Book wherever they accept coexistence. *God loves the virtuous* (*muḥsinīn*; cf. 2:195; 3:134, 148; 5:93) — pardon here is not weakness but *iḥsān*, the excellence of worshipping God as though you see Him. The strong can afford to forgive; the verse teaches the community to be strong.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:14

> **And with those who say, "We are Christians," We made a covenant. Then they forgot part of that whereof they were reminded. So We stirred up enmity and hatred among them, till the Day of Resurrection. God will inform them of what they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Those Who Say, We Are Christians": A Covenant Named by Self-Description**

The Qur'an's phrasing — *alladhīna qālū innā naṣārā*, "those who say, 'We are Christians'" — names the community by its own self-description (*naṣārā*, Helpers, after Jesus' helpers; cf. 3:52; 61:14). Like Israel, they entered covenant; like Israel, *they forgot part of the reminder*. But the consequence differs, and the difference is instructive. Israel's breach brought curse and cardiac hardening (v. 13); the Christians' forgetting brought *internal* fracture — *We stirred up enmity and hatred among them till the Day of Resurrection*. The classical commentators read this as sectarian factionalism over theology (IK, Ṭ) — the christological schisms that split councils, empires, and communions for centuries. Factionalism-as-punishment appears elsewhere too (6:65). The mechanism is painfully recognizable: when a community forgets part of its Founder's teaching, the forgotten part becomes the battlefield; each faction absolutizes a fragment and anathematizes the rest. Unity requires the *whole* reminder held in balance; partial memory produces warring orthodoxies. Every divided religious community — including, soberly, the Muslim ummah — should read this verse as a mirror before reading it as a history.

**Differences Deferred to the Day of Clarification**

*God will inform them of what they used to do* — religious differences, the Qur'an repeatedly teaches, will not all be resolved by debate in this world; they await Divine clarification in the next (2:113; 3:55; 5:48; 6:164; 10:93; 16:92, 124; 22:69; 32:25; 39:3, 46). This is not relativism — the Qur'an is unsparing in judging doctrines true or false — but eschatological patience: final verdicts belong to the Final Court. The practical ethic follows: argue firmly but live peacefully, witness without coercing, and leave the gavel to God. The verse thus quietly rebukes two opposite errors — the zealot who would settle all theology by force now, and the indifferentist who concludes that since God will sort it out, truth does not matter. Truth matters infinitely; that is precisely why its final arbitration is reserved for infinite Wisdom.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:15

> **O People of the Book! Our Messenger has come unto you, making clear to you much of what you once hid of the Book, and pardoning much. There has come unto you, from God, a light and a clear Book,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messenger Who Reads the Hidden Pages**

*Our Messenger has come unto you, making clear* (*yubayyinu*) *much of what you hid of the Book, and pardoning* (*yaʿfū*) *much*. Concealment of scripture (*kitmān*) is a recurring Qur'anic charge (2:42, 140, 146, 159, 174; 3:71, 187; 6:91). The commentators give examples: prophecies of Muḥammad's coming (7:157) and the Torah's penalty of stoning for adultery — which the Prophet ﷺ brought back to light when two Jewish adulterers were brought to him for judgment, ordering the Torah's own penalty carried out (JJ, Ṭ, Z; cf. 3:23). But note the second verb: *pardoning much*. Revelation does not expose everything hidden; it exposes what guidance requires and passes over the rest. Divine pedagogy is surgical, not vindictive — it uncovers enough to establish the proof and covers the rest in mercy. Human reformers should imitate the sequence: reveal what must be revealed to right the wrong, and veil what veiling harms no one. Exposure is a medicine with side effects; the All-Wise Physician prescribes the minimum effective dose.

**Light and Book: Two Names for One Arrival**

*There has come unto you, from God, a light* (*nūr*) *and a clear Book* (*kitāb mubīn*). Most commentators read "light" as Muḥammad ﷺ and "clear Book" as the Qur'an (Ṭ) — the Qur'an is called *mubīn* repeatedly (12:1; 26:2; 27:1; 28:2; 43:2; 44:2) — though some read both as the Qur'an (Z). Light is the Qur'an's master-image for guidance through prophets and revelation (6:91; 7:157; 21:48; 42:52; 64:8), and there are those who would extinguish God's light (9:32–33; 61:8–9). The pairing teaches that guidance needs both a *person* and a *text*: the Book without the Messenger becomes a dead letter fought over by literalists; the Messenger without the Book becomes a personality cult dissolving at his death. Together — living example plus fixed text — they form a complete guidance system, each correcting the other's misreadings across the centuries. The congregation that holds both tightly will not lose its way; the faction that drops either will wander, lamp in hand but map discarded, or map in hand but lamp unlit.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:16

> **whereby God guides whosoever seeks His Contentment unto the ways of peace, and brings them out of darkness into light, by His Leave, and guides them unto a straight path.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Seeking God's Contentment: The Sincerity Condition**

*Whosoever seeks* (*ibtighā'*) *His Contentment* (*riḍwān*) — guidance is offered universally but received conditionally, and the condition is sincerity of seeking. The *mubtaghī* — the seeker of God's good pleasure — is the Qur'an's portrait of the sincere believer (2:207, 265; 4:114; 5:2; 59:8). The point cuts against both despair and presumption: no one is guided who does not want to be, and no one who truly wants to be is refused. Revelation is light, but light benefits only open eyes; the verse makes the opening of the eyes a human responsibility. In practical terms: approach the Qur'an asking "what does my Lord want from me?" and it will answer; approach it asking "how can I refute my opponents?" and it will seem silent. The Book mirrors the seeker's intention — a mercy to the sincere, a locked door to the manipulative.

**Many Darknesses, One Light: The Grammar of Guidance**

*Subul al-salām* — "ways of peace" (or the way to God, since *al-Salām*, Peace, is His Name (Ṭ)). *From darknesses* (*ẓulumāt*, always plural) *into light* (*nūr*, always singular) — the Qur'an's grammar never varies here (2:257; 14:1, 5; 33:43; 57:9; 65:11), and the commentators draw the metaphysics: falsehood is multiple because it is fragmentation — every error a different shard — while truth is one because it is unity, reflecting the Oneness of God Himself, who is *the Light of the heavens and the earth* (24:35; Nūr among His Names). Error has dialects; truth has a mother tongue. The verse ends with *a straight path* (*ṣirāṭ mustaqīm*) — the life lived in accord with Divine Guidance (1:6; 2:142; 3:101; 6:87; 43:61; 67:22). Three images in one verse — ways, light, path — converge on one promise: the sincere seeker is not left to grope. Roads are marked, darkness is exited, and the path underfoot runs straight. Guidance is not a puzzle God sets but a hand God extends — *by His Leave*, always by grace, never by entitlement.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:17

> **They indeed have disbelieved who say, "God is the Messiah, son of Mary." Say, "Who would have any power over God if He desired to destroy the Messiah, son of Mary, and his mother, and those on earth all together?" Unto God belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them. He creates whatsoever He will, and God is Powerful over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Qur'an's First Charge Against Christological Excess**

*Laqad kafara'lladhīna qālū inna'Llāha huwa'l-masīḥu'bnu Maryam* — "They indeed have disbelieved who say, 'God is the Messiah, son of Mary.'" The *laqad*...*inna* construction is among Arabic's strongest affirmations; the Qur'an leaves no doubt about its verdict on identifying God with a human being (cf. 4:171; 5:72, 75, 116; 9:31). Note the title used: *al-Masīḥ*, the Messiah — the Qur'an honors Jesus with his messianic title even while refuting his divinization (cf. 4:157, 171–72; 5:72, 75; 9:30–31). The refutation does not demean Jesus; it defends him from an honor that would destroy his true honor — the honor of perfect servanthood. To call a flawless ambassador the king is not to praise the ambassador but to abolish his ambassadorship. The verse's mention of *his mother* alongside extends the critique to Mary's divinization (cf. v. 116) — the Qur'an sees where unchecked exaggeration travels and meets it at the destination in advance.

**The Destroyer Argument: Contingency as Proof of Creatureliness**

The refutation is devastatingly simple: *who would have power over God if He desired to destroy the Messiah, his mother, and all on earth together?* Whatever can be destroyed is contingent; whatever is contingent is created; whatever is created is not God. The argument's beauty is that Christians themselves supply its premises — Jesus hungered, thirsted, suffered, and (in Christian telling) died; Mary bore, nursed, and aged. A being with a birthday and a body is a being under power, not a being *as* power. *He creates whatsoever He will* (cf. 24:45; 30:54; 42:49) answers the unspoken objection — "but Jesus had no father!" — with a principle that dissolves it: fatherlessness proves nothing about divinity, since God creates as He wills; Adam had neither father nor mother (3:59), and no one deifies him. The verse thus implicitly refutes the Nicene formula "begotten, not made": whatever is begotten in Mary's womb is made, and whatever is made belongs to *those on earth all together* — under sovereignty, not as sovereignty.

**"God Is the Messiah" Versus "The Messiah Is God": A Theological Precision**

Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī note a remarkable precision: the Qur'an criticizes the claim that *God is the Messiah* — the confinement of the unlimited Divine Being to a human manifestation. Strictly, official Christian doctrine says the reverse predication, "the Messiah is God," while affirming Christ's humanity alongside. But the two commentators argue the distinction collapses in practice: a doctrine that attributes creation, universal rule, and incarnation to Christ is *tantamount* to confining Godhead within him, whatever its careful formulations. The lesson for interfaith engagement is balance: represent the other's doctrine accurately (the commentators knew the difference), but follow ideas to their logical terminus (they did that too). And the verse's closing — *unto God belongs sovereignty* (*li'Llāhi mulk*) *over the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them* — sets the positive doctrine against the negated one: reality is a kingdom with one King, and Jesus and Mary are among its most radiant subjects, which is glory enough for any creature.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:18

> **And the Jews and the Christians say, "We are the children of God, and His beloved ones." Say, "Why then does He punish you for your sins?" Nay, but you are mortals of His creating. He forgives whomsoever He will, and He punishes whomsoever He will, and unto God belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them, and unto Him is the journey's end.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Children of God": The Only Verse of Its Kind**

This is the Qur'an's sole critique of Jews and Christians calling themselves *children of God* — though it tirelessly refutes divine sonship in general (2:116; 6:100; 9:30; 10:68; 17:40, 111; 18:4; 19:35, 88–93; 21:26; 25:2; 37:149, 153; 39:4; 43:16, 81–82; 52:39; 72:3). The claim has scriptural roots the commentators recognized: Israel as God's "firstborn son" (Exodus 4:22–23), David and Solomon as His "sons" (Psalm 2:7; 1 Chronicles 28:6), the Gospels' "children of God" (Matthew 5:9; Luke 20:36; John 11:52; John 20:17). Ibn ʿAbbās reports the occasion: some Madinan Jews, warned by the Prophet ﷺ of punishment, answered that as God's children and beloved they had nothing to fear (Ṭ, Z). The commentators understood the claim as metaphorical or genealogical — an extension of the "sonship" of Ezra and Jesus (9:30; IK, R, Z) — not a literal assertion of divinity (JJ, R). Al-Rāzī links it to the broader claim of exclusive favor running through 2:111 and 2:135. The Qur'an's response respects the metaphor's poetry while demolishing its presumption — a model for critiquing beloved religious language without mocking it.

**Punishment as Disproof: The Argument from Your Own Admissions**

*Why then does He punish you for your sins?* The refutation is a judo throw, using the opponents' own admissions: both Jews and Christians affirm divine punishment (Jews typically limiting it to a brief period; cf. 2:80; 3:24). But beloved children exempt from accountability cannot simultaneously be punished sinners — the two descriptions contradict. *Nay, but you are mortals* (*bashar*) *of His creating* — bounded by birth and death you did not choose and cannot escape, exactly like the Jesus and Mary of verse 17. The argument generalizes into a universal solvent for religious entitlement: no lineage, no communal membership, no inherited title exempts anyone from the moral law. The Qur'an applies the same solvent to Muslims who might presume upon their own titles — the ummah is "the best community" only *on condition* of enjoining right and forbidding wrong (3:110). Chosenness in the Qur'an is always vocational (chosen *for* a task) and conditional (kept *by* fidelity), never a blanket immunity. Every community that reads this verse about others must overhear it about itself.

**Sovereignty and the Journey's End: Forgiveness as Royal Prerogative**

*He forgives whomsoever He will, and He punishes whomsoever He will* — mercy and punishment are not tribal entitlements but sovereign acts, flowing from the *mulk* (sovereignty/ownership) over heavens, earth, and all between, unto whom all journeys end (*maṣīr*). The verse thus relocates hope from the right address to the right one: not "we are safe because of who we are" but "we may be safe because of who He is" — Forgiving toward the repentant, Just toward the defiant. For the anxious believer this is liberation: your fate rests not on the strength of your communal card but on the mercy of your Lord, which no sin outnumbers. For the presumptuous believer it is a summons: the same mercy that encourages the despairing terrifies the complacent. Both stand before the same throne, and the throne's rule is single — *unto Him is the journey's end*.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:19

> **O People of the Book! Our Messenger has come unto you, making things clear to you, after an interval in [the series] of messengers, lest you should say, "There came unto us no bearer of glad tidings, nor any warner." But there has come unto you a bearer of glad tidings and a warner. And God is Powerful over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Interval Ends: Prophecy After the Silence**

*After an interval* (*fatrah*) *in the series of messengers* — *fatrah* denotes a pause, a stillness between movements, used for gaps between prophetic eras and even between revelations to Muḥammad ﷺ (17:106). Here it names the long centuries between Jesus and Muḥammad ﷺ. Some commentators held no prophet came between them (IK); others counted four minor figures — three in the Judeo-Christian line (perhaps the unnamed "message bearers" of 36:14) and the Arab Khālid ibn Sinān al-ʿAbsī (Q, Z). Either way, the theological point stands: the silence is over, and with its ending expires the excuse *there came unto us no bearer of glad tidings, nor any warner*. The Qur'an treats unreached peoples with principled mercy — *We never punish until We send a messenger* (17:15) — which makes the arrival of a messenger an event of double edge: mercy to those who accept, proof against those who refuse. Several Companions reported the verse answered Madinan Jews who claimed no warner had come after Moses (Ṭ). The claim, whatever its sincerity, is now time-barred. History had paused; history has resumed; the court is in session.

**Clarification as a Prophetic Office**

*Making things clear to you* (*yubayyinu lakum*) — clarification (*bayān*) is prophecy's standing office. Muḥammad ﷺ clarifies what was hidden (v. 15) and what was disputed (16:64); Jesus clarified disputes before him (43:63). Human beings left alone do not converge on truth; they accumulate obscurity — through error, forgery, faction, and simple forgetting. The messenger arrives as a restorer of legibility, the way a conservator cleans a darkened painting: the colors were always there, but centuries of grime made them arguable. And he comes as *bearer of glad tidings and warner* (*bashīr wa nadhīr*) — the Qur'an's inseparable pair (2:213; 4:165; 6:48; 18:56). Tidings without warning produce complacency; warning without tidings produces despair. The complete messenger carries both medicines and prescribes each to its patient: hope to the despairing, fear to the heedless. Any preaching that deals in only one has halved the prophetic pharmacy.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:20

> **And when Moses said unto his people, "O my people! Remember God's Blessing upon you, when He appointed prophets among you, and appointed you kings, and gave you that which He gave unto no other in all the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moses the Reminder: Gratitude Before Command**

The verse opens the sūrah's Moses narrative (vv. 20–26), and Moses begins exactly where the sūrah began with Muslims: *remember God's Blessing* (cf. 2:231; 5:7, 11; 14:6; 35:3). Only after gratitude comes the command to enter the land (v. 21). The sequence is leadership wisdom: obligation lands softly on a heart freshly reminded of favor, and lands as tyranny on a heart that feels only burden. Moses lists three blessings. *Prophets among you* — Abraham and the prophetic line through his descendants, the supreme honor a people can receive: God speaking from within their own ranks. *Appointed you kings* (*mulūk*) — read as sovereignty over their own affairs after Pharaoh's tyranny (Q): a slave people made self-governing, which is what "kingship" means at the communal level. *That which He gave unto no other in all the worlds* — manna and quails (2:57; 7:160; 20:80), prophets and signs (Q), goods of both worlds (IK) — unique *in their time*, the commentators caution (IK, Q), lest favor harden into supremacism (cf. 2:47, 122; 7:140; 45:16). Every community's zenith is dated; gratitude remembers the Giver without idolizing the gift.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:21

> **O my people! Enter the Holy Land, which God has prescribed for you, and do not turn back, or you shall become losers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Holy Land: A Qur'anic Geography of Blessing**

*Al-arḍ al-muqaddasah* — "the Holy Land." The Qur'an uses this resonant title only here, though the land's blessedness echoes elsewhere: the Farthest Mosque *whose precincts We have blessed* (17:1), and the land *We blessed for all peoples* given to Abraham and Lot (21:71). Reports locate it variously — Sinai, Syria, Jericho, the region spanning Palestine, Syria, and Jordan, or simply the Jerusalem sanctuary (R, Ṭ, Z); al-Ṭabarī judged its exact bounds unknowable, somewhere between Nile and Euphrates (Ṭ). Some commentators tie it to Abraham, shown the land and promised it to his progeny — which, Islam notes, includes Arabs through Ishmael (R, Z). *Which God has prescribed* (*kataba*) *for you* — inscribed, some say, in the Preserved Tablet (85:22; R, Ṭ, Z). Two observations follow. First, the land's holiness in the Qur'an is universal ("blessed for all peoples"), not an ethnic deed but a trust bound to obedience — as the following verses prove, the same generation that received the promise was barred from it for forty years. Land-promise and moral condition travel together; either alone distorts the covenant. Second, sacred geography exists to orient hearts, not to license conquests; the command here comes through a prophet, for a specific people, at a specific hour — not as a standing warrant for anyone holding this verse as a title-deed.

**"Do Not Turn Back": Retreat as Ruin**

*Do not turn back* (*lā tartaddū ʿalā adbārikum*) — do not retreat from the land without striving for it; or, in the deeper reading, do not turn your backs on the Divine command and the prophet's obedience. *Or you shall become losers* (*khāsirīn*) — the Qur'an's term for total, eschatological loss. The warning's logic is military, moral, and spiritual at once: armies that break before engaging lose more in rout than they would have in battle; souls that retreat from commanded striving lose the very capacity for courage; and communities that refuse their appointed hour find the hour does not wait. Moses speaks as a commander who knows his troops' fragility — the blessing-recital of verse 20 was meant to steel them. What follows shows it did not. The verse stands as the Qur'an's clearest portrait of opportunity with an expiry date: *prescribed for you* — but only if you *enter*.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:22

> **They said, "O Moses! In this land are a domineering people. We shall not enter it till they leave it. But if they leave it, we shall enter."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scouts' Report and the Tyranny of Fear**

The backstory, preserved in the commentaries: Moses sent twelve men, one per tribe (cf. v. 12), to scout the land; they witnessed inhabitants of terrifying size and might — some reports say a single giant seized them and released them as a warning (Ṭ). Against Moses' orders they reported publicly: *in this land are a domineering people* (*qawman jabbārīn*). *Jabbār* denotes overwhelming, compelling force — the tyrant who bends all wills; as *al-Jabbār* it is God's Name (59:23), for absolute compulsion belongs to Him alone, while in human hands it is usurpation (Ṭs). The scouts' sentence is fear disguised as intelligence. Every reconnaissance mission in history has faced this corruption: the scout who lets terror write his report disarms his own army more thoroughly than any enemy. Moses had assured them of victory with righteousness and obedience (cf. v. 12) — but assurance heard through fear sounds like fantasy. The verse diagnoses a permanent organizational disease: risk reports drafted by the faint-hearted become self-fulfilling prophecies of paralysis.

**"We Shall Not Enter Till They Leave": The Fantasy of Costless Victory**

*We shall not enter it till they leave it; but if they leave it, we shall enter* — the demand for a struggle-free triumph. Enemies who obligingly vacate, lands that conquer themselves, goals achieved without opposition: the Israelites articulate the daydream every fearful soul nurses. The Qur'an's answer, unfolding across the next verses, is that God does not work that way — not because He cannot (He could empty the land in an instant) but because *entering* is the point. The struggle is the formation; the victory without struggle would leave the victors unformed, still slaves in their souls though kings on the map. Modern readers meet the same demand in gentler forms — the student who wants knowledge without study, the reformer who wants change without conflict, the believer who wants paradise without striving. The verse holds up a mirror to all costless-victory fantasies: God prescribes lands, but He prescribes *entering* them.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:23

> **Two men whom God had blessed among those who feared [Him] said, "Enter upon them by the gate, for once you have entered it, you will be victors. And trust in God, if you are believers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Courageous Minority: Joshua and Caleb Against the Crowd**

Against ten fearful scouts stand *two men whom God had blessed among those who feared Him* — identified as Joshua (Yūshaʿ ibn Nūn) and Caleb (Kālib ibn Yūfannah), matching the Biblical account (R, Ṭ, Th, Z); a minor report makes them converted inhabitants who returned with the scouts (R, Ṭ). Ten to two: the mathematics of courage in every age. Majorities do not confer truth — they confer comfort — and the verse pointedly attributes the minority's stance to *blessing* (*anʿama'Llāhu ʿalayhimā*), not temperament. Courage here is grace before it is character. Their counsel is tactical as well as spiritual: *enter upon them by the gate* — assault the strongpoint directly rather than besieging it with dread; *once you have entered, you will be victors* — the decisive moment is the threshold, for commitment itself transforms the equation. Every difficult enterprise confirms it: the project begun in earnest generates resources the project merely contemplated never reveals. Beginnings are divinely subsidized; hesitations are self-taxed.

**Trust as the Proof of Belief**

*And trust in God, if you are believers* (*wa ʿala'Llāhi fa-tawakkalū in kuntum mu'minīn*) — trust (*tawakkul*) is made the *test* of belief, not its ornament. The conditional stings: to refuse trust when commanded is to fail the very thing one claims. The two men's formula binds means and reliance perfectly — *enter* (take the means, march to the gate) *and trust* (rest the outcome on God). Neither quietism (trust without entering) nor presumption (entering without trust) passes. The verse thus becomes a standing examination for every believer facing a fearful majority: when the ten speak prudence and the two speak trust, which voice will you amplify? History remembers the names of Joshua and Caleb; the ten have no names, only a number. Courage is remembered; panic is merely counted.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:24

> **They said, "O Moses! We shall never enter it, so long as they remain therein. So go forth, thou and thy Lord, and fight! We shall sit here."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Shall Sit Here": The Anatomy of Collective Cowardice**

*Lan nadkhulahā abadan* — "We shall never enter it" — *abadan*, never, ever: fear absolutized into policy. Then the sentence that rang through fourteen centuries of Muslim memory: *go forth, thou and thy Lord, and fight; we shall sit here* (*qāʿidūn*). *Go forth, thou and thy Lord* means "go with thy Lord's help," since God does not "go forth" corporeally (Ṭ). The insult is double: they draft the prophet for a war they refuse, and they speak of *thy* Lord — distancing themselves from the very God whose land they were promised. Sitting (*quʿūd*) becomes, in Qur'anic idiom, the posture of dereliction — the opposite of *striving* and *entering*. Psychologists of groups recognize the moment: responsibility diffused across a crowd evaporates entirely; each Israelite felt fractionally braver for sharing his cowardice with thousands. The verse warns every community that cowardice, pooled, feels like consensus. A thousand voices saying "we shall sit" do not make sitting wise; they make it unanimous, which is a different and far more dangerous thing.

**"We Shall Fight Alongside You": The Companions' Reversal**

The Prophet's Companions took this verse as a personal warning. At Ḥudaybiyah (6/628) — outnumbered, barred from Makkah, facing the powerful Quraysh — al-Miqdād ibn al-Aswad declared: "By God, we will not be like Israel when they said to their prophet, 'Go forth, thou and thy Lord, and fight; we shall sit here' — but [we say]: 'Go forth, thou and thy Lord, and fight; verily we shall fight alongside you'" (Ṭ; a variant attaches the scene to Badr, 2/624 (IK)). The Prophet's ﷺ face shone with joy at the words, the reports add. This is how scripture is meant to be read — not as ancient Israel's embarrassment but as every generation's examination. The Companions heard "we shall sit here" and resolved to stand; the reader who hears it as mere history has missed the examination entirely. Each generation gets its own gate, its own giants, its own choice between sitting and entering. The verse asks only which sentence will be yours.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:25

> **He said, "My Lord! I have no power over anyone save myself and my brother; so separate us from the iniquitous people!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet's Lament: Leadership at Its Limit**

Moses has preached blessing (v. 20), commanded entry (v. 21), watched his scouts spread panic (v. 22), seen his two faithful men overruled (v. 23), and heard his people's final refusal (v. 24). Now he turns from the people to God: *I have no power* (*lā amliku*) *over anyone save myself and my brother* — no control, and by extension no moral accountability, beyond himself and Aaron. Al-Zamakhsharī wonders why Joshua and Caleb are excluded: perhaps Moses, in despair, cannot vouch for anyone's loyalty but a fellow prophet's; or perhaps *brother* here is religious brotherhood — all faithful followers (Z). Either reading captures the loneliness of failed leadership. Every reformer, parent, and teacher reaches this hour: the message delivered perfectly, the audience unmoved, the limits of human agency exposed. Moses' response is the model — he does not coerce, manipulate, or despair of God; he *reports* to God. Prophets are not guarantors of results but deliverers of messages; outcomes belong to the Sender. The leader who learns this sleeps at night; the leader who confuses delivery with conversion burns out or turns tyrant.

**"Separate Us": The Prayer for Moral Distinction**

*So separate us* (*fa'fqur baynanā* — literally "decide, judge between us") *from the iniquitous people* (*al-qawm al-fāsiqīn*) — a plea for Divine judgment distinguishing the obedient from the rebellious (Ṭ, Z). *Fisq* is breach, rupture — the soul broken out of obedience's enclosure. Moses asks not for vengeance but for *distinction*: let it be clear, in history's record and eternity's court, that he and the faithful did not consent. The prayer matters because collective punishment was coming (v. 26), and the innocent dread being swept with the guilty. God's answer will show His precision: the land forbidden for forty years, yet Moses and Aaron sustained without hardship within the wandering (R, Z) — like Abraham in the cool fire (21:69). The lesson: when judgment falls on a community, God does not blur persons into crowds. The faithful suffer the weather of their people's winter, but the frost does not reach their roots.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:26

> **He said, "Then verily it shall be forbidden them for forty years, while they shall wander on the earth. So grieve not for the iniquitous people."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Forty Years of Wandering: Punishment as Pedagogy**

*Forbidden them for forty years* — the land (*muḥarramah ʿalayhim*), barred either physically or as devotional prohibition (R); *wandering* (*yatīhūn*) *on the earth*. The verse may also be read "forbidden them [permanently]; for forty years they shall wander" — permanent for the disobedient adults, who would die before entry (Ṭ). And so it happened: of that generation only Joshua and Caleb entered, under Joshua's later leadership as prophet after Moses (Q, Ṭ, Z; cf. Numbers 14); some reports have Moses himself entering Jericho and slaying a giant (R, Ṭ, Z). Why forty years? A generation must pass. Slaves freed from Egypt carried Egypt in their souls — the reflexes of the whipped cannot build a free polity. God did not re-educate them; He outwaited them, raising desert-born children without memories of chains. Organizations replicate the pattern: cultures of fear cannot be reformed by memo, only outgrown by new cohorts formed under new norms. The wandering was thus mercy disguised as penalty — forty years of manna, water from the rock (2:60; 7:160), and divine sustenance (Ṭ, Z) while a slave generation died and a free one grew. Sometimes God's kindest answer to "we cannot change" is: very well — your children will.

**"Grieve Not": Comfort for the Prophet Who Prayed the Prayer**

*So grieve not for the iniquitous people* — addressed to Moses, who was dismayed at the severity his own supplication had invoked and regretted it (R). The tenderness is extraordinary: God consoles the prophet for the answered prayer. It teaches that intercessors may plead for judgment in righteous frustration and then ache at judgment's weight — both the anger and the ache are human, and both are held by God. But the command stands: *grieve not*. Grief for the defiant, past its proper measure, becomes a quarrel with justice. The prophet must release his people to the consequences they chose — the land remains *prescribed* (v. 21) in the Tablet, held in suspension (Z), not cancelled. Judgment here is never God's last word about a people; it is His severe middle word. The last word belongs to mercy, in its season, for those who turn.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:27

> **And recite unto them, with truth, the account of Adam's two sons, when they each offered a sacrifice, and it was accepted from one of them, though not accepted from the other. One said, "I will surely slay you!" [The other] said, "God accepts only from the reverent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why Cain and Abel Here: Envy as the Sūrah's Hidden Theme**

*Recite unto them, with truth* (*bi'l-ḥaqq*) — the Cain-and-Abel account (cf. Genesis 4) arrives as humanity's prototype of fratricidal violence. But why *here*, in a sūrah about covenant and the People of the Book? The commentators trace the threads: *stretch forth hands* against the Prophet (v. 11) echoes the *stretching forth* of the murderous hand (v. 28); spiritual envy toward Muḥammad ﷺ (cf. 2:90, 109, 213; 3:19; 4:54; 45:17; 5:59) mirrors Cain's envy of Abel (R); and the account closes with a ruling *prescribed for the Children of Israel* (v. 32) on murder's gravity (Z). Al-Ṭabarī adds a pastoral purpose: Muslims are to answer plotters with Abel's forbearance, citing the ḥadīth, "God gives you the example of Adam's two sons; take the path of the better, leave the path of the worse" (Ṭ). The sūrah's deepest diagnosis thus surfaces: beneath doctrinal disputes and broken treaties runs the oldest poison — seeing another's acceptance and wanting him dead for it. Everyhonor killing, sectarian bombing, and assassination of the righteous is Cain's hand restretched.

**Two Sacrifices: What Made One Acceptable**

Cain (Qābīl) the crop-grower offered poor produce; Abel (Hābīl) the herdsman offered a prized fattened sheep (Ṭ) — some add a rivalry over marrying Cain's twin sister (R, Ṭ, Z), though al-Ṭabarī doubts this, insisting both sought nearness to God (Ṭ). *Qurbān* (sacrifice) comes from *q-r-b*, nearness: sacrifice is nearness-technology, the offering that bridges creature and Creator. Like Genesis, the Qur'an does not state why one was accepted — Abel himself supplies the criterion: *God accepts only from the reverent* (*muttaqīn*) — with proper *taqwā*, reverent fear (Ṭ, Z). The offering's quality revealed the heart's quality: Abel gave his best because he revered the Recipient; Cain gave his worst because he did not. Al-Ṭabarī extends the principle to Islam's own "sacrifices" — prayer, fasting, charity (*qarraba* can denote giving charity (R, Z)) — crowned by the ḥajj's own *ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā* slaughter, of which God says:

> *"Neither their flesh nor their blood reaches God, but the reverence from you reaches Him."* (Qur'an 22:37)

The altar tests nothing but the offerer. Two identical acts — two opposite receptions. The difference was never in the sheep or the grain; it was in the breast behind them.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:28

> **Even if you stretch forth your hand against me to slay me, I shall not stretch forth my hand against you to slay you. Truly I fear God, Lord of the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abel's Refusal: The Strongest Weakness in Scripture**

*Even if you stretch forth your hand against me to slay me, I shall not stretch forth mine against you* — Abel's sentence has puzzled and inspired for fourteen centuries. Why not resist? The commentators offer layers: to expose the enormity of fratricide by refusing to dignify it as combat (R, Ṭ); because defensive killing was not yet legislated in humanity's dawn (Ṭ, Z); or — most subtly — Abel renounces only the *intent to kill*, whether preemptively or in self-defense, not necessarily all self-protection (Q, R). Some reports have Cain striking the sleeping Abel, mooting the question (Q, R); others suggest Abel judged being killed better than killing (R). Al-Qurṭubī records the verse's afterlife: pious early Muslims like ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar, refusing to take sides in the First Civil War (36–40/656–61), cited Abel — greater strength lies in abstaining from violence against a fellow monotheist, even a disobedient one, than in joining it. The verse does not abolish self-defense (elsewhere legislated); it establishes its limit. There are conflicts — brother against brother, Muslim against Muslim — where victory costs more than defeat, and the refusal to kill, even at the price of being killed, is the last prophetic witness available. Abel lost his life and won eternity's argument.

**"I Fear God, Lord of the Worlds": Reverence as Restraint**

Abel's reason is one clause: *innī akhāfu'Llāha rabba'l-ʿālamīn* — "Truly I fear God, Lord of the worlds." Not "I fear you," not "I am unable," but God-fearing. *Khawf* here is reverent dread — the awareness that the universe has a Lord to whom hands will answer. Notice *Lord of the worlds* (*rabb al-ʿālamīn*): Abel invokes God's universal lordship at the moment his brother acts as though no Lord existed. Every murderer is a practical atheist for the duration of the murder; every refuser of murder, a theologian. The sentence also quietly universalizes the story beyond two brothers in a field: the Lord of *all worlds* watches *every* stretched hand in *every* age. Street violence, domestic brutality, state terror — each perpetrator stretches a hand Abel refused to stretch, under the gaze of the Lord Abel feared. Civilization advances exactly to the extent that Abel's sentence multiplies in human breasts.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:29

> **I desire that you should be burdened with my sin and your sin and so become one of the inhabitants of the Fire. Such is the recompense of the wrongdoers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"My Sin and Your Sin": Unpacking Abel's Hard Sentence**

*I desire that you bear my sin and your sin* — the verse's most exegetically delicate clause. It cannot mean transferred guilt, for the Qur'an is adamant that *no bearer bears another's burden* (6:164; 17:15; 35:18; 39:7; 53:38). The commentators resolve it by ellipsis: "my sin" means *the sin of killing me* (*ithm qatlī*, with *qatl* elided), while "your sin" means Cain's accumulated disobedience (R, Ṭ, Z). Abel thus declares he will add no counter-violence for which *he* would answer, leaving Cain to carry the full weight of both the murder and his prior record. "I desire" (*innī urīdu*) is not vindictiveness but forensic clarity — Abel states the outcome, leaving the choice to Cain. There is a courtroom gravity here: the victim, refusing vengeance, simply reads the sentence the crime carries. The murderer who expected a fight receives instead a verdict.

**The First Eschatology: Fire for the Wrongdoer**

*So become one of the inhabitants of the Fire; such is the recompense of the wrongdoers* (*ẓālimīn*) — among the Qur'an's earliest narrated warnings of Hell, placed on righteous lips at humanity's dawn. *Ẓulm* (wrongdoing) is putting things where they do not belong — and murder is *ẓulm*'s archetype: a soul removed from its body before its term, a brother made into a corpse. That the warning comes from the victim rather than a prophet gives it unique pathos: Abel evangelizes his killer even while refusing to fight him, offering truth to ears about toclose forever. The Prophet ﷺ later confirmed Abel's doctrine of murder's cumulative burden:

> *"No soul is killed unjustly but that a share of its blood is upon the first son of Adam, for he was the first to institute killing."* (al-Bukhārī 3335; Muslim 1677)

Every murderer pays his own crime; the institution's founder pays a commission on all. Cain's innovation — solving envy by homicide — became humanity's most franchised sin, and the franchise fees accumulate against him still.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:30

> **Then his soul prompted him to slay his brother, and he slew him, and thus came to be among the losers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Soul That Commands Evil: Inside the First Murderer**

*Fa-ṭawwaʿat lahu nafsuhu qatla akhīh* — "Then his soul *prompted* (literally: made easy, made compliant, emboldened) him to slay his brother" (Aj, R, Ṭ, Z). *Ṭawwaʿa* is the psychology of temptation in one verb: the soul does not merely suggest evil but *facilitates* it — smoothing objections, minimizing horror, supplying technique (some reports: Satan showed him the two rocks; the victim slept (R, Ṭ, Z)). *Nafs* here is the lower soul, the seat of passion:

> *"Surely the soul commands to evil."* (Qur'an 12:53, Joseph's confession)

Al-Qushayrī reflects that once the soul, driven by vain desire, has settled its evil intention (v. 27), subsequent appeals — Abel's whole noble speech — arrive muted, as though heard underwater (Qu). Neuroscience now describes the same sequence:resolve forms, then rationalization recruits itself, then empathy circuits dim. The verse is thus a case study in how murder becomes possible — not in a moment but by a *process*, the soul's gradual compliance-training. Every killer was first a rehearsed: the intention entertained, the victim dehumanized, the act facilitated. The time to arrest murder is at the *ṭawwīʿ* stage — when the soul first starts making it easy. Once facilitation completes, hands follow automatically.

**"Among the Losers": The Arithmetic of Eternity**

*Fa-aṣbaḥa min al-khāsirīn* — "and thus came to be among the losers." He gained the field and lost everything: his brother, his peace (Adam, the reports say, never laughed again (R, Z)), and his Hereafter. *Khusrān* (loss) is the Qur'an's term for the trade where one surrenders eternity for appetite — the worst exchange rate in existence. Cain's story is the template: every sin trades infinite capital for finite pleasure, and the pleasure expires while the invoice does not. The verse's terseness is its power — *he slew him* (*faqatalahu*), two syllables in Arabic for the act that invented homicide. Scripture refuses to sensationalize the killing; it will not grant violence the dignity of description. The camera, so to speak, looks away from the gore and fixes on the consequence: loss. All murder mysteries end with the discovery of the body; this one ends with the discovery of the loser.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:31

> **Then God sent a crow, scratching the earth, to show him how he might conceal his brother's nakedness. He said, "Oh, woe unto me! Am I not able to be even as this crow and conceal my brother's nakedness?" And he came to be among the remorseful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Crow as Teacher: When a Bird Outranks a Murderer**

Abel was, the commentators say, the first human to die — Cain knew killing but not burial (Ṭ, Z). So *God sent a crow, scratching* (*yabḥathu* — digging, searching) *the earth* — burying another dead crow, in most reports (Ṭ) — *to show him how to conceal his brother's nakedness* (*saw'ah*: corpse, shame, exposedness; al-Zamakhsharī stresses shame, al-Ṭabarī corpse (R, Ṭ, Z)). The scene's irony is bottomless: the creature that reasoned, sacrificed, envied, and murdered must take instruction from a bird following instinct. Humanity's first school of funeral rites had a crow for a master and a killer for its only pupil. God thereby establishes burial as a divinely taught rite — the dead are concealed with dignity, shielded from beasts and exposure — while humbling the murderer beneath the animal kingdom. Knowledge unused for good degrades below instinct: the crow, knowing nothing, did right; Cain, knowing good and evil, did wrong and then could not even manage the aftermath. Every tyrant who must be taught basic decency by his victims' simple dignity reenacts this field.

**"Woe Unto Me": Remorse That May or May Not Be Repentance**

*Yā waylatī* — "Oh, woe unto me!" — the Qur'an's cry of realized ruin (2:79; 18:49; 21:14, 47, 97; 25:28; 36:52; 37:20; 68:31). *Am I unable to be even as this crow?* — the first recorded instance of self-contempt, and *he came to be among the remorseful* (*nādimīn*). But remorse (*nadam*) is not yet repentance (*tawbah*): remorse looks backward at the exposed self, repentance turns forward to God. Cain grieves his shame and helplessness; the text records no turning, no plea, no *Lord, forgive me*. The distinction diagnoses countless modern sorrows — the caught corrupt official's press-conference tears, the exposed abuser's "apology" that centers his own suffering. True repentance names the wrong *against God*, makes amends *to victims*, and changes *course*; remorse merely aches at consequences. Cain buried his brother having learned burial from a bird — whether he ever learned repentance, the Book pointedly does not say. The silence is the warning.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:32

> **For this reason, We prescribed for the Children of Israel that whosoever slays a soul — unless it be for another soul or working corruption upon the earth — it is as though he slew mankind altogether, and whosoever saves the life of one, it is as though he saved the life of mankind altogether. Our messengers have certainly come unto them with clear proofs. Yet even after that, many of them are prodigal on the earth.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Soul as All Souls: The Arithmetic of the Sacred**

*For this reason* (*min ajli dhālika*) — because of Cain's fratricide — *We prescribed for the Children of Israel*: to kill one soul unjustly is *as though he slew mankind altogether*; to save one is *as though he saved mankind altogether*. The Mishnah and Talmud preserve the same teaching rooted in the same story, making this verse a documented bridge between the Qur'an and rabbinic ethics. The commentators unfold the equivalence several ways: the sanctity violated is one — God's hallowing of life — whether the victim count is one or millions (Q); the murderer of a prophet or just imām kills guidance itself (Q, Ṭ); Hell's sentence for murdering a believer is not multiplied by body count, since one such murder already earns the maximum (Q, Ṭ, via Mujāhid); and the earthly penalty is likewise one life for lives taken (Q, Ṭ). Beneath all readings runs one principle: *human life is not priced by quantity*. Utilitarianism counts lives; revelation weighs them — and each soul, bearing the divine breath, weighs infinite. A civilization that internalizes this verse cannot produce body-count morality, collateral-damage euphemisms, or "acceptable losses." One is all. That is the whole ethic.

**The Two Exceptions: Justice and the Defense of Order**

*Unless it be for another soul* — lawful retribution for murder (and, by extension, other capital crimes such as married adultery (R)) — *or working corruption upon the earth* (*fasād*) — idolatry-as-treason, war against the community, terrorizing roads (Q, Ṭ, Z; cf. v. 33). The exceptions prove the rule's seriousness: life may be taken only by *lawful authority* (*the state, the court*), for *defined causes*, through *due process* — never by private hands, mobs, or vigilantes. Cain is precisely what the exceptions exclude: personal killing, however provoked. And *saving* a life spans the direct (rescue from fire or drowning (Ṭ, Z)), the legal (the victim's kin forgoing retaliation for compensation or forgiveness (2:178; Ṭ, Z)), and — in the widest reading — the spiritual and economic: lifting one from poverty or guiding one from error (Qm). The verse thus commissions every reader twice: *do not destroy; do rescue*. Non-violence is the floor; life-saving is the calling. The paramedic, the peacemaker, the poverty-lifter, the suicide-preventer — each, by this verse's arithmetic, has saved not one but all.

**Why Israel? The Universality Inside the Particularity**

The prescription is addressed *to the Children of Israel* — some say because they were first threatened with eternal punishment for murder (Q); al-Rāzī ties it to Madinan plots against the Prophet ﷺ (R). But all agree the principle binds Muslims equally (IK, Q, Ṭ) — indeed all humanity, since its rationale (life's sanctity) precedes and exceeds any one community. The verse closes in sorrow: *Our messengers came to them with clear proofs, yet even after that, many are prodigal* (*musrifūn* — excessive, wasteful) *on earth*. *Isrāf* (prodigality) is expenditure beyond limit — here, the spending of blood beyond all bounds. Messengers brought the clearest proofs; still the extravagant kept spending lives. Revelation can prescribe, warn, and promise — it cannot compel. The prodigal with blood, like the prodigal with wealth, must choose thrift himself. The verse leaves him the arithmetic and walks away.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:33

> **Verily the recompense of those who wage war against God and His Messenger, and endeavor to work corruption upon the earth is that they be killed or crucified, or have their hands and feet cut off from opposite sides, or be banished from the land. That is their disgrace in this world, and in the Hereafter theirs shall be a great punishment,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥirābah: The Crime of Terrorizing the Road**

This verse ordains one of the Qur'an's five *ḥudūd* (Divinely fixed penalties), for *ḥirābah* — waging war (*muḥārabah*) against God and His Messenger, understood as armed terror against the community, since none can literally war against God (Q). The crime comprises armed robbery, assault (including rape), and murder — especially of travelers — distinguished by weapons, extreme violence, and the deliberate spreading of fear; cities are included with highways by scholarly consensus. *Working corruption* (*fasād*) here is thus technically narrowed to violent predation, though the Qur'an elsewhere widens *fasād* to spiritual ruin (2:11–12, 27; 7:56; 10:40, 81; 16:88) — the angels' primordial forecast that vicegerents would "work corruption and shed blood" (2:30). Cain's story (vv. 27–31) is the archetype; *ḥirābah* is its professionalization. The law's target is precise: not dissent, not sin, not even ordinary theft — but *armed terror that makes roads impassable and societies unlivable*. Every age names it differently — brigandage, banditry, terrorism — but the Qur'anic category captures what all share: violence as a livelihood and fear as a product.

**The ʿUraynah Incident: The Occasion That Fixed the Law**

The widest-attested occasion: men of ʿUraynah and ʿUkl feigned conversion, complained to the Prophet ﷺ that Madinah's climate sickened them as Bedouin, were lent milch-camels with a herdsman — then outside the city maimed and murdered the herdsman and stole the camels. Apprehended, they were punished by amputation, blinding, and exposure till death (Q, Ṭ, W). The verse then fixed the four penalties: execution, crucifixion (public exposure), cross-amputation, banishment (cf. Pharaoh's threats, 20:71). Commentators divide on the relation: clarification or endorsement of the Prophet's ﷺ act (Ṭ); partial correction, since blinding and death-exposure are absent here — some reading an implicit ban on torture (Q, Ṭ), noting the Prophet ﷺ never tortured before or after (Q); or the blinding as *qiṣāṣ* (equal retribution) for the herdsman's maiming specifically (Q, Ṭ). A small minority extended the verse to apostates generally since the criminals had also apostatized (Ṭ) — but the mainstream confines it to *violent terror*, not belief-change. The law punishes what hands do to travelers, not what hearts do with convictions.

**Graduated Penalties and the Ban on Torture**

Al-Shāfiʿī and others correlated penalty to crime: murder-without-theft → execution; theft-without-murder → cross-amputation; both → execution plus crucifixion-exposure; terror-without-either → banishment; a minority left the choice to the ruler's discretion (JJ, Q, Ṭ, Ṭs). Juristic humanity hedged the penalties round: crucifixion historically meant post-execution public exposure as deterrence, not execution by cross; those permitting pre-execution exposure required near-immediate killing lest suffering become torture, which is forbidden (Q, Ṭs); banishment was read as pursuit-into-exile, territorial transfer, or — most humanely — imprisonment until repentance, first implemented by ʿUmar (Q). *That is their disgrace in this world, and in the Hereafter great punishment* (cf. 5:41; 2:114; 9:74; 13:33–34). Two reflections close the matter. First, severity here protects the vulnerable: the severed hand of one highwayman keeps a thousand travelers' hands on their goods. Second, even this severest law keeps mercy's door open — the very next verse says so.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:34

> **save those who repent before you overpower them. And know that God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Repentance Before Capture: The Door That Stays Open**

*Save those who repent before you overpower them* — even *ḥirābah*, the Qur'an's gravest public crime, yields to timely repentance. The parallel is deathbed theology: repentance avails before capture as it avails before death, but not after the sentence becomes certain (4:17–18). Once the criminal is caught, convicted, and brought before authority, the *ḥadd* becomes mandatory in most schools; but if victims forgive and the matter never reaches the authorities, punishment may be averted (Q, Ṭ). Note the precision: repentance lifts the *Divine* penalty (the ḥadd) and the Hereafter's punishment, but victims' rights persist — stolen property must be restored, and *qiṣāṣ* (retribution) or compensation remains claimable (cf. 2:178). God forgives His rights freely; human rights require human forgiveness or restitution. The architecture is exquisite: mercy for the criminal, justice for the victim, deterrence for society — all three served without sacrificing any. And the higher path is named by implication: victims who forgive outright participate in the very Names sealing the verse — *Forgiving, Merciful* — turning their injury into an imitation of God.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:35

> **O you who believe! Reverence God, and seek the means to approach Him, and strive in His way, that haply you may prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Wasīlah: The Means of Approach to God**

*Seek the means* (*wasīlah*) *to approach Him* — *wasīlah* is whatever draws one near: a station, a deed, a door. Nearness (*qurb*) to God is the Qur'an's loftiest goal and reward (17:57; 38:25, 40), earned by obedience and virtue (9:99; 34:37), possessed in highest rank by *those brought nigh* (*muqarrabūn*; 56:11, 88; 83:21, 28). Coming right after the repentance verse, *wasīlah* may hint at the criminal's repentance or the victim's forgiveness as approaches to God. The celebrated ḥadīth qudsī defines the hierarchy:

> *"My servant draws not nigh unto Me with anything I love more than what I have made obligatory upon him. And My servant continues drawing nigh unto Me with supererogatory works until I love him..."* (al-Bukhārī 6502)

Obligations first, supererogation second — the order corrects both the minimalist (obligations only, no love-offerings) and the enthusiast (voluntary excess atop neglected duties). Sufi commentators add the interior means — patience, contentment, sincerity (Su, Qu) — and Ibn ʿAjībah the guide (*shaykh*) who helps purge the soul's impurities. False *wasīlahs* — intercessors of invention (39:3; 46:28) — are rebuked. The verse thus maps the entire spiritual path in three words: *reverence* (the guard), *approach* (the journey), *strive* (the effort).

**Striving in His Way: Jihād in Its Native Habitat**

*Strive in His way* (*jāhidū fī sabīlihi*) — the Qur'an's constant injunction (2:218; 4:95; 8:72, 74; 9:20; 49:15). *Jihād* (striving, exertion) here sits between reverence and prosperity, framed by approach-to-God — its native habitat is devotion, not aggression. Al-Qushayrī reads it purely spiritually: perfect obedience, detachment from worldliness, witnessing God at every moment (Qu). The mainstream holds both the martial and spiritual senses in their proper places: armed struggle under lawful authority against aggression, and the greater struggle against the soul's own *ḥirābah* — its inner highwaymen of lust, rage, and heedlessness. *That haply you may prosper* (*tufliḥūn*) — *falāḥ* is the farmer's success (*falḥ*: to split earth for seed): prosperity as harvest, not windfall. Reverence plants, approach waters, striving tills — and God gives the increase.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:36

> **Truly those who disbelieve, were they to possess all that is on the earth and the like of it besides with which to ransom themselves from the punishment of the Day of Resurrection, it would not be accepted from them. And theirs shall be a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ransom That Cannot Be Paid: Wealth's Final Bankruptcy**

*Were they to possess all that is on earth and the like besides* — double the world's wealth — *as ransom* (*fidyah*) *from the Day's punishment, it would not be accepted*. The Qur'an hammers this futility across a dozen passages (2:48, 123; 3:91; 10:54; 13:18; 39:47; 57:15; 70:11). Why the repetition? Because wealth's power in *this* world is so total — buying lawyers, legislators, and luxuries — that the soul naturally assumes it spends in the next. The verse declares the ultimate currency reform: on that Day, earthly riches are demonetized absolutely. The Prophet ﷺ illustrated with God's question to Hell's lightest sufferer:

> *"If you possessed all on earth, would you ransom yourself with it?" He will say, "Yes." God will say, "I asked you for less than that when you were in Adam's loins — that you associate nothing with Me — but you refused."* (al-Bukhārī 3340; Muslim 2805, paraphrased)

The economics of salvation invert worldly economics: here the poor struggle and the rich glide; there, only *taqwā* spends, and it was free to all. Every act of worship is thus a foreign-exchange transaction — converting perishable hours into imperishable currency before the markets close.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:37

> **They will wish to come forth from the Fire, but they shall not come forth from it. And theirs shall be a lasting punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wish That Defines Damnation**

*They will wish* (*yurīdūn*) *to come forth from the Fire, but they shall not come forth* — the damned retain desire but lose efficacy. Will without power is the precise formula of Hell: on earth, wishing can become striving and striving can become escape; there, the machinery connecting wish to outcome is disconnected. *Theirs shall be a lasting* (*muqīm* — established, abiding) *punishment*. The verse's terror lies in its stillness — no pealing thunder, just a door that does not open. Theologians across schools affirmed the Fire's eternity for persistent disbelievers while debating its edges; the Qur'an's own emphasis is pastoral rather than speculative: *you can still come forth now*. Every sermon, every Ramadan, every moment of repentance is a door still open. The verse shows the door closed only so that readers will walk through it while it stands wide. Regret in this life is a vehicle; regret in the next is a venue. The wise transport themselves with the first before they are housed in the second.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:38

> **As for the male thief and the female thief, cut off their hands as a recompense for what they have earned, as an exemplary punishment from God. Truly God is Mighty, Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ḥadd for Theft: Severity in Its Legal Habitat**

*As for the male thief and female thief, cut off their hands* — another of the Qur'an's five *ḥudūd* (cf. v. 33), applied equally to both sexes (as with adultery, 24:2–3). The Prophet ﷺ stressed its universality:

> *"By God, if Fāṭimah the daughter of Muḥammad were to steal, I would cut off her hand."* (al-Bukhārī 3475; Muslim 1688)

Al-Qurṭubī notes amputation was also pre-Islamic Arabia's penalty — revelation regulated rather than invented it. But the *ḥadd* operates inside a dense thicket of conditions developed from ḥadīth and early caliphal precedent: a minimum threshold (*niṣāb*) — commonly a quarter-dīnār (a gram of gold) or three dirhams, or a shield's worth (Q, Ṭ); theft by stealth from secure custody (*ḥirz*), excluding embezzlement, snatching, and familial taking; absence of doubt (*shubhah*) — hunger (ʿUmar suspended the penalty in famine), need, disputed ownership, or necessity all void it; and full due process with testimony or confession. Below the threshold: restitution and discretionary discipline; al-Shāfiʿī held restitution mandatory even with amputation (Q). The procedure must preserve life — bleeding stanched, death prevented. What emerges is not a chopping regime but a rarely-applied maximum penalty whose *existence* deters while its *conditions* mercifully restrict. Classical crime statistics are unavailable, but the design logic is clear: make the penalty severe enough to protect property, and the proof strict enough to protect suspects.

**"Exemplary Punishment": Deterrence as Public Mercy**

*Nakālan mina'Llāh* — "an exemplary punishment from God": *nakāl* is deterrent chastisement, punishment-as-warning-to-others. The *ḥudūd* are God's rights against crimes that destabilize society itself — each serves as *recompense* (*jazā'*) for the criminal and *deterrence* for the public. The modern reader must weigh the system's own moral claim: a penalty that keeps theft near zero across a society protects millions of poor owners for whom a stolen goat or tool is catastrophe. Whether one accepts amputation or not, the verse forces the question every penal theory must answer — *deterrence for whom?* The Qur'anic answer prioritizes the vulnerable many over the convicted few. *Truly God is Mighty* (*ʿAzīz* — irresistible in executing judgment), *Wise* (*Ḥakīm* — placing each penalty exactly where wisdom requires). Might without wisdom would be tyranny; wisdom without might, wishfulness. The pairing seals every ḥadd verse: severity exactly measured, never vengeful, never lax.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:39

> **But whosoever repents after his wrongdoing, and makes amends, God will relent unto him. Truly God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Repentance Plus Repair: The Two Wings of Return**

*Whosoever repents after his ẓulm* (wrongdoing) *and makes amends* (*aṣlaḥa* — sets right, repairs) — repentance here has two wings: the inward turn (*tāba*) and the outward repair (*aṣlaḥa*; al-Qurṭubī: abandoning all disobedience). The thief must return the goods or compensate; the verse refuses cheap grace. *God will relent unto him* (*yatūbu'Llāhu ʿalayh*) — literally "God will turn toward him": repentance is a turning met by a Turning, the human rotation answered by Divine approach. Commentators debated whether repentance before capture lifts the amputation: some said yes, others that God's "relenting" concerns the Hereafter while the ḥadd proceeds (Q, Ṭ) — with aḥādīth assuring punished-and-repentant thieves of forgiveness and purification (IK). Abū Ḥanīfah and al-Shāfiʿī held the penalty avertable only by the victim's forgiveness before the case reaches authority (JJ, Z). The architecture mirrors verse 34: God's rights yield to repentance; human rights require restitution. *Forgiving, Merciful* closes the severest penal passage in the sūrah — the Qur'an will not let the reader leave the courtroom without passing the mercy-seat. Justice amputates the hand; mercy reattaches the soul.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:40

> **Dost thou not know that unto God belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth? He punishes whomsoever He will, and He forgives whomsoever He will. And God is Powerful over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sovereignty as the Last Word on Punishment**

*A-lam taʿlam* — "Dost thou not know?" — the question is rhetorical, addressed to the Prophet ﷺ and through him to every reader: *unto God belongs sovereignty* (*mulk* — dominion and ownership) *over heavens and earth*. Al-Rāzī reads here the Ashʿarī thesis in miniature: God's right to punish some and forgive others rests on Creator-ownership — the potter's right over clay — even where human minds cannot trace the wisdom (R). The verse closes the sūrah's penal section (vv. 33–39) by lifting eyes from earthly courts to the Ultimate Court: human judges execute fixed penalties; God alone dispenses final deserts. Two errors die here. Presumption dies: no one can claim forgiveness as wages — it is *whomsoever He will*. Despair dies: no one is beyond forgiveness as though sin created entitlement to punishment — it is equally *whomsoever He will*. Between the two wills stretches the entire life of faith: strive as though all depended on striving, hope as though all depended on mercy. *Powerful over all things* (*ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr*) — including the pardon of the worst thief and the punishment of the most polished hypocrite. The gavel is His; the courtroom below is a shadow of the Court above.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:41

> **O Messenger! Let them not grieve thee, those who hasten unto disbelief, those who say, "We believe" with their mouths, while their hearts believe not, and those who are Jews, who listen to lies and to others who have not come to thee. They distort the meaning of the word, saying, "If you are given this, then take it, but if you are not given this, then beware!" For whomsoever God desires that he be tried, thou hast no power to avail him aught against God. They are those whose hearts God desired not to purify. Theirs is disgrace in this world, and in the Hereafter they shall have a great punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Let Them Not Grieve Thee": Consolation for the Rejected Warner**

*Lā yaḥzunka* — "Let them not grieve thee." The Prophet ﷺ grieved over rejecters the way a physician grieves over patients who refuse cure — and God repeatedly consoles him (v. 68; 3:176; 6:33; 10:65; 16:127; 31:23; 36:76). Here the grief's objects are two: hypocrites — *those who say "we believe" with mouths while hearts believe not*, *hastening* (*yusāriʿūn* — racing, competing) *unto disbelief* — and hostile Madinan Jews — *listeners to lies*, whether believing rabbis' misrepresentations or listening to the Prophet ﷺ only to deny him (R). Some construe the groups separately: "while their hearts believe not. Those who are Jews listen to lies" (R, Z). The consolation's logic unfolds at the verse's center: guidance is God's act, not the warner's achievement. The messenger delivers; God disposes. Every teacher, parent, and preacher must internalize this division of labor or break: responsibility for *delivery* is total, responsibility for *reception* is zero. Grief beyond that border is a trespass on Divine prerogative — love the student, perfect the lesson, and release the outcome.

**The Adulterers of Khaybar: A Case Study in Scriptural Fraud**

The widest-reported occasion: two Jewish adulterers from Khaybar (or Fadak) were brought to the Prophet ﷺ by Madinan Jews; asked for the Torah's ruling, they concealed its stoning penalty, coached from afar by Khaybar Jews — *listeners to others who have not come to thee* — with the instruction: *if you are given this* (flogging) *take it; if not* (stoning), *beware* (Ṭ, W, Z). The Prophet ﷺ demanded the Torah; the reader covered the stoning verse with his hand until ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām exposed it; the young rabbi Ibn Ṣūriyā confirmed it; the Prophet ﷺ ordered stoning as revival of the Torah's own ruling (Ṭ, Z). Their leaders had substituted flogging-plus-humiliation when reluctant to stone high-status offenders (Ṭ, Z). *Distorting the word from its places* (*yuḥarrifūna'l-kalima min baʿdi mawāḍiʿihi*) — moving text from context (JJ) — is *taḥrīf* in its literal form (cf. 2:75; 4:46; 5:13). A minority report ties vv. 41–47 to a Naḍīr–Qurayẓah dispute over blood-money versus execution across tribal ranks (Q, Ṭ). Either way the disease is one: scripture edited to flatter the powerful. Every communion that softens texts for donors and sharpens them for dissidents reenacts Khaybar.

**Trial, Unpurified Hearts, and the Two Disgraces**

*For whomsoever God desires trial* (*fitnah* — trial, temptation, or being left in error), *thou hast no power to avail him aught against God* — when God leaves one to error as consequence of evil choices, no intercession, not even the Prophet's ﷺ, overrides it (R, Ṭ). *Those whose hearts God desired not to purify* — refusing them spiritual aid as consequence of their deeds, or refusing to cleanse the passions driving their denial (R). *Theirs is disgrace in this world* — exposure, like the uncovered hand on the scroll — *and great punishment in the Hereafter* (cf. v. 33). The verse's severity must be read whole: it condemns specific deceivers and hypocrites, not Jews as such — verse 5 licenses their food and marriage, verse 44 honors their Torah as guidance and light. The Qur'an's critique is prosecutorial, not racial: it names acts (lying, concealing, forum-shopping for lenient verdicts), tries them publicly, and sentences them — while leaving the door open for every Jew or hypocrite who abandons the acts.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:42

> **Those who listen to lies, and consume what is unlawful — if they come to thee, then judge between them, or turn away from them. If thou turnest away from them, they will not harm thee in the least. But if thou judgest, judge between them with justice. Truly God loves the just.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bribery and the Corruption of Judgment**

*Listeners to lies, consumers of the unlawful* (*suḥt* — *suḥt*, illicit gain, specifically bribery (Ṭ)) — the pairing is causal: ears that consume lies produce hands that consume bribes. Bribery among the Prophet's ﷺ Jewish contemporaries recurs in verses 62–63. The Prophet ﷺ cursed both ends of the transaction:

> *"God's curse is upon the briber and the bribed."* (Abū Dāwūd 3580; al-Tirmidhī 1337; Ibn Mājah 2313; Aḥmad)

Why does the Qur'an treat bribery as a theological crisis rather than a regulatory one? Because bribery privatizes justice — converting the court from God's scales into a market stall. A judge who sells verdicts does not merely err; he abolishes the very idea that right has claims independent of price. Societies can survive many corruptions, but the sale of judgment dissolves the last referee — after which only force arbitrates. The verse's placement is thus strategic: before discussing *how* the Prophet ﷺ should judge (with justice), it establishes *why* his judgment is needed — because purchasable justice is no justice at all.

**To Judge or Not to Judge: Jurisdiction Over Non-Muslim Disputes**

*If they come to thee, judge between them or turn away* — the Prophet ﷺ (and by extension Islamic authority) may adjudicate intra-non-Muslim disputes or decline them to their own communal courts. Al-Shāfiʿī and Mālik upheld this option for cases involving no Muslim; cases involving a Muslim must be heard — and then by Islamic law (Q, Ṭ; cf. vv. 48–49). Some held the option abrogated by verse 48's *judge between them* (Q, Ṭ); others kept it. Beneath the jurisprudence lies a pluralist architecture: religious communities retain internal legal autonomy, with the Islamic state as optional appellate forum — and a mandatory one where Muslims are parties. *If thou turnest away, they will not harm thee in the least* — declining jurisdiction carries no risk; God's protection covers the judge who refuses to be used. *But if thou judgest, judge with justice* (*bi'l-qisṭ*; cf. 4:58, 135; 4:65) — no communal favoritism, no forum-shopper's discount. *God loves the just* (*muqsiṭīn*; cf. 60:8) — justice here is not procedural nicety but a loves-of-God virtue. The judge who rules fairly for a hostile litigant has done something God *loves* — a motive beyond any judicial code.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:43

> **And how is it that they come to thee for judgment, when they have the Torah, wherein is God's Judgment? Yet even after that, they turn their backs, and they are not believers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Forum-Shopping for Verdicts: The Question That Exposes Motive**

*How is it that they come to thee for judgment when they have the Torah, wherein is God's Judgment?* The question is not about jurisdiction but sincerity: litigants who possess a clear ruling yet shop for another forum want not justice but leniency (Ṭ). The Khaybar adulterers' case (v. 41) is the exhibit — the Torah prescribed stoning; they sought flogging from Muḥammad ﷺ. The verse universalizes the exposure: whenever parties bypass their own clear law for a hoped-for softer verdict, the maneuver confesses what argument denies — that they know what the law says and wish it said otherwise. Modern analogues abound: jurisdiction-shopping corporations, fatwa-shopping consumers of scholarship, partisans who cite principles only when convenient. The verse's question follows them all: *you have the Book — why are you here?* If the answer is genuine doubt, welcome; the Prophet ﷺ is a judge. If the answer is verdict-hunting, the question itself is the verdict.

**"They Are Not Believers": Disbelief as Disobedience to One's Own Book**

*Yet even after that, they turn their backs* — after the Prophet's ﷺ ruling (or after the Torah's own clarity), they withdraw — *and they are not believers*. The charge is devastating in its specificity: their disbelief is measured not against Islam's claims but against *their own* scripture's demands. One need not accept Muḥammad ﷺ to stand condemned here; it suffices to betray Moses while claiming him. The verse thus establishes a principle the sūrah will press repeatedly (vv. 44, 45, 47, 66, 68): each community is accountable to the revelation it *acknowledges*. The Torah remains, in this verse's frank admission, a vessel of *God's Judgment* — valid, binding, luminous — even in the Prophet's ﷺ own presence. The Qur'an does not say "your Book is void, so submit to mine"; it says "your Book is true, so submit to *it*." That is a far more searching challenge — and, for interfaith relations, a far more respectful one.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:44

> **Truly We sent down the Torah, wherein is a guidance and a light, by which the prophets who submitted [unto God] judged those who are Jews, as did the sages and the rabbis, in accordance with such of God's Book as they were bidden to preserve and to which they were witnesses. So fear not mankind, but fear Me! And sell not My signs for a paltry price. Whosoever judges not by that which God has sent down — it is they who are disbelievers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Torah as Guidance and Light: The Qur'an's Tribute**

*Truly We sent down the Torah, wherein is guidance and light* (*hudan wa nūr*; cf. 6:91) — the Qur'an's tribute to Moses' Book, ungrudging and explicit. By it judged *the prophets who submitted* (*aslamū* — Israelite prophets between Moses and Jesus (Z); a few read Muḥammad ﷺ via *islām* (Ṭ)), *the sages* (*rabbāniyyūn* — legal authorities (Ṭ), Aaronide ascetics/priests (Z), or supreme jurisprudents (R)) and *rabbis* (*aḥbār* — scholars). They judged *by what they were bidden to preserve* (*istuhfiẓū*) *of God's Book, to which they were witnesses* (*shuhadā'*). Two offices: *preservation* (guarding text and meaning) and *witness* (testifying it publicly). Scholarship that preserves without witnessing is hoarding; witness without preservation is noise. The verse quietly defines the Rabbinate's — and by extension every clergy's — job description: keep the Book intact, speak it aloud. Whereverscribes altered penalties for the powerful (v. 41) or swallowed rulings for bribes (v. 42), they failed both offices at once.

**"Sell Not My Signs": The Economics of Religious Betrayal**

*Fear not mankind, but fear Me! Sell not My signs for a paltry price* (*thamanan qalīlan*) — the Qur'an's standing idiom for trading eternal truth for worldly gain (2:41, 79, 174; 3:77, 187, 199; 9:9; 16:95). Two motives for distortion are named: *fear* (of the powerful whose adultery or murder the text condemns) and *price* (wealth, status, position). Every pulpit faces the same two bidders: intimidation and purchase. The verse prices the transaction honestly — whatever is gained is *paltry* (*qalīl*), however large it looks: all the world's gold against one verse of God is small change against a kingdom. *Whosoever judges not by what God sent down — they are the disbelievers* (*kāfirūn*) — *kāfir* also meaning *coverer*: those who cover scripture's rulings (Ṭ). Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have calibrated the trilogy across verses 44, 45, 47: active rejection of God's judgment is disbelief; mere failure to apply it is wrongdoing (*ẓulm*) and iniquity (*fisq*) (Z). The calibration matters: it reserves the gravest verdict for defiant repudiation, not for every judicial lapse — severity with a scale, not a blanket.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:45

> **And therein We prescribed for them: a life for a life, an eye for an eye, a nose for a nose, an ear for an ear, a tooth for a tooth, and for wounds, retribution. But whosoever forgoes it out of charity, it shall be an expiation for him. Whosoever judges not by that which God has sent down — it is they who are wrongdoers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Retribution Without Rank: The Torah's Egalitarian Code**

The verse rehearses the Torah's *lex talionis* (Exodus 21:24; Leviticus 24:20; Deuteronomy 19:21) — *a life for a life, an eye for an eye...* — to indict its corruption: the Banū Naḍīr (high-status) paid mere compensation for killing a Qurayẓah member, while the reverse killing brought execution or multiplied payment (R, Ṭ). The Torah's formula knows no such arithmetic of rank: *a life for a life* — the prince's eye and the pauper's eye weigh identically. The Qur'an's endorsement here (with Islam's parallel in 2:178; 4:92; 42:40) makes equality before retributive law a Mosaic *and* Muḥammadan principle. Note what *lex talionis* actually limits: in blood-feud Arabia, one killing licensed seven; the formula caps vengeance at equivalence — a ceiling as much as a sentence. Civilization's first victory over revenge was not forgiveness (that comes next) but *proportion*: only an eye for an eye, never a village for an eye. The verse then adds what the Biblical passages lack — the higher option.

**Forgiveness as Expiation: The Better Justice**

*Whosoever forgoes it as charity, it shall be expiation for him* (*kaffārah*) — debated: expiation for the forgiver's sins (al-Ṭabarī's view, citing ḥadīth that charity-forgiveness expiates the forgiver (Ṭ)) or for the offender (Ṭ, Z); 42:40 promises the forgiver a Divine reward either way. The structure offers three tiers: equivalent retribution (justice), compensation (mercy — available in Islamic law though, per the commentators, the Torah allowed only retribution or outright forgiveness (Q, R)), and free forgiveness (excellence). The tiers ascend without abolishing: retribution remains *available* so forgiveness remains *meaningful* — pardon costs nothing where punishment is impossible. *Whosoever judges not by it — they are the wrongdoers* (*ẓālimūn*) — the trilogy's middle term (cf. v. 44): distorting equal retribution into rank-based pricing is *ẓulm*, putting persons where they do not belong — some above the scales, some beneath them. Justice holds one scale for all; the verse holds one verdict for all who tamper with it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:46

> **And in their footsteps, We sent Jesus son of Mary, confirming the Torah that had come before him, and We gave him the Gospel, wherein is a guidance and a light, confirming the Torah that had come before him, as a guidance and an exhortation to the reverent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**In Their Footsteps: Prophecy as a Single File**

*In their footsteps* (*ʿalā āthārihim*) — of the submitted prophets of verse 44 (R, Z) — *We sent Jesus son of Mary*. Prophecy is a single file, each messenger stepping where predecessors stepped, not a scatter of rivals. Jesus is *confirming* (*muṣaddiq*) *the Torah before him* (cf. 3:50; 61:6; John the Baptist likewise, 3:39) — upholding every Torah ruling the Gospel does not specifically abrogate (Ṭ), and relaxing some prohibitions (3:50: *making lawful part of what was forbidden*). The confirmation runs both directions in the verse: Jesus confirms the Torah, and *the Gospel* — *wherein is guidance and light*, the Torah's exact honors (v. 44) — *confirms it too, as guidance and exhortation to the reverent* (*mawʿiẓah li'l-muttaqīn*). The Qur'an's Jesus is thus no law-abolisher but a law-renewer: same covenant, deepened interiority, lightened load. The passage quietly answers two opposite errors — those who claim Jesus abolished the Law entirely, and those who claim Muḥammad ﷺ dishonors Jesus. The Qur'an honors him precisely *as* a confirmer: greatness in prophecy is measured by fidelity to the file, not by deviation from it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:47

> **Let the people of the Gospel judge by what God has sent down therein. Whosoever judges not by that which God has sent down — it is they who are iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gospel's Standing Court: Validity in the Prophet's Presence**

*Let the people of the Gospel judge by what God sent down therein* — the Torah's endorsement (vv. 43–44) extended to the Gospel: Jesus lived largely by Torah rulings except where the Gospel abrogated (IK), the Gospel supplying teachings and exhortations more than codes (Z). The command's plain sense is present-tense validity: Christians *now*, even with Muḥammad ﷺ among them, are to judge by their Gospel — matching verse 43's question (why come to the Prophet ﷺ when you have the Torah?) and verse 68's *observe the Torah and the Gospel*. Some commentators restricted the command to the pre-Qur'anic era, holding the Qur'an abrogated earlier scriptures (IK, R) — but the source commentary judges this implausible precisely because the Prophet ﷺ himself *revived* Torah rulings (the stoning judgment) rather than superseding them (see vv. 41–44). The sūrah's architecture supports the plain sense: verses 43–47 form one chamber in which both Books stand as living law for their peoples. *Whosoever judges not by it — they are the iniquitous* (*fāsiqūn*) — completing Ibn ʿAbbās's trilogy: disbelief (44), wrongdoing (45), iniquity (47) (Z). Three verdicts, one disease: possessing God's judgment and judging by something else.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:48

> **And We have sent down unto thee the Book in truth, confirming the Book that came before it, and as a protector over it. So judge between them in accordance with what God has sent down, and follow not their caprices away from the truth that has come unto thee. For each among you We have appointed a law and a way. And had God willed, He would have made you one community, but [He willed otherwise], that He might try you in that which He has given you. So vie with one another in good deeds. Unto God shall be your return all together, and He will inform you of that wherein you differ.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Muḥaymin: The Qur'an as Guardian of the Scriptures**

*Confirming* (*muṣaddiq*) *the Book before it, and as protector* (*muḥaymin*) *over it* — *muḥaymin* (trustee, keeper, guardian; Ṭ, Z) shares its form with God's own Name *al-Muḥaymin* (59:23): as God guards creation, the Qur'an guards revelation — testifying to earlier scriptures' validity, preserving their truths, and arbitrating their corruptions (cf. 2:41, 89, 91, 97, 101; 3:3, 81; 6:92; 35:31; 46:30). The Prophet's ﷺ revival of Torah penalties (vv. 41, 45) demonstrates the office in action: the Qur'an does not bulldoze previous revelation but *restores* it. *Judge between them by what God sent down* — most read: by the Qur'an (Bḍ, Ṭ, Z); others: each people by its own scripture (as the Prophet ﷺ did with the Torah) — *and follow not their caprices* (*ahwā'*) *away from the truth*: neither their distortions of their own law (Ṭ) nor their pressure to bend his (Bḍ; cf. 2:120, 145; 5:49; 42:15). *Hawā* (caprice) is desire disguised as judgment — the verse's standing villain across this whole section. The judge's only loyalty is to *what God sent down*; every other loyalty — to tribe, donor, faction, or flatterer — is caprice in costume.

**"A Law and a Way": The Providence of Religious Plurality**

*For each among you We have appointed a law* (*shirʿah* — same root as *sharīʿah*) *and a way* (*minhāj* — smooth, clear path; Ṭ, Z) — distinct ritual-legal formulations Divinely *appointed* per community, independent yet rooted in shared essential truths (IK, Q, R, Ṭ). Exclusivist readings strain here — Mujāhid's "one law, Islam" (Ṭ), Ibn Kathīr's elided "Qur'an" (IK), the era-by-era succession view (Bḍ, Z) — but the next clause resists them: *had God willed, He would have made you one community* — a counterfactual declaring plurality itself *willed* (Ṭ; cf. 2:213; 10:19; 11:118; 16:93; 42:8). Like gender, race, and status (49:13), religious difference is providential raw material — *that He might try you in what He gave you*: obedience within one's own form (Ṭ), differing qiblahs testing who follows the Messenger (2:143–45). The trial reading that reduces plurality to supersession-abrogation (IK, Q) captures part-truth; the verse's plain drift captures more: difference as Divine pedagogy, not merely human deviance.

**"Vie in Good Deeds": Competition Without Coercion**

*So vie with one another in good deeds* (*fa'stabiqū'l-khayrāt*; cf. 2:148; 3:114) — addressed, most plausibly, to humanity as such (Ṭ): communities coexisting *contemporaneously*, racing in virtue. The race's track is *practice*, not polemics — feeding, sheltering, healing, beautifying — while *theological* differences await the only competent Tribunal: *unto God shall be your return all together, and He will inform you of what you differed in* (cf. v. 14). This verse anchors Islamic pluralism: truth is one and worth witnessing to, yet God Himself ordained multiple lawful forms and deferred final arbitration to Himself. The believer's marching orders follow: hold your convictions without apology, honor others' God-given forms without indifference, compete in goodness without ceasing — and leave the gavel, with all clashing claims, to the Day when the Informed will inform.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:49

> **And judge between them in accordance with what God has sent down, and follow not their caprices. And beware of them, lest they tempt thee away from some part of that which God has sent down unto thee. And if they turn away, know that God desires to smite them for some sin of theirs, and surely many among mankind are iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Temptation of the Just Judge**

The injunction repeats — *judge by what God sent down* (by the Qur'an, say most (Q, Ṭ)), *follow not their caprices* — then adds a warning of lethal specificity: *beware of them, lest they tempt thee* (*yaftinūk*) *away from part of what God sent down*. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the occasion: Madinan Jewish leaders, including Ibn Ṣūriyā (cf. v. 41), offered the Prophet ﷺ a bargain — rule for us against our rivals, and we will believe in you and bring our people with us (IK, Q, Ṭ, Z). The bribe was not gold but *converts*: bend one ruling, gain a community. The Prophet ﷺ refused. The episode exposes the subtlest corruption of authority — temptation disguised as *daʿwah strategy*, flattery promising mass guidance at the price of one small compromise. Some theologians note the warning implies even prophets face real temptation-pressure (Q, R) — sinlessness is not immunity to pressure but victory over it. Others read the danger as misinformation: lying about Torah contents to extract wrong rulings (Ṭs). Either way the armor is one: *part* of revelation is non-negotiable — concede a part and the whole becomes haggleable. Integrity, like virginity, is lost in parts but only possessed whole.

**When They Turn Away: Consolation Through Theodicy**

*If they turn away* — from the Prophet's ﷺ just ruling — *know that God desires to smite them for some of their sins*: refusal is not the judge's failure but the refusers' sentence maturing; for al-Rāzī, proof that all events unfold through Divine will toward ultimate purpose (R). *Many among mankind are iniquitous* (*fāsiqūn*) — breach is humanity's statistical norm, not its exception. The consolation is double-edged and must be handled with care. Rightly used, it steadies just judges, truthful witnesses, and faithful inviters when the corrupt walk out: their exit indicts them, not you. Wrongly used, it breeds complacency — "they turned away, so God must hate them." The verse guards the line: it speaks of *specific* turners from *specific* just judgments, not a license to damn the unconvinced. Justice delivers its verdict and releases the verdict-dodger to God — grief forbidden, gloating forbidden, duty done.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:50

> **Is it the judgment of the Age of Ignorance that they seek? And who is fairer in judgment than God, for a people who are certain.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jāhiliyyah on the Bench: Caprice in Judicial Robes**

*Is it the judgment of Jāhiliyyah* — the Age of Ignorance (cf. 3:154; 33:33) — *that they seek?* *Jāhiliyyah* is not illiteracy but *unguided knowing*: judgment by opinion, caprice, tribe, and price, unlit by revelation (IK). The immediate referent may be rank-based blood-penalties (Naḍīr versus Qurayẓah; R, Z; cf. vv. 45, 2:178; 4:135): tribal courts where the killer's status set the sentence. The verse's question universalizes: *any* adjudication that weighs persons instead of evidence — the celebrity's discount, the donor's acquittal, the foreigner's surcharge, the sectarian's verdict — is *jāhilī* judgment, whatever century's courthouse hosts it. Modernity did not abolish this; it bureaucratized it. The Qur'an's test for any legal system is brutally simple: does the same act by different actors draw the same sentence? Where yes, Divine justice casts its shadow; where no, Jāhiliyyah sits on the bench in a wig.

**"Who Is Fairer in Judgment Than God?": The Challenge to Certainty**

*Who is fairer* (*aḥsan* — fairer, better, more beautiful) *in judgment than God, for a people who are certain* (*yūqinūn*)? The question concedes nothing and challenges everything: compare systems — revealed justice versus every alternative — and judge the judges. *Aḥsan* fuses ethics and aesthetics: God's judgments are not merely correct but *beautiful* — proportioned, luminous, satisfying to the uncorrupted mind the way just music satisfies the ear. But the verdict is visible only *to a people who are certain*: certainty (*yaqīn*) is the eye that perceives juridical beauty; the doubting, the bought, and the biased see only constraint. The verse thus ends the section (vv. 41–50) where it began — with the judge's independence: the Prophet ﷺ was offered converts for compromise (v. 49) and forum-shoppers sought leniency (v. 43); God's answer is to table the rival system itself for judgment. Jāhiliyyah's courts may preen in marble; beside *aḥsan al-ḥukm*, they are what they have always been — caprice in robes.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:51

> **O you who believe! Take not Jews and Christians as protectors. They are the protectors of one another. And whosoever takes them as protectors, surely he is of them. Truly God guides not wrongdoing people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Walāyah: Protection, Alliance, and the Meaning of Belonging**

*Take not Jews and Christians as awliyā'* — *awliyā'* (singular *walī*) spans friend, ally, protector, patron, guardian; here, the commentators stress, it means *protectors* — dominant authorities and mutual-protection allies (cf. 3:28; 4:139) — not ordinary friends. *Walāyah* names the loyalty-protection bond defining communal membership (8:72; 9:71); *they are protectors of one another* — Jews and Christians realize it within their own communions (while vv. 80–81 fault them for extending it to idolaters). *Whosoever takes them as protectors is of them* — seeking another community's protection assimilates one to it, since *walāyah* partly constitutes membership (Q, Ṭū add inheritance consequences (Q, Ṭs)). The occasions anchor the abstraction: ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit renounced his Jewish-clan alliances for God, Prophet, and believers (cf. v. 55), while ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy insisted on keeping his (Q, R, Ṭ); others sought Jewish allies after Uḥud's defeat (Ṭ). The fledgling ummah, ringed by enemies, had to decide where its *protection* lay — and protection sought from hostile camps in wartime is not tolerance but treason. Later Islamic law, from established strength, permitted treaties and alliances with non-Muslim polities — proof the verse governs *existential dependence amid hostility*, not diplomacy as such.

**What the Verse Does Not Forbid: Friendship, Kinship, and Kindness**

Misreading this verse has caused real harm, so its boundaries must be drawn in ink. It does not forbid personal friendship: verse 5 permits *marriage* to Jewish and Christian women — the most intimate bond — which presupposes love, tenderness, and kinship; forbidding friendship while permitting marriage would be incoherent. It does not forbid kindness or justice: *God forbids you not* — toward those who fight you not for religion — *from dealing justly and kindly with them* (60:7–8). ʿUmar's (unsuccessful) citation of the verse against Abū Mūsā's Christian scribe (IK, R, Z) shows even Companions debated its administrative reach — and the scribe stayed. Al-Shāfiʿī's strict reading (no alliances even against idolaters (Q)) reflects his context's prudence, not the verse's ceiling. The verse forbids *one* thing precisely: taking as *protecting authority* — the power you depend on against your own community's interests — those committed to your religion's defeat. Everything else — neighbors, colleagues, in-laws, friends, allies, fellow citizens — remains governed by verses 5, 8, and 60:8: justice, kindness, and peace. Read whole, the Qur'an's map has no contradiction: guard the gates (v. 51), open the doors (v. 5).

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:52

> **Yet thou seest those in whose hearts is a disease hastening to them, saying, "We fear lest a change of fortune should befall us." It may be that God will grant victory, or a command from Him. And then they shall be remorseful for that which they secretly harbored in their souls.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hedging Bets with Hypocrisy: The Disease of Double Insurance**

*Those in whose hearts is a disease* (*maraḍ* — impure intention (33:32), secret hypocrisy and doubt (2:8–11; 24:47–50; 33:12, 60; 47:20, 29; 74:31)) — *hastening to them* (the verse-51 protectors) *saying: we fear a change of fortune* (*dā'irah* — a turn of fate's wheel) *may befall us*. The hypocrites' strategy is portfolio diversification applied to loyalty: hold shares in both camps so whichever triumphs, they profit — ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy's reported logic (R, Ṭ). The image is timeless: the official with two passports of allegiance, the cleric with a sermon for every victor, the power-broker who funds all candidates. The Qur'an diagnoses it as cardiac disease because divided loyalty eventually divides the self — the hedger becomes incapable of conviction, trusted by neither camp, at home nowhere. Communities sense it before they prove it: the hastening (*yusāriʿūn* — racing) betrays the anxiety; sincere allies walk, hedgers sprint. And history's verdict on hedgers is uniform — when fortunes finally turn, both sides remember the sprint.

**Victory, Command, and Remorse: The Future That Exposes Secrets**

*It may be that God will grant victory* (*fatḥ* — triumph, supremely Makkah's conquest, 8/630 (Ṭ); *fatḥ* also *judgment*, reconciled as conquest-manifesting-judgment (Ṭ)) *or a command from Him* — the jizyah regulation (Q, Ṭ; cf. 9:29), the Arabian expulsion (R, Z), or sudden exposure of hypocrites (Ṭ, Z) — *and then they shall be remorseful for what they secretly harbored*. Al-Rāzī notes *it may be* (*ʿasā*) from God expresses certain promise, not possibility (R). The mechanism is eschatological in miniature: hidden disloyalties are time-bombs detonated by events; every *fatḥ* unmasks the hedgers, whose alliances with the defeated become exhibits. Remorse (*nadam*) then arrives — but like Cain's (v. 31), it is exposure-grief, not repentance. The verse counsels believers to endure the hedgers' ambiguity without paranoia: God schedules revelations; Deanonymization Day comes with victory. Hold your post, keep your records clean, and let events audit the double-hearted. Time is the ally of the sincere and the auditor of the false.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:53

> **And those who believe will say, "Are these the ones who swore by God with their most solemn oaths that they were with you?" Their deeds have come to naught, and they have become losers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Are These the Ones?": Astonishment at Unmasked Oaths**

*Those who believe will say: are these the ones who swore by God with their most solemn oaths* (*jahda aymānihim* — the utmost effort of their oaths) *that they were with you?* — astonishment at exposure (if verse 52's "command" is unmasking (R, Ṭ, Z)), mingled with gratitude for vindication; or, alternatively, addressed to the Jews: are these hypocrites the ones who swore they were with *you* (Z; cf. 59:11)? Either way the scene captures perjury's public collapse: oaths sworn in God's Name, at maximum solemnity, revealed as theater. The Qur'an treats oath-breaking as a window into faithlessness (5:89; 16:91–92, 94; and hypocrites' oaths, 5:53; 6:109; 16:38; 24:53; 35:42). Solemnity of swearing correlates, in the hypocrite's economy, inversely with sincerity — the loudest oath protest covers the emptiest chest. Believers learn evidentiary wisdom: weigh *conduct over protestation*; the constantly-sworn loyalty is the least credible kind. Trust is built by presence in hardship, not volume in comfort.

**Deeds Without Faith: The Naughting of the Losers**

*Their deeds have come to naught* (*ḥabiṭat*) — voided, swollen-emptied (cf. v. 5) — *and they have become losers* (*khāsirīn*). The theology is systematic: salvation requires belief *and* deeds (2:25; 3:57; 4:57); works without faith merit nothing eternally (2:217; 5:5; 6:88; 7:147; 9:17; 18:105; 33:19; 39:65; 47:32 — R, Ṭ, Z), for deeds unstamped by faith are, in the deepest sense, not fully "good" (Z). The hypocrites prayed, fasted, marched, and swore — a résumé of righteousness with a forged signature. The verse terrifies rightly: external observance guarantees nothing; the audit examines the heart's allegiance beneath the limbs' performance. But it equally consoles the sincere-but-stumbling: faltering deeds with genuine faith outweigh polished deeds with hollow hearts. God grades the *student*, not just the *script* — and the student who truly enrolled, however messy his notebook, is not among the losers.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:54

> **O you who believe! Whosoever among you should renounce his religion, God will bring a people whom He loves and who love Him, humble toward the believers, stern toward the disbelievers, striving in the way of God, and fearing not the blame of any blamer. That is the Bounty of God, which He gives to whomsoever He will. And God is All-Encompassing, Knowing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Apostasy and Replacement: Religion Needs No One Indispensable**

*Whosoever among you renounces his religion* (*yartadda* — turns back, apostatizes) — *God will bring a people whom He loves and who love Him*. The verse is Islam's doctrine of dispensability: the religion is God's project, not any cohort's property; deserters are replaced, never missed. History's first fulfillments were named by commentators: Abū Bakr's wars against the apostate tribes (*riddah*) after the Prophet's ﷺ death; the Helpers (*Anṣār*); the Yemenis; the Persians (Salman's people) — each generation finding its own "brought people." The psychology liberates and warns at once: no community, scholar, dynasty, or movement is load-bearing for Islam — God has infinite reserves. The corollary convicts complacency: imagining "Islam needs *us*" inverts reality; we need Islam, and neediness that mistakes itself for necessity is one sermon away from apostasy of the heart. Every revival in Islamic history — from the *riddah* wars to every desert-reared reform — has been this verse re-enacted: the withdrawing wave replaced by an advancing one, the religion marching on with new feet.

**"Whom He Loves and Who Love Him": The Charter of Mutual Love**

*Yuḥibbuhum wa yuḥibbūnah* — "whom He loves and who love Him": five words that became Sufism's charter-text. How can the Eternal love the temporal? Al-Ghazzālī: God's love is His love of Himself overflowing, since nothing exists outside His Being; human love is His self-unveiling in the seeking heart (*Iḥyā'*, IV.437). Rūmī: love belongs to God alone; humans partake derivatively (*Mathnawī* V.2184–90). Al-Qushayrī: Divine love exceeds universal Mercy — Mercy blesses all, Love ennobles some; the servant's love is a "subtle state" of delight in the Beloved's presence, an intoxication never sobered, an illness no cure sought for (Qu). The verse orders the loves deliberately — *His first*: human love for God is always response, never initiative. *That is God's Bounty, given to whom He wills* — for al-Rāzī, proof all good, including righteousness itself, originates Divinely (R); for others, His leading-grace (*luṭf*; Ṭū, Z). The portraits that follow — humble (*adhillah*: gentle, merciful, tender (R, Ṭb, Ṭū, Z)) to believers, stern (*aʿizzah*: mighty, unyielding) to disbelievers (cf. 48:29), striving (al-Qushayrī: obedience, detachment, constant witnessing (Qu)), fearing no blamer's blame (the hypocrites' inverse (Z; cf. vv. 51–53)) — sketch love's public manners: tenderness inward, firmness outward, effort always, fear of none but God. Love that cannot be blamed out of its Beloved is love indeed.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:55

> **Your protector is only God, and His Messenger, and those who believe, who perform the prayer and give alms while bowing down.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Only These Protectors: The Positive Side of Verse 51**

Verse 51 forbade hostile protectors; verse 55 commands the true ones: *innamā waliyyukumu'Llāh* — "Your protector is *only* God" — *innamā* restricting, excluding all rivals — *and His Messenger, and those who believe*. *Walāyah* cascades: God primarily (He is *al-Walī* by Name), the Prophet ﷺ and believers derivatively, as extensions of loyalty to God (Bḍ, R). The occasions ground it: ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit renouncing Jewish-clan alliances for this triple loyalty (IK, R); Companions ostracized by Qurayẓah and Naḍīr, answered with truer belonging (JJ, W). The verse diagnoses the exile's cure: those cut off by hostile networks for faith's sake are not thereby unprotected — they are *re-protected*, transferred from fragile human patronage to patronage that cannot fail. Every believer who lost friends, employment, or family for conscience's sake inherits this verse's arithmetic: subtract every *walī* who demanded compromise, and the remainder — God, Messenger, believers — outweighs all.

**"While Bowing Down": ʿAlī's Ring and the Verse of Wilāyah**

*Who perform prayer and give alms while bowing* (*rākiʿūn*) — read generally (believers' humility in worship (Ṭ; cf. 2:43), the sincere untainted by hypocrisy (R, Z; cf. 9:71)), but widely tied to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib giving his ring to a beggar mid-prayer while bowing — reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, and others across Sunni and Shīʿī commentaries (Bḍ, IK, Ṭ, Z, W; a minority names Abū Bakr (R)). The act models charity's urgency: need recognized is need answered *immediately*, not after convenient delay (Z). Shīʿīs call this the "verse of *wilāyah*": *walī* here crowns ʿAlī as the believers' authority after God and Prophet, evidencing his succession-right (Ṭb, Ṭū). Sunni respondents — while often accepting the occasion's early attestation — read *wilāyah* as mutual support, not successorship (al-Rāzī notes ʿAlī never cited it at the *shūrā* after ʿUmar's death (R)); some question the reports' soundness (IK). The dispute need not obscure the agreed core, luminous for all: prayer that does not interrupt compassion, worship that opens the hand rather than closing it. The bowing giver is Islam's icon of integrated devotion — body low before God, hand extended to man, both gestures one act.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:56

> **And whosoever takes as his protector God, and His Messenger, and those who believe — the party of God, they are the victorious!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥizb Allāh: Membership and Its Guarantee**

*Whosoever takes as protector God, Messenger, and believers* — fulfilling verse 55 — *is of the party of God* (*ḥizb Allāh*), *and they are the victorious* (*ghālibūn*). *Ḥizb* is a party, faction, organized company — the Qur'an recognizes only two: God's (here; 58:22 — the prospering, mutually content with God) and Satan's (58:19 — the losers). Partisanship is thus inescapable; the only choice is *which* party. The verse's guarantee — *they are the victorious* — is stated as accomplished fact (*ghālibūn*, victors, not future victors): triumph is membership's entailment, whether in history's headlines or eternity's ledgers. History's scoreboard often disagrees in the short term — prophets killed, reformers jailed, the righteous ground down — which is precisely why the guarantee transcends headlines: Badr and Uḥud both befell the same party. The name has been adopted across centuries, most famously by Lebanon's contemporary Hezbollah — a reminder that potent Qur'anic titles attract political claimants, and claimants must be measured by verses 54–55's membership criteria (love, humility, sternness, striving, prayer, charity), not by self-labeling. The party's uniform is character; its flag, conduct.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:57

> **O you who believe! Take not as protectors those who take your religion in mockery and as play, from among those who were given the Book before you, or the disbelievers, and reverence God, if you are believers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mockery as Disqualification: Why Ridicule Voids Alliance**

*Take not as protectors those who take your religion in mockery* (*huzuwan*) *and play* (*laʿiban*) — from Scripture's people or idolaters alike. Mockery (*istihzā'*) in the Qur'an is never mere rudeness: prophets were mocked (15:11), signs mocked (30:10), warnings mocked (11:8) — and mockers are invariably *destroyed by what they mocked* (6:10; 16:34; 39:48; 40:83; 45:33; 46:26). Ridicule is aggression's reconnaissance: contempt expressed as jest today authorizes violence tomorrow. *Play* (*laʿib*) is engagement without commitment — religion handled as entertainment (cf. 6:70; 7:51). The occasions: hypocrites befriended by unwary believers (R, Ṭ, W, Z); Jews and idolaters mocking prostrating Muslims (Q); the Prophet ﷺ declining Jewish military help at Uḥud (3/625; Q; cf. 3:28, the Trench, 5/627). Al-Shāfiʿī extended the verse against alliances with Scripture's people even facing idolaters (Q). The principle: *protection requires respect*. One does not take as guardian those who laugh at one's prayers — dependence on the contemptuous funds one's own humiliation. Discern mockery early: the "joke" about your faith at dinner, the smirk at your practice — these are the verse's early-warning signals. Distance, politely but firmly, before dependence forms.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:58

> **And when you call to prayer, they take it in mockery and as play. That is because they are a people who do not understand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Adhān Under Mockery: Scripture's Sole Mention**

*When you call to prayer* (*nādaytum ila'l-ṣalāh*) — this verse is the Qur'an's only direct mention of the call to prayer (R, Z), though without the technical term *adhān*. The adhān's words — *God is Greatest; I bear witness none is god but God; I bear witness Muḥammad is God's Messenger; come to prayer; come to prosperity; God is Greatest; none is god but God* (with Shīʿī additions witnessing ʿAlī as *walī* of God and summoning to "the best of acts") — were instituted after the qiblah's change, when Companions dreamed the call and the community recognized its need for public summons (Q); some trace the Prophet's ﷺ hearing of it to the Night Journey (Q). Reports say Madinan Jews or Christians mocked the call, casting ill omens on the Prophet ﷺ and believers (IK, Q, Ṭ, Z), or derided it as innovation or ugliness (Q, R, W). *Because they are a people who do not understand* (*lā yaʿqilūn*) — al-Rāzī: the understanding know prayer is humanity's noblest act, quoting "the noblest movement is prayer, the most beneficial stillness fasting" (R). The mockers heard noise; the wise hear coronation — five daily announcements that God outranks every meeting, market, and monarch. Civilizations are graded by what interrupts them: the adhān interrupts commerce for worship, declaring the economy subordinate to eternity. Those who mock the interruption confess they have never grasped the hierarchy.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:59

> **Say, "O People of the Book! Are you vengeful toward us for no other reason than that we believe in God and in that which has been sent down unto us and in that which was sent down before, and because most of you are iniquitous?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Our Only Crime Is Belief": The Question That Reverses the Trial**

*Are you vengeful* (*tanqimūn* — seeking vengeance, finding fault) *toward us for no reason but that we believe in God, in what was sent to us, and in what was sent before?* The question puts the accusers in the dock, echoing Pharaoh's repentant sorcerers — *you take vengeance on us only because we believed our Lord's signs* (7:126; cf. 85:8). The reported occasion: Madinan Jews breaking with the Prophet ﷺ over his affirmation of Jesus' prophethood — *what was sent before* — which they rejected (R, Ṭ). Islam's offense, the verse archly observes, is *believing too much*: God *plus* Qur'an *plus* Torah *plus* Gospel — a comprehensiveness that exclusivists experience as provocation. *And because most of you are iniquitous* (*fāsiqūn*) — deviated from God's own law, breeding ill will toward the community that keeps it. The psychology is projection: the iniquitous cannot bear the sight of integrated belief; rather than reform, they resent. The verse arms every maligned believer with a two-sentence defense: name your actual crime (belief), and name their actual motive (iniquity's discomfort). Persecution's paperwork, honestly filled, always reads the same.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:60

> **Say, "Shall I inform you of something worse than that by way of recompense from God? Whomsoever God has cursed and upon whom is His Wrath, and among whom He has made some to be apes and swine, and who worship false deities, such are in a worse situation, and further astray from the right way."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Worse Than That": Ironic Recompense and Real History**

*Shall I inform you of worse than that as recompense* (*mathūbah* — reward-word used ironically for punishment (Z), like "glad tidings" of torment (4:138)) *from God?* The catalogue: *whom God cursed* (exiled from Mercy (Ṭ)) *and grew wrathful against; among whom He made some apes and swine; and who worship ṭāghūt* (false deities; cf. 4:51–52 — here, Israel's calf (Z; cf. 7:148–56; 20:86–97)). *Apes* recalls the Sabbath-violators — *be apes* (2:65; 7:163–66) — read literally by most, and spiritually by others as hearts hardened and heedless (Aj; cf. 2:74; 5:13; 6:43); some assign *apes* to Sabbath-breakers and *swine* to Jesus' betrayer at the supper (Q, R). The verse's logic is retributive symmetry: you mock believers for believing (v. 59); behold what befell *your own* record's violators. *Worse in situation, further astray* — the superlative shames the mockery: find the beam in your archive before the mote in ours. Modern polemicists should note the method — the Qur'an answers religious mockery not with counter-mockery of persons but with *history*: look at what your own scriptures record of covenant-breakers. Argument from shared sources outranks insult; the past, honestly read, humbles every present.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:61

> **When they come to you, they say, "We believe." But they certainly entered with disbelief and they have certainly left with it, and God knows best what they were concealing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Entry and Exit Audited: The Visit That Changed Nothing**

*When they come to you, they say "we believe"* — Madinan Jews visiting the Prophet ﷺ with professed faith, inwardly unchanged (Ṭ, Z). *They entered with disbelief and left with it* — the visit was transit, not transformation: disbelief carried in, disbelief carried out, the Prophet's ﷺ presence merely walked through. The verse audits *spiritual tourism*: bodies that enter sacred spaces while hearts remain sealed gain nothing from proximity. Geography never saved anyone — hypocrites prayed in the Prophet's ﷺ own mosque behind the Prophet ﷺ himself. What matters is the heart's posture on entry: the open enter and are filled; the closed enter and merely circulate. *God knows best what they concealed* (*yaktumūn*) — the verse's consolation to the Prophet ﷺ and warning to performers: every concealed motive is inventoried. The audience that applauds the sermon while resolving nothing has left with what it entered with; only the resolved leave lighter than they came.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:62

> **Thou seest many of them hastening to sin and enmity, and consuming what is forbidden. Evil indeed is that which they were doing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hastening to Sin: Velocity as a Moral Symptom**

*Thou seest many of them hastening* (*yusāriʿūn* — racing, competing) *to sin* (*ithm* — glossed as lying (R, Z) or disbelief, or all sin (Ṭ)) *and enmity* (*ʿudwān* — hostility to the Prophet ﷺ, wrongdoing, transgressing God's limits (Ṭ, Z)) *and consuming the forbidden* (*suḥt* — illicit gain, bribery (cf. v. 42)). *Hastening* is the verse's diagnostic: sin committed sluggishly, with dragging feet and residual shame, differs from sin *raced* toward. Velocity reveals appetite — the eager liar, the enthusiastic embezzler, the volunteer persecutor. Communities should watch not only *what* members do but *how fast* they do it: reluctance in wrongdoing signals surviving conscience; alacrity signals its death. *Evil indeed is what they were doing* (*yaʿmalūn* — general doing) — set deliberately beside the next verse's *working* (*yaṣnaʿūn*) for the scholars who permitted it. The laity's evil is *doing* wrong; the learned's evil, as verse 63 will show, is *manufacturing* the conditions for it. Both are evil; the second is worse.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:63

> **Why do the sages and the rabbis not forbid them from their sinful speech and their consuming what is forbidden? Evil indeed is that which they were working.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Silence of Scholars: When Knowledge Abdicates**

*Why do the sages* (*rabbāniyyūn*; cf. v. 44) *and rabbis not forbid them their sinful speech and forbidden consumption?* The verse turns from sinners to *scholars who watched*: men who knew the bribery (v. 62), knew God's judgment (v. 44), and said nothing — failing to "judge by God's judgment" (Ṭ). *Evil indeed is what they were working* (*yaṣnaʿūn* — crafting, producing): the laity *do* (*v. 62*), but scholars *manufacture* — their silence mass-produces sin, their inaction operating at industrial scale (R). Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Ḍaḥḥāk (d. 105/723) reportedly counted this among the Qur'an's most frightening verses (R, Ṭ, Z) — *they* were scholars, and felt the blade's edge. The terror is transferable to every learned class in every religion: knowledge confers not privilege but *liability*. The ignorant sinner answers for his acts; the silent scholar answers for his acts *plus* every act his silence licensed. Tenure, titles, and followings do not mitigate the verse — they magnify it. Scholarship that will not forbid wrong is not neutrality; it is *production*.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:64

> **The Jews say, "God's Hand is shackled." Shackled are their hands, and they are cursed for what they say. Nay, but His two Hands are outstretched, He bestows as He wills. Surely that which has been sent down unto thee from thy Lord will increase many of them in rebellion and disbelief. And We have cast enmity and hatred among them till the Day of Resurrection. As often as they ignite a flame for war, God extinguishes it. They endeavor to work corruption upon the earth. And God loves not the workers of corruption.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"God's Hand Is Shackled": Mockery Turned Back on the Mockers**

*God's Hand is shackled* (*maghlūlah*) — "shackled hands" elsewhere signify Hell's helplessness (13:5; 34:33; 40:71) and miserliness (17:29); miserliness is meant here: God withholds provision, at least from them (Ṭ). Al-Rāzī doubts any Jew held this doctrinally — they knew God's power — and reconstructs the jibe: literalizing *who will lend God a goodly loan?* (2:245; 5:12; 57:11) to jeer it, or gesturing at early Muslims' poverty — "Muḥammad's God's hand is shackled" (R); others tie it to Jewish fortunes' decline under Islam's rise (R, Z), or to limiting God's punishment to days (2:80; 3:24). The Divine answer is reflexive, as so often (cf. 2:9): *shackled are their hands* — the curse returns to sender — *cursed* (exiled from Mercy (Ṭ)) *for what they say*. Then the positive doctrine: *His two Hands are outstretched* (*mabsūṭatān*), *bestowing as He wills* — basis of the Name *al-Bāsiṭ*, the Open-Handed (cf. 13:26; 17:30; 28:82; 29:62; 30:37; 34:36, 39; 39:52; 42:12). On *two Hands* (cf. 38:75, Adam's creation): some read metaphor — blessings, power (cf. 38:45, *ūlū'l-aydī* (Ṭ)); others affirm the attribute literally *without modality* (*bilā kayf*), unlike human hands (Ṭ); some hear in the dual two blessing-kinds — world and Hereafter, outward and inward, benefit granted and harm repelled (R) — though al-Ṭabarī insists the dual means two (Ṭ). Beneath the theology runs the pastoral point: no creature's miserliness indicted here exceeds God's generosity asserted — the universe runs on open Hands, not shackled ones.

**Revelation That Increases Rebellion: The Paradox of the Criterion**

*What was sent down to thee will increase many of them in rebellion* (*ṭughyān*) *and disbelief* — revelation as *Furqān* (Criterion; 2:53, 185; 3:4; 21:48; 25:1): the same rain that nourishes wheat rots neglected timber (cf. 3:19; 5:68; 8:2; 9:124; 17:41, 60, 82; 35:42; 71:6; 74:31). The disbelievers flee the Reminder *like frightened asses fleeing a lion* (74:49–51). The paradox convicts the recipient, never the message: light blinds only diseased eyes. *We have cast enmity and hatred among them till Resurrection* — Jews, or People of the Book generally (some: between Jews and Christians (Ṭ); cf. v. 14 on Christians) — internal fracture as sentence. *As often as they ignite war's flame, God extinguishes it* — plots against the Prophet ﷺ (Ṭ), or the long archive of military catastrophe from Assyria and Babylon (cf. ch. 17) — *they endeavor corruption* (*fasād*; cf. vv. 33, 30:41) *and God loves not corrupters*. The verse's movement is complete: mockery answered, generosity asserted, rejection explained, fracture sentenced, aggression quenched. Every war on God's message ends with the same fire-brigade.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:65

> **Had the People of the Book believed and been reverent, We would surely have absolved them of their evil deeds, and caused them to enter Gardens of bliss.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Open Counterfactual: What Might Have Been**

*Had the People of the Book believed and been reverent* (*āmanū wa'ttaqaw*) — *We would have absolved their evil deeds and admitted them to Gardens of bliss* (*naʿīm*). *Believed*, most hold, means in Qur'an and Muḥammad ﷺ (4:136; cf. 2:103; 7:96) — "believer" in full Qur'anic use means all prophets and books — though *believe* sometimes spans all God-and-Last-Day believers (2:62; 5:69). The counterfactual's poignancy is its openness: the sentence describes a *still-available* future grammatically dressed as past hypothesis. Absolution (*takfīr* — covering) plus Gardens: the identical promise given Muslims (v. 9; 4:31; 25:70) extended across the aisle without surcharge. The verse forbids despair *about others*: no community's record, however stained by verses 60–64's catalogue, exceeds absolution's reach. Evangelism's motive stands here — not triumphalism but arithmetic: the Gardens stand ready, the covering stands ready, the only missing element is the turning. Every People-of-the-Book neighbor is this verse's addressee in potentia; the believer's task is to keep the invitation legible.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:66

> **Had they observed the Torah and the Gospel and that which was sent down unto them from their Lord, they would surely have received nourishment from above them and from beneath their feet. There is a moderate community among them; but as for many of them, evil is that which they do!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Nourishment from Above and Below: The Agronomy of Obedience**

*Had they observed* (*aqāmū* — upheld, established) *the Torah, the Gospel, and what was sent down to them from their Lord* — the Qur'an, say most (Q, Ṭ); some, the non-Torah Hebrew books like Daniel (Q, R) — *they would have received nourishment* (*rizq*) *from above and beneath their feet*. *Observing* spans covenant-uprightness generally — entailing, some argue, following the Muḥammad ﷺ inscribed in those Books (7:157; 61:6 (R)) — and continued ritual-legal fidelity within each community's appointed form (v. 48 (R)); in practice: Torah and Gospel where the Qur'an endorses or does not falsify them (Ṭ). The nourishment is mostly read as worldly prosperity — heaven's rain, earth's crops (R, Ṭ, Z) — answering verse 64's "shackled Hand" (Ṭ) or complementing verse 65's spiritual promise (R). But the verse yields a lovelier allegory: *from beneath* — their own scriptures, seeds already planted in their hearts; *from above* — the Qur'an as rain, nourishing directly *and* reviving those very seeds. Revelation waters revelation; no prior planting is wasted when the later rain falls. Obedience, the verse insists, is not merely remunerated hereafter — it *irrigates* the present.

**A Moderate Community: The Praised Remnant**

*Among them is a moderate community* (*ummah muqtaṣidah* — middle-course; distinct from the Muslim *ummatan wasaṭan* (2:143), yet kin to all Qur'anic loves of balance (15:19; 17:29; 68:28)). Read as those whose Christology the Qur'an moderates — neither deifying Jesus (Christian excess) nor denying his prophethood (Jewish deficit), but confessing him prophet (Ṭ); or the just dealers (3:75 (R)); or the middle-paced between wrongdoers and foremost saints (35:32); or converts to Islam (Q, R) — though conversion would dissolve rather than moderate communal identity. *But many of them — evil is what they do.* The verse's pairing is the Qur'an's perennial fairness: never condemn a community wholesale while a righteous remnant stands within it (cf. 3:113). Polemic that cannot say "among them are the moderate" has left Qur'anic discourse for tribalism. Praise the remnant publicly; it strengthens their hands and proves your quarrel is with deeds, not blood.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:67

> **O Messenger! Convey that which has been sent down unto thee from thy Lord, and if thou dost not, thou wilt not have conveyed His message. And God will protect thee from mankind. Surely God guides not disbelieving people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Convey Everything: The Non-Negotiability of the Message**

*O Messenger* (*yā ayyuhā'l-rasūl* — the office-title, used at moments of maximal gravity) — *convey* (*balligh*) *all that was sent down to thee*. The warning is absolute: *if thou dost not, thou hast not conveyed His message* — concealing even a fragment equals concealing the whole (Z); one report has the Prophet ﷺ saying God threatened him with punishment for concealment (Z). ʿĀ'ishah cited this verse to refute any claim the Prophet ﷺ hid revelations (IK, Q, Ṭ, Ṭs). The immediate content may be the sūrah's critique of the People of the Book (Ṭ) — hard words the Prophet ﷺ might have softened. The principle generalizes to every trustee of truth: the scholar's unpopular fatwa, the auditor's inconvenient finding, the witness's dangerous testimony — *balligh* brooks no editing for palatability. Partial conveyance is mis-conveyance; the omitted fragment is invariably the load-bearing one. Integrity of transmission is the messenger's entire job (vv. 92, 99); everything else — reception, reform, victory — belongs to God.

**"God Will Protect Thee": The End of the Bodyguard**

*God will protect thee* (*yaʿṣimuka*) *from mankind* — Divine security detail. Reports cluster: in Makkah the Prophet ﷺ concealed some revelations under Quraysh threat, then proclaimed all as strength grew; Abū Ṭālib posted guards until this verse, when the Prophet ﷺ dismissed them (R, Ṭ), fear leaving him entirely (IK, Q); the lone Bedouin with the sword — "who protects you from me?" — "God" — the sword trembling from his hand (Q, Ṭ; cf. v. 11). The promise does not mean invulnerability (Uḥud wounded him ﷺ) but *mission-inviolability*: no hand can silence him before delivery completes. Twelver Shīʿī commentaries attach the verse to Ghadīr Khumm's *mawlā* declaration for ʿAlī — the Prophet ﷺ hesitant before Companion reaction, emboldened by this guarantee (al-ʿAyyāshī, Qm, Ṭb, Th, Ṭū); some Sunnis likewise link it to Ghadīr or the Farewell sermonircle (Āl, Th, IK) — chronologically plausible for this late sūrah. Whatever the near occasion, the standing message addresses every fearful truth-teller: protection is promised *for conveyance*, not for silence. Speak, and the Shield deploys; hide, and no shield is owed.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:68

> **Say, "O People of the Book! You stand on naught till you observe the Torah and the Gospel, and that which has been sent down unto you from your Lord." Surely that which has been sent down unto thee from thy Lord will increase many of them in rebellion and disbelief. So grieve not for disbelieving people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"You Stand on Naught": The Emptiness of Unobserved Scripture**

*You stand on naught* (*lastum ʿalā shay'* — you are upon nothing) *till you observe* (*tuqīmū* — uphold, establish) *the Torah, the Gospel, and what was sent to you from your Lord* (the Qur'an, per most (cf. v. 66)). The image is architectural: a community's scripture is its ground; unobserved scripture is air beneath feet — standing on nothing while imagining a floor. The verse (cf. v. 66) demands *both* fidelities: their own Books *and* the Qur'an — some suggesting this very demand was what the Prophet ﷺ feared to convey (v. 67 (R, Ṭ)). The logic convicts scriptural nominalism in every religion: the Torah on the shelf, the Gospel on the wall, the Qur'an on the dashboard — venerated objects without observed contents leave their venerators standing on naught. *Observance* (*iqāmah*) is erection-language: scripture must be *stood up* in life — prayed, judged, forgiven, and fasted into verticality. Until then, heritage is costume, and identity is echo. The verse asks every community one question: if your Book vanished tonight, what in your tomorrow would change? Nothing changed, nothing stood.

**Rebellion Increased, Grief Forbidden: The Warner's Equanimity**

*What was sent to thee will increase many of them in rebellion and disbelief* (cf. v. 64) — the Criterion-effect, restated as prophecy so the Prophet ﷺ will recognize it, not be shattered by it. *So grieve not* (*lā ta'sa*) *for disbelieving people* (cf. vv. 26, 41; 27:70). The sequence — convey (v. 67), confront (v. 68a), expect backlash (v. 68b), forbid grief (v. 68c) — is the complete emotional curriculum of truth-telling. Backlash is priced into the mission; grief over it is insubordination against the pricing. Note the grief forbidden is *despairing* grief, not compassion — the Prophet ﷺ wept for his uncle Abū Ṭālib and for his ummah; what is banned is the grief that questions the message or the Messenger. Equanimity is not coldness: feel the sorrow fully, then release the outcome finally. The sower who weeps over stony soil instead of sowing the next field has misunderstood both soil and sowing.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:69

> **Truly those who believe, and those who are Jews, and the Sabeans, and the Christians — whosoever believes in God and the Last Day and works righteousness, no fear shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charter of Hope: Salvation Beyond Communal Walls**

Near-identical to 2:62 (cf. 22:17, adding Magians), this verse — lodged inside the Qur'an's most sustained critique of the People of the Book — is among its most hope-bearing: *believers, Jews, Sabeans, Christians — whosoever believes in God and the Last Day and works righteousness: no fear upon them, nor shall they grieve*. The operative clause is universal (*whosoever*), opening salvation's grammar beyond Abrahamic lines. Read with verse 5 (intermarriage), verse 48 (appointed laws-and-ways), and verses 82–85 (Christian virtue), it anchors the Qur'an's dual witness: sharp doctrinal critique *and* refusal to damn communities wholesale. The classical spectrum on its scope is wide — from those who read it of pre-Muḥammadan adherents or converts, to those who let its *whosoever* breathe eschatologically. What all readings share: *God judges persons by belief-and-righteousness, not by tribal registration*. No community owns the Gardens' gate; the Gatekeeper admits whomsoever meets His stated terms. The verse forbids two certainties: presuming others damned, presuming ourselves saved. Both verdicts await the Knower of breasts.

**No Fear, No Grief: The Tense of Paradise**

*No fear* (*khawf* — dread of the future) *shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve* (*yaḥzanūn* — sorrow over the past): the saved inhabit neither anxiety about what comes nor regret over what went — al-Rāzī's "eternal now" of paradise (R). The pairing diagnoses earthly misery exactly: human suffering is nearly all time-displaced — fear borrows tomorrow's pain, grief replays yesterday's. Salvation, negatively described, is release from both displacements into presence. The verse thus offers a foretaste-test: whatever brings a soul, even now, toward fearlessness and un-regret — trust in God dissolving anxiety, repentance dissolving guilt — is paradise's advance party. The communions named will dispute theology till the end (v. 48); but the verse sets before all four the same exit from time's twin tortures. Believe, do right — and step, even in this life's measure, into the now that never aches.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:70

> **We indeed made a covenant with the Children of Israel, and sent messengers unto them. Whensoever a messenger brought them what their souls did not desire, some they would deny and some they would slay.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Messengers Measured Against Appetite: The Recurring Crime**

*We made a covenant with Israel and sent them messengers* (cf. 2:63, 83–84, 93; 4:154; 5:12; 7:134; and generally 3:187; 5:14; 7:169) — *whensoever a messenger brought what their souls did not desire* (*lā tahwā anfusuhum*), *some they denied, some they slew* (cf. 2:87, 91; 3:183–84; 4:155). The criterion is damning: revelation weighed not against truth but against *taste* — *hawā* (caprice), the section's standing villain (vv. 48–49). Palatable prophets were honored; unpalatable ones were called liars or corpses. The pattern is not Israel's alone; it is appetite's universal veto over truth. Every age denies-or-slays its warners — literally in assassinations of the righteous, socially in cancellations of the inconvenient, intellectually in the refusal to read what disturbs. The verse's grammar (*kullamā* — *whenever*, iterative) stresses habituation: each rejection eased the next, denial graduating to murder by practice. Communities should audit their own receptions: which contemporary voices do we call false — and is the evidence their error, or our appetite? The murdered prophets of old were, in their day, merely "controversial figures" their establishments dismissed.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:71

> **And they supposed that there would be no trial, and so became blind and deaf. Then God relented unto them; then [again] many of them became blind and deaf. And God sees whatsoever they do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Blind and Deaf: The Sentence of Untested Presumption**

*They supposed there would be no trial* (*fitnah*) — no testing, no consequence — *and so became blind and deaf*. Spiritual blindness-deafness pervades the Qur'an (2:171; 6:104; 7:64; 10:43; 11:28; 13:19; 27:4, 66, 81; 30:53; 41:17, 44; 43:36–37, 40): *it is not eyes that go blind, but hearts in breasts* (22:46), a blindness continuing hereafter (17:72, 97; 20:102, 124–25). The verse traces the causal loop: presumption of immunity licenses transgression; transgression films the eyes; filmed eyes presume further. Whether blindness precedes rejection (preexisting deformity) or follows it (punitive sealing, cf. 2:14–18; 47:23), the outcome converges — the soul that will not see *cannot* see. Since humans are born in *fiṭrah* (primordial soundness; 30:30) with innate Lord-awareness (7:172), blindness is always *acquired* — rust on native shine, never original darkness. The diagnosis carries its cure: what was acquired by presumption can be removed by *trial-acknowledged* — owning that testing is real, consequences certain, and the Physician's eye-drops (repentance, remembrance) effective.

**Relent, Relapse, Repeat: The Cycle of Unlearned Mercy**

*Then God relented* (*tāba'Llāhu ʿalayhim* — turned toward them in mercy) *unto them; then again many became blind and deaf*. The rhythm — sin, mercy, relapse — is Israel's cycle and humanity's: forgiven generations forget forgiveness. Each relent (*tawbah* from God) answered contrition or intercession; each relapse squandered it. *God sees whatsoever they do* (*baṣīr*) — the verse's closing Name answers its opening verbs: they are blind, but He sees; they are deaf, but every deed is witnessed. The asymmetry is the only stable ground in moral life: human perception fails cyclically, Divine perception never. Societies in relapse-phase — mercy received, lessons unlearned, blindness returning — should read the verse as early-warning: the cycle's next turn is not guaranteed to include relenting. *Today* is the relenting's day; tomorrow's senses depend on today's use of it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:72

> **They certainly disbelieve, those who say, "Truly God is the Messiah, son of Mary." But the Messiah said, "O Children of Israel! Worship God, my Lord and your Lord." Surely whosoever ascribes partners unto God, God has forbidden him the Garden, and his refuge shall be the Fire. And the wrongdoers shall have no helpers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jesus Against His Deifiers: The Witness of His Own Words**

*They certainly disbelieve who say "God is the Messiah, son of Mary"* (cf. v. 17) — *but the Messiah said: "O Children of Israel! Worship God, my Lord and your Lord"* (*rabbī wa rabbakum*; cf. 3:51; 5:117; 19:36; 43:64). The refutation calls Jesus himself as witness: his reported words assert full servanthood — God is *his* Lord before He is theirs. The Gospels' nearest parallel (John 20:17, "my Father and your Father") lacks the worship-command context, and "Father"-language is itself Qur'anically problematic (cf. v. 18). The verse presents the deifiers with their Deified: *your God commands worship of his God* — resolve it. Some commentators confine the explicit claim to sects like the Jacobites, who fused Christ's natures into essential unity with God (R, Ṭ), while the verse's closing broadens the warning to all *shirk* (ascribing partners; cf. 9:31 (Z)). The method is worth preserving: refute exaggeration about the righteous *with the righteous ones' own statements*. The saints would be the first to condemn their own cults; let their words, faithfully transmitted, dismantle what devotion corrupted.

**Shirk's Sentence: Garden Forbidden, Fire Assigned, Helpers Absent**

*Whosoever ascribes partners to God — God has forbidden him the Garden* (*ḥarrama ʿalayhi'l-jannah*), *his refuge is the Fire, and wrongdoers* (*ẓālimīn*) *shall have no helpers* (*anṣār*). *Shirk* is *ẓulm*'s summit — worship misaddressed to its non-owner — and its sentence is total: exclusion, assignment, abandonment. The severity is proportionate to the crime's object: offenses scale with the offended, and the Offended here is infinite. Yet the verse's placement after 69's universal hope and before 74's call to repentance frames even this sentence evangelistically: the verdict targets *persistence*, and repentance's door (v. 74) stands explicitly open. The warning is thus a rescue flare, not a damnation decree — fired over the precise reef where ships wreck, in time to steer. Those who heed find the flarer's mercy; those who sail on find the reef was charted truly.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:73

> **They certainly disbelieve, those who say, "Truly God is the third of three," while there is no god save the one God. If they refrain not from what they say, a painful punishment will befall those among them who disbelieved.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Third of Three": Which Trinity Is Condemned?**

*They disbelieve who say "God is the third of three"* (*thālithu thalāthah*) — the Qur'an's verdict on trinitarianism, or at least its exaggerated popular form as three separate worshipped entities. The commentators were better informed than polemicists suppose: many knew orthodoxy's triad is Father-Son-Spirit, not the God-Jesus-Mary triad the Qur'an elsewhere engages (vv. 75, 116; IK, Q, R), attributing three-deities belief to sects like Jacobites and Nestorians (IK, Q). Al-Rāzī even renders the orthodox Trinity sympathetically — Essence, Word, Life — mapping it onto Essence-and-Attributes grammar, yet still judges it clear error (R). *While there is no god save the one God* (*illā ilāhun wāḥid*) — *wāḥid* (one, unique, indivisible) against *thalāthah*: arithmetic as theology. The verse (with 4:171's *say not "Three"*) does not parse inner-Christian distinctions to exempt them; it judges the doctrine's *effect* — worship diffused beyond the One — while leaving persons to God's mercy (v. 74). Precision about others' beliefs and firmness about one's own are not opposites; this verse's tradition models both.

**"Those Among Them": Punishment's Careful Restriction**

*If they refrain not* (*lam yantahū* — desist, as commanded in 4:171), *painful punishment will befall those among them who disbelieved* (*alladhīna kafarū minhum*). The restriction — *among them* — is doing heavy work: not all Christians are sentenced, only persisters (R, Z) — a reading demanded by verses 69, 2:62, 3:113, and 82–85's portraits of the virtuous and saved among Christians. Some commentators widen the threat to Christians generally (Ṭ, Z), but the verse's own grammar resists: *minhum* (among them) partitions. The Qur'an's critique of doctrines is absolute; its sentencing of *persons* is scrupulously particular — weighing persistence, knowledge, and repentance. The distinction rebukes blanket damnation in every direction: affirm *there is no god but the One God* without flinching, and leave the seating-chart of Hell to its Keeper. Doctrine is public and debatable; destiny is Divine and deferred.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:74

> **Will they not turn to God in repentance and seek His forgiveness? And God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Is an Invitation**

*A-fa-lā yatūbūna ila'Llāhi wa yastaghfirūnah* — "Will they not turn to God in repentance and seek His forgiveness?" After the severest christological verdicts (vv. 72–73), the Qur'an does not slam the door — it *asks a question through it*. The interrogative is pure mercy: the same mouths that said "third of three" are invited to say "forgive us," and the invitation's timing — immediately after sentencing — proves no doctrine, however grave, outruns repentance while breath remains. *God is Forgiving* (*Ghafūr*), *Merciful* (*Raḥīm*) — the two Names that close a hundred Qur'anic doors against despair. The verse's brevity is its eloquence: seventeen Arabic words against two verses of verdict. Mercy speaks briefly because it need not argue — it need only be *true*. Every invitation to Islam's *tawḥīd* should carry this verse's aftertaste: clarity without cruelty, verdict with venue for return. The heresy-hunter who omits verse 74 has preached half the passage; the half omitted is God's own last word on the matter.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:75

> **The Messiah, son of Mary, was naught but a messenger — messengers have passed away before him. And his mother was truthful. Both of them ate food. Behold how We make the signs clear unto them; yet behold how they are perverted!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Naught but a Messenger": Christology by Subtraction**

*The Messiah, son of Mary, was naught but a messenger* (*illā rasūl* — except/only a messenger); *messengers passed before him* — the identical formula honors Muḥammad ﷺ in 3:144, placing Jesus squarely in the prophetic file (cf. 4:171: *only a messenger*, yet also *His Word* and *a Spirit from Him*). The subtraction is honorific: *messenger* in the Qur'an is among the highest ranks creation affords — to be God's chosen envoy to nations outranks every invented dignity. *His mother was truthful* (*ṣiddīqah* — feminine of *ṣiddīq*, the rank of Abraham (19:41), Idrīs (19:56), Abū Bakr *al-Ṣiddīq* (Z), and company of prophets, witnesses, righteous (4:69); Mary *confirmed* (*ṣaddaqat*) her Lord's Words (66:12 (R))). Mother and son thus hold two of heaven's supreme human ranks — messengerhood and truthfulness. The verse gives Jesus and Mary everything except divinity, and presents the package as *more* glorious than deification: exalted humanity outshines confused theology. True honor fits the honored; oversized crowns topple the statue.

**"Both of Them Ate Food": The Domestic Argument**

*Both of them ate food* (*kānā ya'kulāni'l-ṭaʿām*) — the Qur'an's most disarmingly domestic refutation. Eaters hunger, digest, depend, decay: neediness incompatible with self-sufficient Godhead. The argument concedes Christian sympathies — Christ's full humanity (cf. Luke 18:19; Philippians 2:6–8) — while insisting humanity *excludes* divinity rather than accompanying it. There is polemical gentleness here worth imitating: no scorn, no caricature — just a kitchen-table fact. *Behold* (*unẓur*) *how We clarify the signs for them; yet behold how they are perverted* (*yu'fakūn* — turned away, deluded): twin beholds — clarity offered, clarity refused. The tragedy is not obscurity but *evasion*: signs clarified to daylight brightness, eyes averted. Every age's denials repeat the structure — evidence sufficient, aversion decisive. The verse's final verb leaves the perversion agentless (*passive*): whether self-deluded or other-deceived, the outcome is one — truth clarified, backs turned.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:76

> **Say, "Do you worship, apart from God, that which has no power to benefit or harm you, when it is God Who is the Hearing, the Knowing?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Benefit and Harm: The Two-Power Test of Divinity**

*Do you worship, besides God, what has no power to benefit or harm you?* — the Qur'an's portable idol-test, deployed through prophets across history (10:18, 106; 13:16; 20:88–89; 21:62–66; 22:12; 25:55; 26:71–73). Divinity's minimum portfolio: *nafʿ* (benefit) and *ḍarr* (harm) — power over weal and woe. Whatever cannot deliver or avert — stone idols, dead saints, deified humans insofar as human — fails the test by its own devotees' admission: no petitioner ever claimed his idol *answered*. *When it is God who is the Hearing* (*Samīʿ*), *the Knowing* (*ʿAlīm*) — the positive portfolio: He *hears* every plea (no intercom needed) and *knows* every need (no briefing required). The two Names dismantle the entire economy of intermediaries: why petition the deaf through the dead when the Hearing hears directly? The verse's logic extends beyond statues to every misplaced reliance — wealth that cannot ransom (v. 36), patrons who cannot shield (v. 51), selves that cannot save. Audit every worship — ritual or practical — by the two-power test; whatever fails it is *besides God*, whatever its name.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:77

> **Say, "O People of the Book! Do not exaggerate in your religion beyond the truth, and follow not the caprices of a people who went astray before, and led many astray, and strayed from the right way."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ghuluww: Exaggeration as the Mother of Heresies**

*Do not exaggerate* (*lā taghlū* — *ghuluww*: exceeding bounds) *in your religion beyond the truth* (*ghayr al-ḥaqq*) — the twin of 4:171, aimed chiefly at Christian deification of Jesus. *Ghuluww* is devotion overshooting its object: love for the righteous inflated past truth into worship. The Prophet ﷺ warned his own ummah against the same disease:

> *"Do not extol me as the Christians extolled Jesus son of Mary. I am but a servant — so say: God's servant and His Messenger."* (al-Bukhārī 3445)

Every cult of personality — saint-veneration curdling into saint-worship, leader-love into leader-infallibility, madhhab-loyalty into madhhab-bigotry — is *ghuluww* in local dress. The verse's qualifier matters: *beyond the truth* — honor within truth's bounds is commanded (honor prophets, honor parents, honor scholars); only the *excess* is banned. Truth sets the ceiling of praise; love must decorate beneath it, never punch through. Communities should post this verse over every shrine, portrait, and podium: revere greatly, exaggerate never.

**Inherited Error: The Triple Straying**

*Follow not the caprices of a people who went astray before* (*ḍallū*), *led many astray* (*aḍallū*), *and strayed from the right way* (*ḍallū ʿan sawā'i'l-sabīl*) — a triple straying: erring, misleading, and persisting off-path. Candidates: the Jews who wholly denied Christ (Ṭ); early Christian authorities who forged the christological doctrines and later rejected Muḥammad ﷺ (IK, Z); or both communities' past errors generally (Q, R, Ṭs). The structure warns against *inherited* caprice — errors absorbed as heritage, defended as identity. Secondhand error is the hardest to shed because abandoning it feels like betraying ancestors. The verse severs the equation: ancestors astray deserve pity and prayer, not imitation. Each generation must re-derive its convictions from *truth*, not inherit them from *caprice* — even caprice wearing a mitre, a turban, or a family name. The strayed, the straying-leaders, and the strayed-persisters form one chain; the verse commands: break it at your link.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:78

> **Those who disbelieved among the Children of Israel were cursed by the tongue of David and Jesus son of Mary. That was because they disobeyed and used to transgress.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Cursed by Two Prophets: The Testimony of Psalms and Gospel**

*Those who disbelieved among Israel were cursed* (*luʿina* — exiled from Mercy, per most) *by the tongue of David and Jesus son of Mary* — in the Psalms and the Gospel, say early commentators (with Moses' Torah and Muḥammad's ﷺ Qur'an completing the fourfold witness (Ṭ, Z)). Some tie David's curse to the Sabbath-violators of Eilat (7:163–66 (JJ, R, Z)) and Jesus' to post-Table disbelievers (vv. 114–115 (JJ, R, Z)); others to the ape-and-swine transformation (v. 60 (Ṭ)). *Cursed by tongue* — prophetic speech as Divine instrument: the curse is God's, the tongue His prophets'. The verse's ecumenical structure deserves notice: the Qur'an validates *David's and Jesus'* curses as its own evidence — Israel's disbelievers stand condemned by *Israel's own prophets*. Internal witness outranks external charge; the prosecution's star witnesses are the defendants' own luminaries. Polemicists of every stripe should learn the move: the most devastating critique of a community's deviance is its own founders' words. Quote their Psalms to them before composing your own.

**Disobedience Plus Transgression: The Two Counts**

*Because they disobeyed* (*ʿaṣaw* — rebelled against commands) *and used to transgress* (*yaʿtadūn* — habitually crossed limits; iterative *kāna + yaʿtadūn*: transgression as lifestyle). Two counts: *omission* (disobeying orders) and *commission* (crossing boundaries) — the full spectrum of *fisq*. The pairing closes every loophole: the disobedient cannot plead they harmed none (omission itself indicted); the transgressor cannot plead zeal (excess itself indicted). Virtue's narrow road runs between the ditch of neglect and the ditch of excess — the verse maps both ditches from Israel's wreckage. And the curse's *cause* (*dhālika bi-mā* — "that was because") establishes moral causation publicly: curses in this Book are never arbitrary lightning but *sentenced* consequences, with counts specified. Read the counts, check your own docket, and repent the matching entries before the tongue of your own prophet testifies.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:79

> **They would not forbid one another from the wrong they committed. Evil indeed is that which they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Community That Cancelled Correction**

*They would not forbid one another the wrong they committed* (*lā yatanāhawna ʿan munkarin faʿalūh*) — enjoining right and forbidding wrong (*amr bi'l-maʿrūf, nahy ʿan al-munkar*; 3:104, 110, 114; 7:157, 199; 9:67, 112; 22:41; 31:17) abandoned *within* the community — not merely tolerating outsiders' sins but refusing to correct *their own*. The failure is mutual (*yatanāhawna* — reciprocal form): each exempted each, a non-aggression pact against conscience. The Prophet ﷺ warned where this leads:

> *"When people see wrong and do not change it, God is near to covering them all with punishment."* (Abū Dāwūd 4338; al-Tirmidhī 2168; Ibn Mājah 4005)

And the commentator's cited ḥadīth: *"The best striving is a word of truth"* (Th; cf. Abū Dāwūd 4344: "before a tyrannical ruler"). *Evil indeed is what they used to do* (*yafʿalūn*) — the evil named is not the wrongs themselves but *the non-forbidding*: omission indicted as commission. Modern communities replicate the pact under new names — "not my business," "their choice," "don't judge" — each a thread in the same mutual-exemption treaty. The verse voids the treaty: membership *obligates* correction, delivered with wisdom (16:125) but delivered nonetheless. A community that will not say "brother, stop" has stopped being a community and become an audience.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:80

> **Thou seest many of them allying with those who have disbelieved. Evil indeed is that which they have sent forth for their souls: the anger of God is upon them, and in punishment shall they abide.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Alliances with Disbelief: The Treason of Convenience**

*Thou seest many of them allying* (*yatawallawna* — taking as *awliyā'*) *with those who disbelieved* — Madinan Jews (or hypocrites among them) aligning with idolaters against Muslims (Q, Ṭ, Z; cf. 4:51–52). The verse pointedly distinguishes *People of the Book* from *disbelievers* (*alladhīna kafarū* — here the idolaters): sharing scripture-tradition yet choosing pagan alliance over prophetic fellowship. The Qur'an bars Muslims from the same maneuver in reverse — no protectors among disbelievers, wrongdoers (8:73; 45:19), or hostile Scriptuaries (v. 51). Alliance is worship's shadow: *walāyah* given reveals *qiblah* held. The political is never merely political — every pact confesses a scale of loves. *Evil is what they sent forth for their souls* (*qaddamū* — deeds as advance consignments to the Hereafter (cf. 75:13)): alliances are investments maturing in eternity's market, and this portfolio's yield is specified — *God's anger* (*sakhṭ*) *upon them, abiding in punishment*. Choose allies the way you choose destinations: you will arrive where they are going.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:81

> **And had they believed in God and the Prophet, and that which was sent down unto him, they would not have taken them as protectors, but many of them are iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Alliance Test of Faith: Protection Reveals Belief**

*Had they believed in God and the Prophet* (*nabī* — Muḥammad ﷺ and the Qur'an (Ṭ), or alternatively Moses and the Torah (R)) — *they would not have taken them* (idolaters) *as protectors*. Both readings converge on one exposure: genuine scripture-faith *precludes* pagan alliance — the Torah no less than the Qur'an forbids it. Their Madinan moment forced the choice starkly: ally with Muḥammad ﷺ, whose message affirmed-and-challenged their own, or with idolaters, whose creed their own scriptures anathematized. They chose *anathema over challenge* — comfort over truth. *But many of them are iniquitous* (*fāsiqūn*) — breach explains choice: the already-broken find idolaters' company congenial because it demands nothing. The verse offers every believer an alliance-audit: examine your protectors, patrons, and partners — would your scripture approve the list? Protection sought from God's enemies while God's friends stand available is not strategy but symptom. Faith, fully held, chooses its friends undeniably; *walāyah* is belief's public signature.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:82

> **Thou wilt surely find the most hostile of men toward those who believe to be the Jews and those who ascribe partners unto God. And thou wilt find the nearest of them in affection toward those who believe to be those who say, "We are Christians." That is because among them are priests and monks, and because they are not arrogant.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hostility and Nearness: A Contextual Verdict, Not an Eternal Ranking**

*Most hostile* (*ashadda ʿadāwah*) *toward believers: the Jews and the idolaters* (cf. 2:96) — *nearest in affection* (*aqraba mawaddah*): *those who say "we are Christians."* The verse's occasion is its context: Madinan Jews challenging the Prophet's ﷺ authority, some colluding with pagans — against Christians like Baḥīrah (who recognized the young Muḥammad ﷺ), Waraqah (who confirmed the first revelations; cf. Sūrah 96's background), and the Negus who sheltered Muslim refugees from Quraysh persecution. Absolutizing the ranking beyond context contradicts history — Crusades and Reconquista reversed it bloodily — and the commentators who contextualize it read more faithfully (Th). The verdict describes *responses to the early message*, not essences of peoples: hostility is a deed-word, recording what specific communities *did*. Every community's placement on this spectrum is re-earned by each generation's conduct. The verse warns against presuming either permanent enemies or permanent friends among religions — only permanent *criteria*: hostility earns distance, affection earns nearness, deeds decide all.

**Priests, Monks, and Humility: Why Nearness Was Earned**

*Because among them are priests* (*qissīsīn* — scholars (IK, Z); Salmān al-Fārsī reports the Prophet ﷺ glossing them as *ṣiddīqīn*, the truthful-saintly (IK)) *and monks* (*ruhbān*), *and because they are not arrogant* (*lā yastakbirūn*). Exemplars of devotion plus institutional humility produced receptivity. The Qur'an's view of monasticism stays mixed — no Islamic parallel, celibate excess discouraged by the Prophet ﷺ, corrupt monks indicted (9:31, 34), monasticism itself called Christian invention (57:27) — yet its sincere practitioners earn this tribute (cf. 3:113; 57:27). *Non-arrogance* is the hinge: arrogance (*istikbār*) is scripture's master-block to truth (2:87; 6:93; 7:75–76, 146; 10:75; 23:67; 31:7; 35:42–43; 39:59, 72; 41:15; 45:8, 31; 46:10; 63:5; 71:7), while humility (*khuḍūʿ*, prostration-readiness (4:172–73; 16:49; 21:19; 32:15; 40:60; 41:37–38)) opens every seal. The Christians' nearness was thus *moral*, not tribal: devotion's exemplars plus unarrogant hearts equal truth-receptive communities. Any community — Muslim, Jewish, Christian, other — that cultivates studied devotion and kills arrogance inherits the same nearness. The formula is published; the results are reproducible.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:83

> **And when they hear that which was sent down unto the Messenger, thou seest their eyes overflow with tears because of the truth they recognize. They say, "Our Lord, we believe, so inscribe us among the witnesses.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tears of Recognition: The Negus's Delegation**

*When they hear what was sent to the Messenger, their eyes overflow with tears* (*tafīḍu mina'l-damʿ*) *for the truth they recognize* (*ʿarafū mina'l-ḥaqq*). The occasion: the Negus's delegation visiting Madinah, weeping at Qur'anic recitation and believing (IK, Ṭ, Th, Z) — some adding the Negus himself believed on hearing it (Ṭ, Th), though history records no conversion, and the verse praises Christians *as Christians* ("nearest in affection," v. 82), not merely as converts (Th); some say they believed in Muḥammad's ﷺ prophethood while living by Jesus' law (IK). The weeping matches others' responses to revelation: *falling prostrate, weeping, increased in humility* (17:107–109; cf. 28:52–53) — and the ḥadīth *"Recite the Qur'an and weep; if you cannot weep, make yourselves weep"* (Ibn Mājah 1337 (Th)). Tears here are *recognition's* physiology — truth perceived by prepared hearts overflows bodily. The delegation had Gospels in memory and humility in character; the Qur'an struck both like flint on steel. Preparation plus presentation equals ignition — the verse explains why identical recitations melt some audiences and bore others. The variable is never the Qur'an.

**"Inscribe Us Among the Witnesses": Belief's Enlistment Plea**

*Our Lord, we believe* (*āmannā* — in Muḥammad ﷺ as prophet, the Qur'an as God's truth (Ṭ)) — *so inscribe us* (*fa'ktubnā*) *among the witnesses* (*shāhidīn*) — witnesses to truth and Divine Oneness, or to Muḥammad's ﷺ community specifically (R, Ṭ, Z). *Inscribe*: enrollment-language — the Kingdom's registry, written citizenship. The plea models conversion's proper sequence: *hearing* (v. 83a) → *recognition* (truth known) → *emotion* (tears) → *confession* (we believe) → *enlistment* (inscribe us). Feeling without enrolling evaporates; enrolling without feeling calcifies. The delegation completes the circuit — and verse 85 will show the Registrar approved the application. Every moved heart faces the same next step: tears are the application fee; *enlistment* is the application. File it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:84

> **And how should we not believe in God and the truth that has come unto us, when we desire that our Lord should cause us to enter among the righteous people?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"How Should We Not Believe?": The Rhetoric of Inevitable Faith**

*How should we not believe* (*wa mā lanā lā nu'min* — "what is with us that we would not believe?") *in God* — His Oneness — *and the truth come unto us* (Ṭ) — *when we desire our Lord to admit us among the righteous* (*ṣāliḥīn* — the Prophet ﷺ and Companions, or God's Oneness-affirmers (Ṭ))? The rhetorical question presents faith as the only rational option given their desire — disbelief would contradict their own stated hope. Some hear in it trinitarianism's abandonment (R); all hear *desire's* evidential value — disbelievers desire no such company (Z). The logic is desire-consistency: audit your hopes, then adopt their prerequisites. Whoever hopes for righteous company hereafter yet refuses belief here is, the verse implies, either insincere in hope or irrational in means. The Christians' question turns evangelistic method inside-out: instead of threatening hellfire, it *displays heaven's guest-list* and asks — do you want in? Desire, honestly owned, drags doctrine behind it. Show souls the righteous and their radiance; belief will present itself as the admission ticket souls suddenly find themselves reaching for.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:85

> **So God rewarded them for what they said with Gardens that have rivers running below, to abide therein, and that is the recompense of the virtuous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rewarded "For What They Said": Speech That Earned Gardens**

*So God rewarded them for what they said* (*bi-mā qālū*) — *for speech*: the confession (v. 83), the rhetorical faith (v. 84) — *with Gardens with rivers below, abiding therein* (cf. 2:25), *the virtuous'* (*muḥsinīn*) *recompense*. Words, sincerely meant, outrank lifetimes of ritual mechanically performed: the delegation *said*, and saying-with-heart minted eternity. The Qur'an elsewhere confirms speech's salvific weight — the good word as the good tree (14:24), the testimony admitting paradise. Yet *what they said* included tears, recognition, and enlistment-plea — speech as *soul's summary*, not mere phonetics. The verse comforts the inarticulate-in-deeds but articulate-in-longing: God audits the heart's utterances, and sincere words are deeds of the tongue's limb. Speak faith plainly when truth appears — confession delayed calcifies into denial. The delegation spoke the same hour they heard; the Gardens' title-deeds were dated that day.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:86

> **As for those who disbelieved and denied Our signs — such shall be the inhabitants of Hellfire!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Antiphon of Verse 85: Denial's Address**

*Those who disbelieved and denied Our signs* (*kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā*) — *they are Hellfire's inhabitants* (*aṣḥāb al-jaḥīm*; cf. 2:39; 5:10; 7:36; 22:51; 57:19; 64:10). The verse is verse 85's photographic negative: *said-and-believed* → Gardens; *disbelieved-and-denied* → Fire. *Takdhīb* (calling truth a lie) is disbelief's public half — inward covering (*kufr*) plus outward branding (*takdhīb*): the full offense. Set deliberately after Christianity's tenderest portrait, the verse prevents misreading tenderness as universalism: affection for Christians (v. 82), tears honored (v. 83), confession rewarded (vv. 84–85) — *and* denial sentenced (v. 86). Mercy and verdict coexist without contradiction because they address different responses. The Qur'an's steadiness here models pastoral balance: never dilute the warning to keep hearers comfortable; never wield the warning to crush the tearful. Verse 85 for the weeping, verse 86 for the scorning — each audience its verse, each verse its audience.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:87

> **O you who believe! Do not forbid the good things that God has made lawful unto you, and do not transgress. Surely God loves not transgressors.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Asceticism Heresy: Forbidding What God Allowed**

*Do not forbid* (*lā tuḥarrimū*) *the good things* (*ṭayyibāt*) *God made lawful for you* — the verse behind which stands a famous incident: Companions vowing celibacy, sleepless nights, endless fasting, and meat-abandonment — extreme renunciations the Prophet ﷺ repudiated:

> *"I fast and I break fast; I pray and I sleep; and I marry women. Whoever turns away from my way is not of me."* (al-Bukhārī 5063; Muslim 1401, paraphrased)

The Qur'an's polemic against unauthorized forbidding runs deep (6:138–45, 150–51; 7:32 — even dress-adornment; 16:116). *Do not transgress* (*lā taʿtadū* — cross limits): self-imposed prohibition *is* transgression — trespass on God's legislative monopoly from the direction of piety. *God loves not transgressors* (cf. 2:190; 7:55; their hearts sealed, 10:74). The psychology: eltist asceticism breeds either pride ("holier than the lawful-enjoyers") or collapse (vows unsustainable, then abandonment of all). Islam's middle path — lawful enjoyment with grateful moderation — sustains lifelong: the believer feasts at weddings and fasts Ramaḍān, marries and worships nights, earns and gives. Sanctity through the lawful outlasts sanctity against it. Enjoy the *ṭayyibāt* — gratitude, not renunciation, is the commanded response to abundance.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:88

> **Eat of that which God has provided you that is lawful and good, and reverence God, in Whom you are believers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Eat": The Imperative of Grateful Enjoyment**

*Eat* (*kulū*) *of God's provision to you — lawful* (*ḥalāl*) *and good* (*ṭayyib*) — the command-form of enjoyment (cf. 2:168, 172; 16:114). Eating the lawful is not concession but *obedience*: the appetite, disciplined to lawful-and-good, becomes worship's instrument. *Lawful* (juridical permission) plus *good* (wholesome quality) — both filters required: the lawful-but-foul (stolen-then-"purified" goods, earnings tainted at source) fails the second; the good-but-unlawful (wine's conviviality, carrion's protein) fails the first. *Reverence God, in whom you are believers* — *taqwā* yoked to *īmān*: belief in *Him* specifically funds reverence generally. The verse's order completes verse 87's prohibition: verse 87 forbids forbidding; verse 88 commands partaking. The Law's liberality (vv. 4–5) thus ends not in silent permission but *audible invitation*: the Host commands His guests to eat. Refusing lawful bounty from misplaced piety insults the Host's generosity; devouring it heedlessly insults His majesty. Eat — lawfully, wholesomely, reverently, gratefully. The table is spread; grace is said; the command is *kulū*.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:89

> **God will not take you to task for that which is frivolous in your oaths, but He will take you to task for the oaths you have pledged in earnest. The expiation thereof is the feeding of ten indigent people with the equivalent of that which you feed your own family, or clothing them, or freeing a slave. And whosoever finds not [the means], let him fast for three days. That is the expiation for your oaths when you have sworn [them]. But keep your oaths. Thus does God make clear unto you His signs, that haply you may give thanks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Frivolous Oaths Versus Pledged Oaths: The Taxonomy of Swearing**

*God will not take you to task for frivolous* (*laghw* — idle, unintended) *oaths* — casual *wallāhi*s, habit-interjections — *but for oaths pledged in earnest* (*ʿaqqadtum* — knotted, contracted; cf. 2:225). Some reverse the expiation's target (frivolous oaths expiated-and-forgiven (Ṭ)); others tie frivolous oaths to food-vows after verses 87–88 (Ṭ) — swearing off the lawful neither binds nor forbids (Q). Deceptive/lying oaths (*kadhb* — false swearing) admit *no* expiation (Q, Ṭ) — perjury's debt is repentance-scale, not feeding-scale. The taxonomy teaches speech-ethics: God's Name is currency — casual spending debases it (*laghw*, forgiven but deplored), earnest spending binds it (*ʿaqd*, enforceable), counterfeit spending (*kadhb*) forges it (unpardonable-by-payment). The Prophet ﷺ set the Name's mint-standard: oaths sworn *by God or silence* (Q; cf. al-Bukhārī 6646), never by fathers, idols, or selves. Modern equivalents — "I swear" as punctuation, promises as politeness — fall under the same audit. Knot words carefully; the Knotted hears knots.

**Expiation's Ladder: Feeding, Clothing, Freeing, Fasting**

*Expiation* (*kaffārah* — covering): *feed ten indigents* at household standard (bread-dates-oil minimum, meat best (Ṭ); or grain-measures (Ṭ); or lunch-and-supper (Ṭ)) — *or clothe them* (one-two garments, head-covering per some (Q, Ṭ)) — *or free a slave* (healthy, unblemished (Ṭ)). Recipients: truly poor, non-dependent non-kin (Q); Ḥanafīs admit non-Muslim poor (Q; cf. 2:272; 60:8); slave-recipients excluded (master's duty), Muslim-only disputed. *Whoever lacks means: fast three days* — consecutively or not (unstipulated here, unlike 58:4's consecutive-two-months (Q, Ṭ)) — fasting substitutes *only* for the unable (Ṭ). The ladder's design is welfare-engineering: broken oaths fund the poor — private speech-failures converted to public provision. *Keep your oaths* (*iḥfaẓū* — guard them): expiation is remedy, not license — the guarded oath needs none. *That haply you may give thanks* — clarity itself is the gift; gratitude, the intended yield. A legal system that monetizes oath-breaking into orphan-feeding has understood both justice and mercy.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:90

> **O you who believe! Wine, and gambling, and idols, and divining arrows are but a means of defilement, of Satan's doing. So avoid it, that haply you may prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Prohibition: Four Defilements, One Avoidance**

*Wine* (*khamr*), *gambling* (*maysir*), *idols* (*anṣāb* — altar-stones of idol-sacrifice (cf. v. 3)), *divining arrows* (*azlām*; cf. vv. 3, 90-comm.) — *are but defilement* (*rijs* — filth, impurity) *of Satan's doing* — *so avoid it* (*fa'jtanibūh* — shun wholly). This is revelation's fourth and final word on intoxicants: provision-sign (16:67) → sin-exceeding-benefit (2:219) → not-drunk-at-prayer (4:43) → total avoidance (here). The graduation models legislative wisdom: entrenched habits are tapered, not snapped — a mercy-strategy for addictions personal and societal. *Khamr* (grape-wine technically) extends to all intoxicants — ʿUmar's pulpit-definition: *"khamr is whatever clouds* (*khāmara*) *the intellect"* (IK) — covering drinks, drugs, and whatever veils reason; the ban spans consumption, sale, and profit (IK). *Maysir* (pre-Islamic gambling; cf. 2:219) covers all gambling; some stretched it to time-squandering games (chess, backgammon (IK)) — never enacted as law, both remaining Muslim pastimes. *Rijs-of-Satan* classifies the four as spiritually contaminating, not merely inadvisable: contact defiles. *Avoid* — not "moderate": the command recognizes addiction's binary — the alcoholic's moderation is mythology. Total avoidance is total mercy.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:91

> **Satan desires only to sow enmity and hatred among you through wine and gambling, and to turn you away from the remembrance of God, and from prayer. Will you, then, refrain?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Satan's Two Harvests: Broken Brotherhood, Abandoned Prayer**

*Satan desires only* (*innamā yurīdu* — his exclusive intent): *enmity* (*ʿadāwah*) *and hatred* (*baghḍā'*) *among you through wine and gambling* — *and barring you from God's remembrance and prayer*. Two harvests from one sowing: horizontal (brotherhood broken) and vertical (worship abandoned). The occasion reportedly: drunken Companions brawling till injury (Ṭ) — alcohol's standard dividend, from bar-fights to battered families. Gambling's enmity is structural: every win is another's loss, breeding rancor by design — wealth transferred by chance, not value, leaving rage where trade leaves satisfaction. And both erect the same wall against *dhikr* and *ṣalāh*: intoxication dissolves attention, gambling devours time — prayer requires both. Intracommunal hatred here mirrors the sentenced fractures of Christians (v. 14) and Jews (v. 64): believers who drink and gamble import their own curse. *Will you then refrain* (*fa-hal antum muntahūn*)? — the question ʿUmar answered for the ummah: *"We refrain, our Lord, we refrain!"* (cf. IK) — whereupon Madinah's streets ran with poured-out wine. Revelation asks; the righteous answer with action, not essays.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:92

> **Obey God and obey the Messenger, and be wary. But if you turn away, then know that only the clear proclamation is incumbent upon Our Messenger.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Obedience Doubled, Duty Delimited**

*Obey God and obey the Messenger* (*aṭīʿū'Llāha wa aṭīʿū'l-rasūl*) — the Qur'an's doubled command (3:32, 132; 4:59; 8:1, 20, 46; 24:54; 33:33; 47:33; 58:13; 64:12; cf. 7:156–57; 59:7) — here likely enforcing verses 90–91's prohibitions (R, Ṭ) — *and be wary* (*iḥdharū* — beware disobedience's consequences). Then the delimitation: *if you turn away — only the clear proclamation* (*balāgh mubīn*) *is upon Our Messenger* (cf. 3:20; 5:99; 13:40; 16:82; 29:18; 36:17; 42:48; 64:12). The Prophet ﷺ controls delivery, not reception (4:80; 6:107; 42:6, 48) — the verse-41/68 consolation restated as job description. The pairing defines all religious authority: *obedience owed* (to God-and-Messenger, never to personalities beyond them) and *coercion refused* (proclamation, not compulsion). Leaders who demand obedience beyond revelation overreach; followers who withhold obedience within it rebel. And inviters learn their limits: proclaim clearly — then release. The turned back is not the proclaimer' failure; the unproclaimed truth would be. *Balāgh* done, conscience clear, outcome God's.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:93

> **There is no blame upon those who believe and perform righteous deeds for that which they have eaten, so long as they are reverent, and believe, and perform righteous deeds, then are reverent and believe, and then are reverent and virtuous. And God loves the virtuous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dead Who Drank: Absolving the Pre-Prohibition Generation**

*No blame* (*junāḥ*) *on believers-and-righteous for what they ate* — revealed for Companions anxious over Badr-and-Uḥud martyrs who drank wine lifelong, dying before verses 90–91 (Q, R, Ṭ, W). The absolution parallels: usury-profits pre-prohibition retained (2:275); prayers toward Jerusalem validated pre-qiblah-change — *God would not let your belief be in vain* (2:143 (Q, R)). The principle: *law does not retroact* — souls are judged by their hour's light, not later legislation's. Some twisted the verse to license drinking-if-reverent (Ṭ) — refuted by Sunnah, consensus, and context (R): the verse covers *the dead before prohibition*, not the living after it. The pastoral wisdom: communities must not posthumously prosecute ancestors for what revelation had not yet clarified — nor prospectively excuse themselves by ancestors' precedent. Each generation stands in its own light. The martyrs who drank unknowingly rest unblamed; the knowledgeable who drink now cannot borrow their excuse.

**Reverence Thrice: Faith, Deeds, and Excellence**

*So long as they are reverent* (*ittaqaw*), *believe, and do righteous deeds — then revere and believe — then revere and are virtuous* (*aḥsanū*) — reverence thrice-woven with the Gabriel-ḥadīth's triad: *īmān* (belief), *islām* (righteous deeds/obligations), *iḥsān* (virtue — *worshipping God as though you see Him*). Righteous deeds here: obligations observed, prohibitions kept; virtue: supererogation drawing-near (Q, Ṭ) — the qudsī ladder: *obligations most-beloved, then supererogation till "I love him"* (al-Bukhārī 6502). *God loves the virtuous* (*muḥsinīn*; cf. 2:195; 3:134, 148; 5:13) — Love itself as virtue's wage, the noblest station (R; cf. "Qur'an and Sufism"). Alternative triple-readings: emphasis; past-present-future prohibitions (Q, R); disbelief-major-minor sins (Q, R). Whatever the parsing, the structure ascends: reverence as the constant, belief-and-deeds as the content, excellence as the crown. The reverent-believing-doing-virtuous are blame-proof — not because flawless, but because *oriented*: faces set Godward, stumbles covered, trajectory true.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:94

> **O you who believe! God will surely try you with something of the game you obtain with your hands and your spears, so that God may know the one who fears Him unseen. So whosoever transgresses after that, his shall be a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Game Within Reach: The Trial of the Unwatched Pilgrim**

*God will try you* (*la-yabluwannakum* — emphatic) *with game within hands'-and-spears' reach* — small game (birds, eggs) graspable by hand, large game spearable (Q, Ṭ) — while in *iḥrām*, when hunting is banned (vv. 1, 95–96). The trial's cruelty is its easiness: helpless creatures, no escape, no witnesses — *so God may know who fears Him unseen* (*bi'l-ghayb*). *Unseen* cuts both ways: God unseen by the pilgrim, pilgrim unseen by men — reverence with neither spectacle nor supervision. The design exposes worship's true object: the pilgrim who abstains before crowds but snatches in solitude worships crowds; who abstains in solitude worships God. Every profession has its *iḥrām*-game: the cashier's uncounted bills, the official's unminuted meeting, the scholar's undetectable plagiarism. *Whosoever transgresses after that — painful punishment*: after clarity, after warning, after the trial's terms published — no mitigation remains. The unwatched moment is the soul's truest biography; God reads it, and the Final Edition cites it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:95

> **O you who believe! Slaughter not any game while you are in a state of pilgrim sanctity. And [for] the one among you who slaughters it intentionally, a recompense of cattle, the like of that which he slaughtered, as judged by two just men among you, as an offering brought to the Kaʿbah, or the expiation of feeding the indigent, or the equivalent of this in fasting, that he may taste the evil consequences of his affair. God has forgiven that which is past, but whosoever relapses, God will take vengeance upon him, and God is Mighty, Possessor of Vengeance.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pilgrim's Game Ban: Harmlessness as Worship**

*Slaughter no game in iḥrām* — ḥajj or ʿumrah (the life-taking itself banned; others eating the kill incur nothing (Q)). Lethal threats — lion, wolf, scorpion — remain killable (necessity overrides). The occasion: a Helper's wild-donkey kill on ʿumrah, 628 (Q). *Intentionally* (*mutaʿammidan*) — deliberate violation; the forgetful and accidental stand excused (the Law's constant mercy to error). The ban's rationale transcends ecology: *iḥrām* rehearses the soul's disarmament before God — the predator practices harmlessness, the taker takes nothing, the hand trained daily in acquisition lies deliberately empty. Makkah's ḥaram extends the logic spatially (no hunting, no tree-felling, even un-consecrated; Madinah per some reports) — geography itself consecrated to non-violence. The pilgrim returns home having *been*, for days, a creature that kills nothing — a curriculum no sermon teaches: peacefulness as bodily habit, mercy as muscle memory.

**Two Just Men and Three Expiations: Restorative Justice at the Kaʿbah**

Violators owe *recompense* (*jazā'*): *like-for-like cattle* (or monetary equivalent (Q, Ṭ)) — valued by *two just men* (*dhawā ʿadl* — communal arbitration embedded in worship-law) — *as offering to the Kaʿbah* (distributed to Makkah's needy (JJ, Q)); *or feeding indigents* equivalently; *or fasting* equivalently (a day per half-liter food-measure (Q)). *That he may taste* (*yadhūqa* — savor, suffer) *the evil consequence* (*wabāl*) *of his affair* — punishment as *tasting*: consequences internalized, not merely priced. *God has forgiven the past* — pre-Islamic violations (Ṭ) — *but relapsers face vengeance* (*yantaqimu*; *ʿAzīz dhū'ntiqām* — Mighty, Vengeance-Possessor (cf. 3:4; 14:47; 39:37)). The system is restorative before retributive: value assessed, poor fed, soul fasted — ledgers balanced across three parties (God, community, self). Relapse after restoration, though, exhausts the pedagogy — what remains is vengeance. Mercy educates once; contempt of its classroom answers to Might.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:96

> **The game of the sea and the food thereof is made lawful unto you — an enjoyment for you and for the travelers — but the game of the land is forbidden unto you so long as you remain in a state of pilgrim sanctity. And reverence God, unto Whom you shall be gathered.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sea's Bounty: Lawful in Every State**

*Sea-game and its food lawful* — fish, fresh and saltwater (Ṭ, Ṭū), *an enjoyment for you and travelers* — residents' fresh catch, wayfarers' salted provisions (Ṭ, Ṭū). Boundaries debated: predatory fish, frogs (Mālikīs allow, others not (Q)); Jaʿfarīs require scales (shrimp admitted); Ḥanafīs dispute shellfish; pilgrims mostly confined to water-only creatures (JJ, Q, Ṭ). *Its food*: shore-washed dead fish for most — the sea's purity-exception to carrion-rules (2:173; 5:3; 6:145; 16:115), the sea being purifier (ablution-valid (Q)) — versus Shīʿī rejection of washed-up dead, reading *game* as fresh-caught and *food* as pre-preserved travel-stock (Ṭs, Ṭū). The sea's liberality carries theology: Earth's most alien element yields food with fewest restrictions — God's bounty overflowing categories. The pilgrim, barred from land's meat, finds the sea still open: consecration narrows some doors precisely so others' openness can be *noticed*. Restriction teaches appreciation; the fish tastes better in *iḥrām* because the venison is forbidden.

**Land-Game in Iḥrām: Eating What Others Killed**

*Land-game forbidden while consecrated* — but how far? Verse 95 bans *killing*; this verse seemingly bans *eating* all hunted land-meat in *iḥrām*. Positions span: ʿAlī — all game-meat discouraged (*makrūh*) for pilgrims; ʿUmar, ʿUthmān — others'-killed lawful (Ṭ); the Prophet ﷺ returning gifted game-meat (as hunted *for him*) — with the ḥadīth permitting others'-killed unless hunted *by* or *for* the pilgrim (Ṭ); Mālikī-Shāfiʿī-Ḥanbalī adoption of that rule; Ḥanafīs permitting even hunted-for-him (only self-killed banned (Q)); ʿAlī-Ibn ʿAbbās-others and Jaʿfarīs banning all hunted-meat for pilgrims (Q, Ṭs); pre-*iḥrām* kills disputed (Ibn ʿAbbās and Shīʿah: still banned (Q, Ṭū)). The spectrum models juristic mercy: one ban, five readings, each anchored in text-and-precedent. *Reverence God, unto whom you shall be gathered* (*tuḥsharūn*) — the Gathering seals the game-laws: appetites now, assembly then. The pilgrim who governs his stomach governs, by training, his Resurrection-stance.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:97

> **God has made the Kaʿbah, the Sacred House, a support for mankind, and the sacred month, and the offerings, and the garlands, this that you might know that God knows whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth, and that God is Knower of all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qiyāman: The Kaʿbah as Mankind's Standing-Place**

*God has made the Kaʿbah, the Sacred House* (*al-bayt al-ḥarām*), *a support* (*qiyāman* — uprightness, maintenance, standing) *for mankind* (*nās*) — with the sacred month(s) (Dhū'l-Ḥijjah specifically (Z), or all four (R, Z; cf. v. 2)), offerings, and garlands (cf. v. 2). *Qiyām* is standing-language: the House keeps humanity *upright* — morally, socially, spiritually. Commentators historicize: stateless Arabia's sole neutral ground — birds unhunted, humans unshed-blooded — holding "strong from weak, evil from virtuous, oppressor from oppressed" (Ṭ); pre-Islamic Arab order, now Muslim rite-orienter (R, Ṭ); plus al-Rāzī's phenomenology — humanity's innate sense of the House's majesty, hearts drawn Kaʿbah-ward by constitution (R). The verse universalizes (cf. 2:125; 3:96; 22:25) even as access particularizes (post-9:1–28 Muslim exclusivity): significance for all, entry for the consecrated — the world's spiritual capital with citizenship by creed. Every civilization needs an *unmovable center*; the Qur'an nominates one stone cube in a valley, and a billion compasses agree.

**Sanctuary as Epistemology: Knowing God Through Protected Things**

*That you might know God knows all in heavens and earth* — sanctuary-legislation as *theology lesson*: the intricate wisdom of sacred places-months-offerings-garlands evidences Omniscience designing human peace. Who but the All-Knowing could engineer, for a war-prone species, a system of inviolable times, spaces, beasts, and travelers that actually *held* for centuries? The institution is the argument: observe the peace-mechanism, infer the Peaceful Mind. *Knower of all things* (*ʿalā kulli shay'in ʿalīm*) — knowledge as the verse's seal because knowledge is the verse's exhibit. The agencies of order — calendars, sanctuaries, customs — are divine pedagogy in social form; political science that ignores them reads effects without Cause. Study the Kaʿbah-system and conclude: this is not tribal accident but Omniscient architecture — and its Architect knows *you* with the same encompassing precision.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:98

> **Know that God is severe in retribution, and that God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Severity and Mercy: The Believer's Binocular Vision**

*Know* (*iʿlamū* — imperative of certain knowledge): *God is severe in retribution* (*shadīd al-ʿiqāb* — cf. vv. 2, 3:11; 40:22; 59:4, 7) *and Forgiving, Merciful* (cf. 13:6; 40:3). The pairing commands binocular faith: *fear* through one eye (*His severity*), *hope* through the other (*His mercy*) — depth perception requires both. The tradition's maxim: *"The believer's fear and hope, weighed, would balance"* (R). Some read mercy's second position as predominance (R) — per the qudsī *"My mercy outstrips My wrath"* (al-Bukhārī 7422; Muslim 2751). The verse's placement — sealing pilgrimage-legislation (like v. 2) — applies both lenses to law-keeping: severity deters violation, mercy welcomes the violated-back. Monocular religion distorts: fear-only breeds despair's paralysis or scrupulosity's prison; hope-only breeds presumption's license. The verse prescribes the ophthalmology of orthodoxy: two eyes open, both fixed on the same God — severe enough to flee toward, merciful enough to flee to.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:99

> **Only the proclamation is incumbent upon the Messenger. And God knows what you disclose and what you conceal.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Proclamation as the Prophet's Whole Burden**

*Only the proclamation* (*balāgh*) *is upon the Messenger* (cf. vv. 67, 92) — the mission's boundary-stone, repeated across the Qur'an (3:20; 5:92; 13:40; 16:82; 29:18; 36:17; 42:48; 64:12). The Prophet ﷺ neither compels reception nor manufactures guidance — delivery, clear and complete, discharges him entirely. The verse liberates every warner in his train: the parent who taught purely, the teacher who explained fully, the friend who advised sincerely — all stand discharged whatever the audience did. Conversely it indicts every proclaimer who adulterates delivery with coercion, manipulation, or concealment: the *only* includes the *mere* — proclamation *alone*, unmixed with compulsion's additives. *God knows what you disclose and conceal* (cf. 2:33; 11:5; 14:38; 16:19, 23; 24:29; 27:25, 74; 28:69; 33:54; 36:76; 60:1; 64:4) — the audience's audit belongs to Omniscience: public compliance and private defiance both inventoried. Proclaimers judge externals; God judges interiors. The division of labor is perfect: messengers speak to ears, God reads breasts — and neither does the other's job.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:100

> **Say, "The good and the wicked are not equal, though the abundance of the wicked may impress you." So reverence God, O possessors of intellect, that you may prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Quality Against Quantity: The Few That Outweigh the Many**

*The good* (*ṭayyib*) *and wicked* (*khabīth*) *are not equal* — persons (or things licit-and-illicit (Ṭs, Ṭū)) — *though the wicked's abundance impress you* (*yuʿjibka kathratu'l-khabīth*). The Qur'an's anti-majoritarian axiom (cf. 9:19; 11:23–24; 16:76; 35:19; 39:29; 57:10; 59:20; 39:9's knower-vs-ignorant): numbers never certify worth. The ḥadīth's image: *"Mankind is like a hundred camels — hardly one fit to ride"* (Aj; cf. al-Bukhārī 6498). Democracy counts heads; revelation weighs hearts — the procedures answer different questions (governance versus goodness) and must never be confused. Majorities choose policies; they do not constitute virtues. The verse arms every righteous minority — the honest office, the faithful family, the unstained circle — against abundance-intimidation: *kathrah* (multitude) impresses eyes, not scales. One *ṭayyib* soul outweighs cities of *khabīth*; eternity's arithmetic counts mass, not census. Never defect to the numerous from awe of numerosity — the crowd is large, and the crowd is light.

**Possessors of Intellect: The Kernel-Knowers**

*O possessors of intellect* (*ūlī'l-albāb* — *lubb*: pith, kernel, innermost essence (Iṣ); cf. 2:179, 197, 269; 3:7, 190; 12:111; 13:19; 14:52; 38:29, 43; 39:9, 18, 21; 40:54; 65:10): knowers of things' *inner essences* — intellect (*ʿaql* as unitive faculty) versus reason (discursive logic): direct kernel-perception versus inferential shell-measuring. Ibn ʿAjībah: *"pure hearts avoiding the wicked though many, cleaving to the good though few."* The address restricts the verse's perception: only *albāb*-possessors *see* quality's superiority — others see only counts. Intellect is thus moral before mental: purity of heart calibrates the scale that weighs good-and-wicked. *Reverence God... that you may prosper* (*tufliḥūn* — farmer's harvest-success): *taqwā* sown, *falāḥ* reaped — the verse's economics complete. Sharpen intellect by purifying heart; purify heart by revering God; revere God by preferring the good-few over the wicked-many. The kernel-knowers inherit the harvest.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:101

> **O you who believe! Ask not about things which, if they were disclosed to you, would trouble you. And if you ask about them while the Quran is being sent down, they will be disclosed to you. God has pardoned this, and God is Forgiving, Clement.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Questions That Burden: The Ḥajj-Every-Year Incident**

*Ask not about things which, disclosed, would trouble you* (*tasu'kum* — grieve, harm, burden). The prime occasion: the Prophet ﷺ announcing ḥajj's obligation; a man demanding *"every year?"* — thrice ignored, then answered: *"No — had I said yes, it would have become obligatory!"* — adding *"leave me regarding what I leave you; those before you were destroyed by questioning and disputing with prophets"* (Q, Ṭ, W, Z). The principle: revelation's silences are often *mercies* — ambiguities as leniencies; interrogating them into specificity *hardens* obligation. Other occasions: test-questions (my father? my lost camel? — answers exposing shame (Q, Ṭ, W; cf. R)) and camel-cult queries (v. 103's *baḥīrah-sā'ibah-waṣīlah-ḥām* (Q, Ṭ)). The verse disciplines curiosity: *legitimate* guidance-questions remain welcome (Q, R) — the Prophet ﷺ answered thousands — but *trouble-inviting* questions (demanding burdens, exposing veiled shames, testing the warner) are banned. Ask to *practice*, not to *problematize*; revelation rewards the first curiosity with light and the second with weight.

**"While the Quran Is Being Sent Down": Revelation's Open Session**

*If you ask while the Qur'an is being sent down* — the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime, revelation's open session (JJ, Z) — *they will be disclosed*: warning (questions then *compelled* answers, hardening law (JJ, Z)) and license (genuine obscurities *clarified* on asking (R)). The window has closed with the Prophet's ﷺ death — no new disclosures, only interpretation — which makes the verse's *pardon* (*ʿafā'Llāh* — past excesses forgiven (Q, R, Ṭ, Z), or exposed sins covered (R)) the generation's closing amnesty. *Forgiving* (*Ghafūr*), *Clement* (*Ḥalīm* — forbearing, unhasty in punishment): the Names suit questioners — sins of curiosity covered, punishment for pestering withheld. Post-revelation believers inherit the etiquette: press scholars for *practiced* guidance, not hypotheticals; accept merciful silences as rulings; and when answers trouble — the unveiled father, the hardened duty — remember the verse warned before you asked. Some doors are closed for your sake; knocking is permitted, breaking-and-entering is not.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:102

> **A people before you asked about these things, then came to be disbelievers therein.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Precedents of Ruin: When Questions Became Unbelief**

*A people before you asked such things, then disbelieved therein* (*thumma aṣbaḥū bihā kāfirīn* — became disbelievers *about them*: rejecting the very answers they demanded). The archive: Israel demanding *show us God openly* — seized by thunderbolt (4:153); demanding a king, then caviling at Saul (2:246–47); demanding cow-specifications till the command hardened near-impossibly (2:67–71); Thamūd demanding Ṣāliḥ's sign, then hamstringing the she-camel — destroyed (7:73–79; 11:61–68; 26:141–58 (Q, Ṭ, Z)); the apostles' Table-request with its unprecedented-punishment proviso (vv. 112–115 (Q, Ṭ, Z)). The pattern: *demanded signs harden into rejected proofs* — each answered question raising the stakes of subsequent refusal. Asking is bidding at auction: every disclosure is a price quoted, and winners must pay in obedience. The hammer falls heaviest on those who bid for spectacle: Ṣāliḥ's camel, Moses' cow-specifications, the Table — signs given, signs spurned, sentences executed. The verse counsels holy reticence: desire signs sparingly, ask weightily, and — once answered — *believe*. The question you press today becomes the testimony against you tomorrow if you disbelieve its answer.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:103

> **God has not established baḥīrah, or sā'ibah, or waṣīlah, or ḥām. But those who disbelieve fabricate lies against God, and most of them do not understand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sacred Camels: Idolatry's Livestock Legislation**

*God has not established* (*mā jaʿala* — never ordained) *baḥīrah, sā'ibah, waṣīlah, ḥām* — four camel-classes the idolaters consecrated: *baḥīrah* (dam of five, fifth male (Q, R) — slit-eared, free-grazing, unmilked-unridden); *sā'ibah* (vow-freed female — for healing, journey-safety, gratitude (R); or fully-emancipated slave (R)); *waṣīlah* (prolific female-bearer (JJ), or twin-bearer male-and-female (R)); *ḥām* (the sire — multi-generational stallion (JJ, R), unburdened-unridden (R)). Slit ears marked their sacrality (cf. 4:119, Satan's livestock-mutilation vow). The Qur'an's livestock-polemic runs parallel (6:136–39, 143–45; 10:59): humanity's first deviation from Abrahamic monotheism, say reports, began with such consecrations (Ṭ). The critique is two-edged: *idolatrous orientation* (dedicated to gods, not God) and *bounty-restriction* (withholding lawful use (cf. vv. 87–88)). Every age mints its *sā'ibahs* — resources sacralized out of use by custom, taboo, or ideology while needy go without. Revelation's audit is blunt: God ordained no such consecration — the dedication is human invention wearing divine livery.

**Fabricating Against God: Invention as the Unforgiven Genre**

*Those who disbelieve fabricate lies against God* (*yaftarūn ʿala'Llāhi'l-kadhib* — forging divine sanction for human inventions (cf. 3:24, 93–94; 6:21, 93, 137–40, 144; 7:37, 152; 10:17–18, 59–60, 69; 16:56, 116)) — *and most understand not* (*lā yaʿqilūn*). *Iftirā'* (fabrication-against-God) is the Qur'an's supreme intellectual crime: misattributing to the Lawgiver what the forger legislated. The camel-cults illustrate its mechanics — custom calcifies into "religion," then "religion" claims God's signature. The Prophet ﷺ reportedly named the innovator: ʿAmr ibn Luḥayy, first to release the *sā'ibah* and alter Abraham's religion — seen dragging his intestines in Hell (al-Bukhārī 3521; Muslim 2856). Institutional founders of misguidance pay founder's rates (cf. Cain, v. 29-comm.). The verse's vigilance-task falls on every generation's scholars: distinguish *jaʿala'Llāh* (God-ordained) from *iftarā* (forged) — demanding proof-texts for consecrations, refusing "we found our fathers" as legislation (v. 104). What God never established, no clergy may establish for Him.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:104

> **And when it is said unto them, "Come unto that which God has sent down, and unto the Messenger," they say, "Sufficient for us is that which we have found our fathers practicing." What! Even if their fathers knew naught and were not rightly guided?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Sufficient for Us Is Our Fathers": Traditionalism on Trial**

*Come to what God sent down and to the Messenger* — *sufficient for us* (*ḥasbunā*) *is what we found our fathers practicing* (cf. 2:170). The fathers-excuse pervades prophetic narratives: outrage at abandoning patrimony (34:43; 7:70; 10:78; 11:62, 87; 14:10; 23:24; 28:36; 31:21; 43:22–23), blame-deflection for indecency (7:28), Abraham's rebuke of uncritical inheritance (21:51–54; 26:69–76). The verse founds Islam's war on *taqlīd* (blind following) in doctrine (R): at minimum, *worthy* sources must be sought (R, Z). Heritage is data, not verdict: fathers transmit both treasure and trash, and unexamined transmission compounds the trash generationally. Traditions deserve a hearing — antiquity sometimes encodes forgotten wisdom — but never a veto: every inherited practice must survive confrontation with *what God sent down*. The idolaters' *ḥasbunā* ("sufficient") ironically inverts the believer's *ḥasbuna'Llāh* ("God suffices us," 3:173): sufficiency rightly belongs to God alone; misplaced onto fathers, it becomes idolatry of ancestry.

**"Even If Their Fathers Knew Naught?": The Devastating Qualifier**

*What!* (*a-wa-law* — incredulous interrogative) *Even if their fathers knew naught* (*lā yaʿlamūna shay'an*) *and were not guided* (*lā yahtadūn*)? The qualifier demolishes traditionalism's premise: inheritance transmits *ignorance* as faithfully as knowledge. Following the unknowing-unguided because they are *ours* is loyalty misaddressed — kinship felt, truth abandoned. The Qur'an's anthropology underwrites the demand: every soul testified pretemporally to God's Lordship (7:172) and carries *fiṭrah* (30:30) — innate equipment making *personal* verification obligatory. No one arrives at Judgment pleading successfully "my fathers misled me" — the equipment was issued to all. The verse commissions intellectual courage as religious duty: examine patrimony, retain its gold, discard its dross — and bear the family's displeasure as Abraham bore his people's. Truth is older than any father; return to the oldest Father of truths.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:105

> **O you who believe! You have charge of your own souls. He who is astray cannot harm you if you are rightly guided. Unto God shall be your return all together, and He shall inform you of what you used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Charge of Your Own Souls: Responsibility's Inner Citadel**

*You have charge of your own souls* (*ʿalaykum anfusakum* — upon you is [care of] yourselves; or imperative: *take charge!* (Q, R)) — against verse 104's father-following: souls are *individually* answerable; neither fathers' error (*he who is astray* — the misguiding fathers, disbelievers generally, or Satan, powerless over souls (14:22; 15:42; 16:99; 17:65; 34:21)) *can harm you if rightly guided*. The verse draws responsibility's inner citadel: others' choices cannot breach your guided soul — no tempter, tyrant, or tradition compels your sin. Victimhood-as-excuse dies here: "society made me," "family pressured me," "Satan overcame me" — all voided, for the rightly-guided are harm-proof. The consolation is immense for minorities-in-corruption: the righteous in wicked cities, the honest in crooked firms, the chaste in shameless cohorts — surrounding astray-ness cannot *harm* (eschatologically) the self-charged soul. Fortify the citadel; the siege, however loud, cannot enter uninvited.

**Forbidding Wrong Survives: What the Verse Does Not Cancel**

Commentators fenced the verse zealously against quietism: it does *not* cancel enjoining-right-forbidding-wrong (3:104, 110, 114; 7:157, 199; 9:67) — it declares the *admonisher unaccountable for rejected admonition* (R, Ṭ, Z). Variants: charge means your *own community* (not outsiders (R)); or grief-ban over recalcitrance (R; cf. vv. 92, 99 — the Prophet ﷺ himself uncontrolled of reception). The apocalyptic reading — verse effective only in a future impervious age (Ṭ, Th) — the tradition deems weak: the verse governs *now*. The balance is Islam's activism-serenity synthesis: *strive* to guide (duty), *release* results (serenity) — effort commanded, efficacy entrusted. Burnout comes from confusing the two — grieving unheeded counsel as personal failure. The verse prohibits precisely that grief: deliver purely, release fully. *Unto God your return all together; He will inform you of your deeds* (cf. vv. 48; 6:60; 9:94, 105; 10:23; 29:8; 31:15; 39:7; 62:8) — the Final Audit settles all: counsel given and counsel spurned, each recorded, each recompensed. Work for that disclosure; rest in it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:106

> **O you who believe! The witness between you, when death approaches one of you, at the time of the bequest, shall be two just men among you, or two from among other than yourselves if you are traveling upon the earth when death befalls you. Detain the two of them after the prayer, and let them both swear by God, if you doubt [them], "We will not sell it for any price, though he were a kinsman, nor will we conceal the witness of God, for then we would surely be among the sinners."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Witnesses at Death's Door: Bequest-Law's Safeguards**

*Witness* (*shahādah* — or "witness to what is with you," variant reading) *at bequest-time: two just men* (*dhawā ʿadl* — two Muslim males, masculine adjective (Q, Ṭ); minority: two neighbors (Q, Ṭ)) *among you — or two from others* (non-Muslims when traveling among them (Q, Ṭ): any community per some, Scripture-and-Zoroastrian per others (Ṭ) — Hindu testimony admitted in Indian courts historically; or non-neighbors per the minority (Q, Ṭ)). Bequest (*waṣiyyah*): up to one-third to non-heirs (heirs assigned fixed shares, 4:11–12, superseding 2:180's general bequest (cf. 2:180, 240-comm.)); bequests execute *before* division (4:11–12). Witness-duality matches debt (2:282) and orphan-property (4:6) laws — property-transfers demand corroboration. The occasion: a Muslim dying journeying with two Christians, entrusted goods delivered minus a silver vessel — discovered via an heir's recognition, Prophet-ordered oaths (Ṭ, Z). Death's door invites theft (the dead cannot audit); the verse posts two guards at it. Estate-planning in Islam is thus *worship-legislated*: write bequests, witness them doubly, and bar the posthumous plunder the dying cannot prevent.

**Detained After Prayer: Oaths at Piety's Peak**

*Detain them after the prayer* (*ṣalāh* — ʿaṣr per Prophetic oath-practice (Ṭ); or their *own* prayer if non-Muslim (Ṭ) — prayer-fresh hearts oath-truest) — *if doubted* (oaths conditional on suspicion (Q, Ṭ)) — *swearing by God* (oaths' proper Object: *"whoever swears, let him swear by God or be silent"* (Q; cf. al-Bukhārī 6646); al-Shāfiʿī admits Qur'an-swearing (Q)): *"we will not sell it"* (the testimony — no profiting (Ṭ)) *"for any price, though he were kinsman, nor conceal God's witness — else we are sinners."* The oath's two clauses seal testimony's two corruptions: *sale* (bribery, bias, kin-favor) and *concealment* (suppression). Post-prayer timing leverages worship's afterglow — hearts softened by standing before God lie hardest. The procedure models evidentiary psychology: solemnize the moment (detention, prayer-freshness, Divine Name), specify the betrayals (sale, concealment), invoke the consequence (sinner-status). Courts secure truth less by threats than by *conscience-architecture* — and this verse is its blueprint.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:107

> **But if it is discovered that the two of them are guilty of sin, then two others shall stand in their place, from those most entitled as claimants, and swear by God, "Indeed, our witness is truer than their witness, and we have not transgressed, for then we would surely be among the wrongdoers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Counter-Oaths: The Claimants' Second Standing**

*If the two are found guilty of sin* (*ithm* — trust-betrayal as bequest-witnesses (Q, Ṭ)) — *two others stand in their place* (*yaqūmān maqāmahumā*): *the most entitled claimants* (*awlayān* — bequest-beneficiaries wronged (Ṭ), or nearest-to-the-deceased in knowledge-and-right (Z)) — *swearing: "our witness is truer than theirs; we have not transgressed* (*iʿtadaynā* — exceeded testimony's bounds (Ṭ)) *— else we are wrongdoers."* Claimants swear *because interested* (disinterested witnesses need no oath absent doubt (Ṭ)): interest discounts credibility, oath restores it. The procedure's genius is adversarial balance — false witnesses face contradiction not by judicial fiat but by *counter-oath*, perjury meeting sworn rebuttal. Truth emerges collision-forged: oath against oath, with God's Name invoked on both sides daring the liar to persist. Legal systems fear interested testimony; this verse *uses* it — channeled, sworn, bounded by transgression-limits. The interested know most; rightly constrained, they prove most. Justice here is not blind to interest but *harnessed* through it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:108

> **Thus it is more likely that they will bear true witness, or fear that their oaths will be countered by oaths thereafter. And reverence God and listen. God guides not iniquitous people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Likelier Truth: Deterrence Designed into Procedure**

*Thus likelier* (*adnā* — nearer, more likely) *they bear true witness* (*bi'l-wajh* — witness on its right face) — *or fear counter-oaths thereafter*: verses 106–107's machinery aims at *probability*, not certainty — law's honest ambition. Two motives engineered: conscience (true witness) and calculation (fear of contradiction) — the righteous moved by the first, the rogue by the second. Procedure that relies on conscience alone is naive; on fear alone, tyrannical — this design runs both engines. Public trust in bequests rises; oath-solemnity impresses all (Q). The passage joins truthful-witness (4:135; 5:8) and trust-fidelity (2:283; 4:58; 8:27; 23:8–11; 70:32–35) legislation — testimony as *amānah* (trust), perjury as breach. *Reverence God and listen* (*ismaʿū* — hear-and-obey) — *God guides not the iniquitous* (*fāsiqīn*): guidance withdrawn from oath-breakers as sentence. The courtroom's ceiling is thus theological: human procedure maximizes truth's likelihood; Divine guidance crowns — or abandons — the hearts within it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:109

> **The day when God will gather the messengers and say, "What response did you receive?" they will say, "We have no knowledge. Truly it is Thou Who knowest best the things unseen."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"What Response Did You Receive?": The Prophets on the Stand**

*The Day God gathers the messengers* (*yajmaʿu'Llāhu'l-rusul*) *and asks: "what response* (*mādhā ujibtum* — what answer were you given)?" — the eschatological audit opening (read *"beware the Day..."* (R, Ṭ, Ṭs, Z), continuing v. 108's *reverence-and-listen* or *guides-not-the-iniquitous* (R, Z)). The question *rebukes peoples through prophets* (Bḍ, Ṭ): communities' responses exhibited via leaders' mouths (Th). Why ask the Omniscient-knowing? For *manifestation* (*iẓhār*): Judgment displays what earth concealed — every nation's answer to its warner, staged publicly. Leaders testify first: prophets for communities, then communities for selves. The scene warns all who inherit prophetic office — scholars, inviters, parents: your *delivery* will be examined, your audience's *response* exhibited, and the two weighed separately. Prepare both dockets: a delivery defensible ("I conveyed") and — by effort, not control — a response hopeful.

**"We Have No Knowledge": Humility Before Omniscience**

*"We have no knowledge* (*lā ʿilma lanā*); *Thou knowest best the unseen"* (cf. v. 116). Explanations weighed: terror-struck silence before composure (Ṭ, Z) — strained against believers' fearlessness that Day (2:62; 21:103 (R, Ṭs)); ignorance of posthumous developments and secret intentions (R, Ṭ, Z) — strained against prophets' witness-office (2:143; 4:69; 22:78; 57:19); *humility* — "none save what Thou taught us" (angels' formula, 2:32 (Bḍ, R, Ṭ, Z)), externals-known-but-secrets-God's (R), judgment-entrusted to God (Z). The last reading crowns: perfect knowledge confessing its limits before Perfect Knowledge — prophets modeling creatureliness at history's climax. Even those who *know* (their communities' deeds) defer to Him who knows *wholly* (deeds-plus-secrets-plus-contexts). The witnesses' first testimony is to their *need* of witness-verification. Learn the etiquette: before pronouncing on others' responses to truth, pronounce on your own knowledge's bounds — *lā ʿilma lanā* first, testimony second.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:110

> **Then God will say, "O Jesus son of Mary! Remember My Blessing upon thee, and upon thy mother, when I strengthened thee with the Holy Spirit, that thou mightest speak to people in the cradle and in maturity; and when I taught thee the Book, Wisdom, the Torah, and the Gospel; and how thou wouldst create out of clay the shape of a bird, by My Leave, and thou wouldst breathe into it, and it would become a bird, by My Leave; and thou wouldst heal the blind and the leper, by My Leave; and thou wouldst bring forth the dead, by My Leave; and how I restrained the Children of Israel from thee, when thou didst bring them clear proofs, and those who disbelieved among them said, 'This is naught but manifest sorcery.'**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jesus' Curriculum Vitae: Blessings Recited by God Himself**

*O Jesus son of Mary! Remember My blessing on thee and thy mother* — the Day's longest individual address, rehearsing Christ's Qur'anic dossier: *strengthened with the Holy Spirit* (*rūḥ al-qudus* — Gabriel, revelation's angel (Ṭ; cf. 16:102; 17:85; 26:193; 42:52; 97:4), or the Spirit-from-God's-Command (17:85), *qudus*-linked to *al-Quddūs* (59:23 (R)); cf. 2:87, 253; Jesus' Spirit-conception, 4:171; 19:17; 21:91; 66:12 — his title *Rūḥ Allāh*); *speech in cradle and maturity* (cf. 3:46; 19:29–30 — peculiarly Qur'anic); *taught Book-and-Wisdom* (scripture-plus-meaning (Ṭ); cf. 2:129, 151, 231; 3:164; 4:54, 113; 62:2), *Torah and Gospel*, *clear proofs* (cf. 2:87, 253). The recitation's setting matters: blessings enumerated *at Judgment*, before all — honor published eternally, and premise laid for verse 116's question (how did followers repay these blessings but by deifying the blessed?). Gratitude's audit begins with inventory; God's inventory of Jesus overflows. Every servant's blessings likewise stand inventoried — the Day's first agenda is always *remember*.

**"By My Leave": Miracles as Borrowed Omnipotence**

*Clay birds breathed alive* (cf. 3:49; Infancy Gospel of Thomas), *blind-and-lepers healed* (cf. 3:49-comm.), *dead raised* (Gospels-attested too) — each stamped ***by My Leave*** (*bi-idhnī*), fourfold. The refrain is the verse's doctrinal load-bearing wall: creation (cf. Adam's fashioning-breathing, 15:26–29; 32:7–9; 38:71–72), healing (God's alone, 26:80), resurrection (God's throughout) — all *lent*, never owned. Deification reads miracles as nature; revelation reads them as *authorization* — power-of-attorney, not power-of-essence. *I restrained Israel from thee* — the thwarted killing (cf. 4:157) — *when thou brought clear proofs; disbelievers said "manifest sorcery"* (cf. Jesus, 61:6; Muḥammad ﷺ, 6:7; 10:2; 21:3; 43:30; 46:7; 54:2; 74:24; Moses, 10:76; 20:57; 27:13; 28:36; messengers generally, 11:7; 34:43; 37:15). Sorcery-accusation is denial's standard solvent — dissolving signs into tricks. The verse preserves both: miracles genuine (Divine Leave), accusers recorded (human spite). Signs never compel; they *oblige* — obliging the honest to believe, the dishonest to explain away.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:111

> **And when I inspired the apostles to believe in Me and in My messenger, they said, 'We believe. Bear witness that we are submitters.'"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Apostles' Inspiration: Belief Cast into Hearts**

*When I inspired* (*awḥaytu* — *waḥy*-word, here "casting into hearts," sub-prophetic inspiration (IK, Ṭ)) *the apostles* (*ḥawāriyyūn*) *to believe in Me and My messenger* — *they said: we believe; bear witness we are submitters* (*muslimūn* (cf. 3:52–53 — there followed by plotting (3:54))). Divine initiative precedes human confession: God *casts*, apostles *speak* — grace first, response second (cf. v. 54's love-order). *Belief* (*īmān*) before *submission* (*islām*): inward assent preceding outward conformity (R) — theology's classic sequence: heart-knowledge, tongue-affirmation, limb-action. The verse (opening the Table-narrative, vv. 111–115) establishes the apostles' *baseline orthodoxy* before their problematic request (v. 112): even the inspired-and-submitted can stumble into doubtful asking — status never immunizes. Their confession's public form (*bear witness*) models conversion's sociality: faith declared, community enrolled, record kept. Private belief unconfessed withers; confessed belief, witnessed, stands examinable — and examinable faith is improvable faith.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:112

> **When the apostles said, "O Jesus son of Mary! Is thy Lord able to send down to us from Heaven a table spread with food?" He said, "Reverence God, if you are believers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Is Thy Lord Able?": The Question That Sounds Like Doubt**

*Is thy Lord able* (*hal yastaṭīʿu rabbuka* — can thy Lord?) *to send down a Table* (*mā'idah* — the sūrah's namesake) *from Heaven?* The question *sounds* like doubt in Omnipotence — startling after verse 110's miracle-catalogue — prompting Jesus' *reverence God* (*ittaqū'Llāh* — fear God (Ṭ)), *if you are believers*: rebuke (ingratitude for given signs (R)) or reminder (reverence obtains requests (R)). Early harmonizations: apostles asking whether *Jesus* could *petition* God (not whether God *could* send (Ṭ)) — faith in prophethood, not Power, at issue (Ṭ, Z); sign-seeking paralleling Israel's demands (4:153; 6:37, 109; 7:203-comm.). Motives mixed: nourishment, heavenly-food blessing (Āl), post-thirty-day-fast need (Th — Jesus promising post-fast grants, paralleling ʿĪd al-fiṭr). Gospel-resonances: loaves-and-fishes multiplications (Matthew 14; Mark 6), Last Supper/Eucharist echoes, faith-rebukes over food (Matthew 6:31; 16:5–10; Luke 12; 17:15–21) — none exact (some: known only via Qur'an (IK)). The verse's caution-target: *redundant sign-seeking* — demanding new proofs while old ones stand unacted-upon. Signs are capital; requesting more while bankrupting current holdings invites audit, not largesse.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:113

> **They said, "We desire to eat from it, so that our hearts may be at peace, and we may know that thou hast spoken truthfully unto us, and we may be among the witnesses thereto."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hearts at Peace: Abraham's Request in Apostolic Mouths**

*We desire to eat from it* — *that our hearts may be at peace* (*taṭma'inna qulūbunā*), *that we may know thou hast spoken truthfully, and be witnesses to it* (*shuhadā'* — to the miracle for absentees, or to God's Oneness-Power and Jesus' prophethood (Ṭ, Z)). The heart-peace plea echoes Abraham verbatim: *"show me how Thou givest life to the dead... Yea, but that my heart may be at peace"* (2:260 (R)). The parallel dignifies the request: Abraham the Friend asked likewise — certainty-seeking (*istiqān*) differs from doubt (*shakk*): doubt questions *whether*, certainty-seeking asks *to witness how*. Faith's stages ascend: knowledge (*ʿilm*), then eye-witness (*ʿayn*), then tasted-truth (*ḥaqq*) — the apostles seek promotion from hearing to seeing. Jesus' rebuke (v. 112) and God's warning (v. 115) nonetheless price the promotion: witnessed-signs raise accountability's stakes — *witnesses* (*shuhadā'*) become *answerable* witnesses. The spiritual law: greater manifestation, greater liability. Seek heart-peace by all means — through worship, reflection, service — but know that *miraculous* peace arrives mortgaged: the Table eaten becomes testimony owed, and testimony spurned draws unprecedented sentence.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:114

> **Jesus son of Mary said, "O God, our Lord! Send down unto us a table from Heaven spread with food, to be a feast for us — for the first of us and the last of us — and a sign from Thee, and provide for us, for Thou art the best of providers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Feast-Prayer: Jesus' Table Petition**

*O God our Lord* (*Allāhumma rabbanā* — the double vocative of utmost entreaty)! *Send down a Table from Heaven — a feast* (*ʿīdan* — festival: joy-plus-return, recurring celebration) *for first and last of us, a sign from Thee — and provide for us; Thou art best of providers* (*khayr al-rāziqīn*). *First-and-last*: all followers sufficed (Ṭ, Z) — Gospel loaves-echo — and/or perpetual feast-day instituted (R, Ṭ, Z) — Sunday per some (Z), Eucharist-resonance. *ʿĪd* (returning joy) consecrates time: the Table's day becomes calendar's jewel — provision remembered annually, gratitude renewed cyclically. Contents disputed: fish (Ṭ, Z), meat-and-bread, paradisal fruits, all-but-meat (Ṭ) — one report: red cloth between two clouds, Jesus praying *"make me thankful; make it mercy, not punishment"* (R, Z). Above all, say contemplatives, the Table descends *spiritual* nourishment (R) — revelation's own feast. Jesus' petition models asking: address God's lordship, specify the need, dedicate its fruit (feast-and-sign), and close with His sufficiency (*best of providers*). Petitions so framed — need plus dedication plus Divine-praise — carry Heaven's recommended format.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:115

> **God said, "I shall indeed send it down unto you. But whosoever among you disbelieves thereafter, I shall surely punish him with a punishment wherewith I have not punished any other in all the worlds."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Descent Granted, Disbelief Priced: The Table's Terrible Proviso**

*I shall indeed send it down* (*munazziluhā* — emphatic promiser) — *but whosoever disbelieves thereafter* — *punishment unprecedented in all worlds* (*ʿālamīn*). The proviso prices witnessed-signs: manifestation-scale determines sentence-scale — the Table's diners face history's heaviest tariff for apostasy. Did it descend? Deniers: parable deterring future requests (R, Ṭ); withdrawal-and-repentance after the warning (IK, Ṭ, Z). The majority: descended — God's promise never fails (IK, R). The majority view preserves the sūrah's name-meaning: *al-Mā'idah*, the Table *sent*. The proviso's logic universalizes: privileges mortgaged — knowledge, miracles, guidance, community — foreclose heavily. Israel's manna-murmurers, Thamūd's camel-hamstringers, the Table's warned diners: one law — *the better fed the sight, the guiltier the blinded eye*. Modern believers, fed daily with revelation's Table (the Qur'an itself, spread and free), inherit the proviso proportionally: disbelief-after-clarity now draws sentence-after-mercy. Eat gratefully — diners are debtors.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:116

> **And when God said, "O Jesus son of Mary! Didst thou say unto mankind, 'Take me and my mother as gods apart from God?'" He said, "Glory be to Thee! It is not for me to utter that to which I have no right. Had I said it, Thou wouldst surely have known it. Thou knowest what is in my self and I know not what is in Thy Self. Truly it is Thou Who knowest best the things unseen.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Didst Thou Say?": The Question to the Deified**

*When God said: O Jesus! Didst thou say "take me and my mother as gods besides God?"* — on the Day (v. 109's session resumed), say most; some, post-ascension (3:55; 4:158). The placement — after the Table-warning (v. 115) — suggests the warned disbelief *is* this deification. Mary-as-goddess: not Nicene orthodoxy (Father-Son-Spirit), but *Theotokos*-trajectories ("Mother of God"), popular excesses approaching worship (cf. 9:31's lord-taking; 4:171; 5:73) — the Qur'an engaging lived religion alongside creeds (the Holy Spirit, being Gabriel Qur'anically, never deified). Al-Rāzī's criterion: Christians attributing *independent* miracle-creation to Jesus (rather than Divine Leave, v. 110) effectively deify (R). The question's purpose is manifestation, not information (Ṭ — informing Jesus of posthumous developments): the Court stages the record — *the Deified disowning deification* — before the deifiers. Every exaggerated cult faces this scene potentially: founder summoned, excesses exhibited, disavowal recorded. Build no devotion its object would disown.

**"Glory Be to Thee": Humility's Perfect Protocol**

*Glory be to Thee* (*subḥānaka* — transcendence-assertion first)! *Not for me to utter what is not my right* (*laysa lī bi-ḥaqq*) — comportment before denial (Bḍ, R): propriety preceding refutation. *Had I said it, Thou wouldst know* — testimony deferred to Omniscience (cf. v. 109 (R)). *Thou knowest what is in my self* (*nafsī* — human soul) *and I know not what is in Thy Self* (*nafsika* — Essence (R)) — knowledge-asymmetry confessed (whatever Jesus knows, God knows; God's knowledge unknowable (Z); God knowing Jesus' hidden, Jesus ignorant of God's hidden (R)). *Thou knowest best the unseen* (*ʿallām al-ghuyūb*). The protocol models all creaturely address to God: glorify first, disclaim second, defer third, confess fourth. No defensiveness, no self-justification, no counter-charge — Jesus the accused becomes Jesus the adorer. Interrogated by Earth or Heaven, adopt the transcript: *subḥānaka* opens every righteous defense, and deference closes it.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:117

> **I said naught to them save that which Thou commanded me: 'Worship God, my Lord and your Lord.' And I was a witness over them, so long as I remained among them. But when Thou didst take me [to Thyself], it was Thou Who wast the Watcher over them. And Thou art Witness over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"My Lord and Your Lord": The Prophetic Message in One Line**

*I said only what Thou commanded: worship God, my Lord and your Lord* (*rabbī wa rabbakum*) — the prophetic *kerygma* uniform across Hūd (11:56), Moses (40:27; 44:20), Jesus (3:51; 5:72; 19:36; 43:64), Muḥammad ﷺ (2:139; 42:15). *My Lord* first: the warner worships before summoning — credibility's prerequisite. *And your Lord*: the summons universalizes — no exemptions, no intermediaries. The sentence's architecture refutes deification grammatically: subject (God), verb (worship), objects (all, *including the speaker*) — the speaker inside the commanded class cannot be the commanded-to. Fourteen centuries of christological edifice meet eleven Arabic words. Brevity is orthodoxy's friend: the longer the creed, the more seams for error; *worship God, my Lord and yours* has no seams. Every invitation should reduce to this before expanding — expansion without this core is architecture without foundation.

**Witness Then, Watcher Now: Custody Transferred to God**

*I was witness* (*shahīd*) *over them while among them; when Thou tookest me* (*tawaffaytanī* — took me to Thyself), *Thou wast Watcher* (*raqīb*) *over them; Thou art Witness* (*shahīd*) *over all*. Custody transfers at death: prophets witness their lifetimes; God watches all time. The limitation humbly confessed (v. 116's protocol continued) — Jesus claims no posthumous surveillance, no grave-side governance. The theology corrects saint-cults expecting the dead to monitor devotees: the dead witness *nothing* currently; the Living watches *all* constantly. *Raqīb* (Watcher) and *Shahīd* (Witness) — the Names close every gap: no unwatched interval between prophets, no unwitnessed deed in any era. The verse quietly answers verse 109's framework: prophets testify their tenures; God testifies eternity. Human responsibility spans presence; Divine vigilance spans all. Serve your tenure wholly — the Watcher covers the rest.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:118

> **If Thou punisheth them, they are indeed Thy servants, but if Thou forgiveth them, then indeed Thou art the Mighty, the Wise."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Punish or Forgive: Intercession's Perfect Form**

*If Thou punish them — they are Thy servants* (*ʿibāduk* — Thine to dispose, justly); *if Thou forgive — Thou art Mighty* (*ʿAzīz* — none constraining pardon), *Wise* (*Ḥakīm* — none questioning it). Jesus neither recommends sentence nor pleads pardon specifically — entrusting all to God (R): intercession's perfect form — presenting the case (*Thy servants*) and the attributes (*Mighty, Wise*), prescribing nothing. The deification-errors, many hold, rank as forgivable sins rather than unforgivable *shirk* (cf. 4:48, 116 (R)) — Ashʿarīs refusing to fetter Divine freedom to human schemata (R). The Prophet ﷺ cherished this verse: Abū Dharr reports him ﷺ reciting it all night in prayer, then explaining he sought — and was granted — intercession for all free of *shirk* (IK). The verse thus bridges Jesus' intercession-style and Muḥammad's ﷺ intercession-office: present servants, invoke Might-and-Wisdom, receive mercy. Supplicants for others should copy the form: name God's ownership (*Thy servants*), name His Names (*Mighty, Wise*), and leave the verdict radiant with trust.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:119

> **God said, "This is the Day wherein the truthful shall benefit from their truthfulness. For them shall be Gardens with rivers running below, abiding therein forever. God is content with them, and they are content with Him. That is the great triumph."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day Truth Pays: Truthfulness as Salvation's Currency**

*This is the Day the truthful* (*ṣādiqīn*) *benefit from their truthfulness* (*ṣidqihim*) — God's closing word on the prophets' reckoning (vv. 109–118 (R, Ṭ); cf. 33:7–8, 24). Only *earthly* truthfulness counts — chosen under pressure, costly, resisted: Judgment-Day truthfulness (universal, even Satan's) earns nothing (R, Z). The criterion crowns the sūrah's truth-motifs: Abel's truthful refusal (v. 28), testimony-truth (vv. 8, 106–108), scripture-truth (vv. 43–48), Jesus' truthful disavowal (vv. 116–117). *Ṣidq* (truthfulness) proves salvation's currency — belief's backbone, deeds' stamp, speech's gold-standard. The Prophet ﷺ systematized it:

> *"Truthfulness guides to righteousness, and righteousness guides to Paradise. A man keeps speaking truthfully until he is inscribed with God as a great truth-teller (ṣiddīq)."* (al-Bukhārī 6094; Muslim 2607)

The inscription-day precedes the benefit-Day: *ṣiddīq* written on earth, profit collected in eternity. Trade truthfully now — the exchange rate on that Day exceeds all markets.

**Mutual Contentment: Paradise's Summit Beyond Gardens**

*Gardens with rivers below, abiding forever* (cf. 2:25-comm.) — *God content with them, they content with Him* (*raḍiya'Llāhu ʿanhum wa raḍū ʿanh* (cf. 9:100; 58:22; 89:28; 98:8)) — *that is the great triumph* (*fawz ʿaẓīm* (cf. 3:185; 4:13; 6:16; 9:72, 89, 100; 10:64; 45:30; 48:5)). Contentment (*riḍwān*) — deeds' proper motive throughout (3:174; 5:2, 16; 9:109; 19:6; 57:27; 59:8), paradise's calm-joy signature (3:15; 9:21, 72; 22:59; 69:21; 88:9; 92:21; 93:5; 101:7) — al-Rāzī calls the mutuality Paradise's indescribable secret (R); some confine *the great triumph* to it exclusively (Qu, R), per 9:72: *"Contentment from God is greater — that is the great triumph!"* — *riḍwān* as highest Paradise. The mutuality staggers: God — needing nothing — *content* with servants; servants — wanting everything — *content* with God. Creation's circle closes: the Lord who opened with blessings (v. 3) seals with contentment; servants who began with pacts (v. 1) end with pleasure. All religion travels from covenant to contentment — and contentment, mutual and endless, is the journey's name for home.

## Sūrah al-Mā'idah 5:120

> **Unto God belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is therein, and He is Powerful over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Seal: Sovereignty Over All Therein**

*Unto God belongs sovereignty* (*mulk* — dominion, ownership, kingship) *over heavens, earth, and whatsoever is therein* (*wa mā fīhinna* — the contents included: angels, humans, jinn, beasts, seas, atoms) — *and He is Powerful over all things* (*ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr*). The sūrah's last word returns to its first themes: verse 1's *God decrees what He wills*, verse 17–18's *unto God belongs sovereignty*, verse 40's identical seal on the penal code. Some read a final anti-deification stroke — Jesus and Mary among the *therein*, under sovereignty, not as it (Aj, Ṭ). Al-Rāzī reads the sūrah's whole arc: opened with servants' pacts (servanthood-to-Lordship), sealed with all-else-than-God's contingency — every power and knowledge dwarfed before His (R). The soul that traveled 120 verses — through kitchens and courts, covenants and Christs, Tables and testimonies — dissolves finally into *tawḥīd's* ocean: one Sovereign, all things subjected, all power His. Begin with obligation, end with absorption: that is the pilgrimage this sūrah prescribes.

**"Powerful Over All Things": The Sentence That Contains Every Answer**

*Qadīr over all things* — omnipotence as the Book's closing attribute for this sūrah: every promise (Gardens, absolution, contentment) guaranteed by it; every threat (Fire, disgrace, unprecedented punishment) armed by it; every command (fulfill, judge, vie, convey) authorized by it. The doubter's every "but how?" — resurrection, reckoning, mercy-after-sin, replacement-after-apostasy — answers in these four words. Ibn Taymiyyah's lifelong insistence finds its quiet home here: God's wisdom fills His power, His mercy precedes His wrath, His sovereignty serves His guidance. The reader closes the sūrah where the believer closes every prayer, every endeavor, every life: *unto God belongs all, and He can do all*. The Table is cleared; the guests depart; the Host remains — Sovereign of the heavens, the earth, and all therein, Powerful over all things. What began with *"fulfill your pacts"* ends with the Pact-Keeper's self-guarantee: keep yours — His omnipotence keeps His.
