# Sūrah al-Qiyāmah (Chapter 75) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Makkan summons to wake before the final awakening**

Al-Qiyāmah, “The Resurrection,” is an early Makkan sūrah of forty compact verses. Its name comes from its opening oath, but resurrection is more than its subject: it is the light in which every human choice in the chapter is examined. The sūrah moves from the cosmic Day of Rising to the conscience already judging within us; from scattered bones to perfectly restored fingertips; from the Prophet’s careful reception of revelation to humanity’s impatient love of the immediate world; and from radiant faces beholding their Lord to the lonely moment when a soul reaches the collarbones. Its closing argument returns the denier to the beginning of bodily life. The One who fashioned a person from a tiny emitted drop is surely able to restore the dead.

The chapter’s movement is therefore deliberate. It does not treat the Hereafter as remote information. It brings the future judgment into present moral awareness. A person who lets the “self-reproaching soul” speak honestly is already conducting a small reckoning before the great Reckoning. A person who silences conscience because he wishes to “sin ahead” is not suffering from lack of evidence alone; he is protecting a preferred way of life from accountability.

**Four tightly connected movements**

The first movement (75:1–15) establishes resurrection and exposes denial. Two oaths join the Day of Resurrection to the self-blaming soul. Both uncover what was concealed: the Day discloses deeds publicly, while conscience discloses them inwardly. The second movement (75:16–19) briefly addresses the Messenger ﷺ, assuring him that God will gather the revelation in his breast, enable its recitation, and explain it. This apparent interruption is profound: the God who gathers revelation without losing a syllable can gather human beings without losing a bone.

The third movement (75:20–35) diagnoses love of the fleeting world, contrasts the faces of the blessed and miserable, and depicts death’s irreversible approach. The fourth (75:36–40) asks whether humanity imagines itself abandoned without command or consequence, then offers embryonic creation as rational evidence for re-creation.

**A sūrah heard in the Prophet’s recitation**

The chapter was part of the Prophet’s public teaching. In an authentic report, Ibn ʿAbbās related that the Messenger of God ﷺ would recite Sūrat al-Sajdah and Sūrat al-Insān in the dawn prayer on Friday; other reports preserve his recitation of resurrection passages in congregational settings. More specifically, Mūsā ibn Abī ʿĀʾishah asked Saʿīd ibn Jubayr about the response to the final question of this sūrah, and he transmitted that the Prophet ﷺ would answer in affirmation. The practice teaches that the Qur’an’s rhetorical questions demand more than literary admiration: they seek the listener’s assent.

**Reading the unseen without empty speculation**

This sūrah speaks of realities beyond present observation: resurrection, judgment, transformed heavens, the extraction of the soul, and the believers’ vision of God. Sound commentary neither reduces these to metaphors nor imagines a physical mechanism revelation has not supplied. The Sunni method affirms what God and His Messenger affirmed, while leaving its manner to God. The unseen is not irrational; it is knowledge received from the One whose knowledge encompasses what our instruments cannot reach.

What follows treats every verse independently while preserving the sūrah’s cumulative argument. Repeated themes are developed from the distinct wording and function of each verse rather than collapsed into grouped commentary.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:1

> **I swear by the Day of Resurrection.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An oath that makes the denied Day present**

The Arabic opens, *Lā uqsimu bi-yawm al-qiyāmah*. The verb *uqsimu* means “I swear,” from the root q-s-m, associated with apportioning and solemn oath-taking. *Qiyāmah* derives from q-w-m, “to stand, rise, or be established.” It is the Day on which creation will rise from graves, stand before its Lord, and see perfect justice established.

The initial *lā* has long drawn grammatical discussion. Many early authorities understood it as an emphatic feature of Arabic oath style, so that the meaning is, “Indeed, I swear.” Others understood an implicit rebuttal: “No! The matter is not as the deniers allege; I swear by the Day of Resurrection.” A further reading treats it as, “I need not swear,” because the truth is intrinsically certain, followed nevertheless by an oath to awaken the hearer. These explanations converge: denial is rejected and resurrection is asserted with maximum solemnity.

**God swears by what reveals His power and justice**

God may swear by anything He wills, for every created reality is His sign. Human beings, however, swear only by God, as the Prophet ﷺ said:

> “Whoever swears, let him swear by God or remain silent.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The Day is worthy of an oath because it manifests Divine names and attributes: God’s power in raising the dead, knowledge in preserving every deed, wisdom in assigning each consequence, and justice in returning rights. Earthly history contains wrongs no court repairs—murderers who die untried, oppressed people whose names are forgotten, private sacrifices no public record notices. If death ended everything, the moral structure perceived by sound reason would remain radically unfinished. Resurrection completes, rather than interrupts, that structure.

The Qur’an states elsewhere:

> “God—there is no deity except Him. He will surely gather you for the Day of Resurrection, about which there is no doubt. And who is more truthful than God in statement?” (Qur’an 4:87)

The oath is therefore not compensation for weak evidence. It is Divine mercy toward inattentive listeners: the truth is repeated in the strongest register because what depends upon it is immeasurably serious.

**Qiyāmah begins by changing how one stands today**

Belief in a future standing reforms one’s present posture. A worker behaves differently when he knows an exact audit will occur; a trustee handles property differently when every entry must be explained. The analogy is limited, because human audits miss facts while God’s record misses nothing. Yet it shows why remembrance of judgment is ethically productive. It transfers moral weight from reputation to reality. A secret kindness matters even if no person applauds it, and a hidden injustice remains dangerous even if every person overlooks it.

The first verse asks the reader to live backward from the certain end. Before ambitions, grievances, and appetites are assigned value, ask how they will appear on the Day when everyone stands. This does not produce paralysis. Properly understood, it produces proportion, courage, repentance, and hope.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:2

> **And I swear by the blaming soul.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The courtroom planted within the person**

*Al-nafs al-lawwāmah* is the soul that repeatedly blames itself. *Lawwāmah* is an intensive form from l-w-m, “to blame or reproach”; it suggests recurrent self-questioning, not a single passing regret. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī described the believer as one who continually asks himself: What did I intend by this word? What did I intend by this food? The heedless person, by contrast, rushes onward without moral review.

Classical commentary preserves several related senses. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have connected it with the soul that reproaches itself over what has passed. Some said every soul will blame itself at the Resurrection: the righteous for not doing more, the sinner for not stopping. Others noted the soul’s instability—obedient and disobedient, grateful and resentful, awake and distracted. These are not mutually exclusive. Human inward life is changeable, and precisely because it can recognize its change, conscience can become an instrument of reform.

**Remorse that heals, not shame that destroys**

The Qur’an distinguishes moral self-reproach from hopeless self-hatred. The former says, “This deed was wrong; I must return, repair, and learn.” The latter says, “I am nothing but wrong; return is pointless.” Satan can exploit both shamelessness and despair. Revelation blocks the first through accountability and the second through mercy:

> “Say, ‘O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of God. Indeed, God forgives all sins. Indeed, He is the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful.’” (Qur’an 39:53)

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> “Regret is repentance.” (Sunan Ibn Mājah; graded authentic by a number of hadith scholars)

Regret becomes repentance when it turns backward error into forward obedience: the sin is abandoned, remorse is felt, return is resolved against, and another person’s right is restored where necessary. Endless rumination without action is not the goal.

**Why conscience is paired with resurrection**

The two oaths illuminate one another. Resurrection is the great outward reckoning; the blaming soul is a present inward reckoning. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb’s famous counsel, transmitted by al-Tirmidhī in suspended form, captures the relationship:

> “Call yourselves to account before you are called to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed.”

A daily review—of speech, money, glances, promises, and motives—is therefore not morbid. It resembles a navigator checking course while correction remains possible. The instrument is useful only if calibrated by revelation: social fashion can praise vice, while an anxious temperament can condemn what God permitted. Conscience is honored here, but it is not made infallible.

**Three conditions of the soul**

Exegetes often relate this verse to two other Qur’anic descriptions. Joseph says:

> “Indeed, the soul persistently commands to evil, except those upon whom my Lord has mercy.” (Qur’an 12:53)

And the faithful soul is finally addressed:

> “O tranquil soul, return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing.” (Qur’an 89:27–28)

These need not be three permanently separate kinds of people. They can describe conditions through which one person moves: appetite commands, conscience resists, and remembrance gradually creates tranquility. The oath dignifies the struggle in the middle. Feeling the wound is painful, but numbness is more dangerous; pain can signal that spiritual life remains.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:3

> **Does man suppose that We shall not gather his bones?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A question that exposes an unexamined assumption**

The verb *ayaḥsabu* comes from ḥ-s-b, “to reckon, calculate, or suppose.” The irony is exact: the human being “calculates” that he will not be gathered, while approaching a Day of perfect calculation. *Najmaʿa* means “We gather,” and *ʿiẓāmahu* means “his bones.” Bones are singled out because they are the body’s durable framework; after flesh disappears, they remain the most recognizable remnant, then they too decay and scatter.

Reports of occasion connect the challenge with Makkan deniers such as ʿAdī ibn Rabīʿah, who asked how God could restore decomposed bones. The individual report supplies a historical face, but *al-insān* makes the diagnosis universal. The argument recurs whenever a person mistakes present inability to observe a process for proof that the process is impossible.

**Gathering is not difficult for comprehensive knowledge**

The objection imagines God as a craftsman who has lost the parts. But nothing leaves Divine knowledge. The Qur’an answers the same doubt through the earth’s record:

> “We certainly know what the earth diminishes of them, and with Us is a preserving Record.” (Qur’an 50:4)

Human identity does not depend on God recovering old atoms as though searching debris. The One who gave the first existence knows the person wholly and commands existence without resistance. Elsewhere the denier asks:

> “Who will give life to bones while they are disintegrated? Say, ‘He will give them life who produced them the first time, and He is Knowing of every creation.’” (Qur’an 36:78–79)

The rational principle is straightforward: originating a thing without a previous specimen is not less demanding than restoring it. Since the denier’s own existence is evidence that origination occurred, resurrection cannot be dismissed as logically impossible.

**Bodily resurrection protects moral seriousness**

Islam does not teach merely the survival of an abstract influence or memory. The human person is raised. The body that acted is involved in judgment: tongues, hands, feet, skin, sight, and hearing testify. This gives embodied choices lasting significance. Charity given by a hand, steps walked toward prayer, a body restrained from aggression, and hunger endured in fasting are not disposable shells around “real” spirituality.

At the same time, bodily resurrection is not materialism. Identity is preserved by God through death, barzakh, and re-creation. The verse teaches humility before the Creator’s competence. A person who did not design one bone, control one cell division, or sustain one heartbeat has little basis for declaring what the Creator cannot gather.

**Scattered lives can also be gathered morally**

Without turning resurrection into metaphor, the wording offers a present lesson. Human beings often experience life as fragments—roles, mistakes, interrupted plans, and forgotten efforts. The Lord who gathers bones also gathers the moral meaning of every fragment. No sincere deed vanishes because it was small; no injustice becomes innocent because records were destroyed. This can comfort the unnoticed believer and sober the concealed wrongdoer at once.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:4

> **Nay! But We are able to fashion even his fingers and toes.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Not merely the skeleton, but its finest extremities**

*Balā* is the decisive answer to a negative question: “Yes indeed.” *Qādirīn* comes from q-d-r, embracing power, measure, and precise determination. *Nusawwiya* means to make even, proportion, or restore in proper form. *Banānahu* refers to the fingers or fingertips. The answer therefore exceeds the challenge: God will not merely heap bones together; He can reproduce the most delicate extremities in their exact order.

Early commentators explained that God is able to restore even the small bones and joints of the fingers, or, if He willed, to fashion the hand differently. The verse’s primary force does not depend on modern fingerprint science. Yet contemporary readers naturally recognize that fingertips bear patterns of striking individuality. This illustrates, without exhausting, an ancient point: Divine restoration reaches details humans overlook.

**Power joined to measure**

The root q-d-r guards against a crude picture of omnipotence. God’s power is not chaotic force; it acts with knowledge and proportion. The same family of words appears when God says:

> “Indeed, We created everything according to a measure.” (Qur’an 54:49)

A hand functions because tiny bones, joints, tendons, nerves, and skin are ordered together. Power without wisdom could produce mass; wise power produces a fingertip that can grasp, write, soothe, build, and testify.

Ibn Kathīr highlights the movement from greater to lesser: if even fingertips are restored, the larger bones are included all the more. Al-Qurṭubī records the linguistic attention given to *banān*, the terminal parts by which fine action is performed. The answer matches arrogance with precision.

**The fingertips that act will not be anonymous**

The Qur’an later declares:

> “Today We shall seal their mouths, and their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify about what they used to earn.” (Qur’an 36:65)

Hands symbolize agency. With them people sign contracts, send messages, transfer money, strike, heal, and conceal. Modern technology lets a fingertip cause consequences thousands of miles away. The verse quietly defeats the fantasy that distance dissolves responsibility. The exact person is restored, and exact agency is known.

**Personal identity without vanity**

The detail also balances two truths. Every human being shares humble biological origins, yet no one is an interchangeable unit before God. He knows each servant distinctly enough to restore the smallest identifying features. This grants dignity without self-deification. One matters infinitely as a morally accountable creature, but remains wholly dependent upon the One who proportions every part.

For practical faith, the question is not whether God can reconstruct us, but what our restored hands will report. The verse moves attention from speculative mechanics to ethical readiness.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:5

> **But man desires to defile what lies before him,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The argument beneath the argument**

The conjunction *bal* redirects the discussion: the deepest problem is not lack of evidence but desire. *Yurīdu* means “he wants, intends.” *Li-yafjura* derives from f-j-r, whose basic image is breaking open; dawn is *fajr* because light breaks through darkness, while *fujūr* is moral transgression that breaks restraining boundaries. *Amāmahu*, literally “before him,” can mean throughout the future lying ahead.

Thus the person wishes to keep sinning forward, postponing repentance while denying the judgment that would interrupt his plans. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and other early interpreters explained it as a person saying, “I will repent later,” while continuing wrongdoing. Another interpretation is that he denies the Hereafter “ahead of him.” Both meet psychologically: denying future accountability clears imagined space for present appetite.

**Motivated disbelief**

Reasoning is not always a neutral judge. Often it acts as an advocate hired by desire. If accepting a truth would require ending an affair, returning unlawful wealth, forgiving an enemy, or disciplining an addiction, the ego has an incentive to find the evidence “insufficient.” The Qur’an elsewhere says:

> “Have you seen the one who takes his own desire as his god?” (Qur’an 45:23)

This does not mean every intellectual doubt is insincere. Islam commands thoughtful answers and forbids judging hidden motives without evidence. The verse instead gives each person a tool for self-examination: “Would I welcome this conclusion if it required no moral change? What desired behavior might my argument be protecting?”

**Delay is itself a choice**

Procrastinated repentance feels less confrontational than rejection, but repeated delay gradually becomes a life. A traveler who postpones every turn is still choosing the road. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> “God accepts the repentance of the servant so long as the death-rattle has not reached his throat.” (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī; Sunan Ibn Mājah)

The open door is vast, but not an excuse to stroll toward it indefinitely. No one controls the arrival of the final moment portrayed later in this sūrah.

A practical response is immediate, specific repentance rather than vague future improvement: stop one identified wrong, return one right, remove one pathway to recurrence, and replace it with a lawful habit. If digital access drives a sin, alter access now; if pride prevents apology, speak now; if debt is concealed, document and arrange it now.

**Freedom requires boundaries**

Modern culture sometimes defines freedom as keeping every option open. The verse reveals the contradiction: a person who must preserve the option to obey every desire is not free from desire. Moral boundaries make higher agency possible, just as lane markings restrict a driver in order to enable safe travel. *Fujūr* breaks the boundary and eventually breaks the person’s capacity to choose well.

Resurrection is denied because it closes the fantasy of endless extension. The cure is not terror alone; it is to see limits as mercy and repentance as recovered freedom.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:6

> **asking, “When is the Day of Resurrection?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A question whose tone reveals its purpose**

The words *ayyāna yawm al-qiyāmah* ask “When is the Day of Resurrection?” *Ayyāna* is an interrogative of time, often carrying rhetorical force. In context, this is not the sincere question of someone preparing for an appointment. It is the scoff of one who treats delay as disproof. Similar challenges recur:

> “They say, ‘When will this promise come, if you are truthful?’” (Qur’an 67:25)

The answer elsewhere distinguishes knowledge of timing from certainty of occurrence:

> “They ask you about the Hour: when will it arrive? Say, ‘Its knowledge is only with my Lord. None will reveal its time except Him.’” (Qur’an 7:187)

Not knowing when does not imply not knowing whether. Every person knows death will occur, though almost none knows its date. Unscheduled certainty should increase readiness, not decrease it.

**The Prophet redirected “when?” toward “what have you prepared?”**

An authentic hadith reports that a man asked the Prophet ﷺ when the Hour would be. The Prophet answered:

> “What have you prepared for it?” He said, “Nothing, except that I love God and His Messenger.” He said, “You will be with those whom you love.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The response does not belittle knowledge. It restores knowledge to its moral purpose. A calendar date could become trivia; preparation changes destiny. The hadith also joins fear with hope: genuine love of God and His Messenger is not a slogan but an orientation expressed in loyalty, imitation, repentance, and longing.

**Delay tests rather than disproves**

The mocker assumes that if judgment were real it would already have arrived. This confuses respite with neglect. The Qur’an explains:

> “And never think that God is unaware of what the wrongdoers do. He only delays them for a Day when eyes will stare in horror.” (Qur’an 14:42)

Delay permits repentance, reveals settled choices, and allows moral consequences to unfold. In ordinary justice, a court date months away does not make the law imaginary. Divine delay is even less evidence of absence, because God is never overtaken by time.

**Turning the question inward**

A believer may ask about signs of the Hour for legitimate learning, but speculation must not consume the obligations already clear. The daily prayers have known times; debts have actual claimants; parents age; opportunities to reconcile close. Obsession with sensational predictions can become another way of avoiding preparation.

The verse therefore converts “When will history end?” into “How am I using the time that remains?” Whether the universal Resurrection is near by human measure or distant, every individual’s barrier to it is death, and death is always near enough to make obedience reasonable.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:7

> **Then when the eyes are dazzled,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The eye that demanded proof is overwhelmed by it**

*Fa-idhā bariqa al-baṣar* abruptly answers the mocker’s “When?” The verb *bariqa* can indicate being dazzled, stunned, or fixed in terror. Some canonical recitational discussion relates its vocalization to flashing like lightning; both senses communicate sensory shock. *Al-baṣar* is sight, or the seeing eye. The singular makes the scene immediate: picture not an anonymous crowd but each person’s own vision failing to master what it sees.

In this world, the skeptic may demand that the unseen be made visible on his terms. On that Day visibility will not be scarce; the capacity to absorb it will be. The Qur’an gives a parallel image:

> “Racing ahead, their heads raised up, their gaze not returning to them, and their hearts empty.” (Qur’an 14:43)

The human observer normally looks, categorizes, and turns away. Here the gaze cannot return to ordinary composure. The Day is not merely a larger event within the familiar world; it is the collapse of the familiar frame.

**Knowledge can arrive too late to benefit**

Seeing truth is not identical to believing in the morally fruitful sense. Faith in the unseen is valuable because it is chosen while obedience, repentance, and trust remain possible. Once judgment is directly witnessed, compelled recognition cannot serve the testing purpose of worldly life. Pharaoh’s confession while drowning did not erase a career of tyranny.

This is why the Qur’an repeatedly calls present creation *āyāt*, signs. A sign gives enough evidence for responsible recognition without abolishing choice. The dazzled eye warns against demanding coercive proof while ignoring proportionate proof already present in revelation, conscience, creation, and one’s own origin.

**The limits of sensory confidence**

Modern people can be tempted to equate “real” with “measurable by current instruments.” Yet the eye sees only a narrow range, the ear hears only a narrow range, and instruments merely extend bounded faculties. The verse does not reject empirical knowledge; it humbles the claim that sensory control is the measure of all reality.

The practical lesson is to train sight before it is overwhelmed: lower it from what God prohibited, use it to reflect on His signs, and refuse the constant overstimulation that makes attention shallow. An eye disciplined by truth today will not meet the truth as a total stranger tomorrow.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:8

> **and the moon is eclipsed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The familiar lamp loses its light**

*Wa-khasafa al-qamar* depicts the moon darkened or eclipsed. The root kh-s-f conveys sinking, disappearance, or loss of light. This is no routine astronomical eclipse with a predictable end. In the eschatological sequence, the moon’s ordered function is withdrawn as the present cosmos is dismantled.

The moon has regulated calendars, travel, worship, tides, and human imagination. Islamic months begin by its crescent; fasting and pilgrimage are timed by it. When this dependable marker fails, ordinary scheduling itself has ended. The Qur’an says elsewhere:

> “When the sun is folded up, and when the stars fall, losing their light.” (Qur’an 81:1–2)

The One who subjected celestial bodies to law is not subject to those bodies. Natural regularity is God’s customary ordering, not an independent power binding Him.

**A sign must not be mistaken for its Source**

Human beings easily transfer awe from Creator to creation. Ancient peoples worshiped sun, moon, and stars; modern people may not bow to them, yet can absolutize “nature” as if its patterns explain themselves. Abraham’s argument rejects celestial divinity precisely through mutability:

> “When it set, he said, ‘I do not love those that disappear.’” (Qur’an 6:76)

Whatever waxes, wanes, is eclipsed, or ends cannot be the unconditioned Lord. The darkened moon therefore teaches tawḥīd through cosmic change.

**Borrowed light and borrowed stability**

Classically, the sun is called a radiant lamp and the moon a light. Whatever scientific account one gives of reflected light, the spiritual analogy remains useful: created brilliance is borrowed. Beauty, intelligence, status, and influence shine only by gifts God sustains. Forgetting this produces arrogance; remembering it produces gratitude and responsible use.

A believer who observes an ordinary eclipse does not treat it as superstition. The Prophet ﷺ taught:

> “The sun and the moon are two signs among the signs of God. They are not eclipsed because of the death or life of anyone. When you see that, pray and supplicate to God.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The prophetic response joins sound causality to worship: eclipses are not omens centered on famous people, but natural signs that should awaken humble remembrance of the Creator and the final transformation.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:9

> **and the sun and the moon are brought together—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two rulers of human time meet at its end**

*Wa-jumiʿa al-shamsu wa-l-qamar* uses the passive, “the sun and moon are gathered together.” The verb echoes verse 3: the denier doubts that God will “gather” bones, then hears that God will gather the greatest visible celestial lights. The rhetorical comparison makes the answer overwhelming. If the cosmic bodies are under His gathering command, scattered human remains present no difficulty.

Classical exegetes offered complementary descriptions: both lose their light, are folded or joined, or are cast away when their appointed service ends. The verse tells us the fact needed for admonition, not an astrophysical sequence to reconstruct speculatively. Its language affirms a real cosmic upheaval while leaving the modality to God.

**The calendar closes**

The sun measures days and years; the moon measures months. Their joining signifies that the world’s clock has no further appointment. The Qur’an says:

> “The sun and the moon move by precise calculation.” (Qur’an 55:5)

Precision is not permanence. A lease may operate by exact terms until its exact termination. Likewise, cosmic law displays wisdom during the test and yields to a new order at the end of the test.

This corrects two opposite errors. One is imagining the universe chaotic and meaningless; the other is imagining it eternal and self-sufficient. Revelation presents ordered temporality: creation has purpose, measure, and conclusion.

**When great lights cannot guide**

People orient themselves by external lights—institutions, experts, family names, technological systems. Such guidance can be useful, but none can replace moral guidance from God. On the Day when even sun and moon offer no direction, inherited status and social navigation are less able still to provide escape.

The believer learns to use created means without worshiping reliability itself. Plan carefully, consult expertise, and respect regular causes, while knowing that all means operate by Divine permission. Reliance on God is not refusal of means; it is refusal to make means ultimate.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:10

> **that Day man will say, “Where is the escape?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The instinct to flee after every exit has closed**

*Yaqūlu al-insānu yawmaʾidhin ayna al-mafarr*. *Mafarr* comes from f-r-r, “to flee,” and means a place or means of escape. The one who once asked “When?” now asks “Where?” Mockery has become panic, but the change comes after the period of moral choice.

Flight is a basic survival response. In worldly danger it is often wise: leave a burning room, escape abuse, avoid temptation. Judgment differs because the danger is not a location but the truth of one’s relationship with God. A person can flee from a pursuer; he cannot flee from comprehensive knowledge, ownership, and justice.

The Qur’an depicts the same realization:

> “And the criminals will see the Fire and be certain that they are to fall into it, and they will find no way to avert it.” (Qur’an 18:53)

**The escape offered before the Day**

Remarkably, revelation does command flight now:

> “So flee to God. Indeed, I am to you from Him a clear warner.” (Qur’an 51:50)

One flees from God’s displeasure to His mercy, from disobedience to repentance, from illusion to truth. There is no refuge *from* Him except *with* Him. The Prophet ﷺ expressed this in supplication:

> “I seek refuge in Your pleasure from Your anger, and in Your pardon from Your punishment, and I seek refuge in You from You.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This is not contradiction. The Judge is also the Merciful Lord who presently invites return. The door unavailable in verse 10 is open throughout life.

**Avoidance as a life strategy**

People practice smaller forms of flight: distraction from grief, denial of debt, blaming others for repeated failure, curating an online identity to outrun inward emptiness. Avoidance provides short relief while allowing reality to gain force. The Day of Resurrection is the final exposure of that pattern.

A practical spiritual discipline is to turn toward manageable truth early. Open the statement, have the difficult conversation, name the sin, seek counsel, and pray two units of repentance. Courage is not the absence of fear but choosing the right refuge before fear removes choice.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:11

> **Nay! But there shall be no refuge.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A categorical closure**

*Kallā lā wazar.* *Kallā* rebukes and stops the preceding hope: “No indeed.” *Wazar* denotes a refuge, fortified shelter, mountain, or place of protection. In only three short Arabic words, the imagined geography of escape disappears.

Mountains were archetypal shelters in Arabia—high, strong, and difficult to penetrate. Yet the Qur’an says that on the Last Day they will be like carded wool. Physical elevation cannot protect against a moral summons. Noah’s son made the same mistake during the flood:

> “He said, ‘I will take refuge on a mountain to protect me from the water.’ Noah said, ‘There is no protector today from the decree of God except for whom He gives mercy.’” (Qur’an 11:43)

The parallel is sobering: created solidity cannot substitute for relationship with the Creator.

**No refuge does not mean no justice or mercy**

The verse describes escape from judgment, not the absence of Divine mercy within judgment. No one can evade God, manipulate jurisdiction, destroy evidence, or hide behind another’s status. Yet God forgives whom He wills without injustice, and the intercession He permits is real. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> “God is more merciful to His servants than this woman is to her child.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Mercy is not a loophole seized against God; it is His attribute and gift. Accountability and mercy are harmonized by the Judge Himself.

**False shelters in present life**

Wealth, networks, ideology, physical strength, and public reputation can all become *wazar* in the heart. They offer relative protection in ordinary circumstances, but become idols when trusted absolutely. The Qur’an repeatedly warns that wealth and children will not benefit except one who comes with a sound heart.

The verse trains reliance. Use insurance, medicine, legal protection, and community support responsibly, but do not let the heart say, “This makes me untouchable.” Contingent shelters deserve contingent confidence. Ultimate confidence belongs only to God.

---
## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:12

> **Unto your Lord that Day is the dwelling place.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The end of every road is personal return to the Lord**

*Ilā rabbika yawmaʾidhin al-mustaqarr.* The prepositional phrase “to your Lord” is placed first, conveying exclusivity. *Mustaqarr* comes from q-r-r, stability and settled residence: the final place of rest, settlement, or decision. The verse answers “Where is escape?” with the only true destination: to your Lord.

The address *rabbika*—“your Lord”—is significant. The One before whom the person stands is the One who created, nurtured, provided, warned, and allowed time. Judgment is not an encounter with a stranger. The Lord’s prior care establishes both gratitude and accountability.

Elsewhere the Qur’an summarizes human movement:

> “O mankind, indeed you are laboring toward your Lord with great exertion and will meet Him.” (Qur’an 84:6)

Every life is motion toward God, including the life that denies Him. Belief does not create the destination; it lets one travel toward it consciously.

**Settlement after radical instability**

Worldly life is marked by movement: homes change, bodies age, relationships shift, and achievements pass into other hands. Humans nevertheless seek permanence in temporary things. *Al-mustaqarr* announces that true settlement belongs to the next life. This does not devalue earthly duties; it assigns them their proper role as provisions for a journey.

The Prophet ﷺ advised Ibn ʿUmar:

> “Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveler.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

A traveler does not neglect the road, but neither does he confuse a roadside stop with home. This balance resists both worldly absorption and irresponsible withdrawal.

**Lordship makes final judgment coherent**

If God is *Rabb*, moral instruction is not arbitrary interference. The Maker knows what perfects and corrupts the creature, just as a skilled physician knows what heals. Divine commands cultivate the soul toward its final settlement. Prohibitions mark dangers whose full effect may remain unseen during the journey.

A useful daily question follows: “Is this becoming a home in my heart, though it is only luggage?” Careers, possessions, and praise may be carried, used, and thanked for. They should not become the imagined *mustaqarr*. Return belongs to God alone.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:13

> **Man shall be informed that Day of that which he has sent forth and of that which he has left behind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A complete moral biography**

*Yunabbaʾu* means he will be informed with weighty, consequential news. *Mā qaddama* is what he sent forward; *mā akhkhara* is what he put back or left behind. Early exegetes offered several complementary applications: deeds done and opportunities neglected; earlier and later actions; or direct acts and continuing effects left after death.

The Qur’an gives a close parallel:

> “A soul will then know what it has sent forth and left behind.” (Qur’an 82:5)

Deeds are “sent” because they arrive at the Hereafter before the doer. This reverses normal intuition: what is spent for God is not lost; it has been forwarded to one’s real account.

**Consequences that outlive their author**

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> “When a human being dies, his deeds come to an end except for three: ongoing charity, beneficial knowledge, or a righteous child who prays for him.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

He also warned:

> “Whoever institutes a good practice in Islam will have its reward and the reward of those who act upon it after him, without their rewards being diminished; and whoever institutes an evil practice will bear its burden and the burden of those who act upon it after him, without their burdens being diminished.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Responsibility is not transferred: followers still choose. But initiating, facilitating, normalizing, or teaching an act creates a real causal share. In a connected age, a useful lesson, defamatory post, exploitative design, or charitable system can continue operating after its author sleeps or dies.

**Sins of omission enter the record**

“What he left behind” warns that the ledger is not limited to visible crimes. Capacity creates some responsibilities: knowledge not shared when needed, dependents neglected, zakāh withheld, apologies postponed, and chances to prevent harm abandoned. Not every missed possibility is sinful—human capacity is finite—but commanded duties and entrusted rights cannot be erased by inactivity.

A sound personal review therefore asks two questions: “What did I do?” and “What should I have done?” The second protects against a spirituality that congratulates itself merely for avoiding scandal while producing little good.

**Designing a merciful legacy**

The verse encourages deliberate after-effects. Teach a child prayer, document beneficial knowledge, reconcile relatives, plant something others will use, establish transparent charity, and remove harmful content. Legacy in Islam is not primarily being remembered by people; it is effects remembered in God’s account. Anonymous benefit can be greater than celebrated fame.

---
## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:14

> **Indeed, man shall be a testimony against himself,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The witness who cannot be excluded**

*Bal al-insānu ʿalā nafsihi baṣīrah.* *Baṣīrah* can mean penetrating insight, clear evidence, or a witness. Though grammatically feminine, it may function as an intensive noun: the person is himself compelling evidence against himself. External proof will be abundant, but inward knowledge already knows much of what excuses attempt to conceal.

The Qur’an commands the person at judgment:

> “Read your record. Sufficient are you this Day to take account of yourself.” (Qur’an 17:14)

It also describes bodily testimony:

> “Their hearing, their eyes, and their skins will testify against them concerning what they used to do.” (Qur’an 41:20)

Divine judgment does not depend on forced confession because every layer of the person belongs to the evidentiary field.

**Self-deception is real but never complete**

People can rationalize persistently until they feel innocent. Yet rationalization usually has seams: irritation when questioned, selective retelling, secret deletion, or standards applied differently to oneself and others. The blaming soul of verse 2 is the inward witness trying to speak before the final hearing.

This does not mean every feeling of guilt proves actual guilt. Trauma, obsessive doubt, and manipulation can produce false accusation within. Revelation, sound reason, and qualified counsel help distinguish moral insight from illness. *Baṣīrah* is clear perception, not uncontrolled anxiety.

**Confession before God restores integrity**

Adam and Eve modeled truthful return:

> “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy upon us, we will surely be among the losers.” (Qur’an 7:23)

They did not blame the tree, circumstance, or one another. Naming the wrong did not annihilate dignity; it opened mercy. By contrast, Satan defended disobedience through superiority and blame.

A practical habit is precise language in repentance: replace “mistakes were made” with “I said this falsely,” “I withheld that right,” or “I sought praise.” Specific truth weakens the ego’s evasions and clarifies repair. The person who becomes an honest witness now may, by God’s mercy, avoid being an accusing witness then.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:15

> **though he proffers his excuses.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Excuses spread like coverings over known truth**

*Wa-law alqā maʿādhīrah.* *Alqā* means to cast or present; *maʿādhīr* is the plural of excuse or plea. Some linguists connect the image to curtains or coverings: a person casts layers over what he knows. Verse 14 establishes the inward witness; verse 15 shows advocacy trying to silence it.

An excuse can be valid. Coercion, forgetfulness, genuine inability, and honest error have legal and moral consequences in Islam. The Prophet ﷺ taught:

> “Actions are only by intentions, and every person will have only what he intended.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The verse condemns excuses that contradict one’s own clear knowledge—not careful attention to intention and capacity. God’s justice knows distinctions more precisely than any human court.

**Explanation is not exoneration**

A difficult childhood may explain anger without making aggression lawful. Workplace pressure may explain dishonesty without turning it into truth. An algorithm may encourage envy without forcing the gaze. Mature accountability can hold cause and agency together: “I was influenced, and I still chose.” Denying every influence is simplistic; using influence to erase every choice is evasive.

The Qur’an says of the final hearing:

> “On that Day the excuses of the wrongdoers will not benefit them.” (Qur’an 40:52)

The problem is not that God has failed to hear their case. It is that comprehensive knowledge has already placed the plea in its exact context.

**From courtroom defense to truthful repair**

In ordinary conflict, people often listen only to prepare a defense. The verse invites a different order: first determine what is true, then accept one’s portion, then explain context only to enable a fair remedy. A sincere apology does not contain a hidden counter-charge—“I am sorry, but you made me.”

This principle also restrains us when judging others. Since only God knows the full inward record, believers should distinguish visible wrong from hidden motive, preserve due process, and welcome legitimate excuses. The verse is primarily a mirror, not a license to prosecute everyone else.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:16

> **Move not thy tongue therewith to hasten it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A tender correction during revelation**

The address shifts suddenly to the Prophet ﷺ. *Lā tuḥarrik bihi lisānaka li-taʿjala bih*: do not move your tongue with the revelation in order to hurry its retention. Ibn ʿAbbās explained in the authentic reports that the Messenger ﷺ experienced strain when revelation descended and moved his lips rapidly, fearing he might lose words before Gabriel completed them. God instructed him to listen.

Ibn ʿAbbās demonstrated the Prophet’s lip movement to his student Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, who demonstrated it to the next narrator. The report states:

> “The Messenger of God used to experience great difficulty from the revelation and would move his lips with it. So God revealed: ‘Do not move your tongue with it to hasten it. Indeed, upon Us is its collection and its recitation.’” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The correction is not for carelessness but for excessive eagerness born of responsibility. That makes its tone reassuring rather than reproachful.

**Why this passage stands inside a sūrah about resurrection**

Several connections appear. First, the same God who gathers bones gathers Qur’anic words in the Prophet’s heart. Second, the passage establishes the reliable delivery of the very revelation announcing resurrection. Third, human haste in receiving truth mirrors the wider haste diagnosed in verse 20—the difference being that the Prophet’s haste arose from devotion and was immediately corrected, while worldly haste resists the Hereafter.

The placement also imitates revelation’s authority over discourse. God interrupts the eschatological scene to address His Messenger, then returns to it. The Qur’an is not a polished human composition assembled after reflection; it preserves Divine instruction given in the living event of reception.

**Learning requires receptive silence**

Many learners rehearse an answer while a teacher is still speaking. Anxiety about retention can itself block understanding. The verse establishes an intellectual ethic: first receive accurately, then repeat and interpret. Haste fragments knowledge.

This applies to Qur’anic study. A reader should not rush to public commentary after encountering a striking phrase. Listen to the whole passage, check language and context, distinguish certainty from possibility, and let revelation question the reader before the reader uses it to question others.

**Reliance does not cancel effort**

The Prophet ﷺ had already exerted himself; God redirected the form of effort. Tawakkul is not passivity but appropriate action joined to trust. When God guarantees an outcome, anxious over-control can become counterproductive. Students, parents, and leaders often need this lesson: responsibility means doing the commanded means, not assuming sovereignty over results.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:17

> **Surely it is for Us to gather it and to recite it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Divine guarantee in two stages**

*Inna ʿalaynā jamʿahu wa-qurʾānah.* The emphatic *inna* assures certainty. “Upon Us” is not an obligation imposed on God by another; it is a promise He freely undertakes. *Jamʿahu* is its gathering—classically, gathering the revealed words in the Prophet’s breast. *Qurʾānah* here can mean its recitation, from q-r-ʾ, to read or recite. God guarantees retention and correct articulation.

According to Ibn ʿAbbās’s explanation, after this instruction the Prophet ﷺ listened when Gabriel came, and after Gabriel departed he recited it as Gabriel had recited. The verse therefore belongs to the Qur’an’s account of its own preservation.

**Preservation begins in a living heart**

The Qur’an was written during the Prophet’s lifetime, but its first human repository was memorized recitation. This oral dimension remains distinctive: millions who do not speak Arabic retain its precise sequence. The communal means—memorization, writing, teaching chains, repeated prayer—are created means under the Divine promise.

Elsewhere God says:

> “Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed, We will surely preserve it.” (Qur’an 15:9)

Affirming Divine preservation does not devalue scholarly labor. It explains why such labor succeeds across generations. Providence commonly works through responsible human means.

**The gathering of revelation and the gathering of persons**

The repeated root j-m-ʿ binds the sūrah. Verse 3 asks whether bones will be gathered; verse 9 gathers sun and moon; here revelation is gathered. No dispersion—physical, cosmic, or verbal—defeats the One whose knowledge never disperses.

This gives the believer epistemic confidence without intellectual laziness. Confidence rests on God’s promise and the demonstrable transmission of the text; understanding still requires language, Prophetic explanation, and disciplined scholarship.

**Memory as entrusted gift**

Anyone who memorizes even a short sūrah participates in this preservation. But memorization is not possession for display. Revelation gathered on the tongue should gather conduct around it. A precisely recited warning against arrogance that leaves arrogance untouched has completed sound but not purpose.

The verse encourages students facing fear of forgetting: ask God, repeat by sound means, and trust that knowledge is a gift rather than an ego achievement. The heart receives; God grants collection.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:18

> **So when We recite it, follow its recitation.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God attributes the angelic recitation to Himself**

*Fa-idhā qaraʾnāhu fa-ttabiʿ qurʾānah.* Gabriel delivered the words, yet God says, “when We recite it,” because Gabriel recites by Divine command. Agency is attributed to the ultimate commander without denying the created messenger. This Qur’anic usage helps theological reasoning: causes are real, but independent causation belongs to none besides God.

*Ittabiʿ* means follow. Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as listening attentively, then reciting after Gabriel. Following includes sequence, pronunciation, and obedient reception. The command is not “race alongside” but “follow after.”

**Listening precedes faithful speech**

The moral order is important. To follow revelation, one must let revelation lead. People frequently reverse this: they begin with a political identity, inherited custom, or personal desire, then search scripture for confirming fragments. That is making the Qur’an follow the reader.

The Qur’an instructs the community:

> “When the Qur’an is recited, listen to it and pay attention so that you may receive mercy.” (Qur’an 7:204)

Attentive listening is bodily and ethical. Put aside competing noise, recognize the speaker, and remain willing to be changed. Recitation that never interrupts one’s assumptions has not yet been fully heard.

**Following the recitation includes the Prophet’s explanation**

The Messenger ﷺ did not merely transmit phonetic units. His worship, legal judgments, character, and explanations embodied the text. The Qur’an says:

> “We revealed to you the Reminder so that you may explain to people what was sent down to them.” (Qur’an 16:44)

Thus loyalty to Qur’an cannot coherently dismiss authentic Sunnah. The same revelation that commands following its recitation identifies the Messenger as its commissioned explainer.

**A discipline for the age of instant reaction**

Digital communication rewards immediate comment. Verse 18 rewards accurate following. Before reposting a translated verse, ask what surrounds it, whether a quotation is complete, and whether one’s application matches sound principles. Silence during learning is not weakness; it is an ethical stage of speech.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:19

> **Then surely it is for Us to explain it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Preserved wording is joined to disclosed meaning**

*Thumma inna ʿalaynā bayānah.* *Bayān* comes from b-y-n, separation and clarity: explanation distinguishes meanings that confusion has mixed. The sequence is pedagogical—receive, retain, recite, then understand. *Thumma*, “then,” marks ordered development, not necessarily a long delay.

God explained the revelation through its own verses, through Gabriel, and through the Prophet ﷺ. Some Qur’anic passages clarify others; the Sunnah specifies prayer, zakāh, pilgrimage, and innumerable applications; qualified reasoning applies clear principles to new cases. These channels are not rivals but parts of the commissioned *bayān*.

**Not every explanation has the same authority**

The verse does not make every interpreter’s claim Divine. God’s explanation is certain; human access varies. Sound interpretation respects Arabic usage, context, the Qur’an as a whole, authentic Sunnah, early understanding, and established legal principles. It also labels inference as inference.

The Prophet ﷺ warned:

> “Whoever lies about me deliberately, let him take his seat in the Fire.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This makes careless quotation a spiritual danger. Reverence is shown not by attaching certainty to every inspiring thought, but by distinguishing revelation, authentic report, scholarly interpretation, and personal reflection.

**Clarity arrives to a patient learner**

Verses 16–19 diagnose a common educational failure: trying to seize meaning before receiving words. Deep understanding often comes by layers. A verse memorized in youth may disclose new relevance during grief, parenthood, leadership, or repentance without its objective meaning changing.

The believer therefore combines confidence and humility: confidence that revelation is intelligible and sufficient for guidance; humility that one’s current understanding is not exhaustive. Asking scholars is not surrender of reason but reason recognizing specialized knowledge:

> “So ask the people of remembrance if you do not know.” (Qur’an 16:43)

**Explanation creates responsibility**

Clarity is a gift, but gifts carry duties. Once a person knows that conduct is wrong, ambiguity no longer shelters delay. The passage returns naturally to accountability: revelation has been gathered and explained, so the human being cannot claim that no guidance came.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:20

> **Nay! But you love the ephemeral,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The hidden preference behind denial**

*Kallā bal tuḥibbūna al-ʿājilah.* *Kallā* arrests heedlessness; *bal* redirects to the real diagnosis. *Al-ʿājilah* means “the hasty” or “the immediate,” a Qur’anic name for worldly life. It is not called false—its pleasures and pains are real—but immediate, because it arrives first and passes quickly.

The plural “you love” broadens the address beyond one denier. Love of immediacy is a human vulnerability. The Qur’an elsewhere states:

> “You desire the commodities of this world, but God desires for you the Hereafter.” (Qur’an 8:67)

The problem is not loving spouses, children, lawful beauty, work, or food. These are blessings. The problem is preferring a smaller immediate good when it conflicts with the lasting good, or treating means as final ends.

**Present bias in spiritual language**

Human psychology discounts delayed consequences. A sweet taste now outweighs distant health; an angry reply now outweighs later reconciliation; unlawful profit now outweighs an unseen account. Revelation trains the imagination to make the future morally present. Fasting is a school of this capacity: immediate appetite is delayed because God’s promise is more real than the sensation of the moment.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> “Paradise is surrounded by hardships, and the Fire is surrounded by desires.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This does not mean every hardship is good or every pleasure evil. It means durable good often requires crossing immediate resistance, while destructive paths often market an attractive entrance.

**Technology amplifies, but did not invent, haste**

Instant delivery, endless scrolling, rapid outrage, and visible metrics intensify *al-ʿājilah*. Yet the verse was revealed before these tools, showing that technology amplifies an older heart-condition. The cure cannot be merely technical, though technical boundaries help. It requires a reordered love.

Practical disciplines include delaying a nonessential purchase, waiting before responding in anger, keeping private worship without posting it, and choosing long-term benefit for family over short-term applause. Each act teaches the soul that “not now” does not mean “never,” and that God’s later gift can outweigh the world’s immediate offer.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:21

> **and forsake the Hereafter.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Preference is revealed by what is left unattended**

*Wa-tadharūna al-ākhirah.* The verb means you leave, forsake, or neglect. *Al-ākhirah* is the later and final life. The contrast is exact: people love *al-ʿājilah*, what comes quickly, and leave *al-ākhirah*, what comes last and lasts.

Neglect may coexist with verbal belief. A person can affirm resurrection doctrinally while budgeting time, money, and concern as though no resurrection existed. The verse audits operative belief—the future one’s habits reveal.

God says:

> “But you prefer the worldly life, while the Hereafter is better and more lasting.” (Qur’an 87:16–17)

“Better” addresses quality; “more lasting” addresses duration. Rational choice considers both. Trading an eternal good for a brief unlawful pleasure is not merely sinful; it is a catastrophic miscalculation.

**The world becomes valuable when used for the Hereafter**

Islam does not command abandoning civilization. Earning lawfully, raising children, seeking medicine, building institutions, and enjoying permitted blessings can all become worship through right intention and method. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> “The world is sweet and green, and God is appointing you as successors in it to see how you act.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The world is a testing ground and field, not the harvest’s final storehouse. Neglect of the Hereafter occurs when cultivation is mistaken for consumption alone.

**Attention is a moral resource**

What people repeatedly remember shapes what they choose. Daily prayer interrupts worldly absorption five times; Friday gathers communal attention; Ramadan changes appetite and schedule; visiting graves reminds without despair. These practices prevent *tadharūn*, abandonment by inattention.

A simple audit is to compare planning horizons. People prepare years for education and decades for retirement. How regularly do they prepare for what has no end? The point is not mathematical guilt but restored proportion: one sincere prayer, returned right, and hidden charity may carry farther than achievements that dominate public memory.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:22

> **Faces that Day shall be radiant,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Inner faith becomes outward light**

*Wujūhun yawmaʾidhin nāḍirah.* The indefinite “faces” introduces one class among humanity. *Nāḍirah*, with the letter ḍād, means fresh, bright, beautiful, and radiant. The face represents the person because joy, fear, dignity, and humiliation appear there before words can hide them.

The Qur’an repeatedly contrasts final faces:

> “On the Day when some faces will become bright and some faces will become dark.” (Qur’an 3:106)

And:

> “Faces that Day will be radiant, laughing and rejoicing at good news.” (Qur’an 80:38–39)

This radiance is not cosmetic. It is the visible result of security, forgiveness, and honor from God.

**The light prepared through hidden obedience**

Worldly appearances can be curated. On the Last Day inward reality is made manifest. Ablution, prostration, patience, truthful repentance, and kindness done without witnesses contribute to a beauty no filter can fabricate. The Prophet ﷺ described his community as coming with bright marks from ablution and said:

> “You will have distinctive marks which no other nation will have: you will come to me with radiant foreheads and limbs from the traces of ablution.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

One should not turn this into vanity about physical appearance. The verse gives hope to those ignored by worldly beauty standards. Final radiance belongs to faith and Divine grace, not age, ethnicity, wealth, or bodily symmetry.

**Joy after accountability**

The surrounding terror makes this brightness remarkable. Believers are not radiant because the Day is trivial, but because God grants security amid its seriousness. Fear and hope therefore belong together in this life. Fear motivates preparation; hope keeps preparation loving and sustainable.

A face becomes spiritually brighter now through gratitude, truthful speech, and mercy toward others. Yet the verse’s promise remains eschatological and should not be reduced to mood. Depression or illness is not evidence of weak faith; worldly facial expression is not an infallible spiritual meter. God alone brings the hidden life to perfect light.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:23

> **gazing upon their Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The highest gift of Paradise**

*Ilā rabbihā nāẓirah.* The preposition *ilā*, “toward,” joined to *nāẓirah*, “looking,” gives the plain sense affirmed by the overwhelming body of Sunni exegetes: the believers will truly see their Lord in the Hereafter. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī cite the Prophetic reports as decisive explanation. This is the beatific vision (*ruʾyat Allāh*), the greatest joy of Paradise.

The Prophet ﷺ looked at the full moon and said:

> “You will certainly see your Lord just as you see this moon; you will have no difficulty crowding one another to see Him.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The comparison concerns clarity and certainty of seeing, not resemblance between God and the moon. God says:

> “There is nothing like Him, and He is the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing.” (Qur’an 42:11)

Sunni theology therefore affirms vision without asking how, imagining shape, or enclosing God in created dimensions. Revelation gives the truth; it does not authorize fantasy about modality.

**“Sight does not encompass Him” is not “no one sees Him”**

Some interpreters denied literal vision by appealing to:

> “Vision does not encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision.” (Qur’an 6:103)

Classical Sunni scholars distinguish seeing from encompassing. A person sees the sky without encompassing it. Even more necessarily, seeing God does not mean comprehending His essence. Moses’ request, “My Lord, show me Yourself that I may look at You” (7:143), would have been improper if vision were intrinsically impossible; its denial concerned worldly capacity in that moment. The Hereafter involves a transformed capacity granted by God.

**Beyond gardens to the Giver**

The Prophet ﷺ explained the “more” promised to those who do good:

> “When the people of Paradise enter Paradise, God will say, ‘Do you desire anything more?’ … Then He will remove the veil, and they will be given nothing more beloved to them than looking at their Lord.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Paradise contains gardens, companionship, peace, food, and freedom from pain. Yet the greatest reward is nearness to and vision of the One for whose sake worship was offered. This prevents religion from becoming mere spiritual consumerism. The believer does not obey only to acquire created pleasures; he seeks the pleasure of the Creator.

**Learning to look toward God now**

No one sees God with the eyes in this life, as the Prophet ﷺ taught concerning the Antichrist:

> “Know that none of you will see his Lord until he dies.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

But hearts can orient toward Him through remembrance, sincerity, watching His commands, and recognizing His signs. Worship with *iḥsān* means serving God as though one sees Him, knowing that although one does not see Him, He sees the servant. A life scattered among competing gazes—public approval, envy, self-display—is gathered by seeking the gaze and pleasure of God.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:24

> **And faces that Day shall be scowling,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A darkness formed by anticipated loss**

*Wa-wujūhun yawmaʾidhin bāsirah.* *Bāsirah* means scowling, distorted by grief, severe, or gloomy. Its sound deliberately contrasts with *nāḍirah* in verse 22: a small phonetic difference carries a total difference in destiny. One group’s faces are fresh with hope; the other’s contract under dread.

The face that once performed confidence may no longer manage appearances. The Qur’an says:

> “And faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, the Sustainer, and whoever carries injustice will have failed.” (Qur’an 20:111)

This humiliation is not arbitrary cruelty. It is inward moral ruin becoming outwardly undeniable.

**The Qur’an warns without inviting contempt**

Believers should not read descriptions of condemned faces with smugness. Final judgment belongs to God, and living people can repent. The proper response is refuge and self-correction. The Prophet ﷺ often sought protection from punishment, though he was the most obedient of creation.

Nor may visible suffering now be interpreted as proof of final condemnation. Illness, grief, disability, and exhaustion alter faces without indicating God’s displeasure. The verse concerns a specifically revealed scene on the Last Day, not a license for physiognomic judgment in this world.

**What the face is rehearsing**

Repeated arrogance, contempt, and deception shape expression and social atmosphere even now. Conversely, mercy and truthful humility often soften interaction. This is not an infallible rule, but conduct does train the person who performs it. Every lie requires the face to participate; every sincere apology teaches it another posture.

The verse calls for a private question: what future am I teaching my face to expect? Hope is built through repentance and good deeds, not self-flattery; fear is relieved through return, not denial.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:25

> **knowing that a spine-crushing calamity will befall them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**They expect what shatters the spine**

*Taẓunnu an yufʿala bihā fāqirah.* Here *taẓunnu* carries the sense of knowing or being certain through overwhelming expectation, not weak conjecture. *Fāqirah* derives from the vertebrae of the back and denotes a disaster that breaks the spine—a ruin whose burden cannot be borne.

The verse explains the gloom of verse 24. Their terror is not confusion about what might happen; it is recognition that deserved consequence is imminent. The Qur’an elsewhere says:

> “And the evildoers will see the Fire and realize that they are to fall into it, and they will find no way to turn from it.” (Qur’an 18:53)

**Moral denial collapses into moral recognition**

Earlier, the person asked mockingly when resurrection would come. Now he knows. Knowledge alone was never salvation; beneficial knowledge must be joined to humble response while action remains possible. This distinction explains why Islamic faith is not bare assent. Satan knew his Lord and the coming judgment yet refused obedient humility.

The spine bears the body’s load. A “spine-breaking” consequence symbolizes total incapacity. Wealth, argument, and alliance cannot carry it away. This is the endpoint of self-sufficiency imagined without God.

**Warning is itself an act of mercy**

Descriptions of punishment can be mishandled through sensationalism. The Qur’an’s purpose is preventive truth. A clear warning sign before a collapsed bridge is mercy, not hostility. Concealing danger to preserve a traveler’s comfort would be betrayal.

The believer balances the warning with God’s open invitation. No living reader is told that his case is closed. So long as life remains, the expected calamity can be met with repentance, faith, restored rights, and God’s pardon. Fear is meant to move the feet, not freeze them.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:26

> **Nay! But when it reaches the collarbones,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The universal threshold arrives**

*Kallā idhā balaghat al-tarāqī.* The subject—commonly understood as the soul—is left implicit, because the context of dying makes it unmistakable. *Balaghat* means reaches; *al-tarāqī* is the plural of *tarquwah*, the collarbone area at the upper chest and throat. The image depicts life withdrawing to its final bodily boundary.

*Kallā* may rebuke love of the immediate world: no, it will not remain. It may also affirm the certainty of what follows: truly, the soul will reach this point. The transition from final judgment to the deathbed shows that every person has a private gateway to the universal resurrection.

The Qur’an gives a parallel:

> “Then why, when the soul reaches the throat, while you are at that moment looking on—and We are nearer to him than you, but you do not see—why, if you are not to be judged, do you not bring it back, if you are truthful?” (Qur’an 56:83–87)

**Human skill meets an appointed limit**

Medicine can relieve pain, restart a heart in some circumstances, support breathing, and prolong life by God’s permission. These are merciful means to use. Yet no physician can make mortality optional. The deathbed reveals both the dignity and boundary of human knowledge: family and clinicians may be intensely present, but they cannot command the departing soul.

Islam neither romanticizes the agony of death nor treats medical care as resistance to God. Seeking treatment is permitted and often recommended; accepting the appointed end is also faith. The two belong at different levels—use means while entrusting decree to their Lord.

**Repentance before the throat**

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> “Indeed, God accepts the servant’s repentance so long as the death-rattle has not reached his throat.” (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī; Sunan Ibn Mājah)

The limit exists because repentance after direct unveiling no longer has the same voluntary character. The verse makes procrastination irrational. People prepare wills and emergency contacts because death is possible; the soul’s moral affairs deserve at least equal preparation.

Write debts, return trusts, reconcile where safe and right, maintain prayer, and keep the tongue moist with remembrance. The purpose is not constant panic but readiness that allows peaceful living.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:27

> **and it is said, “Who will [make him] ascend?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A desperate question around the dying person**

*Wa-qīla man rāq.* The compact word *rāq* admits interpretations recorded by classical exegetes. From *ruqyah*, it can mean “Who will recite a healing incantation?”—that is, who can cure him? From the sense of ascent, it may concern which angels will take the soul upward. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī preserve these readings. The deathbed scene can encompass both: family searches for a healer while the unseen agents of death approach.

The passive “it is said” captures the room’s helplessness. People call doctors, elders, or reciters, each hoping someone possesses one more intervention. Seeking lawful treatment and Qur’anic supplication is sound. The error is imagining any healer acts independently of God.

**Ruqyah between revelation and superstition**

The Prophet ﷺ approved ruqyah free of polytheism:

> “Present your ruqyahs to me. There is no harm in ruqyah so long as it contains no shirk.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Authentic supplications, al-Fātiḥah, al-Ikhlāṣ, al-Falaq, and al-Nās may be recited while medical treatment is pursued. Islam rejects both materialist arrogance and magical exploitation. A reciter cannot guarantee life, sell access to Divine power, use unknown invocations, or blame a dying patient for insufficient faith.

**Care when cure is no longer possible**

The question “Who can cure?” must eventually become “How can we care?” Feeding, pain relief, gentle remembrance, preserving dignity, and allowing family presence can remain duties when reversal is impossible. Not every treatment that extends biological process is automatically beneficial; difficult decisions should involve qualified clinicians, knowledgeable scholars, and the patient’s known wishes.

Spiritually, the verse asks whether one’s entire relationship with God begins only when cures fail. The Lord of health should be known during health. Regular gratitude prevents crisis-faith from being the soul’s only vocabulary.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:28

> **And he knows for certain that it is the parting;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Certainty at the edge of separation**

*Wa-ẓanna annahu al-firāq.* Though *ẓanna* often means “supposed,” here it means the dying person becomes certain. *Al-firāq* is separation: the soul from the body, the person from family, possessions, habits, and worldly opportunity. What was intellectually known becomes immediate recognition.

The verse does not deny grief. The Prophet ﷺ wept when his son Ibrāhīm died and said:

> “The eyes shed tears and the heart grieves, but we say only what pleases our Lord. O Ibrāhīm, we are indeed grieved by your departure.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

Faith regulates grief without abolishing love. Separation hurts because relationship mattered.

**Possession ends before ownership was ever real**

At death, titles remain in documents but no longer empower the departed. Clothes are removed, accounts transferred, and private spaces entered by others. The Qur’an says:

> “You have certainly come to Us alone as We created you the first time, and you have left whatever We bestowed upon you behind your backs.” (Qur’an 6:94)

This is not an argument against owning property lawfully. It is an argument against letting temporary stewardship define the self. What accompanies the person is not the object but the moral quality of obtaining, using, sharing, or withholding it.

**Practicing small separations**

Fasting separates from food temporarily; charity separates from wealth; forgiveness separates from the claim to retaliate; pilgrimage separates from ordinary dress and rank. These acts educate the soul to release what it never absolutely owned.

Preparation also means making separation kinder for others: a valid will, clear debts, organized responsibilities, and words of affection spoken before emergency. Remembering death need not make relationships cold. It can remove trivial pride and make present companionship more truthful.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:29

> **and the shank is intertwined with the shank.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Severity meets severity**

*Wa-ltaffat al-sāqu bi-l-sāq.* *Iltaffat* means wrapped, entwined, or joined. *Sāq* is the shin or leg. Classical commentators gave several compatible explanations: the dying person’s legs come together in weakness; the legs are wrapped in the shroud; the hardship of leaving this world meets the hardship of entering the next; or the last day of worldly life joins the first stage of the Hereafter.

The concrete image prevents death from becoming an abstraction. Legs that carried a person through plans can no longer carry him away. Soon others straighten, wash, and wrap them.

**The Qur’an preserves dignity while speaking plainly**

The verse is physically direct but not voyeuristic. It names mortality without sensational detail. This is a model for speaking about death to families and children: truthful, gentle, and purposeful. Euphemism that makes death unspeakable can leave people unprepared; graphic fascination can violate dignity. Revelation takes the middle path.

**The feet as a record of direction**

The Qur’an says:

> “Do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge. Indeed, hearing, sight, and heart—each of these will be questioned.” (Qur’an 17:36)

And the Prophet ﷺ said that a servant’s feet will not move on the Day of Resurrection until questioned about his life and how he spent it, his knowledge and action, and his wealth—how he earned and spent it—among other matters (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī).

Feet signify life’s direction. They walk toward prayer, family duty, knowledge, forbidden meetings, and oppression. At death they are joined and stilled; agency has passed. The practical invitation is to redirect them while they move.

**Two severities, one possible ease**

For the heedless, worldly departure and otherworldly arrival combine in distress. For the believer, death can still be painful, but Divine mercy grants steadiness. One should neither presume an easy death as proof of righteousness nor read physical struggle as condemnation; illness has bodily causes and hidden states belong to God. The verse warns universally while final judgment remains His alone.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:30

> **Unto thy Lord that Day is the driving.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The journey no creature directs**

*Ilā rabbika yawmaʾidhin al-masāq.* Like verse 12, “to your Lord” comes first. *Masāq* derives from s-w-q, to drive or lead onward. The passive sense indicates that the person is conveyed toward the Lord’s judgment, no longer choosing route or pace.

The repetition with variation is precise. Verse 12 names final settlement (*mustaqarr*); verse 30 names being driven (*masāq*). The former views destination, the latter the irreversible passage beginning at death. Both dismantle imagined autonomy.

The Qur’an depicts resurrection:

> “And every soul will come, with it a driver and a witness.” (Qur’an 50:21)

Classical scholars often identify these as angels, one driving and one testifying, while the verse’s primary lesson is unavoidable presentation before God.

**Willing surrender before compelled return**

Islam means willing submission. Every creature is already under God’s cosmic decree; believers additionally submit morally by choice. The person who lets prayer, truth, and mercy lead him now approaches the inevitable return in conscious surrender. The one who spends life resisting lordship is still driven back, but without the peace of consent.

This resembles a patient choosing to follow life-saving guidance before emergency removes options. The analogy is limited—God needs nothing and His knowledge is perfect—but it clarifies why voluntary obedience is dignity, not humiliation.

**Funeral movement and communal responsibility**

After death, others carry the body. The Prophet ﷺ urged that the funeral be taken forward without needless delay:

> “Hasten with the funeral. If the deceased was righteous, it is a good to which you are taking him; and if otherwise, it is an evil you are removing from your necks.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The living wash, shroud, pray, carry, and bury with dignity, but they cannot carry another’s faith. Love performs the final earthly duties, then entrusts the person to the Lord.

For everyday practice, verse 30 asks what drives us now. Appetite, fear of opinion, and ambition can push invisibly. Revelation offers deliberate direction before all persons enter the final *masāq*.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:31

> **For he neither confirmed nor prayed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The ruin is traced back to two absences**

*Fa-lā ṣaddaqa wa-lā ṣallā.* The verse now explains why the preceding death is terrifying. *Ṣaddaqa* means he confirmed, believed as true, or acted truthfully toward the message. *Ṣallā* means he prayed. In two balanced negatives, revelation names inward rejection and outward abandonment.

Some lexical discussion noted that *ṣaddaqa* can also be associated with giving charity, but the immediate contrast with “he denied” in verse 32 strongly supports “he did not affirm.” The wider meaning is not excluded: genuine affirmation bears fruit, including generosity.

**Faith and prayer belong together**

Prayer is singled out because it is the central repeated enactment of belief. Five times daily the body confirms what the heart claims: God is greater, praise belongs to Him, guidance is needed, and return is certain. When prayer is habitually abandoned, the claim of faith is deprived of its principal public and private structure.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> “Between a man and polytheism and disbelief is the abandonment of prayer.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Jurists differed over the precise ruling on one who abandons prayer from laziness while acknowledging its obligation, but they agreed that the matter is among the gravest dangers. No disagreement turns prayer into an optional symbol.

**Affirmation is more than information**

The Arabic truth-root ṣ-d-q opposes inner fragmentation. To affirm revelation is to let it define reality, not merely check a doctrinal box. A patient who says a diagnosis is true but refuses every implication has not treated it as operational truth. Likewise, resurrection affirmed only on the tongue may remain functionally denied.

Yet the verse should not drive imperfect worshipers to despair. Missing or struggling in prayer calls for immediate return, learning, and consistency. God’s mercy is not reserved for those who never stumbled. The danger is settled refusal defended until death.

**Rebuilding the central practice**

Practical repair begins with the next obligatory prayer, not an imaginary flawless future. Learn purification, set reliable reminders, arrange work and travel responsibly, seek community, and make up duties according to sound juristic guidance. Prayer is not a reward for becoming spiritually healthy; it is among the chief means by which health returns.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:32

> **but he denied and turned away,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Denial becomes direction**

*Wa-lākin kadhdhaba wa-tawallā.* *Kadhdhaba* is intensive denial—declaring the message false. *Tawallā* means turning one’s back, withdrawing, or assuming an opposing direction. Verse 31 gave two neglected goods; this verse gives two chosen evils. The structure is moral: absence hardens into resistance.

The same pairing appears elsewhere:

> “But he denied and disobeyed. Then he turned his back, striving against the truth.” (Qur’an 79:21–22)

A person may initially fail through distraction. If conscience is repeatedly silenced, failure can seek a theory that calls obedience false. Conduct and conviction interact in both directions.

**Turning away is embodied**

The Qur’an speaks as though denial has posture. People avert attention, avoid reminders, mock the messenger, or leave the place where truth is spoken. In modern life, turning away may look like curating every source so no belief is challenged, or reducing religion to content consumed without obedience.

This does not mean one must listen to every claim. Intellectual boundaries and reliable scholarship matter. The diagnosis concerns refusing truth because it is truth or because it threatens desire—not prudently avoiding confusion.

**The justice of judgment after communicated truth**

God does not punish people for revelation that never reached them in an accountable manner. The Qur’an states:

> “We never punish until We have sent a messenger.” (Qur’an 17:15)

Individual cases, capacities, and access belong to God’s perfect knowledge. The person portrayed here encountered the claim, denied it, and deliberately turned away. This safeguards the doctrine of Divine justice from careless human generalization.

**The opposite movement**

Repentance is literally return. If *tawallā* is turning away, tawbah is turning back. One can reverse direction before death through truthful affirmation, prayer, restitution, and renewed listening. The Qur’an exposes the path to prevent its completion.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:33

> **then went to his people swaggering.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Private refusal seeks social applause**

*Thumma dhahaba ilā ahlihi yatamaṭṭā.* He returns to his family or social circle *yatamaṭṭā*—swaggering, stretching himself in pride, walking with self-satisfaction. Reports and commentaries connect the description particularly with Abū Jahl, though the moral portrait extends to everyone who converts rejection into performance.

The sequence is chilling: he does not affirm, does not pray, denies, turns away, and then feels triumphant. Conscience has been replaced by the approving gaze of his group. The Qur’an similarly portrays mockers:

> “When they returned to their people, they returned jesting.” (Qur’an 83:31)

Social belonging can make falsehood feel safe. A room that laughs together can momentarily silence the blaming soul.

**Pride after sin is more dangerous than weakness after sin**

A believer may fall and grieve; that grief can lead home. This figure sins and swaggers. The deed is joined to contempt, making repentance less likely. The Prophet ﷺ defined arrogance with moral precision:

> “Arrogance is rejecting the truth and looking down upon people.” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Fine clothing or confident posture is not inherently arrogance. The disease is inward rejection and contempt, exactly what the verse depicts.

**Family and peers can amplify or interrupt delusion**

“Ahl” can be a shelter of faith or an echo chamber. Families sometimes celebrate unethical success because it raises collective status; peer groups normalize mockery no member would attempt alone. Responsibility remains individual, but social reinforcement is real.

This creates a duty to build circles where correction is safe and sincere obedience is honored. A friend who gently interrupts pride may be more loyal than one who applauds everything. Parents should avoid praising cleverness used to deceive or victory gained through injustice.

**Walking humbly without performing humility**

The servants of the Most Merciful are described as those who walk gently upon the earth (25:63). Humility is not theatrical weakness, bent posture, or denial of skills. It is receiving truth from whoever brings it, attributing gifts to God, and refusing contempt. The verse asks not merely where one walks after an action, but what kind of self returns to its people.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:34

> **So nearer to you! And nearer!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A warning brought unbearably near**

*Awlā laka fa-awlā* is an idiom of threat and impending woe. It can carry the sense, “Woe to you,” “nearer to you is what you hate,” or “you have more claim to destruction.” Its repetition gives solemn finality. The word *awlā* is related to nearness or priority: consequence is not distant from the swaggering denier.

Some exegetical reports apply these words to Abū Jahl and narrate the Prophet ﷺ confronting him, but the Qur’anic address remains available to every similar posture. Classical commentators variously distributed the four repetitions across worldly punishment, grave, Resurrection, and Fire. Such distributions are interpretive illustrations; the plain point is compounded warning.

**Threat after evidence is moral communication**

The sūrah has not begun with threat alone. It presented conscience, rational evidence from creation, preserved revelation, and the deathbed. Warning follows repeated refusal. Divine threat is therefore not uncontrolled anger; it communicates the real consequence of freely maintained rebellion.

The Qur’an joins warning and mercy:

> “Inform My servants that I am the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful, and that My punishment is the painful punishment.” (Qur’an 15:49–50)

Removing either half distorts God’s address. Hope without accountability becomes presumption; fear without mercy becomes despair.

**When “later” becomes “near”**

Verse 5 exposed the wish to sin forward; verse 34 says the outcome is already near. Human beings measure nearness by calendar distance, but anything certain and irreversible is existentially near. Childhood feels recent to the elderly; entire decades contract in memory.

A practical response to warning is not vague unease. Identify the matter to which the warning applies and act: pray, repay, stop, apologize, seek qualified help, or leave the setting of temptation. Revelation’s threats are meant to rescue action from procrastination.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:35

> **Then nearer to you! And nearer!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Repetition that removes every casual hearing**

*Thumma awlā laka fa-awlā.* The addition of *thumma*, “then,” after the previous double warning creates escalation and sequence. Four beats now answer four movements in verses 31–33: failure to affirm, failure to pray, active denial, and arrogant turning away. The rhetoric surrounds the person’s confidence with approaching consequence.

Arabic repetition can intensify, confirm, and make a warning linger in the ear. This is not filler. A fire alarm repeats because a single quiet signal could be absorbed into background noise. The greater the danger, the more merciful insistent clarity becomes.

**No pleasure should be isolated from its ending**

The swaggering walk may last minutes; its moral direction can last into eternity. Revelation teaches consequential vision—seeing the end while choosing the beginning. The Qur’an asks:

> “Have you considered: if We let them enjoy themselves for years, then there comes to them what they were promised, of what benefit to them would their past enjoyment be?” (Qur’an 26:205–207)

Temporary enjoyment is not itself blameworthy. The question concerns enjoyment purchased by denial and injustice. Once duration and consequence are included, the supposed bargain is exposed.

**Warning oneself before warning others**

Repeated threat can tempt religious speakers to harshness. But the first audience is the speaker’s own soul. The Prophet ﷺ was sent as a mercy and warned with tears, patience, and concern. A person who enjoys others’ possible punishment has not learned the moral tone of warning.

The correct fruit is urgency joined to compassion: protect oneself, invite others wisely, and never declare a living person beyond repentance. The fourfold woe is exactly why daʿwah should not be careless; eternity is too serious for ego-driven argument.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:36

> **Does man suppose that he would be left aimless?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The question beneath every question of meaning**

*A-yaḥsabu al-insānu an yutraka sudā.* *Yutraka* means to be left or abandoned. *Sudā* means neglected, aimless, without command or accountability—like livestock left to wander without a keeper. The verse asks whether a creature bearing reason, conscience, and moral agency imagines that no purpose or answer follows.

Al-Shāfiʿī reportedly treated this as an unanswerable rational challenge: God is too wise to create morally capable persons and leave them entirely without command and prohibition. The Qur’an states elsewhere:

> “Did you think that We created you aimlessly and that you would not be returned to Us?” (Qur’an 23:115)

Creation and return are joined by wisdom.

**Purpose is received, then responsibly pursued**

Islam gives universal purpose:

> “I did not create jinn and mankind except to worship Me.” (Qur’an 51:56)

Worship includes prayer and remembrance, but also lawful work, family care, learning, justice, and cultivation when oriented by obedience. Within this universal calling, people exercise judgment about vocation and circumstance. Revelation does not promise each person one hidden career choice that must be guessed perfectly.

This relieves two anxieties. Life is not meaningless, because its ultimate purpose is disclosed. Nor is every personal decision dictated, because the disclosed purpose can be served through many lawful paths.

**Accountability dignifies agency**

A world with no answerability might appear liberating, but it empties sacrifice and cruelty of final distinction. If oppressor and victim simply vanish into the same nothing, justice becomes a temporary human preference. Resurrection establishes that choices matter beyond power’s ability to rewrite them.

Responsibility is proportioned to knowledge and capacity. Children, the mentally incapable, the coerced, and those unreached by a message are not treated identically. God says:

> “God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity.” (Qur’an 2:286)

Purpose without proportional justice would be tyranny; Divine purpose is governed by perfect knowledge and mercy.

**Living deliberately rather than drifting**

Aimlessness often appears not as philosophical atheism but as unexamined routine: work, consume, scroll, sleep, repeat. Verse 36 interrupts drift. A weekly review can reconnect activity to worship: Which duty am I fulfilling? Whom does this benefit? What boundary must I observe? What am I becoming?

One need not perform spectacular deeds to live purposefully. Consistent prayer, lawful provision, patient caregiving, honest craft, and restrained speech can carry immense weight. The opposite of *sudā* is not fame; it is accountable direction.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:37

> **Was he not a drop of semen emitted?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The denier is returned to his unchosen beginning**

*A-lam yaku nuṭfatan min maniyyin yumnā.* *Nuṭfah* is a tiny drop of fluid. *Maniyy* is reproductive fluid, and *yumnā* means emitted or poured forth; it may also carry the passive sense of being destined and measured. The verse reduces human pretension without reducing human dignity: the proud adult began as a drop he did not produce by conscious command.

The Qur’an repeatedly reasons from first creation to resurrection:

> “Does man not see that We created him from a drop? Then suddenly he becomes a clear adversary.” (Qur’an 36:77)

The irony lies in using capacities received through creation to dispute the Creator’s capacity to recreate.

**Humility grounded in biology**

We did not select parents, genetic inheritance, first cell division, or the conditions sustaining gestation. Even parents, though real causes, do not design the child’s formation. Remembering origin challenges racial pride, class arrogance, bodily vanity, and intellectual self-worship. Every ruler and laborer begins dependent.

Islamic humility is not contempt for the body. The very drop becomes, by God’s will, a person entrusted with knowledge and moral choice. Humility says, “My gifts are real and accountable, but not self-originated.” This allows excellence without arrogance and gratitude without denial of effort.

**Revelation and embryological observation**

The verse speaks in accessible observational language, not a modern laboratory textbook. Its theological argument does not require forcing every scientific detail into a premodern term. What remains striking and rationally sufficient is the transformation from a minute reproductive beginning through ordered stages into a conscious human being.

The Qur’an invites observation:

> “We will show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth.” (Qur’an 41:53)

Scientific study can deepen wonder at mechanism; it does not answer away why ordered capacities exist or why they are signs of power. Sound faith welcomes accurate investigation and avoids exaggerated claims that may collapse when terminology is refined.

**A moral argument, not merely a biological fact**

Remembering the drop affects conduct. How can someone formed from dependence despise the poor, abuse the weak, or act self-sufficient before God? Origin is an ethical equalizer. It also provides hope: the Lord who developed us through stages can transform entrenched character through repentance and patient discipline.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:38

> **Then he was a blood clot; whereupon He created, then fashioned,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From attachment to fashioned integrity**

*Thumma kāna ʿalaqatan fa-khalaqa fa-sawwā.* *ʿAlaqah* comes from ʿ-l-q, to cling or attach; it has been rendered a clinging substance or clot. *Khalaqa* means He created, and *sawwā* means He proportioned, completed, and fashioned harmoniously. The repeated Divine subject is understood: the drop does not design its own development.

*Thumma* marks a stage following the first; *fa-* then conveys unfolding consequence. The concise language compresses an immense process without treating it as random. Elsewhere the stages are expanded:

> “Then We made the drop into a clinging form, and We made the clinging form into a lump, and We made the lump into bones, and We clothed the bones with flesh; then We developed him into another creation. So blessed is God, the best of creators.” (Qur’an 23:14)

**Creation and proportion are distinct gifts**

Existence alone is astonishing; coordinated existence is further evidence. Organs differentiate, systems communicate, and growth follows timings no embryo consciously manages. *Fa-sawwā* directs attention to fitness and proportion, not simplistic claims that every bodily condition appears symmetrical or free of trial.

Disability does not negate Divine wisdom or human dignity. The Qur’an’s moral measure is taqwā, and bodily variation belongs to a world of testing whose wisdom may be hidden. Muslims must never weaponize creation language to shame people whose bodies differ. Proper wonder produces care, accessibility, and gratitude.

**The stronger case for re-creation**

The closing argument is *qiyās al-awlā*, reasoning from the greater or prior accomplishment to what is at least as possible. The One who transformed an unseen drop through complex stages can restore a known creature. The Qur’an expresses it:

> “And it is He who begins creation, then repeats it, and that is easier for Him.” (Qur’an 30:27)

“Easier” addresses human understanding; no act is difficult for God. First creation supplies the visible premise, resurrection the necessary conclusion affirmed by revelation.

**Spiritual formation also requires stages**

Without reducing the verse to analogy, embryonic growth teaches patience with lawful development. Knowledge, character, and communities are rarely completed instantly. One should not demand mature fruit from a seed while refusing consistent cultivation. God creates by “Be,” yet in wisdom often unfolds effects through stages. Respecting process can itself be trust in His way of nurturing.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:39

> **and made from him the two genders, male and female.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Human continuation emerges from the tiny origin**

*Fa-jaʿala minhu al-zawjayni al-dhakara wa-l-unthā.* *Jaʿala* means He made or appointed. *Al-zawjayn* is the pair or two kinds, then specified as male and female. *Minhu*—“from him” or “from it”—returns to the reproductive origin. From material apparently undifferentiated to ordinary sight, God brings the sexes through which human generations continue.

The verse reinforces power and measure. Parents do not command the child’s sex; social preference cannot alter Divine knowledge or a child’s equal human worth. The Qur’an says:

> “To God belongs the dominion of the heavens and earth. He creates what He wills. He grants female children to whom He wills, and grants male children to whom He wills, or pairs them as males and females, and He makes whom He wills infertile.” (Qur’an 42:49–50)

This passage begins with daughters to correct cultures that treat them as lesser gifts.

**Equal moral dignity, meaningful created distinction**

Male and female share origin, servitude, accountability, and access to reward:

> “Indeed, the Muslim men and Muslim women, the believing men and believing women … God has prepared for them forgiveness and a great reward.” (Qur’an 33:35)

The verse’s binary naming concerns the two sexes by which human reproduction ordinarily proceeds. It does not authorize cruelty toward people facing atypical development, infertility, bodily ambiguity, or psychological distress. Classical Islamic law discussed intersex conditions (*khunthā*) with legal care, showing that recognition of hard cases need not erase the general created pattern.

A truthful ethic combines affirmation with compassion. Mockery, assault, dehumanization, and intrusion into private matters remain forbidden. Moral guidance is not strengthened by injustice.

**Neither sex is self-sufficient**

The pair points to mutual dependence. Humanity continues through contributions neither sex supplies alone. Marriage in the Qur’an is marked by tranquility, affection, and mercy, not a contest for absolute independence:

> “And among His signs is that He created for you spouses from among yourselves so that you may find tranquility in them, and He placed between you affection and mercy.” (Qur’an 30:21)

Not every person marries or has children, and worth does not depend on doing so. The wider lesson is creaturely interdependence: everyone is born through others and raised by networks of care.

**A social correction to preference and blame**

Pre-Islamic contempt for daughters is condemned with unusual force in the Qur’an. Verse 39 makes both male and female direct products of Divine making. It is therefore irrational to congratulate human choice for a son or blame a mother for a daughter. Modern forms of sex-selective neglect repeat an ignorance revelation dismantled.

The argument returns to resurrection: the One who generates immense human diversity through a paired system is not exhausted by creation. His power is displayed in every birth.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:40

> **Is not such a one able to give life to the dead?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The argument reaches its unavoidable conclusion**

*A-laysa dhālika bi-qādirin ʿalā an yuḥyiya al-mawtā?* “Is not that One”—the One who formed, developed, and differentiated human life—fully able to revive the dead? The construction with *bi-qādir* intensifies the affirmation. *Yuḥyiya* comes from ḥ-y-y, life; *al-mawtā* is the dead collectively.

The expected answer is “Yes, indeed.” Reports preserve that the Prophet ﷺ responded with affirmation when reciting this question. Abū Dāwūd records a related instruction concerning an equivalent ending in Sūrat al-Tīn: when one recites, “Is not God the most just of judges?” one should answer, “Indeed, and I am among the witnesses.” Though hadith gradings and exact applications are discussed, the devotional principle is sound: Qur’anic questions seek believing assent, not passive silence.

**The proof from first creation completed**

The logic has three clear steps. We undeniably began without having created ourselves. Our development displays power, knowledge, and proportion beyond our conscious direction. Re-creation of the known person cannot rationally be declared impossible for the One who originated that person. God says:

> “Were We wearied by the first creation? Yet they are in confusion about a new creation.” (Qur’an 50:15)

And:

> “Your creation and your resurrection are only like that of a single soul. Indeed, God is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.” (Qur’an 31:28)

Divine power is not divided among tasks or depleted by number. Raising all humanity is no harder for God than raising one—not because resurrection is small, but because infinite power does not strain.

**Resurrection of bodies and restoration of justice**

“To give life to the dead” confirms real resurrection, not merely the survival of ideals or memories. The person who acted is restored to receive mercy or justice. This is necessary to the sūrah’s moral architecture: conscience, deeds, faces, and bodily origin all converge in personal return.

The doctrine also answers grief. Death is a genuine separation, but not annihilation. The Prophet ﷺ taught that people will be gathered, and revelation promises reunion for the faithful by God’s grace. Grief can therefore remain grief without becoming nihilism.

**Divine ability does not remove human responsibility**

Because God can raise the dead, present life is not disposable. Every day is a nonrenewable part of the examination. The verse does not invite speculation about when or how; it invites preparation through faith, prayer, truth, restored rights, and hope in mercy.

Nor should a sinner conclude that spiritual renewal is impossible. The God who gives bodily life after death can revive a heart hardened by years of neglect. The Qur’an uses rain as a visible analogy:

> “And you see the earth lifeless, but when We send down upon it water, it stirs and swells and grows every beautiful kind. That is because God is the Truth, and because He gives life to the dead, and because He is over all things powerful.” (Qur’an 22:5–6)

Repentance is a present sign of that life-giving mercy. A heart may look barren, yet revelation, remembrance, and sincere return can make it grow.

**The answer with which the sūrah leaves us**

The chapter began with God’s oath by Resurrection and ends by requiring the human being’s confession of Divine power. Between oath and confession, it exposed the motives of denial, guaranteed the message, contrasted the final faces, carried the reader through death, and returned him to conception. No relevant part of the person lies outside the argument: reason sees first creation, conscience sees guilt, revelation announces return, and mortality prevents endless delay.

So the fitting response is not only spoken but lived: *Balā*—yes indeed. Yes, God can raise the dead; therefore I will not treat conscience as an enemy, revelation as noise, the world as permanent, death as abstraction, or repentance as tomorrow’s work. The One able to begin is able to restore, and to Him is the final return.

---

## Concluding Synthesis

**From inward blame to final standing**

Sūrah al-Qiyāmah joins realities people often separate. Conscience is connected to judgment; accurate reception of revelation to bodily resurrection; love of immediacy to neglect of eternity; private choices to visible faces; death’s physical signs to ultimate purpose. Its argument is not that human beings possess no evidence, but that desire can prevent evidence from governing life.

**Gathering as the chapter’s quiet thread**

God gathers bones, sun and moon, revelation in the Prophet’s breast, and every deed into an intelligible account. Human life appears scattered from within, but nothing is scattered to Divine knowledge. This theme gives fear and comfort together. Hidden wrong cannot disappear; hidden good cannot be lost.

**A complete program of preparation**

The sūrah’s response can be practiced now: let conscience conduct an honest reckoning without falling into despair; receive revelation patiently before speaking about it; resist immediate desire where it purchases lasting loss; establish prayer as embodied affirmation; remember death without abandoning worldly duty; and reason from the signs within one’s own creation to the certainty of return.

Such remembrance makes worldly life more responsible, not less. Rights matter because they will be restored. Bodies matter because they will be raised. Time matters because its opportunities end. Mercy matters because no one survives by achievement alone. The final question remains addressed to every reader: Is the One who did all this not able to give life to the dead? The believing heart answers with tongue, conduct, and hope: Yes indeed.
