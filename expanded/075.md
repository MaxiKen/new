# Sūrah al-Qiyāmah (Chapter 75) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Makkan Sūrah That Makes the Last Day Present**

Sūrah al-Qiyāmah, “The Resurrection,” is an early Makkan sūrah. Its central concern is the reality of being raised after death, but it does not discuss resurrection as an abstract doctrine detached from daily life. It brings the final Day into the conscience of the living person. The one who asks, “When is it?” is made to confront what his own eyes, soul, body, choices, and eventual death already testify. The sūrah moves from oath, to denial, to cosmic upheaval, to personal accountability, to the protection of revelation, to the contrast between faces that are illuminated and faces that are distressed, and finally back to the beginning of human life in the womb. Its conclusion is a direct argument: the One who created a human being from a fluid drop, shaped him, and made him male and female is certainly able to give life to the dead.

The sūrah is also known by its opening words, *Lā uqsimu*, “I swear,” because Arabic sūrahs are sometimes identified by their first expression. The name al-Qiyāmah comes from *q-y-m*, a root connected with standing, rising, and being established. *Al-Qiyāmah* is the Day on which the dead stand, the truth stands exposed, and the divine judgment stands with complete justice. The title therefore describes not merely an event but the final establishment of reality after the temporary arrangements of worldly life have disappeared.

**The Inner Argument of the Chapter**

The sūrah's argument is cumulative. It begins with two witnesses: the Day of Resurrection itself and the self-reproaching soul. It then answers the claim that scattered bones cannot be gathered by pointing out that God can recreate even the smallest details of the human body. The denial is shown not to be an intellectual problem but a moral desire: the human being wants to continue sinning without facing a future account. When the Hour arrives, the question will no longer be whether it is real but where escape might be found. The answer is that every person will be driven to his Lord and informed of what he sent forward and what he left behind.

The middle passage concerning revelation has its own setting and lesson. The Prophet ﷺ was eager to preserve every revealed word and would move his tongue while Gabriel recited. God assured him that the Qur'an would be gathered, recited, and explained by divine care. This passage is therefore evidence of revelation's preservation, but it is also a lesson in receiving knowledge: do not rush to seize a conclusion before the message has been fully heard and understood.

The closing movement returns to the human being's own origin. A person who began as an emitted drop, then passed through stages of created formation, should not regard resurrection as improbable. The sūrah closes with a question that carries its answer within it:

> **“Is not such a one able to give life to the dead?”** (Qur'an 75:40)

The answer is yes. Resurrection is not a second creation beyond God's ability; it is a further act of the same creative power that is already visible in every human birth.

**Its Moral Atmosphere**

This is a sūrah of warning, but its warning is not meant to produce empty terror. It calls the reader to honesty before God, to repentance while the self-reproaching conscience still functions, to patience in receiving revelation, to freedom from the love of the immediate world, and to preparation for the meeting that cannot be avoided. It is severe toward denial and self-deception, yet it preserves the door of return. The very existence of the *nafs al-lawwāmah*, the blaming soul, means that a person can recognize a wrong and turn back before death closes the period of action.

What follows treats all forty verses separately. Verses that naturally form a single scene are still given independent sections, so that the wording and teaching of every verse can be considered on its own.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:1]

> **I swear by the Day of Resurrection.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath by the Day When All Claims Are Settled**

The sūrah opens with *lā uqsimu bi-yawm al-qiyāmah*. The translation “I swear” follows the well-known interpretation of *lā* as an emphatic particle. Some early commentators understood *lā uqsimu* more literally as “I do not swear,” while explaining that the expression then means that the matter is too clear to require an oath, or that the particle rejects the denial of the people addressed before the oath is stated. These readings differ in grammar, but they agree in force: resurrection is not a marginal possibility. It is a decisive truth before which denial must be confronted.

The noun *qiyāmah* comes from the root *q-w-m*, associated with standing upright, rising, and being established. The Day is called the Day of Resurrection because bodies will rise from the earth, humanity will stand before its Creator, and justice will no longer remain hidden behind worldly circumstances. In the present life, a false claim may remain influential because the truth-teller lacks power, a wrong may remain unpunished because the victim dies, and an oppressor may appear successful because the period of examination is not finished. The Resurrection is the day on which every unfinished matter reaches its proper conclusion.

**A Truth Confirmed by Revelation and Reason**

The Qur'an repeatedly presents the Resurrection as a necessary consequence of God's wisdom, not merely as a startling event. If human beings were created with moral awareness, given meaningful choices, and then reduced at death to an identical final nothingness, the difference between truth and falsehood would never be fully disclosed. The Qur'an states:

> **“Did you suppose that We created you in vain and that unto Us you would not be returned?”** (Qur'an 23:115)

The question is logical. A wise action is not purposeless, and the human being is not merely an animal with appetite. People make promises, accept duties, sacrifice for principles, condemn injustice, and feel accountable even when no other person is watching. These features point toward a moral order that worldly life does not complete. The Resurrection is the completion of that order.

Another verse links creation and return:

> **“And He it is Who originates creation, then recreates it, and that is easier for Him.”** (Qur'an 30:27)

“Easier” here is not a limitation upon God, as though one divine act were difficult and another easy in relation to Him. God is not subject to effort, fatigue, or increasing ability. The expression addresses human reasoning: if a person admits that the universe and human life were brought into existence, then he has already admitted the greater premise from which resurrection follows. The question is not whether God can create; it is whether human beings will accept the consequence of what they already observe.

**The Ethical Effect of Remembering the Oath**

An oath by the Resurrection changes the way a person evaluates the present. Without the Hereafter, immediate gain easily becomes the main measure of success. A lie may seem useful if it protects a career. A hidden betrayal may seem harmless if it is never exposed. An act of patience may seem foolish if no reward is expected beyond the world. Belief in the Resurrection restores the missing horizon. It tells the person that no choice disappears merely because it is forgotten by people.

This does not require a Muslim to abandon lawful worldly responsibilities. Rather, it puts them in their correct position. Wealth, work, family, and public service remain important, but none is allowed to become the final purpose of life. The oath teaches the believer to live as a traveler who is already certain that the road has a destination. A traveler can rest, trade, and enjoy the scenery, but he does not mistake a temporary campsite for home.

**The Oath Is Also a Consolation**

The Day of Resurrection is a warning to the arrogant and a consolation to the oppressed. The person whose good is ignored by the world has not been ignored by God. The person who restrains himself from revenge for God's sake has not lost. The parent who sacrifices quietly, the poor person who remains honest, and the wronged person who refuses to answer injustice with injustice all live under the promise that the final account will be complete. God says:

> **“So whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it.”** (Qur'an 99:7–8)

The first verse of the sūrah therefore establishes the governing reality: life is moving toward a Day of standing. Every later discussion—the accusing conscience, the bodily resurrection, the faces of the blessed, the moment of death—unfolds beneath this oath.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:2]

> **And I swear by the blaming soul.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Soul That Will Not Let Wrong Remain Comfortable**

The second oath is by *al-nafs al-lawwāmah*, the blaming or self-reproaching soul. *Nafs* can refer to the self, the person, or the inward life of a human being. *Lawwāmah* comes from the root *l-w-m*, “to blame,” and its intensive form suggests a soul that repeatedly reproaches itself. It notices deficiency, regrets disobedience, questions motives, and refuses to treat moral failure as entirely normal.

This is not the same as destructive self-hatred. The Qur'anic blaming soul does not condemn the person as worthless; it judges the act against the standard of truth. It says, in effect, “You could have been more truthful,” “You knew better,” or “You should return to your Lord.” Such inner criticism can be a mercy when it leads to repentance and reform. It becomes harmful when it is transformed into despair, because despair turns the recognition of sin into an excuse for continuing in it.

**The Three Conditions of the Soul**

The Qur'an describes the human soul in several ways. It speaks of the soul that commands evil:

> **“Indeed, the soul is ever commanding toward evil, except those upon whom my Lord has mercy.”** (Qur'an 12:53)

It speaks here of the self-reproaching soul, which has not become comfortable with that command. And it concludes with the soul at rest:

> **“O soul at peace, return unto thy Lord, content and contenting. Enter among My servants; enter My Garden.”** (Qur'an 89:27–30)

These descriptions need not mean that every person passes through three fixed stages in a simple linear order. A believer may experience all three tendencies at different times. The lower impulse calls toward appetite; the conscience rebukes the failure; faith and disciplined obedience can bring tranquility. The same heart may be courageous in one area and weak in another. Spiritual growth is the gradual strengthening of the voice that returns the person to God.

**Why the Blaming Soul Is Joined to Resurrection**

The connection between the two oaths is profound. The Day of Resurrection exposes the truth of a person's life from outside, while the blaming soul exposes it from within. A person may hide a deed from family, colleagues, and society, but he cannot entirely remove its knowledge from himself. Even when the conscience is silenced, it often returns in anxiety, defensiveness, or the need to construct elaborate excuses.

A report attributed to the Prophet ﷺ states that every person will blame himself on the Day of Resurrection: the one who did good will say that he should have done more, and the one who did evil will wish that he had stopped. The meaning is consistent with the Qur'anic picture of the soul as a witness against itself. At the final Day, the inward awareness that people tried to suppress will correspond to the outward disclosure of the record.

The Qur'an says:

> **“Rather, man will be a witness against himself, even if he presents his excuses.”** (Qur'an 75:14–15)

The two passages illuminate one another. The blaming soul is an early, worldly form of the testimony that will become unmistakable in the Hereafter.

**Healthy Self-Examination Rather Than Paralysis**

The practical question is how to use self-reproach correctly. A conscience should function like a warning light in a vehicle. When the light appears, the wise driver does not smash the dashboard or declare the entire vehicle worthless. He stops, investigates, repairs what can be repaired, and continues more carefully. Similarly, a believer who discovers a sin should acknowledge it, stop it, seek forgiveness, repair harm done to others, and make a realistic plan to avoid returning to it.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“All the children of Adam are sinners, and the best of sinners are those who repent.”** (Sunan al-Tirmidhī, 2499; Ibn Mājah, 4251; graded ḥasan by al-Tirmidhī)

This hadith does not praise sin. It praises the movement from recognition to return. The *nafs al-lawwāmah* is valuable precisely because it keeps that movement possible. A person should fear the condition in which he no longer feels any sorrow over injustice, dishonesty, or neglect of prayer. The absence of remorse is not always strength; it can be the sign that the moral nerve has become numb.

**The Soul That Blames Can Become the Soul That Rests**

The verse also prevents a shallow division between “spiritual” and “ordinary” life. The blaming soul asks about intention, speech, consumption, relationships, and the use of time. It asks whether professional ambition has become arrogance, whether religious practice has become display, whether anger is being called principle, and whether a repeated excuse has become a chosen identity.

This soul is not the enemy of happiness. It is what protects happiness from being built on self-deception. A person cannot be at peace by declaring himself innocent when his heart knows otherwise. True tranquility is not the absence of moral awareness; it is the result of reconciling one's life with the truth. The second oath therefore points toward hope: the fact that the soul blames means that the way back has not been closed.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:3]

> **Does man suppose that We shall not gather his bones?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Objection About Scattered Bones**

The verse asks whether the human being imagines that God will not gather his bones. The question is presented as a rebuke because the objection was not new. The Makkan deniers heard the Prophet ﷺ recite about resurrection and asked how bones, after death and decay, could become a living person again. Some reports connect this passage to men who brought decayed bones and challenged the Prophet ﷺ, asking who could bring them back to life. The historical reports differ in their details, but the central question is clear: does bodily decay make resurrection impossible?

The verb *najmaʿ*, “We gather,” is important. Resurrection is not the creation of an unrelated being. It is the gathering and reconstitution of the same human person who lived, chose, and acted. The body is not treated as a disposable shell whose moral history belongs to someone else. Islam's doctrine of resurrection is embodied: the person who did good or evil will stand for the account.

**The Bone as a Sign of Dependence**

A bone seems to support the denier's objection because it is hard, visible, and apparently permanent. Yet every bone already witnesses dependence. It was not always present. It was formed within the womb from stages that no human being controlled. It grows, repairs itself, ages, and finally returns to dust. The person who regards a bone as proof against resurrection overlooks the fact that the bone itself is a product of prior creation.

The Qur'an records a similar question elsewhere:

> **“And he sets forth for Us an example and forgets his own creation. He says, ‘Who will give life to bones while they are disintegrated?’ Say: ‘He will give them life Who produced them the first time, and He is Knowing of all creation.’”** (Qur'an 36:78–79)

The answer does not depend on finding a hidden reserve of life inside the bone. It depends on recognizing the Creator's knowledge and power. If the One who produced the first form knows every particle, then decay does not erase the identity of the person from divine knowledge.

**The Argument from Identity**

The verse also answers a modern misunderstanding. Some people think that resurrection would be impossible because the physical material of a body changes during life, and after death it may become part of soil, plants, or other bodies. But personal identity is not based on our ability to track every atom. Human beings recognize one another through an organized bodily and personal continuity, not by weighing every particle. The Creator's knowledge is more complete than human observation. Gathering the bones means restoring the person in a form appropriate to the final account, not preserving a museum-like arrangement of the exact dust in the grave.

The Qur'an's argument is therefore rationally simple: the first creation establishes the possibility, divine knowledge establishes the preservation of identity, and divine justice establishes the purpose. A person cannot reject resurrection merely because he cannot imagine the mechanism. Human ignorance of a process is not evidence that the process is impossible.

**Why the Qur'an Says “Man”**

The word translated “man” is *al-insān*, the human being. Its use keeps the verse universal. The problem is not restricted to a particular philosopher or tribe. Every human being is tempted to treat death as the end because death interrupts ordinary experience. The verse asks the reader to examine the hidden assumption beneath that conclusion: “I have not seen a dead person return, therefore no one can be raised.” That reasoning confuses lack of observation with impossibility.

The human being is also forgetful. He sees the annual revival of the earth, the renewal of sleep and waking, the development of a child, and the restoration of a wounded body, yet regards resurrection as unimaginable because it lies beyond his control. The Qur'an repeatedly redirects attention from human power to divine power. The question is not, “Can humans reconstruct these bones?” It is, “Can the Creator of humans gather them?”

**A Warning Against Reducing the Person to Matter**

The verse has an ethical consequence. If the human being is nothing more than temporary matter, moral claims can eventually be reduced to preference or social convention. If he will be raised bodily and judged, then his body is part of an entrusted life. The hand that struck, stole, or served; the tongue that lied or comforted; the feet that went toward worship or wrongdoing—all belong to a person who will answer for how they were used.

This does not teach contempt for the physical world. It teaches responsibility within it. The body is neither a god to obey without limit nor a worthless shell. It is a trust, and resurrection means that the trust will be returned to its Owner.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:4]

> **Nay! But We are able to fashion even his fingers and toes.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Is More Precise Than the Objection**

The emphatic *balā*, “Nay indeed,” rejects the assumption of the previous verse. God is not merely able to gather a rough mass resembling a human being. He is able to restore the most delicate identifying details, including the fingers and toes. The Arabic *banān* refers to the extremities of the fingers and toes, and it can suggest the fine structures by which one person is distinguished from another.

The force of the verse lies in precision. The denier imagines that bones become indistinguishable once scattered. The Qur'an responds that God can recreate the very details that human beings themselves use to establish identity. The challenge is not met by a vague statement of power but by directing attention to a small part of the body.

**The Significance of the Fingertips**

The fingertips are small, but they are not insignificant. They contain complex patterns, sensitive nerves, and fine motor ability. Human beings use them to write, build, feed themselves, greet others, and perform acts of worship. A fingerprint can distinguish one person from millions, while a handprint can reveal a person's work and history. In the ancient setting, the mention of fingers and toes made the point that God knows the minute details of the human form. In the modern setting, it also reminds the reader that bodily individuality is more intricate than ordinary sight perceives.

The verse should not be reduced to a prediction about one scientific technology. Its primary meaning is theological: God can recreate the individual completely. Yet the detail is compatible with the modern recognition that human bodies bear distinctive patterns. The deeper lesson remains even if human science had never discovered fingerprints: the One who created individual form can restore it.

**Creation Is Not Blind Reconstruction**

The word *qādirūn*—“able”—comes from the root *q-d-r*, associated with power, ability, measure, and determining proportion. God's power is not random force. It creates according to knowledge and measure. The Qur'an says:

> **“He created everything and determined it with precise determination.”** (Qur'an 25:2)

The resurrection therefore includes both power and knowledge. Power makes re-creation possible; knowledge ensures that the person raised is the person who lived. This also explains why the Qur'an speaks of records, witnesses, and bodily testimony. The final judgment is not an approximate moral impression. It is based upon complete knowledge of the individual life.

**The Hand as an Instrument of Moral Choice**

The fingers also invite moral reflection. The hand is used to write contracts, give charity, feed a child, rescue the injured, strike an innocent person, take unlawful wealth, or touch a sacred text. It is an instrument, but the hand itself does not decide how it will be used. The human will directs it. The Qur'an later describes a Day when bodily faculties will testify:

> **“That Day We shall seal their mouths, and their hands will speak to Us, and their legs will bear witness to what they used to earn.”** (Qur'an 36:65)

The hand mentioned in 75:4 is therefore more than a biological detail. It is a reminder that the very instruments of action will not be forgotten. The person who says, “No one saw,” has considered only human eyes. The Creator saw, recorded, and can restore the agent together with the evidence of his actions.

**A Correct Understanding of Divine Ability**

Islamic belief does not describe God as a powerful being who works within a larger system of limitations. God is the Creator of the system. His power is joined with wisdom, justice, and knowledge. Resurrection does not violate a law outside Him; it is an event that occurs by His command. The Qur'an says:

> **“His command, when He desires a thing, is only to say to it, ‘Be,’ and it is.”** (Qur'an 36:82)

This does not mean that every human wish is automatically realized. The verse refers to God's creative command, not to the wishes of creatures. It teaches that no material obstacle can prevent what God wills. The fingers and toes that appear too insignificant to matter are within His knowledge; the bones that appear too scattered to gather are within His power.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:5]

> **But man desires to defile what lies before him,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Real Problem Is Moral, Not Intellectual**

After answering the objection about bones, the sūrah reveals the motive beneath it: *bal yurīdu al-insān liyafjura amāmah*. The human being desires to continue in transgression before him. The verb *yurīd* means “he desires” or “he intends.” *Yafjur* comes from *f-j-r*, a root connected with bursting through a boundary, as water bursts through a barrier. In moral usage it means to break through the limits set by God and persist in sin.

The verse therefore diagnoses denial of the Hereafter as more than a failure to solve a scientific puzzle. Some people deny accountability because accountability would restrict their desires. If there were no return, no record, and no judgment, the path would appear open for every appetite. The question “Can God raise bones?” may hide the more practical wish: “Let me act as I please without having to answer.”

**“Before Him” and the Geography of the Future**

The phrase *amāmah* means “before him” or “ahead of him.” It can refer to the future that lies ahead, which the denier wants to pollute with continuing sin. It can also mean that the person pushes his wrongdoing forward, repeatedly postponing repentance: “I will correct myself later.” Both meanings illuminate one another. A person who keeps delaying repentance is not standing still. He is sending a stream of choices into the future, and those choices shape what he will meet.

The Qur'an warns against this habit:

> **“And when death comes to one of them, he says, ‘My Lord, send me back that I may do righteousness in what I left behind.’ No! It is only a word he says.”** (Qur'an 23:99–100)

The problem is not that the person lacked time in an absolute sense. The problem is that he kept treating time as if it belonged to him without limit. The denial of resurrection makes postponement feel safe; belief in resurrection makes every postponement morally serious.

**Fujūr as Breaking Through a Protective Boundary**

The root of *fujūr* gives a useful image. A boundary can protect a river, a city, or a household. When it is broken, water or harm enters places it should not enter. Divine limits have a similar protective purpose. They guard faith, family, property, dignity, life, and the heart. Sin often presents itself as freedom because it appears to remove a boundary. But crossing a protective boundary does not eliminate danger; it exposes the person to danger.

This does not mean that every rule is immediately easy to understand in all its details. A believer may not perceive the full wisdom of a command. But the general logic is visible: human beings are limited in knowledge, desire is short-sighted, and social life collapses when every appetite is treated as a law. A driver who sees a warning barrier at the edge of a mountain road may not know who installed it or how deep the ravine is. Wisdom is to recognize that the barrier is there for protection.

**The Soul's Strategy of Rewriting the Future**

Psychologically, the verse describes a common strategy. When an action threatens a person's self-image, he changes the theory rather than changing the action. A person who wants to cheat may decide that there is no final accountability. A person who wants to exploit may call morality “relative.” A person who wants to indulge may describe restraint as repression. The conclusion is shaped by desire, and then presented as neutral reasoning.

The verse does not ask the reader to accuse every doubter of bad motives. Human beings can ask sincere questions, and the Qur'an itself invites reflection. It asks the reader to be honest about the direction of his own reasoning. Is a question leading him toward truth, or is it being used as a shield against an obligation already recognized? The *nafs al-lawwāmah* knows the difference. It is the voice that says, “You are not merely confused; you are trying to protect an appetite.”

**Repentance Reverses the Direction**

The verse is severe, but its severity points to the remedy. A person can stop bursting through the boundary. God says:

> **“Except those who repent, believe, and do righteous deeds; for them God will change their evil deeds into good.”** (Qur'an 25:70)

Repentance is not simply an emotion. It includes stopping the wrongdoing, regretting it, asking God's forgiveness, and restoring the rights of people harmed. If the sin involved another person's property, reputation, or safety, the repentance must address that person's right as far as possible. The future that the sinner wanted to defile can be redirected toward obedience.

The practical application is to identify the phrase “later” when it becomes a hiding place. There is a difference between making a wise plan to change and using planning as a substitute for change. The blaming soul should be listened to while action is still possible. The verse asks: what is the next choice being sent forward, and what kind of future is that choice building?

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:6]

> **asking, “When is the Day of Resurrection?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Can Be Mockery**

The question *ayyāna yawmu al-qiyāmah* means, “When is the Day of Resurrection?” In itself, asking about the time of an event can be a genuine request for information. Here the surrounding verses show that it is asked in a spirit of denial and challenge. The person who wants to continue in transgression turns the final Day into a subject of mockery: if it cannot be pointed to on today's calendar, he behaves as though it does not exist.

The Qur'an records the same question from different angles:

> **“They ask you about the Hour: when is its appointed time? Say: Its knowledge is only with my Lord.”** (Qur'an 7:187)

And:

> **“They say, ‘When is this promise, if you are truthful?’”** (Qur'an 10:48)

The repeated question is not evidence that the revelation failed to answer. It reveals that the questioner has confused two things: knowing *when* the event will occur and knowing *that* it will occur. The Qur'an gives certainty about the second and withholds the first.

**Why the Exact Time Is Hidden**

If the exact time of death and resurrection were disclosed, human beings might treat obedience as a short-term emergency rather than a stable moral way of life. Many would postpone repentance until the final visible moment, not because they loved God, but because they calculated the deadline. The concealment of the Hour keeps the test genuine. A person must act on trust, evidence, and moral responsibility rather than on a publicly announced countdown.

The hidden time also protects society from permanent panic. The world is to be cultivated, families are to be raised, justice is to be pursued, and knowledge is to be sought. Belief in the Hour should produce seriousness, not a refusal to fulfill worldly trusts. The Prophet ﷺ taught both realities: prepare for the Hereafter, and act responsibly in the present.

**The Question “When?” Can Conceal “What?”**

People often ask about timing because it allows them to avoid asking about readiness. “When will judgment come?” can distract from “What will my record contain?” The sūrah shifts the reader from the calendar to the conscience. The exact date is not the information that determines salvation. The decisive matter is whether a person is truthful, repentant, just, and prepared to meet God.

A patient awaiting a medical appointment may repeatedly ask for the date while ignoring the treatment that the appointment requires. The date matters, but the treatment matters more. In the same way, the Hour's time matters as part of God's knowledge, but its moral purpose is to awaken conduct now.

**The Prophet's Task Was Warning, Not Scheduling**

The Prophet ﷺ was not commanded to calculate or announce the exact time. He was commanded to warn and to convey. The Qur'an says:

> **“They ask you about the Hour, when it will be established. Say: Its knowledge is only with God. And what do you know? Perhaps the Hour is near.”** (Qur'an 33:63)

“Perhaps” in such a divine address does not express uncertainty in God's knowledge. It brings the possibility close to the hearer's experience: you may not have much time. This is how the warning functions. It does not give a date to be memorized; it gives a truth to be lived.

**Rejecting Date-Setters and Sensational Claims**

This verse also protects believers from those who repeatedly announce dates for the end of the world. Such claims have appeared in many cultures and continue in modern forms. They turn revelation into a tool for publicity, fear, or control. The Qur'an's discipline is simpler: affirm the Hour, deny human claims to its exact timing, and prepare through obedience.

The more useful question for a Muslim is not “Which prediction is correct?” but “What have I done with the time God has already given me?” The verse exposes the evasive question and redirects the heart to the certain encounter.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:7]

> **Then when the eyes are dazzled,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Eye That Can No Longer Control What It Sees**

The verse begins the description of the Hour with the eyes: *fa-idhā bariqa al-baṣar*. The verb *bariqa* can indicate being dazzled, startled, or losing the ability to see steadily. The eye that normally gathers information and helps a person navigate will be overwhelmed by a reality beyond ordinary experience. The verse is not merely describing bright light. It describes terror and astonishment so intense that the eye's usual confidence collapses.

The Qur'an often connects the eye with a person's attempt to manage reality. People look, assess, choose a direction, and seek an exit. On that Day, seeing will not give the denier control. The eye will witness what the heart had rejected, but the opportunity to deny or escape will be gone.

**From Ordinary Signs to the Final Disclosure**

In this life, creation contains signs that point to God, but the signs are presented within a world of ordinary causes. A person may look at the sky and choose to interpret it as mere physical fact, or look at death and treat it as only biological termination. The Day of Resurrection removes the veil of ordinary familiarity. The unseen consequences become visible, and the person who spent life avoiding evidence sees reality without the protective distance he once maintained.

The Qur'an describes that condition elsewhere:

> **“Their eyes humbled, they will emerge from the graves as though they were locusts spreading.”** (Qur'an 54:7)

And:

> **“Their eyes will be staring, their hearts empty.”** (Qur'an 14:43)

The first verse emphasizes the eye's submission; the second combines outward staring with inward collapse. The Day does not make the truth less clear. It makes it impossible to ignore.

**The Difference Between Awe and Panic**

The same overwhelming reality does not affect every person in the same way. For one person, a sudden great scene produces wonder because he already acknowledges the One who created it. For another, it produces panic because his entire life was organized around avoiding that acknowledgment. The problem is not simply the magnitude of the event; it is the relationship of the person to the truth revealed by it.

This distinction can be observed in smaller forms. A person who has kept honest accounts can approach an audit with seriousness but without terror. A person who has falsified them may react to the same audit as a catastrophe. The audit did not create the hidden wrongdoing; it disclosed it.

**The Eye as a Moral Witness**

Human sight also carries responsibility in this life. The eye can look at suffering and turn away, look at temptation and deliberately pursue it, or look at God's signs and reflect. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“The two eyes commit fornication, and their fornication is the looking; the heart desires and hopes, and the private parts either confirm that or deny it.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 6243; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2657, with related wording)

This hadith uses moral language to show that the eye is not neutral in every act of looking. Attention can be a choice. The eyes described in 75:7 have reached the final disclosure of everything to which they were directed. The person who trained his sight upon truth will meet a terrifying Day with a different inner condition from the one who trained it upon concealment and appetite.

**Training the Gaze Before the Day**

The verse calls for practical discipline: look deliberately. Notice the needs of people rather than reducing them to scenery. Notice the signs of God rather than consuming the world without reflection. Guard the gaze from what degrades the heart. When the eye encounters a wrong, do not assume that seeing creates no duty; when it encounters a blessing, do not treat it as an entitlement.

A person cannot prevent all disturbing images or sudden thoughts, but he can decide whether to dwell on them, pursue them, or turn away. The final Day will be seen by everyone. Wisdom is to cultivate a way of seeing now that is capable of recognizing truth before recognition becomes forced.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:8]

> **And the moon is eclipsed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Familiar Moon Loses Its Light**

The verb *khasafa* can mean that something is eclipsed, darkened, or made to sink. The moon that people know as a source of reflected light will no longer function within the familiar order of night and month. Its disappearance from its ordinary role is part of the cosmic upheaval that announces the end of the present arrangement.

The verse is brief because its meaning is cumulative. The listener has already been told about the dazzling of the eyes. Now the moon itself is darkened. The world that once appeared stable is no longer a reliable environment. The signs by which people measured travel, time, seasons, and religious dates will be transformed.

**Created Light Is Not Independent Light**

The moon has no light of its own; it reflects light and follows an orbit assigned to it. Its eclipse therefore has a theological lesson beyond the physical event. Created sources of light are dependent. They can guide only within the limits God gives them. Human beings often treat created powers—wealth, political authority, technology, reputation, or expertise—as though they were permanent sources of security. The darkened moon reminds them that every created support is contingent.

The Qur'an says:

> **“And We made the night and the day two signs; then We erased the sign of the night and made the sign of the day illuminating, that you may seek bounty from your Lord and know the number of years and the reckoning.”** (Qur'an 17:12)

The moon and sun serve human life by God's permission. Their signs are not divine beings, and they are not self-sustaining. When the Creator changes the order, the created signs cannot preserve themselves.

**The End of Measured Time**

The moon is connected to the human experience of time. Its phases organize calendars and remind people that life moves through repeated increase and decrease. An eclipse interrupts the normal pattern and, in the context of the Resurrection, points toward the end of ordinary time. The believer learns from the moon before the final Day: everything that rises also declines, and every cycle belongs to God.

The modern world can make time seem like a resource that belongs entirely to human management. Digital calendars, clocks, schedules, and data create an impression of control. Yet the body ages, the seasons change, and death can arrive without appointment. The moon's eclipse becomes an image of the limits of human administration. We can arrange tasks; we cannot own time.

**A Sign Against Cosmic Self-Sufficiency**

Some ancient people worshipped the moon because they saw its regularity and beauty. Modern people may reject literal moon-worship while still treating the cosmos as self-explanatory and morally independent. The verse does not ask people to fear the moon as a deity. It asks them to understand that the celestial order is created and can be changed by its Creator.

The result is neither scientific hostility nor superstition. A Muslim can study eclipses, calculate their occurrence, and recognize their physical causes while also affirming that causes themselves operate by God's permission. Knowledge of the mechanism does not remove the sign. It can deepen it. The moon's dependence is a fact whether or not an observer understands the mathematics of its orbit.

**A Reminder to the Heart**

When a person sees a lunar eclipse in this life, he is reminded of a future cosmic transformation, but he is not meant to treat every eclipse as the Resurrection itself. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the sun and moon are signs of God and that an eclipse is not caused by the death or birth of a human being. The authentic report states:

> **“Indeed, the sun and the moon are two signs from the signs of God. They do not eclipse because of the death or life of anyone. So when you see that, pray and supplicate until it is uncovered.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 1042; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 901)

That teaching joins accurate belief to worship. The event has a physical occurrence, but it also has a spiritual meaning: created light can be darkened, and the heart should turn to the Creator rather than to fear, myth, or arrogance.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:9]

> **And the sun and the moon are brought together—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Great Lights in One Scene**

The previous verse mentioned the moon's eclipse; this verse adds that the sun and moon will be brought together. The expression *jumiʿa al-shams wa al-qamar* can be understood as the joining or gathering of the two, whether through the collapse of their normal order, the loss of their distinction as signs of day and night, or their being brought together in the upheaval of the Last Day. The important point is that the cosmic arrangement upon which human beings depend will no longer continue in its familiar form.

The sun and moon are among the most obvious signs of order. The sun marks day, warmth, and visibility. The moon marks night, months, and changing phases. Their distinct functions make ordinary life possible. To say that they are brought together is to tell the listener that the established system will be folded into a new stage.

**Creation Is Ordered Because God Orders It**

The Qur'an frequently points to the sun and moon as signs of divine governance:

> **“The sun and the moon move according to a precise calculation.”** (Qur'an 55:5)

And:

> **“It is He Who made the sun a radiant light and the moon a reflected light, and determined for it phases so that you may know the number of years and calculation.”** (Qur'an 10:5)

The word “calculation” does not make the system independent of God. It shows that creation is intelligible because it is ordered. Scientific investigation can describe regularity, but it does not turn regularity into a self-existent deity. The verse of al-Qiyāmah then shows the other side of the same truth: the One who gives order can bring this order to an end and establish another.

**The Collapse of False Cosmic Security**

Human beings often look upward for permanence. Civilizations name their empires after the sun, build observatories, and imagine that the regular heavens guarantee a regular human future. But the sun itself will not remain a source of worldly stability forever. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the final transformation:

> **“When the sun is wrapped up, and when the stars fall, losing their light...”** (Qur'an 81:1–2)

These descriptions are not invitations to speculate about a timetable or to force every detail into a modern physical model. They communicate a theological certainty: the world is not eternal in its present form. The universe has a beginning, a measured continuation, and an end known to God.

**The Inner Parallel: When the Heart's Lights Meet**

There is also an inward lesson. People often live between two competing orientations: the “sun” of public image and the “moon” of private conscience, or the light of revealed guidance and the attraction of worldly desire. On the Day of Resurrection, these divisions will no longer be manageable. What a person displayed and what he concealed will be brought together. The Qur'an says:

> **“On the Day when the secrets are tested and exposed.”** (Qur'an 86:9)

The cosmic joining of sun and moon can therefore prompt self-examination. Is the public self built upon the private self, or do they travel in separate orbits? Is religious language joined to honest conduct, or is one merely reflecting the light of other people? The final Day brings the elements together and removes the ability to maintain a divided identity.

**The Sunnah of Responding to Cosmic Signs**

The Prophet ﷺ did not respond to eclipses with panic, superstition, or political exploitation. He prayed, supplicated, gave charity, and reminded people of God and the Hereafter. This is a model for all natural signs. A believer may study the material cause and still allow the event to awaken humility. The proper response to the verse is not to abandon reason, but to prevent reason from becoming arrogance.

The sun and moon are great in human experience, yet they are servants. When their appointed order changes, the human being learns his own scale. The One who is worthy of ultimate reliance is not a created light but the Lord of every light.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:10]

> **That Day man will say, “Where is the escape?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Instinct to Flee**

The question *ayna al-mafarr*—“Where is the escape?”—captures the first reaction of a person confronted by the cosmic collapse and the certainty of judgment. *Mafarr* comes from *f-r-r*, to flee or run away. It is not a calm request for directions. It is the desperate search of someone who realizes that all familiar strategies of avoidance have failed.

In the world, human beings respond to danger by looking for shelter, distance, allies, money, legal protection, or physical strength. These means can be legitimate in their proper setting. Islam does not condemn seeking safety from ordinary harm. But on the Day of Resurrection the question is directed toward a danger from which no worldly strategy can separate the person. The problem is not a temporary threat that can be outrun; it is the disclosure of the person's relationship with God.

**A Question That Reveals Past Neglect**

The person asks for escape only when the time of preparation has ended. The question therefore exposes an earlier failure: he treated the warnings as remote, while the believing person treated them as guidance for present action. The Qur'an says:

> **“The Day when the wrongdoer will bite his hands, saying, ‘Would that I had taken a way along with the Messenger!’”** (Qur'an 25:27)

Regret does not arise because the path was hidden. It arises because the person saw signs, heard warnings, or encountered reminders but preferred another direction. The question “Where is the escape?” is the final form of the earlier habit of evasion.

**False Escapes in This Life**

The verse also speaks to the many escapes people seek before death. Some flee from conscience through entertainment, some from responsibility through blame, some from grief through addiction, and some from truth through constant distraction. These escapes may temporarily reduce discomfort, but they do not solve the underlying problem. A person can leave a room, change a subject, delete a message, or move to another city, but he cannot leave himself or God's knowledge.

A useful analogy is a warning alarm in a building. Covering the alarm does not remove the fire. It only delays recognition and may increase the damage. Likewise, silencing conscience does not remove accountability. It makes repentance less likely while the person still has time.

**The Value of Seeking Refuge Before It Is Too Late**

The question is not telling the believer never to seek refuge. It is teaching where refuge must ultimately be sought. In this life, the proper response is to seek refuge in God through repentance, prayer, and obedience. The Qur'an says:

> **“So flee unto God.”** (Qur'an 51:50)

The same root of fleeing appears in both ideas, but the directions are opposite. The heedless flee *from* the truth and discover that there is nowhere to go. The believer flees *to* God and finds that the One feared in judgment is also the One who accepts repentance and gives protection.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that God is more merciful to His servants than a mother is to her child. In the authentic report, he said:

> **“God is more merciful to His servants than this woman is to her child.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 5999; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2754)

This mercy does not cancel judgment; it opens the way to prepare for it. The one who turns to God before the Hour does not need to ask for an escape from God on the Hour.

**The Moral of the Question**

Every day contains small moments in which a person can either face a truth or look for an escape. If an apology is due, escape delays healing. If a debt is owed, escape increases injustice. If a prayer has been neglected, distraction does not remove the obligation. If a habit is damaging the heart, changing the language around it does not cure it.

The verse asks the reader to identify these smaller escapes before they become a settled character. The wise person does not wait until the final Day to ask where the exit is. He turns now toward the only refuge that remains when every other refuge disappears.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:11]

> **Nay! But there shall be no refuge.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Short Sentence That Closes Every Door**

The emphatic *kallā* rejects the hope of escape: no, there is no refuge. The Arabic *wazar* means a place of protection or a shelter behind which someone hides. It can also refer to a mountain refuge. The image is of a person looking for a stronghold, a cave, a wall, or a powerful protector, only to find that none exists.

The brevity of the verse gives it force. A long argument might allow the frightened person to imagine an exception. This sentence removes the imagined exception entirely. There is no physical hiding place, no legal manipulation, no wealth to offer, and no social rank that can create safety against the divine judgment.

**Why the Qur'an Rejects Every Human Shield**

The Qur'an is not denying that people can benefit one another in worldly life. Parents protect children, courts defend the wronged, and communities support the weak. On the Day of Judgment, however, no created being can independently cancel God's judgment. The Qur'an says:

> **“And fear a Day when no soul will suffice for another soul at all, nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor will compensation be taken from it, nor will they be helped.”** (Qur'an 2:48)

Other verses establish that intercession exists only by God's permission. Thus the doctrine is balanced: there is no autonomous human power against God, but there may be mercy and intercession granted by God according to His wisdom. The absence of refuge is not an absence of divine mercy. It is the absence of any mercy that a creature can force or purchase.

**The End of Privilege as a Substitute for Truth**

Worldly society often treats connections as protection. A person may believe that family name, professional status, political influence, or public admiration will soften every consequence. These things can sometimes shield a person from human accountability, but they cannot alter the divine record. The Qur'an states:

> **“The Day when neither wealth nor children will benefit, except one who comes to God with a sound heart.”** (Qur'an 26:88–89)

The verse does not condemn wealth or children. It denies that they can substitute for a sound heart. A wealthy person may use wealth in righteousness; a parent may raise children in faith; but neither possession becomes a wall against God.

**The Refuge of Repentance Is Still Open Now**

The warning must not be misunderstood as saying that God refuses all refuge. The point is that the final Day is not the time to establish a refuge through last-minute evasion. Before death, the door is open. God says:

> **“And whoever is mindful of God, He will make for him a way out and provide for him from where he does not expect.”** (Qur'an 65:2–3)

The “way out” in this world may be relief from a difficulty, and it certainly includes a way out of moral confusion through obedience. The difference between the two situations is time. The repentant person seeks refuge while action can still change the record. The denier on the Last Day seeks an escape after the record has been sealed.

**Building the Right Refuge**

The verse invites an honest inventory. What does a person currently rely upon more than God? Is it money, approval, physical strength, a title, an ideology, or the ability to hide? None of these is inherently forbidden, but each becomes a false refuge when it is trusted as though it were ultimate. Taqwā means using means without worshipping them: tie the camel, pursue justice, seek treatment, ask trusted people for help, and know that all means remain under God's power.

The wise person builds refuge through faith, repentance, and sound deeds before the final Day. When every worldly shelter disappears, only the relationship with God remains. The verse urges the reader to make that relationship real now.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:12]

> **Unto your Lord that Day is the dwelling place.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Destination Belongs to God**

The verse says *ilā rabbika yawmaʾidhin al-mustaqarr*—“unto your Lord that Day is the dwelling place” or “the final settling place.” *Mustaqarr* comes from *q-r-r*, to become settled, still, or established. The word can refer to the place where a person finally remains, and the verse can be understood as saying that the final destination and judgment belong to God. Every route ends with Him.

The word *Rabb* is especially fitting. It means Lord, Master, Owner, and the One who nurtures and brings creation to completion. The One who sustained the person through worldly life is the One before whom the person finally settles. The verse does not say that the final place is determined by chance, fate detached from wisdom, or the power of competing beings. It belongs to the Lord.

**The Two Destinations Under One Lordship**

The Qur'an speaks of Paradise and Hell as different final abodes, but both are under God's judgment. The believer does not worship Paradise; he seeks God's pleasure, and Paradise is God's gift. He does not regard Hell as an independent force; it is a created consequence and place of punishment under divine justice. This protects Islamic belief from imagining a universe divided between two equal powers.

The Qur'an says:

> **“The Day they will be gathered unto their Lord—those are the ones whose final abode is the Garden.”** (Qur'an 6:51, in meaning)

And it says of the other group:

> **“Their abode is Hell; whenever it subsides, We shall increase for them the blaze.”** (Qur'an 17:97)

The exact destination differs, but the authority is one. God is *Rabb* of both judgment and mercy, and neither mercy is arbitrary nor punishment unjust.

**The End of Every False Journey**

Every person spends life moving toward something. One person moves toward reputation, another toward wealth, another toward knowledge, family, pleasure, or service. These aims can be good or bad depending on how they are ordered. The verse asks whether the chosen direction finally leads to God or merely ends in exhaustion. The human being may not use religious language, but he is always seeking a final resting place—some condition in which he believes he will finally be safe, recognized, or complete.

The world cannot provide that permanent settlement. A promotion is followed by another ambition, a possession by fear of loss, and public praise by the need for renewed praise. The heart remains restless because it is trying to make a temporary object carry an eternal weight. The Qur'an says:

> **“Surely in the remembrance of God do hearts find rest.”** (Qur'an 13:28)

This does not mean that remembrance removes every hardship. It means that the heart finds its proper orientation and no longer asks created things to be its final home.

**The Meaning of Returning to the Lord**

The expression *unto your Lord* contains both accountability and care. A servant returns to a master; a child returns to a nurturing parent; a traveler returns to the one who owns the destination. For the believer, return to God includes hope because God has introduced Himself as Merciful, Forgiving, and Just. For the denier, it is a confrontation with the Lord he ignored.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that a person should love meeting God when God loves meeting him, while the person who dislikes meeting God is treated accordingly. The authentic wording states:

> **“Whoever loves to meet God, God loves to meet him; and whoever dislikes to meet God, God dislikes to meet him.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 6507; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2683)

This does not praise a desire for death caused by depression or a wish to escape responsibility. It describes the believer's attitude when the appointed meeting arrives. The person who has spent life turning toward God can hope that the final settling place will be a meeting of mercy.

**Preparing a Place in the Final Settlement**

The verse is a call to ask what is being built. A house in this world requires foundations; the final abode is prepared through faith and deeds. A small deed done sincerely may become weighty because God knows its hidden cost. A large public deed may be empty if it was performed for attention. The practical preparation is therefore not merely to accumulate visible acts but to cultivate a sound relationship with God.

The final settling place is not reached by claiming ownership over life. It is reached by living as a servant who knows where he will return.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:13]

> **Man shall be informed that Day of that which he has sent forth and of that which he has left behind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Record of What Was Done and What Was Left**

The verse says that the human being will be informed of what he sent forward and what he left behind: *qaddama wa akhkhara*. *Qaddama* means to put forward, advance, or send ahead. *Akhkhara* means to leave behind, delay, or place later. Together they create a broad account of a person's relationship with time. Every deed is either being sent ahead as a provision for the Hereafter or being left behind as a neglected opportunity, an ongoing influence, or an unpaid responsibility.

The wording does not suggest that only obvious actions are recorded. What a person taught, encouraged, prevented, initiated, abandoned, and allowed to continue can all have consequences beyond the moment. The Qur'an states:

> **“Indeed, We give life to the dead, and We record what they sent ahead and what they left behind; and We have enumerated everything in a clear register.”** (Qur'an 36:12)

The verse in Sūrah Yāsīn connects the record to the traces people leave. Their deeds end with death in one sense, but their effects may continue.

**The Deeds Sent Ahead**

A deed is “sent ahead” when it is done for God and becomes part of the person's true provision. Prayer, truthful speech, charity, patience, reconciliation, and useful knowledge are not lost when the moment passes. They have been transferred from the temporary world into the permanent record.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“When a human being dies, his deeds end except for three: ongoing charity, knowledge from which benefit is gained, or a righteous child who supplicates for him.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 1631)

This hadith explains one aspect of what is sent ahead and what is left behind. The person may leave a school, a well, a charitable endowment, a beneficial book, or a child formed by sound upbringing. The original act happened in the past, but its reward continues by God's generosity.

**The Things Left Behind**

“What he left behind” can include good effects that continue after death, but it can also include neglected duties and opportunities not used. The early commentators offered several explanations: deeds one performed and deeds one failed to perform; what a person sent ahead and what he left for others to continue; or the consequences of his actions after death. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive.

A person may leave behind unpaid debts, broken relationships, children without moral guidance, harmful habits passed to others, or public ideas that injure people. He may also leave behind reconciliation, teaching, and a model of patience. The verse teaches responsibility for both kinds of legacy. A life is not measured only by what it consumed while present but also by what it releases into the lives of others.

**The Moral Power of Seeing Legacy Clearly**

Modern life often encourages immediate measurement: views, sales, salary, grades, and public reaction. The verse introduces a longer accounting. A small conversation may shape a child's faith years later. A private act of betrayal may damage a family long after the original event. A policy made for short-term gain may burden generations. Conversely, a quiet teacher may continue to influence people whom he never meets.

This does not mean a person controls every later consequence. God judges intention and reasonable responsibility, not outcomes entirely beyond one's power. But the verse prevents the claim that consequences belong only to the moment. A responsible person asks: What am I sending ahead? What am I leaving behind? Which habits will continue speaking after I am silent?

**The Account Is Informative Before It Is Merely Punitive**

To be informed means to have the record brought to awareness. The Day is not based upon rumor or hidden accusation. The person sees what he did, what he failed to do, and what his actions set in motion. The Qur'an says:

> **“And the record will be placed, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it, saying, ‘Woe to us! What is this record that leaves nothing small or great without enumerating it?’ They will find all that they did present.”** (Qur'an 18:49)

The comprehensiveness of the record is frightening for the wrongdoer and reassuring for the oppressed. Nothing good is too small to be known, and no wrong is too hidden to matter. The best response is not obsessive fear but deliberate living. Repent from what has been sent wrongly, repair what can be repaired, and begin sending forward what will be welcomed when it appears.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:14]

> **Indeed, man shall be a testimony against himself,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Self as Its Own Evidence**

The verse says *bal al-insānu ʿalā nafsihi baṣīrah*: the human being is a witness or clear evidence against himself. *Baṣīrah* is related to inner perception, insight, and clear evidence. It can be understood as the person himself being a witness to his own reality, or as the faculties within him providing testimony. The verse shifts the center of accountability from external accusation to inward knowledge.

A person may persuade an audience, but he cannot fully escape the knowledge of what he intended and did. Human beings can misunderstand, forget, or be deceived, so this verse does not mean that every guilty feeling proves actual guilt. It means that God knows the truth of the person, and the person's own inner awareness will no longer be able to hide it.

**The Difference Between Evidence and Mere Feeling**

Islam does not teach that every feeling of shame is a divine verdict. People can feel guilty for lawful actions because of trauma, manipulation, or unrealistic expectations. The *baṣīrah* of this verse is not an uncontrolled emotion; it is the truthful inward recognition of what one knowingly chose. It is joined to God's objective revelation and justice.

This distinction matters psychologically. A believer should not obey every accusation arising in the mind, but neither should he dismiss every discomfort as anxiety. He measures the feeling against revelation, facts, and the rights of people. If he has wronged someone, he repairs it. If he has only imagined wrongdoing, he seeks clarity rather than self-punishment. The verse calls for honest insight, not emotional chaos.

**Why a Person May Know and Still Deny**

The human being's problem is often not lack of knowledge but conflict between knowledge and desire. A person knows that envy is harming him but continues to feed it. He knows that a lie will damage trust but tells it to avoid immediate embarrassment. He knows that prayer matters but repeatedly postpones it. The evidence is present within him, but he tries to create a second narrative in which the choice was unavoidable or harmless.

This is why self-justification consumes so much energy. A lie about the world must be maintained by further explanations; a lie about oneself must be defended against the conscience. The person becomes both advocate and witness, but the witness cannot be permanently silenced. The Qur'an says:

> **“Rather, they will be clear to themselves, though they offer excuses.”** (Qur'an 75:14–15, in meaning)

**The Body Will Join the Testimony**

Elsewhere the Qur'an expands the idea of internal testimony to include the physical faculties:

> **“On the Day when their tongues, their hands, and their legs will bear witness against them concerning what they used to do.”** (Qur'an 24:24)

And:

> **“They will say to their skins, ‘Why do you testify against us?’ They will say, ‘God made us speak, Who makes everything speak.’”** (Qur'an 41:21)

These verses do not mean that God needs witnesses to discover what happened. They show the completeness of justice. The person is confronted by the very instruments through which he acted. No one can say, “This was not my life,” or “I was not the one who chose.”

**Cultivating Honest Inner Speech Now**

The practical application is to practice truthful self-description before the final Day. Replace “I had no choice” with “I chose the easiest option.” Replace “I was only advising” with “I wanted to hurt him.” Replace “Everyone does it” with “I know this is wrong, and I need help leaving it.” Such honesty is not humiliation; it is the first condition of repair.

The Prophet ﷺ taught the importance of accountability and sincerity through his description of excellence in worship:

> **“Iḥsān is that you worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, then indeed He sees you.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 50; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 8)

Living with awareness that God sees turns the inner witness from a future prosecutor into a present guide. The person who listens to the truthful conscience in this world will not be surprised by its testimony in the next.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:15]

> **Though he proffers his excuses.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Excuses Cannot Rewrite the Record**

The verse completes the previous one: a person will be a witness against himself even while presenting excuses. *Maʿādhīr*—excuses—can refer to verbal defenses, explanations, or attempts to shift blame. The Qur'an is not denying that circumstances matter. Islamic law distinguishes intention, ignorance, coercion, mistake, capacity, and deliberate action. The verse addresses the person who uses excuses to conceal the truth rather than to explain it.

There is a difference between an excuse that God accepts and an excuse invented by a person to protect his image. A person who genuinely forgot, was coerced, lacked knowledge that he could not reasonably acquire, or made an unintentional error is not judged like one who knowingly chose wrongdoing. But a person who knowingly violates a command and then builds a story to avoid responsibility is not made innocent by the story.

**The Psychology of Self-Defense**

Excuses often begin as a way to reduce shame. “I was tired,” “I was provoked,” “I had no alternative,” or “I only did it because they did it first” may contain part of the truth. The danger is using a partial truth to erase the decisive truth. A person can be tired and still responsible for cruelty. He can be provoked and still responsible for refusing injustice. He can be influenced by others and still responsible for choosing whether to follow.

This is why moral growth requires accurate proportion. A person should not claim more blame than he deserves, but he should not claim less. The Qur'an's justice is more precise than either harsh self-condemnation or total self-exoneration.

**Repentance Is Not an Excuse; It Is an Admission**

Repentance begins when the person stops arguing against reality. The one who says, “I did wrong; forgive me,” is not offering an excuse. He is offering a confession and asking for mercy. The Qur'an says:

> **“And whoever does evil or wrongs himself, then seeks God's forgiveness, will find God Forgiving, Merciful.”** (Qur'an 4:110)

The verse does not require a person to defend the sin before seeking forgiveness. It directs him to acknowledge it and return. This is spiritually healthier than either denial or despair. Excuse-making attempts to save the ego; repentance saves the person from continuing in the wrong.

**When Explanation Is Necessary**

There are situations in which explaining circumstances is necessary. A person accused unjustly may clarify the facts. A judge must hear evidence. A teacher should know why a student failed. A family member may need to explain an absence. The verse does not forbid truthful explanation. It warns that explanation must not become evasion.

A useful test is whether the explanation leads to repair. If it leads to apology, restitution, changed behavior, or a fair assessment, it may be legitimate. If it only protects pride while the harm continues, it resembles the excuses condemned here. The final Day will distinguish the two because God knows both the event and the intention behind the words.

**A Daily Exercise in Accountability**

Before sleep, a person can ask four questions: What did I do well today? Where did I wrong God or another person? What excuse am I using to avoid acknowledging it? What repair can I make tomorrow? This is not a ritual prescribed in a specific form, but it expresses the Qur'anic discipline of self-accounting. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is famously reported to have said:

> **“Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed.”** (Reported by Ibn Abī Shaybah and others as a saying of ʿUmar)

The quotation is a wise counsel, not a prophetic hadith. Its meaning is supported by the sūrah: the person who practices honesty before death is less likely to meet the final record with a lifetime of excuses.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:16]

> **Move not thy tongue therewith to hasten it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet's Concern to Preserve Revelation**

The address now turns directly to the Prophet ﷺ: *lā tuḥarrik bihi lisānaka litaʿjala bih*. The Prophet ﷺ would move his tongue while Gabriel recited the revelation because he was eager to retain every word and feared that any part might escape his memory. This was not impatience with God; it was the intense concern of a trustworthy Messenger carrying a message for an entire community.

The verse teaches him not to rush his tongue along with the recitation. He is being taught that the preservation of the Qur'an does not depend on anxious human effort alone. The Messenger must listen and receive, while God guarantees the gathering of the revelation in his heart.

**A Moment of Divine Training**

The passage is a beautiful example of how the Qur'an records the Prophet's humanity without diminishing his rank. He had extraordinary memory and perfect honesty, but he was still a servant receiving revelation. Divine instruction corrects his immediate practice and reassures him of divine care. The correction itself becomes part of the Qur'an, teaching future generations that eagerness for good must still be governed by the proper method.

A similar instruction appears elsewhere:

> **“Do not hasten with the Qur'an before its revelation to you is completed, and say, ‘My Lord, increase me in knowledge.’”** (Qur'an 20:114)

The command does not discourage learning or memorization. It teaches patience in receiving knowledge. A student who interrupts every sentence in order to predict the conclusion may miss the actual argument. A worshipper who rushes to display understanding may fail to listen.

**The Ethics of Listening**

The verse establishes listening as an act of trust. The Prophet ﷺ is told not to seize the words prematurely, and later listeners are instructed to listen when the Qur'an is recited:

> **“So when the Qur'an is recited, listen to it and be silent, that you may receive mercy.”** (Qur'an 7:204)

Listening is not passivity. It requires attention, humility, and willingness to let the message challenge one's first reaction. In an age of rapid information, people often formulate a response before another person has finished speaking. The verse provides a spiritual correction: do not confuse speed of reaction with depth of understanding.

**A Reassurance for Those Who Fear Forgetting**

The verse also comforts people who are anxious about memorization. Effort is required, but anxiety is not the source of preservation. The student should revise, repeat, and seek help, while knowing that benefit ultimately comes from God. The Prophet ﷺ was given a direct promise:

> **“We shall make you recite, and you will not forget, except what God wills.”** (Qur'an 87:6–7)

This promise belongs uniquely to the divine protection of the Messenger and the revelation, but it contains a general lesson about learning: disciplined effort should be joined to reliance, not panic. A person may do everything carefully and still forget an ordinary piece of information. He should correct the error rather than worship anxiety.

**Revelation Is Received Before It Is Explained**

The immediate order of this passage matters. First, do not rush the tongue. Then God will gather and recite. Then follow the recitation. Then comes explanation. The order models sound interpretation: receive the text accurately, listen to its full wording, and only then seek to understand its meaning and application.

This principle is relevant to modern reading. A short quotation taken from a long passage can be made to say almost anything. A serious reader lets context, language, and the complete argument speak before issuing judgment. The Qur'an asks for reverent attention rather than impulsive extraction.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:17]

> **Surely it is for Us to gather it and to recite it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Guarantees the Gathering of the Qur'an**

The pronoun in *innā ʿalaynā jamʿahu wa qurʾānah* refers to the revelation. God says that its gathering and recitation are upon Him. *Jamʿ* means to collect, bring together, or preserve in an ordered whole. *Qurʾān* is connected to recitation and reading. The verse therefore covers both the preservation of the revealed words in the Prophet's heart and the correct manner of their recitation.

The Qur'an had not yet been assembled as a bound volume in the Prophet's lifetime. Verses were revealed over time, memorized by Companions, and written on available materials. Yet God calls it a gathered recitation because its preservation and ordering are under divine care. The later physical compilation served that promise; it did not create the revelation.

**Preservation Through Memory and Writing**

The Islamic tradition preserved the Qur'an through two mutually reinforcing means: oral transmission and written records. The Prophet ﷺ recited to the Companions, they memorized it, and scribes wrote it. The community recited it in prayer, teaching it publicly rather than leaving it in a private archive. This combination is one reason the Qur'an's text could be transmitted across generations with extraordinary stability.

The verse's “for Us” does not mean that human beings have no role. God commonly fulfills His promises through created means. He preserved the Qur'an through the Messenger's memory, the Companions' memorization, written materials, recitation in worship, and later scholarly verification. Reliance on God does not justify neglect of means; it gives the means their proper purpose.

**The Qur'an as Recited Revelation**

The word “Qur'an” emphasizes that the Book is not merely an artifact to be admired. It is speech to be recited and heard. Its meanings are important, but its sounds and words are also part of its revealed form. This is why Muslims recite it in prayer, memorize it in Arabic, and transmit its modes of recitation through teachers.

The Qur'an describes itself as a recitation:

> **“Indeed, upon Us is its collection and its recitation. So when We have recited it, follow its recitation. Then upon Us is its clarification.”** (Qur'an 75:17–19)

These verses also show that recitation is not separate from meaning. To recite responsibly is to follow the revealed wording and allow its explanation to guide understanding. A beautiful voice without obedience is incomplete, while a person who struggles to recite but reveres and follows the message is not deprived of the Qur'an's guidance.

**Divine Preservation and Human Responsibility**

God's preservation of the Qur'an should not be turned into carelessness. A community can neglect reading, misunderstand a verse, or misuse a quotation even while the text itself remains preserved. Textual preservation does not guarantee that every reader is rightly guided. The Book must be approached with taqwā, knowledge, and sincerity.

The Prophet ﷺ warned against making the Qur'an a source of dispute and performance without transformation. The purpose of preservation is guidance. A person who memorizes a passage but knowingly violates its clear ethical commands has carried words without carrying their trust. The preserved text continually calls the reader to be changed by it.

**The Logic of Divine Protection**

A final Messenger requires a preserved final message. If the Qur'an were lost or substantially altered, later generations could not be held responsible for following it. God's promise of preservation is therefore connected to His justice. He has not sent a message for humanity and then abandoned it to uncertainty.

The verse invites gratitude. Every time the Qur'an is recited accurately, understood honestly, and acted upon sincerely, the believer participates in the living history of the promise: God gathers, preserves, and recites His Book, and He allows human servants to become its carriers.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:18]

> **So when We recite it, follow its recitation.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Following Before Speaking**

This verse continues the instruction to the Prophet ﷺ: when God recites the revelation, follow its recitation. The verb *ittabiʿ* means to follow closely, to go behind, or to conform to a path. It suggests attentive repetition rather than independent improvisation. The Messenger's task at that moment is to receive the words as given.

The verse establishes an important order between revelation and human response. First comes listening; then following; only after that may the recipient explain, teach, and apply. A person who speaks before understanding may be confident but is not reliable. A person who follows carefully may speak less quickly but will carry the message more faithfully.

**The Model of Prophetic Reception**

The Prophet ﷺ was the first student of the Qur'an. His knowledge was not self-generated, and his authority did not come from personal invention. He followed what was recited to him and then conveyed it to others. This is one reason the Qur'an includes instructions that address him directly: the community sees in them the proper manners of revelation.

The Qur'an says:

> **“Nor does he speak from his own desire. It is not but revelation revealed.”** (Qur'an 53:3–4)

The verse refers to the Prophet's conveyance of revelation, not to every ordinary statement he made or to the cancellation of human reasoning. It emphasizes that when he communicates God's revealed message, he is not its author. He is a trustworthy recipient and messenger.

**Following Is More Than Repeating Sounds**

At one level, the verse refers to following the recitation in order to memorize it. At another, it asks the believer to follow the Qur'an's direction. A person can repeat a passage accurately while walking in the opposite direction from its moral instruction. True following includes hearing, understanding, believing, and acting.

The Qur'an describes successful believers as those who listen and follow the best of what they hear:

> **“So give good tidings to My servants—those who listen to the word and follow the best of it. Those are the ones whom God has guided, and those are people of understanding.”** (Qur'an 39:17–18)

“Following the best” does not mean selecting only what agrees with desire. It means responding to revelation with the best form of obedience: sincere, balanced, and properly understood.

**A Cure for Impulsive Interpretation**

Modern communication rewards speed. A person may see a headline, hear a short clip, and immediately form a verdict. The verse gives a different discipline: let the full recitation arrive before extracting a conclusion. This is especially important with Scripture, where a command may have context, a story may have a moral sequence, and one verse may clarify another.

A teacher who interrupts every sentence cannot learn the lesson. A reader who searches only for ammunition does not follow the recitation; he uses fragments of it. The verse calls for a posture in which the text is allowed to lead.

**Following Revelation Without Abandoning Thought**

Following does not mean turning off reason. The Qur'an repeatedly asks people to think, understand, and reflect. It means that reason serves truth rather than placing itself above the source of truth. Just as a traveler uses judgment to follow a trustworthy guide through unfamiliar country, the believer uses intelligence to understand and apply revelation while accepting that the guide sees what he does not.

This balance prevents two errors. The first is blind literalism that ignores language, context, and purpose. The second is intellectual arrogance that alters the text whenever it challenges preference. The prophetic model is attentive reception followed by wise explanation.

**The Practical Habit of Following the Recitation**

A Muslim can enact this verse by listening to Qur'an without multitasking, learning pronunciation from a qualified teacher, reading translation alongside Arabic, asking knowledgeable people about difficult passages, and turning a verse into a concrete act. If the Qur'an commands reconciliation, pursue it. If it warns against suspicion, examine the stories being told in the mind. If it praises charity, give. Following is shown by direction, not only by sound.

The verse therefore protects the Qur'an from becoming background music. It is a recitation to be followed—first by the Messenger, then by every person who hears it.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:19]

> **Then surely it is for Us to explain it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Explanation Belongs Ultimately to God**

The final verse of the revelation passage says that its explanation is upon God: *thumma inna ʿalaynā bayānah*. *Bayān* means clarification, making something clear, distinguishing it, and bringing its intended meaning into expression. God promises not only to preserve the words of the Qur'an but also to guide the Messenger to understand and explain them.

This promise is essential. A preserved text without reliable explanation could still be exploited by anyone who wished to impose a meaning upon it. God therefore sent the Qur'an with the Prophet ﷺ, whose teaching, practice, judgments, and character explain how revelation is lived. The Sunnah is not a competing revelation or an independent scripture, but it is the prophetic clarification of the Qur'an's guidance.

**The Qur'an Explains Itself**

One dimension of *bayān* is that God clarifies the Qur'an through the Qur'an. A broad principle may be explained elsewhere by a detailed passage. A general promise may be qualified by another verse. A brief reference to resurrection may be unfolded across many sūrahs. The reader must therefore avoid building a doctrine from one isolated phrase while ignoring the Book's wider teaching.

The Qur'an says:

> **“Indeed, We have sent down the Reminder, and indeed We will preserve it.”** (Qur'an 15:9)

Preservation includes the text, but understanding requires patient comparison of its passages. This is one reason the scholars developed tafsīr, Arabic linguistic analysis, recitation studies, hadith criticism, legal theory, and principles for reconciling texts. These disciplines are servants of the promise of clarification.

**The Prophetic Explanation**

The Prophet ﷺ explained the Qur'an through words and actions. When the Qur'an commanded prayer, he taught its form and said:

> **“Pray as you have seen me pray.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 631)

When the Qur'an commanded pilgrimage, he demonstrated its rites and said:

> **“Take your rites from me.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 1297)

These reports show why understanding the Qur'an cannot be separated from the Messenger's guidance. The Prophet ﷺ did not merely pronounce the words; he clarified how they shape worship, family relations, justice, commerce, mercy, and public responsibility.

**The Place of Classical Scholarship**

Classical scholars such as Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī differed on many details, but their work reflects a shared reverence for the boundary between transmitted knowledge and speculation. They considered Arabic usage, the context of revelation, the explanations of the Companions, authentic hadith, the relationships between verses, and legal principles. Their disagreement does not mean that the Qur'an lacks clarity. It means that some applications require disciplined reasoning and that interpreters are human.

A responsible reader distinguishes between the Qur'an's direct wording, an authentic prophetic explanation, a Companion's interpretation, and a later scholarly possibility. This protects the sacred text from both careless simplification and false certainty.

**Explanation Is Also Illumination of the Heart**

Bayān is not merely technical information. A person may know the dictionary meaning of a verse and still fail to understand its demand. True explanation makes the meaning visible in life. “Do not backbite” becomes clear when one realizes that a person's honor is a trust even when he is absent. “Spend” becomes clear when one sees wealth as provision rather than absolute ownership. “Remember the Hereafter” becomes clear when choices are evaluated beyond immediate applause.

The verse invites the reader to ask God for understanding. The Prophet ﷺ supplicated for Ibn ʿAbbās:

> **“O God, give him understanding of the religion and teach him interpretation.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 143; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2477, with related wording)

Knowledge is therefore both learned through means and granted by divine generosity. The correct response to difficulty is not arrogance or despair but patient study, prayer, and humility.

**The Sequence as a Method of Reading**

Verses 16–19 provide a method: do not rush the tongue; God gathers and recites; follow the recitation; God explains it. The reader should begin with accurate text, continue with attentive listening, seek reliable explanation, and then practice. A person who skips the first stages may create a religion out of impressions. A person who stops at the words without seeking their guidance may create a museum out of revelation.

The Qur'an is preserved speech, followed recitation, and living explanation. All three dimensions belong together.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:20]

> **Nay! But you love the ephemeral,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Disease of Loving What Passes**

The sūrah turns from the Prophet's reception of revelation to humanity's moral condition: *kallā bal tuḥibbūna al-ʿājilah*. The emphatic *kallā* rejects the excuses of those who neglect the Hereafter. *Al-ʿājilah* means the immediate, the quickly arriving, and by extension the present worldly life. The verse does not say that people merely use the world or appreciate its blessings. It says they love the immediate in a way that makes it preferred over the lasting.

Love here is an orientation of the heart. A person may possess wealth without loving it excessively, and may enjoy lawful pleasures without making them his god. The problem is when the immediate reward becomes more compelling than truth, even though the person knows it will pass.

**Immediate Reward and Delayed Truth**

Human beings naturally respond strongly to immediate rewards. A small benefit available now can appear more attractive than a greater benefit promised later. This is visible in spending, diet, study, relationships, and career decisions. The Qur'an does not ignore this psychology; it trains it. Prayer, fasting, charity, and remembrance repeatedly teach the person to choose a lasting good over an immediate impulse.

The Prophet ﷺ described the world as a place of trial and the Hereafter as the enduring home. A famous authentic report states:

> **“The world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2956)

This does not mean that every believer lives in material misery or that every disbeliever enjoys worldly comfort. It means that the believer's ultimate possibilities are restricted by obedience in this temporary life, while the person who denies the Hereafter may treat worldly desire as his entire horizon.

**The Qur'an's Balanced View of the World**

Islam does not teach hatred of creation. The Qur'an says:

> **“Seek, through what God has given you, the home of the Hereafter, and do not forget your share of the world. And do good as God has done good to you, and do not seek corruption in the land.”** (Qur'an 28:77)

The world is a place of provision, worship, family, learning, and service. It is “ephemeral” not because it is meaningless but because it is temporary. A bridge matters to the traveler even though he does not live on it. The mistake is not crossing the bridge; it is building a permanent home upon it.

**When Immediate Desire Becomes a Judge**

The verse describes a moral inversion. Instead of using revelation to judge desire, the person uses desire to judge revelation. Whatever is easy now is called good; whatever requires patience is called oppressive. A person may reject honesty because deception brings immediate profit, or reject modesty because restraint brings immediate social cost. The temporary reward becomes the standard of truth.

This is why the love of the immediate harms the intellect. It does not necessarily make a person unable to understand an argument. It makes him unwilling to accept an argument that requires sacrifice. The mind becomes an advocate for the appetite.

**Training the Heart to Prefer the Lasting**

The cure is not to pretend that delayed goods are immediately pleasant. It is to educate desire through remembrance. A person can break large choices into small acts: pray the next prayer on time, refuse the next lie, give a small charity, stop a harmful conversation, and make one sincere apology. These actions train the soul to value God's pleasure even when the immediate ego receives no applause.

The Prophet ﷺ described the intelligent person as one who brings himself to account and works for what comes after death, while the incapable person follows his desires and merely hopes. This report is transmitted by al-Tirmidhī (2459), with discussion among hadith specialists regarding its chain; its meaning is supported by the Qur'an's repeated contrast between immediate desire and lasting return.

The verse is not asking the human being to stop living. It is asking him to stop allowing what is short to rule what is eternal.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:21]

> **And forsake the Hereafter.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of the Exchange**

Verse 20 describes love for the immediate world; verse 21 describes the corresponding abandonment of the Hereafter. The two actions belong together. When a person gives ultimate love to what passes, he necessarily neglects what lasts. The Qur'an presents the matter as a choice of direction rather than a neutral preference.

To “forsake” the Hereafter does not always mean openly denying its existence. A person may verbally affirm resurrection while arranging his entire life as though it were irrelevant. The heart's real belief is displayed by what it repeatedly sacrifices for. If prayer is always sacrificed for convenience, honesty for profit, and family obligations for status, the person's actions are treating the Hereafter as distant even if the tongue mentions it.

**Neglect Can Be Quiet**

Forsaking the Hereafter is often gradual. A person does not wake one morning and decide to abandon God. He delays a prayer, excuses a small injustice, normalizes an unlawful income, and stops listening to reminders that make him uncomfortable. The soul adapts to each compromise. Eventually the Hereafter becomes a religious idea without practical authority.

The Qur'an warns:

> **“And remember your Lord in yourself, humbly and fearfully, without loudness in speech, morning and evening, and do not be among the heedless.”** (Qur'an 7:205)

Heedlessness is not simply lack of information. It is the failure to keep what is known present in the heart. A person may have heard many sermons and still forsake the Hereafter if knowledge does not direct conduct.

**The Hereafter as the Corrective to Short-Term Thinking**

The doctrine of the Hereafter gives weight to sacrifices that worldly accounting cannot value. A person who returns money he could have kept, cares for a dependent relative, forgives an insult, or performs worship in private may receive little recognition. Belief in the Hereafter prevents these acts from appearing irrational. It also restrains injustice when the wrongdoer believes he can avoid human detection.

The Qur'an describes worldly life in comparison with the Hereafter:

> **“Know that the life of this world is only play, diversion, adornment, boasting among you, and rivalry in wealth and children—like rain whose growth pleases the farmers, then it dries and you see it yellow, then it becomes debris. And in the Hereafter there is severe punishment and forgiveness from God and approval; and the life of this world is only the enjoyment of delusion.”** (Qur'an 57:20)

This is not a command to despise all worldly activity. It is a warning against allowing temporary appearance to deceive the heart about final value.

**The Social Consequences of Forgetting the Future**

A society that forgets accountability tends to measure people by possession, visibility, and power. The vulnerable become expendable if they do not contribute to immediate profit. Future generations are burdened for present comfort. Environmental damage, dishonest finance, exploitation, and family neglect are all intensified by short-term thinking.

Belief in the Hereafter creates a longer moral horizon. It does not eliminate the need for worldly institutions, but it gives them an ethical foundation. A leader remembers that authority is a trust. An employer remembers that workers are not merely units of production. A consumer remembers that choices affect unseen people and future lives.

**Restoring the Forsaken End**

The remedy is to bring the Hereafter back into ordinary decisions. Before speaking, ask whether the words can be defended before God. Before buying, ask whether the acquisition is lawful and beneficial. Before responding to anger, ask whether winning the argument is worth losing the heart. Before sleeping, ask what has been sent ahead.

The verse is brief because the diagnosis is simple: love the immediate as a servant, not as a master; do not abandon the lasting world for what will soon be left behind.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:22]

> **Faces that Day shall be radiant,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Face as the Surface of the Inner State**

After warning about those who love the immediate world, the sūrah presents the people who received the lasting good: faces that Day will be radiant. The Qur'an often uses the face to represent the person's visible state and inner honor. *Nāḍirah* comes from *n-ḍ-r*, freshness, beauty, brightness, and flourishing. It is not merely cosmetic brightness. It is the appearance of joy, safety, and favor.

The contrast is significant. In the present world, a person's face may conceal exhaustion, fear, or guilt behind social performance. On the Last Day, the inner reality becomes visible. The radiant face is the outward form of a soul that has found the reward of faith and obedience.

**Light as a Gift, Not a Self-Created Achievement**

The Qur'an frequently describes believers with light. It says:

> **“Their light will run before them and by their right hands; they will say, ‘Our Lord, perfect our light for us and forgive us.’”** (Qur'an 66:8)

The believers ask God to complete their light even after it has appeared. This teaches humility: a person's good deeds are not an independent source of salvation. Guidance, sincerity, perseverance, and acceptance are all gifts from God. The believer works, but he does not boast as though he created the ability to work.

The radiant face is therefore not the reward of self-congratulation. It belongs to people who combined faith, obedience, repentance, and hope in God. They may have struggled and fallen, but they returned rather than making peace with rebellion.

**The Social Meaning of a Radiant Face**

A radiant face in the Qur'anic sense is not just a private spiritual symbol. People shaped by faith bring security to others. Their faces are “radiant” because their presence is not dominated by suspicion, greed, or the need to humiliate. The Prophet ﷺ encouraged kindness and described a cheerful encounter as charity. He said:

> **“Do not belittle any good deed, even meeting your brother with a cheerful face.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2626)

This hadith does not equate superficial smiling with salvation. It shows that the face can communicate dignity and mercy. A person who carries faith inwardly should not make others feel that they are merely obstacles or objects.

**The Hope of the Final Face**

The verse speaks to people whose worldly circumstances may not look radiant. Illness, poverty, grief, and social rejection can mark a face with fatigue. The promise is not that every believer will look prosperous in the worldly sense. It is that the final Day will reveal a joy not dependent on worldly appearance.

The Qur'an says:

> **“On that Day some faces will be bright, laughing, rejoicing, and other faces on that Day will have dust upon them, covered by darkness.”** (Qur'an 80:38–41)

The believer is not encouraged to despise those with difficult faces in this life. He is encouraged to seek the kind of faith whose final result is divine acceptance rather than temporary admiration.

**A Preparation for Radiance**

The path to radiance begins with what a person allows into the heart. Remembrance, gratitude, lawful earning, truthful relationships, and repentance leave a different mark from envy, mockery, and secret betrayal. No one can manufacture the final light through cosmetic religious performance. The face reflects the account of a life known to God.

The most practical preparation is to ask God for a sound heart and to remove what darkens it. When a believer forgives, prays in private, gives without display, or restrains himself from a wrong that no one would detect, he is preparing a face that will not need to pretend on the Day when pretending is impossible.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:23]

> **gazing upon their Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Highest Gift of Paradise**

The phrase *ilā rabbihā nāẓirah* literally means that they will be looking toward their Lord. The majority of Sunni scholars understood this as affirming the believers' real vision of God in the Hereafter, in a manner befitting His majesty and without claiming that creation encompasses Him. This doctrine is supported by authentic hadith and by the grammatical clarity of the verse. It is one of the greatest honors promised to the people of Paradise.

The verse does not describe God as a physical object contained in a direction or limited by the eye. Islamic theology distinguishes between seeing and encompassing. A created being may see what God permits it to see, while no created sight surrounds or comprehends God.

**The Qur'anic Balance About Seeing God**

Some scholars who interpreted this verse non-literally emphasized the Qur'an's statement:

> **“Vision cannot encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision.”** (Qur'an 6:103)

The mainstream reconciliation is that 6:103 denies encompassing God, while 75:23 and the authentic hadith affirm vision without encompassing. The two truths protect the believer from opposite errors: denying the promised gift altogether, or imagining that God is a created body that can be contained by sight.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“Indeed, you will see your Lord as you see this moon, and you will have no difficulty in seeing Him.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 7434; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 183)

The comparison concerns clarity and absence of confusion, not similarity between God and the moon. The moon is visible to many people at once without their vision colliding; the hadith communicates that the believers' vision of their Lord will not be uncertain or obstructed. It does not say that God resembles the moon.

**Why the Vision Is the Reward of the Heart**

The sequence is meaningful. The faces are radiant, then they look toward their Lord. Their joy is not merely from gardens, food, companionship, or freedom from pain. All these are gifts, but the greatest gift is nearness and the vision of the One whom they worshipped without seeing in worldly life.

This gives spiritual depth to faith in the Unseen. The believer prays, hopes, and sacrifices without seeing God, relying on revelation and the signs of creation. In the Hereafter, God honors that faith with a vision that surpasses worldly knowledge. The reward is not earned as a mechanical price; it is granted through God's mercy to those who believed and acted.

A hadith qudsī reports that when the people of Paradise are asked whether they want anything more, God lifts the veil and they receive nothing more beloved to them than looking upon their Lord. This report is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (181) in the hadith of Ṣuhayb. The wording teaches the hierarchy of delight: the highest joy is not what the servant receives from God, but God Himself as the object of love and vision.

**The Theology of Divine Attributes**

The verse teaches an affirmation without asking the reader to imagine a created form. God is not compared to His creation, and the promise of seeing Him does not imply that He is subject to created limitations. The appropriate method is to affirm what revelation affirms, deny resemblance, and avoid speculative descriptions of “how.” The Qur'an says:

> **“There is nothing like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing.”** (Qur'an 42:11)

This verse joins transcendence and affirmation. God is unlike creation, yet He truly possesses the attributes He has named. The vision in the Hereafter is one of those revealed gifts, and its reality is known even though its manner is beyond worldly imagination.

**Living Toward the Meeting**

A person looks toward what he loves and remembers. The verse asks what currently occupies the gaze of the heart. Is it approval, status, consumption, or the pleasure of God? The world trains attention by repeatedly showing images of what it wants people to desire. Revelation retrains attention toward the final meeting.

This does not require withdrawal from all worldly sight. It means using the eyes without allowing them to become masters. A believer can work, study, enjoy beauty, and care for family while keeping the ultimate gaze directed toward God. The promise of 75:23 is the completion of that orientation: those who turned toward God in faith will be honored with the vision of their Lord.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:24]

> **And faces that Day shall be scowling,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Face of Inner Distress**

The sūrah now contrasts the radiant faces with faces that are *bāsirah*—described by the commentators as gloomy, frowning, or distressed. The word is connected with a face that has become hard or contracted under fear. The contrast is not simply between attractive and unattractive appearances. It is between joy produced by divine acceptance and anguish produced by realizing that acceptance has been lost.

The face is a public sign of a hidden state. In this world, a person can control facial expression for a time. On the Last Day, the heart's condition will appear without the ability to manage it. The face will not be scowling because of a temporary inconvenience; it will bear the knowledge of a permanent crisis.

**The End of Performance**

People often learn to perform confidence. A person may smile in public while fearing exposure, or speak boldly while inwardly uncertain. The final Day removes the need for social performance because the audience of humanity is no longer the decisive audience. The person stands before God, and the face expresses what the heart had chosen.

The Qur'an describes the other faces:

> **“And on the Day when some faces will whiten and some faces will blacken. As for those whose faces blacken: ‘Did you disbelieve after your belief? Then taste the punishment for what you used to reject.’”** (Qur'an 3:106)

The imagery is moral and eschatological, not a license to judge the spiritual state of individuals by their physical appearance in this life. A dark-skinned or tired person is not meant by the verse. It describes the final manifestation of rejection and ruin.

**Fear That Comes From Recognition**

The distressed faces know something. They are not merely frightened by an unknown danger; they recognize that a crushing consequence is approaching. This is important because the Qur'an's punishment is not arbitrary. The person sees the connection between his choices and his end. He may have denied that connection in life, but the final disclosure makes it evident.

This recognition is one reason the Qur'an repeatedly invites reflection before death. It wants the person to know his direction while turning remains possible. A warning received now is mercy; a truth recognized only after the account is sealed is a source of regret.

**How a Face Becomes Hard**

The spiritual hardness described by such verses generally develops through repeated choices. A single sin does not necessarily produce a permanent state. But when a person repeatedly lies, mocks repentance, harms others, and rejects reminders, the heart adapts. The face may remain socially smooth while the moral interior becomes contracted.

The Qur'an says:

> **“Then, after that, your hearts became hardened, being like stones or even harder.”** (Qur'an 2:74)

The remedy is to seek softness through remembrance, humility, service, and honest repentance. A person should be concerned when he can discuss serious wrongdoing without sorrow, or when reminders about death only irritate him. Irritation is not proof of final ruin, but it is a signal requiring treatment.

**Compassion in This Life**

The warning about distressed faces should make believers compassionate, not arrogant. They should not enjoy the thought of other people's punishment or use the Hereafter as a tool for personal cruelty. The Prophet ﷺ grieved over those who rejected truth and continued to invite them. The believer's task is to warn himself first, invite others with wisdom, and leave final judgment to God.

The practical question is: what face am I preparing? Not the face seen in a photograph, but the spiritual expression formed by repeated choices. A face softened by repentance may be honored even if the world never admired it. A face hardened by arrogance may be exposed even if the world applauded it.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:25]

> **knowing that a spine-crushing calamity will befall them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Calamity That Breaks the Strongest Posture**

The phrase *ẓann* in this context can convey certainty or an overpowering expectation: the distressed faces know that a *fāqirah* will befall them. *Fāqirah* comes from *faqar*, the backbone or vertebrae, and denotes a calamity that breaks the back. The word is intentionally physical. A person who stood upright in worldly pride will meet a consequence that breaks the illusion of self-sufficiency.

The “back” is associated with strength and ability to carry burdens. A back-breaking calamity is not a minor discomfort; it overwhelms the person's ability to dismiss or manage it. The phrase communicates the seriousness of the Hereafter without asking the reader to imagine every detail beyond what revelation has given.

**Why the Qur'an Uses Bodily Images**

The Qur'an often describes the unseen through bodily images because human beings understand danger through embodied experience. A crushed back, a trembling eye, thirst, heat, and weight all make the final state emotionally intelligible. These images do not reduce the Hereafter to worldly pain. They bring an unseen reality close enough for the conscience to respond.

The Qur'an also says:

> **“The punishment of that Day is not like the punishment of any other day, and the chains are not like ordinary chains.”** (paraphrased from the Qur'anic descriptions in 69:30–32 and 89:25–26)

It is important not to turn this into dramatic speculation. The purpose is moral awakening: the consequences are real and serious, and a person should not treat divine warning as a metaphor that can be ignored.

**The Connection Between Pride and Collapse**

The image particularly addresses pride. An arrogant person behaves as though he cannot be corrected, needs no forgiveness, and answers to no one. His posture is already spiritually brittle because it depends upon controlling how others see him. When he is confronted with a reality he cannot control, the posture collapses.

The Prophet ﷺ defined arrogance in a concise hadith:

> **“Arrogance is rejecting the truth and looking down on people.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 91)

This definition shows that arrogance is not the same as confidence or dignity. A person may be confident and humble, or outwardly weak and internally arrogant. The essential signs are refusal of truth and contempt for people. The spine-crushing calamity is the final consequence of a life built on that refusal.

**A Calamity Made by the Person's Direction**

The verse does not mean that every hardship in this life is a punishment or that those who suffer are spiritually condemned. Believers can face severe illness, poverty, and oppression as tests, and prophets endured great hardship. The final calamity of the verse belongs to the Hereafter and is linked to persistent denial and moral rejection.

This distinction protects compassion. We do not read a person's worldly pain as proof of God's anger. We read the verse as a warning to the person who has been given time, evidence, and opportunity, yet insists on rebellion and refuses repentance.

**Avoiding the Back-Breaking End**

The way to avoid such an end is not to believe that one is incapable of sin. It is to remain capable of return. Humility before truth, repentance after failure, and mercy toward people are forms of spiritual flexibility. A tree that bends in the wind may survive; a rigid structure can break. The believer bows physically in prayer to train the soul not to live upright in pride.

The verse asks whether a person is carrying his own ego as though it were stronger than God's command. The wise servant lowers himself voluntarily in worship before he is lowered involuntarily by the truth he refused.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:26]

> **Nay! But when it reaches the collarbones,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scene Changes From the Last Day to the Last Breath**

The sūrah now returns from the distant scene of Resurrection to the intimate moment of death. The emphatic *kallā* marks another rejection of false expectation: the person who postpones repentance will face the truth when the soul reaches the collarbones. The phrase *balaghat al-tarāqī* refers to the soul reaching the upper chest or collarbones, a traditional expression for the final struggle of life.

The movement is powerful. The Resurrection may appear remote, but death is the doorway through which every person must pass. The person who asks, “When is the Hour?” may be distracted by its uncertain date, but his personal hour can arrive without warning. The verse brings eschatology into the room of the dying.

**The Limits of Medical Control**

Islam values medicine and encourages treatment. Yet medicine cannot abolish death. It can cure some illnesses, prolong life in some cases, and relieve pain, but every intervention remains within the created order and under God's permission. At the moment appointed by God, the soul departs.

The Qur'an says:

> **“And every community has a term; when their term comes, they cannot delay it by an hour nor advance it.”** (Qur'an 7:34)

The verse is not a reason to refuse treatment or neglect safety. It is a reason not to imagine that treatment grants ownership over life. A doctor can be a means of mercy, but not the ultimate giver of life.

**The Soul and the Body at Separation**

The Qur'an describes the soul's departure in several passages. It says:

> **“Then, when death comes to one of them, Our messengers take him, and they do not neglect their duty.”** (Qur'an 6:61)

And:

> **“Say: The angel of death who has been entrusted with you will take you, then unto your Lord you will be returned.”** (Qur'an 32:11)

These verses affirm the unseen role of the angels without denying that death may appear through physical causes. Illness, accident, and aging are means; the taking of the soul is under divine command. The exact experience differs according to the person's state, but every person discovers that bodily life is not self-owned.

**The Collarbone as a Boundary**

The collarbones are at the threshold between the chest and the head, close to the throat and breath. The expression evokes the body's final weakness. A person who once spoke with authority, moved freely, and planned years ahead becomes unable to control the most basic process of life. The image dismantles arrogance without insulting the human body. It reminds us that dependence is not an occasional condition; it is our permanent reality.

The dying person's awareness may become sharper in some ways. Hidden claims about wealth, status, and future plans fall away. The Qur'an describes the final moment of certain people:

> **“When death comes to one of them, he says, ‘My Lord, send me back.’”** (Qur'an 23:99)

The request is denied not because God lacks mercy, but because the period of action has ended. The dying person sees what should have been done when doing it was possible.

**Preparing Before the Body Weakens**

The practical teaching is to repent before crisis, make wills and debts clear, reconcile where possible, and keep the heart ready for return. A person should not wait for a terminal diagnosis to say what justice and love require. End-of-life preparation is not morbid; it is responsible. It includes medical care, family communication, financial obligations, and spiritual readiness.

The verse does not tell the reader to live in constant fear of death. It tells him to live without assuming that delay is guaranteed. The person who remembers death appropriately becomes more grateful for ordinary life and more careful with the opportunities it contains.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:27]

> **and it is said, “Who will [make him] ascend?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cry Around the Dying Person**

The phrase *wa qīla man rāq* is concise and has several interpretations in the classical tafsīr tradition. *Rāq* may be understood as one who ascends, one who performs healing or spiritual treatment, or one who takes charge of the soul's departure. The ambiguity reflects the confusion around death: those present ask who can help, cure, raise, or receive the dying person.

In ordinary language, family members may say, “Who can save him?” or “Who can bring him back?” The scene includes the helplessness of those who love the dying person. They may call physicians, reciters, relatives, and specialists, but no created helper can override the appointed moment.

**The Human Search for a Final Remedy**

When the body is strong, people often assume that every problem has a technical solution. At death, the limits become visible. Medicine remains honorable and useful, but the final departure is not a failure of a particular doctor. It belongs to the divine decree. The Qur'an says:

> **“Wherever you may be, death will overtake you, even if you are in fortified towers.”** (Qur'an 4:78)

This does not cancel precautions. It places precautions within a larger truth. A person locks a door, wears a seatbelt, visits a physician, and avoids danger because God commands responsible means. But he knows that no measure creates immortality.

**The Possibility of “Rāq” as Healing**

If *rāq* refers to someone attempting healing or recitation, the verse captures the moment when human remedies are exhausted. Islam permits lawful medical treatment and lawful *ruqyah* using Qur'anic recitation and authentic supplications, while rejecting superstition, exploitation, and claims to control the unseen. Healing belongs to God even when a physician or reciter is the means.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“Seek treatment, O servants of God, for God has not sent down a disease except that He has also sent down its cure.”** (Sunan Abī Dāwūd, 3855; Sunan al-Tirmidhī, 2038; the report is authenticated by hadith scholars)

The same tradition teaches that the cure may not arrive, and that death remains appointed. Seeking treatment is obedience; demanding a particular outcome as though it were owed is a different matter.

**The Soul's Ascent and the Unseen Angels**

Some commentators understood the question as referring to the angels: who will take the soul upward? The Qur'an describes the angels as entrusted with taking souls and returning them to God. The unseen details should be affirmed as revelation reports them without invented stories about what has not been specified.

The movement from the collarbones to the unseen realm reminds the reader that death is not annihilation. The body remains in the world, but the soul enters a stage of existence before the final resurrection. The human being has not ceased to exist simply because observers can no longer communicate with the body.

**How the Living Should Stand Around Death**

The verse also teaches the living how to respond. They should seek appropriate treatment, make supplication, speak truthfully and gently, avoid false promises, and respect the dignity of the dying. They should not turn a person's final moments into a spectacle or a debate. If forgiveness is possible, it should be offered. If debts and responsibilities are known, they should be addressed.

The question “Who will help?” ultimately has a spiritual answer: God alone gives life, takes the soul, and grants mercy. Those around the dying can be means of comfort, but they cannot claim the place of the Lord. Remembering this protects both hope and humility.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:28]

> **And he knows for certain that it is the parting;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Certainty Arrives When Choice Is Ending**

The dying person now knows with certainty that this is *al-firāq*, the parting. The noun comes from *f-r-q*, to separate or divide. It can mean separation from the world, separation of the soul from the body, or separation from family and possessions. The person who spent life treating death as an abstract possibility encounters it as immediate fact.

The word “knows” is significant. At death, the truth of the Hereafter becomes evident to the person, but this knowledge arrives at the boundary where ordinary action is ending. Faith is most valuable when it is chosen in the period of the unseen, not when the unseen has become visible and denial is no longer psychologically possible.

**Parting From Everything Temporary**

Death separates a person from possessions, titles, plans, and relationships in their worldly form. The Qur'an says:

> **“You have certainly come to Us alone, as We created you the first time, and you have left behind what We bestowed upon you.”** (Qur'an 6:94)

This does not deny that family may pray for the deceased or that ongoing charity may benefit him. It means that no one arrives with worldly ownership. Possessions remain in the world; deeds and the rights of people become part of the account.

The separation is also from opportunities. The person can no longer add a prayer, return a stolen item through his own action, or apologize directly. That is why the Qur'an repeatedly treats life as a period of work and death as the beginning of recompense.

**The Difference Between Certainty and Faith**

Faith in the Hereafter is certainty based on revelation, reason, and trust before physical sight. The knowledge described here is direct and unavoidable. It does not carry the same moral merit as believing in what is unseen, because the trial has reached its conclusion. A person who refuses to believe until the veil is removed has not demonstrated the same submission as one who believed and acted while the realities were hidden.

Pharaoh provides a Qur'anic example of late acknowledgment:

> **“I believe that there is no deity except the One in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am among the Muslims.”** (Qur'an 10:90)

The reply came that his claim arrived only when drowning had overtaken him. The issue was not that God could not hear words; it was that the words came as the final consequence, not as a free return while life remained open.

**Making Peace With the Truth Before Parting**

A believer should not wait for death to discover what truly matters. Parting is less frightening for the person who has practiced release: he gives charity knowing possessions are temporary, forgives knowing resentment cannot be carried as ownership, and keeps promises knowing reputation is not the final judge. Every act of detachment from ego is a small preparation for the final separation.

This does not mean neglecting family or abandoning lawful work. It means holding them as trusts. A person who loves family for God's sake does not love them less; he loves them in a way that does not demand that they provide the immortality only God can give.

**A Good Ending Is Sought Through a Good Life**

Muslims ask God for a good ending, but a good ending is not usually produced by a last-minute phrase disconnected from the life before it. It is the fruit of repeated return. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“Actions are judged by their endings.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 6607, with related narrations)

The hadith encourages hope for a person who turns sincerely, and fear of relying upon appearances while neglecting the heart. Since no one knows his final moment, the proper response is continuous repentance and consistent obedience. The person who understands the certainty of parting begins to prepare for it before others gather around his bed asking who can help.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:29]

> **and the shank is intertwined with the shank.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Idiom of Overwhelming Severity**

The phrase *wa iltaffati al-sāqu bi-l-sāq* has been interpreted in several ways by the classical commentators. It may describe one leg becoming entwined with the other when the dying body is wrapped or loses strength. It may refer to the severity of death joined to the severity of the next stage. It may also be an Arabic idiom for an overwhelming crisis, as when one difficulty is joined to another.

The image is physical, but its meaning reaches beyond anatomy. The human being who once moved freely can no longer command his limbs. The stages of leaving the world and entering the next are joined. The person cannot pause between them to renegotiate his life.

**The Qur'anic Idiom of the “Shank”**

The Qur'an uses the word *sāq* in another well-known passage:

> **“On the Day when the shank will be exposed and they will be called to prostrate, they will not be able.”** (Qur'an 68:42)

Classical scholars explained this expression as an idiom for the severity of the Day, while also discussing the transmitted wording about God's attribute in a manner befitting Him. The connection demonstrates the richness of Arabic idiom: “the shank being exposed” can indicate the intensification of a crisis. In 75:29, the intertwining of shanks similarly communicates crisis upon crisis.

It is important not to force either verse into a crude bodily picture of God. The present verse concerns the human body and human death. The language of severe crisis must be read through Arabic usage and the principles of divine transcendence.

**Two Worlds Joined at the Moment of Death**

One early interpretation sees the two shanks as the final day of worldly life and the first day of the Hereafter joining together. This is a powerful description of death. To observers, the person is still in this world; in his own experience, he has begun to leave it. The boundary between stages is not a long public interval. It is a transition governed by God.

The same image applies to moral preparation. Every present act is linked to a future consequence. The worldly and the next life are not two unrelated stories. They are joined as cause and result, cultivation and harvest. The person who thinks that death separates him from the consequences of his deeds misunderstands what death does. It separates him from the opportunity to change them, but it brings him closer to their disclosure.

**When Physical Strength Cannot Save**

The leg represents movement and independence. A person may spend life traveling wherever he wants, entering any market, approaching any pleasure, and avoiding any warning. At death, the body shows its dependence. The shanks become intertwined, and the person cannot walk away from the appointed meeting.

This is not a condemnation of bodily weakness. Illness and disability can be tests, and people who live with them may possess great spiritual strength. The verse speaks to the illusion that physical ability proves personal independence. The strongest athlete, wealthiest person, and most advanced medical patient remain servants of God.

**Responding to the Crisis Through Prayer Now**

The verse also recalls the bodily language of prayer. In prayer, a believer voluntarily stands, bows, and prostrates, acknowledging dependence before the final involuntary weakness arrives. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“The closest a servant is to his Lord is while he is prostrating, so increase supplication.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 482)

Voluntary prostration is not a way of controlling death. It is a way of meeting dependence with worship. The person who practices humility in the body and heart is better prepared for the moment when bodily control is removed.

**A Warning Against Delay**

The intertwined shanks are a picture of urgency. One crisis follows another, and the time for slogans has ended. The wise response is not to imagine the scene repeatedly in a sensational way, but to make real changes while life remains flexible. Reconcile where possible, repay what is owed, pray with attention, and do not make “later” the main plan of your life.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:30]

> **Unto thy Lord that Day is the driving.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Driving Is Toward God**

The word *masāq* comes from *s-w-q*, to drive or lead forward. The verse says that the driving on that Day is unto the Lord. It can refer to the driving of the soul from the body at death, the driving of people from the graves to the gathering, or the final movement of every person to God's judgment. These meanings are connected: death begins the movement, resurrection continues it, and judgment completes it.

The human being spends life choosing routes and imagining that he controls the destination. At the end, every person is driven to the true Lord. The verse uses “thy Lord” in the singular address to make the universal event personal. You will be driven. I will be driven. Every human being will encounter the Lord who sustained him.

**Being Driven Is Not the Same for Everyone**

The Qur'an describes believers as being gathered with honor, while the wrongdoers may be driven in humiliation. The destination is God's judgment for all, but the manner of arrival corresponds to the person's relationship with Him. A servant who willingly walked toward God in worship is not like one who spent life fleeing from Him.

The Qur'an says:

> **“The Day We gather the reverent unto the Most Compassionate as a delegation, and drive the guilty to Hell, thirsty.”** (Qur'an 19:85–86)

The use of a delegation for the reverent suggests honor and welcome; the driving of the guilty suggests force and distress. The distinction is not based upon worldly appearance. It is based upon faith, repentance, and conduct.

**Death as a Change of Custody**

At death, the body is returned to the earth, while the soul enters the unseen stage known as the barzakh. The person is no longer in control of worldly affairs, but he has not vanished. The angels carry out their tasks, and the soul's experience begins a new phase. The exact details are known through revelation, and the believer avoids inventing what has not been transmitted.

The Qur'an says:

> **“Behind them is a barrier until the Day they are resurrected.”** (Qur'an 23:100)

This barrier is not a place of escape from accountability. It is an intermediate boundary between worldly life and resurrection. The driving toward God continues according to His decree.

**The Social Illusion of Choosing One's Final Lord**

People may live under different authorities—family expectations, social media, employers, political movements, or personal desires. These influences can be strong, but none is the final lord. The question is not whether a person serves something; every person gives ultimate obedience to some claimed value. The question is whether that obedience is rightly ordered under God.

When the final driving occurs, false lords cannot accompany their followers as protectors. The Qur'an says:

> **“They have taken protectors besides God, but God is the Protector, and He gives life to the dead, and He is over all things powerful.”** (Qur'an 42:9)

The verse calls the reader to transfer ultimate reliance before the forced journey begins. A person can use created means, but he should not make them the objects of worship or absolute trust.

**Moving Toward the Meeting Every Day**

The final driving can be remembered without fear becoming paralysis. Every day a person is moving toward God through time. The direction is shaped by repeated choices rather than a single dramatic claim. A person who turns back after sin is changing direction. A person who persists in arrogance is also moving, even if he feels stationary.

The practical question is, “If my life continues in its present direction, toward what am I being driven?” The answer is not found in aspirations alone but in habits. The verse invites immediate correction because the final destination is certain even though the timetable is hidden.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:31]

> **For he neither confirmed nor prayed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Condensed Biography of Rejection**

The pronoun “he” refers to the person whose death and final driving have just been described. The verse now summarizes his worldly life: he did not *ṣaddaq*—confirm or accept the truth—and he did not pray. Two failures are joined: rejection in the heart and abandonment of worship in the body.

The pairing is significant. Belief in Islam is not a mere private opinion, and prayer is not an empty physical performance. Confirmation should lead to worship; worship should express and strengthen confirmation. When both are deliberately rejected, the person has refused both the truth and the relationship that truth requires.

**Ṣiddq and the Meaning of Confirmation**

The verb *ṣaddaq* means to regard something as true, to confirm it, and to act in a way that shows acceptance. It is related to *ṣidq*, truthfulness. A person may hear a proposition and repeat its words, but Qur'anic confirmation includes the heart's submission. The verse is not condemning someone for struggling with a question or seeking evidence. It describes settled refusal after truth has been presented.

The Qur'an says:

> **“They rejected them, while their souls were convinced of them, out of injustice and arrogance.”** (Qur'an 27:14)

This verse describes Pharaoh's people, who recognized signs yet refused because accepting them would threaten their power. The internal conflict between recognition and refusal is one form of the denial described in al-Qiyāmah.

**Prayer as the Visible Proof of Servitude**

The word *ṣallā* means to pray, and in the Islamic context includes the prescribed prayer that joins recitation, standing, bowing, prostration, time, direction, and intention. Prayer is the clearest recurring act by which the servant confirms that he belongs to God. Abandoning it is therefore not a small omission in the structure of faith.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“The covenant between us and them is prayer; whoever abandons it has disbelieved.”** (Sunan al-Tirmidhī, 2621; Sunan al-Nasāʾī, 463; the wording is discussed by jurists regarding the legal classification of deliberate abandonment)

Scholars differed over whether deliberate abandonment constitutes major disbelief outside the faith or a grave sin short of it, but they agreed on the seriousness of abandoning prayer. The hadith should not be used by individuals to declare specific people outside Islam without the conditions and authority required by Islamic law. Its purpose is to show that prayer cannot be treated as a decorative religious habit.

**The Connection Between Conviction and Practice**

A person may say that faith is only in the heart, but the heart's commitments normally shape behavior. Love leads to attention; fear leads to caution; gratitude leads to thanks. If a person claims that God is Lord, that life has a purpose, and that he will return to God, but makes no regular space for worship, the claim and the life contradict each other.

This does not mean that every missed prayer proves the same interior condition. Human beings may be overcome by sleep, forgetfulness, illness, or confusion, and Islamic law considers these circumstances. The verse describes a pattern of deliberate refusal: neither confirmation nor prayer.

**The Path Back Is Still Open in Life**

The verse's severe summary should produce action rather than despair. A person who has neglected prayer can begin with the next prayer, seek knowledge of its basics, make up what scholars require according to a trusted school, and build a sustainable routine. He should not wait to become spiritually perfect before praying; prayer is one of the means by which the heart becomes sound.

Confirmation also grows through honest study, reflection, and asking God for guidance. The person who can say, “I have rejected what I need to understand; I am willing to listen,” has not reached the final condition described here. The warning is directed at settled refusal, not at the sincere seeker who is moving toward truth.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:32]

> **but he denied and turned away,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Denial Followed by Withdrawal**

The verse continues the portrait with two verbs: *kadhdhaba*—he denied or called false—and *tawallā*—he turned away. Denial is an intellectual and verbal rejection; turning away is a practical refusal to listen, consider, or change. Together they describe a person who does not merely lack information. He dismisses the message and then organizes his life to avoid its claim.

The order is instructive. A person may first reject a truth, then avoid every reminder that might challenge the rejection. Or he may turn away repeatedly until denial becomes easy. Either way, the combination closes the channels through which guidance could enter.

**Turning Away Is an Active Choice**

The verb *tawallā* can mean to turn the back or move away. It is not the same as failing to understand. A student who has not yet grasped a difficult subject needs teaching. A person who closes the book, mocks the teacher, and refuses every honest explanation has chosen a different posture. The Qur'an recognizes the difference between ignorance and obstinate rejection.

The Prophet ﷺ was commanded to convey, not to force hearts. The Qur'an says:

> **“So remind, for you are only a reminder. You are not over them a controller.”** (Qur'an 88:21–22)

This preserves human responsibility. Guidance cannot be forced into a heart, and a messenger cannot be blamed for another person's refusal. But the one who turns away remains responsible for the turning.

**The Danger of Partial Hearing**

A person may “deny and turn away” without ever making a formal argument. He may avoid religious questions because they threaten a relationship, a business, a habit, or a public identity. Silence can be a form of refusal when the person already knows that he is avoiding what he should face.

This is especially relevant in an age of information abundance. Few people lack access to every answer, but many choose environments that confirm them and exclude correction. Algorithms can become a practical form of *tawallī*: the user is shown only what allows him to remain comfortable. The verse calls for intellectual and spiritual courage—seek the truth even when it demands change.

**The Mercy of a Warning Before the Final Verdict**

The fact that the Qur'an describes the pattern gives the living reader a chance to escape it. A person can notice when he automatically changes the subject, becomes angry at reminders, or treats every command as an attack on freedom. These reactions do not prove final denial, but they are opportunities to ask what is being protected.

Repentance reverses the movement. Instead of turning away, the person turns toward God. Instead of calling truth false, he asks to understand. Instead of defending every habit, he allows revelation to judge it. The Qur'an says:

> **“And those who strive for Us—We will surely guide them to Our ways. Indeed, God is with the doers of good.”** (Qur'an 29:69)

The verse is a promise to the person who moves toward God with sincere effort, even if the movement is gradual.

**A Warning to Believers as Well**

Believers must not limit the verse to open disbelievers. A Muslim may confirm some truths and turn away from others when obedience becomes costly. He may accept prayer but reject honesty in business, accept charity but refuse forgiveness, or accept a command in theory while making a life plan that excludes it. This is not necessarily the complete disbelief described in the passage, but it is a dangerous partial imitation of its pattern.

The remedy is to renew *samiʿnā wa aṭaʿnā*—“we hear and we obey”—not as a slogan, but as a willingness to let faith reach the difficult part of life.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:33]

> **then went to his people swaggering.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Returning to Society With Arrogance**

The person who denied and turned away then went to his people *yatamatta*, walking with an affected, proud stride. The source commentary renders the image as swaggering. The verb suggests stretching or strutting, as if the person is displaying his confidence and superiority. His rejection is not kept as a private conclusion; it becomes a public performance.

The verse connects disbelief with social behavior. A person may treat faith as something beneath him, mock those who pray, or use his wealth and status to make obedience appear foolish. The swagger is not evidence of strength. It is often the visible costume of a person who needs an audience to confirm that he has escaped accountability.

**Pride Turns a Choice Into an Identity**

A private refusal can sometimes be corrected. When a person turns his refusal into a public identity, correction becomes more difficult because admitting error would threaten his social standing. He must continue the performance in order to remain “consistent.” The ego prefers a known falsehood to an admitted change of mind.

The Qur'an describes the people who rejected earlier messengers in similar terms:

> **“And when they returned to their people, they returned exultant.”** (Qur'an 83:31)

The pattern is not limited to one era. A person may laugh at religious people in private, boast about ignoring moral limits, or treat repentance as weakness. The crowd's approval becomes a substitute for God's approval.

**Swaggering and the Ethics of Dignity**

Islam does not prohibit walking confidently, dressing well, or enjoying lawful success. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“No one who has an atom's weight of arrogance in his heart will enter Paradise.” A man asked, “A person likes his clothes to be beautiful and his sandals to be beautiful.” He said, “God is beautiful and loves beauty. Arrogance is rejecting the truth and despising people.”** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 91)

This hadith defines the problem clearly. Swaggering is not simply a movement of the body. It expresses rejection of truth and contempt for people. A person can walk quietly and still be arrogant; another can walk proudly in public while remaining humble before God. The measure is the heart's stance.

**The Social Harm of Mocking Faith**

When a powerful person mocks religious commitment, the harm can extend beyond his own soul. Young people may become ashamed of prayer. Employees may feel pressured to compromise conscience. A family may treat a practicing member as backward. Social ridicule can be a form of coercion, even when no law is used.

The believer should respond with dignity, not imitation. The Qur'an says:

> **“And the servants of the Most Compassionate are those who walk upon the earth humbly, and when the ignorant address them, they say, ‘Peace.’”** (Qur'an 25:63)

Humility does not mean surrendering truth. It means refusing to let another person's arrogance recruit one's own ego. The believer can answer clearly, set boundaries, and remain courteous.

**The Cure for the Swaggering Heart**

The cure is remembrance of origin and return. The person who began as a dependent infant and will return as a helpless body has no basis for absolute pride. Wealth, intelligence, beauty, and influence are gifts and tests. They may be used honorably, but they do not make the person self-created.

A practical exercise is to perform a good deed that no audience will see, accept correction without defending the ego, and serve someone who cannot increase one's status. These acts weaken the need to swagger and strengthen the dignity of servanthood.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:34]

> **So nearer to you! And nearer!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Repeated Rebuke**

The phrase *awlā laka fa-awlā* is a severe Arabic expression. It has been translated in more than one way: “So woe to you, and woe,” or “Closer to you is what you dislike, and closer.” The exact nuance has been discussed by the classical commentators, but all readings convey condemnation and warning. The repetition intensifies the rebuke.

The phrase may be understood as a declaration that the consequence is drawing near. It may also be a curse-like expression of deserved ruin directed at the arrogant person. The next verse repeats it, showing that the doubled rebuke is deliberate rather than accidental.

**Why Repetition Matters**

Repetition in the Qur'an can emphasize certainty, severity, and completeness. A single warning may be dismissed as a passing statement. Repeated warning removes the excuse that the person did not understand. In daily life, repetition can also train attention: a parent repeats an urgent instruction because the child is in danger, not because the parent lacks words.

Here the repeated phrase breaks the rhythm of the swaggering scene. The person went away from the truth proudly, but the divine address stops him: the danger he mocked is near. The Qur'an often shifts from third-person description to direct address at moments of judgment, making the scene personal for the listener.

**The Nearness of Consequence**

People tend to imagine consequence as distant. A person may believe that an unhealthy habit, a dishonest deal, or a cruel relationship can continue indefinitely. But consequences often approach quietly. Trust weakens, the heart hardens, families break, and opportunities for repentance narrow. The final punishment is not necessarily the first consequence; worldly corruption can be an early form of the same direction.

The verse does not say that every difficulty is a punishment. It teaches that no person should read delay as safety. The Qur'an says:

> **“And never think that God is unaware of what the wrongdoers do. He only delays them for a Day when eyes will stare.”** (Qur'an 14:42)

Delay is not cancellation. It may be mercy allowing repentance, or it may be an extension of the person's chosen course until the account is complete.

**A Warning Against Turning the Verse Into a Weapon**

Believers should be careful when quoting severe warnings. The verse is not a license to curse every person with whom one disagrees or to treat oneself as the final judge of another's destiny. The Prophet ﷺ warned against arrogance in judgment and commanded inviting with wisdom. A specific person may repent, be ignorant, be mistaken, or possess circumstances hidden from observers.

The first application is to the self. “Nearer to you” should make the reader ask what consequence is approaching in his own life. Is a small dishonesty becoming a character? Is a family relationship near collapse? Is the heart becoming indifferent to prayer? A warning received as self-correction is beneficial; a warning used as a weapon is another form of ego.

**The Door Is Near Because Mercy Is Near**

The same nearness can be read through the possibility of repentance. The punishment may be near, but so is God's forgiveness for the person who turns. God says:

> **“And when those who believe in Our signs come to you, say, ‘Peace be upon you. Your Lord has prescribed mercy upon Himself: whoever among you does wrong in ignorance and then repents after that and reforms, He is Forgiving, Merciful.’”** (Qur'an 6:54)

The rebuke is therefore a final call before the consequences arrive. Nearness should create urgency, not hopelessness. The correct response is to stop swaggering, humble the heart, and return.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:35]

> **Then nearer to you! And nearer!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Repetition Closes the Case**

The verse repeats the previous warning: *thumma awlā laka fa-awlā*. The addition of *thumma*—“then”—can signal another stage of consequence or a renewed intensification. The person who has denied, turned away, and returned swaggering is not merely warned once. His condition is presented as deserving repeated rebuke because every stage has reinforced the previous one.

The Qur'an's repetition also mirrors the way a person may repeatedly choose the same wrong. A first warning is followed by another chance; a second refusal is followed by a deeper warning. Divine patience is not divine approval. The repeated opportunity to turn back is a mercy, but the repeated decision to refuse can harden the heart.

**Stages of Consequence**

Classical commentators connected the repeated warning with stages such as death, the grave, resurrection, and Hell, while others treated the two verses as emphatic speech without assigning a fixed sequence. These interpretations are best held as possibilities rather than claimed as a single definitive detail. The shared meaning is that the arrogant person faces escalating consequences.

The Qur'an describes this principle elsewhere:

> **“So when they deviated, God caused their hearts to deviate. And God does not guide the defiantly disobedient people.”** (Qur'an 61:5)

This does not mean that God unjustly forces an innocent person into deviation. It describes divine judgment upon a person who first chooses deviation. Repeated refusal becomes a condition in which the person is left to the direction he insisted upon.

**The Mathematics of Repeated Choice**

A person's character is formed through small repetitions. One act of anger does not make an angry person, but repeated unrestrained anger trains the body and mind to react that way. One lie does not define an entire life, but repeated lying makes truth more difficult to speak. One missed prayer can be repaired; a deliberate routine of neglect turns absence into identity.

The repeated “nearer” therefore has a psychological application. Each choice moves something nearer: either the heart's return to God or the heart's distance. The direction matters even when the visible change is small. A person should not wait until the entire character is transformed before taking the first corrective step.

**A Community's Duty of Warning and Mercy**

The repetition also teaches communities to warn with patience. A parent, teacher, or reformer may need to repeat a truth many times. But warning must be joined to wisdom, compassion, and respect. The goal is not to dominate the other person or enjoy his embarrassment. It is to create a chance for recognition and return.

The Prophet ﷺ was told:

> **“Remind, for reminding benefits the believers.”** (Qur'an 51:55)

The same reminder may irritate the arrogant and heal the sincere. The caller to truth does not control the response, but he should preserve the quality of the call.

**A Personal Pause Before “Then”**

The word *then* should prompt the reader to interrupt the sequence before it advances. Before the next lie, stop. Before the next act of mockery, stop. Before the next prayer is abandoned, stop. Before pride turns correction into insult, stop. Repentance is a form of breaking the chain of “then.”

The two verses are not written so that the reader may admire the punishment of an ancient denier. They are written so that the living person may recognize the momentum of denial and turn while the warning is still heard.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:36]

> **Does man suppose that he would be left aimless?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Human Life Is Not Without Purpose**

The verse asks whether the human being thinks he will be left *sudā*, aimless, neglected, or without command and accountability. The word can describe something left unattended or an animal released without a guide. The question therefore contains two meanings: will humanity be left without resurrection, and will humanity be left without moral responsibility in this life?

The answer is no. God did not create human beings as abandoned creatures whose existence has no direction. He gave them capacities, revelation, signs, and responsibility, and He will return them for judgment.

**Against Meaninglessness**

A worldview that denies purpose must explain why human beings experience obligation, seek justice, and distinguish between dignity and exploitation. It can describe biological processes, social development, and psychological tendencies, but description alone does not answer the question of what people ought to do. The Qur'an joins origin, purpose, and return:

> **“Did you suppose that We created you in vain and that unto Us you would not be returned?”** (Qur'an 23:115)

The word “vain” does not mean that worldly life contains no pleasure or creativity. It means that creation is not a pointless accident. Human beings are free to choose within their capacity, but they are not free from meaning or accountability.

**The Human Being Is Not Left Without Guidance**

God's purpose is not hidden entirely in abstraction. The Qur'an says:

> **“And I did not create jinn and mankind except that they worship Me.”** (Qur'an 51:56)

Worship (*ʿibādah*) is broader than ritual. Ibn Taymiyyah defined it as a comprehensive name for everything God loves and is pleased with, including inward beliefs, speech, and actions. The purpose of worship includes prayer, but also truthfulness, justice, mercy, lawful work, care for parents, and protection of the weak when done for God and by His guidance.

The verse corrects the idea that purpose means a single occupation or worldly achievement. A person's career may change, health may decline, and social role may be lost, but servanthood remains. This gives dignity to every stage of life.

**Freedom Within Responsibility**

Being created for a purpose does not mean that human beings are robots. The Qur'an repeatedly attributes choices to people and holds them accountable. It also affirms God's complete knowledge and power. Classical Sunni theology rejected both the idea that humans create their acts independently of God and the idea that they are forced without meaningful choice. God creates and enables; the servant intends and acts, and is judged with perfect justice.

The Qur'an says:

> **“For whoever among you wills to take a straight path. And you do not will unless God wills, Lord of the worlds.”** (Qur'an 81:28–29)

The two statements are not contradictory. Human willing is real, but it is dependent. The dependent will remains responsible because the person chooses what he wants and pursues it, even though he could not exist or act outside God's sustaining power.

**Aimlessness in Modern Life**

A person can have many goals and still be aimless. A crowded schedule is not the same as a meaningful direction. People may move from task to task while never asking what kind of person their tasks are making them. The verse invites a distinction between activity and purpose. Wealth, fame, and achievement can be tools, but they cannot answer why one should be honest, whom one should serve, or what makes a life successful.

The believer uses goals under a higher aim. Education becomes a means to beneficial knowledge; work becomes a means of lawful provision and service; family becomes a trust; leisure becomes a permitted renewal rather than an idol.

**A Purpose That Survives Death**

If purpose ended at death, many of the most important sacrifices would be irrational. The martyr, the caregiver, the honest poor person, and the person who forgives when revenge is available all act in ways that make sense under divine accountability. The Hereafter gives final meaning to the moral effort of life.

The question of 75:36 is therefore a question about both creation and judgment. Are you living as though God made you without intention and will leave you without account? The answer should reshape the day's priorities: you are a servant, you have been entrusted with time and choice, and you are returning to the Lord.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:37]

> **Was he not a drop of semen emitted?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Beginning From a Humble Substance**

The final argument begins with the human origin: *a-lam yaku nuṭfatan min maniyy yumnā*. *Nuṭfah* means a small quantity or drop of fluid. *Maniyy* refers to reproductive fluid, and *yumnā* can mean “emitted” or “cast.” The wording directs human pride back to its beginning. The person who questions resurrection began as a tiny, dependent substance without knowledge, strength, or control.

The verse is not degrading lawful human procreation. It corrects arrogance by revealing dependence. Human beings are often proud of achievements that would not exist without a body, family, environment, and chain of causes they did not create. The beginning of life reminds the person that he is a created being from the first moment.

**The Argument From First Creation**

The logic is straightforward: if God can bring a complete, conscious human being from a minute drop, why should re-creation after death be impossible? The person who finds resurrection difficult must explain why the first creation is not equally astonishing. Habit makes birth seem ordinary, but ordinary does not mean self-explanatory.

The Qur'an states:

> **“O mankind, if you are in doubt about resurrection, then indeed We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging form, then from a formed and unformed lump, that We may make clear to you.”** (Qur'an 22:5)

The sequence invites reflection rather than uncontrolled speculation. It tells the reader that development occurs through stages by divine determination, and that the same Lord who initiates life can restore it.

**Human Dependence and Social Humility**

Every person begins dependent upon others. He cannot feed, clean, or protect himself. Later strength can create the illusion of self-sufficiency, but the original condition remains a theological fact. A person does not become self-created because he eventually gains skills.

This has social implications. If human beings remember their origin, they should be less contemptuous of infants, the elderly, the disabled, the sick, and the poor. Dependency is part of human life, not a defect belonging only to other people. The powerful will one day become weak, and the healthy will one day need care.

**The Ethical Meaning of Reproduction**

The verse also places human sexuality within God's creative order. Reproduction is not merely an arena of appetite; it is a trust through which new persons enter the world. Islamic law protects lineage, marriage, parental responsibility, and the dignity of the child. The smallness of the beginning increases the obligation to protect the life that develops from it.

The verse should not be used to shame someone for infertility or to make moral judgments about a person's worth based on biological ability. Its target is human arrogance and denial of the Creator, not those who experience a particular biological condition. God creates with wisdom, and human dignity is not measured by fertility.

**From Microscopic Beginning to Moral Person**

The drop itself does not explain the emergence of consciousness, moral responsibility, language, love, and worship. It is a material beginning, but the human being is more than material description. The Qur'an repeatedly directs attention to the process of formation and the giving of faculties. The person who began without awareness becomes a responsible agent by God's creation and enabling.

This should produce gratitude. The human being did not design his first breath, choose his parents, or manufacture the conditions of his birth. Gratitude toward God and mercy toward other people are rational responses to such dependence.

**A Sign Against Postponement**

The verse is also a warning to the person who says that origin is too small to matter. Life develops through stages, and moral change does as well. A person may begin repentance with a single sincere admission, just as the body begins from a small drop. Do not despise a small beginning if it is directed toward God. The Creator who brings great life from a tiny origin can bring great transformation from a small act of truth.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:38]

> **Then he was a blood clot; whereupon He created, then fashioned,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Stages Governed by Divine Formation**

The verse continues: the human being became an *ʿalaqah*, then God created and proportioned him. *ʿAlaqah* is related to something that clings or hangs, and classical Arabic explanations include a clinging form or blood-like stage. The Qur'an's purpose is not to provide a technical embryology textbook but to direct reflection toward ordered formation. The human being is not self-assembling. He passes through stages under divine knowledge.

The paired verbs *khalaqa fa-sawwā* mean that God created and then proportioned, shaped, or brought the parts into order. Creation indicates bringing into existence; fashioning indicates giving a coherent form and balance. The verse compresses a vast process into two verbs so that the reader sees the governing act rather than becoming lost in detail.

**The Qur'an's Language and Scientific Humility**

A responsible reading avoids two extremes. It should not force every Arabic word into a modern scientific theory that may later change. Nor should it ignore the verse's invitation to observe development. The Qur'an speaks to people of every era in language accessible to them, while its signs may become more deeply appreciated as knowledge expands.

The same principle applies to hadith. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“The creation of each one of you is brought together in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop, then as a clinging form for a similar period, then as a lump for a similar period...”** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 3208; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2643)

Scholars have discussed the relation between the hadith's wording, different narrations, and stages of development. The core teaching is that human life unfolds by God's determination and that the angelic record concerns the person's provision, lifespan, deeds, and final state. It should be cited with care rather than turned into a contest of exaggerated claims.

**Creation and Proportion in Everyday Life**

The verb *sawwā* also appears in the Qur'an's descriptions of the balanced creation of the human being:

> **“O man, what has deceived you concerning your Lord, the Generous, Who created you, proportioned you, and balanced you, and assembled you in whatever form He willed?”** (Qur'an 82:6–8)

The body is not only alive; it is organized. Eyes are positioned to see, lungs to exchange air, the heart to circulate blood, and the brain to integrate perception and action. The human being's capacity to reason and choose depends upon an extraordinary harmony. This should lead to wonder without requiring the believer to deny natural processes. Natural processes are themselves part of the created order.

**The Stages of Moral Formation**

There is an inward analogy. A moral character is also created and fashioned through stages. A person begins with an impulse, repeats an action, forms a habit, and gradually becomes known by a trait. Just as the body is formed by stages, the soul is shaped by repeated choices. The Qur'an says:

> **“Successful indeed is the one who purifies it, and failed indeed is the one who corrupts it.”** (Qur'an 91:9–10)

The person should not expect a mature character without patient formation. Prayer, truthful speech, and restraint may feel small at first, but they proportion the soul. Likewise, small compromises can deform it.

**The Divine Name Behind the Process**

The verse points to God's power, knowledge, and wisdom. He is the Creator who knows what He brings into existence, the Fashioner who gives every part its form, and the Sustainer who carries the process forward. The Qur'an says:

> **“He is God, the Creator, the Originator, the Fashioner; to Him belong the Most Beautiful Names.”** (Qur'an 59:24)

The believer sees his own body as a sign of these attributes. The sign is not worshipped; it directs worship toward the One who formed it.

**A Reason to Reject Despair**

The stages also offer hope. A person may look at his current weakness and assume that change is impossible. But God's creation proceeds through transformation. A drop becomes a person; a heedless heart can become a repentant heart; a damaged relationship can begin repair; an ignorant person can learn. Human effort is necessary, but it is not alone. The One who fashions can refashion a life through guidance and repentance.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:39]

> **and made from him the two genders, male and female.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Human Difference Within One Created Origin**

The verse says that God made from the human being the two genders, male and female. The expression completes the argument from embryonic origin by pointing to the ordered differentiation of human life. Men and women share the same human origin, the same dependence upon God, the same moral capacity, and the same return to Him. Difference of sex is part of creation, not a basis for denying common dignity.

The Qur'an elsewhere states:

> **“O mankind, reverence your Lord, Who created you from one soul and created from it its mate and dispersed from both many men and women.”** (Qur'an 4:1)

The phrase “one soul” establishes unity before difference. Humanity is not divided into separate species of value. Gender difference may involve distinct physical realities and, within Islamic law, some distinct responsibilities, but all persons remain servants of God and accountable according to their own deeds.

**Creation as a Sign of Divine Wisdom**

The verse describes male and female as part of God's creative act. This does not mean that every individual fits every social stereotype or that biological difference answers every question of personality. It means that human reproduction and the continuity of the species are not random accidents outside divine knowledge.

The Qur'an says:

> **“And of everything We created pairs, that perhaps you may remember.”** (Qur'an 51:49)

The existence of pairs in creation points to dependence and complementarity, though the Creator Himself is beyond being a pair or having an equal. Created relationships require one another; God requires nothing. The verse directs gratitude and responsibility within family and community life.

**Equal Worth and Different Accountability**

Islamic justice does not require that every person have identical circumstances or identical tasks. It requires that God judge each person fairly. The Qur'an says:

> **“Whoever does righteous deeds, whether male or female, while being a believer—We will surely give them a good life, and We will surely reward them according to the best of what they used to do.”** (Qur'an 16:97)

And:

> **“Indeed, the Muslim men and Muslim women, believing men and believing women... God has prepared for them forgiveness and a great reward.”** (Qur'an 33:35)

These verses establish shared spiritual worth. A person is not nearer to God by gender, lineage, or public status, but by faith and taqwā. The legal and social responsibilities of men and women must therefore be understood within a larger framework of dignity, protection, justice, and accountability.

**The Family as a Trust**

The existence of male and female makes family and kinship possible, but family is not automatically righteous. It becomes a trust through mercy, fidelity, provision, upbringing, and justice. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **“The best of you are the best of you to their families, and I am the best of you to my family.”** (Sunan al-Tirmidhī, 3895; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Tirmidhī)

The hadith brings the verse into ordinary life. A person's religious claim is tested in the home: how he treats a spouse, child, parent, or dependent. Biological creation is a sign; ethical family life is the response to that sign.

**Beyond Arrogance About Gender**

Human beings may turn created difference into domination, contempt, or envy. Some treat gender as a reason to claim superiority; others deny all meaningful difference and reduce people to social constructions or competing appetites. The Qur'anic approach begins with creation and ends with accountability. Neither gender is a god, and neither is a disposable category.

The verse also comforts people whose family circumstances differ from their hopes. Not every person marries, has children, or experiences an uncomplicated identity. God's creative wisdom is not measured only by the life pattern a society praises. Every person remains honored as a servant and judged by what he knows and chooses.

**A Step Toward the Final Argument**

The verse has moved from a drop to a clot, from formation to sex, and now prepares the conclusion: if God can create and distinguish the human being through these stages, why should resurrection be denied? The biological diversity of humanity is not a problem for divine power. It is evidence that divine power already operates through precise, varied formation.

## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah [75:40]

> **Is not such a one able to give life to the dead?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Question Contains Its Answer**

The sūrah ends with a rhetorical question: *alaysa dhālika bi-qādirin ʿalā an yuḥyiya al-mawtā?* The One who created the human being from an emitted drop, formed him through stages, proportioned him, and made him male and female—is He not able to give life to the dead? The expected answer is yes. The final verse returns to the beginning of the argument and closes it through the evidence of human origin.

The demonstrative *dhālika*—“such a one”—points back to God described through the acts just mentioned. The question is not about an unknown power. It is about the same Lord whose creative activity is visible in every person. The denier has been asked to look at himself and recognize the evidence he carries.

**From First Creation to Re-Creation**

The logic can be stated in three steps. First, the human being exists through a process he did not initiate or control. Second, that process involves precise knowledge, power, and ordering. Third, the One who brought the human being from nonexistence can restore life after death. The Qur'an expresses the same conclusion elsewhere:

> **“And he sets forth for Us an example and forgets his own creation. He says, ‘Who will give life to bones while they are disintegrated?’ Say: ‘He will give them life Who produced them the first time, and He is Knowing of all creation.’”** (Qur'an 36:78–79)

The argument does not require the listener to understand every mechanism of resurrection. It requires him to stop treating his limited imagination as the measure of divine ability.

**Resurrection Is Required by Justice and Wisdom**

The possibility of resurrection is established by creation, but its necessity is also connected to justice. The world contains sincere people who suffer and corrupt people who prosper. Some wrongs are repaired in this life; many are not. If death ended the account, justice would remain incomplete. The Qur'an says:

> **“Did those who commit evil deeds suppose that We would make them like those who believe and do righteous deeds, equal in their life and their death? Evil is what they judge.”** (Qur'an 45:21)

The Hereafter does not mean that God is unable to judge now. It means that the present world is a limited arena in which human choice is tested, not the complete courtroom. The final Day discloses the difference between lives that worldly appearances made to look equal.

**The Closing “Yea” in the Sunnah**

Reports transmitted in the works of tafsīr and hadith, with variations in their chains, state that when the Prophet ﷺ recited the final verse he would respond:

> **“Yes.”** (Reported in the tafsīr tradition; the reports of this practice have varying chains)

The response fits the verse's rhetorical form: the believer does not leave the question suspended. He affirms God's power.

This affirmation is not a formula detached from action. If God can resurrect the dead, He can also revive a heart, restore a community, and bring justice out of circumstances that appear hopeless. Faith in resurrection therefore creates both accountability and hope.

**The Last Day Begins With What We Do Today**

The sūrah began with the Day of Resurrection and the blaming soul, moved through denial and cosmic upheaval, described the final record and the last breath, and ended with the first stages of life. Its structure teaches that the Hereafter is connected to the whole human story. The person who will rise is the person who was formed, tested, warned, and given opportunities to repent.

The final question therefore remains directed at the living reader: if God created you once, why do you doubt that He can raise you? If you will stand before Him, what are you sending ahead? If the self-reproaching soul is still alive within you, what truth is it asking you to face? The answer to the final verse should be heard as a commitment: yes, God is able; therefore I will prepare to meet Him.

**The Chapter's Final Call**

Sūrah al-Qiyāmah does not end by satisfying curiosity about the unseen. It ends by demanding moral clarity. The Resurrection is certain, the record is complete, death is near, revelation is preserved, and creation itself testifies. The person who accepts these truths does not become detached from life; he becomes more trustworthy within it. He worships without pretending, uses the world without being enslaved by it, repents while time remains, and treats other people as fellow servants who will also stand before God.

The final question is a question of power, but it also becomes a question of trust. The One able to raise the dead is able to judge justly, forgive generously, guide the confused, and reward the faithful. To affirm His power is to entrust one's life to His wisdom and to live in preparation for the day when every soul is driven to its Lord.
